CMAH‐Targeted Knock‐In of Inducible Heme Oxygenase‐1 and Constitutive CD47 in GGTA1‐Knockout Pigs for Xenotransplantation
Authors: Haesun Lee, Sang Eun Kim, Won Kil Lee, Miae Oh, Seunghoon Lee, Jin‐Gu No, Mi‐Ryung Park, Seokho Kim, Min Hwa Do, Keon Bong Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPig xenografts offer a solution to human organ shortage; however, immune rejection is a major barrier. Immunomodulatory genes such as heme oxygenase‐1 (HO1) and CD47 are key. Precise promoter control and strategic genomic integration for reliable expression and xenoantigen disruption are critical. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a promoter‐pairing strategy for HO1 and CD47 in genetically modified pigs to achieve context‐appropriate expression and enhance xenograft success.MethodsWe analyzed the transcriptional profiles of xenoantigens (GGTA1, B4GALNT2, and CMAH) in pig tissues using qPCR. Exon 4 of CMAH was selected for knock‐in because of its low activity and xenoantigen role, enabling precise promoter‐driven transgene expression and xenoantigen disruption. A dual‐promoter cassette (inducible HO1 and constitutive CD47) was inserted into the CMAH locus of GGTA1‐knockout fibroblasts using CRISPR/Cas9. The screened clones were used for somatic cell nuclear transfer to generate pigs.Results CMAH showed significantly lower and more consistent expression than did GGTA1/B4GALNT2 across tissues. In 293T cells and primary pig fibroblasts, HO1 was low at baseline but strongly induced by human serum/PMA, whereas CD47 exhibited high basal expression with inducibility. Cloned pigs with the HO1/CD47 cassette in the CMAH locus were successfully generated and validated. HO1 protein localized mainly to the liver and lungs, while CD47 was broadly expressed in tissues/blood leukocytes, confirming the tissue‐specific and stimulus‐responsive functions of the dual promoter.ConclusionThis study successfully established a promoter‐optimized locus‐specific knock‐in strategy for inducing HO1 and constitutive CD47 in GGTA1‐knockout pigs. This dual‐promoter system enabled context‐appropriate expression and enhanced xenograft compatibility. Future in vivo studies are crucial to evaluate long‐term graft survival and HO1 inducibility, paving the way for clinical xenotransplantation.Keywords: CD47, CMAH locus, gene‐specific promoter, heme oxygenase‐1(HO1), knock‐in strategy, transgenic pig, xenotransplantation

Introduction

The immunomodulatory genes heme oxygenase‐1 (HO1) and CD47 have emerged as key molecular tools in developing genetically modified pigs for xenotransplantation. HO1 is the rate‐limiting enzyme in heme catabolism, converting heme into biliverdin, carbon monoxide, and free iron. These bioactive catabolites exert cytoprotective effects by attenuating oxidative stress, suppressing inflammation, and inhibiting apoptosis [1, 2, 3]. Given the role of inflammation in rejection and coagulation in both allo‐ and xenotransplantation, creating transgenic pigs overexpressing HO1 is a valuable approach to enhance xenograft success [4, 5]. Despite the well‐documented benefits, HO1 expression is tightly regulated. Uncontrolled or constitutive overexpression can disrupt redox signaling, alter cellular metabolism, and, depending on the tissue context, may even contribute to pathological angiogenesis or tumor progression [6]. These risks underscore the need for promoter systems that can recapitulate physiological regulation. The human HO1 promoter contains antioxidant response elements (AREs) that respond to redox‐sensitive transcription factors such as Nrf2 and Bach1, enabling inflammation‐ or oxidative stress‐induced expression while maintaining low basal activity [2, 7].

Meanwhile, CD47 plays an essential role in immune evasion by engaging signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) on macrophages and serves as a “marker of self” to prevent phagocytosis [8, 9, 10]. Due to the incompatibility between pig CD47 and human SIRPα, pig cells remain vulnerable to macrophage‐mediated clearance even after triple xenoantigen knockout. Transgenic expression of human CD47 in pigs has been shown to significantly reduce phagocytosis and prolong xenograft survival in non‐human primate models [4].

Because HO1 and CD47 exert distinct immunomodulatory functions, we employed a promoter‐pairing strategy that reflected their biological roles. HO1 expression is driven by its native human promoter, which ensures low basal transcription and selective induction under oxidative or inflammatory stress, thereby reducing the risk of cytotoxicity associated with its constitutive overexpression. In contrast, CD47 expression was controlled by the pig elongation factor 1α (pEF1α) promoter, which we previously showed to maintain strong and stable activity across pig fibroblasts, kidney epithelial cells, and endothelial cells while remaining functionally responsive to xenogeneic serum stimulation [11]. This dual‐promoter configuration allows HO1 to function as a context‐dependent stress‐response gene while ensuring continuous immune suppression by CD47 at the graft interface.

We have progressively developed a modular knock‐in framework by inserting human immune‐regulatory genes into exon 9 of the glycoprotein alpha‐1, 3‐galactosyltransferase 1 (GGTA1) gene to simultaneously achieve antigen elimination and functional transgene expression [12]. In this study, we developed a locus‐specific, promoter‐optimized knock‐in strategy to generate modified cells and functionally enhanced pig donors for xenotransplantation. By inserting a dual‐expression cassette encoding human HO1 and CD47 into the cytidine monophosphate‐N‐acetylneuraminic acid hydroxylase (CMAH) locus of GGTA1‐deficient cells and utilizing CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated homologous recombination and somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT), we achieved controlled co‐expression of these crucial immunomodulatory genes in cloned pigs. This approach, designed to provide inflammation‐inducible HO1 and consistent CD47 expression, offers a promising platform for further investigations to improve xenotransplantation outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval

The protocols and standard operating procedures for the treatment of pigs used in this study were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institute of Animal Science, Rural Development Administration of Korea (Approval Numbers: NIAS2019‐0367 and NIAS2024‐0029). All the methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the committee.

Quantitative Analysis of Pig Xenoantigen Gene Expression in Tissues

To evaluate the transcriptional profiles of the major pig xenoantigen genes (GGTA1, CMAH, and beta‐1,4‐N‐acetyl‐galactosaminyl transferase 2 (B4GALNT2)), quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR) was performed using total RNA extracted from six tissues (heart, liver, lung, spleen, kidney, and aorta) of wild‐type pigs. RNA was isolated using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng of RNA using a SuperScript IV First‐Strand Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, VA, USA). For absolute quantification, standard curves were generated using genomic DNA extracted from wild‐type pig fibroblasts using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). The DNA was serially diluted (100, 10, 1, 0.1, and 0.01 ng), and the copy number corresponding to 100 ng was calculated based on the Sus scrofa 11.1 genome (3.6 × 104 copies). For B4GALNT2, which shares high sequence homology with LOC110255214, the copy number was doubled to account for potential co‐amplification. Both beta‐actin (ACTB) and 18S rRNA (RN18S) were used as endogenous controls for tissue samples. Standard curves for reference genes were generated using cDNA pooled from tissue RNA and serially diluted to 1, 1/5, 1/25, 1/125, and 1/625, with the undiluted sample assigned a relative value of 100. Real‐time PCR reactions were conducted using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) in a total volume of 20 µL, including 10 pmol of each primer (Bionics, Seoul, Korea), on a StepOne Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) under default cycling conditions. Data were analyzed using StepOne Software v2.3 (Applied Biosystems) with the relative standard curve method. Only results with a standard curve R 2 > 0.99 and PCR efficiency between 90% and 110% were included. Final values were expressed as normalized copy numbers per nanogram of RNA. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Construction of HO1/CD47 Expression and Knock‐In Vectors

To construct an inflammation‐ and apoptosis‐modulating expression vector, human HO1 cDNA (accession number: NM_002133.3) and CD47 cDNA (accession number: NM_001777.3) sequences were synthesized. For HO1 expression, a 1.4‐kb region from −1338 to +71 relative to the transcription start site of the gene was used as the promoter. Additionally, a 220‐bp enhancer sequence derived from HO1 intron 1 and part of exon 2 was inserted at the 5′‐end of the promoter to enhance inducibility. To drive CD47 expression, the pEF1α promoter [11] was cloned into the vector using KpnI and BglII restriction sites. The HO1 and CD47 expression cassettes were arranged in opposite orientations within a single plasmid, allowing each gene to be independently regulated by its own promoter. For knock‐in into the pig CMAH locus, the expression cassette was flanked by 504 5′ and 509 3′ homology arms corresponding to the exon 4 region of the pig CMAH gene. To prevent random genomic integration of the cassette, a diphtheria toxin A (DT‐A)‐negative selection marker was inserted upstream of the 5′ homology arms, completing the construction of the site‐specific knock‐in vector.

Transient Transfection and Inducible Gene Expression Analysis in HEK‐293T Cells

Human embryonic kidney HEK‐293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Invitrogen) and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (Invitrogen) under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). The HO1/CD47 dual‐expression vector was introduced into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (#L3000015; Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's protocol. To evaluate inducible gene expression, the transfected cells were exposed to phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) or human serum. For PMA stimulation, cells were treated with 500 nM PMA for 24 h. For human serum stimulation, 10% human serum was added to the culture medium, and samples were collected at 0, 4, 12, and 24 h post‐treatment. The control group did not receive any stimuli.

Relative Quantification of HO1 and CD47 Gene Expression in Cells

qPCR was performed using Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in 20‐µL reaction volumes containing 10 µL of 2× SYBR mix, 0.5 µL each of gene‐specific forward and reverse primers (10 pmol), and 1 µL of cDNA template. Amplification was conducted on a StepOne Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) under the following cycling conditions: 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min for 40 cycles. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH as the internal reference gene, and relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in triplicate. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S2.

Primary Cell Isolation, Culture, Genome Editing, and Nucleofection

Pig ear fibroblasts (pEFs) were isolated from a 1‐week‐old GGTA1‐knockout (GTKO) piglet and cultured in DMEM (Invitrogen) supplemented with 15% FBS (Invitrogen), 1% non‐essential amino acids (Invitrogen), 0.1 mM β‐mercaptoethanol (Invitrogen), 1% antibiotic–antimycotic (Invitrogen), and 0.1× Low Serum Growth Supplement (Invitrogen). Pig aortic endothelial cells (pAECs) were obtained from the thoracic aorta of a 1‐month‐old GTKO male pig. Briefly, one end of the excised vessel was ligated and filled with 0.1% collagenase type I (Sigma‐Aldrich), followed by incubation at 37°C for 10 min. The enzymatically dissociated cells were flushed, seeded onto culture dishes, and maintained in endothelial growth medium (Lonza, Köln, Germany). For genome editing, 5 × 105 pEFs were mixed with 100 µL of nucleofection solution containing 2 µg of the knock‐in vector, 2.7 µg of sgRNA (targeting CMAH exon 4, sequence: 5′‐TCGTCTTGACAGAAGCTTCCAGG‐3′), and 10.8 µg of Cas9 protein. Subsequently, the mixture was nucleofected using the program U‐023 (Amaxa Nucleofector; Lonza) and transferred to a 60‐mm dish for recovery and expansion. The primer sequences used for off‐target site analysis are listed in Table S3.

Selection and Isolation of Knock‐In Clones

After recovery, nucleofected pEFs were labeled with FITC‐conjugated anti‐human CD47 antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and enriched for CD47‐positive cells using anti‐FITC microbeads and MS columns (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergish Gladbach, Germany). Following a second round of magnetic sorting, the enriched cells were seeded as single cells in 96‐well plates and serially diluted. After 7–10 days, monoclonal colonies were identified by microscopic inspection and sequentially expanded in 24‐ and 6‐well and 100‐mm culture dishes.

SCNT and Embryo Transfer for Cloned Pig Production

To produce cloned pigs, oocyte maturation, SCNT, reconstructed embryo production, and embryo transfer into surrogate gilts were performed as previously described [13]. The cloned piglets were delivered via natural parturition.

Genotyping PCR and Off‐Target Analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's protocol. Genotyping and off‐target PCR were performed using Prime Taq Premix (Genetbio, Daejeon, Korea); the primer sets are listed in Table S4. The PCR products were analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis.

Western Blot Analysis

Pig cells were washed thrice with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and 0.5 M EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on ice for 1 h. The total protein concentration was measured using the Bradford assay (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Next, 20 mg of protein were separated on Bolt 8% Bis‐Tris Plus precast gels (Invitrogen) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Invitrogen). Membranes were blocked overnight at 4°C in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 and 5% skim milk, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C for 16 h. After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibody. Protein expression was visualized using an ECL detection reagent (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, UK). The following primary and secondary antibodies were used: mouse monoclonal anti‐HO1 (Abcam) with anti‐mouse IgGκ BP‐HRP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA); rabbit monoclonal anti‐CD47 (Abcam) with mouse anti‐rabbit IgG‐HRP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology); and rabbit monoclonal anti‐Vinculin (Abcam) with mouse anti‐rabbit IgG‐HRP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) as a loading control.

Flow Cytometry

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from heparinized blood using density gradient centrifugation with Ficoll‐Paque (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Thereafter, PBMCs and pEFs were stained with an allophycocyanin APC‐conjugated anti‐CD47 antibody (Invitrogen). Flow cytometry was performed using a Bigfoot Spectral Cell Sorter (Invitrogen), and data were analyzed using FlowJo10.10.0 software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

Tissue sections were first deparaffinized using xylene and mounted on glass slides. Antigen retrieval was performed using a citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 100°C for 30 min. Afterward, endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, and nonspecific binding was blocked using 1.5% normal goat serum. Thereafter, the sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody against HO‐1 (sc‐136960; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at a 1:200 dilution. After washing, a biotinylated secondary antibody (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) was applied for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB substrate (ABC Kit, PK‐4001; Vector Laboratories) for 3 min, while nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, slides were mounted and examined under a light microscope.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using one‐way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 5 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was verified using Tukey's test and was considered at p values < 0.05.

Results

Baseline Expression Profiles of Pig Xenoantigen Genes Across Normal Tissues

To better understand the transcriptional characteristics of pig xenoantigen genes, we analyzed the expression levels of GGTA1, B4GALNT2, and CMAH across six different tissues (heart, liver, lung, spleen, kidney, and aorta) of normal pigs. As shown in Figure 1, GGTA1 exhibited the highest expression in all the tissues examined, followed by B4GALNT2, which showed moderate and tissue‐dependent expression. In contrast, CMAH expression remained consistently lower than that of the other two genes. These results reflect the intrinsic transcriptional profiles of each gene in normal pig tissues and may serve as a useful reference for designing knockout or knock‐in strategies in xenotransplantation models, particularly when considering promoter compatibility or background transcriptional activity.

Stimulus‐Responsive Expression of HO1 and CD47 Under Xenogeneic and Inflammatory Conditions

The stimulus‐responsive expression of HO1 and CD47 in GTKO fibroblasts is shown in Figure 2 through Western blotting (left) and bar graphs. We introduced a dual‐expression construct into GTKO fibroblasts, in which human HO1 was driven by its enhanced native promoter and CD47 by the pig EF1α promoter (Figure 2A). We exposed the transfected cells to 10% human serum and the inflammatory agent PMA (500 nM) and observed that HO1 expression increased progressively over 24 h, confirming the inducible nature of the human HO1 promoter. CD47 expression, which was already high at baseline, further increased upon stimulation. This finding highlights the dual nature of promoter design: stimulus‐dependent regulation of HO1 and constitutive, yet upregulated, CD47 expression, aligning with previous observations of pEF1α activity [11].

Establishment and Validation of GTKO/CMAH‐KI/HO1/CD47/+ Donor Cells

We then developed GTKO donor cells with the targeted integration of HO1 and CD47 at exon 4 of the CMAH locus using the CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated knock‐in strategy (Figure 3A). The dual‐expression cassette was flanked by ∼500 bp homology arms and introduced into GTKO fetal fibroblasts through nucleofection, together with the Cas9 protein and in vitro‐transcribed sgRNA. After 48 h of recovery, nucleofected cells were labeled with FITC‐conjugated anti‐CD47 antibody and enriched using anti‐FITC microbeads and MS columns (magnetic separation, Table S5). From subsequent single‐cell plating, 240 CD47‐enriched single‐cell–derived clones were obtained. We screened 118 clones using two PCR assays: HO1‐F/CD47‐R to confirm internal cassette presence and CMAH‐F/HO1‐R to verify 5′ junctional integration. We identified six positive clones (5.1% PCR‐positive integration efficiency; Figure 3B, Table 1), excluding clone #4 due to chromosomal abnormalities. We also confirmed genome editing specificity by sequencing two potential off‐target loci with ≤3 mismatches to the sgRNA. None of the five validated clones (#112, #175, #211, #215, and #299) exhibited off‐target mutations [14] (Table S3). To evaluate the functional impact of the knock‐in, donor cells were treated with 10% human serum for 4 h. We found that all knock‐in clones showed significantly reduced cytotoxicity compared with that in mock‐transfected controls (Figure 3C), suggesting that HO1 and CD47 expression effectively conferred resistance against xenogeneic serum‐mediated damage.

Generation and Molecular Validation of GTKO/CMAH‐KI/HO1/CD47/ + Cloned Pigs

We selected knock‐in clone #229 for SCNT and transferred reconstructed embryos into LY‐crossbred surrogate gilts. On day 118 of gestation, we obtained two piglets via cesarean section on March 14, 2023. Piglet #1 (485 g) survived and remained healthy, whereas piglet #2 (650 g) died 3 days postpartum. Transgene Expression in Tissues of Cloned Pigs

We examined the tissue‐level expression of the transgenes in cloned piglets using Western blotting, flow cytometry, and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Western blotting revealed strong HO1 and CD47 protein expression in the liver, lung, and spleen, whereas their expression was lower in the heart and kidney (Figure 5). These results aligned with the expected activity of the respective promoters, that is, inducible HO1 expression and constitutive CD47 expression. We further confirmed robust CD47 surface expression in PEFs and PBMCs using flow cytometry. CD47 positivity exceeded 94% in both monocytes and lymphocytes of knock‐in pigs compared with the <2% in GTKO controls (Figure 6). Finally, we evaluated the spatial localization of HO1 using IHC. We observed positive HO1 staining in the liver and lungs but not in the heart, kidney, or spleen (Figure 7). Tissues from the GTKO control pigs showed no HO1 signal. These results support the hypothesis that the human HO1 promoter remains largely inactive under resting physiological conditions but may be inducible upon inflammatory stimulation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a promoter‐optimized, locus‐specific knock‐in strategy for the expression of human HO1 and CD47 in genetically modified pigs. Our goal was to enable context‐appropriate expression of these two immunomodulatory genes, reflecting their distinct biological functions in a xenograft setting. We targeted the CMAH‐specific locus, not primarily for its transcriptional activity, which was consistently low across tissues (Figure 1), but because of its role as a xenoantigen and suitability for site‐specific genome editing. Using CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated targeted insertion into exon 4 of CMAH [15], we generated donor cells and cloned pigs that co‐expressed HO1 and CD47 under customized promoters. Functional and expression analyses confirmed the success of this approach and provided several insights into the future design of transgenic pigs for xenotransplantation.

HO‐1 is a highly inducible cytoprotective enzyme with well‐documented anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti‐apoptotic effects [1, 2, 6]. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that transgenic expression of human HO‐1 in pigs provides significant protection during ex vivo perfusion of pig kidneys with human blood [16]. This observation suggests that exposure of pig endothelial and parenchymal cells to xenogeneic blood components‐including human cytokines, complement factors, and oxidative mediators‐creates a stress environment that can trigger HO‐1 induction as part of an intrinsic cytoprotective response. Although the precise intracellular signaling remains to be elucidated, such xenogeneic stimulation likely activates redox‐sensitive pathways (e.g., Nrf2‐ARE or AP‐1‐dependent transcription) that enhance HO‐1 expression [3]. Consistent with this interpretation, HO‐1 expression in our donor cells remained low at baseline but was markedly upregulated following exposure to human serum or PMA stimulation.

In cloned pigs, HO‐1 protein was predominantly detected in the liver and lungs, with little or no expression observed in other tissues, such as the heart and kidney. These findings supported the functionality of our promoter design in enabling context‐dependent HO‐1 expression. The positive effects of HO‐1 overexpression on graft survival, as shown by Araujo et al. [5], provide a foundation for our approach, which aims to achieve context‐appropriate HO‐1 modulation via inducible expression.

Recent xenotransplantation models have employed strong constitutive promoters to drive continuous HO1 expression, with pig kidneys demonstrating long‐term graft survival in non‐human primates, with some exceeding 6 months of function [4, 17, 18]. These approaches have progressed to early‐phase clinical trials [19, 20, 21]. These results support the therapeutic potential of HO‐1 overexpression during transplantation. However, whether conditionally regulated expression, as implemented in our model, provides comparable graft protection with a potentially reduced physiological burden remains to be determined. Further evaluation using preclinical transplantation models is necessary to assess the efficacy and safety of inducible HO‐1 expression strategies in vivo.

Among the three major xenoantigen genes in pigs, high tissue‐wide expression of GGTA1 has been exploited in recent studies to drive robust transgene expression via an endogenous promoter [22]. This result underscores the utility of GGTA1 as a transcriptionally active locus suitable for driving high‐level expression of immunomodulatory genes. We introduced the HO1/CD47 cassette into CMAH exon 4 in donor cells derived from GGTA1 exon 8 knockout pigs, thereby combining xenoantigen disruption with functional gene addition. Importantly, we employed gene‐specific promoters (the human HO1 promoter and pig EF1α) to regulate transgene expression independently of the endogenous CMAH transcriptional profile. Although GGTA1 remains a promising target for high‐expression knock‐ins, we plan to generate next‐generation donor pigs by targeting the GGTA1 start codon for simultaneous gene disruption and cassette insertion. Therefore, our strategy highlights an alternative approach in which promoter choice, rather than locus activity, dictates transgene expression dynamics.

Given that the vascular endothelium is the first donor‐derived interface that interacts with the recipient's immune system after transplantation, endothelial cells represent a highly relevant model for evaluating the immunomodulatory functions of HO1 and CD47. We attempted to isolate primary porcine aortic endothelial cells (pAECs) from the transgenic offspring to assess their gene expression and functions; however, this initial attempt was unsuccessful due to technical contamination.

To address this limitation and continue progressing toward functional validation, we are currently pursuing a cross‐breeding strategy aimed at generating the multi‐gene model GTKO/MCP/TBM/CMAH‐KI/HO1/CD47/+ pigs. Once this lineage is successfully produced, we plan to conduct non‐clinical kidney xenotransplantation studies. During these procedures, endothelial cells will be isolated directly from the retrieved vasculature (e.g., renal arteries and veins), providing a robust and in vivo–exposed endothelial source that circumvents the previous technical challenges. This approach will enable definitive evaluation of both CD47‐mediated anti‐phagocytic activity and HO1‐dependent anti‐inflammatory and cytoprotective effects. These functional analyses will form the foundation of a dedicated follow‐up study focused on validating the efficacy of the final multi‐gene xenograft model.

In summary, our study presents a targeted and functionally informed approach for transgene integration in pigs for xenotransplantation. By combining promoter‐specific regulation with a strategically selected antigen locus, we demonstrated the feasibility of achieving tissue‐ and stimulus‐appropriate expression of immunomodulatory genes. This dual‐promoter system offers a modular platform that can be adapted to various transgene combinations and biological needs. Future studies are required to evaluate the in vivo performance of this system in transplantation settings, including its impact on immune compatibility, graft survival, and systemic safety. Nevertheless, the principles demonstrated here may help inform the next generation of clinically relevant pig donor designs in which immunological functionality and physiological integrity must be precisely balanced.

Conclusion

This study explored a genetic engineering strategy to overcome immune rejection during xenotransplantation, focusing on the key pig xenoantigen CMAH. Baseline expression analysis of the xenoantigen genes (GGTA1, B4GALNT2, and CMAH) in normal pig tissues revealed distinct expression profiles. In a GTKO background, we successfully knocked in a bicistronic cassette expressing human HO1 and pig CD47 into a specific site within the CMAH locus using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Importantly, in HEK‐293T cells, HO1 demonstrated stimulus‐responsive expression, which increased upon exposure to human serum and the inflammatory agent PMA, while CD47 showed high baseline expression with further upregulation upon stimulation. This suggests that even with low baseline HO1 expression in the generated pig model, its expression can be effectively induced under inflammatory conditions following transplantation, potentially providing significant graft protection. Subsequently, we generated cloned pigs carrying this precise genetic modification, as confirmed by molecular and protein expression analyses. In conclusion, this study establishes a significant step toward enhancing the clinical applicability of xenotransplantation by precisely introducing immunomodulatory genes into the CMAH locus and highlighting the potential for inducible HO1 expression in response to inflammatory cues. Future in vivo studies are crucial to evaluate the long‐term survival and function of xenografts, along with the in vivo inducibility of HO1 under inflammatory conditions.
