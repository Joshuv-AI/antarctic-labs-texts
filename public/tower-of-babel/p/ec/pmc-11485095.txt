Archetypes of Spatial Concentration Variability of Organic Contaminants in the Atmosphere: Implications for Identifying Sources and Mapping the Gaseous Outdoor Inhalation Exposome
Authors: Faqiang Zhan, Yuening Li, Chubashini Shunthirasingham, Jenny Oh, Ying Duan Lei, Zhe Lu, Amina Ben Chaaben, Kelsey Lee, Frank A P C Gobas, Hayley Hung, Knut Breivik, Frank Wania
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhereas inhalation exposure to organic contaminants can negatively impact human health, knowledge of their spatial variability in the ambient atmosphere remains limited. We analyzed the extracts of passive air samplers deployed at 119 unique sites in Southern Canada between 2019 and 2022 for 353 organic vapors. Hierarchical clustering of the obtained data set revealed four archetypes of spatial concentration variability in the outdoor atmosphere, which are indicative of common sources and similar atmospheric dispersion behavior. “Point Source” signatures are characterized by elevated concentration in the vicinity of major release locations. A “Population” signature applies to compounds whose air concentrations are highly correlated with population density, and is associated with emissions from consumer products. The “Water Source” signature applies to substances with elevated levels in the vicinity of water bodies from which they evaporate. Another group of compounds displays a “Uniform” signature, indicative of a lack of major sources within the study area. We illustrate how such a data set, and the derived spatial patterns, can be applied to support the identification of sources, the quantification of atmospheric emissions, the modeling of air quality, and the investigation of potential inequities in inhalation exposure.Keywords: Atmospheric Organic Contaminants, Spatial Variability, Contaminant Sources, Inhalation Exposome, Passive Air Sampler

Introduction

The presence of organic contaminants in air has long been a concern for ecosystems and human health.1,2 Many diseases, such as cancer, diabetes, and obesity, have been repeatedly linked to the exposure to organic contaminants.3−6 Evaluating the potential risk associated with the exposure to organic substances requires an extensive understanding of their sources, concentrations, and spatial distributions.7−9 Despite continued efforts dedicated to this field, there is still much to unravel regarding the spatial patterns of atmospheric organic contaminants and their association with sources and environmental impacts.

In the quest to deepen the understanding of the variability in exposure, researchers may endeavor to conduct studies aimed at identifying consistent and potentially universal patterns within a comprehensive data set. However, achieving this objective is challenging when investigating atmospheric organic contaminants due to the multitude of organic substances with diverse properties and origins.10,11 Data on organic contaminants in the atmosphere are usually obtained through air monitoring programs.12−15 Representative and reliable air sampling is essential to ensuring the success of a monitoring project. Compared to active air samplers, networks of passive air samplers (PASs) have proven to be more suitable for capturing spatial variability of widely dispersed organic contaminants due to ease of handling, low cost, and operation without electricity.16,17 Additionally, time-weighted average air concentrations as provided by PAS are more suited for identifying and characterizing sources of contaminants than short-term active sampling because the latter may miss periods of contaminant emissions if these emissions occur episodically or display large temporal variability. Time-weighted average air concentrations tend to be also more relevant for inhalation exposure at the population level. Many PAS based monitoring networks, aiming to record spatial variability of organic contaminants, have been established, varying from local scale18,19 to regional20−22 or even global scales.12,23

However, few projects generate data sets that are comprehensive and large enough with respect to number and variety of both sampling sites and organic contaminants to serve in the elucidation of universal spatial patterns. Establishing such a data set is labor-intensive, requiring establishment of a large sampling network and the analysis of many samples for numerous contaminants. It also requires a PAS that is capable of unbiased sampling of organic contaminants with a wide range in volatility.24,25 A sufficiently large and diverse data set would enable the application of statistical analyses to unveil distinct patterns among different compounds, providing support for environmental impact investigations, such as the exploration of unequal distribution of the inhalation exposure to organic contaminants. However, such a comprehensive data set for atmospheric organic contaminants cannot be obtained by combining data reported in the literature due to variations in sampling and analytical techniques as well as sampling periods. This inspired us to conduct a comprehensive study to generate such a large data set, despite the tremendous efforts required.

In this study, we established two networks of XAD-2 resin based PASs for sampling gaseous organic substances16 in the coastal regions of southern Canada. Extracts were analyzed for 354 organic contaminants, including 209 polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB) congeners, 45 polycyclic aromatic compounds (PACs) (23 parent polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, PAHs; 21 alkylated-PAHs, alk-PAHs; dibenzothiophene), 24 organochlorine pesticides (OCPs), 5 halogenated methoxybenzenes (HMBs), 6 industrial organochlorine and 3 metabolic transformation products of pesticides, 32 halogenated flame retardants (HFRs), 16 organophosphate esters (OPEs), and 14 neutral poly- and perfluorinated substances (nPFAS). This effort is unprecedented, with respect to the number of sampling sites and the number of targeted organic contaminants. Since many organic chemicals have common sources and share similar atmospheric dispersion behavior, a limited number of archetypes of spatial patterns, representing typical geographical variabilities of organic vapors, can be identified through multivariate statistical analysis of the obtained data set. This is a first, but crucial, step toward mapping the entirety of the complex mix of organic vapors that human populations are exposed to in the ambient outdoor environment, i.e., the overall gaseous inhalation exposome.

Materials and Methods

Passive Air Sampler Networks

Two networks of XAD-2 resin based PASs for sampling gaseous organic substances16 were established in the Salish Sea region of British Columbia (BC) and along the St. Lawrence River and Estuary in Quebec (QC), Canada. Additionally, results from a PAS deployed as part of a calibration study25,26 in the eastern suburb of Toronto were included in the data analysis. In total, 170 samples and 74 field blanks from a total of 119 unique sites were collected (2019–2022) and analyzed. Maps of the PAS networks are provided in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information (SI) and more details on the PAS deployment are given in Text S1 and the SI Spreadsheet, and are also available in previous publications.27,28

Sample Extraction and Analysis

After spiking several groups of labeled surrogates (30–60 ng) onto the XAD-2 resin (Supelpak, 20–60 mesh) from the mesh cylinder of a PAS, it was extracted with a 1:1 mixture of hexane (Millipore Sigma, purity: > 99.9%) and acetone (Millipore Sigma, purity: > 99.9%) using Accelerated Solvent Extraction (Dionex ASE 350, Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). Details are given in Text S2 (SI). The extracts, reduced to ∼1 mL in iso-octane (Millipore Sigma, purity: > 99%) using a rotary evaporator, were dried by passing through a sodium sulfate (EMD Chemicals Inc.) column and further concentrated to 0.5 mL using gentle nitrogen blow-down. Prior to instrumental analysis, 15 ng of four injection standards in iso-octane were added to the extracts. Details on surrogates and injection standards are provided in Table S1 (SI).

Details on all 354 analytes are provided in Table S2 (SI). nPFAS were analyzed by GC-MS operated in selected ion monitoring mode under positive chemical ionization. All other substances were analyzed by GC-MS/MS using electron ionization in the multiple reaction monitoring mode. Quantification ions, confirmation ions and collision energies for 354 compounds are available in SI Spreadsheet. The instruments and details of where to find the instrumentation parameters are specified in Text S3 (SI).

Quality Assurance/Quality Control

All sample treatment was conducted in a trace analytical laboratory. All glassware was washed with detergent, rinsed with deionized water, and baked at 450 °C for 24 h before use. Other materials, such as pipettes, tweezers, funnels, and ASE cells, were cleaned and rinsed with acetone and hexane or dichloromethane three times. In addition to field blanks, ten procedure blanks were prepared along with each batch of extractions. Only a few analytes were detected in the field blanks. The list of detected analytes in field blanks and their concentration levels are available in the SI Spreadsheet; the results for those analytes were blank-corrected. Method detection limits (MDLs) were calculated as three times the standard deviations of levels in field blanks, if a compound was detected in those blanks (with a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) between 3 and 10). Otherwise the MDLs were calculated as the concentrations at which the S/N is 10. The MDLs for target chemicals are given in SI Spreadsheet. The calibration curves for the detected analytes showed good linearity and repeatability; details are provided in the SI Spreadsheet. The recoveries of a variety of surrogates in PASs are summarized in Table S3 (SI).

Data Analysis

Multivariate statistical analyses, i.e., hierarchical clustering analysis and heatmap creation, were performed on deployment-length normalized concentrations in units of picogram per day of sampling (pg day–1) using the R package pheatmap.29 Volumetric concentrations (pg m–3) can be estimated from the quantified amount of a compound in pg day–1 by dividing by a sampling rate in m3 day–1. Sampling rates are not available for all analytes, but generally fall within the range of 0.1–0.6 m3 day–1 for the least and most volatile target compounds, respectively.25,26,30

If there were repeated deployments at a site, we used concentrations from deployments during similar seasons. If there were repeated deployments during the same season, we used the averages. Concentrations were not converted to volumetric concentrations because sampling rates were not available for all analytes and because our focus is on spatial variability. Data were normalized and standardized for the cluster analysis, which was performed using Ward’s method with Euclidean distance. Only analytes with a detection frequency greater than 40% were included in the analysis. A value of half of the MDL was substituted for levels below the MDL for those frequently detected chemicals.31 Maps and visualizations of spatial patterns were created using MATLAB (Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Population count within a radius of 15 km of a sampling location was calculated using 2015 global population data from NASA Earthdata.32

Results and Discussion

Occurrence and Level of Organic Contaminants in Southern Canada

98 of the targeted compounds were detected and quantified in both PAS networks, including 32 PACs and 45 PCB congeners. For the other compound groups, at most 5 individual compounds were detected (SI Spreadsheet). 74 target compounds were frequently detected with detection frequency >40% (Table S4, SI Spreadsheet). Nondetects are mainly a result of concentrations being low relative to the detection limits. In terms of relative concentrations, PACs are dominant with levels being 1 order of magnitude greater than those of the other measured compound groups, followed by industrial organochlorines and HMBs with levels over 100 pg day–1 (Figure S2, SI). The concentrations of PCBs and OPEs ranged between 30–60 pg day–1, while those for nPFAS, HFRs, and OCPs were low and below 2 pg day–1. Concentrations were generally higher in BC than in QC (Figure S2, SI).

Spatial Patterns Revealed by Clustered Heatmap

To present an overview of concentration levels and spatial patterns of organic substances in the study regions, concentrations of frequently detected analytes were subjected to cluster analysis and visualized with a heatmap. The obtained clustered heatmap (Figure 1) reveals four common types of spatial concentration variability in the outdoor atmosphere, which we will discuss in the following paragraphs. These patterns arise because a grouping of compounds has similar source characteristics or a similar environmental fate.

Heatmap obtained by hierarchical cluster analysis for analytes frequently detected in passive air samplers. Sites from the southern Quebec (QC) network start with letter “S” and are in black fonts, while sites from southwestern British Columbia (BC) start with letter “L” and are in red.

“Point Source” Signature

Various clusters of sites had levels for different compound groups that were greatly elevated over those of the remaining sites (green frames labeled I in the Figure 1), indicating point source of contamination. PCBs and PACs are the compound groups that, to some extent, showed that pattern, which we refer to as the “point source” signature. What point source is responsible for such hotspots can potentially be identified by checking for any conspicuous infrastructure or industrial facilities in the vicinity and by further validating if there have been previous reports on a source. Specifically, notable hotspots for many PCB congeners (Zone I, Figure 1) occurred at sites near a waste incinerator in Quebec City (Site S37–40), a site where leaky PCB-containing equipment had been stored (S1, Pointe Clare), a site in the vicinity of a sewage treatment plant (L13, Vancouver, BC) and at a site near an area involving various industrial activities (L25, Maple Ridge, BC). We also found hotspots for some PCB congeners on two university campuses, i.e., at the University of Toronto Scarborough (UTSC) and Simon Fraser University (L3–5, SFU). Hotspots for PACs (Zone I, Figure 1; Figure 2 A & E) occurred in the vicinity of oil and fuel related facilities, for example, fuel storage depots (L1, Port Moody; S6, Trois-Rivières), an oil refinery (S7) and in urban areas in BC (L6, L7, L10, L14).

Spatial distributions of representative compounds for each signature observed from the cluster analysis. Maps show the spatial pattern of PACs (A, E), TCPP (B, F), α-HCH (C, G), and HCBD (D, H) in QC (A, B, C, D) and BC (E, F, G, H) networks. These compounds were selected to represent “Point Source”, “Population”, “Waster Source”, and “Uniform” signatures, respectively. A few triangles (instead of dots) in the spatial distribution map for TCCP represent measured levels that exceed the maximum value of the color scale. The maps were created using the basemap of MATLAB, copyrighted to Esri, HERE, Garmin, USGS, EPA, NPS, and NRCan.

“Population” Signature

For some compounds, i.e., HFRs, fluorotelomer alcohol (FTOH), OPEs and PCB-11, an influence of point sources is less apparent. Instead, elevated concentrations for these compounds generally occurred in populated area, i.e., sites in urban Vancouver, Toronto, Montreal, and Quebec City (Zone II, Figure 1, Figure 2 B & F). Because correlation analysis revealed that the levels of these compounds were highly correlated with population density (Table S5, SI), we refer to this cluster as “population” signature. Some other anthropogenic organic contaminants, e.g., some PCBs and PACs (Table S5, SI) were also correlated with population, indicating that these compounds are also subject to “population” sources in addition to point sources.

“Water Source” Signature

A group of halogenated contaminants, namely, tribromo-anisole (TBA), drosophilin A methyl ether (DAME)) and α-hexachlorocyclohexane (α-HCH) (Zone III, Figure 1; Figure 2 C&G), exhibited a distinct pattern of elevated levels in coastal areas, e.g., near the lower St. Lawrence Estuary in QC and along the coast of the Salish Sea in BC, but also at some sites near fresh water, e.g., at the confluence of the St. Lawrence and Richelieu Rivers in QC (S5) and at the mouth of the Fraser River in BC (L14). Because of this association with water bodies, we refer to this pattern as the “water source” signature.

“Uniform” Signature

A group of compounds comprising hexachlorobutadiene (HCBD), pentachlorobenzene (PeCB) and hexachlorobenzene (HCB) shows a rather uniform distribution across Canada, i.e., the concentration level of these compounds is similar across different sites (Zone IV, Figure 1; Figure 2 D&H). This is supported by a small relative variability of the concentrations of these compounds, which have the smallest relative standard deviations among all frequently detected compounds (Table S4, SI). We refer to this signature as “uniform”.

The clustered heatmap presents some universal patterns across the BC and QC networks but also reveals region-specific patterns. For example, some compounds showed a large variation between the two sampling networks, i.e., a BC governed signature (Zone O, Figure 1) and a QC dominated signature (Zone P, Figure 1). The heatmap also contains some other patterns, presumably indicative of other types of sources/signatures. For example, Zone Q (Figure 1) comprises sources related to rural sites and Zones R&S are suggestive of additional sources for some PCB congeners, i.e., the presence of various types of contaminant sources within a compound group.

Universality of the Observed Patterns and Consistency with Reported Sources

We further investigated the different signatures identified through multivariate statistical analysis of PAS data and assessed their universality and association with reported sources. A variety of contaminant sources for PACs and PCBs have been previously reported. Waste incinerators have been identified as point source for many contaminants, including PCBs.33−35 Increasing atmospheric levels of PCBs with decreasing distance from the Quebec City waste incinerator (S35–40) as observed in this study (Figure S3, SI), demonstrates that the incinerator is a source of PCBs. Li et al. reported a similar decreasing trend of PCB levels away from a municipal solid waste incinerator in China.34

Site S1 in Pointe Clarie (Montreal) is clearly a point source arising from equipment leaking PCBs, even if it has been claimed that this illegal storage site had been thoroughly cleaned up years earlier.36 The issue of PCB contamination from stockpiles and contaminated sites has gained significant attention, particularly in regions like China and Africa where achieving sound management of legacy PCB-containing equipment proves to be challenging.37,38 In addition to industry-associated sources, PCBs were used extensively in construction materials, such as plasticizers, paint, ceiling tiles, insulation, and roofing materials during 1950s–1970s. This leads to another type of point source from buildings made with PCB containing materials.39,40 This could explain the elevated levels found at UTSC and SFU as many of the buildings on these campuses were built in the 1960s.

Point sources of PACs in areas associated with fuel-related facilities are easily rationalized, as leakages or unintentional emissions of PACs from fuel storage depots are highly likely to occur, as has indeed been reported in previous studies.41,42 Whereas point sources in urban areas are complex due to numerous anthropogenic activities emitting PACs, such as fuel combustion, waste treatments, and various household activities,43−45 specific sources responsible for the elevated levels of PACs in these areas are yet to be determined.

The “population” signature could conceivably result from the use of organic chemicals in consumer products. Emissions of such compounds to the atmosphere are expected to be higher in more populated urban areas with intense human activity and higher demand of consumer products. This is also known as “urban pulse” and has been observed repeatedly for OPEs and HFRs around the world.27,46−49 Drage et al. sought to quantify the urban influence by correlating atmospheric levels of a flame retardant with the distance between the sampling location and the city center, finding a negative relation.46 Relying on the same sampling network as the current study, Oh et al. directly correlated the atmospheric level of selected BFRs with population count around the sampling site (p < 0.01).27 These compounds are typically either deliberate additives or unintended byproducts in consumer products, e.g., HFRs and OPEs are used as flame retardants, FTOHs are intermediates remaining as residuals in consumer products (such as polymers, paints, adhesives)50 and PCB-11 is a byproduct of pigment manufacturing.51,52 PCB-11 is clustered separately from other PCB congeners due to its sources being different from those of commercial Aroclor mixtures.53

The release of additives from consumer products to the atmosphere is temperature dependent,54,55 possibly because of greater volatilization of additives at higher temperature outdoors as well as higher ventilation of indoor environments during warmer outdoor temperatures. Concentrations of compounds with a “population” signature were generally higher in BC compared to QC (Figure S2, SI), presumably due to the higher temperature in the former region. This was verified by significantly increased levels of these compounds when sampled during the warm season at the same location (Figure S4, SI). Despite levels of these compounds being influenced by temperature, spatial variability is generally more pronounced than seasonal variability (Figure S4, SI). This is the reason why we could observe overall a significant correlation between concentrations and population (Table S5, SI), and the same clustering of these compounds (Zone II, Figure 1) using the entire data set, which involved varying temperatures across southwestern BC and southern QC.

Interestingly, the “population” signature tends to be universal and may even have applicability beyond the sampling networks investigated here; i.e., correlations observed in this study may be extrapolatable to other regions with similar climates. For instance, we also observed a significant correlation between the reported gaseous concentrations of TCCP and the population density around the sampling location (r = 0.75, p < 0.01, Figure S5, SI). Furthermore, these reported data appear to fit well with the correlation observed in this study (Figure 3 and Table S6, SI). Therefore, it is possible to make orders of magnitude predictions of atmospheric level elsewhere in the world using such correlations if a site is not influenced by a significant point source. This provides a shortcut for estimating the concentrations of population-correlated compounds.

With respect to the halomethoxybenzenes (HMBs) within the “water” cluster, we have previously discussed, relying on the same data, how volatilization from water is the major process for delivering these compounds to the atmosphere,28 irrespective of whether they originate in the marine environment (TBA) or reach aquatic environments via runoff from the terrestrial environments, in which they are formed (DAME). Reports on HMBs outside North America are consistent with this finding, with elevated levels near water bodies suggesting the importance of volatilization from water.59−61 α-HCH clusters with these HMBs, consistent with earlier studies that have shown that volatilization from water bodies is the main source of this pesticide component to the North American atmosphere.62 Two other HMBs, pentachloroanisole (PCA) and tetrachloroveratrole (TeCV), did not fall into the same “water” cluster, because the former, originating from anthropogenic sources, displayed more of an urban pattern, while the clustering of the latter is influenced by another, more pronounced feature, i.e., the regional difference between BC and QC (Zone O), rather than the “water” signature.

Compounds in the “uniform” cluster share extremely long atmospheric residence times, and sources that are largely due to unintentional production during various industrial processes.63,64 The uniform distribution suggests that these compounds have no major sources within the Canadian network region and therefore reached the study area mostly by long-range transport. Uniform atmospheric distributions for PeCB and HCB across North America had been demonstrated previously.65 Long atmospheric residence times can be attributed to relatively high volatility and low water solubility, as well as slow reaction rates with photo-oxidants, enhancing dispersal while limiting atmospheric degradation and deposition. HBCD, another compound in the “uniform” cluster, recently has gained notable attention due to its increasing level on a global scale.66 A relative narrow distribution across different sites around the world suggests uniformity and universality of HBCD in the atmosphere.66 Near emission sources, much higher level of HBCD are expected, e.g. in the more populated and industrialized areas of China.67

Potential Uses of Spatial Patterns Derived from PAS Data

Evaluating Emission of Organic Contaminants Across Industrial Sectors

Industrial activities are often held responsible for various types of pollution. Efforts have been made to attribute the release of organic contaminants to industrial processes and activities, usually by monitoring pollutant levels in the vicinity of industrial facilities. However, there is a large number and diverse range of industrial facilities that could potentially emit various pollutants. Exploring all potential associations between the emissions of organic contaminants and different industrial sectors is challenging.

Collective and comprehensive efforts have led to the development of national emission inventories for some pollutants of concern, in some cases, even on a regional and global scale. In Canada, the National Pollutant Release Inventory (NPRI) compiles data on the release of over 300 pollutants from approximately 7500 industrial facilities.68 NPRI records on the emission of organic contaminants are limited and often have considerable uncertainty. Nevertheless, combining information on the location of industrial facilities reporting to the NPRI with the comprehensive data set containing spatial-resolved concentration data for various organic substances obtained in this study could provide some insights into potential emissions of organic contaminants from different industrial sectors. In particular, “point source” spatial patterns, with elevated levels in the vicinity of one or a few sampling sites, could serve in the identification of industrial sector(s) likely to contribute atmospheric emissions.

For illustration, we first searched the NPRI database to identify facilities that are within a radius of 1.5 km of our sampling sites. We then checked whether the level of any target chemical was significantly elevated (within the upper quartile) at sampling sites with facilities nearby. The industrial sectors of reporting facilities, the associated sites, and the chemicals with elevated levels are summarized in Table S7 (SI). Elevated levels of some compounds, e.g., some PCB congeners, were associated with several industries, making it difficult to assign specific sources. In contrast, for some compounds, the associations were more specific, e.g., HCB levels were significantly elevated at sites in the vicinity of iron and steel plants (Site S4 close to Solumet Metal and Powder Inc.; S22, Bradken Mineral Processing B.C. Ltd.), indicating iron and steel industries may be local sources, raising HCB concentration above the generally uniform background. Degassing of molten aluminum with hexachloroethane has indeed been implicated as a potential HCB emission source.69 Similarly, dibenzothiophene was associated with the industrial sector of oil and gas pipelines and storage, and two alkylated PAHs (2-Methylnaphthalene and 1-Methylnaphthalene), and OPEs may be associated with transportation equipment and some manufacturing industries, respectively.

Estimating Emissions from Consumer Products

Given that atmospheric concentrations of the compounds within the “population” cluster were highly correlated with population (Table S5, SI), it is plausible to assume that their emission intensity scales spatially with population density.70−72 In this case, it should be possible to apply inverse modeling approaches to the measured atmospheric concentrations to derive population-normalized emission factors in units of mg capita–1 year –1.73−75 Specially, we applied a highly spatially resolved environmental fate model, i.e., the Nested Exposure Model (NEM),76 to simulate atmospheric levels of α-1, 2-dibromo-4-(1, 2-dibromoethyl) cyclohexane (α-TBECH) reported for our sampling network by Oh et al. (2023) using emissions distributed spatially based on population density (more details on NEM simulation and input parameters are provided in Text S4 in the SI). Emission factors of approximately 1 and 4 mg capita–1 year–1 were required to reproduce the levels of α-TBECH in the atmosphere of southern QC and southwestern BC, respectively. NEM also reproduced the spatial concentration distributions of α-TBECH in the two regions (Figure 4; Table S8, SI), indicating that it is defensible to use scaled population data as emission input for those population-correlated compounds. The higher emission rate in BC compared to QC may be related to higher average temperatures (3 °C in QC vs to 12 °C in BC) as evaporative emissions of consumer products are enhanced at higher temperature.

Using population as an emission proxy is a more efficient approach compared to traditional approaches of collecting and compiling emission inventories and therefore could greatly reduce the effort required to develop emission inventories. The environmental fate of many compounds has not been assessed quantitatively because suitable emission inventories are missing. In our study, over 50% of organic contaminants exhibited a correlation with population (Table S5, SI), suggesting that many compounds are potentially suited for approaches involving empirical equations and modeling using population density as a proxy for emission strength. While associations between population and contaminant levels have been reported previously, a quantitative relationship has been challenging to establish due to small and inconsistent data sets. This, in turn, underscores the significance of developing such a large data set in this work.

In addition, emissions of volatile chemical products (VCPs), e.g., those emitted from consumer products, are becoming increasingly important for urban air pollution relative to transportation-related sources.77 In industrialized cities, VCPs emission constitutes half of emitted VOCs.77 VCPs have also been revealed to contribute a large quantity to the formation of secondary organic aerosol (SOA) in the atmosphere (up to 50% of anthropogenic SOA).78 The identified spatial patterns and their use in the estimations of VCP emissions are important elements in the assessment of air pollution by SOA and ozone.

Supporting Environmental Justice Investigations

Due to spatial variation in the atmospheric concentrations of organic contaminants, inhalation exposure to these contaminants varies between people residing in different areas, potentially leading to unequal distribution of environmental risk. Studying these disparities is part of environmental justice research.79,80

With an increased awareness of the need for equity in racial and other dimensions of social justice, environmental justice has garnered more attention in recent years. One factor that hinders the expansion of the scope of environmental justice studies are the limited in situ measurement data at locations of interest.79 Large PAS networks such as the one used in this study should be well suited to investigating distributive justice considerations. Different spatial patterns can reveal different types of inequitable distributions of organic contaminants. For example, atmospheric concentrations of some OPEs, PAHs and PCBs in Southern Canada appear to be significantly elevated in areas with a higher prevalence of low-income households (Figure S6 and Table S9, SI). Many of these “ inequitably” distributed compounds are of the “population” signature (Tables S5 and S9, SI). This is because population density and percentage of low-income households are correlated in our sampling network (r = 0.6, p < 0.01; Figure S7, SI), i.e., poorer neighborhoods in our study areas tend to be more densely populated. Interestingly, this suggests that this unequal exposure arises from emissions of contaminants from consumer products or other population related activities rather than emissions from notable point sources. This will be compounded by indoor exposure to these compounds, which has also been found to be elevated in social housing multiunit residential building apartments.81 Despite the network not being initially designed for investigating population disparities in exposure, observing significant correlations with socioeconomic data suggests the applicability of the data set to this field.

Inequality in the distribution of air pollution has been frequently investigated,82−85 with some studies having revealed high exposure to air contaminants in lower-income neighborhoods in North America.82,83 However, the focus of these studies has primarily been on regularly monitored air pollutants such as particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide. This is because data on these pollutants are readily available, often derived from satellite data or open databases reported by environmental monitoring stations. A comprehensive study of environmental justice requires spatially resolved environmental data for a wider range of contaminants. The data set obtained in this study, involving spatial distribution of various organic contaminants at a sufficiently large scale and with enhanced spatial resolution, opens the prospect for expanding the scope of environmental justice investigations.

Uncertainty, Limitations and Outlook

A data set with spatial variability of various organic contaminants at a sufficiently large scale and enhanced spatial resolution has been proven to be effective and valuable in identifying sources and assessing environmental impacts. The utility of the data set demonstrated here constitutes just a glimpse of the full potential of large and consistent PAS data sets for quantifying the outdoor inhalation exposome to organic contaminants. For instance, combining our data set with spatially resolved health data could help probe the relationship between pollutant levels in air and various human diseases, thereby supporting epidemiological studies seeking associations with health effects. Additionally, the data set and observed patterns can assist in prioritizing chemical features detected in nontarget analysis (NTA). One of the challenges of NTA is to prioritize chemical features detected in NTA among the sheer number of chemical features being detected. Spatial pattern in the peak intensity of chemical features detected in the NTA of samples from a PAS network could serve in such prioritisation. For example, if the NTA aims to identify airborne chemicals arising from consumer product use, one could envisage algorithms selecting chemical features displaying patterns correlating with population density. Conversely, NTA can greatly expand the list of organic contaminants that can be detected in the atmosphere, possibly enabling the identification of other spatial patterns.

Several factors contribute to the uncertainty in the spatial variability of organic contaminants, as determined by passive sampling. Even though we derived these spatial patterns from deployments during similar seasons, they could be affected by interannual and seasonal variations in air concentrations occurring during the sampling campaign, which spanned the years 2019 to 2022. Some of the observed spatial concentration variability could also be an artifact of the sampling technique. Because sampling rates depend somewhat on the temperature and wind speed conditions during deployment, some of the observed differences between sites could be due to spatial differences in sampling rates. However, the influence of meteorology on sampling rates of the XAD-PAS is small26 relative to the observed spatial concentration variability. Additionally, some compounds do not exhibit linear uptake in the PAS throughout very long deployment periods. For instance, uptake of some PACs that may no longer be linear after 6 months due to degradation on the XAD-resin could introduce higher uncertainties in the spatial variability of these compounds when the sampling period exceeds six months. Finally, there is the usual analytical uncertainty associated with organic contaminant trace analysis, although this should be reduced when only interpreting relative concentrations.

One limitation of our study is that the XAD-PAS only samples gas-phase organic contaminants. We therefore cannot observe spatial patterns or assess inhalation exposure to the particle-bound fraction, which should be important for less volatile organic compounds. In addition, some organic contaminants, such as the HFRs, may generally have higher concentrations in indoor environments, leading to more pronounced indoor inhalation exposure. It is also worth mentioning that the long-term average concentrations obtained with PASs are lower than the episodic peak concentrations. The exposure resulting from such short but high concentration peaks may warrant additional attention. A more complete characterization of the inhalation exposome will require consideration of the above aspects, quantifying exposure levels with available sampling rates, and further expanding the analyte list through additional targeted and nontargeted analysis.
