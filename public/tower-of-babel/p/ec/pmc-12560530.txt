Cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying cardiovascular aging
Authors: Sen-Yu Zhang, Yu-Hang Yang, Ri Wen, Ni Yang, Shan-Shan Feng, Tie-Ning Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging is a primary risk factor driving the increased prevalence of cardiovascular diseases, significantly contributing to global mortality and healthcare burdens. Aging-related alterations, including genomic instability, telomere shortening, and loss of proteostasis, underpin the pathogenesis of numerous cardiovascular conditions such as heart failure, arrhythmia, cardiomyopathy, myocardial infarction, and atherosclerosis. Recent insights into molecular and cellular mechanisms highlight the roles of senescence, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic disturbances in cardiovascular aging. Cellular and vascular senescence further accelerates the development of aging-related cardiovascular diseases. Emerging therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways, such as metabolic regulators, senolytic agents, antioxidants, stem cell-derived exosomes, and natural bioactive compounds, offer promising avenues for mitigating aging-related cardiovascular pathology.Keywords: Cardiovascular diseases, Aging, Treatment

Introduction

Aging is a progressive decline in physiological and functional capacity that occurs over time. It is recognized as a major risk factor for numerous human diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), and diabetes, significantly contributing to their onset and progression [1, 2]. As life expectancy increases, the burden of CVDs associated with population aging has emerged as a critical public health challenge [3]. CVD is a major cause of premature mortality and an important driver of escalating healthcare costs [4]. Compared with 1990, there were 12 million more deaths globally in 2017 attributable to population aging [5]. From 1990 to 2017, ischemic heart disease remained the leading contributor to aging-related global mortality [5]. From 1996 to 2016, cardiovascular-related expenditures among US adults increased by over $100 billion [6]. The global direct burden of CVDs is projected to reach new levels by 2030 [7].

In the period from 2005 to 2020, China witnessed a reduction in the burden of premature mortality from CVD, but it still remained above the global average. With the widespread presence of unhealthy lifestyles, a vast population carrying CVD risk factors, and the accelerated aging of the population in China, the incidence and mortality of CVD remain on the rise, and the country has yet to see a decline in disease burden [8]. The total number of CVD deaths increased by 48.06%. Population aging is the primary factor contributing to the increase in CVD mortality [9]. Understanding the role of aging in CVD is crucial for reducing mortality rates.

Over the past few years, global experts have developed new and more comprehensive perspectives on the mechanisms of aging in CVD and potential therapeutic approaches. It has been demonstrated through studies that improving cardiovascular health can mitigate the process of biological aging and prolong lifespan [10]. With the deepening of research, new progress has been made in the research of aging in CVDs in the past 5 years. Therefore, this review article summarizes the most recent developments in the research on aging in CVDs.

Aging

Aging is widely defined as the time-associated functional deterioration that affects the majority of organisms. Studies have defined the molecular and cellular processes related to aging, along with aging biomarkers, which are observed in different mammals and may be connected to the underlying biological mechanisms of human aging.

Molecular (including transcripts, proteins, metabolites, and cytokines) alterations are essential for understanding the potential mechanisms of aging and identifying possible therapeutic targets for aging-related diseases. Throughout human aging, only a small fraction of molecules (6.6%) show linear changes, whereas a large proportion (81.0%) follow nonlinear patterns [11]. This nonlinear trend is observed across all types of omics data with remarkable consistency, underlining the widespread functional relevance of these dynamic changes [11]. By identifying the specific functional modules and disease-related pathways exhibiting such nonlinear changes, we can gain deeper insight into the complex molecular dynamics of aging and their implications for disease risk.

There is a strong connection between aging and telomeres. Telomeres, which are unique DNA repeat sequences at the ends of chromosomes, shorten progressively with each round of cell division. Once telomeres reach a critical length, cells undergo replicative senescence, losing their ability to divide and proliferate. In 1990, researchers first proposed that telomere shortening is a fundamental mechanism of replicative senescence and identified telomerase as a key factor in maintaining telomere length [12]. Epidemiological data indicate a negative correlation between telomere length and the increasing prevalence of age-related clinical phenotypes [13]. Notably, individuals with heart disease have significantly shorter telomeres than their healthy counterparts [14].

López-Otin et al. offered an extensive and meticulous review of aging biomarkers over the span of 20 years [15, 16]. They identified nine tentative hallmarks that reflect shared traits of aging in various organisms, with a specific emphasis on mammalian aging. The main hallmarks include genomic instability, telomere attrition [17], epigenetic alterations [18], and loss of proteostasis [19]. In recent studies, Zhu et al. introduced a Physiological Aging Index (PAI) based on 36 common clinical biomarkers to quantify the progression of aging, emphasizing the potential of PAI and ΔPAI as comprehensive biomarkers to assess accelerated aging and support the development of targeted strategies for promoting healthy aging [20].

As time progresses, further studies on the mechanisms of aging will be conducted, and more disease treatment targets will be uncovered based on these findings.

Aging and its relationship with the heart and vascular structures

Aging induces alterations in the structure of the heart and vasculature. Aging hearts exhibit various structural alterations, resulting in ventricular stiffness, reduced pressure-handling capacity, and a higher susceptibility to heart failure (HF) (Fig. 1).

Main effects of aging on the cardiac vein. Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and senescence, together with impaired mitochondrial lipid metabolism, contribute to the progression of cardiac aging. Aging further drives structural and molecular alterations in the heart and vasculature, including reactive oxygen species (ROS)/CaMKII-mediated electrical remodeling, disruption of circadian regulation, Sox9-driven vascular calcification, reduced α1A-adrenoceptor/NO signaling, decline in protease activity, metabolic reprogramming through the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway, and RGS5-mediated pericyte dysfunction, collectively accelerating vascular aging, myocardial fibrosis, and, ultimately, heart failure

In both mice and humans, aging modifies the phosphorylation of the elastic titin I-band domains of titin in the heart. Concurrently, aging diminishes protease activity—an indicator of cardiomyocyte protein turnover—thereby potentially impairing the adaptive capacity of senescent myocardium under pathological stress [21]. Prominin2, a newly identified specific marker of cardiomyocyte senescence, promotes cardiomyocyte aging and hypertrophy when overexpressed. A comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of heart gene expression throughout the mouse lifespan revealed that aging is marked by the reactivation of early gene expression programs and the induction of novel expression programs [22]. Dysfunctional mitochondria are characteristic of senescent cardiomyocytes [23]. Duan’s team identified impaired mitochondrial lipid metabolism as a key mechanism driving this accumulation in senescent myocardial cells [24]. Heart gene expression and the onset of arrhythmias follow a circadian rhythm, and aging notably disrupts the normal circadian variations in cardiac electrophysiology and Ca2+ regulation [25]. Aging facilitates enhanced cardiac electrical remodeling in rodent models through the NOX4/ROS/CaMKII-dependent calcium signaling cascade [26]. Transcriptomic analysis of left ventricular samples from female baboons showed a close relationship between aging and metabolic changes. As age progresses, cardiac metabolism shifts towards glucose-dependent synthetic pathways, particularly the hexosamine biosynthesis pathway, leading to the synthesis, modification, and accumulation of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) in the extracellular matrix (ECM) [27]. The research also found that the accumulation of GAGs enhances the upregulation of ECM signaling pathways, which are consistent with pathways related to cardiac hypertrophy [27]. Aging induces biological changes in the heart, resulting in cardiac hypertrophy, decreased diastolic function, and ultimately HF [28].

Beyond the structural changes in the heart itself, aging markedly affects the blood vessels associated with cardiac function. Early vascular aging has been shown to serve as an indicator of cardiovascular risk in otherwise healthy adults [29]. Aging reduces α1A-adrenoceptor expression and neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS)-mediated nitric oxide (NO) signaling, weakening vascular protective mechanisms and thereby accelerating cardiovascular aging [30]. Vascular calcification and increased ECM stiffness are hallmark features of vascular aging. Sox9 is a key molecule involved in vascular aging, promoting calcification and soft tissue stiffening by regulating vascular smooth muscle cell function, thereby accelerating the aging process of blood vessels [31]. Aging leads to remodeling of the coronary microcirculation, with a reduction in both the intensity of the backward expansion wave and diastolic microvascular conductance [32]. In addition, pericytes are mesenchymal cells associated with capillaries and are essential for maintaining vascular homeostasis. Regulator of G-protein signaling 5 (RGS5) is a key modulator of pericyte function during cardiac aging. The loss of RGS5 leads to cardiac dysfunction and induces myocardial fibrosis [33]. Perivascular adipose tissue is considered a key regulator of vascular diseases and may offer potential benefits in mitigating vascular aging [34].

Cardiovascular aging is not limited to the heart and blood vessels but represents a multisystem aging process involving the metabolic, renal, and nervous systems [35]. A deeper understanding of the effects of aging on the heart and cardiovascular system can provide new insights for future treatments of aging-related cardiovascular diseases.

Aging and cardiovascular diseases

Each year, cardiovascular diseases take the lives of millions, making them the leading cause of death globally. While aging is strongly linked to cardiovascular diseases [36, 37], its specific effects on cardiac function remain incompletely understood. The sharp rise in cardiovascular disease incidence with advancing age underscores the need for in-depth research into the interplay between aging and heart health.

Heart failure

HF is a multifaceted and heterogeneous syndrome marked by decreased ventricular filling or impaired ejection, accompanied by symptoms such as shortness of breath, fatigue, peripheral edema, and/or pulmonary edema [38]. As the global aging population grows rapidly, age-related HF is bound to become a more common health concern [39] and a major cause of death in older adults [40] (Fig. 2).

The role of aging in heart failure. MDSCs accumulate in aged tissues, with granulocyte-derived MDSCs facilitating cardiac fibrosis during aging via S100A8/A9 secretion and modulation of the fibroblast FGF2-SOX9 signaling pathway. Klotho contributes to cardiac aging by disrupting the Nrf2–glutathione reductase pathway. MG53 protects against age-related heart failure by inhibiting the activation of NF-κB. Additionally, aging diminishes the regulatory role of miR-146a-5p on the MMP2/16-MLCK3-p-MLC2 axis, resulting in impaired cardiac contractility and heightened vulnerability to heart failure

Aging-related heart disease is characterized primarily by HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), which is driven by cardiac fibrosis. One of the key contributors to this process is the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in aging tissues, which have been implicated in various aging-related conditions. Among these, granulocytic MDSCs (G-MDSCs) accumulate significantly more than monocyte-derived MDSCs in the aging heart. Recent research has revealed that G-MDSCs promote cardiac fibrosis during aging by secreting S100A8/A9 and regulating fibroblast FGF2-SOX9 signaling [41]. Furthermore, the importance of aging-related genes in diagnosing HF and modulating immune infiltration has been highlighted through analyses of single-cell and bulk RNA sequencing data [42, 43]. Using three different machine learning techniques, researchers have identified 14 essential genes as aging-related biomarkers for HF [44]. Meanwhile, telomere shortening occurring in fully differentiated adult cardiomyocytes has been recognized as an indicator of terminal-stage heart failure [45]. Additionally, aging weakens the regulatory role of miR-146a-5p in the MMP2/16-MLCK3-p-MLC2 axis, leading to impaired cardiac contractile function and an increased susceptibility to HF [46]. Age-related dysbiosis of the gut microbiota has been extensively studied. Recent findings suggest that the aging gut microbiome impairs cardiac function in mice with heart failure, promotes epicardial fat accumulation, and reduces fatty acid oxidation. Notably, these effects can be reversed by the activation of PPARα [47].

Current evidence demonstrates that systemic aging drives the onset and progression of heart failure [48]. Future studies may delve deeper into the expression patterns and molecular regulation of aging genes, offering new perspectives on the mechanisms that drive HF development and providing leads for the development of therapeutic strategies.

Arrhythmia

Arrhythmia refers to a pathological condition characterized by disruptions in heart rhythm, including abnormalities in heart rate, rhythm regularity, or electrical conduction. Among various types of arrhythmias, atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most frequently diagnosed and persistent form encountered in clinical practice.

With advancing age, the heart undergoes several electrophysiological alterations, including increased conduction abnormalities, overall conduction slowing, and the formation of low-voltage zones, particularly within the Bachmann bundle and right atrium. Furthermore, aging leads to progressive lateral decoupling in the BB, which may contribute to electrical instability. These structural and functional modifications provide mechanistic insights into the elevated prevalence of AF in elderly individuals [49]. In addition to its high incidence, AF exacerbates age-associated vascular deterioration, further amplifying the risk of CVDs [50].

One of the primary contributors to AF development in aging hearts is atrial fibrosis. Notably, studies have identified p300 as a key regulatory factor in age-related atrial fibrosis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for AF management in older individuals [51]. Experimental models have further elucidated the molecular basis of aging-related AF. Research utilizing an AF dog model demonstrated that the aging-related genes HSPA9 and SOD2 play pivotal roles in both the initiation and progression of AF [52]. Another critical molecular pathway involves Sirt1, which has been shown to mitigate age-related AF by modulating RIPK1 acetylation and inhibiting atrial necroptosis [53].

Moreover, the interplay between aging and myocardial infarction (MI) further predisposes individuals to AF. Animal studies indicate that aging, in combination with MI, exacerbates atrial conduction slowing and accelerates fibrotic remodeling, thereby increasing AF susceptibility [54]. Anticoagulants are standard therapeutic agents for AF, and non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants are the preferred oral anticoagulant therapy for reducing the risk of heart failure in elderly patients with atrial fibrillation [55].

Aging, cardiac remodeling, and AF pathogenesis are closely intertwined, with molecular changes providing promising avenues for therapeutic intervention in elderly patients.

Cardiomyopathy

Cardiomyopathy refers to a collection of diseases that primarily impact myocardial structure and function, often characterized by myocardial thickening, dilation, or rigidity. These conditions can reduce the heart’s ability to pump blood, potentially resulting in HF, arrhythmias, or sudden death (Fig. 3).

The role of aging in cardiomyopathy. Autophagy plays a critical role in alleviating cellular stress and improving cardiomyopathy by activating the Sirt1/PGC-1α/AMPK pathway while inhibiting the Akt/mTORC1 pathway. Klotho promotes cardiomyopathy by disrupting the Nrf2–glutathione reductase pathway. In aging-related cardiomyopathy, BPIFB4 exerts protective effects by promoting vascularization, thereby preserving cardiac function. Conversely, TMEM43 deficiency leads to late-onset cardiomyopathy, characterized by myocardial fibrosis and apoptosis, through the activation of the DNA damage response (DDR)/TP53 pathway. Additionally, interleukin (IL)-1, a proinflammatory cytokine, accelerates cardiomyopathy progression by exacerbating inflammation and contributing to cardiac dysfunction

Recent research has indicated that the absence of the granulin precursor promotes age-associated myocardial hypertrophy by triggering β-catenin activation mediated by C1q [56]. Dill extract (DE) stimulates elastin synthesis in skin equivalent models and dermal fibroblasts by enhancing the expression of lysyl oxidase-like 1 (LOXL-1), an enzyme critical for tropoelastin crosslinking and elastic fiber assembly [57, 58]. In aged mice, DE induces de novo synthesis of elastic fibers in the ascending aorta, improves vascular function, and reverses age-related myocardial hypertrophy, partially through the action of LOXL-1 [59]. The dysregulation of longevity gene signaling pathways disrupts autophagy, thereby accelerating the onset and progression of cardiomyopathy. As a crucial cellular process, autophagy supports lifespan extension, mitigates cardiac aging, alleviates cellular stress, and improves cardiomyopathy by activating the Sirt1/PGC-1α/AMPK pathway while inhibiting the Akt/mTORC1 pathway [60]. Among longevity-related genes, bactericidal/permeability-increasing fold-containing-family-B-member-4 gene (BPIFB4) plays a protective role in aging cardiomyopathy by preserving cardiac function and promoting vascularization [61].

Genetic mutations also contribute to the pathology of cardiomyopathy. Specifically, mutations in the transmembrane protein 43 (TMEM43) gene have been linked to arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, with its product, TMEM43, playing a critical role in cardiac disease mechanisms. In cardiomyocytes, TMEM43 haploinsufficiency leads to late-onset cardiomyopathy characterized by myocardial fibrosis and apoptosis, potentially mediated by the activation of the DNA damage response/TP53 pathway [62]. Furthermore, inflammation is a key driver of aging-related cardiomyopathy, with IL-1 acting as a proinflammatory cytokine that promotes disease progression [63].

The progression of aging-related cardiomyopathy is shaped by longevity gene signaling, autophagy pathways, genetic variations, and inflammatory responses, highlighting multiple potential therapeutic strategies.

Hypertension

In the USA, hypertension has been diagnosed in 76.5% of the elderly population aged 65 years and above, as reported by the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey [64]. Global estimates indicate that hypertension is responsible for between 7.7 million and 10.4 million deaths each year [65]. The progression and exacerbation of hypertension are commonly observed as individuals grow older [66]. In the past 30 years, mainland China and Taiwan have experienced a marked increase in hypertensive heart disease (HHD) cases and associated mortality. This upward trajectory is anticipated to persist in China throughout the next 5 years [67] (Fig. 4).

The role of aging in hypertension. Hypertension accelerates vascular aging through multiple mechanisms. Upregulation of fibronectin and its integrin α5 receptor disrupts BMP2/MGP signaling, enhances Runx2 activity, and promotes vascular aging. Weakened NPRA/PKG/AMPK signaling further contributes to vascular senescence. Alizarin improves endothelial function via the VEGFR2/eNOS pathway, while isopropyl 3-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-2-hydroxypropanoate (IDHP) mitigates lipotoxicity-induced vascular aging through the ROS/ferroptosis axis. Endothelial senescence is also driven by GPR183 signaling. Circulating Klotho and MFAP4 levels modulate hypertension and vascular remodeling, highlighting potential therapeutic targets

The expression of fibronectin in the aortic wall and its high-affinity integrin α5 receptor may be upregulated under hypertensive conditions, which aggravates the imbalance of BMP2/MGP signaling, enhances the transcriptional activity of Runx2, and facilitates the transdifferentiation of vascular smooth muscle cells into an osteogenic-like phenotype, leading to arterial senescence and calcification [68]. A progressive weakening of NPRA/PKG/AMPK signaling pathways accelerates vascular senescence and facilitates the rise in blood pressure associated with aging [69].

With aging, arterial stiffness increases while vascular compliance declines, resulting in a higher prevalence of hypertension in older adults due to the consequent rise in systolic blood pressure (SBP) [70]. Sustaining normal blood pressure may contribute to the prevention of cardiovascular complications and the preservation of physical function [71]. In spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs), alizarin has been shown to normalize SBP and improve endothelial function by enhancing endothelium-dependent relaxation. Additionally, it mitigates mitochondrial damage caused by oxidative stress and significantly slows vascular aging, possibly by activating the VEGFR2/endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) signaling cascade [72].

Recent research has emphasized the critical role of endothelial senescence in the pathogenesis of hypertension and age-associated vascular diseases [73, 74]. Endothelial senescence in both aged and hypertensive mice is induced by GPR183, and therapeutic intervention through modulation of the oxysterol–GPR183 axis may provide a treatment avenue for hypertension [75].

Vascular aging plays a crucial role in the onset of hypertension. Isopropyl 3-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-2-hydroxypropanoate (IDHP), acting through the ROS/ferroptosis axis, has shown efficacy in mitigating lipotoxicity-driven vascular senescence, suggesting a promising strategy for managing vascular aging in individuals with dyslipidemia [76]. Microfibrillar-associated protein 4 (MFAP4) is an extracellular matrix protein belonging to the fibrinogen-related domain superfamily. The Mfap4 gene is highly expressed in arteries and other elastic tissues, with expression levels increasing with age in mice. Studies have shown that MFAP4 deficiency exacerbates age-related structural alterations in resistance arteries, while concurrently ameliorating hypertension [77]. A lower risk of hypertension has been observed in individuals with increased circulating Klotho levels [78].

Ischemic heart disease

Ischemic heart disease (IHD), commonly termed coronary artery disease (CAD) or atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ACD), has been identified as the leading contributor to global mortality. Globally, IHD stands as the foremost contributor to mortality, disability, and human suffering [79] (Fig. 5).

The role of aging in atherosclerosis and ultimately myocardial infarction. FOXP1 transcriptionally activates IGF-1, which helps counteract oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL)-induced endothelial cell (EC) senescence by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome. Similarly, the TAOK1–CDC20 signaling pathway protects ECs from ox-LDL-induced cellular stress, thereby slowing the progression of atherosclerosis (AS). Additionally, targeting NOX4 or restoring mitochondrial function may serve as effective strategies to reduce vascular inflammation and mitigate AS advancement. At the epigenetic level, a chromatin microenvironment enriched with long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) JPX promotes VSMC senescence, further accelerating AS progression. Moreover, the acetylation of PPARγ has been implicated in aging-related AS, while cathepsin L contributes to endothelial senescence by upregulating CUT-like homeobox 1 and p16INK4a, exacerbating disease progression. miR-217 has been identified as a biomarker of vascular aging and cardiovascular risk. TRAP1 drives smooth muscle cell senescence and promotes atherosclerosis through HDAC3-induced H4K12la

CAD accounts for 17.8 million deaths annually [80]. With advancing age, coronary vasodilatory capacity across the entire myocardium becomes progressively compromised, largely as a result of elevated minimal microvascular resistance [81]. FOXO3 longevity genotypes have been shown to reduce the risk of death from CAD [82, 83], and this protective effect is especially pronounced among men with hypertension [84]. Its primary pathological feature, AS, is driven by multiple cellular and molecular mechanisms. The increased secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype proteins from vascular cells heightens susceptibility to CAD, while cystatin C facilitates atherosclerosis by promoting monocyte–EC adhesion [85]. Furthermore, genetic studies in patients with CAD have identified a strong correlation between the aging-related genes HSP90AA1 and CEBPA and the development of CAD, underscoring their potential role in disease progression [86].

Atherosclerosis (AS) is the primary pathological mechanism underlying most CVDs [87]. As a complex inflammatory condition, atherosclerosis is marked by oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) deposition, endothelial dysfunction, and the progressive buildup of plaques in the vessel wall [88, 89]. Atherosclerotic plaques are composed of endothelial cells (ECs), vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), lipids, and infiltrating immune cells, with VSMCs playing a tumor-like role in disease progression [90].

Cellular senescence is a major contributor to AS [74, 91]. E2F transcription factor 2 (E2F2) is essential for controlling cellular proliferation, differentiation, and responses to DNA damage. Its overexpression has been demonstrated to counteract cellular aging features and restore function in aged cells [92]. Vascular aging is partially driven by cellular senescence and SASP, and emerging anti-aging compounds may offer therapeutic potential to restore vascular function in aging populations [74]. The suppression of long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) NORAD has been shown to exacerbate vascular endothelial cell injury and facilitate the progression of atherosclerosis under ox-LDL stimulation [93]. Moreover, FOXP1 transcriptionally activates IGF-1, which helps counteract ox-LDL-induced EC senescence by suppressing the NLRP3 inflammasome [94]. Similarly, TAOK1–CDC20 signaling protects ECs from ox-LDL-induced cellular stress, offering another defense mechanism against AS [95]. In addition, CUX1 has been shown to modulate p16INK4a-dependent endothelial senescence and is considered a promising target for the treatment of atherosclerosis [96].

VSMC senescence is crucial for the progression of atherosclerosis and is characterized by metabolic dysfunction. Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1) is an aging-related metabolic regulator. TRAP1 drives smooth muscle cell senescence and promotes atherosclerosis through histone H4 lysine 12 lactylation induced by HDAC3 [97]. The chromatin microenvironment enriched with lncRNA JPX promotes VSMC senescence, thereby accelerating AS progression [98]. On the contrary, inhibitors of proprotein convertase subtilin-type kexin 9 have been shown to attenuate vascular aging by decreasing SIRT1 expression at the cellular level [99]. Additionally, deacetylation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) has been shown to strongly inhibit aging-related AS, highlighting a potential therapeutic target [100]. Oxidative stress exerts profound effects on vascular aging [101]. In aging human aortic smooth muscle cells, the inflammatory transcription factor NF-κB and the oxidative stress-responsive transcription factor HIF-1α modulate the transcriptional activity of mineralocorticoid receptors in smooth muscle cells. This regulatory mechanism contributes to age-related vascular stiffening and the progression of cardiovascular disease [102].

Inflammation-driven mechanisms further exacerbate AS. Cathepsin L promotes endothelial senescence by upregulating CUT-like homeobox 1 and p16INK4a, contributing to disease pathology [103]. Moreover, aging-induced isoDGR-modified fibronectin activates monocytes and ECs, facilitating AS development [104]. The progression of atherosclerosis is expedited by mitochondrial dysfunction associated with aging [105]. Macrophages are central mediators of vascular aging, with elevated levels of NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, a shift toward glycolytic metabolism, and a proinflammatory phenotype. Targeting NOX4 or mitochondrial dysfunction may help alleviate vascular inflammation, slow AS progression, and stabilize plaques [106]. BRD4, a chromatin-binding protein involved in gene regulation, has been implicated in LPS-induced macrophage senescence and facilitates the advancement of lipid accumulation linked to atherosclerosis [107]. MicroRNAs also regulate AS-related pathways. miR-217 has been identified as a biomarker of vascular aging and cardiovascular risk, underscoring its role in AS progression [108].

Aging-related atherosclerosis is driven by the interplay of cellular senescence, inflammatory cascades, and metabolic shifts, revealing key pathways for potential therapeutic modulation.

Myocardial infarction

MI is an acute cardiovascular condition resulting from the obstruction of coronary blood flow, causing ischemic necrosis of the myocardium. It is commonly associated with atherosclerotic plaque rupture and thrombus formation. CVDs remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, with MI being the most common cause of death among patients with CVD [109].

As an age-related condition, acute MI is influenced by cellular senescence, immune modulation, and inflammatory responses, with LIMD2 identified as a key gene connecting these processes in acute MI [110]. Additionally, the longevity-associated variant of the BPIFB4 gene has been linked to exceptional longevity, and its expression in both clinical patients and mice has been shown to support cardiac function during ischemia [111]. Moreover, MI progression is aggravated by the downregulation of the transcription factor Nrf2, which amplifies oxidative stress, accelerates cellular senescence in the infarcted heart, and contributes to HF development [112].

Molecular mechanisms underlying aging-related MI offer new perspectives on disease pathology and may inform the development of targeted treatment approaches.

Others

Apart from the aforementioned well-studied diseases, other cardiovascular disorders have also been the subject of research.

Myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury is another major cardiovascular disease, characterized by extensive myocardial cell damage and multiple forms of cell death. In aging hearts, the upregulation of myocardial TLR2 has been identified as a critical factor in amplifying inflammation, promoting adverse cardiac remodeling, and impairing heart function post-I/R injury [113]. As an intrinsic defense strategy, ischemic postconditioning (IPostC) mitigates I/R injury. It enhances the survival of aging cardiomyocytes by inducing miR-30a activation through a DNA methyltransferase 3b-dependent pathway [114].

Aortic aneurysm and dissection (AAD) is a life-threatening vascular condition with a high incidence and fatality rate, particularly among the elderly. Recent findings suggest that ciprofloxacin may accelerate AAD progression by promoting AngII-induced VSMC senescence through the AMPK/ROS signaling pathway [115]. Additionally, elevated levels of miR-1204 in plasma and aortic tissues of elderly patients with AAD have been shown to correlate with disease severity [116]. Aging contributes to AAD progression through the miR-1204–MYLK signaling pathway, further emphasizing the impact of age-related molecular changes on vascular integrity [116]. The overexpression of SIRT6 has been suggested to attenuate abdominal aortic aneurysm development through the inhibition of vascular smooth muscle aging, persistent inflammation, and pathological angiogenesis [117].

The interaction between aging processes, immune responses, and genetic regulation is a fundamental contributor to cardiovascular disease progression, underscoring potential molecular targets for treatment.

CVDs treatment approaches targeting aging

Current treatment strategies targeting established CVD risk factors have proven effective in reducing disease incidence. However, a considerable residual risk persists, and this burden is expected to increase with the aging population. Aging is a complex biological process influenced by multiple interconnected factors and signaling pathways. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying cardiomyocyte aging is essential for advancing our understanding of aging-related CVDs and facilitating the development of novel therapeutic strategies (Table 1).

Metabolic regulation and post-translational modifications

Therapies targeting metabolic regulation and posttranslational modifications (PTMs) provide various benefits in CVDs [118]. As a pivotal direction in future precision medicine and personalized therapy, these treatments stand out owing to their flexible action mechanisms, strong reversibility, and high individual adaptability, making them valuable options for combating aging-related cardiovascular diseases.

Aging-associated upregulation of sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) disrupts mitochondrial and sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ homeostasis, contributing to cardiac dysfunction [119]. Modulating SGLT2 activity may help preserve intracellular Ca2+ levels and support cardiac function in aging individuals [120]. Notably, SGLT2 inhibitors, such as empagliflozin and dapagliflozin, have demonstrated cardiovascular benefits [121]. Empagliflozin has been shown to attenuate the progression of HFpEF by partially inhibiting the senescence-associated STAT1–STING axis [122]. Furthermore, empagliflozin improves endothelial dysfunction in HFpEF by modulating microRNA profiles [123]. Meanwhile, dapagliflozin may slow atherosclerosis progression by suppressing the RXRA–PPARα–FABP4 signaling pathway and modulating aortic smooth muscle cell senescence, further supporting its therapeutic potential in age-related cardiovascular diseases [124]. Urocortin 2 (Ucn2), belonging to the corticotropin-releasing factor family, exerts cardioprotective effects. Gene transfer of Ucn2 has been demonstrated to recover normal cardiac function and prevent the development of left ventricular (LV) dysfunction in mice with age-associated cardiac impairment [125].

Senescence of VSMCs is essential in the advancement of atherosclerosis, with metabolic dysregulation as a defining feature. The obesity-associated gene FTO prevents VSMC senescence and mitigates the progression of atherosclerotic plaques through the stabilization of the MIS12 protein [126]. Metformin mediates cardiac protection and prevents aging-related ischemic necroptosis [127]. MitoTEMPO exerts antiarrhythmic effects in aged rats by reducing mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) [128]. Moreover, anthocyanins enhance the bioavailability of NO by modulating ROS production and reducing eNOS uncoupling, thereby protecting endothelial cells against senescence [129]. As a recognized anti-aging agent, it also mitigates VSMC senescence by upregulating SIRT3 expression through p-AMPK (Ser485)-dependent activation of mitochondrial antioxidants, enhancing cellular resistance to oxidative stress [130]. Additionally, metformin contributes to vascular health by stabilizing telomeres and slowing the progression of atherosclerosis via activation of the AMPK-dependent p-PGC-1α signaling pathway, further highlighting its potential in combating age-associated CVDs [131]. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) inhibits cellular senescence by activating pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), which phosphorylates histone H3T11. This mechanism suggests that enhancing serine biosynthesis may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy for promoting healthy aging [132].

Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) has been shown to mitigate myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury in aged rats by activating the SIRT3/FOXO1 pathway and reducing apoptosis, thereby preserving cardiac function [133]. Additionally, microRNA-203 (miR-203), an aging-associated microRNA, plays a critical role in regulating NAD homeostasis. By targeting the PARP1–NAD axis, miR-203 has been found to improve cardiac dysfunction in aging mice [134]. As a key enzyme in the NAD⁺ biosynthesis pathway, Nmrk2 catalyzes the conversion of nicotinamide riboside into NMN and is essential for maintaining cardiac structure and function [135]. Through the promotion of NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, proferroptosis signaling leads to vascular NAD depletion, cellular senescence, remodeling, and stiffening. Suppressing ferroptosis signaling or activating PPARγ may serve as an effective strategy to delay vascular aging [136]. Given that the heart is a high-energy-consuming organ with an exceptional demand for ATP, nucleotides play a fundamental role as building blocks for ATP synthesis. Notably, exogenous nucleotide supplementation has demonstrated protective effects on the heart tissue of senescence-accelerated mouse prone-8 mice, suggesting its potential in mitigating cardiac aging [137].

Lysine β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb) is a newly identified posttranslational modification that plays a crucial role in cardiac aging. Abnormal Kbhb modifications at key protein sites have been implicated in the progression of aging-related CVDs, suggesting that targeting and reversing these modifications may offer a potential therapeutic strategy [138]. Among proteins involved in cellular repair, MG53, a TRIM family protein, has been shown to facilitate redox-dependent cell membrane repair and prevent age-related HF by inhibiting NF-κB activation [139]. Insulin-like growth factor-binding protein 1 (IGFBP1) is one of the secreted proteins regulated by Jagged1. As a downstream molecule of Jagged1, its levels are closely associated with the severity of coronary atherosclerosis in elderly patients. Studies have shown that IGFBP1 plays a protective role in aging-related vascular diseases and may serve as a novel therapeutic target for anti-atherosclerosis treatment [140].

Sirtuins (Sirt1–Sirt7), a family of NAD⁺-dependent deacetylases [141], play a fundamental role in metabolic regulation and PTMs. Regulating Sirt1 expression has been suggested as a potential strategy for slowing cardiovascular aging, with α-lactalbumin identified as a modulator of this pathway [142]. Additionally, Klotho, an anti-aging protein secreted by renal tubular epithelial cells, enhances diastolic function in aging hearts via Sirt1-mediated mechanisms [143]. Premature vascular aging is promoted by decreased NRF2 activity [144]. The deficiency of Klotho contributes to cardiac aging by disrupting the Nrf2–glutathione reductase pathway, while exogenous Klotho shows potential for treating senile cardiomyopathy and HF [145]. As a novel adipokine, isthmin-1 (ISM1) promotes glucose uptake and plays a vital role in reducing inflammation and fibrosis. ISM1 prevents age-related cardiac dysfunction by promoting glycolysis and enhancing the deacetylase activity of Sirt1 [146]. Sirt2 promotes AMPK activation by deacetylating the kinase LKB1, thereby inhibiting aging-related cardiac hypertrophy [147]. Among the sirtuin family members, Sirt6 has emerged as a key regulator of aging-related cardiac dysfunction. It promotes angiogenesis by enhancing VEGFA secretion in ECs via its demethylation activity [148]. Moreover, Sirt6 exerts dual protective effects against myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis [149]. While also preventing myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury by limiting the accumulation of CHMP2B, a protein linked to aging [150]. Meanwhile, Sirt7 has been identified as a protective factor against vascular calcification, exerting its effects by modulating reactive oxygen species and inhibiting vascular smooth muscle cell senescence [151].

Oxidative stress and endoplasmic reticulum stress modulation

Aging-related CVDs are influenced by multiple factors, with oxidative stress serving as a key contributor to disease progression.

Suppressing P300/CBP-associated factor has been shown to alleviate oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway, thereby playing a protective role in preventing vascular aging [152]. As summarized by Batinac T, hyperbaric oxygen therapy has also been found to enhance antioxidant defenses, protect and restore endothelial function, and contribute to cardiovascular disease treatment [153].Moreover, as an inhibitor of 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase, SW033291 mitigates oxidative stress and chronic inflammation induced by aging, thereby enhancing cardiac function in elderly mice [154]. Furthermore, endogenous SO₂ exerts cardioprotective effects by preventing cardiomyocyte senescence, a process mediated by its ability to inhibit DNA damage through sulphenylation of Cys259 on STAT3 [155]. Similarly, N-acetylcysteine has demonstrated efficacy in mitigating atherosclerosis progression in aging LDL receptor-deficient mice by preserving M2 macrophage populations and enhancing CD146 expression [156].

Another promising therapeutic avenue involves targeting the endoplasmic reticulum stress pathway to counteract oxidative damage, which is a key contributor to aging-associated cardiovascular diseases. Specifically, alleviating endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress or restoring the normal expression of PRDX3 and PRDX4 has been proposed as a potential strategy for delaying disease onset and progression [157]. By targeting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, ruxolitinib-based senomorphic therapy mitigates cardiomyocyte aging in septic cardiomyopathy and restores mitochondrial dynamics and endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis disrupted by SCM [158].

Oxidative stress is a key driver of cardiovascular aging, and these therapeutic strategies slow cardiovascular functional decline through antioxidant mechanisms.

Stem cell-related therapies

Stem cell and exosome-based therapies hold significant promise in the treatment of CVDs [159], primarily by enhancing cardiac function through mechanisms such as promoting myocardial regeneration [160], modulating inflammatory responses, and exerting antioxidant and antifibrotic effects.

Recent studies have introduced a therapeutic approach targeting age-associated hypertension and cardiovascular damage by enhancing insulin-like growth factor-1 signaling through carboxyl-terminus of Hsp70 interacting protein cochaperone overexpression in stem cells, which has shown promising outcomes in vivo studies [161]. With increasing age, the regenerative function of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) gradually diminishes. Research indicates that macrophage migration inhibitory factor can rejuvenate senescent MSCs and enhance their myocardial repair capabilities [162]. The overexpression of myocardin and telomerase reverse transcriptase has been shown by Madonna R and colleagues to rejuvenate senescent MSCs [163]. Moreover, MSCs contribute to myocardial regeneration and cardiac function improvement after ischemic injury, providing a promising approach for treating aging-related cardiovascular diseases [164]. Hemin-preconditioned MSC-derived exosomes (Hemin-MSC-EXO) have demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy in MI compared with conventional MSC-derived exosomes. This enhanced effect is largely attributed to exosomal miR-183-5p, which inhibits cardiomyocyte senescence by regulating the HMGB1/ERK pathway [165]. In addition, in aged hosts, pretreatment with n-butylphthalide (NBP) reversibly ameliorates the hostile microenvironment, facilitates stem cell implantation, and mitigates postinfarction cardiac fibrosis [166]. However, the therapeutic potential of MSCs is compromised by aging-related functional decline. ALKB homolog 5 has been identified as a key driver of MSC senescence, as it stabilizes CDKN1C transcripts through an N6-methyladenosine-dependent mechanism. Suppressing ALKBH5 has been shown to rejuvenate senescent MSCs and enhance their therapeutic efficacy in MI [167]. Similarly, miR-155-5p plays a crucial role in MSC senescence by modulating the Cab39/AMPK signaling pathway, making it a potential target for restoring the regenerative capacity of aged MSCs and improving their cardioprotective effects [168]. Beyond MSC-based interventions, alternative stem cell therapies have also shown promise in cardiovascular regeneration. Based on omics analyses, adipose tissue-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (AT-MSCs) have been identified as clinically valuable owing to their broad therapeutic potential and functional properties [169]. The therapeutic use of AT-MSCs has been shown to be both safe and efficacious in patients with atherosclerosis, according to clinical research findings [170]. AT-MSCs, in combination with Alpinia oxyphylla extract, have been found to reduce mitochondria-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis and improve cardiac function in aging rat models [171]. Additionally, NF-κB signaling has been identified as a key regulator of human cardiac stem cell proliferation through serum-mediated pathways, further underscoring its relevance in regenerative treatments for CVDs [172].

Inflammation and immune modulation

Inflammation and the immune system play a crucial role in the progression of cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, HF, arrhythmias, and myocardial injury.

Several interleukins (ILs) have been implicated in the aging process and its associated cardiovascular dysfunction. In 2017, a research team conducted a landmark study targeting the inflammatory pathway (IL-1β) to reduce cardiovascular event risk, providing a new perspective for treating cardiovascular diseases from an inflammation-focused approach [173]. IL-6 knockout has been shown to reverse macrophage differentiation imbalances and alleviate cardiac dysfunction in aging mice [174]. Additionally, IL-36, a recently identified proinflammatory cytokine belonging to the IL-1 superfamily, has demonstrated vascular protective effects in aging hearts via the IL-36/IL-36R pathway, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for MI in elderly populations [175]. A novel strategy for limiting atherosclerosis progression involves targeting upstream inflammatory processes that drive the formation of vulnerable plaques [176]. Selective inhibition of phosphodiesterase 1 has been shown to improve vascular function in aged animals, suppress inflammatory responses, and lower blood pressure [177]. Moreover, the longevity-associated variant BPIFB4 has been found to reduce atherosclerosis progression by modulating CXCR4 activity, further underscoring its relevance in cardiovascular aging [178].

Pattern recognition receptors of the innate immune system also play a pivotal role in cardiovascular aging. TLR4 deficiency has been associated with improved cardiac function and enhanced endothelium-dependent vasodilation in aging mice [179]. Meanwhile, chloride intracellular channel 1 functions as an oxidative stress sensor in ECs, and its inhibition has been shown to protect against endothelial dysfunction and cellular senescence via activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway [180]. The gut microbiome has also emerged as a critical factor in cardiovascular aging [181]. In elderly patients with AF, Bacteroides fragilis abundance is significantly reduced. Oral supplementation with Bacteroides fragilis has been shown to enhance Treg cell levels, attenuate inflammation, prevent atrial structural remodeling, and suppress d-galactose-induced AF progression in aging rats [182]. Meanwhile, targeting T cells specifically represents a potential therapeutic strategy for managing hypertension in the elderly [183].

Noncoding RNAs, including long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs), have been increasingly recognized for their regulatory roles in cardiovascular aging. LncRNA H19 has been identified as a pro-aging factor in cardiac cells, where it promotes cardiomyocyte senescence by upregulating SOCS1 and activating the p53/p21 pathway via miR-19a targeting, offering a promising therapeutic target for age-related cardiovascular diseases [184]. Conversely, the aging-regulated anti-apoptotic lncRNA Sarrah has been found to facilitate recovery from acute MI, indicating its potential cardioprotective role [185]. In endothelial senescence, circRNAs may exert regulatory effects through the miR-323-5p/neuropilin-1 axis, contributing to endothelial angiogenesis in mice [186]. CircXYLT1 shows high expression levels in VSMCs and ECs. It plays a protective role by suppressing oxidative stress and facilitating vascular remodeling in aging mice [187].

Tissue repair and antifibrotic strategies

Multiple studies have found that cardiovascular diseases can be treated and cardiac activity restored through tissue repair and antifibrotic strategies.

Cisd2, a key longevity-associated gene, plays a crucial role in regulating mammalian lifespan and has been shown to rejuvenate cardiac function when its expression is upregulated [188]. Similarly, among subjects carrying a longevity-associated genotype, elevated FLT1 expression bolsters the stress resistance of the vascular endothelium, thus mitigating damage to key organs and tissues caused by hypertension-induced stress [189]. SKA-31, a Ca2+-activated potassium channel activator, has demonstrated the ability to enhance cardiovascular function in elderly patients without eliciting immune system disturbances or causing tissue toxicity, making it a promising therapeutic candidate for aging-related cardiovascular dysfunction [190]. In the context of MI, secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (Sfrp1) has emerged as a potential small-molecule gene therapy agent. By inhibiting the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, Sfrp1 confers cardioprotection in aging hearts, as evidenced by its ability to enhance cardiac function in aged mice, attenuate myocardial fibrosis, prevent cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and mitigate acute MI-induced injury [191]. Additionally, fibronectin type III domain-containing 5 has been identified as a cardioprotective factor in aging, improving age-related cardiac dysfunction through the activation of AMP-activated protein kinase α [192]. Meanwhile, the RNA-binding protein LARP7 has been shown to alleviate aortic aging and atherosclerosis when overexpressed, further highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in vascular aging [193].

These findings underscore the promise of gene-based and molecular therapies in mitigating age-related cardiovascular decline and enhancing long-term heart and vascular health.

Traditional Chinese medicine and natural bioactive compounds

It has been demonstrated that phytochemicals possess the capacity to delay the development of age-related brain diseases [194] and exert notable therapeutic effects on age-associated cardiovascular conditions.

Ginsenoside Rb1 has been demonstrated to ameliorate age-related myocardial dysfunction by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby exerting cardioprotective effects [195]. Additionally, Rb1 mitigates age-associated vascular damage through the Gas6 signaling pathway, highlighting its potential in vascular aging intervention [196]. Curcumin has been shown to reduce AF susceptibility in aging mice by regulating transfer RNA-derived small RNA (tsRNA) expression in atrial tissue [197]. Furthermore, Panax japonicus total saponins effectively improve cardiac aging phenotypes in senescent rats by enhancing basal autophagy through the AMPK/mTOR/ULK1 pathway, emphasizing the critical role of autophagic regulation in myocardial aging [198].

The combination of Irisin and Dendrobium nobile has been found to attenuate MI in aging by preventing NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis and promoting PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy, reinforcing the importance of mitochondrial quality control in cardiac aging [199]. Additionally, Yixin Fumai granules, a traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulation, have been shown to alleviate sick sinus syndrome in aged mice via activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, demonstrating the potential of TCM-based therapeutics in treating age-related cardiac arrhythmias [200]. The vascular anti-aging effects of Pinggan-Qianyang decoction in spontaneously hypertensive rats may be mediated through regulation of the SIRT1–PTEN signaling axis within aortic tissue [201]. Moreover, Ganoderma lucidum, known for its diverse pharmacological properties, yields GLSP from its reproductive cells. GLSP has been shown to attenuate vascular aging by activating the Sirt7–Nrf2 pathway and enhancing antioxidant responses [202].

Vascular wall aging is a fundamental contributor to CVDs. In vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that a combined extract of Salvia miltiorrhiza (Danshen) and Paeonia lactiflora (Baishao) effectively inhibits vascular aging, likely through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [203]. Additionally, canthaxanthin, a naturally occurring carotenoid, has been shown to attenuate cardiovascular aging in both in vitro and in vivo models [204, 205]. Restoring mitochondrial function has been identified as a crucial strategy for reversing cardiac dysfunction in elderly mice, underscoring the pivotal role of mitochondrial homeostasis in cardiac aging [206].

Aortic valve calcification is a hallmark of calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD), one of the most prevalent valvular heart disorders in aging populations. Morusin effectively reduces aortic valve calcification by preventing valvular interstitial cell senescence through the Ccnd1/Trim25/Nrf2 signaling pathway, offering a potential therapeutic target for CAVD management [207]. Similarly, schisandrol B, a bioactive compound, mitigates aortic valve calcification by modulating p53-associated inflammation and senescence, further supporting the role of senescence-targeted therapies in valvular heart disease [208]. In addition, the compound Danshen dripping pill effectively alleviates vascular calcification by regulating the DKK1/LRP6/β-catenin signaling pathway in endothelial and vascular smooth muscle cells, providing a novel strategy for vascular aging intervention [209].

As a naturally present sulfur-containing amino acid, taurine has attracted attention owing to its antioxidant capabilities and underlying mechanisms, positioning it as a potential component of anti-aging therapeutic approaches [210]. Finally, melatonin has been identified as a promising cardioprotective agent in aging, as it reduces aging-related HF in mice by modulating melatonin receptor 1A/B, further underscoring its potential as a therapeutic intervention for age-related cardiac dysfunction [211].

Future perspectives

To combat the high mortality rate of aging-related cardiovascular diseases, future studies should focus on cellular senescence and senolytic drug interventions [212, 213]. The progressive accumulation of senescent cells in the body promotes chronic vascular inflammation and damage through the secretion of inflammatory factors, ultimately contributing to cardiovascular disease development. Research has demonstrated that senolytic therapy revitalizes senescent cardiomyopathic features in human cardiac organoids [214]. Navitoclax, a senolytic drug shown to selectively target senescent cells, has been demonstrated to enhance cardiac function and electrophysiological characteristics in mice with heart failure by attenuating myocardial fibrosis, hypertrophy, and inflammatory responses [215]. The advancement of senolytic drugs [216], such as dasatinib–quercetin combinations or novel, more selective therapies, offers a promising avenue for mitigating cardiovascular aging and reducing disease risk [217].

Beyond targeting cellular senescence, mitochondrial function represents another critical avenue for future therapeutic exploration. Aging weakens mitochondrial activity, disrupting energy metabolism and increasing oxidative stress, which significantly impacts the function of cardiomyocytes and vascular cells. The development of mitochondrial protectants and mitochondria-specific antioxidants (such as MitoQ) [218] may contribute to more effective cardiovascular disease treatments.

Moreover, artificial intelligence (AI) will play a crucial role in assisting drug development. In recent years, deep learning has been successfully applied to predict the antibacterial activity of antibiotics and efficiently screen potential drug molecules using deep neural networks [219]. In the future, AI technologies such as machine learning and deep learning will further accelerate the identification of novel drug targets, predict drug efficacy and safety, and significantly improve the efficiency and success rate of drug development. This will facilitate the rapid and precise development of new drugs for aging-related cardiovascular diseases, providing strong support for precision medicine and personalized treatment.

Conclusions

Aging significantly contributes to the increasing global burden of cardiovascular diseases through complex molecular and cellular mechanisms involving cellular senescence, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysregulation. Targeting these aging-related processes through novel therapeutic approaches, including metabolic regulation, antioxidant strategies, stem cell therapies, immune modulation, and traditional bioactive compounds, shows considerable promise in combating cardiovascular deterioration associated with aging. Furthermore, advancements in artificial intelligence are expected to facilitate the rapid discovery and optimization of novel therapeutic agents. Future research should continue to focus on the molecular pathways underlying cardiovascular aging, further refining these strategies to enhance cardiovascular health, extend lifespan, and reduce the healthcare burden posed by cardiovascular diseases in aging populations.
