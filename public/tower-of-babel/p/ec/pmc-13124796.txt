Performance, phase composition, and life cycle assessment of marble waste-infused Portland and alkali-activated cements and mortars: A comparative analysis and perspective
Authors: Amira A. Shereiqi, Khalifa Al-Jabri, Mohammed S. Meddah, Kazi Md Abu Sohel, Hamdy A. Abdel-Gawwad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While extensive research has explored the macro-scale effects of marble waste powder (MP) on Portland cement (OPC) mortar and concrete, a significant knowledge gap persists regarding its impact on both the macro- and micro-structural properties of OPC and alkali-activated materials (AAMs). This state-of-the-art review aims to address this gap by providing a comprehensive comparative analysis of MP's influence on the fresh and hardened properties, as well as the phase composition of OPC and AAM pastes and mortars. Additionally, the paper delves into the life cycle assessment and processing costs of OPC-MP and AAMs-MP systems. The limitations of this study and future directions for MP applications in various binding systems are discussed. Generally, replacing OPC with MP decreases mechanical performance and slows the hydration rate due to cement dilution. However, the performance of MP-AAM is primarily influenced by the type and dosage of the alkali activator. MP enhances the performance of AAMs in sodium silicate-activated systems, whereas it negatively affects sodium hydroxide and sodium carbonate-activated systems. In OPC systems, MP primarily acts as a filler material; nevertheless, it contributes to the formation of monocarboaluminate binder. In contrast, in alkali-activated systems, the chemical reaction mechanism of MP predominates over its physical effects. This paper serves as a valuable resource for researchers and practitioners, offering insights into the in-depth reaction mechanisms of MP within both OPC and AAMs systems.

Introduction

Marble waste is a significant contributor to industrial solid waste, posing serious environmental threats such as air and water pollution, land infertility, and clogged underwater reservoirs (Tunc 2019). Marble waste, comprising quarry waste and sludge, is generated during the cutting, polishing, and sawing processes of marble stone (Raklami et al. 2022). The global demand for marble stone in decorative and prestigious construction projects is steadily increasing. Marble stone production constitutes approximately 50% of total natural stone production worldwide (Kore and Vyas 2016). Consequently, the accumulation of marble waste has become a pressing issue. Marble quarry operations typically result in a 20–30% waste by-product during the initial processing stage. Additionally, the polishing process generates a substantial amount of marble sludge, leading to a total waste percentage of 50–60% of the initial stone production (Aukour 2009; Yifru and Mitikie 2020).

Be'ah, Oman Environmental Services Holding Company, is responsible for managing the country's solid waste. Annually, the Be'ah landfills receive approximately 1.6 million tons of marble waste, whereas Oman generates 2.1 million tons (Al-Shereiqi et al. 2024a, b). This poses a significant environmental challenge. As depicted in Fig. 1, the ratio of marble waste to net marble stone is 1:1. Marble waste is primarily composed of calcite and, occasionally, dolomite minerals. Similar to limestone powder, it is considered a non-reactive material that can be utilized as a filler in various construction projects (Nayak et al. 2022). Marble quarry waste has been employed as a partial replacement for fine and coarse aggregates in mortar, concrete, and brick production (Bilgin et al. 2012; Galetakis and Soultana 2016; Kore and Vyas 2016). Additionally, it has been utilized in different sizes as base and sub-base layers for roads (Ural and Kahveci 2023; Pateriya et al. 2022). Marble powder (MP) is commonly used as a cement replacement in pastes, mortars, and concrete (Hou et al. 2024). Several reviews have explored the utilization of MP as a cement substitute. For example, Arel (2016) and Tunc (2019) investigated the impact of incorporating MP as a cement and aggregate replacement on the fresh and hardened properties of pastes, mortars, and concrete. Another literature review by Kuoribo and Mahmoud (2022) examined the application of MP in concrete production from 1999 to 2022. Meanwhile, Nayak et al. (2022) surveyed the effects of MP and granite powder on concrete performance. In contrast, the review by Hou et al. (2024) focused on investigating the influence of MP on the micro and macro properties of ordinary Portland cement pastes, mortars, and concrete.

MP is of great importance for both OPC and alkali-activated systems in terms of environmental protection and processing cost reduction. MP with high fineness is considered a good filling and nucleating agent, resulting in enhanced workability and accelerated hydration of the C3S phase in OPC (Jain 2012; Kadri et al. 2010). Additionally, CaCO3 inside MP can behave chemically, as it can interact with aluminate to yield hemi and monocarbonate (Jain 2012), which in some cases leads to an enhancement in compressive strength, especially at low replacement levels (Hou et al. 2024). In contrast, MP not only enhances the performance of alkali-activated systems containing different aluminosilicates, but it can also be totally used as a main precursor to produce a hardened binder with considerable compressive strength (Al-Shereiqi et al. 2024a, b).

Compared to ordinary Portland cement (OPC) systems, the role of MP in the performance of alkali-activated materials (AAMs) as pastes, mortars, and concretes has been rarely investigated (Coppola et al. 2020a). Furthermore, existing studies have primarily focused on macro-scale properties, such as fresh and hardened performance, with limited attention to micro-scale properties and the underlying reaction mechanisms of MP in alkali-activation processes. Additionally, the differences in reaction kinetics between MP in OPC and AAMs systems have not been adequately addressed. This paper provides a comparative study of MP's role in the fresh and hardened performance of OPC and AAMs. It also delves into the impact of MP on the hydration kinetics of both cementitious systems. By comprehensively examining the micro-scale performance of OPC-MP and AAMs-MP composites, including pore size distribution and phase composition, this review effort offers a clear understanding of the correlation between phase changes/developments and physical and mechanical performance.

A life cycle assessment (LCA) analysis, incorporating cost analysis, of OPC-MP and AAMs-MP composites has been conducted. To highlight novel findings not previously reported in the literature, some unpublished data has been included in various sections of this review. Consequently, this work serves as both an extensive review and a perspective study on the beneficial sustainable applications of MP in the construction sector. This review focused on published works from the past 10 years (2015–2024), as detailed in Tables 1 and 2. Fresh properties

Heat of hydration

Incorporating MP into conventional OPC and alkali-activated binder (AAB) significantly influences the hydration rate and heat evolution. Figure 2 illustrates the impact of MP on the heat flow, occurrence time of the main hydration peak, and cumulative heat of both OPC and AAB systems, as extracted from various previous reports. For OPC systems, Ma et al. (2019) and Prošek et al. (2020) observed a significant decrease in heat flow upon replacing OPC with high MP content, which is primarily attributed to the retardation of C3S hydration and dilution of OPC content. However, unlike OPC, the OPC-MP50 showed two main exothermic peaks. The first peak is mainly attributed to the formation of MCA via the interaction of CaCO3 with the C3A phase, which could be the reason behind the increase in the cumulative heat volume. This finding strongly aligns with the study of Ma et al. (2019), as they reported a slight increase in total cumulative heat with 10 wt.% MP replacement. This is potentially due to the formation of monocarboaluminate phase (Bonavetti et al. 2000). While the occurrence time of the main peak was delayed with increasing MP content, the heat flow increased during the initial 3 h (Table 3). This phenomenon could be attributed to the nucleating effect of MP, which accelerates the hydration rate of OPC, aligning with the findings of Choudhary et al. (2016) and Dobiszewska et al. In contrast, incorporating MP into Na2CO3-activated blast furnace slag (BFS) significantly decreased the heat of the main hydration peak and delayed its occurrence time (Du et al. 2021). The dilution of BFS with increasing MP content is the primary reason for the delayed hydration heat in alkali-activated systems. Unlike conventional OPC-MP systems, no acceleration in the initial stages of hydration has been observed in Na2CO3-activated BFS-MP systems, suggesting a marginal nucleating effect in this alkali-activated system. The type of alkali-activator could potentially influence the nucleating role of MP and its impact on the hydration rate of alkali-activated materials. Further research is needed to investigate the heat evolution of AAB-MP systems with different types of activator. Limestone powder, with a chemical composition similar to MP, has also been found to delay the main activation peaks in Na2CO3-activated BFS, especially at MP content exceeding 10 wt.% (Yuan et al. 2017a). However, limestone powder has exhibited an accelerating effect in NaOH and Na2SiO3-activated systems (Rakhimova et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2023a). This suggests that the alkali-activator plays a crucial role in the hydration kinetics of alkali-activated systems incorporating CaCO3-rich fillers. Comparing the heat evolution of different cementitious systems, it appears that the particle size of MP has a minimal impact on the overall trend of hydration kinetics.

It is recognized that the heat of hydration is primarily related to the reactive components within conventional OPC and alkali-activated systems. As reported by Prošek et al. (2020), the total cumulative heat normalized per unit weight of binder was higher than that normalized per total weight of the mixture. In AAB systems, the heat evolution rate is significantly influenced by the strategy employed for adding alkali-activator concentrations. Na2O equivalent can be added either based on the weight of aluminosilicate materials or the total weight of powdered materials, including fillers and reactive aluminosilicates. Adding Na2O equivalent based on the weight of slag results in a retardation of the activation rate, leading to a significant decrease in heat flow and total cumulative heat values (Du et al. 2021). Conversely, adding Na2O equivalent based on the total weight of powders can exhibit the opposite trend.

To corroborate these arguments, this study evaluates the heat evolution per unit weight of binder and investigates the hydration heat curve of AAB and AAB-MP systems, where the alkali-activator (NaOH) was added based on the total weight of powders (Fig. 3). It was observed that the normalized heat flow of OPC was higher than that of OPC replaced with 50 wt.% MP. Additionally, OPC paste exhibited a shorter induction period and a faster occurrence of the main hydration peak compared to the sample containing 50 wt.% MP. The control OPC paste also displayed a higher cumulative heat value up to 48 h. However, after 48 h, the OPC-MP mixtures exhibited higher total cumulative heat, aligning with the findings of Prošek et al. (2020). Conversely, AAB without MP exhibited a main hydration peak with lower intensity compared to the AAB-MP mixture. Moreover, the induction period and occurrence time of the main hydration peak accelerated with the addition of MP at the expense of BFS. This acceleration is not solely due to MP but rather to the increased alkali-activator density per unit weight of BFS. Replacing BFS with 50 wt.% MP reduces the BFS content, leading to an increase in the Na2O/BFS ratio. This, in turn, enhances the dissolution and condensation rates of BFS, accelerating the formation of activation products. Similar findings were reported by Chan and Zhang (2023) and Yuan et al. (2017b), who observed that incorporating limestone powder (with a similar chemical composition to MP) resulted in an increase in heat flow and cumulative heat per unit weight of BFS.Fig. 3Normalized heat flow and cumulative heat of OPC and AAS containing 50 wt.% of MP (This is unpublished data of the current work)

In conclusion, MP, as a CaCO3-rich system, was found to interact as a nucleating agent in the OPC system, as it enhances early hydration accompanied by an increase in cumulative heat, especially after 48 h (Prošek et al. 2020; Figure 5). In contrast, the use of MP as a substitute for BFS in the AAS system significantly accelerates the hydration rate, as CaCO3 within MP can contribute to the formation of an additional hydration phase via an exchange chemical reaction with the alkali-activator. Further discussion is provided in Sect. "Phase composition".

Setting time

Figure 4 illustrates the impact of MP on the setting time of fresh OPC and AAMs pastes. Generally, increasing the MP content from 5 to 40 wt.% at the expense of OPC leads to an increase in final setting time, as reported by several studies (Vardhan et al. 2015; Munir et al. 2017; Kechagia et al. 2021; Ghorbani et al. 2021). However, some studies (El-Didamony et al. 2020; Seghir et al. 2018) have shown that replacing OPC with up to 10 wt.% MP can result in a decrease in final setting time. Nevertheless, typically, replacing OPC with more than 20 wt.% MP retards the setting time. Interestingly, some studies have observed a shortening of the final setting time with higher MP content, such as 30 wt.% (Ma et al. 2019), 50 wt.% (Prošek et al. 2020), and 80 wt.% (Aydin and Arel 2019). This variation in trends may be attributed to differences in MP particle size, as Prakash et al. (2023) concluded that a shortening of setting time can occur when MP exhibits a fineness exceeding 600 m2/kg.Fig. 4Effect of MP content on the final setting time of OPC-MP pastes as well as effect of MP content, alkali-activator type, and alkali-activator dosage on the final setting time of AAB-MP pastes (AAVT = alkali-activated volcanic tuff; AAMP = Alkali-activated MP; MPS = Major particle size (The data was extracted from the previous studies and was redrawn)

Regardless of its fineness, particle size, or the type of alkali-activated binder, increasing the MP content in alkali-activated binders generally elongates the final setting time (Fig. 4). However, the alkali-activator concentration and composition play a crucial role in influencing setting time. At all MP contents, increasing the Na2O dosage accelerates the activation rate and shortens the final setting time (Tekin, 2016; Liu et al. 2023b). At a constant Na2O dosage of 10 wt.%, changes in the silica modulus (Ms) significantly affect the setting time of alkali-activated MP. Increasing Ms from 0 (NaOH) to 1.0 (Na2SiO3 with Ms = 1) results in a significant shortening of the final setting time. Further increases in Ms to 1.6 led to an elongation of the final setting time (Liu et al. 2023b). Unlike OPC pastes, the setting time of AABs is primarily governed by the type and dosage of the alkali-activator and the content of amorphous aluminosilicate within the alkali-activated system (Luukkonen et al. 2018). As previously discussed (Provis 2018), increasing the alkali-activator dosage can induce the dissolution of aluminosilicate and promote the formation of strength-giving phases, leading to a shorter setting time. Conversely, increasing the content of low-reactivity MP at the expense of high-reactivity aluminosilicate results in a significant retardation of strength-giving phase formation, leading to an elongation of setting time. Nevertheless, MP can be beneficial for inducing the setting of aluminosilicate with low Ca content at room temperature (Perná et al. 2021). Overall, MP exhibits different behaviors in both OPC and AAB systems. This is likely due to the distinct hardening mechanisms of these two binders.

In previous studies, the alkali-activator dosage was often added with respect to aluminosilicates to maintain a constant alkali-activator/aluminosilicate density. However, this approach resulted in a decrease in the alkali-activator content per unit weight of total powder (aluminosilicate and MP). Consequently, the elongation in setting time can be attributed to two factors: the dilution of aluminosilicate and the decrease in alkali-activator density per unit weight of total powdered materials. As shown in the thermal calorimetry (TC) results (Fig. 3), adding the alkali-activator based on the total weight of BFS and MP accelerates the activation rate of the formulated mixture. Similarly, as depicted in Fig. 5, when the alkali-activator is added based on the total weight of powdered materials, alkali-activated slag (AAS) blended with 50 wt.% MP exhibits a faster setting time compared to neat AAS. Flow rate

Figure 6 presents the flow diameter data for OPC-MP and AAB-MP systems, collected from various literature sources. In OPC-MP systems, no definitive trend is observed, as it is significantly influenced by material type (mortar or paste), water-to-binder (w/b) ratio, and MP fineness. Generally, increasing the w/b ratio enhances the flowability of OPC-MP mortars (Li et al. 2018; 2019). Dada et al. (2021) demonstrated that, at a constant w/b ratio of 0.40, adding 0.5 wt.% superplasticizer (SP) increased the flow diameter with increasing MP content up to 30 wt.%, followed by a significant decrease in workability at 40 wt.% MP. Munir et al. (2017) found that, in the absence of SP, increasing MP up to 40 wt.% decreased the workability of fresh mortar. However, Ma et al. (2019) reported an increase in workability of OPC mortar with increasing MP content up to 30 wt.% without the need for SP. Furthermore, the flow diameters reported by Ma et al. (2019) were higher than those reported by Dada et al. (2021) and Munir et al. (2017) at constant MP content. As suggested by Prakash et al. (2023), the high surface area of MP can contribute to a reduction in workability of OPC-MP mortars. However, contrary results were observed in the studies by Munir et al. (2017) and Ma et al. (2019). Munir et al. (2017) found that using MP up to 30 wt.% with a mode particle size of 45 µm led to a decrease in workability, while Ma et al. Regardless of MP fineness, the workability of AAB-MP increases with MP content. Du et al. (2021) stated that the flow diameter increases with increasing MP content up to 50 wt.% in Na2CO3-activated BFS mortar. Incorporating 50 wt.% MP was found to increase the flow diameter of the mortar by 40%. They attributed this finding to the lubricant property of MP, which comes from its smooth surface. Demir et al. (2024) showed the same trend in NaOH-activated BFS-MP. Moreover, the mortars mixed with magnetized water exhibited a higher flow diameter compared to those mixed with tap water. On the other hand, Liu et al. (2023a, b) found that alkali-activated MP is strongly influenced by activator composition and concentration. Regardless of the activator type, increasing the Na2O content decreases the flow diameter of the produced mortar, which is mainly attributed to an increase in the dissolved silica portion, leading to an increase in the consistency of the activator and, as a result, a reduction in fluidity. Additionally, increasing the Ms of Na2SiO3 from 1 to 1.6 enhanced the fluidity of the resulting fresh mortars. This aligns with the setting time retardation with increasing Ms value, which is ascribed to the retardation of the alkali-activation rate (Liu et al. 2023b).

In conclusion, unlike OPC-MP composites, embedding MP in AABs generally results in an increase in the fluidity of the resulting fresh mortars. The improvement in fluidity is primarily attributed to the retardation of the alkali-activation rate with the addition of MP, as MP exhibits lower reactivity towards alkali-activators compared to aluminosilicate precursors (Liu et al. 2024). In contrast, in OPC-MP systems, no clear trend is observed, although there is a tendency towards an increase in fluidity with increasing MP content. This difference between OPC-MP and AAB-MP systems primarily stems from the differences in the hydration mechanisms of each binder and the parameters that influence their hydration rates.

Hardened properties

Compressive strength

Regardless of the type of binding material (paste or mortar), different trends in compressive strength have been observed in OPC-MP and AAB-MP systems. The role of MP in the compressive strength of OPC systems primarily depends on the material type (paste or mortar), MP content, and MP fineness. For OPC-MP pastes (Fig. 7), El-Didamony et al. (2020) and Prošek et al. (2020) found that replacing OPC with up to 10 wt.% MP enhanced the 28-day compressive strength of the resulting hardened paste by 40% and 14%, respectively. However, the compressive strength decreased when OPC was replaced with more than 10 wt.% MP. Selim et al. (2020) showed that the OPC paste with 10 wt.% MP exhibited compressive strength close to the control paste (without MP addition). Other studies (Nežerka et al. 2018; Seghir et al. 2018; Aydin and Arel 2019) concluded that replacing OPC with MP decreased the compressive strength of the resulting hardened paste, regardless of the replacement level. This discrepancy could be related to variations in MP fineness (see Table 1). The desirable content of MP with high fineness can enhance cement hydration through its high nucleating effect, resulting in an increase in compressive strength (Benjeddou et al. 2020). Apart from the fineness of MP powder, replacing OPC with more than 20 wt.% MP causes a significant decrease in 28-day compressive strength. The dilution of cement content is the primary reason behind this finding (Prakash et al., 2023).

Regarding AAM-MP pastes, the compressive strength trend depends on the synergistic type and reactivity of aluminosilicates, as well as the alkali-activator type and dosage (Fig. 7). Apart from curing conditions, increasing the replacement of volcanic tuff in NaOH-activated systems with MP resulted in a decrease in the compressive strength of the hardened materials (Tekin 2016). In contrast, in Na2SiO3-activated systems, replacing calcined clay (CCl) with 30 wt.% MP resulted in an ultimate 28-day compressive strength comparable to that of the control sample, even with a lower Na2Oeq concentration (Mascarin et al. 2022). Additionally, Coppola et al. (2020a, b) found that Na2SiO3-activated MP paste exhibited a considerable 28-day compressive strength, close to those of the hardened systems containing 2.5 and 5 wt.% MP. Furthermore, Salihoglu and Salihoglu (2018) showed a compressive strength gain with replacing fly ash (FA) with MP in Na2SiO3-activated systems. This indicates that, in terms of mechanical properties, MP can be beneficially used in Na2SiO3-activated pastes more than NaOH-activated pastes. Nevertheless, Bikoko and Bayiha (2023) demonstrated that incorporating MP in activated metakaolin (MK) paste at low NaOH/Na2SiO3 concentrations (3 M and 5 M) caused a significant loss in strength. However, using higher concentrations resulted in a significant compressive strength increment, even with high MP content up to 60 wt.%. This suggests that, at high Na2SiO3/NaOH concentrations, MP can interact with sodium and silicate to form binding phases (Coppola et al. 2020a, b), in addition to those formed from MK activation. The reactivity of the aluminosilicate can also play a crucial role in the compressive strength trend.

Like OPC-MP pastes, MP with a lower particle size and high fineness increases the compressive strength of the resulting OPC-MP mortar (Fig. 7). Specifically, compressive strength gains can be recorded by using MP with a particle size lower than 15 µm. Nevertheless, the optimum MP content varies across different studies. For instance, Li et al. (2019) showed that the 28-day compressive strength of the mortar increased with increasing MP content up to 20 wt.%, regardless of the mixing water content. While Ghorbani et al. (2021) found that 10 wt.% is the optimal MP content, regardless of the type of mixing water. Other studies (Munir et al. 2017; Vardhan et al. 2015), using 10 wt.% MP with a fineness of 329–351 m2/kg, resulted in a marginal increase in the 28-day compressive strength of the resulting OPC-MP mortar.

Alkali-activated mortars (Fig. 8) exhibited a similar trend to alkali-activated pastes. A significant decrease in compressive strength was observed in Na2CO3- and NaOH-activated systems (Du et al. 2021; Demir et al. 2024), while a slight change in strength was detected in Na2SiO3-activated mortars, regardless of the source of aluminosilicate and Na2O concentration (Gill et al. 2024). Liu et al. (2023a) found that MP can be fully recycled to produce alkali-activated mortar with a 28-day compressive strength reaching ~ 7 MPa, indicating the ability of CaCO3 within MP to interact with Na2SiO3 to form binding phases. Further discussion on the reaction mechanisms of MP in both OPC and AAB systems is presented later in Sect. "Phase composition".

Drying shrinkage and pore size distribution

Drying shrinkage of cementitious systems primarily depends on the type of binder, mixing water content, and curing conditions (Hou et al. 2024). As reported in the literature (Palacios and Puertas 2007; Jin et al. 2014), in low-humidity environments below 70%, AABs exhibit higher drying shrinkage compared to OPC-based binders. However, at humidity more than 70%, both cements exhibited comparable shrinkage. The primary difference between the two systems is related to several factors, including the composition of binding phases and the type of pores formed within the cementitious matrix (micropores, mesopores, and macropores) (Huang et al. 2021). Regardless of the cement type, drying shrinkage is strongly influenced by the moisture content within the hardened sample (Hou et al. 2024). Additionally, the escape of excess water causes shrinkage in the hardened sample. The magnitude of shrinkage and its rate of growth primarily depend on the rate of moisture loss during the drying of the sample at low relative humidity. Increasing the moisture loss, especially during the first 10 days, increases drying shrinkage (Zhang et al. 2023).

MP was found to strongly affect the drying shrinkage and pore size distribution of OPC and AAB systems (Fig. 9 and Table 4). As reported by Li et al. (2018), replacing OPC with up to 20 wt.% MP resulted in a significant decrease in the drying shrinkage value of the hardened mortar up to 180 days of curing (Table 5). A similar trend was observed by Yamanel et al. (2019). This is primarily attributed to the finer particle size of MP compared to OPC. Li et al. (2018) used MP with 40% of particles smaller than 4 µm, which is finer than that of OPC. The fine particles enhance the packing effect and decrease the pore diameter of the resulting hardened mortar, consequently hindering the moisture escape rate. Ma et al. (2019) found that replacing OPC (d0.5 = 12.2 µm) with 30 wt.% of MP (d0.5 = 12.2 µm) resulted in a decrease in pore diameter and pore volume of the hardened pastes (Table 3). Prošek et al. (2020) concluded that 15 wt.% is the optimal value to decrease the total porosity, pore diameter, and pore volume of the resulting OPC paste. They explained this finding by the micro-filling effect of MP with a higher fineness (median particle diameter 5.2 µm) compared to OPC (12.8 µm). Further increasing the MP content to 50 wt.% increased the total porosity by 5%, suggesting a retardation in the formation of binding phases caused by cement dilution. Nevertheless, Nežerka et al. (2018) found that the optimal MP content was 50 wt.%, as it decreased the total cumulative pore volume of the resulting OPC-MP paste by 30%. On the other hand, regardless of the MP content, no reduction in drying shrinkage of the hardened samples was recorded when MP was used with a coarser particle size compared to OPC (Seghir et al. 2019), confirming the role of the filling effect of finer MP and its impact on the pore system (Prošek et al. 2020).Fig. 9Effect of MP content on the drying shrinkage of the hardened OPC mortars and AAM pastes (AAMP-alkali-activated MP; GW = glass waste; AACCl = alkali-activated calcined clay) (The data was extracted from the previous studies and was redrawn)Table 4Effect of MP on the pore diameter and pore volume of the hardened pastes (Ma et al. 2019)SamplePorosity (mL/g)Pore volume distribution (mL/g) < 20 nm20–50 nm50–200 nm > 200 nmControl0.20830.06260.06640.05470.0246OPC + 30 wt% MP0.19460.05240.08230.04240.0165Table 5Summarizes the effect of MP on OPC and alkali-activated systemsRefMaterialsMix codeBinder typeMP wt.%Particle size, µm (surface area, m2/kg)ShrinkageFindingsAydin and Arel (2019)PastePC-MPOPC (Type 1)0–100 < 50 (n. d.)DecreasedMP is porous and acts like a sponge. As reported earlier (Atiş et al. 2009; Mastali et al. 2018), the drying shrinkage of AAMs increases with increasing alkali-activator concentration. Additionally, Na2SiO3-activated materials exhibited higher drying shrinkage than NaOH-activated mixtures (Kucharczyková et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2022; Mastali et al. 2018). The drying shrinkage of AAM-MP composites primarily depends on the MP content, curing conditions, and alkali-activator dosages (Thakur et al. 2019; Bikoko and Bayiha 2023). As shown in Fig. 9, Coppola et al. (2020a, b) found that the full activation of MP with Na2SiO3 resulted in the formation of a hardened paste with a high 28-day drying shrinkage of 3.95% in air curing. The individual replacement of MP with 2.5 and 5.0 wt.% waste glass (WG) decreased the 28-day drying shrinkage by approximately 14% and 67%, respectively. A similar trend was observed in other curing media (water and 95 ± 2% relative humidity), but with lower drying shrinkage rates and values compared to air-cured samples. The reduction in shrinkage by incorporating WG is primarily attributed to the micro-filling effect of unreacted WG particles and the formation of silicate-containing phases that improve binding capacity and microstructural densification (Zhang and Yue 2018). Both factors contribute to a decrease in water escape during sample drying, consequently hindering the drying shrinkage rate. On the other hand, Mascarin et al. (2022) found that using MP as a substitute for decreased the alkali-activator (Na2SiO3, Ms = 1.6) demand, coinciding with a reduction in drying shrinkage. They observed that replacing CCl with 30 wt.%, 40 wt.%, and 50 wt.% MP decreased the drying shrinkage of the resulting hardened pastes by 21%, 21%, and 44%, respectively, at 7 days of curing in 55 ± 3% relative humidity (Fig. 9).

Unlike OPC, alkali-activated mixtures exhibit complex drying shrinkage behavior due to factors such as capillary tensile force, moisture loss, and pore size distribution (Matalkah et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2019). The majority of pore diameters within hardened samples fall within the mesopores range (2–25 nm), which is considered one of the main reasons for the higher drying shrinkage of AAB compared to OPC (Shi 1996). Specifically, Collins and Sanjaya (2000) demonstrated that 82% of pores within AAS paste are located in the mesopores scale. The elimination of free water or moisture escape during drying through these mesopores results in capillary stress and, consequently, greater drying shrinkage compared to that observed in OPC with a lower mesopores ratio (36.4%).

Overall, the role of MP in drying shrinkage mainly depends on the cement composition, alkali-activator type and dosage, and the type of silicate-rich precursor in the alkali-activated system (Table 5).

Phase composition

The mineralogical phases of MP were determined primarily using X-ray diffraction (XRD). As shown in Table 1 and Table 2, the crystalline phases were primarily calcite and dolomite, with small amounts of quartz, which prevented them from participating actively in the hydration process that led to calcium silicate formation (Al-Shereiqi et al. 2024a, b).

CaCO3 is the main mineral component of MP; however, a small peak corresponding to dolomite was also detected (Fig. 10). The reaction mechanism of CaCO3 strongly depends on the type of cementitious system. In both OPC and AAMs systems, CaCO3 acts as a physical filler/nucleating agent, accelerating the hydration rate and the formation rate of binding phases (Ma et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2021, 2023; Chen et al. 2024). Chemically, in OPC systems, CaCO3 can interact with calcium aluminate to form hemi- and monocarboaluminate (HC and MCA) (Ipavec et al. 2011; McDonald et al. 2022), respectively. It also stabilizes ettringite, contributing to an increase in the volume of binding phases within the OPC hydrated matrix (Zhang et al. 2019). In limestone calcined clay cement, using MP as an alternative to natural limestone powder results in the formation of the same CaCO3-containing phases, such as HC and MCA (Wang et al. 2022; Krishnan et al. 2018). Replacing PC with MP induces the formation of MCA and HC (Kabeer et al. 2021). Moreover, incorporating BFS in PC-MP systems enhances the formation of both phases, primarily due to the increased alumina concentration with the addition of supplementary aluminosilicates (El-Didamony et al. 2020). Overall, the optimal content of MP in OPC and AAM systems can act as nucleation sites to accelerate the hydration rate of the binder and consequently increase the formation of binding phases, leading to improved performance of the resulting binders. MCA can also form within both cementitious systems through interactions with dissolved alumina and calcium. However, incorporating a high content of MP in both systems can dilute the binder, resulting in a retardation of the formation rate of binding phases. Nevertheless, non-hydraulic MP can be easily transformed into a binder through mixing with alkali-activators, as the phase composition of the final products primarily depends on the composition of the alkali-activator. This approach can address the issue of limited availability of aluminosilicates, which are the primary precursors in alkali-activated systems.

Life cycle analysis

It is well known that there is a great difference in CO2 emissions resulting from OPC and AAMs manufacturing, as well as in the energy demand for each binder. OPC manufacturing is the most significant contributor to CO2 emissions worldwide, accounting for 8% of the total CO2 emissions generated from different sources (York and Europe 2021; Jin et al. 2024). The estimated CO2 emissions per ton of OPC range from 0.8 to 1 tonne CO2, primarily resulting from calcite decomposition and fuel burning (Gartner 2004; Gonnon and Lootens 2023). Additionally, manufacturing each ton of OPC consumes 3.2–6.3 GJ of energy (Rahman et al. 2015). AAMs have been found to emit lower CO2 compared to OPC (Munir et al. 2023) because they primarily rely on the reuse of aluminosilicate wastes from other industries. The CO2 emissions mainly stem from the utilization of alkali-activators and waste processing. Nevertheless, alkali-activators are the primary source of CO2 emissions resulting from AAMs (Yang et al. 2013; Alnahhal et al. 2021). Embedding MW as a substituent to OPC and aluminosilicate was found to significantly decrease the carbon footprint resulting from the manufacturing of both binders. Zulcão et al. (2020) stated that in Brazil, as a case study, each 10 wt% of ornamental stone waste containing MP was found to decrease the environmental indicators of OPC (CO2 emissions and energy demand) by 7.7 to 9%. Environmentally, ornamental stone waste was comparable to conventional fillers such as limestone, sand, and stone powder. Nevertheless, these environmental indicators were significantly influenced by the drying method used for stone sludge and the transportation distance (Zulcão et al. 2020). They also stated that using this waste as an OPC substitute is more environmentally beneficial in masonry and self-compacting concrete production than conventional concrete.

The use of MP not only mitigates environmental indicators but also decreases the cost of final concrete products. Singh et al. (2017) found that the environmental impacts and costs of final concrete products depend primarily on the MP incorporation method (MP substitution in OPC or in fine aggregate). Alkali-activated concrete exhibited 64% lower global warming potential, 26% lower fossil depletion, and 11% lower acidification potential compared to OPC-based concrete (Abdulkareem et al. 2022). Unlike OPC concrete, the impact of MP on the environmental indicators and the final cost of concrete was rarely studied. Mascarin et al. (2022) evaluated the role of MP in the global warming potential of sodium silicate-activated CCl binder. They found that replacing CCl with MP led to several environmental benefits, including reducing Na2Oeq, decreasing the energy demand required for thermal treatment of raw clay, and decreasing the environmental impacts of MP waste through valorisation in the production of alkali-activated binders. Specifically, replacing calcined clay with 30, 40, and 50 wt% MP led to a decrease in the Na2Oeq wt% from 11 to 8.4, 7.4, and 6.3 wt%, respectively.

As mentioned by Ouellet-Plamondon and Habert (2015) and Fawer et al. (1999), each kilogram of Na2SiO3 can generate 1.22 to 1.76 kg of CO2. Accordingly, the decrease in Na2Oeq demand by incorporating MP indirectly results in a decrease in CO2 emissions resulting from the manufacturing of Na2SiO3-activated CCl. Moreover, the reduction in the volume of CCl also results in a decrease in the energy demand required for the thermal treatment of raw clay, accompanied by minimizing the processing cost and CO2 emissions, especially those resulting from fuel burning (Mascarin et al. 2022). Additionally, incorporating MP enhances CO2 sequestration inside the alkali-activated calcined clay binders by providing the system with a calcium source, which induces the formation of gaylussite double salt through atmospheric carbonation (Mascarin et al. 2022).

Review limitations and future directions

While MP macroscopic performance is well documented. A significant limitation remains regarding the multi-scale visualization and the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) degradation and dynamic microcrack evolution. Future research must study advanced spatial methodologies, specifically in suit computed tomography (CT), 4D CT, and digital volume correlation (DVC). Although these cutting-edge techniques offer a structured framework for accurately tracking initiation, propagation, and coalescence, as recently demonstrated in recycled aggregate concrete (Wang et al. 2025a, 2025b). This application on the MP incorporated system remains critically limited. Bridging the gap by applying CT and DVC techniques to MP concrete will provide crucial spatial data needed to enhance the mechanism interpretation of the damage pathway under complex stresses. Furthermore, the rare data on LCA analysis of AAMs-MP is the main limitation of this review. More research is needed to fully assess the role of MP from different sources (such as quarrying marble, processing marble sludge, and processing marble waste) on the environmental impacts and processing costs of AAMs-MP concretes. Additionally, the application of MP from different resources on the fabrication of limestone calcined clay cement should be expanded to conserve natural limestone powder resources that could be more beneficial for other critical applications. Calcite mineral inside marble stone represents higher crystallinity compared to that existing in limestone (Miranda 2004; Winkler 2013). Accordingly, this difference in crystallinity may significantly influence the nucleation effect of each material. The use of MP as a substituent to calcium aluminate cement was rarely addressed. More research is needed to fully cover the effect of MP on the physical and mechanical performance, as well as phase composition, LCA analysis, and processing cost of calcium aluminate cement. The role of carbonation on MP incorporating OPC and AAM is another missing point. As demonstrated in recent literature, such carbonation-induced microstructural reorganization plays a critical role in modifying microcrack initiation and dictating crack propagation pathways in MP-free cementitious systems (Wang et al. 2025c). Therefore, this topic is very important to evaluate the role of MP on the durability of OPC and alkali-activated systems. Furthermore, a notable limitation of this study is conducting a mathematical modelling to quantify the dynamic failure process. Future research must address this gap by incorporating advanced computational frameworks, such as strain-variance analysis and a damage evolution function, to better capture progressive material degradation (Wang & Lu 2026).

Conclusions

This paper presented a comparative study on the role of MP in the fresh and hardened properties of OPC and AAMs pastes and mortars. It also focused on the reaction mechanism of MP and its impact on the phase composition of both binders. An LCA analysis of OPC-MP and AAMs-MP systems has been surveyed. Several important findings extracted from this survey can be drawn as follows:I.As a general trend, replacing OPC with MP causes a retardation in its hydration rate, which is associated with cement dilution. Nevertheless, it significantly improves the workability of the produced OPC pastes/mortars. The hydration rate of OPC is not affected by the fineness of MP; however, it is a parameter in OPC-MP’s setting time. In contrast, the rate of hydration of AAMs-MP is significantly governed by alkali-activator composition and dosage. MP causes a retardation in Na2CO3-activated systems; while it has an acceleration effect on NaOH- and Na2SiO3-activated aluminosilicates. Adding activator by total weight of powder (MP and aluminosilicates) increased the intensity of activator concentration per aluminosilicate, associating with an acceleration in the alkali-activation rate and setting time. Unlike the OPC system, MP fineness has a minimal impact on the hydration kinetics and fresh properties of AAMs.II.No clear trend has been observed regarding the compressive strength of OPC pastes and mortars containing less than 20 wt% MP. Some studies have reported compressive strength gains, while others have shown no change or a decrease, which is often attributed to variations in MP fineness. In contrast, the compressive strength of AAMs-MP composites is significantly influenced by the synergistic effects of alkali-activator type, dosage, and aluminosilicate source. At optimal Na2O concentrations, incorporating MP into Na2SiO3-activated systems can enhance compressive strength, while its inclusion in NaOH- and Na2CO3-activated systems may lead to a reduction in strength.III.MP is typically used as a partial replacement for OPC, with a maximum replacement level of 80 wt%. Complete replacement of OPC with MP is not feasible due to MP's low reactivity with water. In contrast, within alkali-activated systems, MP can be fully utilized by interacting with alkaline solutions to produce hardened materials exhibiting significant compressive strength.IV.The reaction mechanism of MP varies significantly between different cement systems. In OPC, it primarily functions as a filler and nucleating agent, with some chemical contribution through the formation of the monocarboaluminate phase. In alkali-activated systems, MP significantly influences the type of activation products formed. Cation exchange reactions between MP and alkaline solutions release calcium ions, which can strongly contribute to the formation of various phases, including monocarboaluminate, calcium silicate hydrate, and pirssonite. The specific phases formed depend on the content of MP and the composition of the alkali-activator.V.A significant reduction in the environmental impact and processing cost of OPC can be achieved by substituting it with MP. Furthermore, incorporating MP into alkali-activated systems reduces the demand for Na2O, consequently lowering CO2 emissions and the overall processing cost of the final building products. However, the LCA analysis of AAMs-MP systems remains incomplete, as the impact of MP source, alkali-activator type, and aluminosilicate source on LCA has not been fully explored. This represents a crucial knowledge gap that warrants further investigation.VI.A comprehensive distinction in the behavior of MP on OPC and AAM system, revealing trends and reaction mechanisms that have not been discussed previously. By addressing the hydration kinetics and binding mechanisms, which represent a significant research gap, this study investigated. This comprises emphasizing the potential for optimizing MPs' properties for different applications.
