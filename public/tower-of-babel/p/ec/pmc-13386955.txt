TSH receptor autoantibodies – a personal experience
Authors: Bernard Rees Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nineteen fifty six was a pivotal year in our understanding of thyroid disease with the discovery of long acting thyroid stimulator (LATS) in the serum of patients with Graves’ disease and autoantibodies to thyroglobulin in Hashimoto’s disease. Soon after its discovery, LATS was shown to be a thyroid stimulating autoantibody and then in 1974 to be an autoantibody to the TSH receptor. Further major advances were made in 1989 (cloning the TSH receptor) and production of the thyroid stimulating human monoclonal autoantibody M22™ in 2002. The availability of M22™ enabled determination of the crystal structure of the TSH receptor in complex with M22™. Subsequently, three more human monoclonal autoantibodies to the TSH receptor were produced. One with stimulating activity (K1-18™) and two with blocking (antagonist) activity (5C9™ and K1-70™). Cryo-EM analysis of these four monoclonal autoantibodies in complex with the TSH receptor has provided a detailed understanding of how TSH receptor autoantibodies with different properties function. The monoclonal autoantibody K1-70™ with powerful TSH receptor blocking activity is in clinical trials and shows the expected beneficial effect on Graves’ hyperthyroidism and Graves’ ophthalmopathy. It is an exciting new TSH receptor specific drug.

Historical perspective

The description of long acting thyroid stimulator (LATS) in the serum of patients with Graves’ disease by Adams and Purves [1] at the University of Otago in New Zealand and autoantibodies to thyroglobulin in the serum of patients with Hashimoto’s disease by Roitt et al. [2] at the Middlesex Hospital in London mark 1956 as a pivotal year in our understanding of thyroid disease.

Soon after its discovery, the University of Otago group showed that LATS was only present in the IgG fraction of serum proteins suggesting that it was a thyroid stimulating autoantibody [3].

In 1958, Porter working at Mill Hill in London isolated the Fab and Fc fragments of IgG [4] and then showed IgG to be composed of two heavy chains and two light chains linked by non-covalent bonds and disulphide bridges [5]. Porter’s studies were quickly applied to IgG with LATS activity [6] and in 1965 I joined Dorrington and Munro’s laboratory in Sheffield to study for a PhD (awarded 1968) and to continue this work [7].

The aims of my research were to obtain pure LATS and pure LATS autoantigen and use them to understand the origins of LATS and how it acted. It was also anticipated that the pure preparations would be the basis of (a) new assays for LATS and (b) new therapeutics for controlling Graves’ disease. This early background was the foundation of a career involving many talented students and colleagues and collaborations with experts in different areas of science, medicine and business. In this review (presented in part in my 2025 Pitt-Rivers lecture to the Society for Endocrinology) I would like to share a few of the key discoveries with you and assess how much progress has been made.

The structural features of LATS autoantibodies were well established in the 1960’s [6, 7] and it was proposed [3, 6] that LATS was an antibody to the same thyroid gland component which interacts with TSH i.e. an autoantibody to the TSH receptor. However this concept took many decades to be accepted widely.

Experiments with homogenates of Graves’ thyroid tissue showed that they absorbed or neutralised LATS activity and much to the surprise of this investigator most of the neutralising activity was present in the homogenate supernatant after centrifugation at 105,000 g rather than in the sediment containing thyroid cell membrane material [8].

Fractionation of the homogenate supernatant (soluble fraction) by gel filtration on Sephadex G-200 showed that the LATS absorbing activity i.e. LATS autoantigen or LAA was only present in the column fraction co-eluting with serum albumin i.e. a 4S protein molecular weight about 50,000 [8].

The 4S LAA material was purified further, characterised and its interaction with LATS studied in detail [9]. It was clear from these studies that purified preparations of LAA had potential for specifically neutralising and inactivating LATS activity in patients. Such potential treatment is considered later in this review.

The relationship between LAA and the TSH receptor was not clear in 1970, particularly as pituitary TSH (human or bovine) could not be absorbed by particulate or soluble preparations of thyroid homogenates in the same way LATS activity could [10].

Many key observations were made using bioassays based on stimulation of 131I release from the thyroids of guinea pigs or mice [3, 6] but the assays were always technically demanding, imprecise and unsuitable for analysing large numbers of patient samples.

In 1974 however there was a major conceptual and technical breakthrough when IgG preparations from Graves’ sera were shown to inhibit 125I-labelled TSH binding to thyroid membranes [11]. Assays for TSH receptor autoantibodies (TRAb) which depended on inhibition of labelled TSH binding to thyroid membranes could only be carried out in specialised research laboratories. Despite this limitation, extensive studies on TRAb activity in patient immunoglobulin preparations were carried out in the 1970’s [12].

These studies showed (a) TRAb were detectable in almost all sera from patients with untreated hyperthyroid Graves’ disease (b) a good relationship between TRAb levels and various parameters of thyroid function including early 131I uptake and TSH control of thyroid function (c) different treatments for hyperthyroid Graves’ disease had different effects on TRAb levels with patients treated by surgery the lowest prevalence, with 131I the highest prevalence and with anti-thyroid drugs an intermediate prevalence.

Although TRAb stimulate the TSHR in untreated Graves’ hyperthyroidism and when present in most patients with treated Graves’ disease, TRAb with blocking activity were described by Endo et al. in 1978 [13] in occasional hypothyroid patients. There was also evidence in 1978 that some patients with Ophthalmic Graves’ disease (defined as the eye signs of Graves’ disease with no history of hyperthyroidism) had serum TRAb which did not stimulate the TSH receptor [14].

The negative response I received in Japan in 1981 to what I thought was convincing evidence that TSH receptor autoantibodies caused Graves’ hyperthyroidism was disappointing. More was clearly needed to convince doubting thyroidologists and we continued our activities. This included the development and manufacture of a diagnostic kit for the detection of TRAb (Smith kit) [15, 16] and formation of RSR Limited in 1982.

Cloning of the TSH receptor was achieved in 1989 by several laboratories [17–20] and in 2002, the RSR team produced the thyroid stimulating monoclonal autoantibody M22™ [21, 22]. Its availability has had a major impact on many aspects of thyroidology. These include improved TRAb diagnostics, particularly automation [23], structural and functional studies on the TSH receptor [24], the pioneering development of small molecule TSH receptor antagonists by the Organon group [25–27] and a panel of human monoclonal TSH receptor autoantibodies for therapeutic, diagnostic and research use [28, 29].

Shortly before we produced M22™ we were able to produce the first three mouse TSHR Mabs with strong thyroid stimulating activity [30, 31]. Also, a hamster Mab with strong thyroid stimulating activity was described [32]. These studies were a culmination of research by many different laboratories and represented an important milestone in the thyroid field [33].

Following on from our success with M22™ we were able to work with Dr Tagami in Kyoto to obtain lymphocytes from one of his female Japanese patients K1. Her lymphocytes were processed by the RSR team resulting in two different human monoclonal autoantibodies – K1-18™ with thyroid stimulating activity and K1-70™ with blocking activity [34].

K1-70™ is a particularly effective TSH receptor blocking autoantibody (Fig. 2). At the time (2010) we suspected that the differences in these binding arrangements were responsible for the different properties of M22™ (stimulating) and K1-70™ (blocking) but needed to wait until structural studies using cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) were carried out in 2022 to understand the mechanism as discussed later in this review.

Human monoclonal autoantibodies to the TSH receptor

RSR has been able to produce four different high affinity human monoclonal antibodies to the TSH receptor. Two with blocking (TSH antagonist) activity – K1-70™ [34] and 5C9™ [39] and two with thyroid stimulating (agonist) activity – M22™ [21, 22] and K1-18™ [34].

Also, we have been able to prepare complexes of the TSHR with each of these autoantibodies, purify the complexes and analyse them by cryo-EM.

In summary, M22™ and K1-18™ can only bind to the TSHR active state and once bound they hold the receptor in its active state causing prolonged stimulation. 5C9™ can only bind to the inactive state receptor and locks the receptor in its inactive state. This results in inhibition of constitutive activity and the activity of activating mutations. 5C9™ also inhibits binding of TSH, M22™ and patient TRAbs to the TSHR LRD. K1-70™ can bind to the active or inactive states of the TSHR and acts by blocking the binding of thyroid stimulating autoantibodies and TSH to the receptor LRD. It is an exciting new TSHR specific drug [41, 42].

Structural and biochemical studies of the TSHR in complex with monoclonal TSHR autoantibodies show the complexes to be monomers [24, 28, 29, 35], in some cases at concentrations as high as 10 mg/mL. Similar structural studies with the FSH receptor in complex with FSH and the LH receptor in complex with hCG show these complexes to be monomeric also [43]. Formation of a monomeric hormone-receptor complex does appear therefore to be sufficient for receptor activation. In the case of the TSH receptor, comparison of activation by thyroid stimulating autoantibody IgG and monovalent Fab show the monovalent preparation to be at least as active as the divalent IgG [12, 21, 22] consistent with binding to a receptor monomer being sufficient for activation. Other reports, principally based on hypothetical models suggest that the formation of receptor dimers/multimers is important in glycoprotein hormone receptor activation by their cognate ligands [43]. However, the inconsistency between models and actual structural information is yet to be explained satisfactorily.

The ability of our human monoclonal autoantibodies to the TSH receptor to form stable complexes with the TSHR facilitated purification of the complexes and determination of their crystal and cryo-EM structures (see above). However, free TSHR LRD (i.e. not bound to autoantibody) has poor stability and until recently purification attempts had not succeeded beyond those with LATS autoantigen (TSHR A subunit) in 1970 [9].

Purification and crystallisation of some other unstable GPCRs such as the β2-adrenergic receptor has been achieved after systematically mutating every residue and identifying which mutations improved thermostability. Combined mutations improved stability further [44].

We used a similar approach to stabilise the free TSHR LRD (TSHR260) and introduction of six mutations resulted in a preparation approximately 900 times more thermostable than wild type TSHR260 [45]. The six mutations did not affect the binding of human TSHR monoclonal autoantibodies or patient serum TSHR autoantibodies. Also when the six mutations in TSHR260 were incorporated into full length TSHR, stimulation by TSH or human monoclonal autoantibodies M22™ and K1-18™ was not affected [45].

Thermostable TSHR260 (TSHR260 STABL™) was purified to homogeneity without ligand, crystallised and the structure solved at 2.38 Å resolution. The unbound TSHR260 STABL™ structure and the structures of wild type TSHR260 bound to M22™ and K1-70™ are remarkably similar. This suggests that neither the mutations nor the binding of M22™ or K1-70™ change the rigid LRD structure of TSHR260 [45].

Now a pure stable preparation of the TSHR LRD is available which interacts well with TSH receptor autoantibodies. TSHR260 STABL™ can be coated onto plastic surfaces and used to absorb out patient serum TRAb. This provides a useful specificity test for TSHR autoantibody assays and proof of principle for the concept of using TSHR260 STABL™ linked to a solid phase for specific absorption of TRAb circulating in patients by plasmapheresis.

Also, TSHR260 STABL™ has potential for use in targeted in vivo therapies aimed at specifically reducing circulating TRAb. For example TSHR260 STABL™ linked to a molecule which will direct the complex formed with TRAb to a particular degradation pathway [46, 47].

In addition TSHR260 STABL™ and K1-70™ have possibilities in applications with CAR-T cell therapies which target TRAb production [48, 49].

How the lymphocytes which synthesise TSHR autoantibodies escape the multiple checkpoints which usually prevent autoantibody production has been the subject of much speculation. In particular the clonal selection theory of Burnet [50, 51] which proposes somatic mutations in immune-regulatory genes enables autoantibody production.

Very recently Nicola et al. [52], have used whole-exome and targeted NanoSeq, an accurate single-molecule DNA sequencing protocol to search for mutations which drive thyroid autoantibody (autoantibodies to thyroglobulin and to thyroid peroxidase) production in thyroid biopsy material. They found many B cell clones had acquired loss-of-function mutations in the key immune checkpoint genes TNFRSF14 (HVEM) and CD274 (PD-L1) as well as less frequent mutations in other immune checkpoint genes. Antibodies synthesised by a significant number of these B cell clones were shown to be thyroid autoantibodies and the clones showed widespread TNFRSF14 biallelic loss.

These results provide key experimental evidence for somatic mutations in immune-regulatory genes enabling production of thyroglobulin autoantibodies and thyroid peroxidase autoantibodies by thyroid lymphocytes.

It seems likely that a similar process of somatic mutation in immune-regulatory genes is involved in driving production of TSHR autoantibodies.
