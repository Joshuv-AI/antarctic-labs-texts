MUREAPLEXE: A multiplex recombinant antigen-based ELISA expanding the serological detection spectrum of urogenital mycoplasmas
Authors: Sarra Abassi, Safa Boujemaa, Imen Chniba, Sabrina Zidi, Nadine Khadraoui, Behija Mlik, Wassim Y. Almawi, Boutheina Ben Abdelmoumen Mardassi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to improve the serological identification of urogenital mycoplasmas through the development of MUREAPLEXE, a recombinant protein-based multiplex ELISA allowing the simultaneous and specific detection of antibodies against Metamycoplasma hominis, Ureaplasma parvum and Ureaplasma urealyticum.MethodsGenes encoding the major surface antigens Vaa and P120’ of M. hominis, MBA3 of U. parvum and MBA8 of U. urealyticum were cloned into the prokaryotic expression vector pGEX-4T-1 and expressed in E. coli BL21. Recombinant GST-fusion proteins were purified by affinity chromatography using Glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads and were used to optimize an indirect multiplex ELISA. Both assays were subsequently evaluated using 162 patient sera that had been previously characterized by indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) and Western blot analysis.ResultsRelative to IIF, both ELISA assays demonstrated high sensitivity. All tested sera showed reactivity with their corresponding recombinant antigens. Statistical analyses demonstrated a significant correlation between the two ELISA tests. However, MUREAPLEXE demonstrated markedly higher specificity.ConclusionGiven its high sensitivity and specificity for the simultaneous serological identification of M. hominis, U. parvum, and U. urealyticum, MUREAPLEXE may provide a robust and reliable complementary tool for the monitoring of urogenital mycoplasmas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10096-026-05566-6.

Introduction

Often considered mere commensals of the human genitourinary tract, urogenital mycoplasmas are increasingly recognized as opportunistic pathogens due to their evolutionary adaptation within hosts. Several studies have demonstrated their involvement in pathological conditions, including infectious and autoimmune diseases, infertility, and, in some cases, certain cancers [1, 2]. As members of the class Mollicutes, these bacteria exhibit distinctive features that underlie their atypical biology. They display pleomorphic morphology due to the absence of a cell wall, which confers intrinsic resistance to antibiotic classes that target cell wall synthesis, including β-lactams, glycopeptides, and cephalosporins. In addition, their small genome size, reduced genomic content, and antigenic variability further distinguish them from non-Mollicute bacteria. This marked antigenic variability is a key virulence mechanism that facilitates immune evasion [3].

The control of urogenital mycoplasma infections remains an important aspect of reproductive health management, as these pathogens have been associated with adverse clinical outcomes and significant complications. Consequently, the development of diagnostic tools with improved specificity, practicality, and cost-effectiveness remains a priority. For the detection of Metamycoplasma hominis (MH), Ureaplasma parvum (UP) and Ureaplasma urealyticum (UU), which are among the most frequently isolated urogenital mycoplasma species in Tunisia, serological approaches such as indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) are commonly used due to their practicality and the minimally invasive sampling requirements, compared to culture-based methods or PCR.

However, conventional serological tests have important shortcomings, including limited sensitivity and specificity, cross-reactivity with Mollicutes and non-Mollicutes bacteria, and subjective interpretation of the results. While ELISA-based methods generally improved analytical sensitivity and standardization, their application in the serological diagnosis of urogenital mycoplasmas remains limited. Notably, IgG-based serology primarily reflects prior exposure rather than active infection and thus cannot serve as a standalone diagnostic tool for active infection. In addition, early infection may yield false-negative results because of the serological window period. Because MH and Ureaplasma spp. are mucosal pathogens, relevant immune responses may also involve IgA or transient IgM, which are not captured in the present assay. Accordingly, serological methods should be considered primarily as complementary tools for epidemiological investigations, retrospective studies, or use in settings with limited access to molecular diagnostics.

This study describes the development of MUREAPLEXE, a multiplex ELISA test based on species-specific recombinant proteins for the simultaneous serological detection of MH, UP and UU. For this assay, we selected major immunogenic surface proteins specific to these species. For MH, we selected the P120’ surface protein and the variable adherence-associated protein (Vaa), which is one of the major surface proteins of MH. The latter is a monomeric protein, rod-shaped in form, and is known for its variable structure [4]. The P120’ protein is a surface lipoprotein that was shown to be relatively conserved in MH strains. It is derived from the P120 surface protein, which is known to be highly antigenic [5]. For Ureaplasma spp. (U spp), we selected the multiple-banded antigen (MBA), the most important of its major surface proteins. It is a lipid-anchored protein composed from a conserved 5’ N-terminal region and a variable 3’ C-terminal region, consisting of tandem repeats that vary in length among serovars [6, 7]. Accordingly, MUREAPLEXE was developed through recombinant expression of these species-specific surface antigens. P120’ and Vaa for MH and MBA3 and MBA8, corresponding respectively to UP serotype 3 and UU serotype 8.

Materials and methods

Sera collection

A total of 162 serum samples, collected from Tunisian patients of reproductive age between 2019 and 2024, were included in this study. All samples had previously been tested using an in-house IIF assay based on crude total antigens derived from urogenital mycoplasmas (MH, Uspp., Mycoplasmoides genitalium, and Mycoplasmopsis fermentans), as well as Mycoplasmoides pneumoniae, included to assess cross-reactivity (Supplementary information Fig. S1).

The IIF technique showed positivity rates of 10.5%, 27%, and 38% for MH, UP, and UU, respectively. All sera included in this study were negative for M. genitalium, M. fermentans and M. pneumoniae. Western blot analyses were performed to verify the IIF results and to characterize the antigenic recognition profiles of patient sera using total antigens from all tested urogenital mycoplasma species and M. pneumoniae.

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Reference Mycoplasma strains; MH PG21 (ATCC 23114), UU (ATCC 27618) and UP (ATCC 27813) were cultured in SP4 liquid and solid media as previously described [8]. Culture and antigen preparation were performed as previously described [9] and were stored at −20 °C until use. Escherichia coli (E. coli) strains DH5α and BL21 (Promega, USA) were transformed with recombinant plasmids and grown at 37 °C in 2YT media supplemented with 100 µg/ml of ampicillin [10].

Cloning of the genes encoding the major surface proteins of M. hominis, U. parvum, and U. urealyticum

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to amplify the genes encoding major surface proteins of mycoplasma species of interest. Specific primers targeting conserved regions of each surface protein were used (Table 1). The MBA-derived recombinant proteins (MBA3 and MBA8) were designed based on conserved regions of the N-terminal domain, which are shared across multiple serovars. This approach was intentionally chosen to enhance cross-serovar reactivity and minimize bias toward specific serovars. To validate this strategy, sequence alignments were performed against representative serovars of UP (SV1, SV3, SV6, SV14) and UU (SV2, SV4, SV5, SV7–13) using GenBank reference sequences, confirming a high degree of conservation within the selected regions (Supplementary information Fig. S2 and S3).

The same approach was used to design the Vaa and P120’derived recombinant proteins; conserved regions of the N-terminal domain were targeted. Sequence alignments were performed for each protein using available ATCC reference strains retrieved from GenBank (Supplementary information Fig. S4 and S5). The PCR was conducted in a thermocycler 2720 (Applied Biosystem, USA) under the following conditions: 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s (s), annealing at 56 °C for 45 s and elongation at 72 °C for 45 s in a reactional mix containing 2.5 mM of MgCl2 (Invitrogen, USA), 10 mM of dNTP (Thermo scientific, Lithuania), 20 µM each primer (forward and reverse) (Carthagenomics, Tunisia) and 5 U/µl Taq polymerase (Invitrogen, USA).

PCR products were then purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) before digestion with XhoI and BamHI (Thermo Scientific, Lithuania), whose corresponding sites were added at the 5’ end of the forward and reverse primers, respectively. The three purified genes were ligated to the pGEX-4T-1 plasmid (GE Healthcare) and then used to transform E. coli DH5α. The integrity of the recombinant plasmids was verified by PCR amplification and restriction enzyme analysis, and the correct reading frame was preserved by cloning into the pGEX-4T-1 vector; additionally, all PCR products were confirmed by Sanger sequencing to verify sequence accuracy and exclude frameshift mutations prior to protein expression. The recombinant plasmids were introduced into E. coli BL21. All E. coli strains were cultured overnight at 37 °C in 2YT medium supplemented with 100 µg/ml ampicillin to select for recombinant clones.

Recombinant protein expression and purification

Overnight culture of recombinant E. coli BL21 was diluted 1:10 and incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 200 rpm. Once the optical density at 600 nm (A600) reached 0.6–0.8, protein expression was induced with 100 mM isopropyl-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (GE Healthcare, Lithuania), with incubation at 30 °C with agitation for 3 hours (GST-Vaa), 2.5 hours (GST-P120’), or 3.5 h for (GST-MBA3 and GST-MBA8). Cells were pelleted, resuspended in ice-cold PBS, and disrupted by sonication (three cycles of 10 s) using a Branson Sonifier 450 (Fisher Bioblock Scientific, Illkirch, France). Subsequently, lysates were centrifuged at 5000 rpm, and recombinant protein expression was confirmed by 10% SDS-PAGE.

The purification of the GST-tagged recombinant proteins was performed by affinity chromatography using Glutathione-Sepharose 4B beads from the GST Bulk Kit (GE Healthcare, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein purity and quality were confirmed by SDS-PAGE, while specificity and immunogenicity were assessed by Western blot, using rabbit-derived specific antisera against target proteins (anti-GST-MBA3, anti-GST-MBA8, anti-GST-Vaa and anti-GST-P120’).

Production of polyclonal antisera to GST-Vaa, GST-P120’, GST-MBA3 and GST-MBA8 proteins and M. hominis, U. parvum, and U. urealyticum crude antigens

The production of rabbit polyclonal antibodies against the GST-fusion recombinant proteins and the total antigens of MH, UU, and UP was performed as detailed elsewhere [10]. Briefly, New Zealand White rabbits were subcutaneously injected with 250 µg of each purified GST-tagged recombinant protein, notably, GST-Vaa, GST-P120’, GST-Mba3 and GST-Mba8. For the first administration, recombinant proteins were mixed with Freund’s complete adjuvant; for the additional 4 booster injections, the incomplete formulation was used. Booster injections were administered at 15-day intervals. The production of the hyperimmune sera against total antigens of each species, MH, UP and UU was conducted according to the protocol outlined by our group [9]. All antibody titers were determined by immunoblotting. The antisera samples were stored at −20 °C until further use. All rabbit immunization procedures and collection of sera were conducted in accordance with the protocol of the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research/Food and Drug Administration Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The ethics statement number for this study is “2015/16/1/LR11IPT01/V0”.

Development of a crude antigen-based ELISA

Before the establishment of MUREAPLEXE, we initially developed an indirect ELISA based on the interaction between the crude antigens of MH, UP, and UU and patient sera. This test was mainly optimized for comparative purposes and to assess the reactivity of Tunisian patients’ antisera against the total proteins of the studied mycoplasma species.

ELISA optimization was performed using a checkerboard titration approach to systematically define the optimal assay conditions [10]. Serial antigen concentrations, ranging from 1000 to 7.8 ng/100 µL, were coated onto 96-well microplates (Sarstedt, USA) and incubated overnight at 4°C. Optimal antigen concentrations for MH (7.8 ng/100µL), UP (20 ng/100µL), and UU (15 ng/100µL) were determined using specific rabbit polyclonal antisera at 1:2000 dilution. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked following washing with a casein-based buffer, and different human sera dilutions (1:200, 1:400, 1:600, and 1:800) were evaluated for the optimal working dilution, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated rat anti-human IgG (Abcam, UK), tested at 1:10,000, 1:15,000, and 1:20,000 dilutions. Each incubation step was optimized for duration and temperature. Signal detection was performed using 100 µl of the 3,3’,5,5’ tetramethyl-benzidine (TMB) (Abcam, UK) substrate, and the reaction was terminated with 100 µl of 7% sulfuric acid prior to optical density (OD450) measurement at 450 nm using MULTISKAN GO microplate reader (ThermoScientific).

The assay performance was assessed using the index of positivity (IP), as previously described by Yacoub et al. [11]. Based on IP values, samples were classified as negative (IP < 0.7), positive (IP > 0.7), or borderline (IP = 0.7).

Development of the multiplex recombinant proteins-based ELISA: MUREAPLEXE

The same optimization strategy was applied for the development of MUREAPLEXE multiplex recombinant protein-based ELISA. Checkerboard titration allowed the determination of the optimal concentrations of GST-Vaa, GST-P120’, GST-MBA3, and GST-MBA8 recombinant antigens, as well as the appropriate serum and conjugate dilutions and incubation conditions, ensuring optimal assay sensitivity and specificity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to assess the correlation between MUREAPLEXE and the crude antigen-based ELISA. Pearson correlation analysis was used to calculate correlation coefficients and to generate regression models. Bland–Altman analysis was performed to assess agreement between the two assays. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using paired t-tests and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, from which sensitivity and specificity were estimated. Graphical outputs and statistical analyses were generated in RStudio (version 30.4.0).

Results

Expression and purification of the recombinant proteins

Recombinant clones were confirmed by PCR amplification of extracted plasmid DNA using gene-specific primers (Supplementary information Fig. S6). Following a series of optimization experiments, the optimal expression conditions for each recombinant fusion protein were established (Table 2). Subsequently, affinity purification with Glutathione-Sepharose beads enabled efficient separation of GST-tagged proteins from other E. coli proteins. Purity was evaluated with Coomassie blue-stained SDS-PAGE, which showed distinct protein bands at the expected molecular weights of 71 kDa, 63 kDa, 61 kDa, and 60 kDa (Fig. 1).

Comparison of MUREAPLEXE with crude antigen-based ELISA

A total of 162 sera were analyzed in parallel using the crude antigen-based ELISA and the MUREAPLEXE assay. The seropositivity rates obtained with MUREAPLEXE for Vaa (51%), P120’ (52%), MBA3 (67%), and MBA8 (52%) were comparable to those observed with the crude antigen-based ELISA for MH (50%), UP (65%), and UU (50%) (Fig. 5). This demonstrates comparable detection performance between the two methods despite differences in antigenic composition. Although these findings differed substantially from those obtained using the IIF technique, they showed good agreement with Western blot results. According to these findings, both ELISA assays suggested a high rate of serological co-reactivity (43%), that was not detected by IIF.

GST-tagged recombinant antigens largely reproduced the seroreactivity of corresponding crude antigen ELISAs of the 162 patient sera, as illustrated in the OD distributions scatter plot (Fig. 6). GST-Vaa and GST-P120′ aligned with MH crude antigen (Fig. 6a), while GST-MBA3 and GST-MBA8 reflected UP and UU crude antigen responses, respectively (Fig. 6b and c). Comparative analysis of OD values showed statistically significant moderate-to-strong correlations (p < 0.0001), with coefficients ranging from 0.66 to 0.93 (Table 3; Fig. 7). Pearson and Bland–Altman analyses demonstrated positive linear relationships across all comparisons, with most values within the limits of agreement. For MH, GST-Vaa showed a positive bias toward higher crude antigen OD (Fig. 7a), whereas GST-P120′ showed smaller differences (Fig. 7b). GST-MBA3 and GST-MBA8 correlated well with UP and UU, showing minimal bias (Fig. 7c) and slight positive bias (Fig. 7d), respectively.

According to the areas under the curve (AUCs), both assays demonstrated strong discrimination between seropositive and seronegative samples, with AUC values ranging from 0.87 to 0.97. Comparative differences between the methods were observed when comparing the recombinant proteins and the total antigens of urogenital mycoplasmas. Discussion

Accumulating evidence implicates urogenital mycoplasmas, particularly MH, UP, and UU, in the pathogenesis of a range of conditions, including gynecological infections, infertility [12, 13], malignancy [1, 2], and extra-urogenital disease [14, 15]. Despite advances in diagnostic microbiology, detecting these organisms remains challenging. Molecular methods have improved sensitivity and specificity but are often inaccessible in low-resource settings because of cost and infrastructure requirements. As a result, conventional approaches such as culture and indirect immunofluorescence remain widely used, despite being time-consuming and less sensitive, underscoring the need for more accessible and reliable diagnostic alternatives.

While practical and cost-effective, serological methods are limited by cross-reactivity among mycoplasma species and with other bacteria, which reduces their specificity. Antigenic variability and immune evasion further compromise their reliability [16, 17], and no current assay achieves both high sensitivity and specificity for urogenital mycoplasmas. To address these limitations, we developed MUREAPLEXE, a recombinant antigen-based multiplex ELISA for the simultaneous detection of antibodies against the most frequently isolated urogenital mycoplasmas in Tunisian patients, namely MH, UP, and UU, providing a more defined and potentially reliable diagnostic alternative.

Immunodominant surface proteins from MH, UP, and UU were selected for inclusion in MUREAPLEXE based on strong immunoreactivity in Tunisian patient sera, as determined by Western blot analysis. Most sera reacted with MH Vaa and P120′, as well as Ureaplasma MBA3 and MBA8. The high prevalence of anti-Vaa antibodies supports its marked immunogenicity, consistent with previous reports [18, 19]. P120′ was selected over full-length P120 because of reduced genetic variability and strong seroreactivity, in contrast to earlier findings suggesting higher immunogenicity for P120 [5]. For Ureaplasma species, MBA is recognized as a dominant antigenic target [20, 21]. To limit the effect of antigenic variability, conserved regions of MBA3 and MBA8 were used, supporting reliable antibody detection across diverse serovars.

The use of species-specific recombinant antigens in ELISA represents an effective strategy to improve assay specificity, as demonstrated for other Mycoplasma species [10]. A key limitation of recombinant antigen-based serology is the potential for reduced sensitivity due to antigenic variation among circulating strains. Structural changes in exposed epitopes may lead to antibody responses that do not fully recognize reference-derived recombinant proteins. To minimize this limitation, conserved immunoreactive domains encoding species-specific epitopes were selected for recombinant expression, thereby enhancing the likelihood of broad serological reactivity. In addition, GST-fused recombinant proteins were used without cleavage, as their neutrality has been previously demonstrated and was further supported by the present findings [10], simplifying antigen production while maintaining assay performance.

Including multiple recombinant antigens specific to MH and U spp. in the same diagnostic test increases its sensitivity and, ultimately, its reliability. Of the 162 sera tested, 67% were positive for MBA3 and 52% for MBA8, hence positive for UP and UU, respectively. Moreover, positivity rates of 54% for Vaa and 52% for P120’ were observed with both specific surface proteins of MH. These results indicate that serum reactivity varies slightly across antigens, as the Vaa protein detects more positive sera than the P120’ protein. The complementary reactivity patterns observed between Vaa and P120’ underscore the importance of using multiple antigens per species to circumvent phase variation and antigenic heterogeneity, thereby reducing false negatives.

A crude antigen–based ELISA was developed as a benchmark for evaluating MUREAPLEXE performance, demonstrating a significant correlation between the two assays. Paired t-test analysis indicated that Vaa and MBA8 had higher sensitivity for detecting MH and UU infections, respectively, likely due to the use of conserved, species-specific domains that reduce antigenic variability compared with crude preparations. Comparison with indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) revealed significant differences, consistent with studies reporting higher ELISA sensitivity in similar diagnostic contexts [22, 23]. In this study, these differences may reflect undetected coinfections and the lower specificity inherent in IIF. Comparable findings have been reported for M. pneumoniae, where ELISA demonstrated higher positivity rates than IIF [24], further supporting its utility.

A high rate of coinfection between MH and Ureaplasma spp. (43%) was observed, consistent with previous reports documenting frequent co-detection of these organisms in reproductive tract infections [25–27]. Odendaal et al. [25]. reported that 96% of MH-positive patients were also positive for Ureaplasma spp., while Park et al. [26]. observed coinfection in all examined cases. Additionally, MH is often detected alongside UU, and such coinfections have been associated with more severe pregnancy outcomes than single-species infections [27]. However, because serological reactivity reflects prior exposure rather than confirmed active infection, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and do not constitute definitive evidence of true coinfection.

In this context, the interpretation of coinfection patterns must be considered alongside the methodological constraints of the study. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this retrospective study relied on archived sera primarily characterized by IIF rather than prospectively collected, culture- or PCR-confirmed cases, limiting robust estimates of diagnostic accuracy and potentially introducing selection bias. Although IIF is widely used in low-resource settings, its lower sensitivity may partly explain the higher seropositivity observed with ELISA. Moreover, as both IIF and MUREAPLEXE are IgG-based assays, they reflect prior exposure rather than active infection and do not capture mucosal IgA or early IgM responses. The absence of validation using monoclonal antibodies targeting conserved epitopes also represents a technical limitation. In addition, the study population was restricted to Tunisia, which may limit generalizability given the known antigenic diversity of urogenital mycoplasmas. The modest sample size further constrains interpretation. Future studies should include larger, geographically diverse cohorts and microbiologically confirmed cases.

Despite these limitations, this study has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, MUREAPLEXE is the first multiplex recombinant antigen–based assay to simultaneously detect antibodies against MH, UP, and UU, addressing an important diagnostic gap. The inclusion of multiple conserved immunodominant domains per species (Vaa and P120′ for MH; MBA3 and MBA8 for U spp.) was intended to limit the impact of antigenic variability and improve analytical specificity compared with crude antigen–based assays. In addition, the recombinant format reduces cross-reactivity, enhancing assay precision. Finally, the platform emphasizes technical simplicity and cost-effectiveness, supporting its potential implementation in resource-constrained laboratory settings and broader applicability in epidemiological and clinical investigations.

Conclusion

MUREAPLEXE is a recombinant antigen–based multiplex serological assay that enables simultaneous detection of antibodies against MH, UP, and UU. The assay demonstrated promising analytical performance compared with conventional serological methods, with improved specificity and broader antigenic coverage, thereby supporting reliable species-level serological profiling. Its multiplex design and use of conserved immunodominant domains enhance antigenic discrimination and support broader applicability in epidemiological investigations. While these findings confirm the feasibility of this approach, further optimization and prospective validation using microbiologically confirmed clinical samples are required before routine clinical implementation. Given its technical simplicity and cost-effectiveness, MUREAPLEXE may serve as a valuable complementary tool for sero-epidemiological studies and diagnostic support, particularly in settings where molecular techniques remain limited.
