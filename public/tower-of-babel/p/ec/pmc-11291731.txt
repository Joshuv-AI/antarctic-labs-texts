Integrated photon pairs source in silicon carbide based on micro-ring resonators for quantum storage at telecom wavelengths
Authors: P M C Tavares Ramírez, J S S Durán Gómez, G J Rodríguez Becerra, R Ramírez-Alarcón, M Gómez Robles, R Salas-Montiel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the design of an on-chip integrated photon pair source based on Spontaneous Four Wave Mixing (SFWM), implemented on a ring resonator in the 4H Silicon Carbide On Insulator (4H-SiCOI) platform, compatible with a solid state quantum memory in the telecommunications band. Through careful engineering of the waveguide dispersion and micro-ring resonator dimensions, we found solutions where the signal photons are emitted at 1536.48 nm with a bandwidth of ∼150 MHz, enabling the interaction with the hyperfine structure of Er3+ ions. Simultaneously, the idler photons are generated at 1563.86 nm, matching the central wavelength of a specific channel in a commercial dense wavelength division multiplexing system. The proposed device fulfill all the spectral requirements in a simple ring-bus coupled waveguide configuration with design parameters within the range of reported values for similar resonators, making feasible its manufacturing with current fabrication capabilities.Subject terms: Other photonics, Quantum information

Introduction

Integrated photonics is considered a platform that will play a central role in the development of quantum technologies, due to the interesting features of photons as information carriers, including high-speed and low-noise transmission1. In the context of quantum communications, photons serve as optimal carriers for encoding quantum states and transmitting them through existing telecom optical networks, but the necessity for quantum repeaters arises due to optical losses affecting the transmission of information when is required to propagate over extended distances. Quantum memories, which are systems designed to store and retrieve quantum information using single-photon quantum states on demand, emerge as promising solutions for the role of quantum repeaters. These systems have been demonstrated using atomic species from rare ions such as rubidium, cesium, europium, praseodymium and erbium2–5.

The main challenge in designing a photon source that emits photons capable to interact with a quantum memory is tailoring the emission wavelength and bandwidth of the generated photons to match the spectral properties of the ions species used to store the quantum state6. In current implementations of quantum memories, the atomic species are typically excited using heralded photons created by Spontaneous Parametric Down Conversion (SPDC) in bulk crystals (BBO, PPLN, PPKTP, etc.). These implementations achieve the required spectral bandwidth through a free-space cavity that enhances photon pair generation and decrease the bandwidth7. However, these approaches result impractical for scalable applications. A promising alternative is integrated quantum photonics, an emerging and on development technology for which many works have reported narrow bandwidth photon pair generation through the use of integrated cavities on chips, based on SPDC and SFWM processes, in a variety of materials such as silicon dioxide8,9, silicon nitride10–12, aluminum gallium arsenide13,14, etc.

Silicon carbide (SiC), particularly 4H-SiCOI, emerge as a suitable material for the development of photonic integrated circuits (PICs) due to its large transparency window and high refractive index (n=2.6 at telecom wavelengths)15, which allows high integration of photonic devices in a single chip, compatibility with current CMOS technology, high Kerr nonlinearity (n2 = 1.3×10-18m2W-1) and wide bandgap (i.e., 3.2 eV)16,17. An additional advantage of SiC in comparison with Silicon is that it does not exhibit two photon absorption and free carrier absorption, two phenomena that affects SFWM process decreasing the photon pair generation rate18,19. In this direction, recently it has been demonstrated the implementation of micro-ring resonators in 4H-SiC with quality factors exceeding 6 million used for non linear applications15 and the first implementation of SFWM photon pair source based in ring resonators in the 4H-SiCOI platform, generating photons in the C-band20.

Furthermore, in a recent work, an integrated photon pairs source operating at telecom wavelengths was reported in the SiN platform, designed for interacting with an erbium ion-based quantum memory21. The main advamge and diference with the reported SiN device is that 4H-SiC present a larger n2 coefficient (1.3×10-18 m2/W) compared to SiN (2.5×10-19 m2/W)16,17. This coefficient is quadratically related to the photon emission rate which improves the photon pair generation22. In addition to the higher Kerr coefficient, SiC possesses a higher linear refractive index (n=2.6 compared to n=2 for SiN at 1550 nm)17, which enables the capacity to implement waveguides with smaller cross sections.

In this work we propose the design of an integrated photon pairs source based on enhanced SFWM in a simple configuration that consist on a single bus ring resonator in the 4H-SiCOI platform. The proposed source is optimized to generate photon pairs with spectral properties tailored to interact with a solid state quantum memory implemented in an Yttrium orthosilicate crystal doped with Erbium ions (Er3+:Y2SiO5). The device is capable to generate photon pairs where the signal photons match the 4I15/2→4I13/2 atomic transition of Erbium ions, centered at 1536.48 nm23 with a bandwidth of 150 MHz24,25, while the idler photons match the central wavelength of a specific channel in a commercial dense wavelength division multiplexing (DWDM) system, allowing its distribution over existing optical fiber telecommunication networks.

The wavelengths of the signal and idler photons were set through the optimization of the transverse dimensions (width and height) of the waveguide, while the bandwidth of the signal photons is constrained to match the level separation of the targeted Erbium ion transition, establishing a minimum Q-factor required for the cavity to achieve it. The design parameters that we obtained are in the order of recently reported values for similar devices, making feasible the manufacturing of the proposed photon pairs source with current fabrication technology.

Materials and methods: optimizing geometrical parameters in engineering design

As it has been mentioned before, we aim to design a photon pairs source which generates heralded signal photon capable to interact with the Erbium atomic transition 4I15/2→4I13/230, schematically shown in Fig. 1a. The proposed device consists on a micro-ring resonator with a single bus coupled waveguide designed to generate photons through the SFWM process, as shown in Fig. 1b. This configuration allows to match the photon spectral properties, such as wavelength and narrow bandwidth, while simultaneously enhance the generation rate of the photon pairs through the resonant characteristics of the cavity. To find the optimal parameters, we needed to engineer the dispersion through the geometry of the waveguide, schematically shown in Fig. 1c. The substrate considered consists of a silicon wafer with a thermally grown 2-μm-thick silicon dioxide (SiO2) layer, and on top a silicon carbide layer. The cross section of the bus waveguide and the ring resonator have the same dimensions. We fixed the height of the SiC layer, which constitute the core of the waveguide, to 400 nm, a thickness accessible at our facilities leaving the width as a degree of freedom for the design and the fabrication process.

After conducting simulations, we narrowed down the search space to 506–536 nm. This adjustment was made after observing that beyond these boundaries, meeting all three conditions simultaneously became unattainable, mainly due to the significant separation between the zero dispersion point and the pump wavelength. After identifying a geometry suitable for the three wavelengths, the next optimization step consists in systematically varying the ring radii within a specified range of interest to find a value that ensures all three wavelengths are resonant within the cavity. For a given radius, we validate this condition by computing the closest resonant frequencies (ω¯s,ω¯i,ω¯p) to the target frequencies (ωs,ωi,ωp). We then calculate the individual absolute differences and check if they meet a threshold condition (|ω¯μ-ωμ|<2π(200MHz)) for the signal, pump, and idler frequencies simultaneously, which ensures that an optimal candidate radius has been obtained.

An alternative criterion for determining an optimal radius is based on evaluating the integrated dispersion, a parameter that quantifies the resonance shift caused by dispersion from an ideal scenario where the free spectral range remains constant between resonances. This parameter is defined by Refs.37,38:

where ω¯η represents the ηth resonance with respect to the central pump resonance labeled as 0 (i.e., η=mω¯η-mω¯0), and D1/2π=c(Lng)-1 is the free spectral range with ng being the group index. The procedure involves varying the radius within a specified range and searching for triads of resonances close to the desired frequencies (ωp≈ω¯0,ωs≈ω¯η,ωi≈ω¯-η), then the optimal radius is that for which the sum Dint(ω¯η)+Dint(ω¯-η)≈0 is minimized. This equality ensures the conservation of azimuthal modes (2mp-ms-mi=0), which can be proven to be an alternative way to express the phase matching condition in the context of SFWM within a cavity10. With this methodology, we can optimize the resonant frequencies and the JSI overlap with the cavity functions simultaneously.

Once we determined the optimal waveguide width, we conducted a refined sweep within the range 100–200 μm to search for optimal radii, this allows us to identify multiple radii suitable for the spontaneous four wave mixing process. We identified a triad of wavelengths λs, λi, λp, such that three of the ring resonances closely matched the three wavelengths of our interest, as verified by imposing a condition where the separation between each wavelength and its corresponding resonance remained below a specified threshold. We chose the radius 136.72 μm, 145.82 μm and 154.91 μm to compare the integrated dispersion and frequency mismatching, where these ring radii meet the threshold for all three wavelengths. Moreover, following this procedure we assessed that R=145.82 μm is the optimal radius as the one that yields the lowest frequency mismatch. Photon pairs spectral properties and emission rate calculation

The subsequent step in the design process consists in determining the minimum Q-factor required for the cavity to achieve the target bandwidth of the signal photons. This bandwidth is mainly determined through the linewidth of the pump laser and the Q-factor of the cavity. The resonances have a bandwidth that can be estimated from Δν=ν/Q but it can be slightly reduced due to the linewidth of the laser. To estimate the final bandwidth of the source, the function |Gcav(νs,νi)|2 is normalized and integrated with respect to the idler frequency then, the bandwidth of the signal photon is estimated as the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of the marginal JSI function.

In Fig. 4 are illustrated the spectral properties of the proposed photon source. The pump laser described with a a gaussian profile, centered at λp=1550.05 nm was simulated with a narrow linewidth of δνp = 200 kHz (according to datasheet35). In Fig. 4a we show the |G(νs,νi)|2 (JSI with cavity absent), while Fig. 4b presents the resonator Airy function in the (νs,νi)- space, centered in the signal and idler generation frequencies. In Fig. 4c we show the cavity affected JSI function |Gcav(νs,νi)|2 which we use to determine the final spectral properties of our source. It is important to mention that the optimization of the waveguide’s cross section and the radius of the device is key to obtain an optimized overlap between the JSI function (|G(νs,νi)|2) and the cavity’s Airy functions (Aμ(ωμ)2). If these functions are not optimally overlapped the main effect will be noticed as a an increment in the bandwidth and a reduction of the photon flux of the source. In Fig. 4d we show the marginal JSI obtained by integrating the function |Gcav(νs,νi)|2 over the idler frequencies and normalizing. The Q-factor of the resonator was varied until achieving a signal photon bandwidth of Δνs = 149.88 MHz, which resulted for a Q-factor of 4.94×105. The resulting required Q-factor is one order of magnitude lower than the maximum Q-factor reported for this kind of ring resonators (Qmax = 6.75×106 )39, making feasible the manufacturing of the proposed device. The same procedure described before was applied to estimate the bandwidth of the idler photon giving a bandwidth of Δνi = 149.81 MHz. If we consider the maximun Q-factor reported of Qmax=6.75×106, the resulting bandwidth for the signal photon is Δνs,max= 28.44 MHz39, making this platform an attractive candidate for the development of narrowband photon sources, required for quantum repeaters based on solid state quantum memories.

Finally, we present the outcomes of our source for the photon flux. We obtained an effective area, Aeff, of 0.31μm2 and calculated a nonlinear coefficient γ=16.9m-1W-1. Using Eq. (10) we computed the flux of photon pairs for P0=1mW and Q=4.94×105, yielding NCW=6.803×105 photon pairs per second. This corresponds to an enhancement factor of E=4.70×103 compared to an integrated straight source of the same length as the cavity, which shows the improvement achieved by the use of a resonant cavity as integrated photon source. Additionally, we calculated the flux for the maximum reported Q-factor in the SiC platform39, resulting in NCW=9.326×106 photon pairs per second. This makes our SiC-based approach an attractive alternative for the implementation of integrated devices for quantum memories applications.

Conclusion

In this work, we proposed the design of an integrated photon pair source based on cavity enhanced SFWM on a 4H-SiC micro-ring resonator in a single bus configuration. The signal photons have the spectral properties required to interact with a quantum memory implemented on a Y2SiO5 crystal doped with Er3+ ions, while the correlated idler photon matches the central wavelength of the DWDM-C17 channel, in order to be distributed over existing optical fiber networks. To design the device we performed an exhaustive optimization process for the engineering of the geometrical parameters of the photon pairs source, in order to satisfy waveguiding and phase matching for the three involved wavelengths, as well as to find resonant conditions for the required photon wavelengths (Supplementary information).

The optimal solution for the geometrical parameters of the source are h=400 nm and w=516 nm for the cross section of the required waveguide and R=145.82 μm for the radius of the resonator. By pumping with a wavelength of λp=1550.05 nm, accessible with a commercially available CW laser, the proposed device produces photon pairs emitting the signal photon with a central wavelength of λs=1536.48 nm and the idler photon with a central wavelength of λi=1563.86 nm. Then, by analysing the JSI function of the source, considering the effect of the resonator over the two-photon state, we obtained a minimal Q-factor of 4.94×105 required to reduce the bandwidth of the signal photons to Δνs=149.88 MHz, matching the ≈150 MHz spectral bandwidth of the 4I15/2→4I13/2 atomic transition of Er3+ ions. The required Q-factor is one order of magnitude smaller than the maximal reported Q-factor (6.75×106) for similar devices39, making feasible the fabrication of the proposed micro-ring resonator with current manufacturing capabilities. Furthermore, we estimated that the bandwidth of the signal photons can be reduced to Δνs,max=28.44 MHz, for the maximum Q factor reported for this material. Additionally, we computed an optimal gap value of 500 nm between the bus waveguide and the ring resonator for the three resonances considered, in order to reach the Q-factor required (4.94×105). Lastly, we estimated the photon pairs emission rate of our source obtaining NCW=6.803×105pairs/(smW2).

In resume, we present the overall design of an optimized integrated photon pairs source compatible with a solid state quantum memory in the C-band. We define the optimal design parameters in order to implement fully integrable and scalable photon pairs sources, based on micro-ring resonators in the 4H-SiC platform, compatible with Er3+ ions-based quantum memories, overcoming the disadvantages of bulky free space optical setups. We believe that our work can serve as a valuable guide in the design of tailored integrated photon sources and also, our findings underscore silicon carbide as a promising platform with significant potential applications in the development of integrated devices for quantum networks.
