Association Between Age at Kidney Transplantation and Risk of Early Graft Loss
Authors: Karina Alas Marroquin, Krystle Frazier, Charles E. McCulloch, Barbara Grimes, Elaine Ku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAdolescence has been identified as a high‐risk period for graft failure. However, existing knowledge on this topic is largely limited to long‐term graft outcomes. The aim of this study was to examine the association between age at kidney transplantation and early graft loss, defined as graft failure within 3 years post‐transplantation in the contemporary era.MethodsThis study was a retrospective analysis of 55 973 first‐time kidney transplant recipients identified in the U.S. Renal Data System from January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2021. Adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the association between age at transplantation and early graft failure including an interaction term for race/ethnicity.ResultsAdolescents (12 to < 18 years) and young adults (18 to < 26 years) were at the highest risk of early death‐censored graft loss compared to young adults (26 to < 40 years), with hazard ratios of 1.95 (95% CI 1.74–2.17) and 2.01 (95% CI 1.86–2.17), respectively. Additionally, we found that the effect of age on early graft loss varied significantly by race, with disparities observed among Asian and Black youth.ConclusionThese findings highlight a persistently elevated risk in adolescents and young adults in the contemporary era.

Background

Transplantation is the preferred form of kidney replacement therapy due to the benefits of improved survival, quality of life, growth, and development [1, 2]. Adolescence has been identified as a particularly vulnerable period for graft failure due to the high prevalence of non‐adherence to immunosuppression [3, 4]. Adolescents (11–17 years) receive the highest number of kidney transplants among pediatric patients, accounting for 60% of all pediatric kidney transplants and 3% of all kidney transplants, while young adults (18–34 years) account for approximately 16% of all kidney transplants [5]. Understanding the contemporary association between age at transplant and early graft loss is important given the volume of organs allocated to this population, especially since kidney transplantation remains a life‐saving and essential therapy for pediatric patients.

Prior studies have examined the association between age and risk of graft failure and confirmed that adolescence and young adulthood are high‐risk periods for overall graft failure, but the majority of these studies do not distinguish between earlier and later graft failure events [6, 7, 8, 9]. One prior study found that age, race, and insurance were important modifiers of the relationship between age and graft outcomes [8]. More recently, studies have confirmed the association between age at transplantation and risk of subsequent graft loss among adolescents who received a second or third transplant [10]. In particular, early graft loss up to the first 3 years following kidney transplantation is a metric that was closely tracked by transplant centers for quality assurance purposes and is of interest to the stakeholder community [11, 12]. The aim of this study was to examine the association between age at transplantation and early graft loss (defined as graft loss within 3 years of transplant) and whether there is an effect modification of any such relations by race/ethnicity, which has not been examined in the contemporary era [8].

Methods

This study was deemed exempt from human subject research by the Institutional Review Board of the University of California San Francisco.

Study Design and Population

This is a retrospective cohort study using the United States Renal Data System (USRDS) database, the national end‐stage kidney disease registry. We included kidney transplant recipients of all ages who received their first allograft between January 1, 2005, and December 31, 2021. Transplant dates were ascertained based on data in the USRDS Transplant file. Recipients were excluded if they did not have a Centers for Medicare and Medicaid 2728 Medical Evidence (MEDEVID) form, if covariates were missing or inconsistent, and if the recipients resided in a US territory. A total of 55 973 patients were included in the main analytical cohort (see Figure S1).

Variables

The primary exposure was age at the first transplant, which was categorized into seven groups (0 to < 5, 5 to < 12, 12 to < 18, 18 to < 26, 26–40 [reference group]). These age groups were constructed to capture points of transition from pediatric to adult healthcare systems and potential points of insurance transitions (at age 18 and 26 years in the US). The primary outcome was graft failure within 3 years of transplantation, and the secondary outcome was death within 3 years of transplantation. Graft failure and death dates were obtained from the USRDS Patients file.

Covariates considered in adjusted models included those known to associate with variations in age at transplant and the risk of graft failure such as sex, race/ethnicity (non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, Asian, Other), the primary cause of end‐stage kidney disease (ESKD), median neighborhood income (by patients' zip codes), and U.S. Census region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West) ascertained using the Patients file and the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid 2728 medical evidence (MEDEVID) form. The MEDEVID form is submitted by the provider and signed by the patient at the onset of ESKD (transplant or dialysis) and includes demographics, comorbidities as well as laboratory data at time of ESKD onset for all patients in the US. Comorbidities, including diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease and myocardial infarction, peripheral vessel disease, heart failure, stroke, malignancy, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, alcohol, drug, and tobacco use, were ascertained based on all MEDEVID forms signed within 45 days of transplantation. Insurance type and body mass index were based on the most recent MEDEVID form. Calculated panel of reactive antigens (cPRA) was determined from the Waitlist and Transplant files. We also included donor age and dialysis vintage prior to transplantation in all adjusted models.

We estimated donor graft quality using the Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI), categorizing this into five groups: 0 to < 20, 20 to < 40, 40 to < 60, 60 to 100, and used a separate category for all living donors who served as the reference group. KDPI values were calculated using data ascertained from the USRDS Donor file.

Statistical Analysis

Our main analysis used Cox proportional hazards regression models for time to graft failure, adjusted for the covariates described above to relate the primary exposure (categorical age at transplant) to graft failure. Follow‐up was censored at 3 years (so we could characterize it as early graft loss) or at the date of administrative censoring on December 31, 2021. Analyses were conducted separately for death‐censored graft failure and all‐cause graft failure. Models were repeated for the outcome of death, again censoring at 3 years or December 31, 2021.

In sensitivity analysis, Fine‐Gray models were used in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses treating death as a competing risk and accounting for the same covariates as described above.

We also tested for an interaction between age and race or ethnicity and reported on clinically and statistically significant interactions (p < 0.05) in adjusted models. Due to the detection of an interaction, we then performed stratified analysis by age category and race/ethnicity and also included analyses including an interaction term between age category and race/ethnicity in adjusted models for clinically and statistically significant comparisons across age groups.

All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1 and Table S1. A total of 55 973 patients were included in the primary analysis. The median age at first transplant was 30 years, with the largest proportion of transplants occurring in the 26 to < 40‐year‐old recipients (65.8%). White recipients represented 42.3% of the cohort, followed by Black (26.2%), Hispanic (23.4%), and Asian (5.8%) recipients. Most kidney transplant recipients were insured through private coverage (55.7%), while 31.3% of the cohort had public insurance (Table 1).

Age at Transplantation and the Risk of Graft Failure Within 3 Years of Transplantation

During a median follow‐up of 3 years (Q1–Q3, 2.4–3.0), 4559 graft failures occurred within 3 years of transplantation (8.14%), and 755 deaths with a functional graft occurred (1.35%). The rates of graft failure and death are shown in Table S3. In an adjusted analysis, the highest risk of early death‐censored graft loss was observed in adolescents (12 to < 18 years) and young adults (18 to < 26 years), with hazard ratios (HR) of 1.95 (95% CI 1.75–2.17) and 2.01 (95% CI 1.86–2.17), respectively, compared to young adults 26–40 years (reference group, Table 2). A similar trend of increased risk was also observed for the outcome of all‐cause early graft failure, with adolescents 12 to < 18 years (HR 1.69, 95% CI 1.52–1.88) and young adult recipients 18 to < 26 years (HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.66–1.92) having the highest risk compared to the reference group (Table S2). The risk of early graft failure for these two age groups remained roughly similar when death was treated as a competing risk in sensitivity analysis (sub‐HR 1.95, 95% CI 1.74–2.17 and sub‐HR 2.01, 95% CI 1.86–2.17, respectively) (Table 2).

Interaction Between Age and Race/Ethnicity in Early Graft Loss

An interaction between age category and race/ethnicity in relation to early graft failure was observed in the adjusted models (p interaction = 0.001). Given the presence of an interaction, subgroup analyses relating age to early graft failure stratified by race/ethnicity are shown in Figure 1 and Table S4. The main driver of this interaction was due to interactions between age and Asian ethnicity in children 0 to < 5 years old along with differences in the effect size for Black young adults (Tables S5 and S6). Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between age at first kidney transplant and early graft loss within 3 years of transplantation using the USRDS data. We found that in the contemporary era, adolescents (12 to < 18 years) and young adults (18 to < 26 years) remained at the highest risk for early graft loss compared to those 26 to < 40 years of age. This trend persisted even after accounting for death as a competing risk, though deaths were uncommon in younger patients. We also found differences in trends by race and ethnicity of the patient, with Asian children 0 to < 5 years, Asian adolescents 12 to < 18 years and Black young adults generally having a higher risk of early graft failure compared with their White counterparts. Our study provides an update on rates of early graft loss and identifies the vulnerable, younger populations who continue to warrant close monitoring post‐transplantation.

Our findings are consistent with prior literature. Andreoni et al. studied a nationally representative cohort of first‐time kidney transplant recipients aged 55 years or younger and identified adolescents aged 14 to 16 at the time of transplant as the highest‐risk group for both short‐ and long‐term graft loss [8]. It is worth noting that these results included data only up to 2010. Similarly, Smith et al. found that adolescents aged 13–17 at the time of transplant experienced the poorest overall graft survival in a multinational cohort [6]. Keith et al. identified 10–19 year‐old patients as having the lowest long‐term graft survival [7]. Our study confirms previous findings relating age at transplantation to graft failure and adds to this body of evidence by providing a more contemporary perspective that is focused on early graft loss within the first 3 years of transplantation, which has not been examined in the last 15 years.

An important finding of this study was the association between age at transplant, race and ethnicity, and early graft loss. One of the only prior studies to directly address racial differences in graft loss rates was conducted by Andreoni et al., which reported that Black adolescents experienced significantly worse outcomes than non‐Black recipients across all donor groups [8]. In our study, when compared to adult White recipients ages 26 to < 40, the risk of early graft failure for Black young adult recipients (ages 18 to < 26) was substantially higher. We also newly identified that Asian children 0 to < 5 years and adolescents of Asian ethnicity had a higher relative risk of graft failure compared with White recipients. The reasons for these findings warrant further investigation, though our findings by race and ethnicity should be interpreted cautiously. The observed differences likely reflect the influence of structural, socioeconomic, insurance, care access, or other unmeasured factors during the transition of care rather than inherent biological differences.

Preventing early graft loss is a priority, as young individuals tend to receive the highest‐quality donor organs. There are several possible explanations for the increased risk of early graft loss observed in the children, adolescents, and young adults in our study. Surgical complications, recurrent disease, rejection, infectious complications, and death with a functioning graft could all contribute to graft loss within the first 3 years of transplant. Additionally, whether donor‐recipient size mismatch impacted allograft outcomes is unclear [13]. However, we did not identify a significant number of children with graft losses within the first 30 days of transplant, even though these are the population most likely to have technical complications during their transplant surgery. Thus, while we did not have information of the exact cause of graft failure for all of our population, we believe that surgical and technical issues related to the implantation of the graft were less likely to be contributory to our findings. Moreover, prior studies have found medication nonadherence to be the cause of graft failure in 58% of pediatric renal graft recipients in the transition phase between early adolescence and young adulthood (14–26), and 30% of our cohort is within this age range [14]. Nonadherence to immunosuppressive medication regimens is well‐documented during these life stages, as patients often experience disruptions related to increased autonomy, changes in healthcare providers, comorbidities such as psychiatric illness, and the transition from pediatric to adult care settings with associated changes in insurance [15, 16]. While medication nonadherence is a plausible mechanism underlying the increased risk of graft loss among adolescent and young adult recipients that we observed, our study cannot determine causality or directly evaluate non‐adherence rates. Nonadherence should therefore be interpreted as one possible contributing pathway among several potential mechanisms underlying early graft loss in this age group, some of which are discussed later in the paper.

Our study has several strengths, including the use of a large, nationally representative cohort with sufficient power to detect effect modification by factors such as race/ethnicity. However, there are important limitations. The use of administrative data limits our ability to identify the exact reasons for graft failure. Due to the lack of available data on medication adherence and missing data on causes of graft failure, we were unable to directly evaluate the extent of the contribution of nonadherence to early graft loss, despite its likely importance in this context. It is worth noting that our study does not examine age using finer categories as in prior literature given the lower number of graft failure events in the first 3 years after transplantation in some age groups.

In summary, we found that adolescents and young adults receiving kidney transplantation between the ages of 12 and 26 years face the highest risk of early graft loss following kidney transplantation compared to those 26 to < 40 years of age, with some noted variations by race and ethnicity despite their receipt of high‐quality kidneys. These findings continue to highlight a persistent challenge, as outcomes have not substantially improved compared to studies published more than a decade ago, despite recognition that these recipients are particularly vulnerable following transplantation. Implementation of targeted interventions, including early, multidisciplinary transition‐to‐adult‐care models, may improve outcomes through closer follow‐up, enhanced adherence surveillance, and optimized therapeutic monitoring [17]. Improved assessments of individual risk factors for graft failure are also critical. Additionally, incorporating psychological and behavioral support throughout the transition period may improve emotional adjustment, adherence, and overall quality of life [18]. At a broader level, addressing social determinants of health, access to care, and care coordination remains essential for improving outcomes during these complex developmental stages. As a future step, more granular studies integrating psychosocial, developmental, behavioral, and healthcare‐system factors may help further clarify mechanisms underlying graft loss beyond what can be captured through registry‐based analyses alone.
