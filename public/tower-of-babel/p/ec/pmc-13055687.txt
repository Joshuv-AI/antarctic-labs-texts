Poststroke Dementia: The Rotterdam Study Shows Why Risk Factors, Stroke Severity, and Premorbid Brain Health Matter
Authors: Michelle C Johansen, Rebecca F Gottesman
Published: 2026

Brain health has entered mainstream discourse. It formed the topic of the first New York Times wellness series in 2026 and has been promoted in professional campaigns by foundations, but for neurologists and other clinicians and researchers, the imperative has been longstanding: preventing brain injury is foundational to cognitive longevity. Stroke is known to result in cognitive impairment, but a comprehensive understanding of modifiers, mechanisms, and determinants of the entity labeled poststroke cognitive impairment and dementia (PSCID) has remained elusive. Progress has been hampered by lack of standardized cognitive measures, selection bias as to who was enrolled, assessments conducted too close to the index event (when delirium, depression, aphasia, and early recovery confound performance), and limited characterization of premorbid cognition and vascular risk. Further, stroke is heterogeneous in mechanism, severity, and topology, each with distinct cognitive signatures. In summary, a complex pathway of preexisting vascular risk factors, the stroke event itself, and premorbid functioning, including premorbid brain health likely all contribute to the incidence of PSCID. Clarifying these components is essential to accurate incidence estimates and to targeted prevention.

Against this backdrop, Dr Claus and colleagues in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA) leverage the Rotterdam study, a population‐based longitudinal cohort from the Netherlands with decades of vascular risk factor data, cerebral imaging, and stroke phenotyping to refine estimates of PSCID and its modifiers. 1 The authors of this study build on prior work evaluating poststroke dementia and its modulating factors. The ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study, a US based cohort study, reported over ~25 years an adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of dementia ranging from 1.76 for 1 ischemic stroke with a National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of ≤10 to 6.68 for ≥2 moderate to severe strokes (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score 11–15, or ≥16). 2 Complementing this, the REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) study, another US‐based cohort study, reported an acceleration of cognitive decline after stroke, with higher odds of incident cognitive impairment post event compared with each participant's prestroke trajectory over a median ~6 years. 3 Together, these data demonstrate dose–response and age‐modified associations and underscore that stroke severity and recurrence shape PSCID risk.

The Rotterdam authors take this foundation and now add to it other cerebrovascular disease markers and conditions that are likely to be on the same etiologic spectrum with stroke: specifically, imaging measures of brain‐health and adjudicated transient ischemic attack events, and they additionally consider modulating risk factors in their population. A key strength is the statistical approach: they report both cause‐specific HRs (Cox models censoring at death) and subdistribution HRs (Fine–Gray models treating death as a competing event). The former addresses a pathogenetic question: Among participants who remain alive and dementia free, how do stroke type and severity change the hazard of incident dementia? The latter addresses a pragmatic question: Given that some participants will die before dementia can occur, how does stroke severity alter the cumulative probability of developing dementia over time? Reporting both estimands improves biological inference and real‐world risk prediction.

The authors found that when compared with matched reference participants, individuals with study‐defined minor stroke had 1.6 times the risk of PSCID, and those with major stroke 1.72 times the risk of PSCID over an average of 6.1 years of follow‐up. They found no association for those with transient ischemic attack (TIA), despite prior studies suggesting its independent association with dementia 4 ; by adjudicating TIA cases, they may be capturing TIA events more precisely than some earlier studies. The adjusted risk for those without stroke, but with covert infarcts on imaging, was not statistically significant, but the magnitude of the effect estimate was higher than TIA, but lower than minor stroke (HR, 1.34 [95% CI, 0.98–1.83]).

When the group accounted for mortality post stroke, they found that the association attenuated or was no longer significant for those with major strokes but was for minor stroke, emphasizing the importance of accounting for mortality in order to accurately describe the epidemiology of PSCID. Finally, the authors report on potential risk factors for poststroke dementia, supporting work from prior studies as well as clinical observations: age, lower educational levels, premorbid cognitive impairment and apoE4 (apolipoprotein E4) carriership were all independently associated with risk of dementia post event. In this study, however, stroke recurrence was not associated with an increase in dementia risk, but recurrence was on average 3.3 years after the index event, and overall follow‐up for this study was shorter than other work suggesting that recurrent stroke increases dementia risk. Furthermore, the sample seen in the Rotterdam study is more homogenous from a racial and ethnic standpoint than other studies or US‐based clinical populations, making generalizability difficult.

So how are we to interpret these findings? First, it confirms that those who develop a stroke are at increased risk for dementia post stroke and that those who survive a major stroke have the highest risk. Second, it also demonstrates that TIA, despite adjudication, is a mixed entity, and therefore the conclusions regarding what a TIA diagnosis means for brain health without richer clinical and imaging context at a population level remains limited. Given what is known, however, about the association between well‐characterized TIA and stroke, 5 it seems reasonable that TIA should still be considered a high‐risk event for later‐life brain health outcomes.

Third, the inclusion of covert infarcts on imaging is an interesting addition, as these changes likely represent overlapping pathology with stroke and TIA. However, in this article, interpretation of these results is limited as the authors note that those individuals who underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may have been healthier than the broader study sample. The point estimates from both cause‐specific and competing‐risk models suggest a 25% to 35% higher relative dementia risk after covert infarcts, with absolute risks rising to ~11% at 10 years. The timeline of this risk from covert infarcts is hard to characterize, however, since identifying chronic covert infarcts on MRI does not provide useful information about the actual date of onset of these infarcts, and thus time‐to‐event analyses are imperfect in this setting. Additionally, the CIs include the null, so the results regarding infarcts are suggestive rather than conclusive, but the magnitude and direction do align with prior cohorts and biological plausibility. As MRI access and capability improve, clarifying management when these lesions are detected is a critical research need. These lesions are not clinically inconsequential, despite being described as subclinical, and at minimum should prompt clinicians to emphasize aggressive control of identifiable vascular risk factors to prevent future infarcts. Furthermore, silent infarcts are clearly linked with risk of stroke, and often co‐occur with other markers of cerebral small vessel disease that are also linked to dementia risk, 6 , 7 so once again, they are at least linked indirectly to dementia risk.

Fourth and importantly, characteristics preceding the stroke play a major role in determining subsequent cognitive trajectories, as pointed out in this study. Some factors (eg, age, genetic risk) are immutable, a point reinforced by this study; others are modifiable. Prognostically, neither hypertension nor atrial fibrillation independently predicted dementia in this cohort, although other studies have reported associations. 8 We agree with the authors that the high prevalence of these risk factors in populations with stroke (eg, ~80% with hypertension) limits their discriminative value for who develops dementia, but this does not imply they are unimportant for dementia risk or prevention. One of the most useful data points in this study is their estimation that >11% (161 out of 1431) of their sample had dementia even before the stroke, emphasizing the importance of considering these shared risk factors but also prestroke cognitive status. The fact that this number is not that much lower than the new poststroke cumulative dementia incidence (14%) further points to the importance of any premorbid conditions. Their results also supported that prestroke cognitive performance was an important determinant of PSCID.

Strengths of this study include its contemporary baseline (2002) reflecting modern care, rigorous treatment of the competing risk of death, incorporation of imaging data, and the core strengths of the Rotterdam study itself (adjudicated events, detailed preevent risk factors and cognition, longitudinal follow‐up). Several limitations warrant consideration. The most significant relate to the timing of assessments relative to index events (stroke and MRI) and attrition. Reliance on documented records may have introduced misclassification of stroke and dementia. Missing data and selective participation could bias estimates, and as noted participants receiving MRI likely represent a healthier subset.

The call to action therefore is that cognition should be treated as a core stroke outcome, not a footnote. Clinically, we should normalize cognitive assessment post stroke and counsel survivors and families, especially after severe stroke, about trajectories and options for support. We should double down on modifiable vascular risk factors, whether or not they discriminate individual risk in this cohort. Similarly, screening for vascular risk and discussing potential brain health sequelae should be considered in patients with both TIA and covert infarcts, as they are linked to poor brain health indirectly via stroke, even if not consistently independent of stroke. Scientifically, we need harmonized definitions of PSCID and an understanding of the best neuropsychological batteries, competing‐risk analyses with longer time follow‐up, and importantly trials that test whether treatments like cognitive rehabilitation, or aggressive lifestyle interventions and vascular risk management (which ones and for whom) measurably change outcomes. The recently released results of the US POINTER (US Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk) randomized trial demonstrated that among adults at risk for cognitive decline and dementia, a multimodal intervention to include a healthy diet, social engagement, physical and cognitive activity, and cardiovascular health monitoring has a benefit on global cognition. 9 Although implementation of such a study may prove difficult in clinical practice, the results are encouraging as we consider ways to mitigate vascular contributions to cognitive decline. We also look forward to the results from the ongoing DISCOVERY (Determinants of Incident Stroke Cognitive Outcomes and Vascular Effects on Recovery) study, which will help us better understand underlying mechanisms of susceptibility for PSCID, and among a diverse range of patients. 10
