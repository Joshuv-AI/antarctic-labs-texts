Quality improvement project to enhance adherence to RCEM standards for patients with paracetamol overdose
Authors: Dayana El Nsouli, Christopher Chung, Holly Wilkins, Tawfiq Alqeisi, Manzar Maqsood, Raminder Sandhu, Paige Emily Bate-Jones, Graham D Johnson, Azaad Jameel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelayed or inconsistent administration of N-acetylcysteine (NAC) for paracetamol overdose in the emergency department (ED) poses a risk to patient safety, with current Royal College of Emergency Medicine (RCEM) standards often not being met. The traditional 21-hour NAC regimen is associated with adverse drug reactions, medication errors and prolonged admissions. The Scottish and Newcastle Acetylcysteine Protocol (SNAP) was introduced as a simpler alternative with comparable efficacy. This quality improvement project (QIP) aimed to improve compliance with RCEM standards by implementing targeted interventions while also reducing the length of inpatient stay and maintaining patient safety.MethodThis QIP was conducted at Royal Derby Hospital using a multidisciplinary, systematic approach based on Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles. Baseline data were collected from 100 randomly selected patients (November 2021–May 2022) and compared with outcomes during a 52-week intervention period (September 2023–August 2024). Interventions included educational sessions, quick reference materials and enhanced prescribing tools. Data were analysed for compliance with RCEM standards, adverse events (liver function derangement and anaphylactoid reactions) and system-level measures, such as length of inpatient stay and timing of paracetamol plasma levels.ResultsA total of 214 patients were included. Compliance with RCEM standard 1 improved from 36% to 43%. No change was noted for standards 2 and 3. Median inpatient stay decreased from 35 hours to 30.5 hours. No significant differences were found in adverse events. Special cause variation was identified in paracetamol plasma level timing, attributed to early sampling in some cases.ConclusionThis QIP addressed problems of delayed or inconsistent NAC administration in the ED by improving compliance with RCEM standard 1 and reducing inpatient stay while maintaining patient safety. Although standards 2 and 3 did not improve, the interventions proved cost-effective, feasible and scalable. Future work should focus on sustaining improvements and exploring patient-centred outcomes across diverse healthcare settings.Keywords: Quality improvement, PDSA, Medication safety

Introduction

Problem description

Paracetamol overdose (OD) is a leading cause of emergency hospital visits and the primary cause of acute liver failure in the Western world.1 In the UK alone, approximately 100 000 people present to emergency departments (EDs) each year due to paracetamol OD, resulting in around 50 000 hospital admissions and approximately 150 deaths annually.2 Fatalities or cases of acute liver failure are uncommon in patients who receive treatment within 8 hours of a single acute OD, due to the availability of the highly effective antidote, N-acetylcysteine (NAC).3 This antidote works by replenishing cellular glutathione, which helps protect liver cells from damage caused by the toxic metabolite of paracetamol, N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine.4

Since the 1970s, NAC has been administered intravenously using the same 21-hour regimen, with only a few dose-finding studies conducted in this area. This regimen involves three sequential weight-based doses of NAC in 5% dextrose: 150 mg/kg over 15 min (extended to 1 hour in the UK in 2012), followed by 50 mg/kg over 4 hours and 100 mg/kg over 16 hours. However, this regimen is associated with a high rate of adverse drug reactions (ADRs), particularly anaphylactoid reactions, which can be distressing for patients and may result in the temporary cessation of therapy, require antihistamine treatment, extend the duration of treatment and hospitalisation and sometimes lead to NAC being withheld by doctors.5 In addition to ADRs, the complexity of the NAC regimen can lead to medication errors,6 and its lengthy administration time contributes to significant hospital bed pressures, totalling around 47 000 bed days per year in England.2

To address these challenges, alternative treatment regimens have been introduced in clinical practice, including simplified 1- or 2-‘bag’ regimens, or stopping NAC before the end of the 21-hour regimen in low-risk patients.7,9 This is known as the Scottish and Newcastle Acetylcysteine Protocol (SNAP) regimen, which has also been proven to be as effective as the standard 21-hour regimen.3 Additionally, the Royal College of Emergency Medicine (RCEM) released standards for the NAC management of patients with a paracetamol OD (box 1).10 These standards state when patients with a paracetamol OD should receive NAC, depending on OD size and type of ingestion. Plasma paracetamol levels should be measured for all patients no earlier than 4 hours after ingestion, as drug pharmacokinetics may still be active.10 This causes a plasma paracetamol level taken earlier than 4 hours after ingestion to give an inaccurately low level, as not enough time has elapsed for enough absorption to take place. Otherwise, these plasma levels would have to be repeated to ensure an accurate representation to determine management.

The most recent national audit report, from 2013 to 2014, showed participating UK hospitals’ performances against several measures related to the management of paracetamol OD.10 The latest data showed 50% compliance with RCEM standard 1 among 42 EDs that submitted their data. This report also states a wide variation in practice nationally, with Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency guidance almost always never completely compliant.10 This may be due to a lack of documentation in patient notes, gaps in knowledge or that the standards are simply not realistically achievable.10

The SNAP regimen was implemented at Royal Derby Hospital (RDH) at the end of November 2021 to replace the previous 21-hour regimen. Prior to this, RDH conducted local audits during the years of 2013, 2016 and 2021. The results showed 20%, 35% and 43% adherence to RCEM standard 1, respectively, and 0% adherence in all years to RCEM standards 2 and 3. After SNAP implementation, adherence to standard 1 reduced to 35% and standards 2 and 3 remained at 0%. The percentages reflect the number of patients who received NAC in time, out of the total number of patients admitted to the ED with a paracetamol OD requiring NAC. The implementation of the SNAP regimen and the results from previous audits served as the initial stimulus for this quality improvement project (QIP).

Specific aims

The specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound (SMART) aims for this QIP were to evaluate whether targeted interventions could improve adherence to RCEM standards for NAC administration in the ED while reducing inpatient stay and maintaining patient safety.

Specifically, the objectives were to:

Increase the percentage of adult patients receiving NAC within 8 hours, as per RCEM standard 1, to 50% by 31 August 2024, at RDH ED.

Increase the percentage of adult patients receiving NAC within 8 hours, as per RCEM standard 1, to 50% by 31 August 2024, at RDH ED.

Increase the percentage of adult patients receiving NAC within 1 hour of arrival, as per RCEM standard 2 and 3, to 10% by 31 August 2024, at RDH ED.

Increase the percentage of adult patients receiving NAC within 1 hour of arrival, as per RCEM standard 2 and 3, to 10% by 31 August 2024, at RDH ED.

A 50% target for standard 1 was chosen as a meaningful and achievable improvement within the 52-week intervention period. Standards 2 and 3 were set at a lower 10% target due to the more complex challenges of ED waiting times and service pressures. Once these SMART objectives are achieved, the aim will be revised to set higher thresholds and drive continuous improvement.

Methods

This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence 2.0 guidelines to ensure rigorous and transparent reporting.11

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Design and setting

This QIP was conducted in the RDH, University Hospitals of Derby and Burton National Health Service rust adult ED. It is a single-centre, 1159-bed acute teaching hospital in the East Midlands, England. On average, the RDH ED sees 3000 patients with paracetamol OD out of a total of 82 000 attendances per year. The site’s catchment area serves 1 million patients from Derby and the surrounding Derbyshire area every year. The QIP team was composed of six clinical fellows, a general practitioner trainee, an ED specialty doctor, a core surgical trainee, three advanced pharmacists and two consultants. The QIP received support from consultants, nurses, healthcare assistants and secretarial staff in the RDH ED. Other stakeholders included the hospital’s Business Intelligence service, which provided a list of patients every fortnight, and managers of the ED who facilitated the interventions.

QI methodology

To understand the challenges around NAC administration, the QIP team conducted preliminary surveys that were distributed to a convenience sample of 15 frontline staff members, including doctors, nurses and allied healthcare professionals of various seniority levels in the ED. The interviews explored current knowledge of paracetamol OD management and prioritisation of NAC administration, including approaches to identifying high-risk cases such as staggered ODs. The qualitative data gathered from these surveys were collaboratively analysed by the QIP team, which informed a driver diagram (figure 1) and guided the design of the interventions (table 1).

QIP interventions

NAC, N-acetylcysteine; QIP, quality improvement project.

A 23-week baseline period (30 November 2021–8 May 2022) included a retrospective audit of 100 randomly selected patients’ electronic case notes admitted with paracetamol OD against the RCEM standards of care. The 52-week intervention period (1 September 2023–31 August 2024) collected data prospectively from a random sample of five patients per fortnight, allowing iterative intervention testing through Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycles. A fortnight was considered as days 1–14/days 15-last day of each month. Adult patients receiving NAC in the ED were included. Patients under 18, not receiving NAC or treated on inpatient wards, were excluded. Children and young people are managed in a co-located but separately staffed children’s ED, where the SNAP protocol is not yet implemented, and therefore those under the age of 18 were excluded. Each patient was assigned the RCEM standard that applies to their case, and compliance for each NAC administration was evaluated by a QIP team member. To minimise subjectivity, the team predefined compliance criteria in consultation with the RCEM national paracetamol OD report.10

The QIP team also recorded the time of admission, timings of paracetamol plasma levels, size of OD and anaphylactoid reactions. To improve consistency, the study team interpreted the first week of data together. Subsequent data was interpreted by one of the team members independently, and any uncertain cases were reviewed by the QIP lead. To ensure that patients were not being harmed by the new regimen, liver function tests (LFTs) were monitored and used as a balancing measure. Inpatient duration of stay in hours was recorded to understand the downstream implications of SNAP as a system measure. Finally, the total number of patients presenting to the ED was also collected to mitigate its potential as a confounding variable.

Measures

The outcome measure was the achievement of RCEM standards, which was determined by collecting the following data for each patient: time (hours) from OD ingestion to NAC administration, time (hours) from patient admission to NAC administration and OD size. Patients were assigned a corresponding RCEM standard based on these parameters. Balancing measures included deranged LFTs and the incidence of anaphylactoid reactions. System measures included total inpatient duration (hours) and timing of paracetamol plasma levels. These measures were chosen as they are simple, objective metrics to determine RCEM standards compliance as per the national report.10

Analysis

The data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel (V.2303 2023). Descriptive statistics were used to report the median and mean time from admission/ingestion to NAC administration, the number of patients, LFT derangement and the median inpatient duration. A statistical process control (SPC) P-chart was created to display the proportion of patients meeting the RCEM standards at fortnightly intervals, with the upper and lower control limits (LCL and UCL) calculated using SD from the overall mean or median proportion. A P-chart was also used for the timing of paracetamol plasma levels in relation to ingestion. The P-charts were analysed using standard criteria to distinguish between common cause variation (normal fluctuations expected in the process) and special cause variation. Similarly, a median control chart was created to display inpatient duration. The median was chosen rather than the mean, as it was a better representation of the central tendency of the data due to the presence of several outliers. The identification of special cause variation indicated that the observed changes were likely influenced by new or external factors, such as the QIP interventions. To determine whether the variation in the number of patients presenting with a paracetamol OD acted as a confounding variable affecting compliance with the RCEM standards, a Spearman correlation analysis was conducted between fortnightly patient volumes and compliance rates.

For inpatient duration, most points fell within the control limits as illustrated in the median control chart (online supplemental figure 2). During the 52-week intervention period, the mean number of patients presenting to the ED with a paracetamol OD each fortnight was 88.9 (SD=16.2), with a range from 59 to 133 patients. The median number of patients was 89 (IQR=23.8). The Spearman test demonstrated a weak negative correlation (rs=−0.092) that was not statistically significant (p=0.670). This suggests that fluctuations in patient volume did not significantly impact the observed changes in compliance, indicating that the improvements in compliance were likely due to the QI interventions.

Discussion

This QIP aimed to improve compliance with RCEM standards for managing patients with paracetamol OD after the introduction of SNAP in the ED. There was a small success in improving RCEM standard 1: 100% of patients with a single paracetamol OD are to be tested and to have started NAC within 8 hours of ingestion. Compliance with RCEM standard 1 improved by 7%. Unfortunately, despite the time and resources spent on the PDSA cycles, the QIP did not achieve two of the objectives it set out, namely RCEM standards 2 and 3.

The RCEM standards for managing paracetamol OD were set over a decade ago in 2008, when the demand for emergency healthcare and the population size were much lower than they are today, both locally in Derby and nationally in the UK. Indeed, between 2013 and 2015, RDH ED had an annual 4-hour breach of between 6% and 7% of all patients. In contrast, in 2022, the annual 4-hour breach percentage was 84%; in 2023, it was 79% and in 2024, it was 77%. Furthermore, the average time for patients to be seen by a clinician has increased from 67 min in 2013 to 164 min in 2025, which is a 145% increase in time to be seen. These statistics illustrate the bottleneck in the exponential increase in demand and system strain our department and others in the UK have faced, and the challenging environment our QIP was operating in. This marginal improvement may be reflective of the difficulty in implementing a complex and lengthy QIP within a very busy, high-risk department, which is now exponentially busier than when the RCEM standards were initially developed in 2008.

The interventions were generally welcomed by the department and received positive feedback. Our nursing staff found that the education videos explaining the correct initial triage management and the manual quick reference cards had increased their confidence. Prior to this, the ED nursing staff would need to find a senior clinician to advise on sample timing. Furthermore, the reference booklets were available throughout the department, ensuring accessibility. The preprinted NAC dosage stickers were welcomed by clinicians as they made prescribing quicker and safer, as they included preprinted dosages. Nursing staff also expressed how this made administration safer due to clear legibility compared with handwritten dosages.

Insights from staff perspectives showed that delays may be due to the unavailability of weighing scales for patients to determine dose and a lack of knowledge or system in place to flag the patients that need to be seen straight away by a clinician. Furthermore, lengthy waits for patients to get triaged are another contributing factor to the delay. We used this feedback to plan further PDSA cycles beyond the timeline of this manuscript in our sustainability plan, which includes a fast-track protocol and enhanced triage training.

Significant workload within the ED and staff shortages may introduce challenges in ensuring prioritisation of patients requiring NAC alongside other time-critical conditions, such as patients requiring thrombolysis for stroke. Regular scheduled changeover of resident doctors also made sustained and continued improvement in practice difficult because each new cohort needed to be trained and taught regarding this QIP and the change interventions. However, the stable nursing staffing roster highlighted sustained improvement in decreasing the number of inappropriate timings of paracetamol level blood tests. Applying this rationale, if the doctor roster had the same level of consistency and stability, sustained and continued improvement may have been possible in standard 1, as a minimum. This QIP also challenges whether the standards are realistic and achievable in line with the RCEM 2013–2014 paracetamol OD national report’s conclusions.10 Similarly, this QIP displayed 0% achievement of standards 2 and 3. There were no other EDs able to meet these standards in 2013. This raises questions as to whether an alternative target would be more achievable, as suggested in the RCEM national audit report.10 Perhaps in the future, the SMART aim going forward could be amended to reflect a percentage reduction in time to administration of NAC.

There was an improvement in total inpatient duration, with a reduction in the median inpatient stay during the intervention period, which resulted in cost savings for the trust. This may have been due to the marginal increase in timely NAC administration, as well as the compounded efforts of staff engagement in this QIP. However, there was an increase in the proportion of plasma levels taken too early, which highlights a future challenge to tackle in order to improve system efficiency while simultaneously reducing the need for unnecessary repeat blood tests, in line with RCEM’s Green ED initiatives.12 Some insights on the root cause of this that contributed to designing education sessions include a pre-emptive workflow and misunderstanding of the protocol, where samples were taken promptly on arrival to avoid delays. Although several newsletters have been disseminated to remind staff, it is difficult to ascertain if they have been engaged with. The efforts to improve compliance did not negatively affect patients, as LFTs and anaphylactoid reactions stayed at baseline. The observed reduction in inpatient stays for anaphylactoid reactions is consistent with the evidence based on SNAP.1 3 4 The data from this QIP demonstrates that performance with standard 1 is slightly under the national average, with some fortnights exceeding this. Of the 42 EDs, 13 were able to achieve a 100% success rate in this standard.10 However, there was a large variation between data points, indicating that consistency is lacking in service provision, and it is an area that requires further improvement.

Although not directly measured, the broader impact of this QIP may have led to reduced healthcare costs, decreased ED congestion and improved staff morale due to streamlined processes within the practice. This project demonstrated that coordinated, multidisciplinary QIP is possible within a busy ED setting. However, there was marginal success that may highlight the need to review project objectives or view standards through a system-wide lens that can be transferrable to multiple time-critical conditions and environments. In general, the interventions were relatively low-cost and easily implementable, making them highly reproducible in other EDs.

Limitations

The number of patients that met standard 1 was not consistent, and therefore, the sample sizes per fortnight were not the same across the baseline or intervention period. This may have skewed the data, particularly the proportion/mean values. Another important limitation of this QIP is that it did not include patient-oriented outcomes. Measures such as patient satisfaction and experience may have provided further insights into the impact of the interventions beyond compliance with clinical standards and process efficiency. Unfortunately, incorporating these measures was not feasible due to time constraints, limitations in data collection resources, as well as patients’ suicidal intent, which meant that capturing this insight would have posed more challenges. Accounting for the potential bias in the data collection process is important. Although efforts were made to standardise data collection, the reliance on local team members for recording compliance and outcomes may have introduced observer bias and interobserver variability. However, since the data collected requires no interpretation, the results should not have been significantly influenced through this process. In addition, the sample size per fortnight of patients qualifying for standard 1 changed due to the random selectivity of the selection process. This may have increased the variation in the data points. Finally, sustaining changes when there are new staff joining our ED, especially the major changes of junior doctors in February 2024 and August 2024, is a challenge and may have affected some of the monthly results. Due to this, continuous teaching sessions in line with new rotations were essential.

It was noted that some PDSA cycles were more effective than others. Staff feedback reflected a lack of engagement with written newsletters compared with progress videos. Furthermore, initial versions of the NAC dosage stickers needed to be revised due to their unclear original layout in indicating the date/time of prescription. Gaps in guideline knowledge of staff members still cropped up during the intervention phase, which emphasises the need for continuous education sessions and engagement. While education was a key driver of this QIP, we did not formally evaluate its impact through knowledge assessments or confidence measures, which limits our ability to correlate content with performance improvements. The choice of interventions is generalisable to other hospitals, and we also infer that the limitations may also be obstacles that other EDs could face, given the nature of staff changes and rotations.

To enhance the sustainability of the interventions, we propose applying principles of Normalisation Process Theory to embed changes into routine practice.13 To support coherence, dedicated teaching sessions for junior doctor induction at every rotation, with preassessment and postassessment, will ensure new staff are updated with the knowledge required on protocols in place. For cognitive participation, we plan to identify a ‘champion’ who can be a consistent point of contact for good practice and engagement. We are also exploring the integration of a customised electronic form which includes a warning on whether the paracetamol level is being requested too early. Lastly, we will continue to use our engagement progress videos that include key performance metrics as part of reflexive monitoring, as well as introduce patient experience surveys to capture satisfaction and understanding of this QIP.

Conclusion

This QIP demonstrated that targeted, multidisciplinary interventions were effective in improving compliance with the RCEM standard for paracetamol OD management in a single-centre ED. The patient safety profile was not impacted, and the PDSA cycles are scalable and generalisable. The interventions led to an increase in timely NAC administration, reduced inpatient duration and maintained patient safety, with no increase in adverse events. The methods and approaches used were simple, cost-effective and adaptable, making them applicable to other EDs seeking to enhance their management of paracetamol OD or similar clinical scenarios. To facilitate a more embedded uptake, we propose to implement an induction training session for new staff, designate a champion, use electronic prescribing forms and establish shared progress videos benchmarking compliance to create a sustainable change. These next steps can be adopted in other EDs looking to make similar improvements both locally within the East Midlands and nationally. Future work should focus on ensuring the sustainability of these improvements and exploring their impact on patient-centred outcomes across diverse healthcare settings.
