Relationship Between Clostridioides difficile Stool Concentration, Nucleic Acid Amplification Test Results, and Environmental Contamination
Authors: Mary Morgan Lee, Kimberly A Reske, Tiffany Hink, Skye R S Fishbein, Erin P Newcomer, Candice Cass, Margaret A Olsen, Gautam Dantas, Carey-Ann D Burnham, Erik R Dubberke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe compared rectal swab nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) with stool culture to detect Clostridioides difficile colonization and correlated results with environmental contamination. Rectal swab NAAT sensitivity (43%) for detecting toxigenic C. difficile colonization was low. Toxigenic culture-positive patients were more likely to have C. difficile detected in the hospital environment.

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is a leading cause of healthcare-associated infections in the United States [1, 2]. As many as 7%–18% of admitted patients are asymptomatically colonized with C. difficile [3, 4]. Colonized patients contribute to C. difficile transmission and are at risk for being diagnosed with CDI [4, 5]; however, the dynamics of C. difficile transmission related to colonized patients is poorly understood. Stool culture is the most sensitive method of C. difficile detection, but stool culture is slow and limited by patients' ability to have bowel movements. Rectal swab nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) is an alternative method for detecting colonization rapidly on admission; however, the sensitivity of rectal swab C. difficile NAAT compared with stool culture is unknown [6]. Other unknowns include the correlation between NAAT results and concentrations of C. difficile in stool and C. difficile environmental contamination. The objectives of this study were to determine the sensitivity of rectal swab NAAT to stool and swab culture and to correlate NAAT results to C. difficile environmental contamination.

METHODS

This was a prospective cohort study performed on a bone marrow transplant unit and an acute leukemia unit at Barnes-Jewish Hospital in St. Louis, MO. Enrollment occurred January to July 2019 (leukemia unit) and April to July 2019 (BMT unit). All patients were housed in single rooms; diluted bleach was used for daily and terminal discharge cleaning. The Institutional Review Board approval was obtained, and all participants provided written, informed consent.

Specimen Collection

Fecal specimens, rectal swabs (eSwab, Copan Diagnostics), and environmental swabs (bed rails, bedside table, and sink [sinks were handless]) were collected from participants on admission and weekly while hospitalized. Remnant stool specimens collected for C. difficile testing were also obtained.

When possible, rectal swabs were collected on the same day that fecal specimens were provided; if not, swabs were collected as soon as possible (usually the next day) after the fecal specimen was collected. Stool specimens and rectal swabs that were collected from the same sampling point (e.g., admission specimens or weekly specimens collected within a day of each other) were considered paired. The study team attempted to collect both a fecal specimen and rectal swab from each patient at each collection point; however, occasionally only a single specimen was obtained, often due to rectal swab refusal.

Specimen Processing

Stool specimens and rectal swabs collected on admission to the study ward were processed the same day as collection (refrigerated up to 3 hours before processing). Specimens from all other collection time points were stored plain in a dry, sterile container and in tryptic soy broth/glycerol at −80°C and processed later.

C. difficile Culture

Broth enrichment for C. difficile was done on all admission and discharge (i.e., last collected) specimens. Semiquantitative culture was performed on all C. difficile–positive specimens. If C. difficile was recovered, all other specimens from that patient were cultured.

Broth enrichment culture in cycloserine, cefoxitin, mannitol broth with taurocholate and lysozyme (Anaerobe Systems) was used for initial cultures; all culture-positive specimens were further cultured semiquantitatively on cycloserine, cefoxitin, fructose agar with horse blood and taurocholate (CCFA-HT) (Anaerobe Systems) [7, 8]. Each specimen was inoculated onto 2 CCFA-HT plates (1 with 10 µL and 1 with 100 µL) and incubated anaerobically at 35°C. The number of colony forming units (CFUs)/g of stool were calculated from colony counts. Specimens where C. difficile was recovered from broth but not agar were categorized as >0 to <10,000 CFU/g of stool. C. difficile was identified by VITEK MS (bioMerieux, Durham, NC). NAAT was performed using the Xpert C. difficile test (Cepheid).

C. difficile isolates underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS), and toxigenicity status was determined by detection of full-length tcdA and/or tcdB in the annotated coding gene sequences from Prokka-annotated genome files [8]. If both rectal swab and stool specimen from the same collection point were culture positive, the stool specimen isolate was used preferentially for WGS. C. difficile isolates were classified into multilocus sequence type (MLST) networks based on single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) distances; a cutoff of ≤2 SNPs was used to define an MLST network group [8]. MLST networks were defined as per Knight et al [9].

Statistical Analyses

Sensitivity of rectal swab NAAT versus semiquantitative C. difficile culture on paired specimens was determined. The relationships between semiquantitative culture results, NAAT, and environmental contamination were evaluated using Fisher's exact or logistic regression. Environmental swabs were considered paired with stool and/or rectal swabs if they were collected within ±3 days of the stool specimen/rectal swab. SPSS version 29 was used for analyses (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

RESULTS

A total of 1,244 stool specimens and rectal swabs were collected from 384 patients. Of these, 738 specimens were cultured (59%), and C. difficile was recovered from 154 (21%); 91/154 (59%) were toxigenic C. difficile. A total of 399 rectal swab NAATs were performed; 81 (20%) were positive.

Multilocus sequence type strain typing results were available for 141 isolates. Strains from clade 1 predominated (93% of isolates). Only 4 isolates from clade 2 were identified, including 1 ST1 isolate and 3 ST41 isolates. The most commonly identified strains were ST42 (N = 18), ST15 (N = 17), ST2 (N = 17), and ST53 (N = 15).

Sensitivity of Rectal Swab NAAT

Semiquantitative stool culture results were compared with rectal swab NAAT results (N = 70 patient-days; Table 1). Overall sensitivity of rectal swab NAAT compared with culture was 43% and specificity was 100%. Sensitivity was highest (80%) when C. difficile stool concentration ≥10,000 CFU/g (χ2 for trend <0.001). There were no time points with a NAAT+ rectal swab and culture-negative stool.

Environmental Sampling Results

A total of 3,045 environmental swabs were collected (1015 from each site), and 1197 (39%) were cultured. Twenty-six (n = 20; 77% toxigenic) environmental swabs were culture positive: 22/398 cultured (6%) from bedrails, 4/399 cultured from keyboards (1%), and 0 from room sinks. Of the 18 time points when C. difficile was recovered from both stool and bedrails simultaneously, 13 (72%) were identical, compared with 1/3 (33%) from keyboards.

C. difficile Stool Culture Results, NAAT Results, and Environmental Contamination

Environmental swab culture results ±3 days of stool/rectal swab collection date and semiquantitative stool culture results were compared (n = 144 paired samples) (Table 2). Among the C. difficile positive specimens, only paired stool specimens and environmental swabs where the same strain was identified were included. Fourteen percent (9/63) of patients with stool concentration of >0 to <10,000 CFU/g had a positive environmental swab (same strain) within 3 days, and 18% (4/22) of patients with stool concentration of ≥10,000 CFU/g had a positive environmental swab (same strain) within 3 days.

Rectal swab NAAT results and environmental swab results within 3 days were also compared for 98 paired specimen sets (Table 2). Two (13%) patients with NAAT-positive rectal swabs had a positive environmental swab within 3 days. The rectal swab NAAT was positive for 2/4 of the patients with positive environmental cultures within 3 days of the rectal swab collection.

DISCUSSION

Our findings indicate that rectal swab NAAT has an overall low sensitivity (43%) compared with stool culture for detecting colonization with toxigenic C. difficile. A greater proportion of rectal swab NAATs were positive when fecal concentrations of toxigenic C. difficile were >10,000 CFU/g, as would be expected; however, our sample sizes were too low to draw definitive conclusions. Our findings of low NAAT sensitivity are substantiated by prior studies showing lower sensitivity of rectal swab NAAT compared with rectal swab culture when testing asymptomatic carriers with low burden of C. difficile [10].

Similarly, our results suggest that stool culture likely is superior to rectal swab NAAT for identifying patients with C. difficile in their hospital environment. Patients with C. difficile detected by culture in stool or rectal swab NAATs ±3 days were significantly more likely to have C. difficile detected in their hospital environment, but C. difficile was recovered from the environment of <20% of colonized patients.

The main limitation to our study was small sample size. Despite conducting our study on wards with patients known to be at increased risk for C. difficile colonization and CDI, only 91 patient specimens and 20 environmental swabs were positive for toxigenic C. difficile. The use of daily bleach cleaning may have resulted in low recovery of C. difficile from the environment. Rectal swab collection and processing may have also limited C. difficile recovery, due to variation in swab soiling among study personnel. Of note, our swabs were collected by research personnel trained to optimize rectal swab soiling with stool, not clinical personnel where rectal swab collection may be more variable. Rectal swabs may require initial culture in broth in order to enhance sensitivity [11].

Overall, our results suggest that rectal swab NAAT may not be sufficiently sensitive to screen for toxigenic C. difficile colonization, particularly at low levels of fecal concentration. Fecal concentrations likely are dynamic, and it is unknown whether fecal C. difficile concentrations on admission are correlated with later risk of environmental contamination or CDI. Patients with low levels of fecal colonization could be at increased risk of CDI after subsequent antibiotic exposure. Further data regarding the relationship between C. difficile colonization and infection, fecal concentration, and environmental contamination would help guide infection prevention efforts.
