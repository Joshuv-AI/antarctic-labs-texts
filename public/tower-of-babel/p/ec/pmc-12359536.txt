Are loneliness and social network size mediators between childhood adversity and depressive symptoms? A cross-sectional replication study in Ethiopia
Authors: Paula Gerbig, Matthias Alexander Reinhard, Henok Ababu, Stephanie Rek, Benedikt L Amann, Kristina Adorjan, Mubarek Abera, Frank Padberg, Andrea Jobst
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Abstract Introduction Loneliness and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) constitute significant risk factors for mental disorders, with loneliness emerging as a serious global public health concern. Recent research highlights the role of loneliness as a potential link between early life adversities and current psychopathology. However, most studies have been conducted in high-income, highly individualistic countries. This cross-sectional study explores the interplay between loneliness, social network size, recalled ACEs and depressive symptoms in Ethiopia—a low-income and collectivistic cultural context. Methods The study included 125 psychiatric outpatients at Jimma University Medical Center in Southwest Ethiopia diagnosed with major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder or psychotic disorders, as well as 131 non-clinical participants. Trained interviewers administered the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Loneliness Scale, the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire, the Social Network Index and the WHO-5 Well-Being Index. We used Mann-Whitney U tests, partial correlation and mediation analysis for data analysis. Results We found mild-to-moderate correlations between loneliness and ACEs (clinical group: rho=0.29, p<0.001, 95% CI (0.13 to 0.45); non-clinical group: rho=0.42, p<0.001, 95% CI (0.27 to 0.56)). In both groups, loneliness fully mediated the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms (clinical group: indirect effect a1b1=0.07, 95% CI (0.02 to 0.13); non-clinical group: indirect effect a1b1=0.03, 95% CI (0.01 to 0.07)). In contrast, social network size was neither correlated with ACEs nor did it mediate the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms in either group. Conclusion This study replicates previous findings that loneliness—rather than social network size—is associated with ACEs and mediates their impact on depressive symptoms. These results support the transcultural and transdiagnostic relevance of loneliness as a universal psychological mechanism, independent of societal structure.Keywords: MENTAL HEALTH, Depression & mood disorders, Schizophrenia & psychotic disorders, Ethiopia, Percieved Social Support

Introduction

Loneliness has gained increasing scientific attention in recent years as a significant psychological adversity in health and disease.1 Recognised as a global public health concern, it poses serious implications for mental and physical well-being across all age groups. The WHO has identified loneliness and social isolation as critical yet often overlooked social determinants of health and has designated social connection as a global health priority.2 Loneliness is defined as a negative emotional state evoked by the discrepancy between desired and actual social relationships.3 Importantly, loneliness is distinct from social isolation, which refers to an objective lack of social contacts.4 Measures of social network size and loneliness typically correlate only weakly, whereas loneliness is more strongly associated with mental health problems.5 In a cross-sectional study, Meltzer et al6 found that individuals diagnosed with depression showed a 10-fold increase in the odds of experiencing loneliness compared with the general population. Similarly, loneliness has been consistently identified as a risk factor for depressive symptoms.7,9 Among patients with bipolar disorder, the related concept of low perceived social support has been repeatedly linked to more severe depressive symptoms.8 In a recent meta-analysis including data from seven countries, Hajek et al reported strikingly high prevalences of loneliness in individuals with bipolar disorder (77.5%) and schizophrenia (75.2%).10 Furthermore, a time-lagged multilevel regression study in patients undergoing cognitive behavioural therapy for psychosis found that loneliness predicted increases in both negative and depressive symptoms, while positive symptoms predicted increased loneliness.11

In addition to loneliness, there is substantial evidence that adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) significantly increase the risk of developing mental health problems. For instance, ACEs have been shown to triple the transdiagnostic risk of mental disorders later in life12 and are associated with a more than twofold increased risk of depression in adulthood.13 Moreover, individuals with bipolar disorder and a history of ACEs tend to experience more severe symptoms of depression and mania, as well as an earlier onset of the illness.14 Similarly, ACEs have been linked to a heightened risk of developing psychosis and schizophrenia,15 16 along with more pronounced cognitive and functional impairments—including difficulties in working memory, executive function and verbal tasks.17

A growing body of research links ACEs with heightened feelings of loneliness later in life.18,22 In a recent longitudinal study,21 adolescents who reported higher levels of abuse and neglect showed higher levels of loneliness 1 year later, compared with adolescents with minimal reported ACEs. Furthermore, Shevlin et al23 found that loneliness mediated the association between ACEs and six psychiatric disorders—namely generalised anxiety disorder, mixed anxiety and depression disorder, phobia, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder and psychosis. Our research unit also recently conducted a study in Germany, which included both a clinical sample—comprising patients with persistent depressive disorder or borderline personality disorder—and a non-clinical sample.24 In both groups, loneliness, unlike social network size, was closely associated with ACEs and mediated the relationship between reported ACEs and depressive symptoms.

Two potential underlying mechanisms linking ACEs and loneliness might be disruptions in attachment security and heightened rejection sensitivity, both arising from negative interpersonal experiences that undermine trust and emotional safety. When caregivers are unavailable or fail to provide a sense of safety and comfort, the development of secure attachment in early childhood can be disrupted.18 25 Such disruption may undermine an individual’s capacity to trust others, form emotional bonds and seek support in relationships later in life.26 27 Guided by a negative internalised working model of disconnection, this can ultimately heighten feelings of loneliness.18 Insecure attachment is often associated with heightened rejection sensitivity—defined as the cognitive-emotional tendency to anxiously expect, readily perceive and overreact to rejection.28 Previous research has shown that ACEs, particularly experiences of parental rejection, can lead to increased rejection sensitivity.28 29 Rejection sensitivity may hinder the capacity to experience intimacy in relationships, reduce relationship satisfaction30 and lead to loneliness. Accordingly, Nenov-Matt et al19 found that rejection sensitivity mediated the association between emotional abuse, emotional neglect and loneliness in a clinical and non-clinical sample.

Most studies referenced so far were conducted in individualistic, high-income countries and caution must be taken when applying these findings to other cultural settings. Factors such as individualistic versus collectivistic cultural norms, income levels, living arrangements, religious practices and access to mental health resources may shape both the experience and impact of loneliness in distinct ways. Understanding these variations is essential for identifying universal versus context-specific mechanisms of mental distress and loneliness, and for developing interventions that are effective and equitable across diverse global populations.

In this study, we aimed to determine whether the associations between loneliness, ACEs, social network size and depressive symptoms, observed in our previous study in Germany, could also be found in independent clinical and non-clinical samples from Ethiopia. Our aim, thereby, was to investigate the generalisability of these findings within a transcultural framework. We tested the following hypotheses: the clinical group shows a higher prevalence of loneliness and ACEs than the non-clinical group (H1). Loneliness is associated with ACEs in both the clinical and non-clinical groups (H2). The association between loneliness and ACEs is stronger than the association between smaller social network size and ACEs in both groups (H3). Finally, loneliness, but not social network size, mediates the path from ACEs to depressive symptoms (H4).

Methods

Participants and study setting

Our study sample comprised 125 psychiatric outpatients diagnosed with major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder or psychotic disorders and 131 non-clinical participants, initially recruited for a study on metabolic syndrome in psychiatric patients and non-clinical participants.31 Sample size was calculated only for the original study using double proportion population formula.32 The DSM-5 diagnoses were obtained from the clinical charts of the outpatient department, where they were made by experienced psychiatrists or mental health professionals with a master’s degree. The participants were simultaneously assessed using the present questionnaires at Jimma University Medical Center in September 2021. All participants gave their written informed consent. Illiterate participants gave their thumbprints as signatures after the data collectors read the information and consent sheet aloud. A neutral third person verified that participants understood the study information and provided consent voluntarily.

Clinical participants were selected using systematic random sampling (selection skip interval: 11), and all patients attending follow-up treatment at the psychiatric outpatient clinic were eligible. For this study, we included psychiatric outpatients with clinical DSM-5 diagnoses of major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder or psychotic spectrum disorders. Seven psychiatric outpatients were excluded from the dataset because they did not have a documented diagnosis. Non-clinical participants were recruited from the attendants, that is, family members or close acquaintances, of psychiatric outpatients. They were screened as healthy based on self-reports and an assessment by a mental health professional.

Study setting

The study took place at the Jimma University Medical Center, which is located in Jimma Town, an urban area located in southwestern Ethiopia, with a population size of around 200 000. Jimma exhibits characteristics of urban and peri-urban life, with strong ties to surrounding rural communities. Livelihoods in the area are diverse, including small-scale trade, informal businesses, service-sector employment, government employment and a large proportion of people involved in agriculture-related work. There is a strong emphasis on religious and spiritual life, with both Muslim and Christian communities, fostering social cohesion and communal gatherings. Neighbours and community members often engage in cooperative practices, such as idir (mutual aid associations for funerals and emergencies) and iqub (rotating savings and credit groups).33

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct or reporting of our research. However, our research questions were informed by clinical experience with patients suffering from various mental disorders, who often report loneliness and ACEs. We chose depressive symptoms as the outcome variable in our mediation analyses because they are commonly experienced by patients across different psychiatric diagnoses and have a profound impact on overall well-being.34,36

Study design

In this comparative cross-sectional study, participants completed the following questionnaires: the UCLA Loneliness Scale,37 the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire Short Form,38 the Social Network Index39 and the WHO-5 Well-Being Index.40 The questionnaires not available in the local languages were translated (forward and back translation process) to Afan Oromo and Amharic.41 Data collection was conducted through interviews led by mental health professionals who had received a 1-day training and were supervised by the study’s local lead investigators (HA and MA).

Measures

Loneliness

Loneliness was assessed using the revised version of the UCLA Loneliness Scale (UCLA), a 20-item self-rating questionnaire that measures the frequency and intensity of feelings of loneliness.37 Response options range from 1=‘never’ to 4=‘often’. The total score was calculated by adding responses after reversing items where needed. Prevalence was defined by moderate (total score ≥28) or high loneliness scores (total score ≥44).42 The internal consistency in this study was good, with Cronbach’s alpha=0.82.

Social network size

The Social Network Index (SNI) was used to measure the participants’ social network sizes and the frequency of contact with other network members.39 The SNI included 12 items (e.g., "How many of your neighbors do you visit or talk to at least once every 2 weeks?"). Each item assessed a different type of relationship (e.g., partner, children, parents, friends, colleagues, neighbors). The total number of people with whom a participant had spoken at least once every 2 weeks represented their social network size.

Adverse childhood experiences

ACEs were assessed using the short form of the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ), which retrospectively measures experiences of abuse and neglect during childhood.38 The CTQ is a 28-item self-report measure with five subscales (emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect and physical neglect), including three validity items assessing minimisation. On a 5-point Likert scale, responses range from 1=‘never true’ to 5=‘very often true’. After reversing items where necessary, responses were added to the subscales and total score. The prevalence of ACEs was defined by a score of ‘moderate to severe’ or ‘severe to extreme’ in at least one of the CTQ subscales (see table 1).43 The internal consistency of the questionnaire in this study was good (Cronbach’s alpha=0.80).

Depressive symptoms

The inverted WHO-5 Well-Being Index (WHO-5) was used to assess depressive symptoms.40 In five items, the amount of time being well is measured on a 6-point Likert scale. The items address core elements of depression including mood, interest and energy and are displayed like so: ‘Over the last two weeks: – I have felt cheerful and in good spirits – All the time=5, (…), At no time=0’. Responses are summed, and the total score is reversed so that higher scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. The WHO-5 is a validated scale for depression40 44 and has been validated in Ethiopia.45 Internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s alpha=0.93).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics V.28 and PROCESS macro V.4.1. Data are given in absolute numbers, percentages, medians and IQRs (IQR: 25th percentile–75th percentile) due to the non-normal distribution of the data.

Preliminary analyses comparing age and gender distribution between the groups were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test and the χ2 test. To compare the group’s levels of ACEs, loneliness and social network size, we used the two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test due to the non-normal nature of the data. To assess the magnitude of the difference between groups, we calculated the effect size |r|=zN following the approach by Fritz et al46 (z=standardised test statistic, N=total sample size). According to Cohen’s interpretation, effect sizes of |r|=0.2, |r|=0.5 and |r|=0.8 correspond to small, medium and large effects, respectively.47 Additionally, we calculated the prevalence of loneliness and ACEs as described in the Measures section.

Spearman’s rank partial correlation was used to calculate relationships between loneliness and ACEs, as well as social network size and ACEs in both groups, controlling for gender.48 We chose this rank-based non-parametric method to fit the non-normal distribution of our data. Significance was assessed using two-tailed tests, and CIs were calculated.49 To interpret the strength of the correlation, we followed Cohen’s guidelines, where coefficients of approximately 0.10–0.29, 0.30–0.49 and 0.50–1.00 are considered small, medium and large, respectively.47 To analyse shared variance, we calculated the coefficient of determination rho2. Correlation strengths were compared between the two groups by calculating the observed z test statistic (zobserved) and assessing significance. In preparation for the z-test, we performed Fisher’s r to z transformation to convert the correlations into a normally distributed measure.50

Finally, we performed simple mediation analyses, a regression-based analysis giving path coefficients for total, direct and indirect effects in both groups. The total effect (c) represents the overall relationship between the independent variable (ACEs) and dependent variable (depressive symptoms). This decomposes into the direct effect (c’=ACEs’ influence on depressive symptoms after accounting for mediator, that is, loneliness or social network size) and the indirect effect (ab=ACEs’ influence on depressive symptoms transmitted through the mediator). As the data lacked normal distribution and homoscedasticity, we performed bootstrapping using 10 000 samples. Heteroscedasticity-consistent CIs and inferential statistics were thus computed.51 At a confidence level of 95%, the confidence intervals that do not include zero indicate significance. We interpreted the mediation analyses according to MacKinnon et al.52

Significance was interpreted at a p<0.05 level. To correct for multiple testing, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg correction with a false discovery rate of 5%.53 There was no missing data for the variables used in the analyses.

We adhered to the reporting guidelines of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist for cross-sectional studies (online supplemental table S2).54

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

The clinical group included 38 individuals diagnosed with major depressive disorder (30%), 35 individuals with bipolar disorder (28%) and 52 individuals with psychotic spectrum disorders (42%). The median duration of illness since diagnosis was 6 years (IQR: 3–9) in the clinical sample. There were no mental disorders in the non-clinical sample according to self-report and assessment by a mental health professional.

Study participants lived in both urban and rural areas: 51% of the clinical participants and 40% of the non-clinical participants lived in Jimma. 49% of the clinical participants and 60% of the non-clinical participants lived in the surrounding rural areas. In the clinical group, 46% of participants completed secondary school or attained a higher level of education. In the non-clinical group, 31% reported the same level of educational attainment. The most frequent occupation was ‘farmer’ (clinical group: 45 (36%), non-clinical group: 52 (40%)), followed by 23 (18%) being ‘merchants’ in the non-clinical group and 22 (18%) being ‘government employed’ in the clinical group. The median income in the clinical group was 28 US dollars per month (IQR: 15–56), while the non-clinical group reported a median income of 38 US dollars per month (IQR: 19–75).

Levels of loneliness, social network size and adverse childhood experiences

In the clinical group, 64.8% of the participants reported moderate or high levels of loneliness, with 13.6% reporting high levels. In contrast, 36.6% of the non-clinical group reported moderate or high loneliness, with 8.4% reporting high levels. Using the continuous scale, Mann-Whitney U test showed that the clinical group scored significantly higher in loneliness (UCLA median=31, IQR: 26–39) than the non-clinical participants (UCLA median=25, IQR: 23–32; p<0.001, |r|=0.30), supporting our first hypothesis (H1).

We found high social network sizes in both groups, with a median of 16 (IQR: 10–23) in the clinical group and 19 (IQR: 11–30) in the non-clinical group. There was no significant difference in social network size between the groups (p=0.049, |r|=0.12).

The prevalence of at least one form of ACEs according to cut-off values by Bernstein and Fink43 was 87.2% in the clinical group and 71.0% in the non-clinical group. In line with H1, the clinical group showed significantly higher total scores of ACEs (CTQ median=45, IQR: 38–55) than the non-clinical group (CTQ median=39, IQR: 36–47; p<0.001, |r|=0.25), (table 3).

Correlations between ACEs and loneliness versus ACEs and social network size did not significantly differ in the clinical group (zobserved=1.49, p=0.069). In the non-clinical group, however, the correlation between ACEs and loneliness was significantly stronger than the correlation between ACEs and social network size (zobserved=2.75, p=0.003).

Loneliness and social network size as mediators between adverse childhood experiences and depressive symptoms

In the clinical group (figure 1A), we found a total effect of ACEs on depressive symptoms (c=0.14, 95% CI (0.04 to 0.25), p=0.008). After entering the two mediators (ie, loneliness and social network size) into the model, ACEs predicted loneliness (a1=0.20, 95% CI (0.04 to 0.36), p=0.015), and loneliness predicted depressive symptoms (b1=0.35, 95% CI (0.26 to 0.45), p<0.001). The direct effect of ACEs on depressive symptoms was not significant anymore (c’=0.07, 95% CI (−0.003 to 0.147), p=0.059). The paths from ACEs to social network size (a2=−0.06, 95% CI (−0.24 to 0.12), p=0.500) and from social network size to depressive symptoms were not significant (b2=−0.02, 95% CI (−0.09 to 0.04), p=0.488). We found that the relationship between ACEs and depressive symptoms was fully mediated by loneliness (indirect effect a1b1=0.07, 95% CI (0.02 to 0.13)), whereas social network size did not mediate the relationship (indirect effect a2b2=0.00, 95% CI (−0.004 to 0.014)).

In the non-clinical group (figure 1B), the total effect of ACEs on depressive symptoms was significant (c=0.07, 95% CI (0.01 to 0.14), p=0.035), though smaller than in the clinical group. Adding both mediators, ACEs predicted loneliness (a1=0.25, 95% CI (0.13 to 0.37), p<0.001), which in turn predicted depressive symptoms (b1=0.13, 95% CI (0.02 to 0.23), p=0.024). The direct effect of ACEs on depressive symptoms was not significant anymore (c’=0.04, 95% CI (−0.02 to 0.10), p=0.198). ACEs did not predict social network size (a2=−0.03, 95% CI (−0.20 to 0.13), p=0.681), and social network size did not predict depressive symptoms (b2=0.01, 95% CI (−0.05 to 0.06), p=0.805). Again, we found that loneliness fully mediated the relationship between ACEs and depressive symptoms (indirect effect a1b1=0.03, 95% CI (0.01 to 0.07)), while social network size did not (indirect effect a2b2=0.00, 95% CI (−0.005 to 0.005)). We could confirm our last hypothesis that loneliness, but not social network size, fully mediated ACEs predicting depressive symptoms in both groups (H4).

Discussion

This study replicates our previous findings on the association between loneliness and ACEs in independent clinical and non-clinical samples with a different sociocultural background in Ethiopia. Perceived loneliness, and not social network size in adulthood, was associated with ACEs. Furthermore, loneliness fully mediated the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms, while social network size did not. Both findings are consistent with results of our previous study conducted with psychiatric inpatients and a non-clinical group in Germany.24 In the previous study, loneliness partially mediated the relationship between ACEs and depressive symptoms in the clinical group and fully mediated this relationship in the non-clinical group. To our knowledge, this is the first transcultural confirmatory study in a collectivistic and low-income country investigating the association between loneliness and ACEs. Our results support the notion that loneliness is generally related to self-reported ACEs (Reinhard et al. 202224) and that this association might constitute a transdiagnostic and universal risk factor for depressive symptoms.

Moreover, our study, like our previous one, highlights the importance of distinguishing loneliness from social isolation, as loneliness was associated with ACEs, whereas social network size was not. Loneliness refers to a negative emotional state that is strongly influenced by the perceived quality of relationships.55 56 In contrast, social network size is a quantitative measure and may be considered less affected by subjective attitudes, even when assessed via a self-report questionnaire. In this study, loneliness fully mediated the association between ACEs and depressive symptoms in both the clinical and non-clinical group, whereas social network size was not a significant mediator. These findings suggest that loneliness is more closely linked to the psychopathological pathway to depressive symptoms than its objective counterpart, social network size. Recent literature supports these findings.23 57 58 For instance, Rodriguez et al58 found that subjective feelings of isolation were more strongly associated with depressive symptoms than the number of social relations among Kenyan adolescents.

While our previous study was conducted in Germany24—a high-income and individualistic society (Hofstede individualism score: 79)—the present study was conducted in Ethiopia, a low-income country and collectivistic society with a much lower individualism score of 7.59 60 According to Hofstede,61 collectivism describes the degree to which people are integrated into groups. People in collectivistic cultures are focused on the group and prefer having a tight, loyal social network. In contrast, people in individualistic cultures value individual goals over group goals and typically have looser social networks.61 62 Consistent with this cultural framework, we found comparably high median social network sizes in both study groups (16 in the clinical group and 19 in the non-clinical group), reflecting a high degree of social embeddedness in the Ethiopian context. In our previous study conducted in Germany, the social network size had a mean of 14.8 in the non-clinical group and 9.4 in the clinical group.24 Data on loneliness in Ethiopia are extremely limited. However, the prevalence of loneliness in our non-clinical group (37%) was comparable to that reported among postpartum Ethiopian women (40.9%) in a study conducted in Gondar city.63 The percentage of our clinical participants experiencing moderate or high levels of loneliness was even higher (65%). These findings suggest that loneliness represents a relevant burden in the general population and is particularly pronounced among individuals with mental disorders—despite the high social network size and collectivistic societal structure. This discrepancy might be partly explained by the widespread stigmatisation of people with mental disorders, which likely contributes to increased feelings of loneliness.64 65

One can assume that loneliness is a culturally shaped experience, especially considering the cultural dimension of individualism vs collectivism. By integrating Peplau’s definition of loneliness (‘cognitive discrepancy’ between actual and desired social relationships3) and Hofstede’s definition of individualism/collectivism (‘degree to which people in a society are integrated into groups’61), higher subjective standards for relationships in collectivistic societies may result in loneliness more often.66 Previous research supports this theory, revealing a higher prevalence of loneliness in collectivistic compared with individualistic European countries.66,69 Interestingly, Heu et al’s70 studies in five European countries present a more complex picture by assessing collectivism/individualism on the individual level: in these studies, higher individual-level collectivism was associated with reduced loneliness. They also examined the discrepancy between desired and actual social embeddedness, which was associated with loneliness but not with collectivism. The high social embeddedness in individuals who describe their social environment as collectivistic might buffer the desired-actual discrepancies, as interpreted by the authors. Still, in line with the existing evidence, mean levels of loneliness were significantly higher in more collectivistic countries (Italy, Portugal) compared with individualistic countries (Sweden, the Netherlands) in the general population.70 Likewise, the loneliness levels we observed in our study in Ethiopia were high both in the clinical and the non-clinical group. Finally, not only collectivism but also income levels in Ethiopia may influence loneliness. Peltzer and Pengpid71 found that loneliness was predicted by living in a low-income country. This country-level factor might also be influenced by the individual-level factor of low income, which has been associated with loneliness.72 73

Taken together, both sociocultural and economic conditions may influence loneliness in ways that have not yet been fully understood. As a central mental health issue for both clinical and non-clinical populations, exploring loneliness and its interplay with cultural and individual factors in future research is crucial—especially in under-represented countries like Ethiopia. The relevance of factors such as urbanisation, living arrangements and spirituality or religion in this context remains largely unknown. Moreover, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to provide more robust evidence for the causal interaction between ACEs, loneliness and psychopathology as hypothesised here.

Our study nonetheless highlights loneliness as a global health burden and critical target for intervention to improve mental health and well-being. Addressing loneliness could be a promising approach to preventing mental health problems at both societal and individual levels, especially for those with a history of ACEs or high loneliness levels. In such interventions, the perception of loneliness should be targeted, rather than focusing solely on social skills,58 as supported by two meta-analyses showing greater success with cognitive approaches.74 75 However, the effectiveness of interventions may differ depending on the cultural context. Furthermore, interventions must be adapted to the resource setting. For example, recent studies show promising results for internet-delivered cognitive behavioural therapy against loneliness, presenting a cost-effective approach.76 77 A culturally sensitive adaptation of such interventions, guided by local researchers, is essential for developing effective strategies to address loneliness across diverse cultural contexts.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations: first, the cross-sectional design of our study only allows the detection of associations but limits any interpretation in terms of causality. Second, the non-clinical sample was not population-based and representative, as it was drawn from individuals accompanying the psychiatric outpatients. Therefore, a sampling bias may have occurred. Third, most questionnaires were translated but have not yet been validated in the regional languages (Afan Oromo and Amharic). Back-translation provides basic translation quality; however, the questionnaires cannot claim the same degree of validity as validated measures.41 Fourth, we limited our mediation analysis to depressive symptoms as an outcome variable, even if, for example, psychotic symptoms would have been relevant in our clinical sample. Finally, subjective biases in the self-report measure CTQ may have influenced our findings, and one may argue that objective ACEs records would have been required. However, Danese and Widom78 found that the severity of psychopathology is rather associated with subjective measures of ACEs than with ACEs according to court documentation. The authors suggest that the subjective experience of early life adversities may be pivotal in the development of psychopathology, so self-report measures of ACEs are nevertheless relevant. Fifth, direct comparison with our previous German study was difficult due to differing methodological approaches. For example, we used median measures because of a skewed distribution, whereas the German sample was analysed using mean measures.

Overall, we wish to emphasise that, given the methodological limitations of our study, these findings should be considered preliminary and encourage future research.

Conclusion

To conclude, loneliness in adulthood appears to be associated with reported ACEs not only in individualistic, high-income countries but also in the collectivistic, low-income context of Ethiopia. Moreover, loneliness may play a key role in mediating the relationship between ACEs and depressive symptoms in adulthood. If this represents a transdiagnostically and transculturally valid relationship, loneliness should be a key target for preventive mental health interventions at both the individual and societal level.
