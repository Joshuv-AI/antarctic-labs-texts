Phonon Directionality Impacts Electron–Phonon Coupling and Polarization of the Band-Edge Emission in Two-Dimensional Metal Halide Perovskites
Authors: Roman Krahne, Alexander Schleusener, Mehrdad Faraji, Lin-Han Li, Miao-Ling Lin, Ping-Heng Tan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo-dimensional metal halide perovskites are highly versatile for light-driven applications due to their exceptional variety in material composition, which can be exploited for the tunability of mechanical and optoelectronic properties. The band-edge emission is defined by the structure and composition of both organic and inorganic layers, and electron–phonon coupling plays a crucial role in the recombination dynamics. However, the nature of the electron–phonon coupling and what kind of phonons are involved are still under debate. Here we investigate the emission, reflectance, and phonon response from single two-dimensional lead iodide microcrystals with angle-resolved polarized spectroscopy. We find an intricate dependence of the emission polarization with the vibrational directionality in the materials, which reveals that several bands of low-frequency phonons with nonorthogonal directionality contribute to the band-edge emission. Such complex electron–phonon coupling requires adequate models to predict the thermal broadening of the emission and provides opportunities to design polarization properties.Keywords: two-dimensional perovskite, excitons, phonons, photoluminescence, Raman spectroscopy, reflectivity

Two-dimensional (2D) layered organic–inorganic perovskites have alternating subnanometer thin semiconductor layers consisting of a single plane of inorganic metal halide octahedra with dielectric layers formed by the organic cations.1 This leads to strong confinement of the electronic carriers, which renders such systems as natural quantum wells with binding energies for electron–hole pairs (excitons) on the order of several hundred meV.2−5 These properties are highly appealing for light emission and manipulation in particular because the emission can be tailored by the choice of the halide and, to some extent, by the composition of the organic layers, which influence the structural and electrical properties of the material.6−8 Here the static distortions induced by the binding of the organic cations to the octahedra layer have a significant impact on the crystal structure and electron–phonon coupling.6 Furthermore, the organic layer defines the dielectric confinement of the charge carriers in the semiconductor metal halide lattice. Several recent works investigated the confinement effects on the exciton fine structure9−13 and provided a detailed understanding with insights on crystal symmetry, polar distortions, and other structural property effects.11,12,14−18 However, the influence of electron–phonon coupling in the recombination dynamics has been much less investigated, and which mechanisms and phonon bands contribute to the band-edge emission is under debate. For example, a series of equally spaced peaks in the emission spectra have been attributed to a vibronic progression due to coupling to phonons of organic cations,19−21 while other works observed emission dynamics that were related to polaron formation.22−26

In this work, we correlate the band-edge exciton emission of single two-dimensional lead iodide perovskite microcrystals with their vibrational properties, where we measure the phonon response by Raman spectroscopy under resonant (above band gap) and nonresonant (below band gap) excitation. We record angle-resolved polarized photoluminescence (PL), reflectance, and Raman spectra from the same region of single, regularly shaped 2D microcrystals, where we resolve emission peaks with a width of less than 0.5 meV and measure the low-frequency Raman response down to 8 cm–1 (equivalent to 1 meV). This allows us to directly correlate the in-plane polarization of the different exciton emission bands with the directionality of the phonon modes of the inorganic octahedra lattice. We find a distinct polarization of the different emission peaks that is strongly connected to the directionality of the low-frequency phonon bands. The phonon spectrum and directionality of the vibrational modes depend on the type of organic cation in the 2D crystal. In particular, in some materials, dominant phonon modes occur along the principal directions of the octahedra lattice and along their diagonals, which enables contributions of combinations of phonons with different directionality in the electron–phonon coupling.

Results and Discussion

For reflectance measurements, a white light source is used instead of the laser. In the experiments, we first performed the PL and reflectance measurements that required much shorter integration time (fractions of a second) and less excitation power, and then we performed the Raman experiments, in which the signal was integrated over several tens of seconds and, if needed, measured with higher excitation power. In all cases, we confirmed that the beam exposure did not lead to degradation or damage of the sample by optical imaging of the microcrystals and inspection of their optical spectra.

As metal halide perovskite samples, we chose two-dimensional lead iodide crystals in the Ruddlesden–Popper phase with butylammonium (BA), undecylammonium (UDA), and phenylethylammonium (PEA) as organic cations. BA2PbI4 is the most studied two-dimensional layered perovskite (2DLP) system with an aliphatic chain cation, and UDA2PbI4 provides insight into the impact of the aliphatic chain length on optoelectronic properties. PEA2PbI4 is the archetype of a 2D perovskite with a phenyl ring in the organic portion that leads to different properties of the organic layer, for example, in its stiffness. Furthermore, the above materials have different symmetries in their low-temperature phase (see the Supporting Information (SI), Section 1 for a discussion of their structural properties).

The angle-resolved PL spectra measured from a single BA2PbI4 flake are shown in Figure 1C. We clearly observe a maximum in the overall emission intensity at an angle of around 150° and a minimum at around 60°. These angles correspond to the orientation of the flake, and therefore, we can associate the main emission intensity roughly to be along one of the major axes of the octahedra lattice. Gaussian fitting of the emission spectra reveals the contributions of two bands to the main peak, as depicted in Figure 1D, where the spectra recorded at 60° and 150° are displayed. The emission at 150° is characterized by a Gaussian peak (P2) at 2.534 eV with a width of 4 meV (we take as the Gaussian peak width twice the standard deviation) that has a broader shoulder fitted by Gaussian (6 meV width) on its low energy side, which we label P1. The spectrum at 60° consists of three Gaussian peaks, two with similar narrow widths of around 4 meV, and a low energy shoulder with a 7 meV width. We associate the peaks with ca. 4 meV widths to the confined band-edge excitons since their polarization behavior in the PL and reflectance data (see Figure S4) agree well with reports in the literature.9,18,27,28 We focus on the peaks labeled P2 and P3 that show orthogonal behavior in intensity with respect to the polarization angle. Figure 1E shows that P3 decreases from 60° to 150°, while P2 increases. Therefore, we assign P2 and P3 to the confined excitons along the orthogonal in-plane directions of the major axes of the 2D single crystal. The resonant Raman spectra recorded from the same region are depicted in Figure 1F. Dominant modes are found at 25 and 49 cm–1, which have their intensity maxima at the same angles as the PL maxima of peaks P2 and P3 (indicated by the white dashed lines in Figure 1C,F). We therefore conclude that these low-frequency phonons, which correspond to octahedral twists (25 cm–1) and Pb–I bond bending and stretching (49 cm–1),22,29−33 are fundamentally involved in the emission dynamics. To summarize, the regular BA2PbI4 flake manifests two emission bands, P2 and P3, that have their intensity maxima at orthogonal angles and that can be associated with the in-plane confined exciton modes along the major axes of the octahedra lattice. Intensity maxima of the low-frequency phonon modes occur at the same angles as the emission intensity maxima, pointing to a strong impact of electron–phonon coupling with these low-frequency vibrational modes in the recombination dynamics, in agreement with the literature.26

We now turn to UDA2PbI4 that features an organic cation with a longer aliphatic chain (11 carbons), which leads to a thicker organic layer and stronger dielectric confinement. Accordingly, the emission of UDA2PbI4 is blue-shifted with respect to BA2PbI4, as shown in Figure S2 in the SI. Furthermore, from the literature we expect the low-temperature crystal phase of UDA2PbI4 to be monoclinic, with a stronger out-of-plane octahedra tilting.34

Figure 2A displays the angle-resolved PL of a single UDA2PbI4 flake. In contrast to BA2PbI4, all emission peaks in the UDA2PbI4 spectrum have their intensity maximum at the same angle, at around 90°, and we observe a more complex emission spectrum that contains a large number of peaks. Focusing on the two main bands that we label as P1 and P2, we can distinguish differences in the strength of the intensity increase with changing angle: P1 is slightly weaker than P2 at 0°, which is reversed at 90° by the strong angle-dependent increase in the intensity of P1 (displayed in detail in Figure S6). The polarization dependence of the emission peaks with a single angular maximum goes along with phonon modes under resonant excitation that manifest no significant angle-dependent intensity variation; see Figure S7. However, we find a very interesting angle-dependent behavior in the Raman spectra recorded nonresonantly with excitation below the band gap at 633 nm, as shown in Figure 2B. Here we observe a very rich phonon spectrum with a large number of peaks below 120 cm–1 (15 meV), where a set of prominent modes have their maxima at 30° and 120°; another set of strong modes have their maximum at 75°, and again another set has two maxima at 75° and 165°. These findings are in very good agreement with our data from UDA2PbBr4 microcrystals,33 and with the regular shape of the microcrystal displayed in the inset of Figure 2B, we can assign the modes that peak at 75° vibrations along the [110] direction of the crystal lattice and those at 165° to the orthogonal [−110] direction. Most interestingly, the sets at 30° and 120° are assigned to diagonal lattice directions along [100] and [010]. We note that for some microcrystals, such a diagonal lattice direction can also be distinguished by well-defined 45° angles of their edges (see Figure S8 for an example). One interesting point is that phonon modes that differ by 45° angles have nonzero vector products, and therefore, coupled modes can participate in the optical recombination processes. This enables a new range of electron–phonon coupling processes that go beyond the typically considered Fröhlich coupling involving only one longitudinal optical phonon mode.35 Furthermore, the coupling of phonon modes along different in-plane lattice directions can lead to a single angle-dependent emission intensity maximum, in agreement with our data in Figure 2A.

The angle-dependent polarized reflectance and PL spectra of a different UDA2PbI4 microcrystal are shown in Figure 3 (see Figure S9 for the full angular data set). Here the orientation of the microcrystal with the vertical axis is around 45°, and accordingly, we observe the minima and maxima in PL intensity at 45° and 150°, respectively. In reflectance, we find a series of minima indicated by the dashed black lines that shift in spectral position with the in-plane polarization angle. Only the minimum marked by the magenta dashed line (at 2.576 eV) does not change with the angle, and therefore, we attribute it to the exciton state confined in the vertical (z) direction. Since the fine structure splitting is expected to be only a few meV,18 we attribute the small dip at 2.573 eV to the confined exciton state in the y-direction, which is corroborated by its decrease in depth with decreasing angle, while at the same time a minimum at 2.571 eV develops, which consequently can be assigned to the x-confined exciton state.27,28 The other three more pronounced dips in the reflectance could be related to exciton polaritons that originate from the open cavity formed by the microcrystal itself, as reported in ref (36). In this scenario, the red-shift with decreasing angle would result from the difference of the confined exciton energies in the two orthogonal directions.

The strong PL band centered at 2.545 eV does not have a counterpart in reflectance and does not shift in energy with the in-plane polarization angle. Thus, we assign it to a phonon sideband of the band-edge emission. Its spectral shape resembles that of the (polariton) peak at 2.558 eV, and thus, it is reasonable to assume that it is a phonon replica of this emission band, which would result in an energy spacing of ca. 15 meV (120 cm–1). We do not observe any phonon bands at such energy in our Raman experiments (Figure 2B); however, this energy could be explained by the sum of the dominant phonon energies (around 60 cm–1) that occur in directions shifted by 45° angles. Therefore, such a large energy shift supports our reasoning that combinations (vector products) of the directional phonons are involved in the electron–phonon coupling.

We also investigated the emission, reflectance, and vibrational properties of single PEA2PbI4 flakes and report the PL and Raman spectra in Section 4 in the Supporting Information. Overall, the behavior of PEA2PbI4 is similar to that of UDA2PbI4, featuring intensity maxima of the emission peaks at a single polarization angle and phonon bands with a strong angular intensity variation under nonresonant excitation in the Raman spectra that comprise dominant modes in the [110] and [100] crystal lattice directions (Figures S10–S12). The main emission band shifts in energy with the in-plane polarization angle, in agreement with the literature,9 and we can identify the confined exciton modes in the x- and y-directions with reflectance measurements (see Figure S13), as reported in ref (28). Interestingly, for some microcrystals, we observe two additional strong sharp peaks in the emission spectrum, and their energy spacing of 9.4 meV corresponds very well to the energy of the out-of-plane phonon mode; see Figure S10. Therefore, we conclude that these sharp peaks originate from a vibronic progression related to this out-of-plane phonon mode that is favored by the stiffer organic layer in PEA2PbI4, in agreement with the literature.20,21 This interpretation is corroborated by the weak angular intensity dependence of both these sharp emission peaks and the out-of-plane phonon mode.

Figure 4 reports temperature-dependent PL experiments in the range from 4 to 80 K that reveal how the band-edge emission observed at 4 K evolves from a single broad peak at 80 K for all samples. That the exciton fine structure emerges in the temperature range below 80 K underpins the key role of the low-frequency phonons with energies below 10 meV in the emission dynamics. With decreasing temperature, we observe a red-shift of the main emission band for BA2PbI4 (Figure 4A) and PEA2PbI4 (Figure 4C,D), in agreement with the literature,37,38 while for UDA2PbI4 (Figure 4B), the broad peak observed in the range from 60–80 K splits into a set of sharper peaks below 40 K. For the systems built with organic cations with an aliphatic chain (BA2PbI4 and UDA2PbI4), the emission intensity increases significantly with decreasing temperature. This is different for PEA2PbI4, where the emission intensity has a maximum at around 50 K, a local minimum at 20 K, and then again a relatively strong emission intensity at 4 K. Such behavior can be explained by the involvement of phonons with different energies in the emission process, namely the out-of-plane phonon at 9.4 meV (77 cm–1) and the low-frequency in-plane phonons in the range from 2.5–8 meV (20–70 cm–1). The weak dependence of the emission intensity at temperatures below 30 K for PEA2PbI4 indicates that the impact of the in-plane phonons to the thermal coupling of the exciton band-edge states is much less compared to BA2PbI4 and UDA2PbI4. Accordingly, we do not find a low-frequency signal in the resonant Raman spectra recorded from the PEA2PbI4 flake; see Figure S10.

The insets show the emission at 80 K on a linear scale. (C, D) Color plot and emission spectra at different temperatures of a single PEA2PbI4 flake.

In conclusion, our combined study of the angle-resolved photoluminescence and Raman spectra of single two-dimensional lead iodide microcrystals evidenced the strong correlation of low-frequency phonons with the band-edge emission. In particular, the exciton polarization is not isotropic in the plane of the octahedra lattice, as would be expected from the electronic structure,13 highlighting the strong influence of the exciton–phonon coupling. Here subtle differences in the organic cation, like a change in the length of the aliphatic chain, can lead to very different emission polarization and PL line width broadening. Our results reveal the complexity of electron–phonon coupling and provide detailed insight into how the directionality of the phonons impacts the polarization and intensity of the band-edge emission. In particular, we find evidence that combinations of phonons with nonorthogonal directionality, along the normal [110] and diagonal [100] directions, participate in the electron–phonon coupling. One consequence is that the typically applied Bose–Einstein modeling of the thermal broadening of the PL, which is based on a single LO phonon energy,35,39 is not adequate to describe the thermal behavior of the 2DLPs. Furthermore, such multiphonon coupling can alter the direction of the polarization of the emitted light. These insights on how phonon directionality impacts exciton polarization are important for designing photonic devices, in particular, where the orientation of the emission dipoles is crucial for their performance, for example, in optical cavities and other resonators. Considering the complexity of the structural and vibrational properties of two-dimensional metal halide materials, approaches using machine learning and neural-network-based pattern recognition could be suitable tools to predict their optoelectronic behavior.

Experimental Section

Materials

Lead(II) iodide (PbI2, 99%), hydroiodic acid (HI, 57%, distilled, 99.999% trace metal basis), hypophosphorous acid (H3PO2, 50%), phenethylamine (PEA, 99%), undecylamine (UDA, ≥98%), butylamine (BA, 99.5%), ethyl acetate (≥99.5%), and toluene (≥99.7%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich without any further purification.

Sample Fabrication

Microcrystalline powders of BA2PbI4, UDA2PbI4, and PEA2PbI4 were synthesized by a modified antisolvent-assisted fast crystallization approach published previously by our group.40 Briefly, 0.25 mmol of PbI2 was dissolved in 200 μL of HI and 108 μL of H3PO2, followed by dilution with 2 mL of ethyl acetate for UDA2PbI4 and PEA2PbI4 and 2 mL of ethyl acetate and 1 mL of toluene for BA2PbI4. Afterward, 0.6 mmol of the respective amine was directly injected into the mixture, leading to the immediate crystallization of orange microcrystals. The reaction mixture was continuously agitated for at least 3 h. Subsequently, the microcrystalline powder was separated from the solution by centrifugation at 6000 rpm, followed by redispersion in the respective solvent system. The washing procedure was repeated twice, and the purified crystals were dried under vacuum for 1 h.

Angle-Resolved PL, Reflectance, and Raman Experiments

Raman experiments were performed under resonant conditions with a wavelength of 457 nm from an Ar+ laser and nonresonant conditions with a wavelength of 633 nm from a He–Ne laser. The spectra were collected in backscattering geometry using a Jobin–Yvon HR800 micro-Raman system equipped with a charged-coupled detector (CCD) and a 50× objective (numerical aperture (NA) of 0.55) with a long working distance. 2400 lines/mm gratings were used, yielding a spectral resolution of 0.19 cm–1 per CCD pixel under 633 nm excitation. The laser plasma lines were removed by Bragg volume grating-based bandpass filters (BPFs) from OptiGrate Corp, and the Rayleigh line was suppressed using three BragGrate notch filters (OptiGrate Corp.). The samples were placed in a closed cycle cryostat (Montana Instruments) and cooled to cryogenic temperatures below 10K. For angle-resolved Raman measurements, two vertical polarizers and one rotational half-wave plate were utilized. One polarizer was inserted in the incident path, and one analyzer with parallel polarization was allocated in the scattered path before the spectrometer. The half-wave plate was positioned in the common optical path of the incident and scattered light to simultaneously alter their polarization directions. Rotating the fast axis of the half-wave plate by an angle of φ/2 is equivalent to a rotation of the sample by an angle of φ relative to the polarization directions of the polarizer and analyzer.41 The laser power was kept lower than 40 μW to avoid laser-induced degradation of the sample. For the temperature-dependent measurements, the sample was first cooled to 4 K and then the temperature was raised stepwise to the indicated values. The angle-resolved photoluminescence spectra were recorded with the Raman setup using a grating with 600 lines/mm and a excitation at a 457 nm wavelength from an Ar+ laser. For PL measurements, the laser power was lower than 0.1 μW. The reflectance experiments were conducted with the same setup, where the laser was replaced by a white light source.
