Characterizing U.S. community well-being indices: a methodological synthesis and measurement systems perspective
Authors: Julie K. Alperen, Michael LaValley, Pengsheng Ni, Tara Tripp, Danielle Emig, Prasad Patil, Shariq Mohammed, Abhi Jain, Michael Rickles, Elizabeth Colyer, Brandeus Davis, Lisa M. Sullivan, Michael Acker, Kimberly A. Dukes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe proliferation of community well-being indices (CWBI) reflects growing recognition that both individual experiences and place-based conditions shape population health and quality of life. These indices are increasingly used to inform public health policy, community planning, and population health monitoring. However, there is substantial variation across indices in how community well-being is conceptualized, measured, and reported. Despite their expanding use, few studies have systematically compared these indices as integrated measurement systems. This study aimed to characterize and compare CWBI using a measurement systems perspective.MethodsWe conducted a structured, purposive review of U.S.-based CWBI to characterize approaches using five methodological components describing domain and indicator selection, statistical estimation and weighting, construction and geographic aggregation, evaluation of measurement properties, and dissemination. Evaluation of measurement properties was characterized based on reported evidence of reliability, validity, and responsiveness. The Boston University-Sharecare Community Well-being Index (BU-SC CWBI) was included as an illustrative example.ResultsOf the 315 measures identified, 11 met inclusion criteria and were characterized, along with the BU-SC CWBI. Across systems, the number of domains ranged from 2 to 10 (median 5), and the number of indicators ranged from 11 to 93 (median 43). Collectively, indices incorporated 86 distinct data sources drawn primarily from publicly available administrative and surveillance datasets; two systems also incorporated individual-level subjective measures. Although not mutually exclusive, systems were heuristically classified into three broad methodological orientations that reflect a continuum of priorities with respect to conceptual organization, aggregation approaches, and the use of empirical and model-based methods. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in statistical estimation, weighting, and evaluation of measurement properties, with limited and inconsistent reporting of reliability, validity, and related evaluation analyses.ConclusionsCWBI differ in both indicators used to represent well-being and methodological approaches used to construct and evaluate them. Conceptualizing these indices as measurement systems provides a structured approach for comparing approaches, clarifying trade-offs, and improving interpretability and comparability. While CWBI may be designed for different goals and applied in different contexts, a systematic approach to their characterization may facilitate transparency and more informed use in research and policy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12963-026-00502-4.

Background

With recognition of the profound influence of place-based factors on health and well-being [1] there is increasing interest in developing assessments that evaluate whether communities provide the conditions necessary for individuals to reach their full health and well-being potential. While a universal definition of community well-being remains elusive [2], measurement approaches may integrate subjective individual assessments and community context, combining individual behaviors, resources, and structural conditions to more comprehensively characterize health and opportunity [3–5], and there is substantial overlap in the domains and conditions considered important for supporting population health and well-being. Despite this synergy, substantial variation exists in how community well-being is conceptualized, measured, and reported across existing indices, and there has been limited systematic characterization and comparison from a measurement systems perspective.

Composite indices, which aggregate multiple indicators across conceptual domains into a single summary measure, are widely used in health research to operationalize multidimensional constructs. For decades, familiar indices like the County Health Rankings and Roadmaps (CHR&R) [6] and America’s Health Rankings (AHR) [7] have quantified place-based well-being across U.S. communities. Recognizing the potential value of a similar metric that integrates both publicly available population-level contextual data and extensive individual-level, subjective well-being data across multiple domains, our team at the Boston University (BU) School of Public Health partnered with the digital health company Sharecare (SC) in 2019. This collaboration led to the development of the Boston University-Sharecare Community Well-Being Index (BU-SC CWBI) [8].

Measurement theory emphasizes that complex constructs, such as community well-being, require careful specification, operationalization, and evaluation of the underlying measurement model. Established guidance on multidimensional composite index development from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) [9] describes key components of index development, including theoretical framework development, indicator selection, multivariate analysis, missing data treatment, normalization, weighting and aggregation, robustness assessment, and dissemination. Similarly, the National Committee on Vital and Health Statistics (NCVHS) [5], 100 Million Healthier Lives [10, 11], and the National Academy of Medicine (NAM) [12] have provided complementary guidance on the selection of domains and indicators for community well-being measurement. Collectively, these sources emphasize the importance of multidimensional measurement across domains such as health, social conditions, economic stability, and environment, and offer a richer characterization of community well-being [13–18]. By contrast, relatively little attention has been devoted to systematically characterizing and comparing how these methodological principles are implemented across existing community well-being indices. Importantly, differences across community well-being indices do not necessarily imply methodological deficiencies or a need for harmonization. Variation may reflect legitimate differences in conceptual definitions, intended uses, populations of interest, geographic and cultural contexts, and data availability. Rather than advocating for a single framework or standardized approach, greater understanding of how methodological decisions and their implications across systems may facilitate interpretation, transparency, and comparison. A measurement systems perspective provides a useful lens for examining community well-being indices as integrated systems composed of multiple interrelated methodological components, including domain and indicator selection, statistical estimation, aggregation, evaluation, and dissemination. Applying this perspective enables a structured comparison of how community well-being indices are constructed, operationalized, evaluated and interpreted in practice.

Measurement of community well-being is also shaped by the characteristics of the data selected to represent each conceptual domain. For example, subjective, self-reported assessments of well-being (e.g., life satisfaction, perceived health status), typically collected via self-administered surveys, capture lived experiences and perceptions but may lack representativeness. In contrast, objective, externally observed or administratively collected measures of social, economic, or environmental conditions that describe aggregate characteristics across groups by geographic units (e.g., counties or ZIP codes) can provide broader spatial and population coverage but lack insight into the individual experience. Decisions regarding the integration of subjective and objective data fundamentally influence construct representation and interpretability. These distinctions represent important dimensions of measurement that are often treated differently across community well-being indices.

Building upon methodological principles commonly described in measurement theory, composite indicator guidance, and community well-being frameworks, this manuscript applies a measurement systems perspective to descriptively characterize and compare community well-being indices across five core methodological dimensions: domain and indicator selection; statistical estimation and weighting; index construction, standardization, and geographic aggregation; evaluation of measurement properties; and transparency, dissemination, and use. We identified a set of conceptually similar community well-being indices and conducted a methodological and conceptual synthesis. Our primary objective was to characterize each selected community well-being index across the five methodological dimensions identified through a measurement systems perspective. Viewing community well-being indices as integrated measurement systems highlights methodological decisions that shape how well-being is represented, quantified and interpreted. This perspective provides a structured approach for comparing community well-being indices based on their underlying methodological components rather than their resulting scores, ranks, or classifications. We use the BU-SC CWBI as an illustrative example and comparator that integrates individual-level subjective well-being data with place-based contextual indicators. By examining multiple community well-being indices side by side, we highlight patterns, methodological trade-offs, and areas of divergence across existing approaches to inform future directions in population-level community well-being assessment.

Methods

Overview

To apply the measurement systems perspective described above, we characterize and compare community well-being indices across five methodological dimensions. The dimensions were used to identify and describe key methodological design choices that shape how well-being is conceptualized, represented, quantified, and reported across systems [19, 20]. A measurement system consists of interrelated methodological decisions that translate an underlying construct into observable measures. We distinguish between the underlying construct of community well-being, its organization into conceptual domains, the indicators used to represent those domains, and the composite indices used to summarize them. Rather than evaluating or ranking indices, we focus on identifying and describing key design, analytic, and dissemination decisions that define each index as a measurement system. These dimensions include (1) domain and indicator selection; (2) statistical estimation, and weighting; (3) index construction, standardization, geographic aggregation; (4) evaluation of measurement properties; and (5) transparency, and dissemination and use strategies (Fig. 1).

Conceptual architecture of community well-being measurement systems

Given the lack of standardized terminology for characterizing index components, different indices often use varying terms to describe similar elements. For consistency, we use the term indicator to refer to individually measured variables, domain to refer to conceptual dimensions of well-being represented by groups of indicators, and index to refer to aggregated scores summarizing indicators or domains.

This section describes the approach used to identify and select community well-being indices for inclusion in this study. We outline the criteria used for inclusion and define the five methodological dimensions used to characterize and compare each index (Fig. 1). For context, we first describe the development of the BU-SC CWBI as an illustrative example of how these dimensions are reflected within a community well-being measurement system.

Boston University-Sharecare Community Well-Being Index

We developed the BU-SC CWBI as a hybrid measurement system to quantify community well-being by integrating subjective, individual-level well-being data with objective, place-based indicators reflecting social determinants of health (SDOH). The BU-SC CWBI comprises two interrelated components: the Well-Being Index (WBI) and the SDOH Index, together representing a structured system of measurement spanning two domains and 10 subdomains.

The conceptual structure of the BU-SC CWBI is illustrated in Fig. 2. The WBI captures five subjective dimensions of well-being, including physical health, financial, purpose, community, and social support. The SDOH Index captures five complementary contextual domains, including economic security, resource access, food access, healthcare access, and housing and transportation. These domains are conceptually aligned to reflect both individual experience and structural conditions and are integrated to produce a composite measure of community well-being. This dual-domain architecture forms the basis of the community well-being index measurement system and guides indicator selection, domain construction, and composite index development.

BU-Sharecare community well-being index (BU-SC CWBI) structure and domains

The measurement system application for the BU-SC CWBI includes the following integrated stages: collection and harmonization of individual-level well-being data and construction of SDOH measures; application of statistical methods, including multiple imputation and small area estimation to generate stable population-level estimates of individual well-being, and weighting; standardization, aggregation of indicators, and spatial linkage to produce domain-specific and composite indices of community well-being; evaluation of internal consistency, reliability, and construct and criterion validity; and dissemination of results and methods through State and Metropolitan Statistical Area Ranking reports, interactive maps, and scientific publications.

Self-reported measures of well-being, domains and indices were derived from Sharecare’s RealAge Test™ [21], a validated mortality risk measure. The WBI contains a subset of items from the RealAge Test™ and is a derivative of the Gallup Well-Being 5 Index [22], a validated population health metric. Approximately 400,000 to 500,000 individuals self-report data annually through the digital RealAge Test™, with supplemental survey modalities used to support data collection. Individual-level responses include ZIP code information, which are mapped to the ZIP Code Tabulation Areas (ZCTAs) and aggregated to the ZCTA level for integration with SDOH indices. For ZIP codes that span multiple counties, population-based weighting is applied to assign individuals proportionally across counties, ensuring accurate geographic representation.

The SDOH Index was developed using publicly available census-based data sources, including datasets from the U.S. Census Bureau, U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, the Institute of Museum and Library Services, OpenStreetMap, and Data Axle. These measures represent objective, contextual indicators of community-level conditions, in contrast to individual-level self-reported measures. Candidate indicators were identified based on literature review and expert input and aligned with established public health frameworks, including the CDC Healthy People 2020 framework [23]. Indicators were screened for national availability, distributional properties, and conceptual relevance. Redundant measures were removed using correlation thresholds, and principal component analysis was applied to reduce dimensionality while preserving variance, resulting in 17 indicators across 5 domains. SDOH measures were initially constructed at varying geographic resolutions, including tract-level data, and harmonized to common spatial units to support integration with individual-level well-being data.

The county was selected as the primary unit of analysis due to sample size considerations and the need to integrate individual-level and contextual data within a consistent geographic framework. A multi-step statistical estimation process was applied to generate county-level measures. This included multiple imputation to address missing demographics and well-being data, and small area estimation methods to produce stable estimates in counties with limited or no direct observations. The methods account for differences in population composition and mitigate potential bias arising from non-representative data coverage. Measures were standardized to reflect county demographic profiles and ensure comparability across geographic areas.

Within WBI and SDOH components, indicator processing and aggregation followed distinct methodological approaches. For the WBI, individual-level responses were scored using a predefined algorithm incorporating domain-specific indictors and weighting procedures to produce domain-level and overall well-being indices. These were then aggregated to the county level and standardized to ensure comparability across geographic units. For the SDOH Index, indicators were standardized prior to aggregation. Domain specific SDOH indices were calculated by averaging standardized indicators within each domain, and the domain scores were combined using equal weighting across the five domains to produce the overall SDOH index. County-level BU-SC CWBI scores were calculated by integrating the standardized WBI and SDOH domain scores using equal weighting, reflecting the combination of subjective well-being and structural determinants. These measures were subsequently aggregated to metropolitan statistical area and state levels using population-based weighting to reflect underlying population distributions and support broader population-level reporting.

Outcomes are reported for all 10 domains, the SDOH composite index, the WBI composite index, and the overall community well-being index, enabling diagnostic, comparative, and benchmarking analyses across all U.S. counties. This structure supported consistent national reporting and facilitated interpretation for both scientific and public-facing applications. Detailed documentation including indicator selection, weighting, aggregation, spatial harmonization, and statistical estimation and dissemination, is provided in previously published work [24] and in the Supplementary Methods. This measurement system supports consistent, reproducible, and scalable characterization of community well-being across diverse geographic and population contexts.

Identification of conceptually comparable community well-being measurement systems

A structured, purposive review was conducted to identify indices that are conceptually comparable to the BU-SC CWBI and represent diverse methodological approaches to measuring community well-being. The BU-SC CWBI was intentionally included as an illustrative example and comparator because it integrates both subjective individual-level well-being measures and objective contextual indicators and reflects the types of methodological decisions examined in this study. To underscore, the goal was not to conduct an exhaustive systematic review, scoping review or evaluative assessment of all community well-being indices. Rather, the objective was to assemble a set of well-developed, documented, and conceptually comparable indices that would permit methodological characterization and comparison across common dimensions of community well-being assessment.

Indices were selected if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) characterized well-being or a related latent construct (e.g., health) at a resolution higher than the individual, based on the index name or description; (2) comprised multiple domains that extended beyond traditional socioeconomic measures of health (e.g., healthcare, income, employment, or education) and included some measure of community coherence or vitality; (3) were developed for use in the United States; (4) aggregated indicators into domains and domains into a composite index; (5) were developed to provide well-being insights for the general adult population; and (6) provided publicly available methodological documentation sufficient to support abstraction and comparison.

We selected the first criterion because many indices assess community well-being without explicitly using the term “community well-being” in their title. In these cases, the stated purpose and description of the index often clarified that its primary objective was to characterize well-being. The second criterion was intended to distinguish, albeit subjectively, between multidimensional well-being indices and systems that focus exclusively on SDOH measures. Restricting the review to indices developed for use in the United States helped reduce variability associated with differences in cultural contexts, data sources, and measurement objectives that could complicate cross-system comparisons. We did not require that indices provide insight coverage for the entire US, as does the BU-SC CWBI; however, indices were required to support comparisons within their geographic area of focus (e.g., counties within a state or census tracts within a city). Finally, because the BU-SC CWBI was developed to characterize well-being in the general adult population, we excluded indices designed specifically for sub-populations (e.g., children, women, Indigenous populations, or individuals with specific diseases or conditions).

The review strategy consisted of a formal search, a citation review of eligible indices, and solicitation of expert opinion. Search terms included combinations of “well-being” or “health,” “community,” “neighborhood,” “state,” or “county,” and “index,” or “ranking.” Searches were conducted from August through November 2024.

An initial search strategy included PubMed, Google Scholar and Google. However, while the PubMed search identified a large number of indices that measured some aspects of well-being, few were of similar scope and intent as the BU-SC CWBI. Of note, well-known population well-being indices are often intended for a broad public audience rather than primarily for research, and methodological details are frequently provided through technical reports or other means rather than peer-reviewed publications. For these reasons, Google and Google Scholar were used as primary search platforms, as they were more likely to capture the type of indices we were targeting. The screening of candidate indices was limited to the first 20 pages of results for each search engine, or until three consecutive pages of irrelevant or duplicate content were identified.

Duplicates identified between Google and Google Scholar search engines and results that were clearly irrelevant based on the title or brief description were removed. A single reviewer screened titles and full-text materials. While reviewing the materials of potentially eligible indices identified through the search engines, additional indices were identified through citation tracking. Lastly, we obtained input from colleagues with content knowledge of the field to confirm if any additional indices meeting the inclusion criteria had been missed by our search.

Characterization of community well-being measurement systems

To support a system-level comparison of contemporary community well-being indices, we characterized each index according to the five methodological dimensions: (1) domain and indicator selection, (2) statistical estimation and weighting, (3) construction, standardization, and geographic aggregation, (4) evaluation of measurement properties, and (5) transparency, dissemination, and use (Fig. 1). These dimensions reflect the progression from conceptualization of community well-being to the generation and dissemination of composite indices and were selected to capture key design choices that define how community well-being is operationalized, quantified, and reported. Although these components are presented sequentially, measurement systems are not strictly sequential, as decisions made at each stage are interdependent and often iterative. Choices related to data sources, indicator selection, statistical estimation, and aggregation influence subsequent steps and final outputs. Data abstraction was conducted using a standardized template. Data was abstracted by a single analyst and reviewed for accuracy by a senior biostatistician.

Indicators were characterized along two dimensions: data type and level of measurement. Data type distinguished between objective indicators (e.g., administrative, environmental, or observed measures) and subjective indicators (e.g., self-reported perceptions or experiences). Level of measurement distinguished between individual-level data (e.g., survey responses collected from individuals) and contextual, place-based measures (e.g., county-level socioeconomic or environmental indicators). Survey-based indicators aggregated to geographic levels (e.g., the Center for Disease Control and Prevention’s Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) were treated as contextual measures representing population-level characteristics, even when derived from self-reported (subjective) data.

Descriptive information for all measurement systems included in the analysis included index name, publisher, website, stated purpose, and unit of analysis. Data sources were characterized as the underlying inputs used to construct indicators, including survey data, administrative records, and publicly available datasets. Data source selection is a defining feature of a measurement system, as it influences population coverage, representativeness, interpretability, and potential sources of bias. Because indices varied in how data sources were reported, with some citing originating organizations and others specifying instruments or databases, reporting was standardized by listing the originating organizations for all sources. Conceptual domains and indicator structures were characterized by identifying domains, subdomains, and indicators for each index, providing insight into how systems define and organize components of community well-being.

To support cross-system comparison of conceptually similar domains, we used the WIN Measurement Framework, a U.S.-based framework that defines core domains of well-being across individual and population levels [10, 11]. The WIN framework was selected for its ability to capture both subjective and objective constructs across individual- and population-level dimensions. WIN-recommended measures were not used to guide this analysis. Instead, index-specific indicators were mapped to WIN domains based on conceptual alignment, regardless of naming conventions or structural organization within individual systems. While this mapping approach was inherently interpretive, it was grounded in established measurement frameworks and our team’s prior experience in composite index development and validation.

Core methodological features of each system were descriptively summarized using the five methodological dimensions. First, Domain and Indicator Selection included assessment of indicator type (objective or subjective), whether individual-level data are included, documentation of indicators mapped to domains, and whether item selection to domains was expert-driven or data-driven. Second, Statistical Estimation and Weighting captured the application of weighting at the indicator or domain level, the use of equal or differential weighting, and the presence of model-based approaches such as regression, small area estimation, or imputation. Third, Construction, Standardization, and Geographic Aggregation included the geographic level of reporting (e.g., census tract, neighborhood tabulation area, county, or state), standardization approaches, score scaling, and handling of missing data. Fourth, Evaluation of Measurement Properties included reported assessments of internal consistency reliability, construct validity, external or criterion validity, and responsiveness, sensitivity, or robustness analyses. Fifth, Transparency, Dissemination, and Use included whether interactive tools were available (e.g., dashboards or maps), supporting peer-reviewed publications, data accessibility, and evidence of external research or analytic use.

The characterization of measurement system by methodological orientation reflected the primary methodological approach used to construct each system. Following abstraction and comparison of included systems, three broad methodological orientations emerged among measurement systems: framework-driven, aggregation-based, and integrated modeling systems. Framework-driven systems are characterized by domain structures largely specified a priori through expert or conceptual frameworks and primarily rely on aggregation approaches with limited statistical modeling. Aggregation-based systems emphasize indicator standardization and aggregation procedures but generally do not employ extensive model-based estimation. Integrated modeling systems combine aggregation approaches with statistical modeling techniques such as regression analysis, imputation, small area estimation, or latent variable methods. Because systems frequently incorporated features from multiple orientations, assignments reflected the dominant methodology of each system along a continuum rather than discrete classes.

Results

Index identification and selection

Our structured, purposive review yielded 315 candidate systems, of which 11 met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 3). Included indices originate from a broad spectrum of sectors, including federal agencies (Environmental Protection Agency), academic institutions (University of Wisconsin), private entities (Elevance Health, Sharecare, U.S. News & World Report), and non-profit advocacy organizations (AARP, Public Health Alliance of Southern California, Social Progress Imperative). While all indices articulated a multidimensional conceptualization of well-being, their stated purposes could be broadly grouped into two general areas: policy-oriented systems designed to inform population-level decision-making and resource allocation, and application-oriented systems intended to assess clinical, community, or behavioral outcomes. For example, the California Healthy Places Index (CHPI) is designed to inform place-based policy and resource targeting, whereas the Whole Health Index (WHI) emphasizes whole-person health to inform clinical and behavioral outcomes. Indices also vary in geographic scope, with most designed to support comparisons between geographies (e.g., states or counties) across the entire U.S. and others focused on more localized applications such as comparisons of counties or neighborhoods within a specific state or city.

Data Sources. Across the 12 measurement systems, 86 distinct data sources were identified (Table 2). The American Community Survey (ACS) was the most frequently used data source and was included in all systems. Additional commonly used sources included federal datasets from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the U.S. Departments of Health and Human Services and Agriculture, and other federal agencies. Despite this overlap, 76% of the identified data sources were unique to a single measurement system. The number of different data sources per system ranged from 5 to 37, with a median of 11 sources. The AARP Livability Index (AARP LI) was an outlier, drawing from 37 total sources, which exceeded the next highest system by 14 sources. Sources used by only 1 system were often associated with either localized data collection efforts or system-specific expansions in scope. Across systems, data sources contributed indicators derived from observed conditions, perceived experiences, or both, depending on how the data were incorporated within each measurement system.

Conceptual Domains and Indicator Structure. Table 3 presents the domain and indicator structures across measurement systems. Substantial heterogeneity was observed in how systems define and organize community well-being, with the number of domains ranging from 2 to 10 and indicators ranging from 11 to 93. The median number of domains was 5, and the median number of indicators was 43, reflecting wide variation in system complexity and granularity. Measurement systems also differed in structural organization. Some systems used relatively flat frameworks with a limited number of domains and minimal subdomain structure, such as the Opportunity Index (OPI), which includes 4 broad domains with relatively few indicators. In contrast, other systems employed more hierarchical architectures with nested subdomains and larger indicator sets. The BU-SC CWBI uses a three-tiered structure in which an overall composite score is derived from 2 primary domains, WBI and SDOH Index, each composed of five subdomains and 32 indicators in total. CHR&R reports two related composite indices, Population Health and Well-Being and Community Conditions, each with its own domain structure and contributing indicators.

Many systems measured similar multidimensional constructs, but the conceptualization of these constructs, as domains, varied. Measures of economic stability, education, health, housing and transportation, resource access, crime and safety, equity and vulnerable populations, and community were each represented in over half of systems (Table 3). However, these constructs were not always represented as distinct domains. For example, food access was grouped with healthcare access in a “Nutrition and Basic Medical Care” subdomain in the U.S. Social Progress Index (US SPI); placed within both “Vulnerable Populations” for food access and, for the general population, a broader “Resource Access” domain in the BU-SC CWBI; represented as a dedicated “Food and Nutrition” domain in U.S. News Healthiest Communities (USN HC); and included as a “Food Insecurity” subdomain in the WHI.

WIN Representation. To support cross-system comparison, index-specific indicators were mapped to the 12 core domains defined by the WIN framework. Table 4 summarizes domain coverage across the 12 measurement systems. Two measurement systems, CHR&R and USN HC, included indicators representing all 12 WIN domains. (Note that the NCVHS “Measurement Framework for Community Well- Being,” which served as the foundation for WIN, is also cited as the foundation for the development of the USN HC.) The remaining systems included indicators representing a subset of domains. Equity, community vitality, health, and housing were represented across all 12 systems, whereas well-being of people was represented in 5 systems and transportation and public safety were each in 8 of the 12 systems.

Measurement System Characteristics and Methodological Orientation. Table 5 summarizes the five methodological dimensions of community well-being measurement systems: (1) domain and indicator selection; (2) statistical estimation and weighting; (3) construction, standardization and geographic aggregation; (4) evaluation of measurement properties; and (5) transparency, dissemination and use. Across the first four methodological dimensions, systems shared a common structural foundation but differed substantially in implementation. All systems defined domains, relied on objective indicators, and applied standardization prior to aggregation. However, they varied in the inclusion of subjective and individual-level measures, the selection of expert- or data- driven methods to determine indicators, weighting strategies and domains, the use of statistical estimation to develop indices, the handling of missing data, and the extent to which measurement properties were formally evaluated.

Domain and indicator selection define the conceptual foundation of community well-being indices by specifying what aspects of well-being are included and how they are operationalized. All systems utilized multidimensional constructs, mapping indicators to explicitly defined domains (e.g., health, socioeconomic conditions, environment, and community context) as detailed in Table 3. All systems incorporated objective indicators derived from administrative, environmental, or survey-based data sources, capturing measurable conditions such as income, employment, housing, and health outcomes. In contrast, subjective indicators reflecting individuals’ perceptions, experiences, and self-reported well-being were incorporated in 8 systems. These measures were most prominently integrated in the BU-SC CWBI, and to a lesser extent in systems such as the EPA Human Well-Being Index (HWBI). Few systems incorporated individual-level data directly into index construction. The BU-SC CWBI and the WHI explicitly integrate individual-level data with contextual indicators, representing hybrid approaches that combine individual-level and place-based measures. In contrast, most other indices rely exclusively on aggregated, population-level indicators. This distinction reflects fundamental differences in how well-being is conceptualized and measured across systems.

Domain and indicator selection was predominantly expert driven across all systems, relying on literature review, conceptual frameworks, policy priorities, and advisory input. However, two systems, the BU-SC CWBI and the WHI, incorporated hybrid approaches in which empirical information was used to inform the final set of indicators with expert-driven selection. These approaches illustrate that while domain and indicator selection is generally conceptually driven, systems differ in the extent to which empirical methods contribute to the refinement of indicators. In addition to differences in selection approaches, systems varied in the types of indicators included, with some emphasizing health outcomes, others focusing on contextual determinants of health, and others incorporating policy-relevant or actionable measures.

Statistical estimation and weighting approaches varied across systems and represent a key source of methodological differentiation among the 12 community well-being systems. Weighting was applied at both the indicator and domain levels across most systems, with some indices using equal weighting. differential weighting, or both. Because multiple weighting approaches may have been applied within a system (i.e., both equal and differential weighting applied at different levels within the same system), these distinctions were not separately represented in Table 5 in order to maintain a consistent level of abstraction for cross-system comparison. Several systems relied primarily on equal weighting and aggregation-based approaches. These systems standardized indicators and combined them using arithmetic or geometric means without model-based estimation. For example, the US SPI used standardized indicators and geometric mean aggregation within domains, followed by averaging across higher levels. Similarly, the OPI applies min–max rescaling and averaged indicators within and across domains using equal weighting.

In contrast, other systems incorporated predefined differential weighting based on conceptual frameworks or policy priorities. The AARP LI applies equal weighting across domains, with overall scores calculated as the average of domain scores. Within domains, indicators may be derived directly from observed data, composite measures constructed from multiple data sources, or modeled estimates, reflecting variation in how individual indicators are operationalized. In contrast, AHR uses structured weighting influenced by earlier models such as CHR&R. These approaches assign fixed relative importance to domains rather than estimating weights empirically. A subset of systems employed empirically derived or structured quantitative weighting approaches. The CHPI derives domain weights using constrained regression models that maximize association with life expectancy outcomes. The HWBI applies a hierarchical weighting structure in which relative importance values derived from expert judgment and public perception are translated into quantitative weights using an analytic hierarchy process.

More complex statistical estimation methods were observed in a limited number of systems. The CORE Score incorporates Bayesian multilevel regression with post-stratification for select components, such as civic participation measures derived from survey data, enabling the generation of small-area estimates by combining individual-level responses with population-level demographic information. The BU-SC CWBI applies a multi-step statistical framework that includes principal component analysis and confirmatory factor analysis for SDOH index development, multiple imputation to address missing data, and small area estimation using hierarchical models to generate county-level well-being estimates from individual-level data. The WHI incorporates model-based components, including clinical risk scores derived from claims data and empirically informed weighting approaches.

Across systems, model-based estimation was not widely applied and was typically limited to specific components rather than implemented consistently throughout the index construction process. Overall, this component highlights a spectrum of approaches ranging from aggregation-based methods using equal weighting to more complex, multi-step statistical frameworks. These differences reflect variation in methodological complexity and approaches to index construction across systems.

Construction, Standardization and Geographic Aggregation. To support comparability across indicators measured on different scales, all systems applied standardization or normalization prior to aggregation. Approaches varied and included z-score transformation, min–max scaling, percentile ranking, and other normalization procedures, with most systems reporting scores on a bounded scale (commonly 0–100) to facilitate interpretation and comparison. In this analysis, system resolution was characterized based on the smallest geographic unit at which index values are reported, recognizing that this may differ from the level at which underlying data are collected. Geographic resolution varied substantially across systems, with county-level reporting most common. Several systems extended to finer geographic scales, including census tract (e.g., CHPI) and neighborhood tabulation areas (NYC WBI). With the exception of CHPI and NYC WBI, which are region-specific, most indices were designed to represent the United States broadly. However, coverage was not always available at finer geographic levels due to data constraints.

Systems differed in how they addressed these constraints. Some systems reduced the number of indicators when data were unavailable at finer geographic resolutions. For example, CHPI incorporated fewer healthcare access indicators at the tract level, and the OPI included fewer indicators at the county level than at the state level. In contrast, the AARP LI maintained broader domain coverage by incorporating indicators at their native geographic resolution and, where necessary, using higher-resolution values when data were not available at finer scales. In contrast, some systems were designed to generate indices at a common geographic resolution and therefore used cross-level substitution or model-based estimation to align indicators collected at different geographic scales. Some systems, including BU-SC CWBI and the WHI, addressed data limitations through multiple imputation and small area techniques, improving stability and coverage across geographies.

Overall, differences in geographic resolution, standardization methods, scale, and handling of data availability constraints reflect trade-offs among geographic granularity, data availability, and methodological complexity. These design choices influence both the interpretability and comparability of index values across systems.

Evaluation of measurement properties was limited and inconsistently reported across systems. Three systems reported reliability testing including internal consistency measures such as Cronbach’s alpha or test–retest reliability, 8 reported validity assessments, including construct and external or criterion validity based on associations with established measures, and 4 reported responsiveness, sensitivity or robustness analysis. However, detailed documentation of these evaluations was generally sparse. Although AARP LI and AHR systems are widely used in policy and research contexts, they lacked publicly available documentation of formal reliability or construct validity testing.

Transparency, Dissemination and Use. Across all systems, there was strong emphasis on dissemination through dashboards, maps, and public reports. Most systems provided both domain-level and overall scores using standardized scaling approaches. However, transparency varied in terms of methodological documentation, availability of underlying data, and subsequent reproducibility. Approximately 67% of systems provided publicly downloadable data, and about half have been used in external research applications. Transparency and data accessibility varied substantially across systems.

Measurement System Methodological Orientation. When characterizing systems by their predominant methodological orientation along a continuum, most systems were primarily aggregation-based, reflecting reliance on combining standardized indicators using equal or fixed weighting approaches (i.e., “How do we combine indicators into a transparent and interpretable score?“). Examples include AARP LI and AHR, where domains primarily serve to organize indicators and support interpretation. Framework-driven systems represented a second methodological orientation and were organized around predefined conceptual domains, with aggregation used primarily to summarize information within those domains (i.e., “What should we measure?“). A third methodological orientation consisted of integrated modeling systems, which combined multiple data sources and incorporated more complex statistical approaches (i.e., “How can statistical methods and diverse data sources be integrated to support estimation?“). These systems integrated individual-level and contextual indicators and applied methods such as imputation or small area estimation to support estimation across geographical areas. The predominant characteristics of the BU-SC CWBI and the WHI aligned most closely with this orientation. Measurement system methodological orientation definitions are found in Table 6.

Although all systems relied on expert knowledge and conceptual input, the relative contribution of empirical and model-based methods varied across orientations. Together, these methodological characterizations illustrate that community well-being indices differ not only in their methodological components, but also in how those components are combined and implemented. These differences reflect trade-offs among conceptual structure, geographic specificity, data availability, and methodological complexity, reinforcing that indices are tailored to different purposes and use contexts rather than representing a single unified approach to community well-being measurement.

Discussion

This study applies a measurement systems perspective to characterize and compare community well-being indices across five methodological components: domain and indicator selection; statistical estimation and weighting; construction, standardization and geographic aggregation; evaluation of measurement properties; and transparency, dissemination and use. While prior work has described composite indicator construction and individual index methodologies, systematic characterization of community well-being indices using a measurement systems perspective has been limited [4, 25]. Our descriptive characterization of 12 distinct measurement systems demonstrates that variation in how well-being is conceptualized and operationalized shapes how indices are constructed, interpreted, and used. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across all five methodological components we examined, including statistical estimation approaches, treatment of missing data, geographic resolution, and evaluation of measurement properties. Heterogeneity is not necessarily undesirable, in some instances reflecting valid differences in goals and contexts.

Frameworks such as those proposed by the NCVHS, NAM, and the WIN Collaborative, and OECD guidance provide complementary perspectives on how community well-being can be conceptualized and measured. Although no universal definition of community well-being is likely to emerge, there is substantial overlap in the domains and conditions considered important for supporting population health and well-being across these frameworks. Our review focuses on how these frameworks are operationalized across existing measurement systems and how methodological decisions influence interpretation and use. All systems produced at least one overall summary score; however, the relative emphasis on composite versus domain-level reporting differed substantially across systems. Some systems prioritize domain-specific results or hierarchical structures of domains and subdomains, whereas others emphasize a single, overall summary metric. The construction of an overall composite is itself a methodological decision, dependent on weighting, scaling, and aggregation choices, and may obscure important variation across domains. Accordingly, interpretation of composite scores should be considered alongside domain-level results to better reflect the multidimensional nature of community well-being.

The many differences observed among the measurement systems highlight an important tension between reproducibility and actionability. These goals are related but not identical. Some systems are designed to provide stable, reproducible measures that support benchmarking and comparison across communities and over time and may rely more heavily on lagging indicators. In contrast, systems designed to support intervention and decision-making may place greater emphasis on domain-level results, more responsive or leading indicators, or context-specific measures that better reflect local conditions and potential levers for change. Similarly, rankings, continuous scores, and cluster-based classifications each represent different approaches to balancing interpretability, statistical uncertainty, and practical utility. Collectively, these observations suggest that differences across indices largely reflect differences in purpose and methodological design.

Spatial resolution emerged as a defining feature of index design that shapes both measurement precision and practical applicability. Systems reporting at coarser geographic resolutions, such as counties, benefit from greater statistical stability and broader data availability, while those reporting at finer resolutions, such as census tracts or neighborhood tabulation areas, enable more targeted insights but face constraints related to data sparsity and reliability. For example, environmental exposures such as air pollution may vary substantially within a county, with individuals living near highways experiencing different conditions than those in less dense areas. Many contextual indicators are derived from administrative and census-based data sources that are updated periodically and may not reflect real-time conditions or finer spatial variation. Consequently, many indicators represent relatively stable structural conditions and may function primarily as lagging indicators, capturing the cumulative effects of social, economic, and environmental conditions. Changes in policy or environment may take time to translate into downstream effects on population health and well-being. In contrast, more responsive or leading indicators may provide earlier signals of change and may be particularly useful for intervention and decision-making. These differences further illustrate the trade-offs between reproducibility, actionability, and geographic specificity. Approaches to addressing these constraints vary across systems, including restricting indicators, incorporating higher-level geographic measure when local data are unavailable, and applying statistical approaches such as imputation or small area estimation to improve stability and coverage.

A key finding of this study is the limited and inconsistent application of formal evaluation of measurement properties across community well-being systems, even among those employing more advanced statistical methods. Many systems aim to quantify complex and multidimensional constructs such as well-being, livability, or opportunity. However, formal evaluation of reliability, validity and responsiveness is not consistently implemented relative to established measurement standards. In contrast, validated health measurement instruments such as the Short Form – 36 Health Survey [26, 27] are developed using rigorous psychometric frameworks. However, while individual-level psychometric scales provide an illustrative paradigm for evaluating measurement properties, community well-being indices operate under different constraints and often serve different purposes. Many place-based indices function primarily as tools intended to support policy and decision making rather than formal latent-variable instruments. Consequently, validation expectations may reasonably differ across use cases, and systems may place varying emphasis on interpretability, transparency, utility, and statistical optimization.

The characterization of systems into broad methodological orientations provides a useful taxonomy for interpreting heterogeneity across community well-being indices. However, these characterizations should be viewed as heuristic rather than mutually exclusive, as many systems incorporate features from multiple approaches. More importantly, they emphasize that community well-being indices are often designed for different purposes and reflect different methodological priorities. In general, framework-driven systems place greater emphasis on conceptual organization, aggregation-based systems on indicator standardization and aggregation, and integrated modeling systems on data integration and statistical estimation. These orientations reflect a continuum, with many systems incorporating elements of all three approaches and differing primarily in their predominant methodological emphasis rather than representing discrete categories.

The incorporation of individual-level data represents an important distinction among measurement systems. Although these data are ultimately aggregated to produce community-level measures, they enable statistical modeling approaches that improve stability and coverage, particularly in areas with limited data and provide opportunities to incorporate more timely information than traditional administrative and census-based indicators. Individual-level and contextual indicators provide complementary information, with subjective measures capturing lived experience and perceived well-being and contextual indicators reflecting structural conditions and environmental exposures. Each approach has limitations, including reporting bias for subjective measures and temporal lag and limited spatial resolution for administrative measures.

Emerging analytic approaches, including machine learning and other modeling strategies, further extend these capabilities by enabling integration of diverse data sources and identification of complex relationships among indicators. In an increasingly technology- and AI-enabled data landscape, future measurement systems may incorporate novel data streams such as electronic health records, aggregated mobility data, and environmental sensor feeds to provide more timely and responsive measures of community well-being. However, these approaches also introduce challenges related to data equity, algorithmic bias, population representativeness, privacy, and reproducibility. Continued emphasis on transparency, documentation, and methodological evaluation will therefore be important to maintain confidence in the interpretation and use of these measures. Although most systems provide public reports and dashboards, relatively few make underlying code or computational methods publicly available, limiting reproducibility and independent evaluation.

An important consideration in the development of community well-being measurement systems is the role of local cultural and geographic context in shaping how well-being is defined and interpreted across populations. Our domain-by-domain analysis revealed important gaps in current systems. While domains related to economic conditions and physical health were represented across nearly all systems, constructs reflecting individual-community interactions, including social connectedness, trust, civic engagement, and community capacity for collective action, were less frequently represented. In addition, many systems emphasize community condition and outcomes, with less attention devoted to factors that may support intervention and change. Greater consideration of how individuals interact with community resources and services may provide additional insight into pathways linking community conditions and well-being.

These findings may have implications for the use of community well-being indices in policy and decision-making, as differences in underlying measurement systems may lead to substantially different conclusions about the same communities. If the goal of community well-being measurement is to produce reproducible and actionable metrics, greater transparency regarding conceptual frameworks, data sources, statistical methods, and evaluation practices may facilitate more informed interpretation and use.

This study has several limitations. First, the selection of systems was based on predefined inclusion criteria and the availability of sufficient public or provided documentation. We acknowledge that the purposeful nature of the review and the subjective selection of conceptually comparable systems may influence characterization. Although efforts were made to identify widely used and conceptually comparable systems, the set reviewed may not represent all existing community well-being measurement systems. Second, methodological transparency varied substantially across systems, and detailed information on statistical estimation, weighting, and validation was not consistently available or, when provided, may have lacked sufficient rigor. As a result, some system characteristics may be underreported or mischaracterized. Characterization of measurement systems required interpretation of available documentation and may be influenced by variability in how methods were reported across systems. Third, while population-based weighting and other aggregation approaches are likely used in many systems, these methods were not consistently documented and therefore were not systematically evaluated. Finally, this study focuses on methodological characterization rather than empirical comparison, which would be challenging given differences in data sources, constructs, and geographic coverage.

Conclusions

Characterizing community well-being indices is important for research, policy, and decision-making because methodological differences across systems can influence how communities are assessed and compared. Considering community well-being indices as integrated measurement systems highlights how design choices across multiple methodological components influence their interpretation, comparability, and use. The measurement systems perspective provides a structured approach for characterizing methodological similarities and differences across indices and underscores that community well-being systems are often designed for different goals, applied in different contexts, and reflect a continuum of methodological priorities. As community well-being indices are increasingly used to inform research, policy, funding, and intervention strategies, greater transparency in how systems are constructed and evaluated may support appropriate interpretation and use. By making these design choices explicit, this approach may facilitate more informed application, evaluation and continued development of community well-being measurement systems.
