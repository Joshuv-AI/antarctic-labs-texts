Cytotoxic activity of biosynthesized nickel oxide nanoparticles against A549 lung cancer cell line
Authors: Hendry Moses Panneerselvam, Ebenezar Jeyasingh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer continues to be the leading cause of cancer-related fatalities worldwide. The advent of nanotechnology offers a promising solution for targeting cancer cells. The biosynthesis approach was chosen because phytocompounds present in plant extracts function as capping and reducing agents. Herein, we produced Piper nigrum seed extract-mediated nickel oxide nanoparticles (PNNO NPs), and the cytotoxicity they induced in the A549 lung cancer cell line was investigated. XRD studies confirmed the formation of PNNO NPs crystallized in a cubic crystal system with the Fm3̄m (225) space group. FESEM analysis showed that these NPs were less agglomerated, with a mean particle size of 26 nm. Evaluation of anticancer activity using the MTT assay revealed that PNNO NPs exert high toxicity towards A549 lung cancer cells, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 19 µg mL−1. H2-DCFDA and JC-1 biochemical staining confirmed a substantial increase in the reactive oxygen species generation and mitochondrial damage in PNNO NP-treated A549 cancer cells, respectively. AO/EtBr staining and flow cytometry analysis indicated that A549 cancer cells subjected to PNNO NPs underwent apoptosis, and this was strongly supported by the downregulation of the mRNA expression of the antiapoptotic gene BCL2. Collectively, these compelling results highlight the substantial therapeutic potential of PNNO NPs as an effective agent for combating lung cancer.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a predominant malignancy type worldwide, with nearly 2.2 million new patients diagnosed and approximately 1.8 million fatalities in 2020 alone. Statistically, one in five individuals diagnosed with lung cancer may succumb to the disease.1 Regions with a high Human Development Index (HDI) show considerably higher incidence and mortality rates than those with a low HDI.1,2 Smoking and diverse environmental factors, including dietary practices and lifestyle choices, may significantly contribute to disease progression.3 Surgical intervention and radiotherapy continue to be the preferred treatment modalities for lung cancer. Nevertheless, the substantial trauma involved, the complexities in distinguishing malignant tumors from lesions with blurred edges, and the challenges in mitigating hypoxia-induced DNA damage limit their efficacy.4 At present, chemotherapy is administered alongside surgery or radiotherapy for the management of metastatic cancers, and systemic toxicity and drug resistance commonly occur during these treatments.4

The advent of nanotechnology offers a prospective alternative for more optimal management of lung cancer, which is attributed to its favorable characteristics, including bioavailability, biocompatibility, and efficient intestinal absorption.4,5 Recent investigations have revealed that metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) such as ZnO, CuO, Fe2O3, MgO, NiO, TiO2, and CeO2 are highly promising for biomedical applications.6–12 Among metal oxides, NiO NPs are characterized by multifunctionality, tunability, cost-effectiveness, high stability, and favorable optical, magnetic, and catalytic properties, which enable their wide use in biomedical applications such as drug delivery, imaging, and as antibiotic and anticancer agents.13–15

NiO NPs can be synthesized by several routes, including physical methods such as spray pyrolysis,16 laser ablation,17 and chemical vapor deposition.18 Chemical methods such as hydrothermal19 and solvothermal methods20 are also utilized. However, biosynthesis is a preferred method of choice due to its environmentally friendly, sustainable, low-cost, and non-toxic nature. The biosynthesis process utilizes natural substrates, such as plant extracts and biomolecules, instead of relying on chemical reducing agents and stabilizers. These natural extracts contain biomolecules such as flavonoids, alkaloids, saponins, phenolics, and terpenoids, which function as capping and reducing agents, thereby eliminating the need for hazardous solvents and toxic reagents.12,15

Indhumathi et al. found that NiO NPs prepared using Cullen tomentosum plant extract exerted strong cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells, with an IC50 value of 12.5 µg mL−1.21 In another study, Sani et al. reported an IC50 value of 47 µg mL−1 for biosynthesized NiO NPs using Callistemon viminalis flower extract against HepG2 liver cancer cells.22 Recently, Punitha and Saral demonstrated the anticancer efficacy of NiO NPs prepared using Sargassum wightii against HepG2 cancer cells, with an IC50 value of 52.25 µg mL−1.10 The aforementioned studies demonstrate that biosynthesized NiO NPs can significantly reduce the cell viability of various cancer cell lines and improve anticancer activity.

Spices are essential components of our daily diet that improve the flavor and taste of food.23 Black pepper (Piper nigrum), which has been referred to as the ‘king of spices’, is a well-known and widely used household spice that is predominantly cultivated in tropical regions.24 This medically important spice aids in improving appetite and alleviating symptoms of a cold, cough, and fever. It possesses a wide range of biomedical applications, including anti-asthmatic, anti-cholera, anti-oxidant, anti-inflammatory, hepatoprotective, immune-modulatory, anti-microbial, and anti-cancer properties.24,25P. nigrum seed extract contains various phytocompounds, namely piperine, piperanine, erucamide, hexadecanamide, oleamide, didecyldimethylammonium, docosanamide, d-sphingosine, and cetrimonium.26 Indeed, the alkaloids, phenolic compounds, terpenoids, flavonoids, coumarins, and tannins in P. nigrum seed extract function as reducers and stabilizers during the formation of NPs.26,27 Recently, several NPs, namely Ag, Cu, Ni, SnO2, ZnO, and CuO, have been synthesized using P. nigrum seed extract.26,28–32

Herein, we report the synthesis of NiO NPs using P. nigrum seed extract and investigate their cytotoxic effects and apoptosis-inducing mechanism in A549 lung cancer cells. It is expected that the biomolecules and phytocompounds present in the aqueous extract of P. nigrum seeds will serve as reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents for PNNO NPs formation from a nickel nitrate hexahydrate precursor. Subsequently, the formed PNNO NPs may induce cytotoxic effects in cancer cells and cause apoptosis.

To this end, a systematic investigation was undertaken involving (i) structural, morphological, optical, and stability characterization using XRD, FESEM, HRTEM, XPS, FTIR, DLS, zeta potential, Raman spectroscopy, UV-Vis, and PL analysis; (ii) assessment of cell viability using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay; (iii) estimation of apoptosis using flow cytometry and acridine orange/ethidium bromide (AO/EtBr) staining; (iv) generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2-DCFDA) staining; (v) rescue experiment using ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC); (vi) detection of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) damage using 5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimidazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC-1) staining; and (vii) analysis of the expression of the anti-apoptotic B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2) gene in control and PNNO-treated A549 cancer cells. Hence, this comprehensive mechanistic approach will facilitate a deeper understanding of the cell death mechanism induced by PNNO NPs in human A549 lung cancer cells.

Materials and methods

Materials

P. nigrum seeds were purchased from Tiruchirappalli, Tamil Nadu, India. Nickel(ii) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) with ≥ 98% purity was procured from Merck, India Pvt. Ltd, and used as received.

Preparation of P. nigrum seed extract

The seeds of P. nigrum were washed using double-distilled water to eliminate any contaminants or foreign materials. The seeds were subsequently air-dried and processed into a fine powder. A total of 10 g of this powder was added to 300 mL of double-distilled water, and the mixture was boiled for 1 h at 60 °C. Following boiling, the extract was allowed to cool to room temperature. Then, the resulting supernatant was filtered with Whatman No. 1 filter paper and stored for future use.

Biosynthesis of PNNO NPs

A 0.3 M of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O was mixed with 100 mL of prepared P. nigrum seed extract and continuously stirred at 60 °C for 3 h. After stirring, the mixture was subjected to drying at 60 °C for 6 h and then allowed to cool to ambient temperature. Then, the obtained PNNO NPs were calcined for 5 h at 500 °C in a muffle furnace.

Characterization

The synthesized PNNO NPs were characterized using a range of analytical techniques. (i) XRD patterns were recorded using the PANalytical X'Pert Pro powder diffractometer; (ii) Raman spectrum was recorded with the XploRA Plus Micro-Raman spectrometer; (iii) FESEM was performed using an Ultra-55 Carl-Zeiss scanning electron microscope; (iv) HRTEM was executed with a JEOL/JEM 2100 transmission electron microscope operating at 200 kV; (v) XPS measurements were performed using a PHI 5000 VersaProbe instrument; (vi) DLS and zeta potential analyses were conducted using a Malvern Zetasizer; (vii) FTIR spectra were conducted using a Shimadzu FTIR-8400S spectrometer; (viii) UV-Vis spectrum was conducted using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer; and (ix) photoluminescence spectrum was assessed with the PerkinElmer LS-45 fluorescence spectrometer.

Application studies (SI)

Cell culture (SI-1), cellular uptake studies (SI-2), MTT assay (SI-3), AO/EtBr staining (SI-4), flow cytometry studies (SI-5), H2DCFDA staining (SI-6), JC-1 staining (SI-7), qPCR studies (SI-8), and statistical analysis (SI-9).

Results and discussion

Physicochemical characterizations

XRD studies

XRD analysis is an important microstructural technique based on a coherent scattering mechanism that provides valuable information, including the purity, phase formation, and crystallite size of the formed NPs.33,34Fig. 1a shows the diffraction pattern and phase of PNNO NPs in the diffraction angle (2θ) range between 30° and 80°. Five diffraction peaks are observed at 2θ angles of 37.28°, 43.34°, 62.96°, 75.51°, and 79.49°, corresponding to the Miller indices (hkl) values of (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222), respectively. All diffraction peaks closely matched ICDD PDF number 01-078-0643, indicating the formation of a cubic NiO crystal system with an Fm3̄m space group.35 The absence of additional peaks indicates the complete conversion of the (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) precursor into NiO without any impurities (Fig. 1a). The well-defined, sharp diffraction peaks presented in Fig. 1a indicate the high crystallinity of the synthesized PNNO NPs. As seen in Fig. 1b, the calculated crystallite size along different crystallographic planes is plotted, and the average crystallite size of the PNNO NPs was estimated to be approximately 16.30 nm. (a) X-ray diffraction spectrum, (b) crystallite size along different crystallographic planes, (c) lattice parameter (Å) and volume (Å3) along different planes, (d) W–H plot, (e) Rietveld refinement, and (f) packing diagram of PNNO NPs.

The Williamson–Hall (W–H) method precisely determines the crystallite size and microstrain in a crystal lattice.36 The W–H analysis is performed by plotting β cos θ as a function of 4 sin θ, as depicted in Fig. 1d. The linear fitted plot of β cos θ versus 4 sin θ provides values of intercept (c = 0.00526) and slope (m = 0.00188), from which crystallite size and microstrain (ε = m) were calculated to be 29.17 nm and 0.00188, respectively. A larger crystallite size value was found using the W–H method (approximately 29.17 nm) compared with the Debye–Scherrer method (approximately 16.30 nm), which may be due to the fact that the strain component is not taken into account in the Debye–Scherrer method.37,38

The Rietveld refinement analysis was performed using the General Structure Analysis System-II (GSAS-II) program to confirm the phase purity and determine the lattice parameters of PNNO NPs, as shown in Fig. 1e. The value of the weighted residual factor (wR) is 4.632%, and the reduced χ2 value is 1.510, indicating a good fit between the experimental and theoretical plots. As presented in Fig. 1e, the observed XRD intensities (Yobs) and calculated XRD intensities (Ycalc) are in satisfactory agreement with each other. Furthermore, the refinement analysis revealed that the PNNO is characterized as a cubic crystal with an Fm3̄m (225) space group. The lattice parameters were determined as a = b = c = 4.1715 Å; α = β = γ = 90°, and the cell volume was calculated to be 72.593 Å3. Fig. 1f shows a packing diagram of the NiO crystal structure, generated using the Visualization for Electronic and Structural Analysis (VESTA) software. As seen in Fig. 1f, six oxygen anions (O2−) are octahedrally coordinated with nickel cations (Ni2+). Likewise, six Ni2+ cations are octahedrally coordinated with O2− anions, forming a NiO cubic crystal system.

FESEM analysis

FESEM provides a clear topographical analysis of nanomaterials with less electrostatic distortion and high spatial resolution.39 The FESEM images of PNNO NPs captured with scale ranges of 200 nm and 100 nm are depicted in Fig. 2a and b. The micrograph indicates that the PNNO NPs predominantly show spherical and cubic morphology, with minimal signs of agglomeration (Fig. 2a and b). A histogram bar graph illustrating the particle size distribution (PSD) of PNNO NPs is depicted in Fig. 2c. From the PSD analysis, it is evident that the size distribution of PNNO ranged between 16 and 36 nm, with a mean particle size of 26 nm. The atomic composition and elemental distribution of PNNO NPs were identified using EDX spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. 2d–f. The PNNO NPs are solely composed of nickel (Ni) and oxygen (O), with atomic percentages of 37.94% and 62.06%, respectively (Fig. 2d). As revealed by the elemental color mapping images of Ni and O (Fig. 2e and f), both elements are homogeneously distributed in PNNO NPs.

(a and b) FESEM image with different scale, (c) particle size distribution histogram, (d) EDAX spectrum, (e and f) color mapping of Ni and O, (g and h) HRTEM micrograph, and (i) SAED pattern of PNNO NPs.

HRTEM analysis

HRTEM utilizes transmitted and scattered electron beams to produce an interference image, which yields a micrograph of crystal structure with high spatial resolution and detailed insights into the crystal system.40 The HRTEM images taken with different scale ranges (10 and 2 nm) and SAED patterns are depicted in Fig. 2g–i. The HRTEM micrographs clearly show that the PNNO NPs are primarily composed of spherical, cubic, and hexagonal-like morphologies with particle sizes ranging between 19 and 44 nm (Fig. 2g). From Fig. 2h, the lattice spacing was calculated to be 0.20 nm, which corresponds to the (200) plane of the PNNO crystal structure. The SAED patterns depicted in Fig. 2i were well matched with X-ray diffraction planes, affirming the formation of PNNO NPs.

XPS studies

XPS analysis was performed to identify the elements present and oxidation state of the synthesized PNNO NPs. The survey spectrum of PNNO NPs presented in Fig. 3a reveals the presence of nickel (Ni), oxygen (O), and carbon (C). XPS measurement in the Ni region (Fig. 3b) shows four peaks centred at 853.2 eV, 855.2 eV, 860.4 eV, and 871.5 eV. The peaks at approximately 853.2 eV and 855.2 eV, assigned to the Ni2+ (2p3/2) and Ni3+ (2p3/2) states, indicate the coexistence of Ni2+ and Ni3+ species.41 The peak at 871.5 eV corresponds to the Ni2+ (2p1/2) transition, while the peak at 860.4 eV was attributed to the characteristic shake-up satellite of nickel.41,42Fig. 3c shows that the XPS measurement taken at the O region revealed the presence of two peaks centered at 528.9 and 530.5 eV. The lower binding energy peak at 528.9 eV was assigned to lattice oxygen associated with the Ni–O bonds in cubic NiO, whereas the higher binding energy peak at 530.5 eV was attributed to surface oxygen species.42,43

DLS and zeta potential analysis

The size distribution and surface potential of biosynthesized PNNO NPs were investigated using DLS and the zeta potential technique, respectively. As seen in Fig. 3d, the hydrodynamic size of PNNO NPs was found to be 164.2 nm. Comparable size distributions have been reported for biosynthesized NiO NPs using Apium graveolens44 and Rhamnus virgata,45 with hydrodynamic sizes of 189.1 nm and 173.7 nm, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3e, the zeta potential of PNNO NPs was measured to be −21.1 mV, indicating moderate colloidal stability.

Raman studies

Raman spectroscopy is an indispensable, non-destructive technique based on the inelastic scattering of photons that describes the crystalline nature and quality of materials.12,50 The Raman spectrum of PNNO NPs recorded in the wavenumber range between 120 and 1800 cm−1 is depicted in Fig. 5a. A single highly resolved peak is observed at 497 cm−1, with a small hump at approximately 1068 cm−1. A distinct prominent peak at approximately 497 cm−1 is indicative of one-phonon first-order longitudinal optical (1P-1LO) mode, which arises due to nickel vacancy defects.51–53 Furthermore, a small broad hump observed at approximately 1068 cm−1 denotes two-phonon second-order longitudinal optical (2P-2LO) mode.51,53 The 1P-1LO and 2P-2LO arise due to the vibration of Ni–O bonds.51 These obtained results are in satisfactory agreement with previous reports, confirming the formation of PNNO NPs.54–56

UV-Vis studies

UV-Vis spectroscopy is a widely used optical characterization technique that measures the absorption of light when there are electronic transitions within a sample.57 The UV-Vis investigation of PNNO NPs was conducted in the range of 250 to 1050 nm, as displayed in Fig. 5b. PNNO NPs exhibited an absorption band at approximately 325 nm because of the electronic transition of electrons from the valence band (O2p) to the conduction band (Ni3d) (Fig. 5b).58 The band gap energy (Eg) of PNNO was estimated using Tauc plot.59(αhν)2 = A(hν − Eg)where α denotes the absorption coefficient, hν denotes the energy of the incident photons, and n denotes whether the transition is direct (n = 2) or indirect (n = 1/2). By taking the tangent at the point of intersection between hν on the x-axis and (αhν)2 on the y-axis, the energy bandgap value is obtained, as shown in Fig. 5b (inset). The bandgap value of PNNO NPs was calculated as 2.77 eV. The observed absorbance peak and estimated band gap values of PNNO NPs are in agreement with those from previously reported studies.13,58,59

PL studies

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy is an effective technique for identifying the presence of defects in nanomaterials. Fig. 5c shows the PL emission spectrum of PNNO NPs, with a primary peak centred at 363 nm, a secondary band at 467 nm, and three small humps observed at 391 nm, 441 nm, and 481 nm, which are consistent with results from previous studies.21,60–62 For a detailed analysis of the PL spectra, the PNNO emission peak was deconvoluted in the wavelength range of 355–550 nm to examine the various defects and vacancies present in the system. The deconvoluted PL spectrum showed seven peaks at 363 nm, 391 nm, 409 nm, 441 nm, 467 nm, 481 nm, and 517 nm (Fig. 5c). The deconvoluted peaks in the UV region (363–391 nm) were ascribed to near-band emission that corresponded to radiative recombination of free excitons.21,60 Multiple emission bands observed in visible regions (409–517 nm) are associated with defect-related states in NiO NPs , including oxygen vacancies, nickel-related defects, and surface defect states.21,61,62

Anticancer studies

Cellular uptake studies

The cellular uptake behavior of biosynthesized PNNO NPs was investigated by monitoring the fluorescence signal of RhB-labelled PNNO NPs in A549 cancer cells incubated for 24 h with 4′6-diamidino-2-phenylindole·2HCl (DAPI) used as a nuclear counterstain (Fig. 6a–c). As presented in Fig. 6a, DAPI staining produced bright blue fluorescence that was confined to the nuclei . The intense red fluorescence observed in RhB-labelled PNNO NPs in Fig. 6b indicates effective internalization of PNNO NPs via cell endocytosis. The merged image depicted in Fig. 6c clearly shows blue nuclear fluorescence from DAPI that is surrounded by strong red fluorescence, suggesting the cytoplasmic accumulation of RhB-labelled PNNO NPs following endocytic uptake. Similar cellular uptake behavior for determining the internalization of nanoparticles has been reported elsewhere.63,64 Encouraged by the effective internalization of PNNO NPs into A549 cancer cells, we further evaluated the therapeutic potential of PNNO NPs.

Cell viability assay

The biocompatibility of the biosynthesized PNNO NPs was evaluated using an MTT cell viability assay in human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells. A bar graph representing the viability percentage of HEK293 cells with respect to varying PNNO concentrations (10–100 µg mL−1) is presented in Fig. 7a. The cell viability percentage was found to be 91.42 ± 0.56 for 10 µg mL−1, 86.63 ± 0.13 for 20 µg mL−1, 67.63 ± 0.41 for 30 µg mL−1, 57.64 ± 0.11 for 40 µg mL−1, 43.60 ± 0.14 for 50 µg mL−1, 32.61 ± 0.29 for 60 µg mL−1, 23.57 ± 0.31 for 70 µg mL−1, 16.20 ± 0.24 for 80 µg mL−1, 11.70 ± 0.29 for 90 µg mL−1, and 7.03 ± 0.20 for 100 µg mL−1 (Fig. 7a). A dose–response curve was drawn by plotting PNNO concentration along the x-axis and cell viability percentage on the y-axis, from which the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value was found and drawn at 45 µg mL−1 (Fig. 7b). A representative morphological image of untreated HEK293 cells (control cells) and PNNO NP-treated HEK293 cells is shown in Fig. 7c and d, respectively. As expected, the control cells (Fig. 7c) retained their original morphology, whereas PNNO NPs induced minimal morphological changes to HEK293 cells (Fig. 7d).

An MTT cell viability assay was performed to evaluate and compare the cytotoxicity of P. nigrum seed extract, the anticancer drug cisplatin, and biosynthesized PNNO NPs against A549 lung cancer cells. A bar graph representing the percentage of viable A549 cells with respect to varying concentrations of P. nigrum seed extract, anticancer drug cisplatin, and PNNO NPs is presented in Fig. 7e, i, and m, respectively. In the case of P. nigrum seed extract, the cell viability percentage was found to be 87.13 ± 0.48 for 10 µg mL−1, 74.31 ± 0.29 for 20 µg mL−1, 58.63 ± 0.42 for 30 µg mL−1, 41.89 ± 0.18 for 40 µg mL−1, 21.88 ± 0.38 for 50 µg mL−1, 10.76 ± 0.30 for 60 µg mL−1, and 3.27 ± 0.23 for 70 µg mL−1 (Fig. 7e). At concentrations of 80 µg mL−1 and above, cell viability was reduced to negligible levels; for instance, cell viability of A549 cancer cells was less than 1% at 80 µg mL−1 concentration of P. nigrum seed extract (Fig. 7e). In the case of cisplatin, the cell viability percentage was found to be 70.52 ± 0.41 for 10 µg mL−1, 52.28 ± 0.39 for 20 µg mL−1, 30.39 ± 0.22 for 30 µg mL−1, 15.10 ± 0.43 for 40 µg mL−1, and 2.83 ± 0.29 for 50 µg mL−1 (Fig. 7i). At concentrations of 60 µg mL−1 and above, cell viability was reduced to negligible levels; for example, the cell viability of A549 cancer cells was less than 1% at 60 µg mL−1 concentration of cisplatin (Fig. 7i). In the case of PNNO NPs, the cell viability percentage was found to be 73.01 ± 0.41 for 10 µg mL−1, 45.59 ± 0.47 for 20 µg mL−1, 22.86 ± 0.35 for 30 µg mL−1, and 5.07 ± 0.36 for 40 µg mL−1 (Fig. 7m). At concentrations of 50 µg mL−1 and above, cell viability was reduced to negligible levels; for instance, the cell viability of A549 cancer cells was less than 1% at 50 µg mL−1 concentration of PNNO NPs (Fig. 7m).

The dose–response relationship between P. nigrum seed extract concentration and A549 cell viability, cisplatin concentration and A549 cell viability, and PNNO NPs concentration and A549 cell viability is shown as line graphs in Fig. 7f, j, and n, respectively. From these dose–response graphs, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value was found to be 36 µg mL−1 for P. nigrum seed extract against A549 cancer cells (Fig. 7f), 21 µg mL−1 for cisplatin against A549 cancer cells (Fig. 7j), and 19 µg mL−1 for PNNO NPs against A549 cancer cells (Fig. 7n). Among the tested samples, the lowest IC50 value (19 µg mL−1) was for PNNO NPs, as compared to cisplatin (21 µg mL−1) and P. nigrum seed extract (36 µg mL−1), which signifies that the biosynthesized PNNO NPs demonstrate superior anticancer activity against A549 lung cancer cells compared to the crude P. nigrum seed extract and the standard anticancer drug cisplatin. The morphological images of untreated A549 cells (control cells) and A549 cells treated with P. nigrum seed extract, cisplatin, and PNNO NPs are depicted in Fig. 7 (g and h), (K and l), and (o and p), respectively. The control cells presented in Fig. 7(g, k and o) retained their original morphology, whereas the A549 cancer cells treated with P. nigrum seed extract (Fig. 7h), cisplatin (Fig. 7l), and PNNO NPs (Fig. 7p) underwent nuclear shrinkage and cell separation, which led to cancer cell death.

From the cell viability studies, it is apparent that at a 19 µg mL−1 concentration of PNNO NPs, the cell viability of A549 cancer cells was below 50%, whereas the viability of A549 cancer cells was slightly above 50% for cisplatin and above 74% for P. nigrum seed extract for the same concentration (Fig. 7e, i and m). It is also interesting to note that the PNNO NPs at 50 µg mL−1 were able to reduce the viability of A549 cancer cells to negligible levels, whereas 60 µg mL−1 of the anticancer drug cisplatin was required, and 80 µg mL−1 of P. nigrum seed extract was required to achieve the same effect (Fig. 7e, i, and m). From the dose–response graphs in Fig. 7f, j, and n, it was found that the cytotoxic potency of PNNO NPs (IC50 = 19 µg mL−1) is 1.89-fold greater compared to P. nigrum seed extract (IC50 = 36 µg mL−1), and 1.1-fold greater potency compared to cisplatin (IC50 = 21 µg mL−1) against A549 lung cancer cells. These observations indicate that P. nigrum seed extract exhibits weak anticancer activity when used alone, whereas the cytotoxicity of biosynthesized PNNO NPs is higher than that of the anticancer drug cisplatin against A549 cancer cells.

In the current study, biosynthesized PNNO NPs exhibited enhanced anticancer efficacy against A549 cancer cells compared to the anticancer drug cisplatin, with little effect on HEK293 cells. For comparison, cell viability studies indicated that more than 85% of cells were viable in HEK293 cells treated with 20 µg mL−1 of PNNO NPs (Fig. 7b), whereas the percentage of viable cells was below 46% in A549 cancer cells for the same PNNO NP concentration (Fig. 7n). Furthermore, the PNNO NPs at 50 µg mL−1 decreased the viability of A549 cancer cells to negligible levels, whereas more than 43% of HEK293 cells were viable after treatment with 50 µg mL−1 of PNNO NPs (Fig. 7b and n). Even at a higher concentration of PNNO NPs (100 µg mL−1), 7% of HEK293 cells remained viable.

AO/EtBr staining

AO/EtBr staining was performed to detect whether the A549 cancer cells underwent apoptosis after treatment with an IC50 concentration (19 µg mL−1) of PNNO NPs. The AO dye penetrates the membranes of viable and early apoptotic cells, intercalates with DNA, and emits green fluorescence. In contrast, the EtBr dye can only enter cells with damaged membranes, such as dead cells or those that are in the late stages of apoptosis, resulting in orange/red fluorescence.12,69–71 The AO/EtBr-stained images of the untreated cell lines (control) and PNNO-treated cell lines are depicted in Fig. 8a and b, respectively. As presented in Fig. 8a, the control cells only showed green fluorescence because of the absence of apoptotic bodies. The PNNO NP-treated cancer cells (Fig. 8b) showed a minimal trace of green and higher red/orange fluorescence, which signified the decreased presence of viable cells and additional apoptotic bodies in PNNO NP-induced A549 cancer cells.

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry analysis was carried out to quantify the proportion of viable, apoptotic, and necrotic cell populations in A549 cancer cells treated with the IC50 concentration (19 µg mL−1) of PNNO NPs. The quadrants B1, B2, B3, and B4 represent necrotic (FITC-negative and PI-positive), late apoptotic (FITC-positive and PI-positive), viable (FITC-negative and PI-negative), and early apoptotic cells (FITC-positive and PI-negative), respectively. Fig. 8c and d show the distribution of cell populations in control and PNNO NP-treated A549 cancer cells. As seen in Fig. 8c, the control cells exhibited 99.97% viability, 0.01% early apoptosis, 0.00% late apoptosis, and 0.03% necrosis, respectively. In contrast, PNNO NP-treated cells (Fig. 8d) showed 52.18%, 20.07%, 22.28%, and 5.47% of viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic cells, respectively. In comparison to the control cells, the PNNO NP-treated A549 cancer cells displayed a significant decrease in the population of viable cells from 99.97% to 52.18%, and a marked increase in the population of apoptotic cells (from 0.01 to 42.35%) and necrotic cells (from 0.03 to 5.47%). These flow cytometry results indicate that PNNO nanoparticles at an IC50 concentration of 19 µg mL−1 can effectively induce cell death in A549 cancer cells.

H2-DCFDA staining

In the majority of cases, anticancer drugs promote apoptosis in cancer cells by elevating the level of reactive oxygen species (ROS).72 Hence, H2-DCFDA staining was performed to determine the level of intercellular ROS in A549 cancer cells after treatment with the IC50 concentration (19 µg mL−1) of PNNO NPs. The H2-DCFDA-stained fluorescence images of control cells (untreated cell lines) and PNNO-treated cell lines are shown in Fig. 9a and b. The control cells pictured in Fig. 9a show minimal traces of green fluorescence, indicating the presence of ROS at low levels. The PNNO NP-treated cells (Fig. 9b) showed a significant increase in green fluorescence, which indicates a higher level of intercellular ROS activity in PNNO NP-induced A549 cancer cells.

H2DCFDA-stained images of (a) control cells, (b) PNNO NP-treated cells, (c) NAC-treated cells, and (d) NAC- and PNNO NP-treated cells. JC1-stained images of (e) control cells and (f) PNNO NP-treated cells. (g) Relative mRNA expression level of BCL2 in control and PNNO NP-treated cells.

To investigate whether ROS generation contributes to the cytotoxicity of PNNO NPs, A549 cells were exposed to PNNO NPs in the presence of the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC). Representative H2-DCFDA fluorescence images of NAC-treated A549 cells and PNNO NP-treated A549 cells in the presence of NAC are depicted in Fig. 9c and d. As shown in Fig. 9c, NAC-treated cells exhibited only minimal green fluorescence, indicating low basal intracellular ROS levels. Furthermore, the fluorescence image of A549 cells co-treated with PNNO NPs and NAC (Fig. 9d) showed that NAC markedly suppressed the ROS generation induced by the IC50 concentration of PNNO NPs, as evidenced by the reduced green fluorescence intensity. These findings strongly suggest that ROS generation plays a crucial role in PNNO NP-induced cytotoxicity.

JC-1 staining

Previous studies have reported that an increase in the level of ROS could cause a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), which is an early indicator of apoptosis.73,74 To investigate the effect of the IC50 concentration (19 µg mL−1) of PNNO NPs on MMP, the JC-1 staining assay was performed. As observed from the control cells in Fig. 9e, there is a greater amount of red fluorescence and a small trace of green fluorescence, indicating no depolarization in the MMP. In contrast, the PNNO NP-treated A549 cancer cells depicted in Fig. 9f showed a substantial shift from red to green fluorescence, signifying marked depolarization of the MMP and the initiation of mitochondrial-mediated apoptotic events.

qPCR studies

It has been reported that NPs upregulate pro-apoptotic genes and downregulate anti-apoptotic genes, thereby inducing apoptosis through the intrinsic mitochondrial-mediated pathway.48,75–77 To verify this, the mRNA expression levels of the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2 and the housekeeping gene β-actin were studied using qPCR for the untreated (control) and PNNO NP-treated A549 cancer cells (IC50: 19 µg mL−1). The raw cycle threshold (Ct) value of the control cells was 17.65 ± 0.64 for β-actin, and 31.97 ± 0.68 for BCL2. The mRNA expression level of PNNO-treated cancer cells was 17.48 ± 0.03 and 37.68 ± 0.67 for β-actin and BCL2, respectively. Using the β-actin gene as an internal control, BCL2 relative gene expression was determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method. From Fig. 9g, the relative gene expression of BCL2 in control cells and PNNO NP-treated A549 cancer cells is statistically significant, with a p-value < 0.05. Fig. 9g shows that the mRNA expression level of BCL2 in control cells was 55.61-fold higher compared to PNNO NP-treated A549 cancer cells. From the qPCR results, it is clearly evident that PNNO NPs significantly downregulated the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2 in A549 cancer cells, causing cell death through the intrinsic mitochondrial-mediated pathway.

It was observed in the present in vitro study that P. nigrum seed extract-mediated NiO NPs (PNNO) induced apoptotic cell death in the A549 lung cancer cell line by promoting oxidative stress and activating intrinsic mitochondrial-related pathways in the microenvironment. From Table 1, it is evident that the IC50 value of previously biosynthesized NIO NPs using Areca catechu65 is 4.91-fold (93.34 µg mL−1), Helianthus annuus66 is 2.66-fold (50.72 µg mL−1), Medicago sativa66 is 2.17-fold (41.37 µg mL−1), Euphorbia milii Des Moul.66 is 2.03-fold (38.73 µg mL−1), Fioria vitifolia (L.) Mattei67 is 1.48-fold (28.15 µg mL−1), Codiaeum variegatum (L.) A. Juss.66 is 1.52-fold (25.92 µg mL−1), and Macroptilium lathyroides68 is 1.32-fold (25.12 µg mL−1) higher than the IC50 value of Piper nigrum seed extract-mediated NiO (PNNO) NPs (19 µg mL−1), which indicates that PNNO NPs exhibit significantly higher cytotoxic effects against A549 lung cancer cells.

The enhanced anticancer activity could be due to the increased surface-to-volume ratio, attributed to the small crystallite size of PNNO NPs (16.30 nm) as determined from the XRD Debye–Scherrer formula (Fig. 1a), which led to a higher number of NP interactions with cancer cells, causing increased ROS production and an imbalance in the antioxidant defence mechanism.78,79 A noticeable increase in the green fluorescence for PNNO NP-treated cancer cells was observed from the H2DCFDA assay (Fig. 9b), which emphasizes that the small-sized PNNO NPs significantly elevated the intercellular reactive oxygen species level in A549 cancer cells. This observation is consistent with previous studies that showed a similar ROS-producing effect of metal oxide NPs in A549 cancer cells.78–80 Furthermore, a marked increase in the shift from red to green fluorescence for PNNO NP-treated cancer cells was found from JC-1 staining (Fig. 9f), which revealed that excessive production of ROS within cancer cells induces mitochondrial dysfunction by causing depolarization of the membrane potential.81

There is increasing evidence that the anti-apoptotic BCL2 exerts a death-sparing effect by controlling mitochondrial membrane permeability, whereas the pro-apoptotic BAX translocates to the mitochondria in response to stress signals and counteracts the anti-apoptotic activity of BCL2.75,77 An increase in the BAX/BCL2 ratio, driven by the downregulation of anti-apoptotic BCL2, induces permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane, and thereby promotes the release of cytochrome c from the mitochondria.75,77 Once released into the cytosol, cytochrome c binds to apoptotic protease activating factor-1 (APAF1), leading to the activation of initiator caspase-9.75 The activated caspase-9 subsequently triggers the activation of executioner caspase-3, culminating in apoptotic cell death.75,77 A previous study showed that NiO NPs exerted cytotoxic effects in HepG2 liver cancer cells by upregulating the mRNA expression level of BAX and caspase-9, and downregulating BCL2.75 In the current study, the prepared PNNO NPs were found to significantly downregulate the mRNA expression level of the anti-apoptotic gene BCL2, which could lead to intrinsic mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis.

A significant increase in orange/red fluorescence was observed from AO/EtBr-stained images of PNNO NP-treated cells (Fig. 8b), and a higher percentage of early apoptotic cells, late apoptotic cells, and necrotic cells determined from the flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 8d) proves that the IC50 concentration of PNNO NPs (19 µg mL−1) qualitatively and quantitatively induces cell death in A549 cancer cells. The flow cytometry results of this study (viable: 52.18%; early apoptotic: 20.07%; late apoptotic: 22.28%; necrotic: 5.47%) with an IC50 treatment of PNNO NPs (19 µg mL−1) were comparable to those of previously reported metal oxide NPs such as TiO2 with an IC50 value of 76.25 µg mL−1 (viable: 53.85%; early apoptotic: 18.39%; late apoptotic: 13.34%; necrotic: 14.42%), and CuO with an IC50 value of 147.48 µg mL−1 (viable: 58.06%; early apoptotic: 11.80%; late apoptotic: 29.23%; necrotic: 0.91%) against A549 cancer cells.80,82 Even with a lesser IC50 value of 19 µg mL−1, PNNO NPs induced a 1.33-fold (42.35 vs. 31.73) and 1.03-fold (42.35 vs. 41.03) higher apoptotic population in comparison with TiO2 80 and CuO,82 respectively.

These encouraging results suggest that PNNO NPs may be potent for treating lung cancer. The possible anticancer mechanism of PNNO NPs against A549 lung cancer cells is that the small-sized PNNO NPs induce mitochondrial damage by increasing ROS levels, lowering mitochondrial membrane potential, and increasing the BAX/BCL2 ratio by significantly downregulating the anti-apoptotic BCL2, which causes permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane, thereby leading to intrinsic mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis.

Conclusion

The utilization of aqueous extract from Piper nigrum seeds resulted in PNNO NPs with small sizes ranging from 16 to 36 nm, a characteristic absorbance peak near 325 nm, an optical band gap value of 2.77 eV, and the presence of surface defects, all of which favored the observed anticancer effects. In vitro anticancer investigation revealed that PNNO NPs induce higher cytotoxicity towards A549 cancer cells and exhibit 1.89-fold greater cytotoxic potency compared to P. nigrum seed extract, and 1.1-fold greater potency compared to cisplatin against A549 lung cancer cells. The results of various biochemical assays, including H2-DCFDA, JC-1, and dual AO/EtBr staining, revealed that PNNO NP treatment led to an excessive production of reactive oxygen species in A549 cancer cells, which caused mitochondrial dysfunction that resulted in apoptosis.

Furthermore, the flow cytometry analysis quantified the proportion of viable (52.18%), apoptotic (42.35%), and necrotic (5.47%) cells, which indicated that the PNNO induced A549 cancer cell death. The qPCR studies revealed that the BCL2 level in PNNO NP-treated A549 cancer cells was significantly downregulated in comparison to the control. This downregulation could lead to the initiation of apoptotic events, which ultimately cause the death of A549 cancer cells. Nevertheless, a detailed in vivo study with an animal model is warranted to validate the therapeutic efficacy and biosafety of PNNO NPs before translation into a clinical trial.
