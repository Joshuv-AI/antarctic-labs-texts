Clinicopathological characteristics of BRCA1-associated breast carcinoma patients in Kazakhstan
Authors: Ainur Samigatova, Nursulu Altaeva, Yerlan Suleimenov, Petr Sibiryakov, Kuantkan Zhabagin, Zhanar Zhakypbekkyzy, Bakhytzhan Seksenbaev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBreast cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide, including Kazakhstan. Germline mutations in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of breast cancer, particularly in its aggressive subtypes. Despite the well-established impact of BRCA1-associated tumors on clinical outcomes, their specific characteristics within the Kazakhstani population remain insufficiently studied. This knowledge gap underscores the necessity for further research, including the present study.ObjectiveTo analyze the clinical, morphological, and molecular-biological features of BRCA1-associated breast carcinomas in Kazakhstani patients, with the aim of identifying more effective strategies for diagnosis and treatment.Materials and methodsThis study included 86 female patients diagnosed with primary invasive breast cancer who received treatment at the Oncology Center in Astana between December 2023 and June 2024. This retrospective study was conducted at a single center. Molecular genetic testing was performed via next-generation sequencing to identify pathogenic variants in the BRCA1 gene. Clinical and pathological data were retrospectively extracted from patients’ medical records.ResultsThe mean age at diagnosis for patients with BRCA1-associated breast cancer was 44 years, which is 6 years younger than that of patients without BRCA1 mutations (50 years). BRCA1 mutation carriers more frequently presented with advanced-stage disease, higher histological grade, and elevated proliferative activity. The incidence of metastasis was significantly greater among BRCA1-positive patients (41.7% vs. 5.4%, p = 0.002). Compared with BRCA1-negative tumors, BRCA1-positive carcinomas exhibit markedly lower expression of estrogen and progesterone receptors, a molecular signature characteristic of triple-negative breast cancer.DiscussionThe findings of this study confirm that BRCA1-associated breast carcinomas are distinguished by distinct clinicopathological features, including early onset, aggressive biological behavior, and high-grade histology. These characteristics highlight the importance of personalized treatment and diagnostic strategies and underscore the need for tailored clinical risk assessments for Kazakhstani patients.ConclusionThis study revealed significant differences in the clinical and morphological characteristics of BRCA1-associated breast carcinomas among Kazakhstani women. These results provide a foundation for improving diagnostic and therapeutic approaches in Kazakhstan and for developing population-specific clinical guidelines.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide and remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality, including in the Republic of Kazakhstan. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 2.3 million new cases of BC were diagnosed globally in 2020 [1]. Each year, an estimated 685,000 women die from this disease, making it a major contributor to female cancer mortality [2]. A similar epidemiological trend is observed in Kazakhstan, where over 5,500 new cases of BC and more than 1,000 related deaths were registered in 2023 [3]. The five-year survival rate for BC patients varies significantly depending on multiple factors, including tumor biology, stage at diagnosis, and genetic background. Notably, approximately 10–15% of BC cases are hereditary in origin, primarily associated with germline pathogenic variants (PVs) in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes [4]. Genetic predispositions, particularly germline mutations in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, play a critical role in the development and progression of breast cancer [5]. These genes are essential for homologous recombination repair of double-strand DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid) breaks. Mutations compromise the accuracy of this repair mechanism, leading to defective DNA damage resolution [6, 7]. Also, they are frequently mutated in hereditary BC [8] and, in some cases, allele-specific BRCA1 mRNA levels are significantly decreased in sporadic forms of BC [9]. Breast carcinomas associated with BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations present distinct histopathological characteristics, specifically BRCA1-associated hereditary breast cancers among Japanese patients exhibit biologically aggressive phenotypes, whereas BRCA2-associated cases are clinically and pathologically indistinguishable from sporadic breast tumors [10].

Recent advances in molecular genetics have revealed significant differences in tumor biology and the clinical course between breast cancers harboring BRCA1 mutations and those harboring BRCA2 mutations [11, 12]. Although both genes encode tumor suppressor proteins essential for maintaining genomic integrity, loss-of-function mutations in either gene result in increased susceptibility to BC due to defective DNA repair mechanisms and in turn lead to distinctive molecular profiles compared with sporadic cases [13, 14]. Specifically, BRCA1-associated BC represents a unique molecular and clinical subtype of hereditary breast cancer [15]. Mutations in the BRCA1 tumor suppressor gene account for a significant proportion of hereditary breast cancers, with a lifetime risk of disease development approaching about 80% in mutation carriers [16].

Given the overlapping clinical significance of BRCA1 and BRCA2 in hereditary breast cancer syndromes, it is important to consider their combined impact on disease risk and clinical outcomes. Carriers of PVs in the BRCA1 or BRCA2 genes face a substantially elevated lifetime risk of developing breast cancer compared with the general population. Beyond increasing susceptibility to primary breast cancer, these variants also confer a markedly higher risk of contralateral disease, with annual incidence rates increasing from approximately 0.5% in unselected patients to as high as 3% among BRCA1/2 carriers. In a recent cohort of 139 patients with bilateral breast cancer (BBC) who underwent next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based multi-gene panel testing, germline PVs were detected in 37.4% of cases across both high- and intermediate-penetrance susceptibility genes. Notably, 14.4% of these variants would have been missed without the use of a multi-gene approach, highlighting the clinical relevance of comprehensive genetic screening for all BBC patients, regardless of family history or age at diagnosis. These observations underscore the global need for broad genetic testing strategies and provide an important context for the present study [17].

Contemporary oncogenetic research emphasizes the need for differentiated analysis of BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations, given their divergent roles in tumor pathogenesis, clinical behavior, and morphological features [18, 19]. Although both mutations confer a markedly elevated risk of BC [20], substantial evidence supports the distinct biological and clinical nature of BRCA1-associated tumors [21]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that BRCA1-mutated breast cancers are typically more aggressive, occur at a younger age, and are more frequently triple-negative (ER-, PR-, HER2-) than those associated with BRCA2 mutations [22–24]. In contrast, BRCA2-associated tumors tend to be hormone receptor positive [25] and are associated with more favorable prognostic indicators [26]. BRCA1-related carcinomas are often poorly differentiated (G3), exhibit high recurrence rates, and are associated with poor survival outcomes [27, 28].

Furthermore, the pathogenic mechanisms underlying BRCA-deficient tumors are closely linked to impaired DNA repair and unique immunogenic interactions, highlighting their potential responsiveness to targeted therapies, particularly PARP inhibitors [29]. Given the distinct clinicobiological profile of BRCA1-associated BC and the paucity of population-specific data in Kazakhstan, there is a compelling need to investigate the mutation spectrum and associated tumor characteristics within this population.

One of the defining molecular features of BRCA1-mutated breast tumors is their receptor status. The majority of such tumors demonstrate a triple-negative phenotype—lacking expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2/neu—greatly limiting the efficacy of hormonal and HER2-targeted therapies [30]. This phenotype significantly limits the range of available therapeutic strategies, as it excludes the possibility of targeted hormonal and HER2-directed therapy. Triple-negative breast cancers are considered among the most aggressive subtypes, with poorer clinical outcomes and limited therapeutic options. The established association between BRCA1 mutations and triple-negative disease has significant clinical implications for tumor classification, treatment planning, and prognostication [31, 32].

Several international studies have explored the distinguishing clinical and pathological features of BRCA1-related tumors in an effort to refine diagnostic criteria and risk stratification [33, 34]. The high biological aggressiveness of these tumors is reflected in their predominance as histological grade 3 (G3) neoplasms, which is consistent with poor differentiation and unfavorable prognostic profiles [35]. Moreover, the reduced expression of hormone receptors among BRCA1 mutation carriers further presents therapeutic challenges and contributes to worse outcomes [36].

Despite the high incidence of BC among women in Kazakhstan, the role of BRCA1 mutations in disease pathogenesis, clinicopathologic heterogeneity, and progression remains underexplored. The absence of comprehensive molecular genetic studies in the Kazakhstani population has created critical gaps in our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying aggressive BC phenotypes in this cohort. The literature, which is largely derived from non-Kazakhstani populations, indicates that BRCA1 mutations are associated with a spectrum of unfavorable features, including early onset, high-grade histology, triple-negative status, and poor prognosis. Therefore, there is an urgent need to characterize BRCA1-associated BC specifically in Kazakhstani women. Population-specific studies are essential for elucidating how ethnicity and genetic background may influence molecular tumor profiles and clinical behavior.

We hypothesize that Kazakhstani patients with BRCA1-associated BC exhibit distinct clinical, histopathological, and molecular characteristics compared with noncarriers. The identification of these features is of critical clinical importance, as it may inform more individualized approaches for early detection, therapeutic decision-making, and prognostic assessment. The present study aimed to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the clinical, pathological, and molecular-biological attributes of these tumors and to generate practical recommendations for improving diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic outcomes in the Kazakhstani population.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study was based on the analysis of molecular genetic testing results obtained from peripheral blood samples of 86 patients, aged between 21 and 90 years, diagnosed with BC. Between December 2023 and June 2024, patients with primary invasive breast cancer of all clinical stages receiving treatment at the Oncology Center in Astana were consecutively enrolled in the study, regardless of age or family history of cancer. Voluntary screening for BRCA1 mutations was conducted following written informed consent obtained prospectively from all participants. Informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment and before collection of clinical and genetic data, in full compliance with institutional ethical standards and the Declaration of Helsinki. Genetic testing was performed using next-generation sequencing (NGS) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) after DNA extraction from peripheral blood. All laboratory procedures were carried out at the Diagnostic Laboratory “OLYMP” in Astana. As part of the pre-study protocol, each participant completed a detailed questionnaire detailing family history of malignancies, with particular focus on breast, ovarian, gastric, pancreatic, and prostate cancers. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Local Bioethics Committee of the Oncology Center in Astana (Approval No. 14, dated December 15, 2023).

Clinical data collection

Clinical and anamnestic data were collected through patient interviews and review of medical records. These data included age at diagnosis, family history of cancer, tumor size, axillary lymph node status, preoperative therapy, histopathological diagnosis, and immunohistochemical (IHC) profile. Molecular subtypes of breast cancer were defined based on IHC assessment in accordance with ASCO/CAP guidelines: ER and PR positivity was defined as ≥ 1% tumor cell nuclei staining [37]; HER2 positivity was defined as IHC score 3 + or 2 + with confirmed gene amplification by FISH [38]; triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) was defined as ER-negative, PR-negative, and HER2-negative.

Detection of BRCA1 mutations

Identification of BRCA1 mutations was performed using two approaches:

Next-generation sequencing (NGS): A subset of patients (n = 72) underwent comprehensive BRCA1 gene sequencing using NGS, a highly sensitive and specific technique covering all coding exons and flanking intronic regions. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral venous blood using commercially available reagent kits. Library preparation was performed with the “Quasar-BRCA1/2” reagent kit according to TU 21.20.23-038-97638376-2020 (TestGen, Russia), adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was executed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, USA). The average sequencing depth exceeded 50×, allowing reliable detection of heterozygous and homozygous variants. Bioinformatic analysis included quality filtering, alignment to the human reference genome, and identification of nucleotide substitutions and other variant types. All detected variants were classified according to ACMG pathogenicity criteria established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [39]. Among NGS-tested patients, 11 were carriers of BRCA1 PVs, whereas 74 patients were negative for pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with real-time detection: Additionally, one patient was tested via PCR-based multiplex real-time detection for a pathogenic BRCA1 mutation. This approach, using the “BRCA1,2-Diagnostics” reagent kit (TestGen, Russia), identifies only common recurrent (founder) mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 and does not detect rare variants. Consequently, patients with negative results by this PCR method were excluded from the study, as it does not provide comprehensive mutation detection like NGS.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed by comparing clinical and tumor characteristics using χ² test or Fisher’s exact test when the expected frequencies were < 5. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using SPSS-PC version 28.

Results

The mean age at diagnosis for the 12 patients with BRCA1-associated carcinomas was 44 years, whereas it was 50 years for the 74 women without BRCA1 mutations (95% CI: 40.4–48.0 and 48.5–53.0, respectively). Patients with BRCA1-positive carcinomas more frequently presented with advanced-stage disease and metastatic involvement (Table 1). Moreover, these tumors demonstrated high histological grades and elevated proliferative activity (Tables 2 and 3). Despite more pronounced lymph node involvement and larger tumor sizes in the BRCA1-positive group, no statistically significant differences were observed compared with the control group (Table 1). Additionally, 54 patients received neoadjuvant chemotherapy in accordance with the malignancy treatment protocol of the Republic of Kazakhstan. BRCA1-associated carcinomas were significantly more likely to be negative for estrogen and progesterone receptors (both p = 0.001). HER2/neu overexpression was less common in BRCA1-positive patients, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.06). Notably, two BRCA1-positive patients were diagnosed with concurrent ovarian cancer.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated and confirmed the unique clinicopathological features of BRCA1-associated breast carcinoma compared with those of tumors in patients without BRCA1 mutations within the Kazakhstani population.

A key observation was the significantly younger age at diagnosis among BRCA1 mutation carriers compared with noncarriers (mean age 44 years vs. 50 years, 95% CI: 40.4–48.0 and 48.5–53.0, respectively). This finding aligns with previous studies, confirming that BRCA1 mutation carriers are more likely to develop breast cancer at an earlier age [40, 41], which is associated with impaired DNA repair mechanisms and enhanced proliferative activity of tumor cells [42]. Further research has emphasized [43] that early disease onset is a major risk factor for the presence of mutations in genes predisposing patients to BC development. The probability of detecting BRCA1 mutations in women under 45 years of age with diagnosed BC substantially exceeds that in the general population. A strong association has been established between age ≤ 45 years at diagnosis and the presence of pathogenic BRCA1 variants (OR 3.95, 95% CI: 3.64–4.29) [44]. Additional data confirm this pattern: a study by Zang et al. (2022) revealed that the prevalence of pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants in patients with early BC (age ≤ 40 years) was significantly greater than that in patients over 40 years (9.7% vs. 5.1%) [45].

An important finding of our study was the absence of BRCA1 mutation carriers among patients diagnosed at stage I, which distinctly differentiates this group from BRCA1-negative patients (p = 0.006) and reflects the more aggressive clinical course of BRCA1-associated BC, typically diagnosed at later stages. Specifically, the proportion of stage IV disease was significantly higher among BRCA1-positive patients (41.7%) than among BRCA1-negative patients (5.4%). These results are consistent with the PRAEGNANT study, which revealed a significantly higher frequency of BRCA1 mutations in patients with metastatic BC compared with nonmetastatic BC (10.4% vs. 6.6%, p < 0.01) [46]. Similar findings were reported in the international observational study BREAKOUT, which examined BRCA1/2 prevalence among patients with metastatic HER2-negative breast cancer. The study demonstrated a higher frequency of BRCA1/2 mutations in this cohort, confirming their association with a more aggressive clinical course [47].

In our study, tumor size did not differ significantly between the groups (p = 0.358), aligning with previous reports. According to several studies [48, 49], BRCA1-associated tumors are characterized less by increased size and more by high biological aggressiveness and rapid progression. The relationship between BRCA1 mutations and tumor size in BC remains a topic of discussion. Some studies suggest a trend toward increased tumor size in BRCA1 mutation carriers, indicating a more aggressive disease course. Soman et al. (2023) reported that in 57.1% of BRCA1 mutation carriers, the tumor size ranged from 2 to 5 cm, with a significant correlation to mutation status (p < 0.01; r = 0.714) [50]. However, other studies, including retrospective analyses from Turkey and Heerma van Voss et al. (2010), did not find significant differences in tumor size, type, or axillary lymph node involvement based on BRCA mutation profile [51, 52].

The high frequency of metastases in BRCA1 mutation carriers (41.7% vs. 5.4% in BRCA1-negative patients, p = 0.002) is noteworthy, indicating a greater tendency for tumor dissemination and explaining the predominance of stage IV disease. Several studies have confirmed the increased risk of metastasis in BRCA1 mutation carriers, with central nervous system metastases occurring more frequently in BRCA1 carriers (53% BRCA1, 50% BRCA2, 25% noncarriers; p < 0.001) [53]. Additionally, rapid tumor spread has been associated with high BRCA1 expression, highlighting its potential role in aggressive metastasis mechanisms [54].

Axillary lymph node involvement did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.726), although the total number of patients with lymph node metastases was slightly higher among BRCA1-negative patients, potentially reflecting differences in invasion mechanisms due to BRCA1 mutation. Data on this issue are contradictory. For instance, one study reported lymph node metastases in 45.9% of 74 BRCA mutation carriers vs. 36% of noncarriers (p = 0.15) [55]. Other studies indicate a trend toward increased lymphovascular invasion in BRCA1 mutation carriers, although differences often did not reach statistical significance [56].

Histopathological analysis confirmed that BRCA1-associated breast carcinomas exhibited higher histological grades and elevated proliferative activity. Among the BRCA1-positive tumors, grade distribution was as follows: G1, 1 patient; G2, 4 patients; and G3, 7 patients (Table 2). The predominance of grade 3 (G3) tumors reflects aggressive biological behavior and rapid progression, consistent with international studies. These findings are consistent with international studies reporting that most BRCA1-associated breast carcinomas are high-grade malignancies that exhibit marked mitotic activity and pronounced nuclear pleomorphism [57]. Extensive research has examined the impact of BRCA1 mutations on tumor cell differentiation, with numerous studies corroborating the observed trends. For example, Bujassoum et al. (2017) reported that 74% of BRCA1-associated breast tumors were classified as grade 3, which was correlated with elevated proliferative indices, increased mitotic figures, and a high frequency of necrotic changes [58]. Similarly, other investigations have demonstrated significantly higher tumor grades and proliferation indices in women harboring pathogenic or likely pathogenic BRCA1 variants than in noncarriers [59].

Molecularly, BRCA1-associated tumors displayed distinct receptor status differences. Estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression was observed in only 25% of BRCA1-positive tumors, compared with 95.8% in BRCA1-negative tumors (p < 0.001), reaffirming low hormonal receptor expression in BRCA1-associated cancers [57]. The association between BRCA1 mutations and TNBC has been widely confirmed, with approximately 80% of BRCA1 mutation carriers developing TNBC. Larsen et al. (2013) reported that most BRCA1-associated tumors exhibit a basal-like phenotype, unlike BRCA2-associated tumors, which are predominantly ER-positive [62]. HER2/neu overexpression was not observed in BRCA1-positive patients, while all 20 HER2/neu-positive tumors were BRCA1-negative (p = 0.06), suggesting a lower frequency of HER2/neu amplification in BRCA1 mutation carriers, though not statistically significant due to sample size. These findings support the classification of BRCA1-associated BC as predominantly triple-negative, with poorer prognosis, limited targeted/endocrine therapy options, and heightened sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors [63].

From a molecular standpoint, compared with BRCA1-negative tumors, BRCA1-associated tumors in our study presented distinct differences in receptor status. Estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) expression was observed in only 25% of BRCA1-positive tumors, whereas in the BRCA1-negative group, receptor expression was present in 95.8% of cases (p < 0.001). These results reaffirm the low hormonal receptor expression in BRCA1-associated tumors and are in agreement with existing global data. BRCA1 mutation carriers have a significantly greater likelihood of developing estrogen receptor-negative BC (p < 0.001), with only approximately 3.9% of such tumors exhibiting ER positivity [60].

The association between BRCA1 mutations and the development of TNBC has been widely investigated and confirmed. Carraro, D. M. et al. (2013) reported that approximately 80% of BRCA1 mutation carriers develop TNBC [61]. In addition, Larsen, M. J., et al. (2013) reported that majority of BRCA1-associated tumors exhibit a basal-like phenotype, in contrast to BRCA2-associated tumors, which are predominantly estrogen receptor-positive [62]. HER2/neu overexpression was not detected in BRCA1-positive patients in our cohort, whereas all 20 patients with HER2/neu-positive tumors were BRCA1-negative (p = 0.06). Although this suggests a lower frequency of HER2/neu amplification among BRCA1 mutation carriers, the difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited sample size. These findings support the characterization of BRCA1-associated breast cancers as predominantly triple-negative (ER-, PR-, HER2-), a subtype associated with poorer prognosis, limited options for targeted and endocrine therapy, and heightened sensitivity to platinum-based chemotherapy and PARP inhibitors [63]. Notably, both patients in our study who were diagnosed with ovarian cancer (2 out of 2) were BRCA1 mutation carriers, whereas no cases of ovarian cancer were observed among BRCA1-negative patients (p = 0.018). This observation is consistent with the well-established increased risk of both breast and ovarian cancers in BRCA1 mutation carriers, as described in numerous international studies [64]. In interpreting our findings, it is important to place the clinicopathological features observed in BRCA1-associated breast carcinoma within the broader context of both BRCA1-negative cases and previously published data from other populations. In our cohort, BRCA1-positive tumors demonstrated a higher prevalence of triple-negative phenotype and high histological grade compared with the general breast cancer population in Kazakhstan, consistent with global reports [20, 65]. Similar patterns have been described in European, North American, and East Asian cohorts, where BRCA1-associated tumors are typically characterized by aggressive histopathological features, early age at onset, and reduced hormone receptor expression [66, 67]. While direct statistical comparison with BRCA1-negative patients was beyond the scope of the present study, the alignment of our observations with these established trends supports the biological consistency of our results and underscores the potential relevance of our data to hereditary breast cancer research in diverse populations. Future multicenter studies including both BRCA1-positive and BRCA1-negative patients from Kazakhstan will be essential to formally quantify these differences and assess their prognostic implications.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size, the retrospective nature of the study, and the possibility of selection bias may limit the statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Second, the study was conducted in a single oncology center, which may reduce the applicability of the results to broader populations. Third, the absence of long-term follow-up and outcome data prevents evaluation of the prognostic significance of the identified BRCA1 variants. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up are needed to confirm and expand upon these observations.

Conclusion

The findings of this study provide compelling evidence that PVs in the BRCA1 gene are associated with the development of more aggressive forms of breast cancer characterized by distinct clinicopathological features. These include early age of onset, high histological grade, low hormonal receptor expression, and frequent absence of HER2/neu expression. These attributes substantially limit the efficacy of standard treatment regimens and necessitate personalized therapeutic approaches. The high frequency of metastatic disease and the predominance of late-stage diagnosis in BRCA1-associated patients highlight the critical importance of identifying BRCA1 mutation carriers and expanding genetic screening programs among high-risk populations. Collectively, these results underscore the need for further research aimed at elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the aggressiveness of BRCA1-driven tumors and enhancing strategies for their early detection and management.
