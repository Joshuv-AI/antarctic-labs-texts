Refining cardiovascular risk prediction after renal transplantation: a cross-sectional study focusing on NT-proBNP and the revised cardiac risk index
Authors: Tara Moghaddasfar, Fereshteh Beiranvandi, Maryam Mehrpooya, Reza Bahramrafiee, Babak Geraiely, Farnoosh Larti, Seyyed Mohammad Reza Khatami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular complications significantly influence outcomes in kidney transplant recipients, predominantly driven by the multifactorial risks associated with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). This study investigates the utility of N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and the revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) in predicting cardiovascular events (CVE) after kidney transplantation (KT), aiming to refine risk assessment and management strategies pre-transplantation.MethodsFrom May 2021 to May 2023, 122 ESRD patients scheduled for kidney transplantation were enrolled in a prospective cohort study at a single center. Pre-transplant NT-proBNP levels and RCRI scores were evaluated for their predictive accuracy for in-hospital and 1-month post-transplant CVEs.ResultsThe median age of participants was 47.7 years. Pre-existing hypertension was present in 80.3%, diabetes in 26.2%, smoking history in 9.0%, and cardiovascular disease in 23%. A revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) score of 1 was present in 64.8% of cases. Over half of the patients had NT-proBNP levels above 1200 pg/mL. Patients with higher NT-proBNP levels exhibited lower left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and higher systolic pulmonary artery pressure (PAP). Patients who experienced at least one CVE were older, with a trend toward a higher NT-proBNP level in this population. The RCRI showed no significant difference between patients with NT-proBNP levels above or below 1200 pg/mL. Still, it differed among patients with at least one CVE compared to those without. After adjusting the NT-proBNP level, RCRI = 3 had an odds ratio of 9.6 (1.6–57.4) for cardiovascular endpoints compared to those with RCRI 1 and 2.ConclusionNT-proBNP alone could not predict 30-day CVE after KT. Regarding the trend toward higher NT-proBNP levels in individuals with at least one CVE, further studies with a larger sample size should be done to assess whether integrating NT-proBNP and RCRI before KT can improve cardiovascular outcomes in this high-risk population.Keywords: NT-proBNP, End-stage renal disease, Kidney transplantation, Cardiovascular event, Cardiovascular risk

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases pose a significant challenge in managing patients undergoing kidney transplantation, reflecting a complex interplay between end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and increased cardiovascular risk (Stevens et al. 2024).

Cardiovascular assessment is highlighted as crucial before any major non-cardiac surgery, especially for kidney transplant candidates. The guidelines recommend a comprehensive evaluation approach, including echocardiograms, stress tests, and coronary angiography. Echocardiography is advised for evaluating left ventricular function and other structural heart conditions. At the same time, coronary angiography is recommended for more detailed visualization of the coronary arteries to identify any significant obstructions or atherosclerotic disease, according to the guidelines. These diagnostic tools help assess cardiovascular health and address potential complications proactively (Thompson et al. 2024).

N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) is a biomarker released by cardiac myocytes in response to ventricular volume expansion and pressure overload (Stevens et al. 2024; Thompson et al. 2024). ESRD often exacerbates these conditions due to fluid overload and altered hemodynamics (Stevens et al. 2024). Importantly, ESRD can elevate NT-proBNP levels independently of heart failure, complicating the interpretation of this biomarker in kidney transplant candidates (Stevens et al. 2024).

Major guidelines have partly integrated NT-proBNP measurement into standard preoperative evaluation, reflecting the result of recent research, which highlights its predictive potential. The 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on cardiovascular assessment before non-cardiac surgery recommend NT-proBNP measurement in the following situations: class I in suspected or known heart failure (HF) patients scheduled for high-risk non-cardiac surgery and class IIa in patients with known cardiovascular (CV) disease, CV risk factors, or symptoms suggestive of CV disease before intermediate and high-risk non-cardiac surgeries. The 2022 ESC guideline did not provide specific recommendations for patients before kidney transplantation (KT). The reason stems from the elevated level of NT-proBNP in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients—attributed mainly to fluid overload and impaired clearance—that precludes reliance on the routine NT-proBNP for all kidney transplant candidates to estimate the risk of the surgery (Halvorsen et al. 2022).

Similarly, the 2024 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guideline acknowledges NT-proBNP’s utility as a biomarker for cardiovascular risk stratification with class IIa in patients with known CV diseases, age ≥ 65 years, or those with age ≥ 45 years with CV disease symptoms before elevated risk non-cardiac surgery, with no focus or specific recommendation before KT (Thompson et al. 2024).

Notably, a study by Schwab et al. demonstrated that elevated pre-transplant NT-proBNP levels significantly correlate with an increased risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) in transplant recipients, underscoring its critical role in preoperative risk assessment (Schwab et al. 2023). Additionally, research by Jia et al. indicated that longitudinal changes in NT-proBNP levels could predict heart failure and cardiovascular mortality, emphasizing its potential utility for ongoing monitoring in transplant recipients (Jia et al. 2023).

This underscores the need for further research into the prognostic value of NT-proBNP within the transplant setting. Our study complements this approach by investigating whether combining NT-proBNP measurements with the revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) enhances cardiovascular risk prediction in the ESRD population before KT.

Beyond NT-proBNP, several other biomarkers have been explored for cardiovascular risk prediction in transplant populations. High-sensitivity cardiac troponins (hs-cTnT and hs-cTnI) have been associated with major adverse cardiovascular events and mortality among ESRD and kidney transplant patients (Emrich et al. 2022). Copeptin, a surrogate marker of vasopressin, has also shown prognostic value in dialysis and transplant populations (Firth et al. 2019). Inflammatory markers such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) have been correlated with worse cardiovascular outcomes in both ESRD and transplant cohorts (Cos Gomez et al. 2020).

Moreover, some studies suggest that a multi-marker approach—integrating natriuretic peptides, troponins, and inflammatory biomarkers—may offer enhanced prognostic accuracy compared to individual biomarkers alone (Yeung et al. 2020).

Recent studies have also evaluated the revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) in the context of kidney transplantation. Patients in this setting often present with complex cardiovascular profiles due to ESRD (Palamuthusingam et al. 2024; Rocha Olsen et al. 2021). The RCRI, which includes factors such as ischemic heart disease, congestive heart failure, diabetes requiring insulin, renal insufficiency (serum creatinine ≥ 2 mg/dL), and cerebrovascular disease, has traditionally been used to predict perioperative cardiovascular risks in non-cardiac surgeries (Stevens et al. 2024; Thompson et al. 2024). However, its application in kidney transplantation has yielded mixed results. Some studies suggest that the RCRI may underestimate risk in this specific patient population, potentially due to the unique pathophysiological changes associated with chronic kidney disease and the transplant procedure itself (Palamuthusingam et al. 2024). Other research indicates that with certain modifications—such as adjusting for dialysis status and incorporating novel biomarkers like NT-proBNP—the predictive accuracy of the RCRI can be significantly improved (Rocha Olsen et al. 2021). These adaptations aim better to capture the heightened cardiovascular risk inherent to transplant recipients and tailor perioperative management accordingly (Palamuthusingam et al. 2024; Rocha Olsen et al. 2021).

Interestingly, while the guidelines extensively cover the use of various risk assessment tools, such as the revised cardiac risk index (RCRI), and emphasize a patient-centered, team-based approach to perioperative care, they do not explicitly integrate NT-proBNP into standard preoperative evaluation protocols for all patient groups, including those undergoing kidney transplantation. Instead, the guidelines focus on specific perioperative considerations and general cardiovascular risk management strategies.

Our study aims to assess the effectiveness of NT-proBNP in the variable clinical scenario of kidney transplantation, expressed as different RCRI scores. We also seek to refine the predictive accuracy of cardiovascular risk assessments and enhance patient management strategies.

Methods

Study population

This single-center, prospective cohort study enrolled all ESRD patients eligible for kidney transplantation (KT) between May 2021 and May 2023. This study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. The Research Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences approved the study protocol (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.309), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All ESRD patients on routine hemodialysis who were considered for kidney transplantation (KT) were included. Exclusion criteria included the absence of NT-proBNP levels on the day before transplantation (7 patients), simultaneous kidney and pancreas transplantation (8 patients), simultaneous kidney and heart transplantation (2 patients), and a prior history of kidney transplantation (1 patient). This resulted in a final cohort of 122 patients (Fig. 1).

Summary of patient enrollment

Study protocol

Nephrologists determined KT indications, while cardiac evaluations were conducted independently by a cardiologist involved in pre-transplant assessments, in accordance with current guidelines. A history of cardiovascular disease encompassed prior coronary revascularization (PCI or CABG), chronic stable angina, chronic heart failure, previous valve surgery, cardiac resynchronization therapy, or an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. Routine laboratory tests, including NT-proBNP measurement, were performed the day before transplantation, with NT-proBNP drawn 4 h before the final hemodialysis session. Elevated NT-proBNP levels were defined as ≥1200 pg/mL, based on research by Anwaruddin et al. (2006).

All the patients had deceased donor transplants. During the post-transplant period, patients were closely monitored for symptoms such as chest pain and dyspnea, as well as new pulmonary rales. Daily electrocardiograms (ECGs) were performed for the first 3 days, and troponin levels were measured on both the first and third post-transplantation days. Following discharge, patients were contacted for a 1-month follow-up to assess new symptoms, rehospitalization, or death.

Cardiovascular endpoints or events included any troponin rise, new ECG changes (ST-T changes, arrhythmias, or conduction abnormalities), acute heart failure (new or worsening), and mortality due to cardiovascular causes. Non-cardiovascular mortality was also documented.

The NT-proBNP kits remained consistent throughout the study (IMMULITE 2000®SIEMENS). However, because different troponin measurement kits were used, troponin levels on days 1 and 3 post-transplant were compared against the normal upper limits of their respective kits, and the deviation was used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 25 software. A significance level of 0.05 was established. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Quantitative variables were described using the mean and standard deviation (SD), while qualitative variables were presented as percentages. Due to the non-normal distribution of NT-proBNP, the median and interquartile range (IQR) were used to represent it.

Quantitative variables were compared using a t-test, while qualitative variables were analyzed using the chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests. Additionally, comparisons were made between groups with and without at least one endpoint, as well as between groups with NT-proBNP levels above and below the specified cutoff. The Mann–Whitney U test was also employed in conjunction with the t-test to compare groups with and without at least one endpoint. For secondary analysis, logistic regression was used to assess the association between the RCRI and the occurrence of cardiovascular events (endpoints).

Results

Baseline clinical data

The study included 122 patients with an average age of 47.7 ± 13.5 years. Patients with at least one endpoint had a significantly higher average age (53.5 ± 10.8 years) compared to those without any endpoints (46.2 ± 13.8 years, P < 0.05). Pre-existing hypertension was present in 98 patients (80.3%), diabetes in 32 patients (26.2%), and smoking history in 11 patients (9.0%). Cardiovascular disease (CVD) before renal transplantation was reported in 28 patients (23%). Specific histories included coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in 12 patients, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in 11, previous valve surgery in 3, and chronic stable angina in 2. One patient with a CABG history also received cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT). A familial history of CVD was noted in 5 patients (4.1%). No significant differences in outcomes were observed regarding the presence of cardiovascular risk factors (see Tables 1 and 2).

NT-proBNP and estimated risk of operation

The median NT-proBNP level was 1780 pg/mL [IQR1-IQR3: 587, 7179]. Sixty-seven patients (55%) had NT-proBNP levels above 1200 pg/mL. Among the 122 patients, 79 (64.8%) had a revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) score of 1, 37 (30.3%) had an RCRI score of 2, and 6 (4.9%) had an RCRI score of 3.

Comparing groups with NT-proBNP levels above versus below 1200 pg/mL showed no significant difference in RCRI, but LVEF and PH differed significantly (P value = 0.003 and 0.019, respectively). Regarding cardiovascular events, NT-proBNP levels showed no significant differences among patients who experienced at least one CV event compared to those who had not (albeit a trend toward difference was detected with P value = 0.058). The RCRI differed significantly in those who experienced at least one CV event compared to those with none.

For secondary analysis, patients with RCRI = 1 were combined with those with RCRI = 2 to form a lower-risk group compared to those with RCRI = 3. In the multivariable logistic regression model, patients with RCRI = 3 had an odds ratio of 9.6 (1.6–57.4) for the occurrence of MACE.

To evaluate whether the prognostic value of NT-proBNP differed by clinical risk, we tested the interaction between NT-proBNP levels and RCRI categories (low-risk: RCRI 1–2; high-risk: RCRI ≥ 3). The interaction was not statistically significant (P = 0.383), suggesting that the association between NT-proBNP and outcomes did not vary significantly across RCRI risk groups.

Echocardiographic data

Systolic pulmonary artery pressure (sPAP) classifications were considered as follows: sPAP < 35 mmHg was normal; sPAP 35–44 mmHg indicated mild pulmonary hypertension (PH); sPAP 45–60 mmHg indicated moderate PH; and sPAP > 60 mmHg was considered as severe PH. In this study, 93 patients (76.2%) had normal sPAP, 19 patients (15.6%) had mild pulmonary hypertension (PH), and 10 patients (8.2%) had moderate PH. None of the patients exhibited severe PH. Patients with NT-proBNP levels above 1200 pg/mL had significantly higher sPAP (P < 0.05). The mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 50.7% ± 5.7, with a notably lower LVEF in patients with NT-proBNP levels above the threshold (49.3 ± 6.1%) compared to those below (52.3 ± 4.6%, P < 0.05).

Cardiovascular events (endpoints)

Twenty-five patients (16.3%) experienced at least one cardiovascular endpoint: troponin rise in 16 patients (13.1%), atrial fibrillation (AF) in 2 patients (1.6%), new left bundle branch block (LBBB) in 1 patient (0.8%), and heart failure (HF) in 1 patient (0.8%). Non-cardiovascular causes led to the death of 5 patients (4.1%). Three patients had two endpoints simultaneously: troponin rise and atrial fibrillation (AF), troponin rise and heart failure (HF), and troponin rise and death. For asymptomatic patients with troponin rise, only close surveillance was performed. These outcomes were assessed during the intrahospital period and at a 1-month follow-up after discharge.

Discussion

A comprehensive cardiovascular assessment is imperative in the context of kidney transplantation due to this population’s heightened susceptibility to heart-related complications (Thompson et al. 2024). A thorough evaluation employs diagnostic modalities such as echocardiograms, stress tests, and coronary angiography to assess cardiovascular health and identify potential complications, as outlined in the guidelines (Stevens et al. 2024; Thompson et al. 2024).

Although NT-proBNP has yet to be fully integrated into standard preoperative protocols, its prognostic significance in assessing cardiovascular risk is increasingly acknowledged. Major guidelines have partly integrated NT-proBNP measurement into standard preoperative evaluation, which highlights its predictive potential. The 2022 ESC guideline on CV assessment before non-cardiac surgery recommends NT-proBNP measurement in the following situations: class I in suspected or known HF patients scheduled for high-risk non-cardiac surgery and class IIa in patients with known CV disease, CV risk factors, or symptoms suggestive of CV disease before intermediate and high-risk non-cardiac surgeries. The abnormal NT-proBNP level was considered to be ≥125 pg/mL. The 2022 ESC guideline did not provide specific recommendations for patients before KT. The reason stems from the elevated level of NT-proBNP in ESRD patients—attributed mainly to fluid overload and impaired clearance—that precludes reliance on the routine NT-proBNP for all kidney transplant candidates to estimate the risk of the surgery (Halvorsen et al. 2022).

Similarly, the 2024 ACC/AHA guideline acknowledges NT-proBNP’s utility as a biomarker for CV risk stratification with class IIa in patients with known CV diseases, age ≥ 65 years, or those with age ≥ 45 years with CV disease symptoms before elevated risk non-cardiac surgery, with no focus or specific recommendation before KT (Thompson et al. 2024). Abnormal NT-proBNP threshold is defined as ≥300 ng/L. The cost of the NT-proBNP, along with the lack of robust evidence for the added value of the routine NT-proBNP measurement to the patient’s management before non-cardiac surgery, is the limiting factor for the widespread recommendation.

The pathophysiological effects of ESRD, characterized by fluid overload and hemodynamic instability, frequently result in elevated NT-proBNP levels independent of heart failure, complicating its interpretation in transplant evaluations (Stevens et al. 2024). Normal NT-proBNP levels exhibit considerable variability with age and are typically heightened in ESRD patients due to diminished renal clearance and increased cardiac wall stress (Stevens et al. 2024; Thompson et al. 2024). In the general population, the normative ranges are below 450 pg/mL for individuals under 50 years, below 900 pg/mL for those aged 50–75, and up to 1800 pg/mL for patients over 75 years (Stevens et al. 2024).

Our study adopts a 1200 pg/mL threshold for NT-proBNP through ROC curve analysis, as established by Anwaruddin et al. (2006). This threshold effectively differentiates between cardiac and non-cardiac causes of dyspnea in emergency settings, striking a balance between sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing acute heart failure (Anwaruddin et al. 2006). However, end-stage renal disease will complicate this reliable cutoff for identifying at-risk patients.

In this prospective cohort study, we assessed 122 patients to evaluate the role of NT-proBNP levels and RCRI in predicting cardiovascular events post-kidney transplantation. The study population had an average age of 47.7 ± 13.5 years. Older patients were at an elevated risk of cardiovascular complications following kidney transplantation. Cardiovascular risk factors were prevalent in our cohort: 80.3% had hypertension, 26.2% had diabetes, and 9.0% were smokers. Intriguingly, 77.0% of patients reported no history of cardiovascular disease despite these risk factors. Concerning the RCRI, most of our patients (64.8%) were classified as low risk with an RCRI score of 1. This was followed by 30.3% of patients with an RCRI of 2 and a small fraction (4.9%) with an RCRI of 3. The cardiovascular endpoints (CVEs) were not associated with NT-proBNP levels above the defined threshold (1200 pg/mL), and only a trend for significance was detected. On the contrary, CVEs were significantly associated with RCRI. Patients with RCRI of 3 had an odds ratio (OR) of about 9.6 (1.6–57.4) for CVE compared to RCRI of 1 and 2. The presented OR range was wide due to the small number of patients with RCRI = 3 in our study. The mean NT-proBNP level in this cohort was markedly high, with a median level of 1780 pg/mL, and over half of the patients (55%) exhibited NT-proBNP levels exceeding the 1200 pg/mL threshold. LVEF and sPAP differed significantly in those with NT-proBNP levels above 1200 pg/mL compared to below the threshold, not RCRI. Thirteen patients (10.6%) had an asymptomatic troponin rise with no significant ECG changes or hemodynamic instability. Regarding the recent KT, they had only undergone close surveillance during in-hospital admission and a 1-month follow-up course. Arroyo et al. studied 177 stable asymptomatic patients at least 1 month after KT. The prevalence of increased high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T was 42.4% which showed age, male sex, CKMB level, and lower eGFR were independent predictors of elevated troponin level after KT (Arroyo et al. 2012).

It is crucial to address the classification of kidney transplantation (KT) in terms of surgical risk. According to the 2024 AHA/ACC guideline, kidney transplantation is categorized as an intermediate-risk procedure, not a high-risk vascular surgery (Thompson et al. 2024). This classification is based on an estimated 30-day cardiac event risk of 1% to 5%, which is crucial for interpreting risk prediction tools, such as the RCRI, in this patient population (Thompson et al. 2024). Therefore, while kidney transplantation involves significant considerations for cardiovascular risk, it is not classified as a high-risk vascular procedure. This distinction is essential for the correct application of the RCRI in assessing perioperative cardiovascular risk in this cohort.

Some studies cast doubt on the utility of the revised cardiac risk index in chronic kidney disease. A retrospective survey by Palamuthusingam et al. assessed the efficacy of the RCRI in predicting cardiovascular events (CVEs) after gastrointestinal surgeries among patients with chronic kidney replacement therapies (KRT), including dialysis and kidney transplant recipients, from 2000 to 2015. RCRI had poor discrimination in patients on chronic KRT undergoing elective gastrointestinal (GI) surgery (AUC = 0.67), particularly in patients aged older than 65 years (AUC = 0.591). Discrimination was moderate in patients under 65 years and kidney transplant recipients, with AUCs of 0.740 and 0.718, respectively (Palamuthusingam et al. 2024). Despite the precious findings, this study has some limitations. First, it excludes surgically turned down patients, and second, coding bias was inevitable. The study above focused on GI surgeries, not kidney transplantation, so comparing these two types of surgery is unreasonable.

Another study by Olsen assessed the RCRI in 325 patients undergoing kidney transplants from deceased donor grafts and found no net benefit from utilizing the RCRI (Rocha Olsen et al. 2021). RCRI could not predict a 30-day major adverse cardiovascular event (MACE) in this cohort. More than 75% of the study population had an RCRI score of 2. The reported MACE was 5.8%. There is a statement in this paper implying that all KT candidates automatically score 2 of RCRI due to creatinine levels of more than 2 mg/dL and the high-risk nature of the surgery (kidney transplantation). KT is not considered a high-risk vascular surgery. In this way, their study population with RCRI of 2 resembles our participants with RCRI of 1, highlighting that both studies mainly consisted of low-risk patients. MACE’s definition differed in these two studies. We included any rise in troponin as CVE, even without symptoms. Moreover, they did not have a uniform and precise protocol for detecting MACE after KT and cardiac consult had been performed upon suspicion of the charge physician. In contrast, uniform and dedicated post-operative cardiac surveillance was used in our research. The mentioned differences resulted in the observed CVE of 16.3% in our study compared to 5.8% MACE in Olsen’s.

The study by Schwab et al. assessed the value of NT-proBNP in kidney transplant recipients to predict 1- and 5-year survival rates (Schwab et al. 2023). This retrospective study involved 176 patients and evaluated how pre-transplant NT-proBNP levels could predict MACE following kidney transplantation (Schwab et al. 2023). Schwab and colleagues measured NT-proBNP levels immediately before transplant surgery and tracked these patients for 38 months to identify post-transplant cardiovascular events (Schwab et al. 2023). Utilizing statistical methodologies such as Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox regression, the study determined that patients with NT-proBNP levels exceeding 4350 pg/mL (established as a threshold during the survey) faced a significantly elevated risk of MACE (Jia et al. 2023). Despite its noteworthy findings, the study has some limitations, including the retrospective nature of data collection (between 2000 and 2015) and probably the absence of a uniform kit for NT-proBNP measurement. More importantly, the time from NT-proBNP measurements relative to the last hemodialysis session was unavailable in this retrospective study. This time frame has a significant impact on the NT-proBNP level in ESRD patients. Finally, including a specific patient group with preserved ejection fraction may restrict the generalizability of the results to the broader population of kidney transplant recipients (Schwab et al. 2023).

It is imperative to acknowledge the limitations of NT-proBNP as a standalone marker. While NT-proBNP is instrumental in risk stratification, evidence suggests its diagnostic efficacy is markedly enhanced when combined with other biomarkers, particularly in patients with chronic conditions that influence biomarker levels. For instance, the study by Wang et al. demonstrated that the predictive accuracy of NT-proBNP for heart failure significantly improves when used in conjunction with cystatin C (Wang et al. 2021). This finding underscores the necessity of not relying solely on NT-proBNP in clinical settings characterized by diverse patient complexities.

Furthermore, comprehensive research by Duceppe et al. explored how integrating preoperative NT-proBNP with the RCRI could refine the prediction of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in non-cardiac surgery patients (Duceppe et al. 2020). Their findings emphasized that while NT-proBNP enhances risk assessment when added to the RCRI, its effectiveness remains contingent on the specific clinical context, necessitating the adjustment of risk stratification thresholds based on individual patient and procedural characteristics (Stevens et al. 2024). ESRD patients were not included in Duceppe’s study.

Our study showed no role of NT-proBNP in predicting CVE after KT in a relatively low-risk ESRD population. On the other hand, the RCRI had utility in the pre-kidney transplant assessment, particularly in high-risk candidates with RCRI = 3. The observed trend toward a higher NT-proBNP level in patients with at least one CVE necessitates conducting a larger study, with a higher number of high-risk ESRD patients, to understand whether incorporating NT-proBNP into the presenting practice guideline may result in more accurate and comprehensive cardiovascular assessments and effective tailored management strategies.

This study encountered some limitations that influenced the interpretation of its findings. First, the COVID-19 pandemic reduced the number of kidney transplants performed, which may affect the generalizability of our findings to periods of standard healthcare operations. Second, the single-center nature of this study may limit the external validity of our findings, as patient characteristics and clinical practices may vary across institutions. Third, a change in the troponin assay kits during the study altered our baseline cardiac enzyme assessment methods. To overcome this limitation, troponin levels on days 1 and 3 post-transplant were compared against the normal upper limits of their respective kits, and the deviation was used for analysis. Fourth, although consecutive sampling was performed for patient allocation, most of the study population had no prior history of cardiovascular disease, and patients with severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction or severe pulmonary hypertension were not scheduled for kidney transplantation in our center. Fifth, the relatively small sample size and limited number of events may have constrained the robustness of multivariable analyses, which should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Finally, no patients with an RCRI score of more than 3 were included in our study, and most of the study population had an RCRI score of 1, corresponding to relatively lower-risk candidates. Future studies employing multicenter designs with larger sample sizes and including high-risk patients with an RCRI above 3 are warranted to validate and extend our findings, particularly in populations at greater cardiovascular risk.
