Plastic Diets Drive Microbiome and Metabolic Reprogramming in Wax Moth Larvae (Achroia grisella)
Authors: Rohan Shah, Anna Marcora, Angela Ruffell, Georgia M Sinclair, Inka Vanwonterghem, Andrew Bissett, Andrew Hulthen, Gene Wijffels, Cate Paull, David J Beale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe burgeoning global plastic crisis necessitates transformative solutions beyond current recycling and disposal methods. This study investigates the ability of wax moth larvae (Achroia grisella) to biodegrade low‐density polyethylene (LDPE) and polylactic acid (PLA), emphasizing the complex interactions between the physiology of larvae, their gut microbiome, and the plastic degradation process. Using 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing, Seahorse bioassays, and advanced metabolomic and lipidomic profiling, we demonstrate that plastic consumption is associated with microbial and metabolic restructuring in larvae. LDPE‐fed larvae displayed elevated microbial diversity, dominated by Bacillus spp., which correlated with shifts in carbohydrate metabolism and amino acid biosynthesis pathways critical for energy production and detoxification. Conversely, PLA‐fed larvae were enriched with Enterococcus spp., linked to oxidative stress mitigation and nucleotide turnover. These diet‐induced adaptations, such as the proliferation of Bacillus spp. in LDPE‑fed larvae, known to express alkane‑hydroxylase enzymes that initiate polyethylene depolymerization, and enrichment of Enterococcus spp. in PLA‑fed larvae, linked to ester bond hydrolysis, underscore a symbiotic co‐metabolism that may play a contributory role in plastic processing, albeit at the cost of reduced larval growth and suppressed mitochondrial function. By unraveling these complex biological interactions, this study establishes a foundation for harnessing insect‐microbiome ecosystems to develop scalable and eco‐friendly strategies for plastic waste management. Future research should explore the genetic and enzymatic mechanisms underpinning plastic metabolism in insect‐microbiome ecosystems.Keywords: gut microbiome, Lesser wax moth larvae (Achroia grisella), low‐density polyethylene (LDPE), metabolomics and lipidomics, plastic biodegradation, polylactic acid (PLA)

Introduction

The escalating accumulation of plastics has emerged as a critical environmental concern. Plastics, particularly ubiquitous forms such as polylactic acid (PLA) and low‐density polyethylene (LDPE), have become pervasive pollutants with profound ecological implications. These materials, widely used in packaging, manufacturing, and various industries, contribute significantly to the growing issue of plastic pollution. Continuous plastic production has led to substantial global plastic waste, with three primary disposal routes: recycling, thermal destruction (pyrolysis or incineration), or landfill (Geyer et al. 2017). Of the 8.3 billion metric tons produced between 1950 and 2015, 30% is still in use, 6.5% recycled, 8% thermally destroyed, and 55.5% discarded (Geyer et al. 2017). Recycling, while preferable, only delays final disposal, as most plastics can be recycled once or twice, ultimately ending up in landfill or incineration (Geyer et al. 2017). Recent data reveal a continued increase in global plastic production, surpassing 400 million metric tons by 2022 (Jaganmohan 2024), with less than 10% being recycled effectively (Alves 2024). This surge underscores an escalating environmental dilemma and has prompted stricter waste‐management and recycling policies globally. However, policy and recycling improvements alone are unlikely to resolve legacy and dispersed plastic pollution, reinforcing the need for complementary biological mitigation strategies. Recent multi‐omics studies demonstrate that polymer exposure can drive pronounced microbiome restructuring while simultaneously imposing physiological and energetic constraints on the host, highlighting that microbial community shifts do not necessarily equate to net energetic or fitness gains (Sanluis‐Verdes et al. 2022). Here, we examine an insect–microbiome system that interacts with two widely used polymers (LDPE and PLA) to link diet‐driven microbiome restructuring with host metabolic consequences.

The increased production and improper disposal of plastics have led to their presence across terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems, where plastics and microplastic fragments can interact with biological systems (Allouzi et al. 2021; Kumar et al. 2021; Sridharan et al. 2021; Kumar et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023; Ullah et al. 2023). While many studies have focused on environmental distribution and ecological risk, fewer studies have examined how consumer‐grade plastics interface with biological degraders at the organismal and microbiome level (Sanchez‐Hernandez 2021). Insect–microbiome systems offer a tractable biological model to investigate these interactions, linking polymer exposure to physiological and microbial responses that may inform sustainable biodegradation strategies.

Limited biological solutions for plastic degradation further compound the challenge. While PLA and LDPE are prominent pollutants, the similar challenges extend to a wider range of plastics such as polyethene terephthalate (PET) and polyvinyl chloride (PVC), each presenting distinct degradation barriers due to their chemical and physical properties. While nature is highly efficient at recycling organic matter, the chemical recalcitrance of many synthetic polymers limits the effectiveness of microbial communities and enzymatic processes responsible for plastic degradation, particularly for high‐molecular‐weight and chemically inert plastics such as polyethylene (Jaiswal et al. 2020; Boschi et al. 2024). Advances in biotechnology, including genetic engineering and synthetic biology techniques are being explored to augment the natural capabilities of microorganisms, these methods offer promising strategies to enhance microbial plastic degradation (Jaiswal et al. 2020). Additionally, the development of bio‐based plastics, designed for easier degradation, presents an innovative approach to reducing future pollution levels.

The escalating plastic waste crisis and the limited effectiveness of current disposal and degradation methods underscore the critical need for innovative and sustainable solutions. Biological plastic degradation by insects represents a promising frontier, complementing research efforts involving bacteria, and fungi, that can break down plastic polymers. Understanding the mechanisms by which these larvae interact with and degrade plastics lies at the intersection of environmental sustainability and the need to address the global crises, offering a promising avenue to develop innovative, nature‐inspired solutions. LDPE, with its hydrophobic properties and high molecular weight, presents unique challenges for enzymatic breakdown, whereas PLA, despite its biodegradability, often requires specific environmental conditions to degrade effectively. This study focuses on these two polymers to address critical gaps in understanding their biodegradability in biological systems.

Wax moth larvae (Achroia grisella and G. mellonella) have garnered attention for their fascinating biology and adaptive traits (Wojda et al. 2020). Wax moth has historically been considered a pest in beehives (Wojda et al. 2020), but recent research has demonstrated an intriguing capacity to metabolize plastic materials, offering a unique biological approach to addressing plastic pollution (LeMoine et al. 2020). These larvae possess specialized enzymes and microbial symbionts in their digestive system, allowing them to break down complex plastic polymers (LeMoine et al. 2020). However, exposure to plastic diets has also been shown to elicit substantial metabolic and lipidomic perturbations, including altered energy metabolism, oxidative stress responses, and membrane lipid remodeling, indicating significant physiological cost alongside any biodegradative capacity (Venegas et al. 2024). Recent studies have demonstrated the ability of wax moth larvae to biodegrade various types of plastic, including polyethylene, polystyrene, and plastics from waste electrical and electronic equipment (Lou et al. 2020; Sanchez‐Hernandez 2021; Zhu et al. 2021; Beale et al. 2022). The process involves the consumption of plastic, leading to mass loss and the formation of biodegraded intermediates (Beale et al. 2022). However, the extent and rate of biodegradation are influenced by the physicochemical structure of the plastic and the presence of additives (Sanchez‐Hernandez 2021). Recent ecotoxicological studies further demonstrate that even when biodegradation occurs, plastic exposure can impair growth, metabolism, and physiological performance in insect models, underscoring the importance of evaluating host fitness costs alongside biodegradative outcomes (Kehkashan et al. 2025). The larval gut microbiome plays a crucial role in this process, with changes observed in response to different plastic diets (Cassone et al. 2020; Lou et al. 2020; Ruiz Barrionuevo et al. 2022). Further research is needed to understand the toxicological implications of plastic biodegradation by wax moth larvae and to explore the potential for a multi‐kingdom synergy in the process. Although fungi can contribute to polymer degradation, the present study focuses on bacterial gut‐community restructuring (16S rRNA profiling) and its association with host metabolic responses, providing an initial, bacteria‐centered view of diet‐driven shifts in A. grisella.

The ability of wax moth larvae to interact with, consume and potentially degrade plastics opens new avenues for sustainable waste management practices. By linking polymer‐specific feeding conditions to gut‐community restructuring and host metabolic constraints, this study provides biological context relevant to designing and evaluating polymer–biota interactions (e.g., assessing whether a material coincides with adaptive microbial shifts vs. host energetic stress). The biological processes at play within the digestive system of the larvae may hold the key to unlocking sustainable approaches for mitigating plastic pollution on a global scale.

While plastic degradation by wax moth larvae has been previously demonstrated (Beale et al. 2022), key knowledge gaps remain regarding how diet‐driven microbiome restructuring interfaces with host metabolic physiology. Here, we integrate gut microbiome profiling with mitochondrial bioenergetics, metabolomics, and lipidomics to resolve how polymer‐specific microbial adaptations align with host metabolic constraints. This systems‐level perspective extends prior work by linking microbial composition, metabolic pathway engagement, and host energetic trade‐offs under plastic diets.

Materials and Methods

Model Insects

The Lesser wax moth (Achroia grisella) colony was established from larvae which had infested beehives at two different sites in Brisbane (Australia). Larvae were collected from the two sites and taken to the CSIRO Insect Laboratory (Ecosciences Precinct, Dutton Park, Brisbane, Australia) where they were placed in 1 L jars with a mesh lid and fed on control diet, that is, a mix of commercially sourced yeast, honey, glycerol, and Farex baby cereal. The control colony for these experiments had been reared for six generations in 1 L glass jars and fed control diet.

The two experimental insect colonies were maintained on plastic substrates (LDPE or PLA) for five consecutive generations. During each generation, larvae that survived and completed development under plastic feeding conditions were allowed to progress to pupation and reproduction, forming the basis of the subsequent generation. This approach resulted in colonies enriched for individuals capable of persisting on polymer‐based diets, although no formal quantitative selection criteria were imposed. Control and experimental insects were reared at the CSIRO Insect Laboratory in a constant environment room (CER) set at a 14:10 h Light‐Dark (LD) cycle, with a 50% relative humidity (RH) at 26°C.

Artificial Feeding of Plastics

A total of 150 4‐week‐old control larvae were set up in two small glass jars containing 35 g of control diet (75 larvae per jar) and allowed to feed on their diet for 7 days before processing for analysis. Starved controls were included with the PLA experiments. Sixty four‐week‐old control larvae were placed into six small jars without diet (10 larvae per jar) and starved for 3 days, the jars were sprayed with (reverse osmosis) RO water each day prior to processing for analysis.

Experimental treatments were set up in six small jars containing either pre‐weighed pieces of LDPE or PLA with 25 4‐week‐old, plastic selected larvae per jar (LDPE selected larvae for the LDPE treatment and PLA selected larvae for the PLA treatment). Experimental insects were sprayed with RO water every second day and were exposed to plastic for 7 days prior to processing as samples for analysis.

The jars used for the experiments were secured with a metal screw top lid which had a 40 mm diameter hole was cut from the center of each lid and aluminum insect mesh was placed inside the lid to cover the hole. Fine nylon (1 mm2) gauze was placed between the metal lid and metal mesh to stop larvae from escaping. Experiments were conducted in a CER set at a 14:10 h LD cycle, with a 50% RH at 26°C. LDPE and PLA materials were sourced from consumer packaging materials representative of commonly encountered environmental plastics. Polymer identity was analytically verified using Mettler Toledo TGA2 instrument (Mettler‐Toledo Ltd., Port Melbourne, VIC, Australia) prior to use (Beale et al. 2022), confirming classification as LDPE and PLA. Materials were used as obtained, without artificial weathering or additive removal. Consequently, these substrates represent environmentally relevant, heterogeneous plastics, and detailed characterization of additive composition was not undertaken. Consequently, observed biological responses reflect realistic consumer‐grade materials. No cannibalistic behavior was observed during the experimental period. Feeding on plastic substrates was confirmed by direct visual observation of larval interaction with plastic materials and by the presence of frass during the exposure period.

Larval Gut Dissections

On day 8 of the experiment, 10 experimental (LDPE/PLA) and 10 control larvae were dissected in insect media (Schneider's Drosophila Medium, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Australia) at room temperature. Each larva was triple rinsed in RO water prior to dissection and dissected individually under magnification using a dissecting microscope. Dissections were performed in a small glass petri dish containing 1 mL of insect media, which was replaced between samples to minimize cross‐contamination.

The larval cuticle was carefully opened using a sterile scalpel, and the digestive tract was gently removed to isolate the gut. Fat body tissue, visible as a diffuse, creamy‐white layer lining the internal cavity, was identified and separated from surrounding tissues using fine forceps. Dissected gut tissues were collected separately into cryotubes containing 500 µL of insect media at room temperature.

Following dissection, samples were equilibrated under controlled conditions prior to placement into individual wells of a 24‐well Seahorse plate for downstream bioenergetic analysis. Dissected carcasses remaining after tissue isolation were discarded.

Whole Larvae

On day 8 of experiment, 10 experimental (LDPE/PLA),10 control larvae and 10 starved control larvae were triple rinsed in RO water, dried on paper towel, placed into pre‐weighed cryotubes and weighed. Larvae in cryotubes were placed in a constant environment room with the same previously mentioned conditions to equilibrate before they were placed into separate wells of a 24 well seahorse plate for analysis.

Larval Gut Dissections for Metabolomics and Lipidomics Analyzes

On day 8 of experiment, 5 experimental (LDPE/PLA), 5 control and 5 starved control larvae were dissected for metabolomics and lipidomics analyzes. Larvae were triple rinsed in room temperature RO water prior to dissecting in small glass petri dish sitting on ice containing 10 mL cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 (Medicago AB, Sweden). Buffer was changed after each dissection. Larvae collected into pre‐weighed cryotubes sitting on dry ice, to freeze the guts on collection. Once dissections were completed, tubes were weighed and then stored at −80°C. Insect bodies were discarded.

16s RNA Sequencing

For each diet, gut contents from five individual larvae were dissected and pooled prior to DNA extraction and sequencing, generating one composite microbiome sample per treatment. DNA extractions were performed using the Qiagen DNeasy Bio PowerSoil Pro Kit, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Pooling was used to obtain a representative treatment‐level community profile; however, this approach does not permit estimation of within‐treatment variability or support statistical inference, and results are therefore interpreted as descriptive of major compositional differences among diets.

Bacterial diversity was investigated via amplicon sequencing targeting the V1–V3 region of the 16S rRNA gene with the bacterial forward 27 F and reverse 519 R primer set (Lane et al. 1985; Lane 1991). Sequencing was performed at the Ramaciotti Center for Genomics (UNSW, Sydney, Australia). Negative control extractions were included following the same procedure of samples. Library preparation and sequencing with 300‐bp paired end reads, were performed according to the manufacturer's directions using an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Amplification comprised 35 cycles (98°C denaturation [20 s], 55°C annealing [10 s], 72°C extension [45 s]) followed by a final 72°C extension cycle (300 s). Data was provided in fastq format.

Zero radius OTUs (zOTU) tables were prepared from fastq files (Bissett et al. 2016). Briefly, paired reads were merged using FLASH2 (‐‐min‐overlap=20 ‐‐max‐overlap=150) (Magoč and Salzberg 2011). The UNOISE3 algorithm was used to define zOTUs and remove chimeric sequences with USEARCH v11.0.667 (–min size 4) (Edgar 2010), after which a read abundance table (usearch‐out_tab) was constructed by mapping paired reads to defined zOTUs. All processes used default arguments except where specified. Taxonomy was assigned to the SILVA database (ver. 138) (Quast et al. 2012; Yilmaz et al. 2013) using two‐step process. First, sequences were aligned to SILV using the usearch_global aligner (–query_cov 1.0 ‐id 1.0 ‐max hits 10), and exact matches to fully covered targets (up to 10) returned. If all exact matches shared the same taxonomy, this was assigned to the sequence. For sequences without 100% taxonomy consensus to exact matches or with no exact matches, then the QIIME2 (Bokulich et al. 2018; Bolyen et al. 2019) sklearn Bayesian classifier was employed (‐‐p‐confidence 0.8). Putative contaminants were removed from the abundance table using the Decontam R package (Davis et al. 2018) with the “prevalence” method and threshold = 0.5, as suggested by the documentation to “identify as contaminants all sequences that are more prevalent in negative controls than in positive samples.” Finally, zOTUs classified as: Domains: “unknown,” “Archaea” and “Eukaryota”; or Phylum: “Bacteria_unclassified”; or Family: “Mitochondria”; or Order: “Chloroplast” were removed prior to further analyzes.

Metabolomics and Lipidomics

The targeted analysis of central carbon metabolism (CCM) metabolites was conducted using an Agilent Infinity Flex II UHPLC system coupled to an Agilent 6470 Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (LC‐QqQ‐MS, Agilent Technologies, USA), following previously established methods (Beale et al. 2022). In parallel, untargeted lipidomics data were generated using an Agilent Infinity Flex II UHPLC system coupled to an Agilent 6546 Quadrupole Time‐of‐Flight Mass Spectrometer (LC‐QToF‐MS, Agilent Technologies, USA). In brief, freeze‐dried biomass of insect larvae gut tissue or digestive tissue (20 mg) was extracted with a mixture of 100 μL MilliQ water and 450 μL ice‐cold methanol: ethanol (1:1, vol/vol; LiChrosolv, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), spiked with 1 ppm Succinic Acid 13C2 (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories Inc., Tewksbury, MA, USA). The mixtures were homogenized using a Precellys Evolution homogenizer (Bertin Instruments, Montigny‐le‐Bretonneux, France) at 5800 rpm (2 × 15 s cycles, with a 30 s pause), followed by centrifugation (Centrifuge 5430 R, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 14,000 g at 4°C for 5 min. The supernatant was collected and filtered using Captiva EMR cartridges (40 mg, 1 mL; Agilent Technologies, Australia) to remove the lipid fraction via a positive pressure manifold (Agilent PPM48 Processor, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Cartridge washing was performed with two 200 μL aliquots of MilliQ water: methanol: ethanol (2:1:1, vol/vol/vol). The filtered supernatant and cartridge washes were collected into 1.5 mL high recovery vials (30 μL reservoir, silanised glass vials, Agilent Technologies, Australia) and subsequently dried using a SpeedVAC (10 mBar). The metabolite fraction was reconstituted with 100 μL MilliQ water: methanol (4:1, vol/vol), spiked with 100 ppm of l‐Phenylalanine (1‐13C), and analyzed on the LC‐QQQ‐MS platform. The lipid fraction was reconstituted with 100 μL butanol: methanol (1:1, vol/vol) and analyzed on the LC‐QToF‐MS platform. Procedural blanks (n = 3) and pooled biological samples (n = 3) were randomly interspersed throughout the analysis sequence for quality control.

Seahorse Analysis

Primary insect cells were isolated from insect larvae and prepared for extracellular flux analysis using insect cell culture medium (Schneider's Drosophila Medium, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Australia). Larval tissues were gently dissociated in 2 mL Eppendorf tubes, and isolated cells were washed and resuspended in insect medium. Cells were seeded into Seahorse XF24 microplates at a density corresponding to 20 µg total cellular protein per well (measured using a DeNovix DS‐11 FX+ Spectrometer/Fluorometer, DeNovix Inc., USA). Plates were pre‐coated with Cell‐Tak to promote adhesion and gently centrifuged to facilitate cell attachment. Cells were incubated at 25°C in a non‐CO2 environment for 60 min to allow equilibration prior to assay.

Prior to measurement, culture medium was replaced with Seahorse XF assay medium (buffered, serum‐free medium) appropriate for insect cells. Basal mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic activity were measured using an Agilent Seahorse XFe24 Bioanalyzer over a ~36 min assay period comprising four repeated mix–wait–measure cycles.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were recorded using Seahorse Wave software (v2.6.1.56). Each replicate (n = 4) represents an independent biological sample derived from separate larvae, with one well per replicate. OCR and ECAR values were normalized to total cellular protein per well (20 µg) to account for variation in cell number and tissue biomass across treatments, and values represent mean basal rates across measurement cycles. Measurements reflect basal mitochondrial respiration and glycolytic activity only, as no pharmacological mitochondrial stress test (e.g., oligomycin, FCCP, rotenone/antimycin A) was performed. Consequently, these data describe basal energetic state and cannot resolve maximal respiratory capacity, coupling efficiency, or spare respiratory capacity.

Protein‐based normalization was used to account for differences in tissue size, cellular biomass, and potential diet‐induced variation in larval condition (e.g., reduced growth under plastic or starvation diets), thereby enabling meaningful comparison of metabolic rates across treatments.

Statistical Analysis

The omics data underwent several preprocessing steps before analysis. First, a log10 transformation was applied to the data. Subsequently, each variable was scaled using a mean‐centered value and divided by its standard deviation. These steps ensured that the data were appropriately standardized for subsequent analysis. Normalized Seahorse OCR and ECAR data were processed and curated in Microsoft Excel prior to downstream statistical analysis. Both univariate and multivariate statistical analyzes were performed on the pre‐processed data. This analysis was conducted using two software platforms: SIMCA (v18, Sartorius AG, Göttingen, Germany) and MetaboAnalyst (v6.0) (Pang et al. 2021). Significant metabolites were identified based on a p‐value of ≤ 0.01. To assess the biological relevance and significance of these metabolites, pathway enrichment and pathway impact analyzes were performed using MBROLE3 (Lopez‐Ibañez et al. 2023). MBROLE3 facilitated a functional interpretation of metabolomic data, shedding light on diverse feeding regime‐induced metabolic pathways. Host–microbiome analysis was performed using MetOrigin, an online tool for the integrative analysis of the gut microbiome and discriminating between host (wax moth larvae), microbiome, and co‐metabolism activities associated with microbial metabolites (Yu et al. 2022). MetOrigin identified bacterial contributions to metabolic reactions, enhancing the understanding of plastic degradation by insects. Statistical methods and Sankey networks highlighted key metabolites associated with microbial species. The KEGG database was used for MetOrigin analysis to investigate the metabolic responses in Achroia grisella (wax moth) larvae under various feeding conditions. For MBROLE3 analysis, however, Drosophila melanogaster (fruit fly) was used, as wax moth‐specific data was unavailable. Drosophila is a well‐established model organism with a comprehensive metabolic pathway map, making it an appropriate choice for this analysis. Microbial diversity analysis. Alpha diversity was calculated using Shannon's diversity index from the zOTU abundance matrix. Overall pooled diversity was calculated by applying Shannon's index to the combined abundance table across all samples. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray‐Curtis dissimilarity on abundance profiles, where values range from 0 (identical communities) to 1 (maximally dissimilar communities). Because each treatment was represented by a pooled gut sample, microbiome results were interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than inferential.

Results and Discussions

Impact of Plastic Diets on Basal Respiration and Health in Wax Moth Larvae

The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) data represent basal mitochondrial respiration levels in fat bodies of larvae subjected to different diets: LDPE, PLA, and starvation (Figure 1A). All values are normalized relative to the control group (set to 1.0), so only fold‑change values for experimental treatments are shown. While the fat bodies of PLA‐fed larvae tend to show higher OCR levels compared to LDPE‐fed and starved groups, these differences are not statistically significant (p > 0.01). This suggests that the type of diet (or lack thereof) does not significantly alter metabolic rates at the basal level under the experimental conditions. However, the observed trend of higher OCR in PLA‐fed larvae is consistent with altered basal mitochondrial activity or substrate availability, rather than definitive mitochondrial dysfunction, given that these measurements reflect basal respiration only.

Seahorse energetic profiling in fat body and gut tissues of larvae under LDPE, PLA, and starvation diets. (A) OCR (oxygen consumption rate; i.e., mitochondrial stress test) examines basal respiration rates. (B) ECAR (extracellular acidification rate; i.e., glycolytic stress test) examines basal metabolic activity related to acid production (through glycolysis). Values are expressed as fold‐change relative to matched tissue controls (control fat body = 1; control gut = 1). Colors correspond to diet: LDPE , PLA , and starvation . All OCR and ECAR values were normalized to total protein content per well and represent basal measurements.

The average wet weight of larvae (n = 10) reared on plastic diets (PLA: 35.30 ± 13.13 mg, LDPE: 28.39 ± 12.03 mg) was substantially lower than control larvae (54.42 ± 10.48 mg), indicating that a plastic diet does not support normal growth. Starved larvae (16.60 ± 7.70 mg) also showed reduced weight, consistent with reduced nutrient availability and overall physiological stress rather than direct evidence of impaired mitochondrial efficiency. Reduced larval mass likely reflects nutritional limitation rather than mitochondrial efficiency per se. While suppressed basal respiration coincides with reduced growth, the present data do not allow causal linkage between body mass, tissue composition, and mitochondrial performance. Notably, the insect fat body is a metabolically dynamic tissue that responds to dietary availability and energy storage demands; therefore, diet‐induced changes in tissue composition may partially obscure bioenergetic signals despite protein‐based normalization. This highlights the limited nutritional value of plastics, with PLA providing slightly more sustenance than LDPE, likely due to its biodegradability and partial digestibility. The hydrolysable ester bonds in PLA may allow some enzymatic or microbial breakdown, whereas the high molecular weight, hydrophobicity, and chemical inertness of LDPE make it highly resistant to degradation, amplifying toxic stress and reducing energetic benefit.

The analysis of the insect gut data (Figure 1A) revealed significant differences (p‐value < 0.01) in metabolic activity among LDPE‐fed, PLA‐fed, and starved larvae, underscoring the sensitivity of the gut to dietary changes compared to the fat bodies. Significant reductions in basal OCR in the gut of LDPE‐ and PLA‐fed larvae indicate altered mitochondrial activity under plastic exposure. As measurements reflect basal respiration only, these differences are consistent with constrained substrate utilization or altered energy demand rather than resolvable mitochondrial uncoupling or dysfunction. This suppression could indicate a limited ability of larvae to derive energy from plastics, with an energetic benefit that may be minimal or even negative. The slight advantage of PLA over LDPE could be attributed to its physical characteristics, supporting minimal ATP production while avoiding complete mitochondrial collapse. Notably, the magnitude of OCR reduction was greatest when comparing control larvae to all non‐control groups, whereas differences among LDPE‐fed, PLA‐fed, and starved larvae were comparatively modest despite reaching statistical significance.

The reduced gut metabolic rate could also be a protective mechanism to conserve energy when processing indigestible or toxic substances, implying a trade‐off between maintaining basic physiological functions and the energetic costs of processing foreign materials. Additionally, the observed difference between control and starved groups suggests that food absence coincides with shifts in gut metabolism, potentially related to mitochondrial adaptation, increased reliance on stored energy, or microbial shifts (Beale et al. 2022; Shah et al. 2023).

The gut microbiome plays a crucial role in digestion and energy extraction, and changes in gut activity could impact or be influenced by microbial dynamics. Observed alterations in basal OCR in response to plastic diets may reflect indirect effects of microbial activity or host‐microbiome interactions, although inefficient ATP coupling and energetic constraint remain possible. This interplay may have broader implications on the overall health of insects and its ability to process diverse diets, including plastics. Understanding the mechanisms underlying these metabolic changes could contribute to biotechnological advances in plastic degradation.

OCR differed significantly between diets in the gut tissue (p < 0.01), whereas fat body OCR did not differ significantly (p > 0.01), highlighting the importance of localized metabolic responses to dietary inputs. Notably, the insect fat body is a highly plastic tissue that responds dynamically to dietary availability and functions as a central metabolic reserve. Consequently, diet‐induced changes in tissue composition and energy storage may partially obscure bioenergetic signals in this tissue, even when protein‐based normalization is applied. In contrast, the gut, being directly exposed to ingested substrates, may provide a more immediate and sensitive readout of diet‐specific metabolic stress.

From an ecological perspective, the observed reduction in gut OCR, together with reduced larval growth, suggests energetic constraint under plastic feeding rather than a metabolically favorable state. These findings challenge the assumption that insects function as simple bio‐remediators for plastic pollution, instead indicating potential physiological costs associated with plastic ingestion.

The ECAR data for fat bodies (Figure 1B) does not show significant differences (p‐value > 0.01) between control and any of the conditions (LDPE‐fed, PLA‐fed, and starved). This suggests that acidification‐linked metabolic activity in the fat body is relatively buffered against dietary variation. The insect fat body functions as a central metabolic reserve and is highly plastic in response to nutritional status, capable of mobilizing or storing energy depending on food availability. Consequently, this tissue may maintain core metabolic function even under conditions of nutritional stress, potentially obscuring diet‐specific metabolic signals.

In contrast, ECAR data for gut tissues (Figure 1B) differed significant across treatments (p‐value < 0.01), indicating a pronounced effect of diet on glycolytic activity. The gut, as the primary interface for substrate uptake and microbial interaction, is more directly exposed to dietary inputs and therefore provides a more sensitive readout of metabolic responses to plastic ingestion and starvation. Elevated ECAR in plastic‐fed and starved larvae likely reflects a compensatory increase in glycolytic flux in response to energetic stress and disrupted metabolic homeostasis.

In the case of PLA, partial hydrolysis of ester bonds may release small organic acids, including lactic acid, which could directly or indirectly contribute to increased glycolytic activity in gut tissues. These locally available substrates may enhance glycolytic flux without necessarily providing a net energetic benefit to the host, consistent with the observed reduction in growth and overall metabolic constraint. Together, these findings highlight tissue‐specific metabolic responses, where gut metabolism reflects immediate dietary and microbial interactions, while fat body responses are moderated by broader energy storage dynamics.

The observed metabolic suppression in LDPE‐fed larvae, alongside reduced growth, raises concerns about potential toxicological effects linked to dietary plastics. Elevated ECAR in the gut suggests that glycolytic pathways are upregulated, which could indicate contaminant‐induced stress and disruptions in normal cellular processes. While reduced energetic capacity may have downstream consequences for development or reproduction, these outcomes were not directly assessed in this study.

The insect fat body is a metabolically plastic tissue that responds dynamically to nutrient availability and energy storage demands, whereas the gut reflects more direct and immediate diet–substrate interactions. Consequently, diet‐driven metabolic effects may be more pronounced in gut tissue, with fat body responses partially buffered by stored energy reserves. In the case of PLA, hydrolysis products such as lactic acid may contribute to increased glycolytic flux in the gut, providing a plausible link between polymer chemistry and observed metabolic responses.

Changes of Gut Microbiome in Plastic‐Fed Larvae

An in‐depth taxonomic exploration of microbial communities across the diet treatments was conducted using 16S rRNA gene sequencing to uncover the microbial diversity present. Our analysis identified a remarkable 26,956 zOTUs, of which a small number of dominant zOTUs accounted for the vast majority of the total microbial composition within each treatment. This striking concentration of microbial diversity within a handful of taxa highlights the highly uneven structure of these microbial communities and suggests that dietary regime strongly selected for a limited number of predominant taxa. Because each microbiome profile was derived from pooled guts of five individuals, these results describe treatment‑level community composition and dominant taxa rather than within‑treatment variability.

The diversity of microbial communities associated with larvae differed across diets in this dataset, as summarized using Shannon's Diversity Index (H'). Starved larvae exhibited very low diversity (0.0364), likely indicative of a stressed microbial community with minimal composition. Similarly, control larvae showed very low diversity (0.0405), reflective of a baseline or limited microbial composition. PLA‐fed larvae displayed slightly higher diversity than starved and control larvae (H' = 0.1917). In contrast, LDPE‐fed larvae demonstrated high alpha diversity (1.3649). However, increased diversity under plastic diets may reflect dysbiosis or opportunistic microbial expansion rather than a beneficial or stable microbiome state.

The gamma diversity, defined here as Shannon diversity calculated on the pooled abundance table across all treatments, was 1.202, reflecting the entire microbial diversity observed across all treatments within the study. Despite the high alpha diversity in the LDPE‐fed group, the overall gamma diversity indicates that other conditions contribute minimally to the total diversity. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray‐Curtis dissimilarity, which indicated substantial compositional differences between feeding conditions, particularly between LDPE‐fed larvae and the other groups. These results support the view that dietary inputs strongly shaped distinct microbial community structures, with LDPE‐fed larvae showing the most pronounced divergence. Noting that the 16S profiles were generated from single pooled gut samples per diet (comprising pooled guts from individuals), and therefore these microbiome results should be interpreted as exploratory, describing large compositional shifts rather than supporting formal inference about within‐diet variability.

Our findings reveal a consistent dominance of Firmicutes phylum across various feeding conditions, with its prevalence reaching over 99% in all larvae groups examined. Lou et al. (2020) identified Firmicutes and Proteobacteria as core gut bacteria in Greater Wax Moth larvae, highlighting their prevalence across nine diverse feeding conditions. The genus Enterococcus was found to be overwhelmingly dominant in PLA‐fed (97.9%), control (99.8%), and starved larvae (99.7%), with its abundance nearing total microbial composition, while it was virtually absent in LDPE‐fed larvae (0.14%). Conversely, Bacillus spp. emerged as the predominant genus in LDPE‐fed larvae, constituting 99.3% of the microbial community. Bacillus spp., which dominated LDPE‑fed larvae, are documented to produce alkane‑hydroxylase enzymes capable of initiating polyethylene depolymerization (Sapountzis et al. 2015), whereas Enterococcus spp., enriched in PLA‑fed larvae, possess esterases that hydrolyze ester bonds in PLA, which may be relevant to polymer breakdown (LeMoine et al. 2020). This stark contrast in microbial dominance between regimens underscores the significant impact of diet‐specific nutrients on microbial community structure, pointing to a dynamic interaction between plastic‐derived nutrients and microbial ecology. Additionally, the observed shifts in Lactococcus spp. and Clostridium sensu stricto 1 populations across different diets highlight the microbial responses to dietary variations, with potential implications for understanding the ecological dynamics within larval systems. While enzymatic activities were not directly measured here, the dominance of taxa previously associated with hydrocarbon or ester metabolism suggests potential functional relevance that warrants targeted gene expression or proteomic validation in future studies.

The pronounced dominance of specific microbial taxa, such as Enterococcus in PLA‐fed, control, and starved larvae, and Bacillus in LDPE‐fed larvae, has profound ecological implications. The overwhelming presence of Enterococcus could indicate a microbiome configuration associated with the PLA‐fed condition, suggesting a symbiotic relationship between these larvae and their microbial inhabitants that may be relevant to plastic biodegradation. These results align with the findings of Cassone et al. (2020). On the other hand, the dominance of Bacillus in LDPE‐fed larvae might reflect an adaptive response to metabolize LDPE components. Interestingly, the dominance of Bacillus spp. in LDPE‐fed larvae aligns with previous studies showing its capacity to metabolize hydrocarbons, suggesting potential enzymatic parallels with plastic degradation (Sapountzis et al. 2015). This raises the possibility of leveraging Bacillus spp. as a microbial bioremediation agent. These findings suggest that microbial community dynamics are closely tied to the dietary inputs of their hosts, impacting not only the health and development of the larvae but also their ecological roles in plastic degradation processes. Further research into the functional capabilities of these dominant taxa could uncover key enzymes or metabolic pathways critical for plastic biodegradation, offering new avenues for enhancing bioremediation efforts.

In comparison with existing literature, diet‐specific microbial dominance is a common theme (Russell et al. 2009; Sapountzis et al. 2015; Cassone et al. 2020; Przemieniecki et al. 2020; Peydaei et al. 2021), yet the pronounced dominance of Enterococcus and Bacillus in response to plastic‐derived nutrients in the current study is relatively novel. For instance, studies in similar larval systems have shown microbial community shifts in response to diet (Ruiz Barrionuevo et al. 2022); however, the extreme dominance of a single genus in the degradation of specific plastics, as observed in our study, underscores the unique adaptive responses of microbial communities to anthropogenic materials. The replacement of beneficial symbionts such as Enterococcus with Bacillus in LDPE‐fed larvae potentially highlights that this shift in composition may have also contributed to the decrease in growth due to potential loss of beneficial symbionts. This comparison not only validates our findings but also highlights the innovative aspect of our research in identifying microbial taxa with potential roles in plastic biodegradation. Although pooling across individuals strengthens confidence that dominant taxa reflect shared community members rather than single‐larva artefacts, the absence of biological replication limits statistical interpretation. Accordingly, microbiome findings are discussed as descriptive, hypothesis‐generating observations.

Central Carbon Metabolism in Wax Moth Larvae

The ANOVA analysis of the CCM metabolite dataset identified 87 significant metabolites (p‐value < 0.01) (Supporting Information Table S2, Figure S3) linked to the diet. These findings provide a refined focus on key metabolites pivotal in delineating variations associated with diverse feeding regimes. The significant metabolites were subjected to pathway analysis using MBROLE3. Based on the 70 metabolites that were mapped to known pathways, the analysis revealed a diverse array of significant pathways (p‐value < 0.01) crucial to cellular function and regulation (Figure 2, Supporting Information Table S3). These pathways span a spectrum of biological processes, ranging from fundamental carbohydrate metabolism pathways (including citrate cycle, galactose metabolism, the pentose phosphate pathway, the pentose and glucuronate interconversions, and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism), amino acid biosynthesis (including alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism), cofactor metabolism, and nucleotide regulation to intricate signaling pathways such as aminoacyl‐tRNA biosynthesis. The identification of ABC transporters underscores the importance of transport processes in cellular dynamics.

The pathway enrichment analysis identified several significant metabolic pathways (p‐value < 0.05) that are central to cellular energy homeostasis under plastic and starvation diets. Enrichment of the citrate cycle, carbon metabolism, and purine and pyrimidine metabolism pathways highlights increased energetic demand alongside maintenance of nucleotide pools required for DNA repair, replication, and RNA synthesis under metabolic stress. These pathways are indicative of a cellular state prioritizing basal energy maintenance and macromolecular turnover rather than growth, consistent with constrained nutritional conditions imposed by LDPE and PLA diets.

Several carbohydrate‐related pathways, including galactose metabolism, the pentose phosphate pathway, and pentose and glucuronate interconversions, were significantly enriched. These pathways support metabolic flexibility by enabling the generation of alternative substrates for energy production and providing reducing equivalents (NADPH) required to counter oxidative stress. The enrichment of glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism further suggests a shift towards alternative carbon utilization pathways, which may reflect adaptation to unconventional and energetically poor substrates such as plastics.

Amino acid‐related pathways, including biosynthesis of amino acids and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, were prominently enriched, underscoring increased amino acid turnover under plastic exposure. Activation of aminoacyl‐tRNA biosynthesis further supports increased protein synthesis and repair demands, potentially reflecting a compensatory response to oxidative stress and substrate limitation. Together, these pathways suggest that both host and microbiota prioritize metabolic flexibility and cellular maintenance rather than anabolic growth under plastic‐based diets.

The enrichment of ABC transporter pathways highlights the importance of transport systems involved in nutrient uptake, metabolite exchange, and toxin efflux. Under plastic exposure, efficient transport mechanisms are critical for maintaining cellular and microbial homeostasis, particularly in the presence of polymer‐derived compounds and stress‐associated metabolites. Enrichment of biosynthesis of cofactors and vitamins further supports detoxification and enzymatic resilience, facilitating metabolic adaptation under chemically challenging dietary conditions.

The pathway enrichment analysis highlights several significant metabolic pathways (p‐value < 0.05), each playing a critical role in maintaining cellular function, adapting to dietary stresses, and supporting microbiome‐host co‐metabolism. Among these, the biosynthesis of amino acids and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism pathways underscore the heightened demand for amino acid turnover. These pathways not only support protein synthesis but also facilitate metabolic flexibility, enabling the host and its microbiota to adapt to environmental and dietary stresses. The activation of aminoacyl‐tRNA biosynthesis pathways appears to be a critical factor in facilitating amino acid turnover and the activation of interconnected metabolic pathways, enabling the larvae to adapt to oxidative stress and unconventional diets (Vemuri et al. 2018).

The enrichment of biosynthesis of cofactors suggests an increased demand for essential vitamins and coenzymes that play pivotal roles in enzymatic reactions crucial for energy production, detoxification, and macromolecule synthesis. This aligns with the observed adaptation to metabolize unconventional and difficult substrates such as LDPE and PLA. Similarly, purine and pyrimidine metabolism reflect the importance of maintaining nucleotide pools for DNA repair, replication, and RNA synthesis, processes that are crucial under conditions of increased oxidative or metabolic stress. The citrate cycle and carbon metabolism pathways are central to cellular energy production and the synthesis of metabolic intermediates required for biosynthetic pathways. Their enrichment suggests a strategic response to altered energy demands imposed by LDPE and PLA diets, ensuring the larvae and their gut microbiota maintain energy homeostasis. The involvement of pentose phosphate pathway and pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathways highlights the generation of reducing equivalents (NADPH) necessary for combating oxidative stress and supporting anabolic reactions.

Metabolic pathways such as galactose metabolism and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism point to the flexibility of carbohydrate metabolism, enabling the microbiome and host to adapt to varying energy sources. The observed shift toward these pathways may reflect a direct response to the unique dietary challenges posed by LDPE and PLA, including the generation of alternative substrates for energy and biosynthesis. The identification of ABC transporters underscores the critical role of transport systems in facilitating nutrient uptake, metabolite exchange, and toxin expulsion. This is particularly significant in environments altered by plastic exposure, where efficient transport mechanisms become essential for maintaining cellular and microbial homeostasis.

Lipid Variations in Wax Moth Larvae

Multivariate analysis of the lipidome revealed diet‐dependent differences across feeding regimes. PCA and PLS‐DA analyzes revealed clear lipidomic separation across feeding regimes (Figure 3). PCA demonstrated separation between control, PLA/starved, and LDPE‐fed larvae (Figure 3A), explaining 81.1% of total lipid variance. These differences were more clearly resolved using PLS‐DA (Figure 3B), with high model performance (R2Y = 95.3%, Q2 = 87.3%) and significant group separation confirmed by CV‐ANOVA (p < 0.01, Supporting Information Table S4, Figure S4). The multivariate analysis of the lipidome dataset revealed 502 significant lipid features (p‐value < 0.01, Supporting Information Table S5). Biologically, lipid shifts across diets were consistent with altered membrane composition and energy homeostasis under plastic and starvation conditions. Changes in lipid profiles, particularly those involving structural and signaling lipids, suggest membrane stress and adaptive remodeling in response to nutritionally and chemically challenging substrates. Such lipid alterations are commonly associated with impaired energy balance, oxidative stress, and constrained growth in insects exposed to environmental stressors. Together with the observed reductions in growth and altered mitochondrial bioenergetics, the lipidome data indicate that plastic diets impose physiological stress rather than supporting a metabolically favorable state, reinforcing the interpretation of plastic consumption as energetically constraining.

Biological and Statistical Correlations Between Bacteria and Metabolites

The network analysis using MetOrigin further visualized their statistical correlations and biological relationships between microbiota and metabolites. The analysis of the microbiome‐metabolite integration has highlighted the co‐metabolism between the wax moth larvae exposed to PLA or LDPE and their gut microbiota, showcasing how both entities share a metabolic interface that is both complex and essential for their mutual survival and health. The prevalence of co‐metabolism underscores a symbiotic relationship where metabolic products are exchanged or shared between host and microbiome, facilitating a harmonious existence that benefits both parties. Because multiple taxa can share overlapping metabolic functions, metabolite‐to‐taxon assignments should be interpreted as associative and putative, highlighting candidate contributors rather than uniquely identifying causative organisms.

Exposure to LDPE

The integrative analysis of microbiome‐metabolite interactions in larvae consuming LDPE reveals a complex metabolic network influenced by LDPE consumption (Supporting Information Table S6). This diversity in metabolic pathways suggests that the gut microbiome undergoes considerable adaptation to process LDPE components or to counteract the stress induced by this unconventional diet. Such adaptation is evident in the activation of a wide range of metabolic pathways, reflecting the ability of the microbiome to metabolize LDPE components and/or to respond to the dietary stress it imposes. Taxonomic analysis further reveals the role of specific microbial groups in this metabolic adaptation, with Gammaproteobacteria emerging as the dominant bacterial class (Supporting Information Table S8). This class of bacteria, known for its metabolic versatility, appears to be a cornerstone within the gut microbiome of LDPE‐fed wax moth larvae, playing a critical role in the metabolic dialog between host and microbiome. The metabolic proficiency of Gammaproteobacteria suggests its importance in facilitating the adjustment to dietary shifts in hosts and environmental stress. The 16S profiles reflect community composition in pooled gut samples, whereas MetOrigin‐based associations reflect metabolite‐linked functional annotations and correlation structure. A taxon that is less abundant can still be functionally influential if it contributes disproportionately to specific reactions/metabolites, and functional assignments may aggregate across related reference taxa.

The results indicate significant changes in the presence of other bacterial classes, such as Bacilli, Alphaproteobacteria, and Myxococcia, alongside a decrease in Clostridia, within these co‐metabolic pathways (Supporting Information Table S8). This alteration in microbial composition underscores the rich diversity of the gut microbiome and its contribution to metabolic adaptability. Such diversity provides a wide array of enzymes crucial for processing diverse dietary components, neutralizing toxins, and synthesizing vital nutrients and signaling compounds. This enzymatic versatility is likely essential for the survival of the host in fluctuating environments and under various dietary conditions.

In the metabolic landscape shaped by LDPE exposure, our analysis revealed significant shifts (p‐value < 0.05) that highlight flexible metabolic machinery of the wax moth larvae. A notable pivot toward carbohydrate metabolism was evidenced by sixteen reactions in starch and sucrose metabolism and nine in glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, along with seven reactions in the pentose phosphate pathway (Supporting Table S8). Carbohydrate metabolism is a crucial pathway for energy homeostasis, playing a vital role in their growth and development (Zhang et al. 2021). These findings underscore a potential upsurge in energy demand or an adaptive response to the modified carbohydrate profile presented by LDPE (Beale et al. 2022; Shah et al. 2023). The significant metabolites (p‐value < 0.05, d‐xylulose‐5‐phosphate, glyceric acid, l‐glutamic acid, trehalose, and d‐maltose) linked to carbohydrate metabolism exhibited significant (p‐value < 0.05) positive correlations with Gammaproteobacteria, Bacilli, and Myxococcia (Figure 5A). Concurrently, purine metabolism, encompassing fifteen reactions, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism, with fourteen reactions, emerged as significantly enriched pathways (Supporting Information Table S8). This enrichment reflects the critical role these pathways play in maintaining nucleotide pools and coordinating amino acid turnover, extending beyond energy homeostasis (Muhammad et al. 2024). l‐glutamic acid, l‐asparagine, glyceric acid, o‐phospho‐l‐serine, l‐threonine, l‐histidine and l‐serine are significant (p‐value < 0.05) metabolites associated with amino acid metabolism, exhibiting positive correlations with Gammaproteobacteria, Bacilli, and Alphaproteobacteria (Figure 5A). The increased metabolism of amino acids is reported to enhance growth and aid the host in adapting to oxidative stress (Cao et al. 2022; Liang et al. 2022). Furthermore, the emphasis on glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, through ten significant reactions, coupled with the seven reactions in histidine metabolism (Supporting Information Table S8), underscore the adjustment in amino acid and carbon metabolism. These adjustments may signify not just a metabolic reprogramming but also a strategic detoxification of LDPE‐related by‐products. Beyond these primary metabolic shifts, the enrichment of aminoacyl‐tRNA biosynthesis suggests an increased focus on protein synthesis, a foundational response ensuring the translation machinery aligns with the altered metabolic demands. Similarly, pathways like methane metabolism, though less intuitive in the context of LDPE exposure, could hint at an ancillary metabolic route being exploited for energy conservation or secondary metabolite production. Thiamine metabolism also stood out, hinting at the possible role of thiamine (negatively correlated with Gammaproteobacteria) as a co‐factor bolstering enzymatic resilience against LDPE‐induced stress. Lastly, the d‐amino acid metabolism pathway was highlighted, which might reflect a broader spectrum of amino acid processing capabilities, potentially contributing to cellular homeostasis and offering an adaptive advantage in LDPE‐rich environments. Concurrently, the enrichment of xenobiotic metabolism pathway such as naphthalene degradation suggests an effort to mitigate the effects of plastic exposure by breaking down and eliminating plastic (Muhammad et al. 2024). Salicylic acid (associated with biosynthesis of siderophore group nonribosomal peptides–ko01053) and pyridoxic acid (linked to Vitamin B6 metabolism–ko00750) demonstrate significant (p < 0.05) negative correlations with Gammaproteobacteria (Figure 5A).

Exposure to PLA

Analyzing the larval gut microbiome under the influence of a PLA‐rich diet uncovered a notable shift in metabolic programming, marked by significant enrichments (p‐value < 0.05) in various pathways (Supporting Information Table S7). Significant metabolites linked to these pathways, such as o‐phospho‐l‐serine, l‐threonine, l‐asparagine, and l‐histidine, displayed positive correlations with Gammaproteobacteria and Bacilli (Figure 5B). In contrast, xylitol, orotic acid, and uracil exhibited negative correlations with Gammaproteobacteria and Bacilli (Figure 5B). These modifications indicate considerable adaptation of the larval gut microbiome in adapting to the PLA components or countering the stresses imposed by this unconventional diet. The pyrimidine metabolism pathway, which, with 14 significant reactions, reflects a surge in the nucleotide synthesis machinery (Supporting Information Table S9). The upsurge in pyrimidine metabolism suggests an adaptive recalibration to support nucleotide synthesis, which could be crucial for the repair and replication processes under the stress of PLA consumption. The enrichment of the pentose and glucuronate interconversions pathway is suggestive of an enhanced cellular antioxidant response, potentially a strategic reaction to the increased oxidative stress in the gut environment due to PLA assimilation. The significant reactions within the amino acid metabolic pathways, including glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, beta‐alanine metabolism and the aminoacyl‐tRNA biosynthesis pathways (Supporting Information Table S9) underscore an increased demand for amino acid turnover, reflecting a dual role in protein synthesis and the provision of metabolic intermediates for other biochemical pathways. The enrichment of aminoacyl‐tRNA biosynthesis (Supporting Information Table S9) indicates a robust synthetic response, possibly to produce enzymes and structural proteins that facilitate PLA metabolism or counteract its stressors. The beta‐alanine metabolism pathway (Supporting Information Table S9) represents another layer of the microbiome's metabolic versatility, interlinking with vitamin B5 (pantothenate) and coenzyme A synthesis. Its significant activity could indicate a broader metabolic reconfiguration, as these molecules are pivotal in numerous enzymatic reactions, including energy metabolism and fatty acid synthesis.

The taxonomic landscape under PLA conditions also presents intriguing shifts. Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria are marked as major phyla, with Gammaproteobacteria extensively participating across diverse reactions (Supporting Information Table S9). This implies their central role in remodeling the gut microbiome's metabolic network in response to PLA. Interestingly, while Gammaproteobacteria was upregulated in the LDPE environment, a contrasting downregulation is observed under PLA conditions, suggesting variable microbial response to different polymer exposures.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

This study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the use of pooled gut samples for microbiome analyzes, and its subsequent integration with unpooled metabolomics and lipidomics analyzes restricts statistical power and precludes assessment of inter‑individual variability.

Second, the absence of axenic or antibiotic‐treated larvae, which would enable direct separation of host‐ and microbiome‐derived contributions to plastic metabolism. The current findings therefore describe integrated host–microbiome responses under ecologically relevant conditions. Future studies employing gnotobiotic insects will be required to mechanistically dissect microbial causality.

Third, Seahorse bioenergetic measurements were limited to basal OCR and ECAR, without a full mitochondrial stress test, constraining interpretation of mitochondrial capacity. Finally, plastic‑degrading enzymes were not directly measured at the gene or protein level. Accordingly, links between enriched microbial taxa and polymer degradation are inferred from known metabolic capabilities rather than experimentally demonstrated. Future studies incorporating controlled microbial manipulation, enzyme‑level validation, and higher replication will be essential to mechanistically resolve insect–microbiome plastic metabolism.

Conclusions

This study uncovers pivotal aspects of the biological processes underlying plastic degradation by wax moth larvae, advancing the understanding of host‐microbiome interactions in the context of environmental sustainability. The pronounced microbial shifts observed, Bacillus dominance in LDPE‐fed larvae and Enterococcus in PLA‐fed larvae, highlight the specificity of microbial adaptation to distinct polymer types. Metabolomic and lipidomic analyzes further revealed that plastic diets invoke a multifaceted metabolic response, encompassing enhanced carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid biosynthesis, and detoxification pathways. These findings not only elucidate the metabolic and ecological dynamics of plastic degradation but also underscore the physiological constraints faced by larvae, including reduced growth and energy trade‐offs. By integrating microbiome and host metabolic data, this research identifies key microbial taxa and pathways with potential for biotechnological exploitation.

Future research should explore the scalability of insect‐based systems for bioremediation, ensuring ecological risks are minimized. Integrating genetic and enzymatic knowledge into synthetic biology platforms could accelerate the development of microbial consortia capable of efficiently degrading diverse plastic types. The interplay between host and microbiome co‐metabolism highlighted in this study presents a promising avenue for innovation.

Moving forward, leveraging this understanding to optimise microbial consortia and metabolic pathways for industrial applications holds transformative potential. However, the ecological implications of utilising insect‐based systems for large‐scale bioremediation warrant careful evaluation. The findings presented here not only address critical gaps in understanding plastic biodegradation but also pave the way for designing innovative, nature‐inspired solutions to combat the escalating plastic pollution crisis. Future work should focus on the genetic and enzymatic mechanisms driving microbial plastic metabolism to fully realise the potential of these systems in sustainable waste management.
