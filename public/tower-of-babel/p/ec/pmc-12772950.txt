Hypofractionated radiotherapy for glioblastoma: correlation with prognostic factors and survival outcomes
Authors: Dimcho Georgiev, Svetlana Bilyukova, Cyril Kounin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe purpose was to evaluate the efficacy of hypofractionated radiotherapy (HFRT) followed by conventional fractionated radiotherapy (CFRT) in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) patients and identify prognostic factors.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 120 GBM patients treated postoperatively between 2010 and 2017 with HFRT (30 Gy in 3 Gy fractions) followed by CFRT (24 Gy in 2 Gy fractions), for a total dose of 60 Gy. All patients received concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide (TMZ).ResultsMedian age was 62.5 years. Median overall survival (OS) was 11.3 months; 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates were 43.3%, 16.1%, and 4.6%, respectively. Full dose completion correlated with improved OS (12.2 vs. 7.3 vs. 4.6 months for ≥ 100%, 90–99%, and < 70% dose realization, respectively). Age ≤ 63 years and gros tumor volume (GTV) ≤ 44.5 cm3 were associated with significantly better survival. Gender had no significant impact.ConclusionsHFRT followed by CFRT with TMZ is an effective alternative to standard CFRT in patients with subtotal or partial resection. Age and tumor volume are independent prognostic factors for overall survival.

Introduction

In Bulgaria brain tumors account for 3.7% of all patients admitted to oncology. In 2013, 660 new cases were reported, which gives us an epidemiologic ratio of 9.1:100 000 [1]. Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most aggressive primary brain tumor and comprises 60–70% of all brain neoplasms in adults. Despite the major advances in brain imaging that have led to quicker and more accurate diagnosis, better surgical techniques, medical equipment, novel chemotherapeutic and radiotherapeutic modalities — prognosis for patients with GBM remains poor [2–7]. This necessitates the exploration of new therapeutic possibilities such as the substitution of standard radiotherapy (RT) performed over a 6-week period with a hypofractionated regiment. The goal of the current retrospective analysis is to analyse the data of patients with GBM who had been treated with hypofractionated radiotherapy (HFRT) based on OS and determine the prognostic factors.

Materials and methods

We examined the patient data of 120 patients referred to our department for radiotherapy following surgical resection of glioblastoma multiforme in the period between July 2010 and December 2017.

The subsequent information was obtained from the patients’ medical records: 1) Patient characteristics (age, sex and gender); 2) Treatment details [type of surgery and volume of gros tumor volume (GTV)]; 3) Follow-up data.

A combined protocol of HFRT followed by CFRT was applied. 120 patients underwent subtotal or partial surgical resection and, subsequently, received radiation therapy. HFRT — 5 fractions per week 3 Gy each for a total dose of 30 Gy, equivalent to 36 Gy (EQD2). When these patients showed good tolerance and no adverse effects, they went on to receive CFRT — 5 fractions per week 2 Gy each for a total of 24 Gy. The total dose that was ultimately achieved is 60 Gy. 17 patients did not proceed with conventional radiotherapy. The most common reason for discontinuation of radiotherapy was disease progression. During RT all patients received temozolamide (TMZ) at a dose of 75 mg/m2/day.

After the conclusion of a course of adjuvant chemotherapy with temozolomide, there is a lack of comprehensive and systematic information regarding the subsequent treatment of patients in the event of disease progression. We are unable to provide reliable data on whether salvage treatment, such as repeat surgery, radiation therapy, or other oncologic approaches, were implemented in the event of progression due to the retrospective nature of the study and the limited follow-up in certain cases. This constrains the capacity to conduct a thorough evaluation of the therapeutic strategy in relapse and requires that the results be interpreted with caution in this context.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was done using IBM SPSS version 29.0. OS was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and prognostic factors were determined by log rank test. The Cox proportional hazards model was used for univariate analysis. P-values lower than 0.05 were accepted as significant.

Results

The median age at baseline was 62.55 ± 8.91 years, with a minimum age of 25 and a maximum age of 79 years. 67(55.8%) were male and 53 (44.2%) females. The average GTV volume was 44.5 cm3 (range 11.9–179 cm3). Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1.

Patients were followed up from 1 month to 73 months (median 11.3 months) or until death. The observed median survival for the entire cohort was 11.3 months [95% confidence interval (CI): 9.7–12.6] (Fig. 2). The 1-, 2- and 3-year survival rates were 43.3%, 16.1% and 4.6% of patients, respectively.

The impact of age (p = 0.005) shows a statistically significant influence on overall survival (OS) (Fig. 3). Median survival for patients aged ≤ 63 years was 12.4 months (95% CI: 10.1–14.7), for patients aged > 63 years, median survival was 4.55 months 95% CI: 5.7–9.9). Survival in months according to age distribution is shown in Table 2.

A statistically significant dose-response difference was found in overall survival in patients with GBM. When 100% of the prescribed dose was realized, the observed OS was 12.2 months (95% CI: 10.4–13.9). When 99-90% of the dose was realized, the observed OS was 7.3 months (95% CI: 0.96–13.7), whereas when < 70% of the dose was realized, the reported survival was 4.6 months (95% CI: 2.3–6.9) (Fig. 4).

The impact of various patient and treatment-related factors on prognosis in patients is described in Table 3. The univariate analysis showed that age, interruption of RT and > 44.5 cm3 GTV volume were found to be independent prognostic factors for OS. The patients who had GTV volumes higher than 44.5 cm3 were at 1.53 times increased risk negatively impacted OS. There was no significant impact of gender on OS (p = 0.984). OS was adversely affected in patients aged 63 years or older, who were at a 1.57 times increased risk (Tab. 3).

Discussion

The results of this study provide further evidence supporting the feasibility and efficacy of HFRT as a viable alternative to conventional fractionated radiotherapy (CFRT) in the treatment of GBM. The combination of HFRT followed by CFRT, along with concurrent and adjuvant TMZ, demonstrated comparable OS outcomes to those reported for CFRT, while offering significant advantages in terms of treatment duration and patient convenience.

The natural history and prognosis of patients treated for GBM have not changed significantly in the recent decades [3, 5–7, 11]. Best surgical resection followed by radiotherapy combined with chemotherapy remains the mainstay of GBM treatment. However, gross total resection is not always feasible due to the tumor’s infiltrative nature and presence of eloquent brain structures. Postoperative radiotherapy aims to reduce the inevitable microscopic spread and progression of the disease and increase survivability [5–7, 11]. Despite best surgical and RT management overall survival remains low with a mean of 12 months.

The high focal dose requirement in the treatment of high-grade gliomas was well established as far back as 1979 by Walker et al. and was later confirmed by Bleehan and Stenning in 1991 who observed an overall beneficial effect of CFRT up to 60 Gy total radiation dose compared with a lower dose of 45 Gy [8, 23]. Additional benefit to survivability is yet to be observed with total dose over 60 Gy and higher doses are not recommended.

Based on a retrospective analysis in 1993, the radiotherapy oncology group (RTOG) defines 6 distinct prognostic groups in patients treated with anaplastic astrocytoma and glioblastoma multiforme with a mean survival of 4.6 to 58.6 months [9]. With the addition of new chemotherapeutical agents to the existing regiments leading to prolonged survivability in patients with GBM, Li et al. (2011) recognize the need to update these prognostic parameters [13].

The median overall survival of 11.3 months observed in our cohort aligns with the outcomes reported in other studies of hypofractionated radiotherapy regimens. Similar studies using HFRT have reported median OS ranging between 9 and 13 months, with comparable 1- and 2-year survival rates [19]. However, the protocol used in this study-combining HFRT and CFRT to deliver a total dose of 60 Gy may provide a more balanced approach by optimizing tumor control while minimizing long-term toxicities. Additionally, the reduced treatment duration from 6 to 4 weeks represents a significant improvement for patients with limited life expectancy, allowing for better quality of life during their remaining time.

A prospective study by Curran et al. observed lower overall survivability in older patients with low Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) — 4–5 months. Initial reports by Roa et al. in 2004 compare CFRT and HFRT (40 Gy in 15 fractions) in adults with KPS > 50, their findings show no significant difference in overall survival (5.1 and 5.6 months, respectively) between the two groups [18]. In contrast a study by Malmstrom A. et al. in 2012 reveals a significant benefit of HFRT in patients over 70 years of age [14].

McAleese et al. (2003) conducted a study on 92 patients with low KPS, each patient was assigned to HFRT of 30 Gy divided in 5 fractions over 2 weeks. Mean reported survivability was 5 months and 1-year survivability — 12% [15]. Another study by Phillipsa et al. compares HFRT (35 Gy over 10 fractions) to CFRT in 69 patients. They report a mean survivability of 8.7 months in the HFRT group and 10.3 months in the CFRT group with very mild direct adverse effects and no delayed side effects of the treatment [17].

The prognostic value of hypofractionated radiotherapy in glioblastoma has been supported by several recent studies. Notably, Trone et al. (2020) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis that demonstrated encouraging survival outcomes associated with hypofractionated regimens. Their findings emphasized the potential benefits of this approach, particularly in patients with unfavorable prognostic factors such as advanced age or poor performance status. This supports the notion that hypofractionation may be a viable alternative to standard fractionation, especially when tailored to individual patient profiles [21].

Takahashi et al. (2023) underscore the importance of tailoring hypofractionated RT not only to tumor characteristics but also to preserve immune function, noting that moderate hypofractionation reduces treatment-related lymphopenia – a recognized negative prognostic factor [20].

Our analysis identified three independent prognostic factors associated with improved OS: younger age (< 63 years), absence of interruptions in radiotherapy, and smaller gross tumor volume (GTV ≤ 44.5 cm3). These findings are consistent with existing literature. Younger age has been repeatedly shown to correlate with better prognosis in GBM, likely due to improved performance status, more effective treatment tolerance, and less aggressive tumor biology in younger patients. Interruptions in radiotherapy, often caused by edema, disease progression, or poor overall condition, were significantly associated with worse OS. This underscores the critical importance of maintaining uninterrupted treatment schedules to ensure optimal dose delivery. Efforts should be made to manage treatment-related toxicities proactively, such as early intervention for radiation-induced edema, to minimize interruptions. Finally, GTV volume was identified as a significant predictor of survival, with smaller tumors showing better outcomes. This highlights the importance of accurate preoperative imaging and surgical resection to reduce tumor burden prior to radiotherapy. Advances in surgical techniques and the use of molecular markers, such as MGMT promoter methylation and IDH mutations, could further refine treatment strategies for patients with GBM [22].

Given the encouraging results of this study, prospective randomized trials are warranted to further validate the benefits of HFRT combined with CFRT in patients with GBM. The inclusion of advanced imaging modalities, molecular profiling, and real-time adaptive radiotherapy techniques could refine dose delivery and improve treatment outcomes. Moreover, the integration of novel therapeutic agents, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted therapies, into hypofractionated protocols offers exciting opportunities to enhance the efficacy of radiotherapy for GBM. This discussion emphasizes the significance of HFRT as a practical and effective treatment option for GBM, while highlighting areas for future research and clinical optimization. The findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of hypofractionated regimens in managing this challenging disease.

Conclusions

A combined protocol of HFRT followed by CFRT with continuous TMZ showed equivalent efficacy to CFRT with TMZ, which makes it a viable alternative in patients who underwent subtotal or partial surgical resections of GBM. The length of RT is reduced from 6 to 4 weeks which is significant for the quality of life of these difficult to treat patients. Patients allocated to our HRT protocol exhibited a longer mean, 1-, 2- and 5-year survival rate compared to other such studies using different HFRT protocols. The age < 63, discontinuation of RT, and ≤ 44.5 cm3 GTV volume were identified as independent prognostic factors that were associated with an improved overall survival.
