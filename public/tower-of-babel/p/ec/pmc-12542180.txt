Estimating the catastrophic and impoverishing health expenditure risk among malnourished under-five children in Uganda: secondary analysis of national panel data
Authors: Mine Robert Maurice, Orech Sam, Okello Francis, Nabachenje Proscovia, Bisimwa Nankafu, Kagali Annitah Uside, Lubaale Yovani A Moses
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalnutrition remains a significant public health issue in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), contributing to long-term health risks and economic hardship. Despite the recognized connection between malnutrition and health-related financial risks, the intersection of these factors, particularly in relation to catastrophic (CHE) and impoverishing health expenditures (IHE), has received limited attention. This study aimed to estimate the risk of CHE/IHE among households with under-five children affected by malnutrition in Uganda.MethodsUsing data from the 2019/2020 Uganda National Panel Survey (UNPS), this study estimates the risk of CHE and IHE, with CHE defined as out-of-pocket health expenditures exceeding 10% of total household consumption, and IHE as health expenditures that push a household below the poverty line. Both measures were evaluated on a per capita basis to ensure consistency and equity in the comparison of financial hardship across households of different sizes. Bivariate and multivariate probit regression models were used to assess the association between child malnutrition (stunting, wasting, underweight), household wealth, and health expenditure risk. Marginal effects were calculated to quantify the probability changes in relation to malnutrition and wealth.ResultsThe analytical sample comprised 815 households with children under five. The study found that 18.0% of households experienced CHE/IHE, with 17.4% facing CHE and 4.5% facing IHE. Wasting was significantly associated with increased risk of CHE/IHE (β = 2.184, p < 0.001), with a marginal effect of 54.7%, indicating that households with wasted children were 54.7% more likely to experience CHE/IHE compared to those without wasted children. Households in the second and third wealth quintiles had higher odds of incurring CHE/IHE, with marginal effects of 34.3% and 37.7%, respectively. The study identified a U-shaped relationship between socio-economic status and CHE/IHE risk, where wealthier households did not significantly differ from the poorest households.ConclusionsThis study underscores the intersection between child malnutrition and health-related financial vulnerability in Uganda, highlighting acute malnutrition as a key marker of risk for catastrophic or impoverishing health expenditures. The analysis supports the need for an integrated, equity-sensitive approach to financial protection in health that considers both the nutritional and economic vulnerabilities of households. These findings contribute to the limited but growing body of evidence linking child health status to financial risk, especially in LMICs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01098-5.

Introduction

Malnutrition, particularly among children under five, remains a significant global public health concern, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). A form of malnutrition—under-nutrition—is characterized by deficiencies in energy and essential nutrients and is commonly classified into four categories: stunting (low height-for-age), wasting (low weight-for-height), underweight (low weight-for-age), and micronutrient deficiencies [1, 2]. Globally, approximately 149 million children under five are stunted, 45 million are wasted, and 29% of all under-five deaths are attributed to malnutrition [3, 4]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and South Asia endure a disproportionate share of this burden, accounting for over two-thirds of all malnourished children globally [5]. In Uganda, 24.4% of children under five are stunted, 3.2% are wasted, and 9.7% are underweight [6]. Furthermore, malnutrition accounts for nearly half of all under-five child deaths and costs the country approximately Uganda shillings 1.8 trillion annually, equivalent to 5.6% of the national gross domestic product (GDP) [7, 8].

Health financial risk, in terms of catastrophic or impoverishing health expenditures (CHE/IHE) is a related public health challenge. CHE is expressed as out-of-pocket (OOP) health spending that exceeds a household’s capacity to pay, often defined as exceeding 10% of total consumption or 40% of non-food expenditure [9, 10]. CHE compromises financial risk protection (FRP), a cornerstone of universal health coverage (UHC), and exposes households—particularly those in LMICs—to the risk of impoverishment [11]. Globally, nearly 2 billion people experience CHE or impoverishing OOP health expenditure annually, with nearly 10% of these cases occurring in Africa [12, 13]. In Uganda, 14.2% of households face CHE due to limited health financing mechanisms, overreliance on OOP payments, and under-resourced social safety nets [14]. Households impacted by poverty, illness, or malnutrition are especially vulnerable to CHE, leading to further socioeconomic decline, reduced health-seeking behavior, and long-term inequities [15].

Despite the public health significance of child malnutrition and health-related financial risk, their intersection has received limited attention. Emerging evidence from Nigeria and other LMICs suggests that households with children affected by malnutrition face a higher risk of CHE [16]. Existing studies often examine CHE and IHE separately and use different methodological frameworks, making it difficult to compare the two or interpret them equitably across household sizes. This study introduces a harmonized, equity-sensitive approach by estimating CHE using per capita household consumption expenditure, aligning it conceptually with IHE, which is also assessed against a per capita poverty line. By evaluating health-related financial hardship on a per capita basis, the analysis provides a more accurate reflection of how healthcare costs impact households relative to their actual resources, enabling fairer comparisons across socio-economic and demographic groups. Using nationally representative panel data from the 2019/2020 Uganda National Panel Survey (UNPS), this study aimed to estimate the risk of catastrophic and/or impoverishing health expenditure among households with under-five children affected by undernutrition. It represents the first population-level analysis of this kind in Uganda and offers new insights into the intersection of child malnutrition, health financing, and equity in LMIC settings.

Methods

Study design and data source

This study employed a cross-sectional analytical design using secondary data from the 2019/2020 UNPS, conducted by the Uganda Bureau of Statistics (UBOS). The UNPS is a nationally representative household survey that collects multi-sectoral data, including information on health, nutrition, income, consumption, education, employment, and poverty indicators. The survey is conducted in waves, and the 2019/2020 round—used in this study—provided both cross-sectional and longitudinal data, enabling trend analysis and regional comparisons.

UNPS utilized a two-stage stratified sampling design to ensure regional and national representativeness. In the first stage, 322 Enumeration Areas (EAs) were selected from the 2014 Uganda National Population and Housing Census sampling frame. These were stratified by region and urban/rural classification. In the second stage, 3,123 households were selected across five geographical regions: Kampala, Central (excluding Kampala), Eastern, Northern, and Western Uganda. Data collection occurred from October 2019 to September 2020 in two phases, beginning with training and instrument piloting, followed by main data collection using Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing (CAPI) and GPS-enabled devices for real-time validation and geo-referencing.

Variable extraction, definition and measurement

A variable extraction tool (Supplementary Table S1) was developed from the UBOS’ household questionnaire for the UNPS 2019/2020. The tool guided in the process of variable classification, definition and measurement.

Measurement of child malnutrition

For this study, malnutrition was defined as undernutrition, measured using three standard anthropometric indicators: stunting (low height-for-age), wasting (low weight-for-height), and underweight (low weight-for-age). The terms malnutrition and malnourished are used throughout the paper to refer specifically to undernutrition.

Child malnutrition was assessed using WHO growth standards based on z-scores: height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-height (WHZ), and weight-for-age (WAZ). A z-score less than − 2 standard deviations (SD) from the median reference population defined malnutrition [2]. Children were classified as stunted (HAZ < − 2SD), wasted (WHZ < − 2SD), or underweight (WAZ < − 2SD). For severity analysis, children with z-scores < − 3SD were considered severely malnourished.

Data management and analysis

Data were cleaned, merged, and analyzed using Stata version 15. UNPS sampling weights were applied to adjust for unequal probability of selection and non-response. The analysis incorporated primary sampling unit (PSU) and strata variables to account for the survey’s complex design using the svy suite of commands.

Study population and sampling criteria

The analytical sample consisted of 815 households meeting the following inclusion criteria: (1) had at least one child under five years of age with complete anthropometric measurements (age, height, weight); (2) sought healthcare and incurred OOP health expenditures during the reference period; and (3) were not covered by any form of health insurance. Households were excluded if they did not seek care, had no recorded OOP health spending, lacked anthropometric data, or were insured. How the inclusion-exclusion process was undertaken to obtain the analytical sample is herein summarized in form of a flowchart (Supplementary Figure S1).

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics summarized key household and child characteristics. Categorical variables were presented as proportions and percentages; continuous variables were reported as means or medians, depending on distribution. A map of Uganda showing the distribution of malnutrition (including the severe form) by region was included to help appreciate the health-related financial risk. Rao-Scott chi-squared tests were used to assess associations in the distribution of variables.

We used probit regression models to examine the relationship between malnutrition and the risk of CHE/IHE. Bivariate analysis estimated unadjusted associations, while multivariate probit models adjusted for potential confounders such as household income, maternal education, region, and urban-rural residence. Results were reported as marginal effects with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To assess the robustness of risk estimates for CHE/IHE, sensitivity analyses were conducted. CHE was redefined using an alternative threshold of 15% of total household consumption per capita. IHE was reassessed using an alternative PL of U$1.25 per person per day (equivalent to approximately 142,500 Ugandan shillings per person per month). Additionally, probit models were re-estimated under these alternative definitions to examine consistency of associations.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Busitema University Faculty of Health Sciences (Approval No. BUFHS-2023-114). Permission to access and use the UNPS 2019/2020 data was obtained from UBOS. All data used in this study were anonymized and publicly available for research purposes.

Results

Background characteristics of under-five children and their households

A total of 815 under-five children were included in the analysis. Of these, 13.4% were stunted, 5.3% were wasted, and 5.8% were underweight (Table 1). More than a half (51%) of the children were female and the majority (73.2%) were from rural households. The central region accounted for the largest share (36.6%) of the children. The mean age, weight and height of the children was 39.2 months, 13.6 kg and 92.4 cm respectively. Severe acute malnutrition was most prevalent (0.8%) in eastern Uganda (Supplementary Figure S2).

Association of the independent and dependent variables

Bivariate probit regressions were conducted to assess the unadjusted association between CHE/IHE and selected child and household characteristics (Table 3). Only wasting showed significant association with CHE/IHE (β = 1.106, p = 0.027), suggesting that households with wasted children were more likely to incur CHE/IHE (marginal effect = 27.8%, 95% CI: −5.4%, 50.2%). Other indicators, including stunting, underweight, child sex, location, and region were not significantly associated with CHE/IHE in the unadjusted models. Notably, households in the second (β = 1.229, p = 0.011) and third wealth quintiles (β = 1.349, p = 0.010) had a significantly higher likelihood of CHE/IHE compared to the poorest households, indicating a non-linear pattern across socioeconomic strata.

Households in the second and third wealth quintiles also had significantly increased odds of CHE/IHE (quintile 2: β = 1.698, p = 0.001; marginal effect = 34.3%; quintile 3: β = 1.798, p = 0.003; marginal effect = 37.7%), suggesting a U-shaped relationship between socioeconomic status and CHE/IHE risk. Surprisingly, the richest quintile showed no significant difference compared to the poorest. Stunting, underweight, and regional variation were not significantly associated with CHE/IHE in the multivariate model.

Sensitivity analysis

In the alternative model specification, child wasting remained a significant predictor of CHE/IHE risk, though marginal effects were slightly attenuated at higher thresholds (Supplementary Table S2). The direction and significance of associations for wealth quintiles also remained broadly consistent. These findings suggest that the relationship between undernutrition and financial hardship is robust to variations in CHE/IHE definitions.

Discussions

The study introduces an equity-sensitive approach to assessing the relationship between child malnutrition and household financial vulnerability, using per capita household consumption to harmonize the measurement of CHE and IHE. This method addresses inconsistencies in how financial hardship is typically assessed, improving comparability and equity relevance. By accounting for household size and consumption capacity, the analysis provides a clearer view of how health-related financial risk is distributed in resource-limited settings. These findings underscore the importance of linking child nutrition and financial protection in health and social policy planning.

Our findings indicate a significant association between wasting and increased likelihood of CHE/IHE, suggesting that children suffering from severe forms of malnutrition pose a dual burden of both poor health and financial hardship for their households. Wasting is often associated with sudden onset illness, complications such as infections or dehydration, and urgent need for medical attention [18]. These episodes can drive families to seek care quickly, increasing OOP expenses [19]. This supports previous research showing that malnourished children are more likely to increase household health expenditures, pushing families further into poverty and reducing health-seeking behaviors [20, 21]. In contrast, stunting and underweight, while serious in terms of long-term development, may not result in immediate care-seeking or acute health expenditure, which could explain their lack of association with CHE/IHE in this study. This distinction underscores the importance of recognizing how different forms of child malnutrition place varying demands on household finances and health systems.

It is interesting to note the U-shaped relationship between wealth quintiles and health financial hardship, where households in the second and third quintiles are at higher risk of incurring CHE or IHE compared to the poorest. This may be indicative of a “safety net” effect, where the poorest households may have access to some form of external aid or safety nets (e.g., community support systems or government programs) that protect them from catastrophic health expenditures [22]. This finding aligns with other studies in LMICs showing that near poor households are particularly vulnerable to OOP spending [23]. The negative association between female child sex and financial hardship may reflect gendered differences in healthcare-seeking behavior, where male children are more likely to receive formal care [24]. Moreover, female children may benefit more from maternal health services, social protection programs, or community-based health interventions that target children and offer financial protection [25]. The resilience observed in female children may also be linked to household coping strategies that prioritize the health of female children, particularly in the context of preventive care [26].

The absence of significant regional or urban-rural variation in CHE/IHE risk suggests that financial vulnerability linked to child health is widespread, not confined to specific geographic areas [27]. This reinforces the need for protection mechanisms that move beyond geographic targeting and instead prioritize house-level risk factors such as nutritional status and per capita resource availability. Equity-sensitive metrics, like those used in the study, offer a more accurate picture of financial hardship across diverse household types and can inform fairer, more inclusive health financing strategies [28]. Integrating FRP into child health and nutrition programs – especially for acutely malnourished and the near-poor – can help prevent health spending from exacerbating poverty and support progress toward UHC.

Conclusions

This study underscores the intersection between child malnutrition and health-related financial vulnerability in Uganda, highlighting acute malnutrition as a key marker of risk for catastrophic or impoverishing health expenditures. The analysis supports the need for an integrated, equity-sensitive approach to financial protection in health that considers both the nutritional and economic vulnerabilities of households. These findings contribute to the limited but growing body of evidence linking child health status to financial risk, especially in LMICs.

Recommendations

Integrate nutrition into financial risk protection policies by explicitly including households with malnourished children—particularly those with acute malnutrition—as priority groups in national health financing strategies.
