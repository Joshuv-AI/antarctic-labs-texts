Exploration of patient blood management metrics for emergency departments: a 4-year retrospective monocenter study
Authors: Fabien Coisy, Florian Ajavon, Alberto Di Castri, Mélodie Lagarrigue, Céline Occelli, Xavier Bobbia, Romain Genre Grandpierre
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRed blood cell (RBC) transfusions are essential in emergency departments (ED), but their practices lack standardization, often leading to inefficiencies and risks. Patient blood management is poorly developed in ED, particularly because of the diverse and acute nature of patients.ObjectivesDescribe RBC transfusions in ED and develop a metric to benchmark RBC transfusion efficiency in the ED.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed red blood cells transfusions at a French University Hospital (2020–2023). The yearly adjusted number of transfusions per ED-patient (YANTED) was calculated by adjusting the number of ED visits to exclude low-acuity cases. Metrics included transfusion timing, urgency level (immediate life-threatening emergency, life-threatening emergency, relative emergency and nonemergency), and the proportion of RBC transfused in the ED compared with hospital-wide transfusions.ResultsA total of 5,537 RBC units were transfused in the ED over four years, with a median YANTED of 16.3 (15.6-17.3) per 1,000 ED patients. Relative emergency transfusions accounted for 67% of all transfusions. Nighttime transfusions represented 45% of the total. The median time from RBC order to transfusion initiation was 182 min. The delivery and transfusion times for the immediate life-threatening emergency, life-threatening emergency patients were detailed, with 23% completed within the 30-minute threshold. ED accounted for 11% (95% CI: 10–12%) of total hospital transfusions.ConclusionOver a 4-year period, the YANTED was 16.7 (15.6–17.3) RBC units per 1,000 ED patients. Relative emergency prescriptions accounted for 67% of the transfusions and occurred mostly at night. Introduction

Red blood cell (RBC) transfusions are frequent in emergency departments (EDs) and play a crucial role in managing acute medical conditions where prompt intervention is critical. The efficiency of transfusion delivery can significantly influence patient outcomes, especially in life-threatening situations [1, 2]. However, the ED setting presents unique challenges to optimizing transfusion practices, including managing variable patient flow, ensuring rapid blood product availability, and preventing transfusion-related complications [3, 4].

Patient blood management (PBM), a multidisciplinary approach to optimizing transfusion practices, has emerged as a strategy to address these challenges. PBM focuses on three key pillars: enhancing the patient’s own RBC mass, minimizing blood loss, and improving tolerance to anemia [5]. Despite its growing adoption in hospital settings, the implementation of PBM in EDs remains underexplored, particularly owing to the complexity and diversity of ED operations [6, 7]. Moreover, transfusion practices within EDs often lack standardization, with limited data available to assess their appropriateness or efficacy [8].

Recent studies have suggested that tailored metrics could better evaluate transfusion practices in EDs and support the implementation of PBM strategies [9, 10]. However, metrics, especially those designed for the ED, are poorly described in the current literature. Most studies examining transfusion activity include all ED consultations, which often encompass a large proportion of low-acuity patients who should not be treated in an ED settings [11]. To address this limitation, transfusion activity in the ED should reflect ED-specific activity, excluding low-acuity cases, thereby providing a more accurate and meaningful measure of transfusion practices. The yearly adjusted number of transfusions per ED-patient (YANTED) emerges as a particularly relevant indicator because it adjusts for the actual workload of the ED, allowing for more precise benchmarking and insights into transfusion efficiency.

This study hypothesizes that the YANTED is a relevant metric for evaluating transfusion practices in the ED. The primary objective was to assess RBC utilization in the ED using the YANTED as the main indicator. The secondary objectives included analyzing the distribution of transfusion urgency categories.

Material and methods

Study design and ethics

This retrospective study analyzed RBC transfusions issued to the ED at the University Hospital of Nîmes from January 2020 to December 2023. Ethical approval was granted by the local ethics committee (IRB No. CHU Nîmes 24.07.01). The research was conducted ethically, with all study procedures being performed in accordance with the requirements of the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki. The hospital where the research was conducted is a tertiary care center located in an urban area, serving a population of approximately 259,000. It is a Level 1 trauma center with 1,923 beds, including 118 critical care beds (51 intensive care and 67 intermediate care). Each year, the hospital records around 119,000 hospitalizations and performs approximately 3,500 emergency surgical procedures. The study adheres to the STROBE guidelines [12].

Study objectives

The primary objective was to evaluate RBC utilization in the ED using the YANTED as the main indicator. YANTED represents the yearly adjusted number of transfusions per 1,000 ED patients, accounting for ED-specific activity by excluding low-acuity cases. Adjusted ED visits were calculated by subtracting the number of patients classified as low acuity on the French clinical classification of emergency from the total annual ED visits [11]. This classification ranges from level 1 (patients requiring medical examination without complementary tests) to level 5 (patients requiring immediate life-sustaining therapy). Level 1 corresponds to patients requiring medical examination without complementary tests. This adjustment ensures a more accurate reflection of ED activity by excluding patients who do not align with the core mission of emergency medicine and would otherwise dilute transfusion rates.

The secondary objectives included the following: (1) Assessing concordance between the patient and delivered blood groups for the ABO and Rhesus systems, as well as the ABO + Rhesus combination. (2) Analyzing the distribution of transfusions by urgency category (immediate life-threatening emergencies (ILTE), life-threatening emergencies (LTE), relative emergencies (RE) and nonemergency (NE)) and their respective frequencies during the daytime (6 AM–10 PM) and nighttime (10 PM–6 AM). (3) Evaluating transfusion timing, including the time from RBC order to delivery and from delivery to transfusion, categorized by urgency level. (4) The proportion of RBCs transfused in the ED relative to the total number of RBC zf transfused in the hospital (PTEH) was calculated to benchmark ED transfusion practices against overall hospital activity.

Data collection

Data were extracted via the RBC prescription software Cursus 2 (Guyot Walser Informatique, Reims, France). The collected data included the urgency level according to French regulations: ILTEs, which must be immediately provided; LTEs, which must be delivered within 30 min; REs, which must be delivered within 3 h; and NEs, which can be delivered at any time following prescription [13]. Additionally, the durations of RBC order, delivery, and transfusion were recorded. RBC ordered in the ED but transfused in another department were excluded from the analysis. Nighttime was defined as the period between 10 PM and 6 AM, as it coincides with the hours when fewer physicians and nurses are present in the ED, aligning with the natural sleep cycle and potentially increasing the risk of medical errors [14, 15]. This study focused on RBC delivery, and the reasons for transfusion were not collected.

For patients admitted to the resuscitation room in the ED with hemorrhagic shock, there is a supply of RBC concentrates containing two units of O-negative RBCs that can be used immediately while waiting for additional supplies to be delivered, if available. However, these situations were not included in the analysis because there is no computerized ordering system for these concentrates. This hospital does not have a delivery protocol for massive transfusions. In fact, pre-established protocols pose a high risk of losing labile blood products (LBP), so the decision was made to implement rapid orders adapted to patients’ needs [16].

Description of the interactions between ED and blood bank

Blood samples for pre-transfusion analysis are regularly sent to the blood bank via established shuttle services. In emergencies, samples are sent by courier upon request. Upon arrival at the blood bank, the samples are recorded and analyzed on automated machines 24/7. The turnaround time for urgent ABO and Rh/Kell red cell typing and irregular antibody screening is less than 45 and 60 min, respectively, not including transport time. These times may be extended if clinically significant red blood cell antibodies are present, requiring additional investigations such as manual techniques or antibody identification. If an irregular antibody is identified, a blood cross-matching test is performed in the laboratory before any RBC transfusion. The turnaround time for this test is less than 60 min. The pre-transfusion test results are sent electronically to transfusion software, which allows the nursing staff to verify compatibility prior to transfusion. A delay in obtaining pre-transfusion test results does not affect the time it takes for RBCs to be available in emergency situations, though it may play a role in non-urgent contexts. The blood bank generally respects the urgency level indicated by the prescribing physician and uses it to guide the choice of RBC group without causing delays. When valid pre-transfusion test data is lacking, a protocol permits the immediate delivery of O Rh-negative RBC for women under 50 years of age and O Rh-positive RBC for other patients.

LBP prescriptions are carried out in a connected manner between the hospital and the blood bank. Prescribing physicians write them using Cursus 2 software, and the blood bank integrates them into its medical-technical software. The software quickly processes electronic deliveries and hands LBPs over to the courier at the desired time. Dedicated hospital couriers transport LBP and blood samples for immunohematological analysis between healthcare departments and the blood bank. Fixed schedules are established for picking up blood samples at various points in the hospital and at the blood bank (approximately every hour and ten minutes during the day). This allows for the management of non-emergency LBP requests. In the event of an emergency LBP prescription, the prescribing department or the blood bank contacts the couriers directly by telephone to collect and deliver the LBP as quickly as possible. Couriers access a list of patients awaiting blood products using Cursus 2 software. They print the list before each visit to the blood bank. They present this list to delivery technicians to collect the LBP. LBP are packed in insulated coolers provided by the couriers. Due to the proximity of the two establishments (less than 500 m), the LBP are transported in an air-conditioned vehicle in a relatively short amount of time. Figure 1 shows the usual blood delivery and blood bank analysis pathways. For ILTE, two Rh-negative and two Rh-positive O RBC units are pre-positioned in the intensive care unit blood depot and can be delivered to the ED in under five minutes.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables are expressed as medians with first and third quartiles (Q1–Q3), and categorical variables are reported as counts and percentages. Comparisons between qualitative variables were performed via the chi-square test, and a Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Comparisons between quantitative variables were performed via the Mann‒Whitney U test. Concordance between the patient and delivered blood groups was assessed for the ABO and Rhesus systems, as well as the ABO + Rhesus combination. Concordance was defined as the proportion of correspondence between the two groups for each case. Analyses were performed on the entire population and on subgroups according to the degree of urgency. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) of the proportions of agreement were calculated. The 95% CI was calculated for PTEH too. All tests were two-sided, and the level of significance was set at 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted via R software version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, no a priori sample size calculation was performed.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, the YANTED was 16.7 (15.6–17.3) per 1,000 ED patients. Notably, 45% of the transfusions occurred during nighttime hours (between 10 PM and 6 AM), although only 26% of the orders were placed during this time frame. RE transfusions were more common during the nighttime than during the daytime.

The YANTED corresponds, for this ED, to 4 RBC units every day. A study in 7 hospitals reported between 1 and 13 RBCs per 1,000 patients admitted to the ED [9]. The decrease of YANTED over time may be linked to the presence of an emergency physician who has been in regular contact with the blood bank and the hemovigilance representative since 2021. This makes it easy for either party to report any problems. Additionally, this emergency physician provides training and education to medical and paramedical teams. In parallel, activities have been developed for day hospital treatment of non-urgent anemia, which will likely result in fewer transfusions in the ED. However, the authors did not consider patients’ illness severity, which may underestimate their findings. The variability in transfusion practices between hospitals suggests a need for standardization and the development of tailored protocols for ED transfusions [17]. These protocols could improve efficiency while maintaining patient safety in diverse clinical contexts [18]. YANTED will permit comparisons between ED transfusion activities. Descriptions of such parameters in other studies will allow us to assess their variability and determine common ED-PBM recommendations. First, we will do this for tertiary care hospitals, and then for other types of hospitals. For PTEH, the results align with the reported range of 3–12% reported in other studies conducted in France [19]. Transfusions are frequent in the ED but constitute a small part of tertiary hospital activity.

Another indicator of ED-PBM could be the proportion of RBC transfusions occurring at night. In the intensive care unit, 48% of RBCs are transfused at night, especially for acute bleeding patients [20]. A retrospective German study revealed that critical incidents occurred in 14% of emergency situations and in 21% of nighttime or weekend situations [21]. Roche et al. reported that 43% of transfusions occurring at night in the ED were NE transfusions [22]. Thus, our results are consistent with previous studies on this topic. The discrepancy between the timing of RBC prescriptions and transfusions suggests that many RBC units ordered in the late evening (between 7 PM and 10 PM) were ultimately administered during the night. The overall increase in ED activity during the late evening hours corresponds to typical patient influx patterns, which may extend the time needed to process and deliver RBC units [8]. Additionally, logistical limitations, such as the reduced frequency of transportation between the hospital and the blood bank after 7 PM, may contribute to delays in transfusion [23].

The delivery times reported in our study were not different between LTE and ILTE (28 vs. 29 min, p = 0.40). In contrast, the transfusion time for ILTE was shorter than that for LTE (15 vs. 33 min, p < 0.001). However, 54% of the ILTE and LTE RBC units were transfused more than 30 min after delivery. The availability of laboratory results largely aligns with French recommendations. It takes 10–60 min to receive a blood count and 20–60 min to receive hemostasis parameter results [24]. However, delays could be further exacerbated by the need for pre-transfusion tests. These tests can extend the time from order to transfusion, particularly when determining blood group compatibility or when clinically significant red cell antibodies are present. Delays may be related to communication issues, courier availability problems, or blood bank or courier understaffing, particularly outside of daytime hours. Accumulated delays warrant attention because they could indirectly affect the overall efficiency and safety of transfusion practices in the ED. Nevertheless, further study should focus on ILTE and LTE transfusions in the ED, given that the time between reception and transfusion was 15 and 33 min, respectively. One possible explanation for this narrow time difference is the inherent complexity of managing multiple urgent cases simultaneously, especially during peak hours [25]. It is possible that there is a prioritization bias. Lin et al. reported that elderly and black-skinned patients were at greater risk of nonpriority than were younger and non-black-skinned patients in the ED [26]. Racial and age bias could also lead to poor practices in terms of RBC ordering. Furthermore, delays in blood sample processing could contribute to the extended times observed for urgent cases. New tools, such as rapid ABO and RhD tests, could permit faster blood group determination at the bedside for ILTE and LTE patients [27]. Although the overall delivery times are compliant with current regulations, implementation of a clinical decision support tool could permit time savings for real ILTE and LTE patients [28]. The ABO and rhesus discrepancy, particularly in ILTE and LTE may be related to the emergency procedures permitting to deliver rapidly group O RBCs.

Our study highlights the complexity of implementing PBM strategies in the ED. The high percentage of patients receiving two units of red blood cells per transfusion may indicate non-restrictive transfusion strategies [29]. More work is needed to understand the requirements for using more than one RBC unit in the ED especially for non-hemorrhagic patients. These findings echo those of previous studies showing regional heterogeneity in transfusion habits and underscore the need for more consistent PBM protocols tailored to the ED setting [30]. The real need for RBC transfusion in the ED should be evaluated, as it is related to improved ED length of stay [31, 32]. Pathways must be developed for nonhospitalized patients [33]. Finally, other studies should focus on other pillars of PBM, such as reducing blood loss and improving anemia tolerance without RBC transfusion.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be discussed. The main limitation is its monocentric nature within a large, level-one trauma center with easy access to intensive care unit and operating rooms. Additionally, this study focused exclusively on RBC transfusions, and we did not evaluate the use of other LBP, such as plasma or platelets, which could also be subject to similar logistical challenges. A multicenter retrospective study is currently underway to explore the broader use of blood products across various university hospitals, which will provide a more comprehensive understanding of transfusion practices and delays in different settings. Moreover, we excluded RBC units prescribed in the ED but transfused in other departments, which limited our ability to assess interdepartmental coordination. The proportion of such cases was relatively small and may not significantly affect our findings. Future studies should explore this aspect further. Finally, we did not evaluate the clinical outcomes related to transfusion timing, which limits our ability to evaluate the impact of delayed transfusions on patients’ health. Future prospective studies should examine this relationship in greater detail, including factors such as the correlation between patient admission times, staffing levels at different times of day, and rates of transfusion-related adverse events during the daytime versus nighttime hours [34, 35]. Understanding these dynamics could help optimize the balance between transfusion safety and efficiency in the ED setting.

Conclusion

Over a 4-year period, the YANTED was 16.7 (15.6–17.3) RBC units per 1,000 ED patients. RE prescriptions accounted for 67% of the transfusions and occurred mostly at night. Further studies should focus on how to decrease these indicators in the ED.
