Introduction

Coronary-cameral fistulas (CCFs) are the anomalous connection between coronary arteries (CAs) and a great vessel or cardiac chamber that can be seen as a congenital or acquired pathologies [1]. Acquired CCF can be seen as a result of cardiac trauma or prior cardiac surgeries [2]. The incidence of CAF is very low, ranging from 0.002 % in the general population to 0.25 % in patients undergoing cardiac catheterization and it can be either symptomatic or asymptomatic [3].

If symptomatic, they usually manifest during childhood [4]. CCFs are often asymptomatic and usually found incidentally during coronary angiography (CAG), occurring in up to 0.2 % of patients undergoing coronary catheterization [5]. Approximately 90 % of CCFs are congenital, originating from persistent sinusoids during embryonic development [6]. They can be associated with other congenital heart diseases such as septal defects, tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), and pulmonary atresia (PA) [6]. Most fistulas arise from the right and left anterior descending CAs, with the circumflex CA being rarely involved [7].

Most fistulas create small shunts, leaving patients asymptomatic. However, large fistulas can cause symptoms like dyspnea, angina, and severe cardiac conditions such as congestive heart failure (CHF), endocarditis, or arrhythmias [1]. The diagnosis of CCFs involves medical history, physical examination, and paraclinical tests. Key physical exam findings may include a collapsing pulse, wide pulse pressure, diffuse apex beat, palpable third heart sound (S3), and a loud continuous murmur peaking in mid to late diastole, best heard at the mid to lower sternal border depending on the fistula's drainage site [8]. Although rare, heart failure due to CCF can present with signs such as elevated jugular venous pressure, S3 gallop, lung base crepitations, hepatomegaly, ascites, and pitting edema of the lower limbs [8,9]. Additional diagnostic tools like chest X-rays, electrocardiograms (ECGs), computed tomography angiographies (CTAs), and CAG are crucial in identifying these anomalies [[10], [11], [12]].

This case report presents an unusual instance of a CCF in a young patient with a structurally normal heart. It emphasizes the importance of considering this diagnosis even in asymptomatic individuals and discusses the current literature to provide a comprehensive understanding of this condition.

Case presentation

History and physical examination

A 20-year-old male was referred to a tertiary heart hospital center due to continuous retrosternal chest pain (CP) radiating to the left shoulder, associated with exertional dyspnea classified as functional class III. The CP was described as a squeezing sensation that subsided after resting in the car during transport to the hospital. The patient reported experiencing similar episodes of CP over the previous six months, which were typically triggered by exertion and relieved by rest. However, he had not sought medical evaluation prior to this presentation. He denied any palpitations, dizziness, syncope, or associated gastrointestinal symptoms.

The patient denied any history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, smoking, or illicit drug use. There was no family history of cardiovascular diseases or sudden cardiac death. He reported no significant stressors, did not engage in regular high-intensity exercise, and his lifestyle was unremarkable. Social and occupational histories were also non-contributory. Upon presentation, the patient's vital signs were stable, including a blood pressure of 120/80 mmHg and oxygen saturation of 98 %. Cardiovascular examination revealed no murmurs or signs of heart failure, with strong and symmetrical peripheral pulses. Respiratory and abdominal examinations were unremarkable, with clear lung fields and no tenderness or masses. The neurological exam showed no deficits. Given his symptoms of CP and dyspnea, the patient was admitted for further diagnostic workup, including advanced imaging and CAG to determine the cause.

Initial investigations were promptly conducted on admission, including serial measurements of cardiac biomarkers such as troponin levels, complete blood count (CBC), renal function tests, electrolytes, and coagulation profiles. All these tests were within normal limits, and troponin levels were unremarkable (Table 1). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was performed as part of the initial evaluation. The TTE revealed a significantly dilated left coronary artery (LCA) with an aneurysmal enlargement measuring approximately 2 cm in diameter. A small orifice connecting the aneurysm to the right ventricle (RV) body was also identified. The RV size and function were within normal limits, and the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated to be 60 %, indicating preserved systolic function. No other structural abnormalities were detected (Fig. 1). To further delineate the anatomy and plan for potential intervention, a cardiac CTA was performed, which confirmed the presence of a dilated LCA and a large aneurysm with a fistulous connection to the RV (Fig. 2). Subsequently, the CAG confirmed the findings of a dilated LCA and a fistulous connection to the RV. The patient was prepared for the procedure under sterile conditions. A femoral artery and vein access were obtained using the Seldinger technique, providing a reliable entry point for both diagnostic and interventional procedures. After successful cannulation, an introducer sheath was placed, and anticoagulation was administered to prevent thromboembolic complications during the procedure. A guiding catheter was advanced to the coronary ostium through the femoral artery under fluoroscopic guidance. The aneurysmal segment of the LCA and the fistulous connection to the RV were carefully navigated using a guidewire. Once the anatomy was thoroughly assessed, the decision was made to proceed with coil embolization to occlude the aneurysm and the fistula.

A total of eight coils were deployed sequentially into the aneurysmal sac to achieve complete occlusion. The coils used were as follows: one 18 × 40 mm, one 20 × 40 mm, two 20 × 50 mm, two 22 × 50 mm, and two 25 × 50 mm coils. Care was taken to ensure proper placement of each coil, confirming cessation of flow within the aneurysm and fistula as verified by post-deployment angiography. The final CAG demonstrated no residual filling of the aneurysm, indicating successful occlusion of the CCF (Fig. 3). Post-procedural follow-up

Post-procedure, the patient was transferred back to the CCU for close monitoring. His clinical condition stabilized, with no immediate postoperative complications were noted. Vital signs, cardiac rhythms, and oxygen saturation were continuously monitored. The femoral access sites were managed using compression devices to achieve hemostasis, and the patient was observed for signs of bleeding, hematoma, or other vascular complications.

The day after the CAG, the patient's symptoms were completely resolved, with no CP or dyspnea complaints. A thorough physical examination revealed normal cardiac, respiratory, and neurological function. A chest X-ray performed post-procedure was normal (Fig. 4). The patient remained stable during his post-procedural admission, with no complications observed.Fig. 4Chest x-ray taken one day after the procedure (Red arrow). After 48 h, the patient was discharged from the hospital in stable condition. Follow-up appointments were scheduled at 1, 3, and 6 months post-procedure. The patient reported no new symptoms during these follow-up visits, and physical examinations were consistently normal. Follow-up imaging confirmed the sustained success of the intervention, with no evidence of recurrence or complications associated with the CCF.

Discussion

CA anomalies involve irregularities in the origin, distribution, or termination of the arteries. CAFs are classified as termination anomalies, where the arteries typically arise from the aorta but terminate abnormally in a vessel or cardiac chamber. [7]. In the present case, a large coronary fistula from the LCA to the RV caused significant shunting, requiring coil embolization to prevent heart failure and other complications.

CCFs are single or multiple connections between one or more CAs to one of four heart chambers, avoiding the myocardial capillary. These connections can be small or large, causing left-to-right or left-to-left shunts in the cardiac circulation [11]. The right coronary artery (RCA) is the most common site of fistula origination (50–60 %), followed by the LAD (25–42 %) and the circumflex (18.3 %). The RV (14–40 %) and right atrium (RA) (19–26 %) are the two most frequent sites of fistula drainage in these patients [14]. Smaller fistulas are often asymptomatic and are incidentally found during unrelated cardiac evaluation. However, larger fistulas can be three times larger than the normal CA and can cause symptoms like angina, dyspnea, heart failure, arrhythmia, and endocarditis [14]. Furthermore, the site of origin and termination of the fistula is another crucial factor in patients' presenting symptoms [15].

Most CCF cases have a congenital etiology; however, acquired etiologies, including traumatic, iatrogenic, accidental, and spontaneous CCF, are also reported [16]. The primary cause of symptoms in CAF is the coronary steal phenomenon, where blood diverts from high-pressure coronary circulation to the fistula, reducing flow to distal myocardial segments and causing symptoms, especially during increased oxygen demand. [14]. More distal shunts cause a more severe steal phenomenon, and ischemic symptoms are more likely to occur [17]. Heart failure symptoms are primarily developed by volume overload due to the shunting of blood from a large fistula, causing pulmonary congestion [1].

CAG is the conventional and gold standard method of diagnosis, allowing precise determination of the fistula's size, course, origin, and termination site, as well as detecting the suitable type of intervention needed for the management [18]. With recent developments in cardiac imaging, cardiac computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and TTE are now available noninvasive techniques for diagnosis [14]. Multidetector CT angiography (MDCT) has recently increased in use due to its non-invasiveness, multi-planar image reconstruction, and high three-dimensional spatial resolution [19]. In a study by Lim et al. [20], coronary CTA detected a higher prevalence of CAF than conventional angiographic reports. ECG may show ischemic changes or ventricular volume overload in some cases, but it is rarely used as the initial method of diagnosis [14].

The goal of the treatment is to block the fistula while maintaining normal coronary blood flow. Treatment indications include developing or increasing a large left to right shunt, left ventricular dysfunction and volume overload, CHF, presence of myocardial ischemia (MI), and also to prevent endocarditis [18]. Asymptomatic patients do not require any treatment in most cases; spontaneous closure may occur, but this rarely happens. Although there is no consensus on the treatment of symptomatic CCF due to its rare prevalence, treatment options are surgery and catheter closure [21].

CCFs can be treated using either surgical closure or percutaneous techniques, such as coil embolization or Amplatzer Vascular Plugs (AVP), depending on the fistula's size and anatomy [22]. Transcatheter closure (TCC) is preferred when CCF is the only heart anomaly, and the communication is single, narrow, and more proximal [23]. Both the surgical and TCC approaches provide an overall good prognosis and carry a low risk of morbidity and mortality [23]. Coil embolization is ideal for smaller fistulas, offering a precise, minimally invasive option with fewer complications compared to surgery [24]. Studies have demonstrated the successful use of Amplatzer devices for closing CCFs, highlighting the importance of advanced imaging and tailored treatment [[25], [26], [27]]. In cases with complex anatomy or large fistulas unsuitable for catheter-based techniques, surgical ligation is effective but carries higher risks, such as bleeding or infection. [28].

Complications may occur, especially with larger fistulas, including incomplete closure, dilation, clip migration, thrombus, and aneurysm formation [29,30]. A higher complication rate accompanies surgical treatment. It is indicated in patients with several cardiac anomalies or with multiple and tortuous fistulas [14,23]. Anesthetic management of CCF includes avoiding increased left-to-right shunting during the procedure and preventing MI. Intraoperative ECG and TEE are helpful modalities for observing the ischemia and myocardial wall motion during surgery [31]. Generally, repairing CAF has an excellent prognosis. Based on previous literature, the fistula's recurrence rate is 10 % [32]. Short-term and long-term follow-ups on patients are essential to check for any residual shunts, recanalization, thrombus formation, and other procedural complications [32].

Upadhyay et al. (2020) reported a 20-year-old male with a CCF from the RCA to the right atrium, which led to infective endocarditis and congestive heart failure. Their patient underwent surgical repair of the fistula, combined with aortic valve replacement due to the presence of aortic valve endocarditis. The surgery was successful, and the patient recovered well. Our case also involves a young male, but with an LCA fistula to the RV. Both cases required intervention due to fistula size and symptoms, but ours was managed with transcatheter coil embolization, avoiding open-heart surgery. This highlights the use of minimally invasive approaches for larger fistulas when suitable.

Chirumamilla et al. (2024) described a 56-year-old woman with a large CCF draining into the left ventricle, which contributed to non-ischemic cardiomyopathy and heart failure. Despite initial signs of ischemia, further investigation revealed the fistula as the underlying cause. The patient was successfully managed with medical therapy and scheduled for elective closure, highlighting the importance of recognizing coronary fistulas as contributors to heart failure in such cases [33]. Similarly, Trang et al. (2023) reported a 35-year-old woman with a giant coronary artery fistula from the RCA, who presented with symptoms of dyspnea and CP. This case was managed using percutaneous transcatheter closure with Amplatzer Vascular Plug II and LifeTech Konar M VSD Occluder, demonstrating the efficacy of transcatheter closure in large symptomatic fistulas with favorable anatomy [34].

In the other case reported by Qarajeh et al. (2020), a 40-year-old woman presented with acute heart failure due to a CCF originating from the left circumflex artery. After multiple imaging modalities, including CAG and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, confirmed the diagnosis, surgical ligation of the fistula was performed. This intervention led to significant improvement in her heart failure symptoms, with her ejection fraction increasing from 42 % to 65 % post-surgery [35].

Clinical learning point (conclusion)

CCFs represent an uncommon cardiac anomaly characterized by diverse anatomical variations and clinical manifestations. CAG remains the most accurate diagnostic test for determining fistula anatomy and identifying potential therapeutic options. Regardless of symptoms, small symptomatic and large fistulas require intervention through either a transcatheter approach or surgical repair. In this case, we presented a rare instance of a left CAF to the RV, successfully closed using a transcatheter approach with coil embolization.

Patient perspective

The patient expressed relief after the procedure, noting that his symptoms of chest pain and shortness of breath were completely resolved. He appreciated the clear communication from the medical team and was grateful for the minimally invasive approach, which allowed for a quick recovery and return to normal activities. Overall, the patient was satisfied with the care and follow-up provided.
