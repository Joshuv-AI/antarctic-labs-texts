Grain boundary segregation and solute drag in multicomponent alloys
Authors: Milad Taghizadeh, Malek Alkayyali, Fadi Abdeljawad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Grain boundary (GB) solute segregation has been proposed as a strategy to tailor the processing pathways and properties of a wide range of polycrystalline materials. Of particular interest is GB solute drag, which results when segregated solutes exert a resistive force hindering GB migration. However, we do not have a mechanistic understanding of the migration kinetics of doped GBs in multicomponent alloys, as most treatments only consider binary systems. In this work, we model GB solute drag in multicomponent alloys through theoretical and computational studies. We show that solute-solute interactions of various alloying element types result in varied GB segregation behaviors, such as synergistic cosegregation and induced desegregation, which greatly influence the characteristics of GB solute drag. Simulation results reveal that in multicomponent alloys GB desegregation can be as effective in inducing boundary solute drag. More broadly, predictions from our modeling framework motivate future research into the migration kinetics of doped interfaces, with the potential to guide the design of advanced alloys with tailored chemistries and properties.

Introduction

Most structural and functional materials are polycrystals, they are composed of crystalline grains that are joined at internal interfaces, termed grain boundaries (GBs). Many of the engineering properties of polycrystalline metals, such as mechanical strength1, corrosion2 and wear3 resistance, and embrittlement4 can be controlled by tailoring the grain size and GB types present in these materials. In addition to microstructure control, alloying offers another strategy by which the properties of polycrystalline metals can be manipulated. Indeed, recent interest in metal physics has been geared towards multicomponent alloys with far-from-dilute concentrations, so-called high entropy alloys5.

In metallic alloys, atoms within the GB region are at higher energy states than those in the perfect crystalline lattice, and, as a result, GBs provide preferential segregation sites for alloying elements. In this context, GB segregation is not viewed as an undesired effect, but rather a strategy that allows for the direct manipulation of materials properties and functionalities6. GB solute segregation has been the subject of active research with studies focused on the dependence of GB energy on boundary concentration7, segregation anisotropy due to the boundary’s geometry8–11, and segregation-induced GB transformations12–16. Recent analytical thermodynamic treatments investigated the impact of multi-element segregation on GB energy17. Experimental studies employing atom probe tomography18–20 and microscopy techniques11,21–24 have been used to reveal and quantify GB chemistry in a wide range of alloys.

The chemistry of GBs dictates boundary motion and, in turn, the evolution of microstructure. For example, solute segregation to GBs has been shown to suppress grain coarsening and enhance the stability of polycrystalline alloys11,24–26; however, in other systems it has been reported to promote abnormal grain growth27,28. In solid-state activated sintering of particle compacts, GB segregation has been used to modulate densification and coarsening rates29,30. Recent experimental studies on multicomponent alloys revealed sluggish GB migration and grain coarsening rates, which were attributed to GB solute drag33–36. Such observations, however, present several conundrums. First, these studies rely on classical solute drag models to explain the observed sluggish GB migration rates34. Most solute drag treatments consider binary alloys31,32,37. For example, Cahn’s treatment31 assumes ideal and dilute alloys in which GB-solute interactions are characterized by a constant segregation energy. A key result of Cahn’s model is that solute drag scales linearly with the nominal bulk concentration31. Existing studies on GB solute drag in multi-component alloys38–40 extended Cahn’s treatment to ideal multicomponent alloys, and, as a result, these investigations do not account for solute-solute interactions of various alloying element types. For example, Mendelev and Srolovitz38 and Bréchet and Purdy39 examined solute drag in ideal ternary alloys where they assumed constant segregation energy for each solute type. In such models38–40, not accounting for solute-solute interactions in the bulk and GB results in decoupled governing equations, in which the total GB solute drag is the sum of solute drag of the constituent binary alloys. Additionally, these studies38–40, similar to Cahn’s model, predict a solute drag that increases linearly with bulk concentration. There are no GB solute drag models that consider multicomponent alloying effects with solute-solute interactions. Second, it is not clear whether it is the segregation of one type of solute to GBs or the segregation of multiple elemental species that is responsible for the observed sluggish GB migration rates. Third, these experimental studies do not consider the possibility of more complex GB-solute interactions that could give rise to solute drag. Indeed, the interaction of multiple alloying element types with GBs introduces varied segregation behaviors, including competitive segregation, synergistic co-segregation, and induced desegregation, whose impact on GB migration remains unexplored. For example, a study by Li et al.18 on the multicomponent FeMnNiCoCr alloy revealed cosegregation of Ni and Mn and desegregation of Fe and Co. A critical need, therefore, exists for a fundamental and quantitative understanding of GB segregation and boundary solute drag in multicomponent alloys, and this is precisely the goal of this study.

In this work, we present a theoretical and computational mesoscale model to elucidate GB solute drag in multicomponent alloys. While atomistic simulations have provided critical insights into GB phenomena, such as boundary energy41 and mobility42, atomic structure and site-specific segregation behavior43,44, and solute transport coefficients45,46, the direct atomistic simulations of concurrent GB segregation and dynamic solute drag remain computationally prohibitive. The pronounced disparity between the time and length scales of atomistic simulations and those governing GB dynamics and chemical re-distribution motivates and highlights the importance of the mesoscale modeling framework developed in this work. Our proposed framework accounts for solute-solute interactions of various alloying element types in the bulk and within the GB, captures mass transport processes of various alloying elements, and provides the means to quantify the impact of complex GB concentration profiles on solute drag. Our simulation results reveal a multitude of solute drag behaviors that are sensitively dependent on the interactions of various solute types and operative mass transport processes. Furthermore, we show that cases with GB solute desegregation can be as effective in inducing GB solute drag effects.

The foregoing exposition underscores several key advantages of the proposed model. First, the model requires as input physical parameters that can be directly obtained from atomistic simulations. For example, atomistic methods have been employed to calculate GB diffusivity45, bulk Onsager transport coefficients46, GB chemical free energies via thermodynamic integration62, and bulk heat of mixing derived from alloy phase diagrams63–65. Second, our model enables the direct simulation of coupled diffusive processes governing GB solute drag, thereby establishing a quantitative link between atomic-scale mechanisms and experimentally observed GB phenomena. Here, we note that the GB geometry is described by five macroscopic degrees of freedom66, three for misorientation and two for plane normal, and that each GB geometry has its unique thermodynamic and kinetic properties. We also note that establishing GB structure-property linkages relating the GB’s thermodynamic and transport properties to its geometric degrees of freedom is one of the most active areas of research in GB physics. While this work does not consider anisotropic boundary segregation, it is straightforward to include anisotropy effects in our modeling treatment by allowing the model parameters describing the GB segregation energy and diffusion coefficients to be functions of the boundary geometry. In addition to the dependence of GB segregation on boundary geometry9,10,67, several studies revealed site-specific GB segregation, in which the segregation energy depends on the local atomic environment within the GB68–70. This results in distributions for the GB segregation energy in terms of the atomic environment, even for the same GB geometry43,71,72. Accounting for site-specific segregation for a given GB can be achieved in our treatment by allowing our model parameters to be distributions rather than single-valued quantities.

Model assumptions

Our proposed modeling treatment considers multi-element segregation and migration of planar GBs using a one-dimensional bicrystal geometry. As a result, the relevant transport coefficients are solute diffusion across the GB. A future extension of our model to 2D/3D geometries would enable the incorporation of a tensorial description of GB diffusion in which both diffusion along and across the GB are incorporated. Indeed, atomistic simulations of Cu-Ag binary alloys, for example, showed that accelerated lateral GB diffusion leads to nonuniform redistribution of solute atoms, which in turn influences GB solute drag73. Such a 2D/3D extension of our model will capture the dynamics of GB networks, thereby allowing us to investigate the interplay between GB segregation and phenomena such as abnormal grain growth. Our model treats GBs as regions with thermodynamic properties that are distinct from the bulk, resulting in a segregation energy that is single-valued. It is interesting to extend our treatment to account for site-specific GB segregation, in which the segregation energy is given by a distribution due to the local atomic environment of the GB. This can be incorporated into our treatment by allowing the model parameters that describe the GB segregation energy to be distributions rather than single-valued quantities. Our work considers solid solution alloys in order to deconvolute solute drag effects due to multi-element GB segregation from GB pinning due to second-phase precipitates. It would be interesting to investigate in future work the interplay between GB segregation and phase separation and their net impact on GB migration kinetics. Finally, another extension of our modeling framework is to explicitly consider vacancies and their interactions with GBs, as several studies revealed vacancy generation and emission at migrating boundaries51,52. This can be achieved by treating GBs as sources/sinks of such point defects with prescribed interaction energies.

Model parametrization and validation

The proposed modeling framework, together with the simulations it produces, provides several pathways for cross-validation with established databases, experimental observations, and atomistic simulations. First, our modeling treatment explicitly accounts for both bulk and GB free energies, parametrized using regular solution thermodynamics. The bulk regular solution model parameters can be obtained from existing thermodynamic databases82. For example, for the PtAuPd50, FeNiZr83, and CuAlY84 ternary alloys, in which experimental observations revealed GB segregation, Table 1 lists reported values for the bulk regular solution model parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\bar{\Omega }}_{ij}^{b}$$\end{document}Ω¯ijb of the constituent binary pairs50,85. On the other hand, GB-solute interactions for each solute type can be computed using atomistic simulations68,86. For example, for the Cu-Ag alloy atomistic simulations employing thermodynamic integration in conjunction with the Gibbs adsorption equation were used to calculate the GB free energy as a function of Ag concentration62. These atomistically-obtained GB free energies can then be fit to regular solution free energy expressions to obtain the GB heat of mixing model parameters. A second avenue for validating our modeling results is comparison with experimental GB segregation data. Here, it is important to note that direct, in situ characterization of concurrent GB migration, solute segregation, and solute drag is constrained by limited spatial and temporal resolution and by the difficulty of isolating individual boundaries in polycrystalline metallic alloys. Nevertheless, recent advances in microscopy have enabled detailed quantification of chemical profiles across static GBs, providing a direct means to validate predictions from our model. To demonstrate this cross-validation strategy, we consider the PtAuPd ternary alloy in which recent experiments revealed GB segregation50. We simulate GB segregation in Pt0.76Au0.18Pd0.06 (in at.%) using reported bulk heat of mixing model parameters50 shown in Table 1 while assuming ideal GBs, and the results are shown in Fig. 8. Our modeling results depicting concentration profiles across a stationary GB are shown in Fig. 8a, where segregation of Au is observed accompanied by depletion of Pt while the Pd concentration profile does not show any significant enrichment at the GB. Xing et al.50 experimentally investigated GB segregation in the PtAuPd alloy with various compositions and temperatures. For a Pt0.76Au0.18Pd0.06 alloy annealed at 800 ∘C for 24 h, Fig. 8b shows chemical line scans across a GB using energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS), revealing Au segregation to the Pt GB with no tendency for Pd segregation. In summary, we presented a theoretical and computational model of GB segregation and solute drag in multicomponent alloys that accounts for both GB and bulk solute-solute interactions of various alloying elements. Ternary alloys were employed as they represent a model system with multicomponent effects while offering computational tractability. Analytical analysis recovered a multicomponent segregation isotherm, describing the GB solute content as a function of the thermodynamic properties of the alloy. Simulation results revealed that solute interactions of various elemental pairs resulting in segregation behaviors, such as synergistic cosegregation and induced desegregation, greatly influence the characteristics of GB solute drag-velocity profiles. In addition to the segregation energy of solutes, our work highlights the need to account for the transport coefficients of various alloying elements in order to have a quantitative understanding of GB solute drag in multicomponent systems. Computational studies demonstrated that GB solute drag can exhibit a non-monotonic dependence on bulk concentration, in contrast to existing solute drag models, which consider only binary alloys. Furthermore, it was shown that in multicomponent alloys GB desegregation can be as effective in inducing GB solute drag, thereby expanding the alloy design space in applications where controlling GB dynamical processes is a key microstructural goal. While GB segregation has been extensively studied, prior work has largely been confined to the chemistry of static boundaries. Predictions from our modeling framework motivate future research by shifting the focus to GB dynamics, coupled evolution of boundary segregation and solute drag, as a strategy for designing advanced alloys with tailored microstructures.
