Collateral pathways arising from internal jugular venous occlusion in Lemierre's syndrome
Authors: Kazuhiro Ishikawa, Nobuyoshi Mori
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Clinical MessageThe use of anticoagulants in Lemierre's syndrome is not well‐defined, lacking clear evidence for their efficacy. This report describes a patient with complete occlusion of the internal jugular vein by a thrombus who did not develop expected complications due to the formation of collateral venous channels.Keywords: collateral pathway of external jugular vein, Lemierre's syndrome, occlusion of internal jugular vein, septic pulmonary embolism

CASE PRESENTATION

Lemierre's syndrome arises from oropharyngeal infections, typically due to oral anaerobes like Fusobacterium nucleatum, leading to complications such as septic thrombophlebitis. 1 , 2

An 85‐year‐old man with a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and oral cavity was transferred from another hospital to our hospital due to Lemierre's syndrome, which included a peritonsillar abscess, pyogenic thrombophlebitis, and septic pulmonary embolism (Figure 1A–C).

(A) Contrast‐enhanced neck CT scan on admission revealed a left peritonsillar abscess (arrow). (B) The cervical abscess communicated with the IJV with thrombosis. (C) Chest CT revealed septic pulmonary embolism (arrow). (D) Echography revealed a diminished vascular flow due to the occlusion of IJV resulting from thrombosis. CT, computed tomography; ECA, external carotid artery; ICA, internal carotid artery; IJV, internal jugular vein.

He underwent emergent cervical abscess drainage performed by an otorhinolaryngologist, receiving intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g every 24 h, metronidazole 500 mg every 8 h, and oral rivaroxaban 15 mg twice a day to cover oral pathogens. Cultures of the blood and abscess identified Parvimonas micra, Prevotella sp., Streptococcus anginosus, and Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron by matrix‐assisted laser desorption ionization. All pathogens were susceptible to our antimicrobial therapy. Because a brain surgeon recommended discontinuing rivaroxaban due to the risk of rupture from an fistula between the internal jugular vein and abscess (Figure 1D), we discontinued rivaroxaban. Echocardiography did not reveal infective endocarditis, and his symptoms were clinically improving. Therefore, upon discharge, he was prescribed amoxicillin/clavulanate 500 mg three times a day (susceptible to the detected pathogen), and antibiotics were ceased after the abscess and septic pulmonary embolism diminished at the 4‐month mark. Subsequent vascular echography revealed sustained occlusion of the internal jugular vein; however, collateral circulation through the external jugular vein prevented facial swelling (Figure 2).

(A) The yellow arrow indicates the operation scar around the IJV, while the red arrow indicates the distenction of the EJV. He did not develop the facial swelling. (B) The size of the left EJV (red arrow) is larger than that of the that of the right. EJV, external jugular vein; IJV, internal jugular vein.

DISCUSSION

Lemierre's syndrome is a recognized cause of internal jugular vein thrombosis. The impact of anticoagulation on halting thrombus progression or preventing septic emboli stemming from internal jugular vein thrombosis remains ambiguous, with insights primarily derived from anecdotal case reports. It is rare for complete occlusion of the internal jugular vein and the presence of an asymptomatic collateral pathway through the external jugular vein without anticoagulant treatment.
