Habitat-based imaging and peritumoral radiomics on ultrasound images for predicting lymphovascular invasion in breast invasive ductal carcinoma: a two-center study
Authors: Taixia Liu, Guojia Zhao, Wei Wei, Qingling Zhang, Jing Wu, Xuying Chen, Dong Liu, Xiangming Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of employing habitat-based radiomic distributions in ultrasound (US) images to quantitatively characterize intratumoral heterogeneity. It also explored the potential of this approach to predict lymphovascular invasion (LVI) in breast invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) patients and to identify the optimal extent of multiple peritumoral regions.MethodsA total of 408 women diagnosed with IDC from January 2020 to October 2023 were enrolled in this retrospective cohort study from two medical centers. Intratumoral areas were partitioned into four distinct habitat areas using K-means cluster analysis, while peritumoral regions were expanded at increments of 2, 4, and 6 mm. Radiomic features were independently extracted from the intra- and peri-tumoral areas, and habitat subregions for developing predictive models. These models incorporated three machine learning classifiers: Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Support Vector Machine (SVM), respectively. We subsequently established an integrated model encompassing intra- and peri-tumoral areas, and habitat radiomic features, and clinicopathological factors. The model performance was assessed through receiver operating characteristic (ROC), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Finally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and nonogram were applied to enhance model interpretability.ResultsThe Random Forest model exhibited superior performance in terms of the area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.861 (95% CI: 0.809–0.912), 0.832 (95% CI: 0.746–0.919), and 0.810 (95% CI: 0.684–0.935) for the training, validation, and test sets, separately. Additionally, the peri-2 mm model surpassed the performance of the other models (peri-4 mm, peri-6 mm) in LVI prediction. The integrated model, encompassing peri-2 mm features, clinicopathological factors, and habitat models, achieved robust predictive performance with AUC values of 0.940 (95% CI: 0.907–0.973), 0.924 (95% CI: 0.875–0.973), and 0.852 (95% CI: 0.732–0.972) for each respective set.ConclusionThe integrated model yields the improved predictive performance in predicting LVI status, and the model offer a reliable and feasible preoperative prediction method to enhance the clinical management and therapeutic planning for IDC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40644-026-01088-8.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is a highly heterogeneous disease and remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1, 2]. Its majority mortalities are primarily attributed to the uncontrolled proliferation and distant metastases [3]. A critical step in BC dissemination is LVI, pathologically defined as tumor cell infiltration into lymphatic and vascular channels in the peritumoral area [4, 5]. LVI is considered a critical early event in the invasion-metastasis cascade and shows a notable correlation with an elevated risk of lymphedema, distant metastasis, and locoregional recurrence in patients with IDC [6, 7]. Additionally, LVI serves a potential biomarker for early axillary lymph node metastasis (ALNM) and resistance to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [8–10]. Therefore, the preoperative LVI prediction in IDC is critical for determining optimal surgical decision, determining the necessity of additional therapy, and enhancing personal postoperative management [11–13].

Currently, LVI is confirmed through percutaneous biopsy or postoperative examination of surgical specimens, both of which offer limited preoperative guidance [14]. Besides, BC heterogeneity contributed to discordance of 10–40% rates between percutaneous biopsy findings and final surgical pathology [15]. Additionally, biopsies are often invasive and inadequate for capturing peritumoral features, further challenging the accurate assessment of LVI status. All these limitations together make it urgent to establish a robust and non-invasive preoperative method to predict LVI precisely and refine the therapy decision-making for IDC patients.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) has been established as robust platform for BC evaluation and effective IDC phenotyping, including peritumoral edema and adjacent vascular signs, which aid in predicting LVI status [16–19]. Despite its benefits, MRI is limited by its time-intensive nature, high costs, and patient-related constraints, thus making it inadequate as the primary screening modality in current clinical practice. In contrast, US imaging offers advantages over MRI, including real-time imaging capabilities, suitability for serial examinations, greater accessibility and more affordability, and the absence of patient-specific contraindications. Despite these advantages, US imaging remains lacking functional characterization, confers morphological overlap and variability in inter- and intra-observer interpretations [20, 21]. These drawbacks underscores the critical need for a novel predictive tool that can overcome current limitations and enhance the accuracy of predicting LVI presence from preoperative US images in IDC patients.

Radiomics is a sophisticated technique that extracts high-dimensional features from medical images, facilitating the extraction and objective analysis of numerous characteristics that typically remain imperceptible to the human eye of radiologists [22]. Previous research has emphasized the potential of radiomic analysis in preoperative prediction of LVI in IDC patients [23–25]. Despite these advancements, conventional radiomic approaches often analyze tumors as a whole, neglecting regional heterogeneity and the potentially missing clinically relevant data. Habitat imaging, a more defined image analysis method, tackles these limitations by segregating the entire lesion into distinct subregions that are either heterogeneous or homogeneous. These subregions underlying the histopathological and molecular differences, facilitating for more detailed visualization and quantification of intratumoral heterogeneity [26]. Given that the tumor microenvironment notably affects tumorigenesis and progression, it is a precious source of information for assessing LVI status. However, the link of the features derived from habitat imaging of IDC and LVI status remains poorly characterized. Thus, it is imperative to develop an objective and robust method to accurately assess the LVI.

In the present study, we carried out LVI prediction preoperatively in IDC patients using habitat analysis of intratumoral and the peritumoral regions radiomics, in combination with clinicopathological features. By analyzing habitat-based spatial distributions, we aimed at identifying precise imaging biomarkers that reflect tumor heterogeneity. We also employed the SHAP method, which holds promise as an objective, interpretable, and clinically accessible tool for assessing LVI risk in IDC.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This retrospective study was performed in line with the ethical principles established by the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) for research involving human participants. The hospital’s Ethics Committee(Ethics Approval Number: NA-2025-02-20) approved the study and informed consent was not required. This research was conducted on 408 patients diagnosed with IDC from two hospitals, namely Linyi People’s Hospital (Center A) and the First Affiliated Hospital of Wannan Medical University (Center B), between January 2020 and October 2023. Patients from Hospital A were randomly divided into a training set (n = 245) and a validation set(VS) (n = 105) at a ratio of 7:3, while patients from Hospital B (n = 58) were used as an independent test set (TS).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria:

(I) Pathological confirmation of IDC following surgery; (II) clearly determined LVI status via histopathology; and (III) US examination conducted within two weeks prior to surgery.

The exclusion criteria:

(I) Absence of complete clinicopathological data including immunohistochemistry and pathology results; (II) prior treatment with neoadjuvant therapy, chemotherapy, or radiofrequency ablation; (III) poor- quality US image. Datasets

The dataset encompasses clinicopathological data, including patient age, tumor size (the largest US-reported cross-section of the lesion), histological grade, immunohistochemical profiles, and axillary lymph node (ALN) status. The biomarkers evaluated included estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), Ki-67, and growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-was assessed through immunohistochemistry (IHC). The fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was utilized when HER2 results were equivocal. Molecular subtypes were classified per the ASCO/CAP guidelines by experienced pathologists blinded to US data. LVI status was confirmed by using IHC staining utilized hematoxylin and eosin (HE), CD34 for vascular structures, and D2-40 for lymphatic vessels. The presence of either type of invasion was noted as LVI+(18). ALN metastasis status was obtained through sentinel lymph node biopsy or fine-needle aspiration prior to therapy. Pathological analysis serves as the definitive diagnostic standard.

US image acquisition

US images were captured using a range of equipment: the LOGIQ E9 (GE, USA), Esaote Mylab Twice (Esaote, Genova, Italy), Siemens S2000 (Siemens, Concord, CA, USA), and Philips EPIQ5 (PA, USA), each coupled with a linear array transducer. For optimal image quality, patients were positioned in either a supine or lateral stance to fully expose the upper chest area. The focal zone was carefully aligned with the lesion’s center, and images were acquired and archived within the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). Image acquisition was conducted by a licensed sonographer (H.H.L.) possessing > 5 years of experience in breast imaging and diagnostic interpretation.

Intratumoral ROI segmentation

Manual segmentation of intratumoral regions of interest (IT-ROIs) was performed on the largest cross-sectional area of lesions using ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0). Two radiologists (Radiologist A and Radiologist B) have 8 and 14 years of BC imaging experience, respectively, performed segmentation independently to eliminate the bias from pathological outcomes. Initially, Radiologist A segmented the regions of interest(ROIs)for data from Center A. Following a two-week interval, both radiologists independently segmented the ROIs in a randomly selected subset of 50 patients. Feature reproducibility was assessed using ICC, with an agreement threshold set at ≥ 0.75. These manual segmentation outputs were utilized as the ground truth for developing an automated segmentation model.

In our study, three widely used segmentation algorithms, including DeepLabv3, Unet, and Unet++, were evaluated for their performance. To improve segmentation accuracy, a post-fusion strategy was implemented to integrate the results from the individual models. The model demonstrated a robust capability for accurately identifying and segmenting the ROI, with a Dice coefficient of 0.889 for the training set, 0.893 for the validation set, and 0.855 for the test set. Some results from this analysis were initially reported in our previous study [27].

Peritumoral region dilation

Following the generation of intra-tumoral regions of interest (IT-ROIs), peritumoral ROIs (PT-ROIs) were constructed with widths 2, 4, and 6 mm, respectively, by expanding the IT-ROIs using Python (version 3.8). When these PT-ROIs exceeded the limits of the breast parenchyma, the extraneous regions were manually trimmed. Figure 3b depicts the stepwise expansion of these peritumoral regions.

Habitat generation

A 5 × 5 neighborhood window was employed to extract local spatial features from the ROI for subregion analysis, thereby facilitating the segmentation of subregions in habitat imaging. The mean squared error (MSE) value was used to precisely measure the heterogeneity of these tumor subregions. The application of K-means clustering enabled efficient segmentation of intra-tumoral subregions and facilitated the computation of MSE values. In this study, clustering was evaluated using 2 to 10 clusters, with the optimal number being determined by maximizing the Calinski-Harabasz (CH) index, which reflects clustering compactness. Detailed methodologies are provided in Supplementary E1.

Radiomics feature extraction

Radiomics features were derived from both IT-ROIs and PT-ROIs, as well as from distinct habitat subregions. Three groups were used to categorize these features: intensity-based, geometric, and texture features. Intensity-based features captured grayscale distributions, geometric features described tumor morphology, and texture features computed via GLCM, GLRLM, GLSZM, and NGTDM, characterized spatial heterogeneity, respectively. For partially segmented subregions, the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) method was applied to estimate missing values, ensuring radiomic feature stability. Feature extraction adhered to the guidelines of the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) as implemented in PyRadiomics version 3.0.1. All features underwent Z-score normalization. T-test was employed to evaluate the discriminative power of each feature, with a p-value ≥ 0.05 were excluded. Subsequently, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of ≥ 0.9 were removed to avoid multicollinearity. Dimensionality was further reduced using the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was performed, with the regularization parameter tuned using 10-fold cross-validation.

Machine learning model development

Three widely used machine learning classifiers were applied for construction of the model, including Random Forest, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Support Vector Machine (SVM), respectively. A five-fold cross-validation was conducted on the training set. Further evaluation was performed using the configuration that exhibited the highest median performance across the validation folds. The detailed hyperparameter configurations for each machine learning model used in our study on breast cancer, as follows:

Support Vector Machine (SVM): We employed a radial basis function (RBF) kernel with the probability estimation enabled (probability=True), a maximum number of iterations of 900 (max_iter = 300 × 3), and a regularization parameter C set to 0.8 (C = 4/5).

Random Forest (RF): The classifier was configured with 4 decision trees (n_estimators = 4), a maximum tree depth of 4 (max_depth = 4), a minimum of 2 samples required to split an internal node (min_samples_split = 2), and a fixed random seed (random_state = 0) to ensure reproducibility.

XGBoost (XGB): The model was built with 4 boosting rounds (n_estimators = 4), the binary logistic objective (objective=’binary: logistic’), a maximum tree depth of 4 (max_depth = 4), a minimum child weight of 2 (min_child_weight = 2), and the evaluation metric set to classification error (eval_metric=’error’). We also set use_label_encoder=False to avoid deprecation warnings in the current version.

Construction of the predictive model

Predictive models were developed individually for each region, incorporating habitat imaging models, conventional radiomics models, and peritumoral models with radial expansions of 2 mm, 4 mm, and 6 mm (Peri-2 mm, Peri-4 mm, and Peri-6 mm). The clinicopathological features were characterized to compare patients with LVI (-) and LVI (+). Initial development of clinical models aimed identifying significant variables through univariate analysis, followed by multivariate logistic regression for feature selection and model construction. Finally, an integrated model was formulated by integrating the habitat models, clinicopathological factors, and the most informative peritumoral features.

Model evaluation and explanation

The model’s predictive performance was assessed through the use of the ROC curve, with comparative analysis facilitated via the Delong test. The potential clinical utiility of the integrated model was evaluated utilizing decision curve analysis (DCA), while the calibration curve was employed to evaluate the precision and dependability of the predictions.

To improve model interpretability, we employed SHAP analysis to quantify each feature’s contribution to the predictive outcomes. The resulting visualizations offered an in-depth insight into the influence of each specific features on prediction outcomes. Additionally, A nomogram was constructed to provide an intuitive graphical representation of the integrated model’s predictions.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses and modeling were performed using Python 3.7.12 with Statsmodels (version 0.13.2). The implementation of machine learning models was performed using Scikit-learn (version 1.0.2). The development of deep learning components was carried out using (version 1.11.0) with CUDA (version 11.3.1) and cuDNN (version 8.2.1) acceleration. K-means clustering was implemented using the sklearn.cluster.KMeans module from the scikit-learn library (version 1.0.2). SHAP analysis was performed using the shap library (version 0.4.2) for model interpretability.

The normality of clinical variables was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, whereas Independent t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests were utilized for the comparison of continuous variables, with statistical significance at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients

In the current study, we enrolled a total of 408 patients diagnosed with IDC. Of these, 350 cases were recruited from Center A, with ages ranging from 25 to 75, averaging 51.79 ± 10.07 years, while other 58 cases were enrolled from Center B, with ages ranging from 35 to 77, averaging 52.24 ± 9.72 years. The clinicopathological baseline data were systematically presented in Table 1. Comparative analyses among the training set, VS, and TS found no significant variations (P > 0.05) statistically, indicating an equitable distribution of data across groups without evidence of allocation bias. Baseline differences between the LVI (−) and LVI (+) groups were evaluated using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. (Appendix 1), the odds ratio (OR) and corresponding p-value were calculated for each variable. The univariate analysis identified significant disparities in tumor size and ALNM between the two patient groups (P < 0.05). Furthermore, multivariate analysis confirmed tumor size (OR = 1.009) and ALNM (OR = 1.499) as independent predictors of LVI (+). All variables were numerically encoded and leveraged for establishing machine learning models. The various clinical models performance was detailed in Appendix 2. Among the evaluated models, XGBoost demonstrated the best performed, attaining an ROC curve area of 0.638 (95% CI: 0.481–0.796) in TS and 0.744 (95% CI: 0.619–0.869) in VS.

To characterize intratumoral heterogeneity, 3,332 radiomic features were extracted and categorized into shape (n = 56), first‑order (n = 648), and texture (n = 2,628) features. Supplementary Fig. 1B display the distribution and relative proportions of these feature categories across the dataset by means of pie charts, bar charts, and violin plots.

Prior to analysis, all features were standardized to ensure comparability. A univariate t‑test was first performed to preselect significant features (p ≤ 0.05). Next, Pearson correlation analysis was applied to remove redundant features with pairwise correlation coefficients > 0.9, retaining only the most representative feature from each correlated cluster. Finally, Lasso regression with L1 regularization, with the penalty parameter tuned via 10‑fold cross‑validation, was employed to determine the optimal predictive subset, which yielded six features with non‑zero coefficients for the habitat prediction model, as shown in Fig. 5. In addition, within the intratumoral region and the peritumoral regions at 2 mm, 4 mm, and 6 mm, 9, 6, 9, and 4 features were retained, respectively, as detailed in Appendices 3–7. Model comparison

Of habitat-based models, the Random Forest algorithm achieved the highest AUC on the TS, reaching 0.810 (95% CI: 0.684–0.935), outperforming both the SVM AUC values of 0.661 (95% CI: 0.508–0.813) and XGBoost models, 0.663 (95% CI: 0.510–0.816), respectively. In the TS and VS, the Random Forest model performed superior prediction as well, with AUC values of 0.861 (95% CI: 0.809–0.912) and 0.832 (95% CI: 0.746–0.919) accordingly. For the peri-tumoral models, the SVM model applied to the Peri-2 mm region performed the best, achieving AUC values of 0.641 (95% CI: 0.514–0.767) in VS and 0.631 (95% CI: 0.479–0.783) in TS. Discussion

Previous studies in radiomics have rigorously confirmed the effectiveness of traditional approaches in predicting LVI status in IDC [28, 29, 17]. However, most of these studies analyzed the tumor as a whole, inherently assuming a uniform intratumoral heterogeneity and consequently overlooking regional phenotypic variations. Moreover, a comprehensive examination of the correlation between heterogeneity in BC and LVI status remains scant. To overcome these shortcomings, we utilized habitat imaging analysis-an advanced imaging processing method that stratifies the entire lesion into subregions, based on histopathological and molecular differences of homogeneity or heterogeneity -for a non-invasive assessment of LVI in IDC patients. Our aimed to elucidate the association between habitat imaging-derived heterogeneous radiomic patterns and LVI status in IDC patients. Significantly, We developed and validated an integrated predictive model, visualized as a nomogram. This nomogram integrates intratumoral habitat imaging features, Peri-2 mm region radiomic features, the tumor size, and the ALNM demonstrated robust predictive performance, potentially enhancing clinical decision-making. In the realm of preoperative prediction, the integrated model achieved AUCs of 0.924 in the VS and 0.852 in the TS, achieving better results than models that rely only on clinical features or radiomics from the intra- and peri-tumoral area.

LVI is considered a critical early event in the invasion-metastasis cascade in BC [6]. While the preoperative biopsies provide insights into the histological subtypes and immunohistochemical markers of BC, their limited sampling often fail to confirm the presence of LVI [30, 31]. Traditional medical imaging features, such as interstitial edema, blurring of subcutaneous fat, and skin thickening in digital mammography, along with prepectoral edema and the adjacent vessel sign on MRI, assist in gathering information about LVI status [32–34]. However, these insights rely predominantly on morphological assessments and the radiologist’s expertise. Radiomics, by transforming imaging data into high-dimensional quantitative features, provides observer-independent insights into tumor heterogeneity without artificial bias. In addition to offer comprehensive imaging information, habitat imaging extends its capability to provide valuable insights into the tumor microenvironment by analyzing subregional features and characterizing tumor heterogeneity [35, 36].

Multiple earlier studies has validated the capability of radiomics features in LVI prediction in BC patients. For instance, Wang et al. [37] reported an AUC of 0.835 (95% CI: 0.712–0.958) on VS, using a model based on digital breast tomosynthesis. Similarly, Zhang et al. [29] recorded an AUC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.86–0.94) on their TS by incorporating clinical factors with tissue features derived from tumors using grayscale ultrasound and color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI). while Zhang et al. [17] obtained an AUC of 0.863 (95% CI: 0.726–0.999) using a model developed from multiparametric MRI radiomics. In our study, a comprehensive set of radiomic features, including intensity, shape, and texture, were extracted at the pixel level to characterize intratumoral heterogeneity from an imaging perspective, and K-means clustering, optimized for the cluster count, identified four distinct intratumoral habitat subregions. Models established from these subregions yielded significant insights into LVI prediction, achieving AUCs of 0.924 (95% CI: 0.875–0.973) in the VS and 0.852 (95% CI: 0.732–0.972) in the TS, comparable to the results from Ge et al., who demonstrated an AUC of 0.931 (95% CI: 0.863–0.999) in VS using a combined nomogram from multiparametric MRI [35]. Compared to MRI, US imaging offers significant clinical utility and cost-effectiveness, highlighting the potential of combining habitat imaging with US imaging for preoperatively identifying LVI status in IDC patients.

While most previous studies [25, 28, 29] focused on intratumoral radiomic features, the peritumoral microenvironment play vital roles in the progression of BC, influencing immune cell infiltration, angiogenesis, and stromal remodeling. Given the instrumental functions of these processes in enhancing tumor aggressiveness and facilitating metastasis [38], it is crucial to incorporate the peritumoral characteristics into prediction models [39]. To date, the studies on the predictive effectiveness of habitat imaging via US imaging for both intra- and peritumoral areas of BCs are still at the infantcy, and the specific effects of the tumor microenvironment components on the outcomes of habitat imaging remains largely unknown. Therefore, in subsequent studies should aim to elucidate these underlying mechanisms and on refining this imaging modality.

Our multivariate analysis also confirmed the tumor size and ALNM as an independent predictor of LVI, corroborating the findings of previous studies [18, 40]. While some studies [32, 41–43] have identified Ki-67 levels, histologic type, and molecular subtype as independent predictors of LVI in BC, such relationships were absent in our findings. This difference may be linked to the inherent limitations of clinical variables in fully capturing tumor heterogeneity. To overcome this challenge, we integrated habitat-derived radiomic features and traditional clinical parameters. The integrated model was significantly superior to the model solely - based on clinical parameters, with a notable increase in AUC from 0.638 to 0.852 (p < 0.05).

Furthermore, our study evaluated the impact of peritumoral region sizes by defining ROIs at 2 mm, 4 mm, and 6 mm from the tumor boundary. Integrating a 2 mm intratumoral margin significantly enhanced the predictive accuracy for LVI status. This finding contrasts with prior investigation result [44] that suggested a 4 mm peritumoral margin as optimal for the diagnosis of malignant lesions. The discrepancy may arise from the automated delineation of the IT-ROI area in our study, which extended beyond the conventional boundaries of the ROI. From a pathophysiological perspective, the optimal performance of the 2‑mm peritumoral region in predicting LVI may be attributable to its anatomical localization and distinct microenvironmental characteristics. The essence of LVI lies in the invasion of tumor cells into lymphatic vessels and capillary networks situated at the invasive front of the tumor, where the 0–2 mm zone surrounding the tumor margin represents the most active interface for tumor–host vasculature interaction [45]. Within this spatial scale, a hypoxic microenvironment can drive the upregulation of angiogenic factors such as VEGF‑C/VEGF‑D, thereby inducing peritumoral lymphangiogenesis and neovascularization [46]. These alterations may in turn affect local tissue density, blood perfusion, and extracellular matrix organization, giving rise to quantifiable signal fluctuations on imaging textures.

Notably, the model incorporating both intra- and peri-tumoral features demonstrated improved performance compared to the model relying only on intratumoral features. However, increasing the size of the peritumoral region does not consistently yield beneficial outcomes. When the peritumoral region is extended to 4–6 mm, the proportion of normal parenchymal or stromal components increases substantially, and the resulting dilution effect on imaging signals may obscure the subtle textural changes associated with LVI. Collectively, these findings support the 2‑mm margin as the “optimal observation window” that strikes a balance between enriching LVI‑relevant signals and minimizing background noise interference. Addressing the importance of optimizing peritumoral region size to maximize model efficacy. Our results emphasize the critical role of peritumoral features and region size in the performance of predictive models. Furthermore, our comparative analysis demonstrated that models integrating radiomic and clinical data significantly outperformed those relying on either type of data alone.

The interpretability of the radiomics-based predictive models is essential for their adoption and application in routine clinical practice [47]. The SHAP summary and force plots enabled clinicians to interpret the influence of model features on assessment outcomes, thereby identifying the most impactful features of the model in this study. This model facilitates the quantification of tumor characteristics, thereby enhancing disease assessment accuracy and supporting informed clinical decision-making.

Despite its significant advantages, the integrated model described in our current study bears several limitations. Firstly, its retrospective design may render it vulnerable to selection healthcare-seeking behavior bias, and LVI assessment was not independently re-reviewed, and we have explicitly called for prospective studies to incorporate independent central review or pre specified inter observer agreement analyses to improve reproducibility. Secondly, the exclusive reliance on two-dimensional imaging could fail to capture the full dimensionality of the tumor, possibly omitting crucial details inherent in the three-dimensional structure of the lesion. Thirdly, incomplete data impede a comprehensive analysis of the link of habitat characteristics and tumor heterogeneity markers, such as transcriptomics and metabolomics. Future research will aim to enlarge the participant pool and implement combined imaging modalities, including MRI, elastography and contrast-enhanced US (CEUS). Fourthly, due to technical constraints and ethical considerations, the habitat subregions derived from K-means clustering could not be validated against histopathological findings on a one-to-one basis; therefore, the precise biological correlates of these subregions remain to be fully characterized. Future prospective studies incorporating imaging-pathology spatial co-registration are warranted to establish the microstructural and functional basis of habitat-defined subregions.

In conclusion, we have developed a comprehensive approach for extensive evaluation of tumor heterogeneity by integrating habitat imaging, peritumoral radiomics, and clinical factors to adeptly predict LVI status in IDC patients. Thanks to its robust predictive performance, this model makes a significant promise as a novel prediction tool in supporting personalized therapeutic planning and informed clinical therapeutic decision-making for IDC patients.
