Lipid apheresis in the management of severe hypertriglyceridaemia in an adolescent girl with global developmental delay
Authors: Lara Al-Agailat, Stephen Guertin, Elizabeth Littlejohn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryHypertriglyceridaemia-induced acute pancreatitis (HTG-AP) remains one of the common metabolic causes of acute pancreatitis in the paediatric population and the third most common cause after alcohol and gallstones in the adult population. We report a case of an early adolescent girl with global developmental delay and moderate cognitive impairment of unknown aetiology who presented with recurrent acute pancreatitis and uncompensated hypovolaemic shock. She was found to have serum triglyceride level of 7877 mg/dL (reference range<150 mg/dL) and hyperglycaemia with ketosis (no prior history of diabetes mellitus) that was successfully treated with lipid apheresis. This sometimes is an early modality for treatment in adults; however, it remains a last resort in children, used only for severe cases. A brief literature review on severe HTG-AP and its management is also provided.Keywords: endocrine system, paediatrics (drugs and medicines)

Background

Severe hypertriglyceridaemia (SHTG) remains a well-known and reported cause of acute pancreatitis in adults and children1 that can result in significant morbidity and mortality. However, evidence-based guidelines for the management2 of SHTG-induced acute pancreatitis (SHTG-AP) remain scarce, institutionalised and mainly based on adult guidelines. There is no clear evidence in the paediatric population as to which cases can be treated safely with conservative measures, such as diet, lifestyle changes and hypolipidemic medications, and which cases should be treated with more invasive measures.3

Case presentation

An adolescent girl, 16 years of age, with a history of global developmental delay of unknown aetiology with moderate cognitive impairment, congenital imperforate with displaced anus (post correction and repair as an infant), normocephaly, morbid obesity, hepatomegaly with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and menorrhagia, previously controlled on oral contraceptive pills with no family history of dyslipidaemia, recurrent pancreatitis or cardiac disease, presented to the emergency department (ED). On presentation, she had a 1-day history of severe abdominal pain associated with nausea and vomiting.

The patient’s history revealed that 2 months prior, she had been seen in the ED for acute pancreatitis (first episode). At that time, she was found to have severe acute pancreatitis with extensive phlegmon without abscess or cyst formation in the setting of compensated hypovolaemic shock requiring hospital admission for elevated amylase and lipase, and moderate hypertriglyceridaemia (HTG) with triglycerides (TG) of 662 mg/dL (table 1).

At the time of first hospitalisation (precase report), she had a normal glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) of 5.6%, vitamin D deficiency (25-hydroxy vitamin D3 12.6 ng/mL—reference range 25–80 ng/dL) and an elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) with normal free T4, thought due to euthyroid sick syndrome in the setting of the acute pancreatitis, impending compensated hypothyroidism or other (goitrogen exposure).

A multidisciplinary team, including but not limited to paediatric endocrinology and dieticians, was involved. Once the first episode of acute pancreatitis resolved, she was started on vitamin D 2000 IU daily and managed conservatively for her hypertriglyceridaemia with a low-fat/high-fibre/low-carbohydrate diet, as well as lifestyles changes with increased physical activity and recommendations to follow-up in the outpatient setting with endocrinology and nutrition after discharge. She had repeat laboratory studies performed that included thyroid function testing done when not ill. Genetic, metabolic and autoimmune workups (table 2) were initiated at that time to look for the cause of cognitive impairment, congenital imperforate anus and acute pancreatitis, as determining the underlying aetiology would help to coordinate care. However, these evaluations did not reveal any underlying causes and were essentially negative. She continued the recommended medications and management outpatient until presenting again to the ED 2 months later with similar symptoms of severe abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting (subject of case report—episode 2).

On presentation during the second episode, the patient was febrile (temperature 102.7°F), tachycardic (heart rate 169 beats per minute), tachypneic (respiratory rate 35 breaths/minute) and hypotensive (blood pressure 71/47 mm Hg). She denied alcohol or any other ingestions.

Physical examination revealed an ill-appearing, diaphoretic girl with no focal neurologic symptoms.

She was tachypneic and tachycardic with delayed capillary refill, found to have cool and clammy extremities, with generalised severe abdominal tenderness (unable to assess for organomegaly due to pain) and had decreased bowel sounds.

Investigations

In the ED (second time case report), further workup included a CT scan of the abdomen that showed acute pancreatitis (second episode) with moderate phlegmon in the peripancreatic region and associated stranding of the adjacent fat with small free fluid in the pelvis. Moderate steatosis and prominent hepatomegaly were also noted. Management including volume and fluid resuscitation was initiated, and she was admitted to the paediatric intensive care unit for closer monitoring, evaluation and management of severe acute pancreatitis in the setting of hypovolaemic shock.

Testing revealed acute pancreatitis with markedly elevated amylase (599 U/L—reference range 20–100 U/L), lipase (1254 U/L—reference range 7–60 U/L) and severe hypertriglyceridaemia (TG 7877 mg/dL) (table 1). The latter was believed to be the cause of the acute pancreatitis. Though hypothyroidism has been found to exacerbate pancreatitis and previously the patient had a mild elevation in TSH with normal free T4, repeat thyroid function testing was normal. Hyperglycaemia with ketoacidosis is associated with pancreatitis, though her HbA1c was 5.4%, which indicated she did not have uncontrolled diabetes mellitus. Finally, acute kidney injury with dehydration and lactic acidosis could be attributable, but was not present in this case. She also had hypocalcaemia in the setting of acute pancreatitis (likely due to saponification), exacerbated by vitamin D deficiency with elevated parathyroid hormone and hypophosphatemia (data not shown).

Differential diagnosis

Given the diagnosis of acute pancreatitis in the setting of extremely elevated TG, the aetiologies and differential diagnosis included gallstones (abdominal ultrasound [US], MRI and magnetic resonance cholangio pancreatography [MRCP] completed after resolution of acute pancreatitis were negative), alcohol or toxin ingestion, infection, malignancy, medication side effects including estrogens, hypercalcaemia, hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism; the latter of which were all unlikely given the history, physical examination findings, testing and imaging, and autoimmune disease, all of which were negative at the time.

Given her history of global developmental delay and moderate cognitive impairment of unknown aetiology, she was at risk for several genetic causes of acute or chronic pancreatitis. Differential diagnoses included inborn errors of metabolism, cystic fibrosis, Johanson-Blizzard syndrome, alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency or other ectodermal genetic defects and autoimmune diseases, such as Sjogren’s syndrome, chronic pancreatitis or inflammatory bowel disease.

Laboratory investigation for autoimmune causes of pancreatitis including isolated autoimmune pancreatitis, syndromic autoimmune chronic pancreatitis, Sjogren’s syndrome-associated chronic pancreatitis, inflammatory bowel disease-associated chronic pancreatitis (with increased association with ulcerative colitis) and primary biliary cirrhosis-associated chronic pancreatitis were obtained and negative. Testing for anti-smooth muscle antibody, anti-mitochondrial antibody, liver/kidney microsome type 1 antibody, antinuclear antibody, inflammatory bowel disease extended panel (gASCA, ACCA, AMCA, ALCA, atypical pANCA) and cytoplasmic neutrophilic antibodies (c-ANCA, p-ANCA) were also negative. The patient had normal total immunoglobulin A (IgA) and immunoglobulin G (IgG) levels.

Genetic workup at the time of the second admission included single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) chromosomal microarray analysis with full gene sequence analysis for CFTR (cystic fibrosis gene) as well as a cystic fibrosis mutation panel, testing for SPINK1 (pancreatic secretory trypsin inhibitor gene) mutations, trypsinogen gene (PRSS1), chymotrypsin C (CTRC), as well as testing for alpha 1-antitrypsin deficiency were found negative.

Previous records showed testing that was initiated in infancy and early childhood for concerns about global developmental delay, imperforate anus and microcephaly (at the time). Her workup included genetic testing with high-resolution chromosome analysis, subtelomere flourescence in situ hybridisation (FISH) studies, 22q FISH study and FISH for Langer-Giedion syndrome, all of which were negative and none revealed a diagnosis. At the time of testing in infancy, she had negative urine ketones as well as normal glucose, electrolytes, plasma amino acids and urine organic acids. Her lactate was within the normal range. A CT of the head showed no evidence of craniosynostosis and was otherwise normal. Renal US was normal.

Prenatal labs including a first-trimester screen were unremarkable. The anatomic ultrasound (US) at ~20 weeks gestational age was concerning for a two-vessel cord but had normal anatomy.

Although a thorough evaluation was obtained, it was unclear how the combination of cognitive impairment, obesity, imperforate anus and hypertriglyceridaemia fit into a single unifying diagnosis. It was also possible that the patient has more than one distinct process occurring, that would separately lead to hypertriglyceridaemia with its attendant risk of pancreatitis, with the underlying developmental delay and imperforate anus. A single unifying diagnosis may reveal itself as genetic testing evolves or more cases emerge (new syndrome).

Treatment

She was immediately started on an insulin intravenous drip, electrolyte replacements and pain management. Once stabilised, a decision was made to start plasmapheresis to rapidly lower the TG given the possibility of death due to stroke from the hypercoagulable state (Quinton catheter was placed and she received a total of four cycles until TG levels were in the 200 mg/dL range over a 2–3-day period, slowly monitoring her response; plasmapheresis was obtained through an outside manufacturer). It was tolerated well and led to a resolution of the hypertriglyceridaemia (see table 1). Once the TG reached a level that could be managed by diet and medication, she was started on an oral fibrate (gemfibrozil 600 mg two times per day) and vitamin D 50,000 IU once weekly.

The patient’s clinical status stabilised and the patient was discharged on gemfibrozil 600 mg two times per day, vitamin D 50,000 IU once weekly, with recommendations to follow a low-fat/high-fibre/low-processed carbohydrate diet, with daily physical exercise.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient recovered from the second episode of acute pancreatitis and transitioned to oral fibrate and lifestyle management. To date, 6 years later, she has not developed any other episodes of acute pancreatitis. She continues to follow as an outpatient with paediatric endocrinology. She takes gemfibrozil 600 mg daily, metformin 1000 mg daily (additional help for management of hypertriglyceridaemia) and omega-3 1200 mg daily with controlled TG.

She developed overt hypothyroidism for which she takes levothyroxine 50 µg once daily and maintenance vitamin D of 1000 IU daily with 2000 IU daily on Sunday, weekly for vitamin D deficiency. Her guardian is considering a hysterectomy for the management of severe dysfunctional uterine bleeding as oral contraceptives (OCPs) were stopped given the history of recurrent pancreatitis. Her body mass index has normalised.

Discussion

HTG, defined as TG levels>150 mg/dL, remains the most common dyslipidaemia in adults and a common dyslipidaemia in childhood. It is further classified into mild, moderate and severe HTG with SHTG, defined as plasma TG levels≥1000 mg/dL. It remains a relatively uncommon condition in adults but is a rare, understudied, very serious problem in childhood. SHTG in the paediatric population is believed to be either a primarily genetic disorder or secondary to obesity, diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome or certain medications. To date, the only genetically well-described types of SHTG are lipoprotein lipase (LPL) deficiency and apolipoprotein C-II deficiency, which typically present in infancy with chylomicronaemia syndrome causing SHTG in very early childhood.4

SHTG is a well-established cause of recurrent abdominal pain and severe acute pancreatitis, with rapid lowering of TG as a well-known modality to prevent these complications. However, to date, there are no specific guidelines thus a gap in knowledge regarding treatment of SHTG-associated AP and hyperglycaemia with ketosis in the paediatric population which can be devastating due to its complications such as septic shock, multiorgan failure and death.

Current guidelines for the treatment of adult patients with SHTG include extreme dietary fat restriction, alcohol abstinence, medium chain TG, fibrates, omega-3-fatty acids and nicotinic acid with the goal of reducing the TG level to <1000 mg/dL. However, as those modalities and drugs of choice are either not powerful enough to rapidly lower TG or do not offer fast onset of action, there is a need for urgent, fast and effective treatments to lower TG levels to prevent complications of SHTG-AP.4

Also, rarely described in the literature is the triad of hyperglycaemia with ketosis, severe hypertriglyceridaemia and severe recurrent acute pancreatitis. The exact pathophysiology resulting in this triad remains unclear. The most accepted theory is that insulin resistance or deficiency in patients with undiagnosed or uncontrolled diabetes mellitus can lead to activation of lipolysis and inhibition of lipoprotein lipase (LPL), leading to an increase in TG. High TG, is then hydrolysed by high levels of pancreatic enzymes to free fatty acids (FFA) in high concentrations that can self-aggregate and cause pancreatic capillary and acinar cell injury. This creates an acidic environment which further triggers FFA toxicity leading to SHTG-AP,5 which, at present, has no approved therapeutic guidelines in either the adult or paediatric populations to prevent this cascade.

Although different treatment modalities for SHTG-AP such as fluid resuscitation, complete avoidance of oral intake with total parenteral nutrition, insulin (as LPL activity is increased by insulin infusion which degrades TG-rich chylomicrons and very low density lipoprotein [VLDL] particles)6 and the addition of fenofibrate, implemented initially, other treatment options are sometimes required for urgent management in severe complicated cases.5 A therapeutic tool, as in cases reported by Wassay et al7 and well known as an effective, fast method to decrease TG in a short period of time, is lipid apheresis or plasmapheresis, which is not frequently used for SHTG, as there is still no strong evidence available to recommend its routine use in these patients. However, several case reports have shown promising results in decreasing morbidity and mortality especially when plasmapheresis or lipid apheresis is initiated within 24–96 hours of symptom onset8 while other studies detected no difference in outcomes regarding early versus late apheresis. While still a less conventional method, standard therapeutic guidelines need to be formulated. We propose lipid apheresis be considered as an early means of treatment for patients with SHTG-AP with severe systemic manifestations (shock). Knowing that its effect remains transient, long-term lipid-lowering treatment must be added in conjunction to achieve a sustained effect for prevention of ongoing episodes and complications.9 Potential challenges in the paediatric population in using lipid apheresis remain, such as gauging weight-based fluid replacement in contrast to the adult population where fluid replacement is standardised. As we encounter more of these cases, developing standard guidelines will be essential for the paediatric community. This case illustrates how lipid apheresis is beneficial, lifesaving and could potentially serve as a guideline for treatment of severe life-threatening SHTG in this arena that lacks experience in the paediatric population. Clearly, lipid apheresis is a modality that requires further study.

Summary/conclusion

As paediatricians, we are often put in the position of adapting adult protocols for the care of children. We use our knowledge of paediatric physiology and the inherent constraints, then alter life-saving treatments accordingly. This is likely why four rounds of plasmapheresis were used in this case; underestimating the number of rounds at first then repeating if successful but not detrimental, with the goal of lowering the TG levels slowly and safely. In the absence of a clear paediatric guideline, this case is an illustrative example of this adaptative process. It also serves as the starting point for developing a paediatric standard of care as we face more lipid disorders. These problems once only present in adult medicine are now potentially catastrophic problems in the paediatric landscape. Future research on when to use plasmapheresis and dosing is needed as paediatricians encounter severe lipid disorders in children. This is a seismic shift in our world; thus, we need to be prepared as paediatric healthcare providers.
