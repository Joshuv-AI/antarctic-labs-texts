AC frequency effects on high-throughput electrospinning for nanofibre production
Authors: Ondrej Batka, Ondrej Friedrich, Josef Skrivanek, Jan Valtera, Petr Zabka, Martin Bilek, Jaroslav Mikule, Jaroslav Beran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alternating current electrospinning (ACES) is emerging as a promising approach for industrial scale nanofibre production, offering the potential for higher throughput and energy efficiency compared to conventional direct current methods. This study presents a series of experiments conducted with a wire-type electrode system, aiming to evaluate the influence of key process parameters, namely alternating field frequency, applied voltage, current limitation, and wire speed-on both the productivity and quality of nanofibrous layers produced from polymer solutions. The results show that an appropriate combination of these parameters significantly enhances both areal weight (GSM) and productivity, while also identifying technological limits related to power supply performance. Furthermore, the effects of these parameters on fibre diameter and the overall quality of the nanofibrous structure are examined. These findings confirm the strong potential of ACES with wire-type electrodes for advancing continuous nanofibre production on an industrial scale, while also revealing critical technological constraints related to power-supply performance and jet stability. The insights gained provide an important foundation for the design of next-generation production lines with higher capacity, improved process stability, and enhanced energy efficiency, moving ACES closer to widespread industrial deployment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46392-2.

Introduction

Electrospinning is an effective and widely used method for producing nanofibres. It uses a strong electric field to create fine fibres from polymer solutions. There are two main variants of this technology: direct current electrospinning (DC electrospinning, DCES), which has already established a firm position across various fields of human activity1–12 and a newer technology using alternating current (alternating current electrospinning, ACES), which has been gradually developed, from experiments with low voltages to a highly productive process using high voltages13–17. For technical applications, the most widely adopted technology today is Nanospider™, which represents a significant advancement in the field of DCES. It enables needleless production of nanofibres on an industrial scale, based on the DCES principle. This technology uses a wire electrode, which significantly increases both the efficiency and scalability of the process compared to traditional needle-based methods. In the case of DCES, the influence of technological parameters such as voltage on the properties of nanofibres and production productivity has already been described in detail18–29.

The ACES technology is still the focus of intensive research and development and is proving particularly promising for fabricating of linear nanofibrous structures. Examples include core–shell AC yarns4,30, which are used, for instance, in the production of filter candles31,32 into which additives such as activated carbon can be incorporated33. Another line of research targets the incorporation of calcium into the nanofibre structure for filtering cadmium-contaminated water, especially in the vicinity of industrial zones34. Recent attention has also been devoted to producing braided yarns for tissue-engineering applications, specifically for scaffold construction35,36.

All of the applications share a common requirement for industrial deployment: the need to enhance the efficiency of nanofibre production on an industrial scale. The presented research, therefore, focuses on exploring possibilities to increase the productivity of 2D nanofibrous structures using ACES technology. A key parameter in this context is the frequency of the alternating voltage applied to the spinning electrode. Previous studies have shown that frequency influences productivity, particularly for needle-based or weir-electrodes (AC electrospinning electrodes with a continuously flooded spinning surface, ensuring stable fibre formation; see Refs37–40). However, these types of electrodes typically achieve production rates of hundreds of milligrams per minute for PVB polymers, or only tens of milligrams per minute for polyamide. Furthermore, the productivity of weir electrodes40 is limited by their ability to efficiently supply a sufficient amount of solution to the spinning surface. Experimental results indicate that higher frequencies affect not only productivity but also the quality of nanofibrous structures40. This raises the question of whether the frequency effect can also be leveraged in wire electrodes, which are much more promising for the industrial production of planar (sheet-like) nanofibre layers.It has been demonstrated that ACES technology can be applied to electrode types other than rods or needles, and that with appropriate electrode design, it is possible to produce planar nanofibrous structures for example, by using belt-type electrodes41. However, the technology for producing planar nanofibre structures using ACES has not yet been thoroughly explored or described, particularly with regard to wire electrodes and the potential for their use. Therefore, this research aims to evaluate the potential of wire electrodes for nanofibre production using selected polymer solutions, with a particular focus on investigating the influence of frequency on both the productivity and quality of the resulting nanofibrous structures. The study seeks to monitor individual fibre diameters and to determine the maximum applicable frequency that still ensures the desired quality and efficiency of production.

Materials and methods

Reversing wire electrode system

The technology used for the experiments was developed at the Technical University of Liberec and is based on the ACES principle combined with a wire-type electrode42. The experimental setup for nanofibre production was designed to achieve homogeneous fibre deposition on a non-woven textile and enable continuous production. The experimental setup consists of two main parts: the ACES system with reciprocating wire motion (Fig. 1a), and the nonwoven textile rewinding system (Fig. 1b). At the core of the ACES system is a conductive stainless wire with a diameter of 1 mm, stretched between two rotating pulleys. The wire has a 1 × 19 construction, meaning it consists of one central wire surrounded by 18 helically wound outer wires. This construction ensures high tensile strength while maintaining the flexibility required for reciprocating motion across the pulleys. The pulleys, driven by servo motors, perform a reversing motion, which causes the wire to move back and forth horizontally. Each pulley is equipped with a helical guide groove that precisely guides the wire. As one pulley rotates, the wire is wound onto it while simultaneously unwinding from the other, with the direction of motion regularly alternating. Four spinning wires operate in parallel, spaced 350 mm apart in the spinning zone. Each wire is individually guided by its pair of servo motors, allowing independent control. This setup offers a high degree of process control and individual optimisation of operating parameters. The length of the spinning zone, i.e., the segment of wire over which electrospinning occurs, is approximately 1.6 m. The pulleys are partially immersed in the polymer solution, which adheres to their surface during rotation. A squeegee is then used to mechanically control the thickness of the solution and to form a uniform polymer film. The squeegee consists of a thin metal plate spanning the entire width of the pulley and is positioned at a defined distance from the pulley surface. By adjusting this distance, the amount of polymer solution remaining on the pulley surface and consequently the thickness of the deposited film is controlled. The working principle is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. The squeegee is set to produce a film thickness of 0.5 mm. An electric field is applied to the polymer solution coated wire, initiating the AC electrospinning process and the formation of nanofibres. These fibres are carried upward and deposited onto a continuously moving nonwoven textile not only due to the negative pressure generated by an industrial hood, but also by the initial electric forces acting on them. This textile is fed through the spinning zone by a rewinding system (Fig. 1b), starting from an unwind roll, moving through the spinning zone where nanofibres are deposited, and finally being wound onto a take-up roll. The rewinding speed is synchronised with the wire movement to ensure uniform fibre layer deposition. The spinning area is housed under an industrial hood with fan-assisted extraction, providing a constant airflow and efficient fibre suction onto the textile surface. The polymer solution is continuously replenished from a reservoir located below the spinning zone. The entire process is monitored using a camera, which captures detailed images of the shape and nanofibrous plume stability. The device allows dynamic regulation of the process through the assessment of nanofibrous plume parameters, with particular emphasis on fibre plume volume. The experimental device is shown in Fig. 1c and d shows the spinning process with the rewinding wire system inside the spinning chamber.

High voltage power source

To supply the electrodes with electrical voltage, a TREK 50/12 high-voltage amplifier (Trek, Inc., USA) was used. Its input was connected to an Owon AG 1022 function generator (Fujian Lilliput Optoelectronics Technology Co., Ltd., China) which was used to set the signal frequency and amplitude in terms of Vpp voltage, e.g., 16 Vpp, corresponding to an output voltage of 40 kVAmp. The maximum input signal that can be applied to the amplifier is 20 Vpp, which results in a maximum output of 50 kVAmp. The amplifier has a gain factor of 5000, and throughout the text, the reported set parameters will refer to the assumed amplitude of the amplifier’s output signal. The TREK amplifier allows monitoring of output signals, such as voltage and current, via external outputs. These signals were displayed using a Lecroy WaveAce 234 oscilloscope (Teledyne LeCroy, Inc., USA) and the signal amplitude and RMS values were evaluated. All reported electrical measurements are provided as RMS values.

The polymer solution

Polyvinyl butyral (PVB, Mowital™ B60H; Kuraray Europe GmbH, Germany) was dissolved in 96% (v/v) ethanol (Technisolv®, VWR Chemicals, VWR International) to obtain a polymer solution with a concentration of 10.5% (w/w). Polyamide 6 (PA6, Ultramid® B24 N 03; Ultrapolymers, Slovakia) was dissolved in a 1:2 (w/w) mixture of formic acid (98% p.a.) and acetic acid (99.8% p.a.) (Penta Chemicals, Czech Republic), yielding a polymer concentration of 14 wt%. The PVB solution was magnetically stirred at 400 rpm and room temperature (22 ± 1 °C) for 12 h, whereas the PA6 solution was mechanically stirred at 80 °C for 12 h, in both cases to ensure complete polymer dissolution.

The surface tension of the polymer solutions was determined using a bubble pressure tensiometer (BPT Mobile, Krüss, Germany) under standard laboratory conditions. Viscosity measurements were performed with a rotational viscometer (ROTAVISC lo-vi, IKA, Germany) at 21 °C, while electrical conductivity was measured using a conductivity meter (FiveEasy, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland).

Setup of industrial hoods for air intake control

A separate industrial exhaust hood was installed above each of the four spinning wires to provide suction-assisted fibre capture on the nonwoven substrate. As the number of deposited fibre layers increased along the line, web permeability decreased and flow resistance increased. Suction was governed by fan rotational speed (rpm). Fans of type Elektor S-MP 425/31 with a rated electrical input of 1.5 kW were used. The preliminary tests proved an operating range of 4.0–5.6 m·s−1 to be sufficient for reliable nanofibre layer deposition. To maintain the airflow within the operating range, depending on layer thickness, the fan-speed setpoints were increased from 1800 rpm (Hood 1) to 2600 rpm (Hood 4), with intermediate setpoints of 2000 rpm (Hood 2) and 2200 rpm (Hood 3).

Spunbond textile

Experiments were conducted on a spunbond NT 100% PP-S substrate (30 g·m−²; Pegatex, s.r.o., Žilina, Slovakia). Nanofibrous layers were fabricated using a rewinding line (SKV, Dvůr Králové nad Labem, Czech Republic) with adjustable speed from 1 to 30 m·min−1.

Measuring of GSM

The areal weight (GSM) was measured using a sensor NDC series 9 (NDC Technologies—Nordson, USA) that detects wavelengths reflected from the analysed material. Calibration samples of the deposited layer were simultaneously collected, and their mass per unit area was determined to verify and calibrate the sensor. The final GSM values were thus obtained from calibrated sensor data.

Analysis of nanofibrous plume width

The Raspberry Pi 4 Model B with Camera Module was used to capture the image. Recording being performed for 1 min for each frequency. The video was processed so that only a row of pixels representing a cross-section across the plume width was taken from each frame. These rows were then stacked, creating an image where each row corresponds to one frame. The change of the plume width over time was then evaluated from this image. Given that the nanofibrous plume is white and partially transparent, the presence of a plume in the image was evaluated as a change in the brightness of the respective pixel compared to the brightness of the background, which was assumed to be the darkest color in time.

Morphological characterization—SEM images, histograms

The morphology was analysed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) Vega 3 (Tescan, Czechia). Samples for SEM analysis were coated by gold to ensure surface conductivity using sputter coater Q150R ES (Quorum, United Kingdom). Fibre diameter was measured by image analysis software ImageJ, average fibre diameter for each sample was determined as average value of 300 measurements from 5 SEM images.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using MATLAB R2024a (MathWorks, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test43 and indicated non-normal distributions. Consequently, group differences were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test44 with Holm–Bonferroni correction. Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation analyses were used to assess the relationships between frequency and fibre diameter as well as plume width, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Experiments

The objective of the experimental measurements was to systematically evaluate the impact of electrical and operational parameters on the productivity of nanofibre production via ACES using polymer solutions. Unless stated otherwise, all experiments were performed with a polyvinyl butyral (PVB) solution. All measurements were conducted under laboratory conditions, with ambient temperatures maintained between 20.5 and 21.5 °C and relative humidity kept within the range of 40–42%. Unless specified in the relevant subsection, the take-up speed of the supporting nonwoven substrate was set to 3 m·min−1. The distance between the wire electrode and the suction opening of the industrial hood was kept constant at 110 mm in all experiments.

Influence of voltage and frequency

In the main series of experiments, the relationship between productivity and the combination of high-voltage amplitude and signal frequency was investigated. Three voltage levels were used: 40 kV, 45 kV, and 50 kV. For each voltage level, the signal frequency was varied within a suitable range. The overall tested frequency range was approximately 30–170 Hz, with specific intervals for each voltage to ensure a stable ACES regime without significant loss of fibre quality or current collapse. In all these experiments, the Trek 50/12 high-voltage power supply was used, with the current limit set to 12 mA, corresponding to the maximum output capacity of the power supply.

Current limitation influence

To assess the effect of limited output current, an additional experiment was conducted at a voltage of 50 kV, with the signal frequency again varied within a range suitable for stable ACES. In this case, the high-voltage power supply’s current limit was set to 90% of its maximum value, corresponding to approximately 10.6 mA. This mode was then compared to previous measurements performed at full output, i.e., with the current limit set to 12 mA.

Transporting of polymer solution by wire speed

The next series of measurements focused on evaluating the influence of the transport speed of the polymer solution along the spinning wire, i.e., a mechanical parameter affecting material supply to the spinning zone. The measurements were again carried out at a voltage of 50 kV and variable signal frequency, using PVB polymer, with three different transport speeds tested: 1.4 m·s− 1, 1.6 m·s− 1 and 1.9 m·s− 1. The aim of these measurements was to verify whether a higher solution transport speed leads to increased productivity without negatively impacting fibre quality.

Alternative polymer solution testing

The effect of signal frequency on nanofibre production efficiency was also investigated using a polyamide 6 (PA6) polymer solution. These measurements were conducted at a constant voltage of 50 kV while systematically varying the signal frequency within a range commonly applied for PVB. For PA6, the take-up speed of the supporting nonwoven textile was reduced to 1 m·min−1 because at 3 m·min−1 the deposited nanofibrous layer could not be reliably removed for accurate weighing across the full frequency range, particularly at lower frequencies. The lower take-up speed was therefore used to obtain a sufficiently thick layer suitable for evaluation. For direct comparison with PVB processed at 3 m·min−1, the PA6 areal mass (GSM) values were presented as values corresponding to 3 m·min−1. This extrapolation was based on the experimentally determined inverse (hyperbolic) proportion of GSM on take-up speed obtained from previous experiments. Therefore, the hyperbolic dependence of GSM on take-up speed was applied uniformly across all investigated frequencies. The objective was to compare PA6 and PVB under equivalent operating conditions directly, enabling the assessment of material-specific behaviour in response to frequency changes.

Sample collection and preparation for SEM analysis of fibre morphology and distribution

Selected experiments involved systematic collection of nanofibre samples for subsequent characterization, with the main objective of analyzing fibre morphology and structural integrity by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The SEM analysis was aimed at evaluating the uniformity of the nanofibrous layer, quantifying the distribution of fibre diameters, and identifying any structural defects, such as bead formation, fibre fusion, or inconsistencies in alignment. Samples were prepared and analyzed for both polymer types tested: polyvinyl butyral (PVB) and polyamide 6 (PA6).

Recording of the nanofibrous plume using a camera

The shape of the nanofibrous plume was recorded for different frequencies during spinning using a camera. The effect of frequency on plume shape and width was monitored, together with its relation to the overall stability of the ACES process.

Results

Figure 2a and b together illustrate the relationship between the GSM of the fibre layer, productivity, and the associated electrical parameters (RMS voltage and RMS current) as a function of the supply signal frequency.

In Fig. 2a, GSM (left axis) and the corresponding productivity (right axis) are plotted against frequency for various operating conditions. The applied signal has a sinusoidal waveform with voltage amplitudes of 40 kV, 45 kV, and 50 kV. For each voltage level, the current limit of the power supply was set to 12 mA. For 50 kV, an additional variant was measured with the current limit reduced to 90% of the maximum output, corresponding to approximately 10.6 mA.

The results clearly show that GSM and productivity rise with increasing frequency. However, for each combination of voltage and current limit, there is a well-defined maximum, beyond which both productivity and GSM stagnate and then decrease. For 50 kV and 12 mA, the maximum occurs at approximately 100 Hz; for 50 kV and 10.6 mA, it shifts to around 80–90 Hz; for 45 kV, it is roughly at 130 Hz; and for 40 kV, the maximum is around 150 Hz.

This behaviour is closely related to the operation of the power supply, as shown in Fig. 2b. In this graph, RMS voltage (left axis) and RMS current (right axis) are plotted as a function of frequency for identical operating conditions. As the frequency increases, the RMS current rises due to the predominantly capacitive character of the ACES system, which can be regarded, from an electrical perspective, as an air-gap capacitor in an AC circuit. The effective capacitance of the ACES system is determined by the geometric arrangement of the electrodes and the surrounding air gap and can be considered constant over the investigated range of voltages and frequencies. Consequently, the capacitive reactance decreases with increasing frequency, which results in a corresponding increase in current. When the current reaches the preset limit of the power supply (12 mA or 10.6 mA), the supply switches from constant-voltage mode to constant-current mode. At this point, the current cannot increase further and the RMS voltage decreases to maintain the current at the limiting value. At higher applied voltages, the current therefore increases more rapidly with frequency, causing the current limit to be reached at lower frequencies. In contrast, at lower voltages the current increases more gradually, allowing higher operating frequencies before current limitation occurs. This voltage drop is clearly visible in Fig. 2b. For higher voltage variants, the current limit is reached at lower frequencies due to the higher current draw, which corresponds to the drop in voltage and the subsequent decrease in productivity and GSM observed in Fig. 2a. Conversely, at the lower applied voltage (40 kV), the current load remains lower and the power supply reaches its limit only at higher frequencies, explaining the shift of the productivity maximum towards higher frequencies.

These graphs demonstrate that the limiting factor for maximum productivity and GSM is not the ACES principle itself, but the capability of the power supply to maintain sufficient voltage under increasing current load due to frequency influence. Once the current limit is reached and the RMS voltage is reduced, the intensity of the electric field drops, directly reducing the tensile forces necessary for stable ACES. As a result, productivity and GSM decline beyond the maximum. Figure 2c compares productivity for two different polymers, PVB and PA6, at the same voltage amplitude of 50 kV with identical current limitation. The left axis shows GSM for PVB, while the right axis shows GSM for PA6. Both curves follow the same trend: productivity rises with frequency, reaches a maximum, and then decreases as frequency increases further. The lower absolute productivity observed for the PA6 solution compared with the PVB solution can be rationalised by differences in their physicochemical properties. As reported by Lukáš et al.45 for DCES, the characteristic wavelength (λ) of fluid instabilities which can be interpreted as the distance between adjacent polymer jets on the electrode decreases with increasing electric field intensity and increases with increasing surface tension. In this context, the distinct properties of the two solutions, namely viscosity, electrical conductivity and surface tension, are relevant. The PVB solution exhibited a lower surface tension (22.27 mN m−1), lower viscosity (166.7 mPa s) and higher electrical conductivity (7.44 µS cm−1). In contrast, the PA6 solution showed a higher surface tension (30.39 mN m−1), higher viscosity (241.7 mPa s) and a markedly lower electrical conductivity (0.51 µS cm−1).

Figure 2d shows the effect of wire speed on GSM as a function of frequency. For all curves, the voltage was set to 50 kV, with wire speed being the only variable (1.4 m·s− 1; 1.6 m·s− 1; 1.9 m·s− 1). Higher wire speed leads to higher GSM across the entire frequency range. A higher wire speed increases the supply of polymer solution to the spinning zone, enabling the formation of a layer with higher GSM. Again, the GSM maximum reflects the power supply limitation: when the current limit is reached, RMS voltage drops, reducing tensile forces and lowering productivity.

To further characterise the dependence of fibre diameter on frequency, correlation analyses were performed using mean fibre diameter values across the applied frequencies. No statistically significant relationship between frequency and fibre diameter was observed (Pearson’s r = − 0.30, p = 0.52; Spearman’s ρ = −0.43, p = 0.35), despite a weak decreasing tendency (Fig. 4a).

Figure 8 shows examples of nanofibre structure images for different frequencies, where defective areas are manually marked with red ellipses. The analysis was carried out to qualitatively assess the occurrence of defects as a function of frequency in the range of 40–180 Hz. The primary aim was to illustrate that the increase in productivity achieved at higher frequencies may also be accompanied by certain drawbacks related to nanofibre morphology, rather than to provide a quantitative evaluation of defect density. Defects in the nanofibre layers are defined as morphological deviations from the ideal fibrous structure. The most commonly observed defects include fused fibres, excessively thick fibres, droplets, and various non-fibrous agglomerates.

The results reveal the following pattern: In the frequency range of 40–80 Hz, the occurrence of defects is significantly lower, likely due to better conditions for ACES, particularly the longer time available for fibre stretching and drying during formation. In the 90–130 Hz range, a noticeable increase in defects is observed. At frequencies from 140 to 180 Hz, the occurrence of defects increases more markedly. The presence of defects, particularly droplet-like formations and unspun agglomerates of solution, suggests that at high frequencies, a massive jet forms, leaving insufficient time for proper stretching and solidification of fibres.

Findings from other studies focusing on spinning electrodes indicate that increasing frequency leads to a higher number of defects in fibrous structures37. The experiments conducted and the structural analysis confirm that the same phenomenon is evident for wire electrodes. It can therefore be stated that higher frequencies promote a greater incidence of defects in nanofibre structures, most likely due to reduced stability of the Taylor cone and intermittent polymer jet flow, which results in the formation of fused fibres, droplets, and other morphological abnormalities.

Conclusion and discussion

The presented results provide a comprehensive overview of the influence of operating parameters on the resulting GSM and productivity of ACES of polymer materials. The data obtained confirm that all key factors—power frequency, voltage, current, wire take-off speed, and polymer selection—have a significant impact on the efficiency of fibre formation and deposition.

In terms of frequency, an initial increase in GSM and productivity is observed as the frequency rises; however, this trend is valid only up to a certain threshold. At frequencies around 100 Hz and above, the used high-voltage power supply (Trek 50/12) becomes overloaded because the circuit current increases to a level at which the supply switches from constant-voltage mode to constant-current mode. This limited mode causes the actual RMS voltage to decrease, which directly affects the AC electrospinning process—lower voltage weakens the electrostatic forces necessary for fibre formation and elongation, thereby reducing productivity and the resulting GSM. This explains the stagnation or slight decrease in GSM and productivity at higher frequencies.

Voltage remains the dominant parameter determining the intensity of the electric field. Higher voltages (e.g., 50 kV) allow achieving higher GSM and greater productivity. Current limitation (e.g., to 10.6 mA) results in a lower RMS current, keeping the supply in a safe operating mode but simultaneously restricting the energy available to sustain a stable ACES.

The results confirmed, that in the ACES, a challenging factor is the influence of electrical capacitance, as the process behaves physically like a capacitor. From this perspective, machine design should aim to minimize parasitic capacitances as much as possible, ideally limiting them only to the spinning zone—which is difficult to achieve in practice. Therefore, an optimal balance must be found between productivity and the energy efficiency of the equipment. In the case of using the TREK 50/12 voltage amplifier, the process is limited by current overload. If the circuit current reaches the limit, the voltage automatically drops to prevent overloading. For industrial solutions, it is therefore essential to use a power supply capable of delivering sufficient power at higher frequencies. In the current system used for these experiments, the maximum GSM reached nearly 4.5 g·m− 2 (for PVB) with a power consumption of 336 W at 100 Hz and 50 kVamp (35.4 kVRMS), with the measured system capacitance being (450 ± 20) pF.

Frequency does not have a practical effect on the diameter of the produced nanofibres, which is advantageous, as a higher frequency (and thus higher productivity) yields a structure with the same parameters. On the other hand, this may also be considered a drawback, as frequency cannot be used to deliberately control the properties of the produced structure, which could be beneficial for certain applications. Additionally, it was observed that with increasing frequency, the number of defects in the resulting structure rises, leading to reduced homogeneity. For some applications, this may present an issue, while for others, it might not. Further research will be required to determine how these defects affect the usability of nanofibre structures.

It was also demonstrated that increasing frequency leads to a reduction in the cross-sectional area of the nanofibrous plume, i.e., the radius over which the jet disperses. This enables a reduction in the distance between adjacent electrodes, thereby decreasing the footprint of the equipment. In the present study, the selected spacing between wire electrodes was determined based on electric-field simulations, which revealed mutual interactions between neighbouring electrodes. The results of these simulations are provided in the Supplementary Informations. The simulations indicate that, at electrode spacings of approximately 350 mm and above, the mutual influence on the electric field intensity at the wire becomes negligible. From a practical perspective, this distance therefore represents a suitable compromise between reducing the footprint of the equipment and avoiding adverse inter-electrode interactions. At smaller spacings, the simulations show a noticeable modification of the electric field intensity, indicating that electrode spacing may affect productivity. This observation is consistent with previous findings for rod-type weir electrodes operated at 50 Hz, where reducing electrode spacing led to a decrease in productivity46. For wire electrodes, this effect has not yet been examined across different frequencies, which opens up new possibilities for future research aimed at optimizing the ACES process with multiple wire electrodes. The industrial ACES device presented in this work does not allow variation of inter-electrode spacing due to its mechanical design. A dedicated laboratory-scale experiment with adjustable spacing between wire electrodes would therefore be required to systematically study this effect. Such an experimental investigation is planned as part of future optimisation work aimed at further improving the performance and industrial applicability of multi-wire AC electrospinning systems.

Fibre diameter remains unchanged, while productivity increases. The distance between the cones decreases, which means more cones are formed along the length of the wire, resulting in the generation of more fibres.

From the above, it follows that optimal parameter settings cannot be viewed in isolation. Frequency, voltage, and current define the system’s ability to form fibres, while wire speed controls the supply of polymer solution to the process.

Furthermore, the experiments highlight the importance of polymer selection. The materials PVB and PA6 exhibit distinctly different spinning behaviours—PVB achieves significantly higher GSM compared to PA6 under comparable conditions (e.g. 50 kV). This difference is related to the physicochemical properties of the polymers—solution viscosity, conductivity, and the ability to form stable jets play a crucial role in the rate of fibre formation and deposition.

Another important parameter is the speed of the spinning wire. Unlike voltage or frequency, which directly govern the physical mechanisms of jet formation and thus the intrinsic productivity of the process, wire velocity acts as an indirect, yet critical, limiting factor. Its influence arises from the requirement to supply a sufficient amount of polymer solution along the entire length of the wire to fully exploit its maximum fibre-forming capacity. If the wire moves too slowly, the solution deposited on its surface may be entirely electrospun before reaching the opposite end, leading to underutilization of the wire. Conversely, an excessively high wire speed transports more solution than can be effectively electrospun, resulting in material losses and reduced efficiency. Therefore, wire speed does not directly increase productivity, but determines the balance between efficient fibre formation and solution wastage, and must be optimised to match the inherent productivity set by the primary process parameters.

Another related aspect influenced by the wire speed is the GSM uniformity across the nonwoven width. While effective transport of the polymer solution along the wire electrode towards its ends is important for achieving uniform deposition, the uniformity is not governed by wire speed alone. A slight decrease in GSM is observed near the edges compared with the central region, which is attributed to the edge electric boundary conditions imposed by the pulley system, as this decrease is also observed at low frequencies where sufficient polymer supply along the wire electrode can be assumed. The corresponding GSM uniformity distribution is provided in the Supplementary Information. The primary focus of the present study, however, remains on controlling process productivity through frequency variation and on analysing the overall process behaviour.

A comparison between AC electrospinning (ACES) and DC electrospinning (DCES) naturally arises. However, such a comparison is complex and, in many respects, nontrivial. First and foremost, the suitability of each technology strongly depends on the required structure and its intended application. A wide range of applications can be envisaged for nanofibrous materials, and each of them requires a specific approach to process design and optimisation. Consequently, it is difficult to provide a universal answer as to which technology is inherently superior. In industrial practice, the final verdict is ultimately determined by market demands and the competitive environment.

Numerous parameters can be considered when comparing ACES and DCES. In particular, areal mass (GSM) and overall productivity are strongly material-dependent. The incorporation of functional additives or active substances further affects productivity, fibre morphology, and the resulting product quality. From a scientific perspective, only a limited set of parameters can be directly observed and quantified in a controlled manner, most notably the nanofibre production rate in relation to applied process parameters such as voltage, wire diameter, and the electrical power required to maintain a stable spinning process in both technologies.

It should be emphasised that the technological setups are not interchangeable: the ACES device is not designed for DC operation, and conversely, DCES systems are not suitable for ACES operation. The electrode geometries, wire diameters, and applied high-voltage regimes differ substantially between the two technologies and cannot be directly transferred in a general or universal manner.

We note that additional comparison criteria may be relevant for specific applications, such as pressure drop, filtration efficiency, or particle penetration, rather than GSM alone. Although these aspects are beyond the scope of the present study, they are currently being investigated and will be addressed in a forthcoming publication, which will provide a more comprehensive comparison of ACES and DCES in terms of resulting structures and application potential.

In conclusion, this study provides clear experimental evidence that frequency control of the ACES using a spinning wire is an effective approach to increasing the specific productivity of nanofibres. At the same time, it demonstrates that the technological limitations lie in the power supply design, precise polymer dosing, and stable geometry of the spinning zone. The results thus form a robust foundation for the design of the next generation of industrial production lines, aiming to push the boundaries of nanofibre manufacturing toward higher capacity, improved energy efficiency, and reduced product costs, while ensuring stable, continuous production.
