Enpatoran, a first-in-class, selective, orally administered toll-like receptor 7/8 inhibitor, in systemic and cutaneous lupus erythematosus: results from a randomised, placebo-controlled phase Ib study
Authors: Torsten Witte, Ruth Fernandez-Ruiz, Nadezda Abramova, Dominika Weinelt, Flavie Moreau, Lena Klopp-Schulze, Jamie Shaw, Deborah Denis, Joerg Wenzel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate multiple-ascending doses of enpatoran, a selective toll-like receptor 7/8 (TLR7/8) inhibitor with the potential to modulate processes central to lupus pathophysiology, in patients with systemic and cutaneous lupus erythematosus (SLE/CLE).MethodsIn this randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase Ib trial (NCT04647708), patients with active SLE/CLE were randomised 3:1 to enpatoran or placebo across four cohorts. Treatment duration was 12/24 weeks. All patients had a 2-week safety follow-up. The primary endpoint was safety and tolerability. Secondary endpoints included pharmacokinetics (PK) and clinical response. Change in lupus biomarkers, including interferon gene signature (IFN-GS), was an exploratory endpoint.Results25 patients received placebo (n=6) or enpatoran (n=19); 80% were female, all were white, and the median age was 44 years. By week 12, treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported by 42% of patients across the four enpatoran dose groups and 33% of placebo-treated patients. Enpatoran remained well tolerated to week 24. Most TEAEs (96%) were mild or moderate in severity. There were no serious TEAEs. Enpatoran PK was dose proportional, with peak concentration reached 1–2 hours postdose and an apparent half-life of 6–10 hours across doses. Greater reductions in Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000, Physician’s Global Assessment and 28-joint count were observed at week 24 with enpatoran versus placebo. Rapid, dose-dependent and reversible suppression of IFN-GS was observed.ConclusionsEnpatoran was well tolerated and demonstrated favourable safety and PK profiles in patients with SLE and CLE. Although preliminary, the SLE disease activity and biomarker results support the therapeutic targeting of TLR7/8 for lupus.Trial registration numberNCT04647708.Keywords: Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic; Clinical Trial; Pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, heterogeneous autoimmune disease, characterised by the gradual accumulation of irreversible organ damage.1 2 Due to the systemic nature of SLE, many organ systems can be affected.2 Symptoms can be debilitating and considerably impact quality of life, and some manifestations are life-threatening.1 3 Treatment of SLE includes antimalarials, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants and biologics targeting B cells (belimumab) and type I interferon (IFN; anifrolumab).4 However, many patients experience inadequate disease control and require long-term use of glucocorticoids, which are associated with serious adverse effects.14,6

Although the majority (72%–85%) of patients with SLE experience skin manifestations during their lifetime, the skin is the only organ involved in cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE), which can also negatively impact quality of life.7,9 CLE is classified into subtypes, of which discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE; a subtype of chronic CLE) and subacute CLE (SCLE) are most common.10 The pathogenesis of CLE and SLE are similar, involving loss of immune tolerance, deposition of immune complexes in tissues, inflammation and type I IFN production, but in CLE the inflammation and damage are restricted to the skin.11,13 There are currently no targeted treatments for CLE.11

Toll-like receptors 7 and 8 (TLR7/8) are endosomal pattern-recognition receptors that play a key role in the innate and adaptive immune responses to single-stranded RNA viruses.14 15 TLR7/8 are expressed in a range of immune cells and their downstream effects include the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (including IFN-α), the activation of antibody-secreting B cells and production of autoantibodies.15 16 Despite these protective effects, aberrant activation of TLR7/8 by endogenous nucleic acids may drive autoimmune diseases including lupus, with the pathogenic role of TLR7 confirmed by gain-of-function mutations that cause human lupus.1415 17,19 Dual TLR7/8 inhibitors are therefore currently under investigation for the treatment of diseases driven by overactivation of this pathway.20,22

Enpatoran is an orally administered, first-in-class, small molecule that binds to TLR7 and TLR8 with high selectivity, potently inhibiting downstream TLR7/8 signalling in immune cells to modulate key innate and adaptive immune processes involved in lupus pathogenesis.20 Preclinical and early clinical studies showed that enpatoran reduces the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin 6 and IFN-α in a dose-dependent manner.20 21 23 Enpatoran was efficacious in mouse models of lupus, improving survival, kidney damage, autoantibody levels and interferon gene signature (IFN-GS).20 Efficacy in mouse models that are not driven by TLR7/8 (IFN-α-accelerated NZB/W) and the reduction of autoantibodies against DNA-containing antigens (such as anti-double stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA)) suggest that the broad mechanism of action of enpatoran may benefit a wide range of patients, not just those with autoantibodies against RNA-containing antigens.20 Preclinical evidence suggests that TLR7/8 activation is involved in glucocorticoid resistance and enpatoran may enable glucocorticoid tapering due to synergistic effects that increase the overall effectiveness of dexamethasone in an inflammatory setting.24 Enpatoran was well tolerated in previous clinical studies, including two phase I studies in healthy volunteers and a phase II study in patients hospitalised with COVID-19 pneumonia.21 23 25

The objective of this phase Ib study was to evaluate the safety and pharmacokinetics (PK) of multiple-ascending doses of enpatoran in patients with active SLE and CLE. An initial evaluation of clinical response and pharmacodynamics (PD) was also conducted.

Methods

Study design

In this randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multiple-ascending dose phase Ib study of orally administered enpatoran in patients with active SLE and CLE, patients were screened at nine centres in Bulgaria, Germany, Moldova, North Macedonia, Spain and Ukraine (ClinicalTrial.gov identifier: NCT04647708). All patients provided informed consent before participation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Council for International Organisations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, applicable International Conference on Harmonisation Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, and applicable laws and regulations.

The study consisted of two parts (online supplemental figure 1). Part A included three dose-escalating cohorts (enpatoran 25 mg, 50 mg and 100 mg two times per day or placebo two times per day), with each subsequent cohort activated after a cumulative safety data review from >6 weeks of treatment in the previous cohort. Part B was optional, activated after safety data were available from the 12-week treatment period for at least eight patients in the enpatoran 100 mg two times per day cohort, and included one cohort (enpatoran 150 mg two times per day or placebo two times per day). Patients were randomised to enpatoran or placebo (3:1) within each cohort. The treatment period was 12 weeks for the first two cohorts (enpatoran 25 mg and 50 mg two times per day) and was extended to 24 weeks for the last two cohorts (enpatoran 100 mg and 150 mg two times per day). This treatment period extension was specified in a protocol amendment prior to completion of the study to enable the collection of additional safety and clinical response information. All patients completed a 2-week safety follow-up period following treatment completion.

Enpatoran or matching placebo tablets were self-administered at a set time twice per day (every 12 hours±2 hours). For Part B, patients remained under onsite observation for the first 28 hours. Patients were monitored with a phone call after 1 week of study intervention, followed by biweekly study visits during the 12-week treatment period, and monthly visits thereafter for patients with a 24-week treatment period. If a study visit was planned, the study intervention was administered onsite after the scheduled predose study assessments had been performed.

Participants

Eligible patients were aged 18–75 years with active SLE or CLE and a disease duration of at least 6 months (online supplemental table 1). Patients with SLE fulfilled the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics criteria or at least four of the 1997 American College of Rheumatology classification criteria, had a Cutaneous Lupus Erythematosus Disease Area and Severity Index Activity Scale (CLASI-A) Score of ≥6 and/or at least one active Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) manifestation. In addition, patients with SLE were positive for antinuclear and/or anti-dsDNA antibodies during the screening period. Patients with CLE had a diagnosis of SCLE or DLE in their medical history (preferably including biopsy) and a CLASI-A Score of ≥6. Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosis of an autoimmune, rheumatic or dermatological disease other than SLE or CLE, drug-induced lupus, significant cardiovascular events or active cardiac arrhythmia, or a history of malignancy in the past 10 years. Permitted standard of care therapy included stable doses of corticosteroids (up to a prednisone-equivalent dose of 15 mg/day), antimalarials (hydroxychloroquine 400 mg/day or chloroquine 500 mg/day) and one non-biologic immunosuppressant.

Randomisation and blinding

The study was double-blind. Following enrolment by study investigators and completion of the screening period, patients were centrally allocated to either enpatoran two times per day or placebo two times per day. This allocation was done using interactive voice and web response systems along with a computer-generated randomisation list in a 3:1 ratio within each cohort. Due to the small size of the study, and the primary objective being to evaluate safety and tolerability, no stratification factors were used.

Enpatoran and placebo tablets were identical in physical appearance. Safety and PK data were provided to the Safety Monitoring Committee for review prior to each dose escalation step. The Safety Monitoring Committee was blinded to treatment.

Clinical endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoint was safety and tolerability in patients with SLE or CLE. Secondary endpoints included PK and changes in SLEDAI-2K Score, Physician’s Global Assessment (PGA) Score, 28-joint count and CLASI-A Score through week 12/24 (depending on the treatment period for each cohort). Effects on lupus biomarkers, including IFN-GS Scores and serum IFN-α levels, were evaluated as exploratory endpoints.

Safety was continually assessed throughout the study by evaluating adverse events, clinical laboratory results, ECGs, electroencephalogram (EEG) and vital signs. For SLE participants with a 12-week treatment period, SLEDAI-2K, PGA and 28-joint count were assessed at screening, day 1, and weeks 4, 12 (end of treatment) and 14 (end of safety follow-up). CLASI-A was evaluated at the same visits plus weeks 2 and 8 in all patients. For SLE participants with a 24-week treatment period, SLEDAI-2K, PGA and 28-joint count were evaluated at screening, day 1, and weeks 4, 12, 20, 24 (end of treatment) and 26 (end of safety follow-up). CLASI-A was evaluated at the same visits plus weeks 2, 8, 16 and 20 in all patients.

Blood samples were collected for PK and/or PD assessments at study visits on day 1, weeks 2, 4, 8, 12/24 (end of treatment) and 14/26 (end of safety follow-up). Serial PK samples were taken up to 10 hours after dosing on day 1 and up to 6 hours after dosing on day 29. Serum levels of IFN-α were evaluated predose by an independent analytical laboratory (Myriad RBM, Salt Lake City, Utah, USA) using Quanterix’s single molecule array (Simoa) technology and a fully automated immunoassay platform (Simoa HD-1 Analyzer). Expression of type I IFN-GS was measured in whole blood using the Modular Immune Profile Test Kit (DxTerity, Rancho Dominguez, California, USA). This test measures the relative gene expression of four IFN response genes (HERC5, IFI27, IFIT1 and RSAD2) relative to three housekeeping genes (CTB, GAPDH and TFRC). The cut-off between high and low IFN-GS Scores (−0.5) was set to the mean plus two SDs (ie, the 95th percentile) of IFN-GS Scores from a cohort of healthy human donors (data not shown).

Statistical analysis

Given the exploratory nature of the study, the sample size was not based on formal statistical testing. The sample size was typical for this type of study and was expected to provide sufficient safety and tolerability data to achieve the objective of the study, while exposing as few patients as possible to the study intervention and procedures.

Safety, clinical response and PD data were described descriptively, and enpatoran plasma concentrations were analysed using non-compartmental methods to determine relevant PK parameters. Patients receiving placebo were pooled into a single group and, unless otherwise specified, patients with SLE and CLE were also pooled. Missing data were not imputed.

The safety analysis set included all patients who were administered at least one dose of study intervention. These analyses considered patients as treated. Evaluation of clinical response was based on the full analysis set, which included all randomised patients with at least one postdose measurement of CLASI-A, SLEDAI-2K, 28-joint count or PGA. Patients in the full analysis set were analysed according to the treatment assigned at randomisation. The PK and PD analysis sets included all patients who received at least one dose of enpatoran, with no significant protocol deviations or events affecting PK or PD, respectively, and at least one measurable postdose reading (in addition to a baseline reading for the PD set). Patients in these analysis sets were analysed per the actual study intervention they received.

Results

Study population

The study was conducted between 16 December 2020 and 19 December 2023. A total of 46 patients were screened for eligibility and 25 were randomised to receive either placebo (n=6), enpatoran 25 mg two times per day (n=4), enpatoran 50 mg two times per day (n=3), enpatoran 100 mg two times per day (n=9) or enpatoran 150 mg two times per day (n=3; figure 1). All 25 patients were included in the safety, full and PD analysis sets, and the 19 patients who received enpatoran were included in the PK analysis set. Twenty-two (88%) patients completed the study treatment and three (12%) discontinued the study due to relocation (n=1) or the war in Ukraine (n=2). The majority of patients completed the full course of enpatoran treatment (mean±SD compliance with treatment: 99%±3.4%).

The majority (80%) of patients were female and all were white, with a median (Q1 to Q3) age of 44 (36 to 53) years (table 1). The primary diagnosis was SLE for 18 (72%) patients and CLE for 7 (28%) patients, with a median (Q1 to Q3) disease duration of 6.4 (3.1 to 14.3) years and 7.3 (4.0 to 17.0) years, respectively. All patients used concomitant medications. Overall, 76% (n=19) used hydroxychloroquine, 64% (n=16) used systemic glucocorticoids and 24% (n=6) used other immunosuppressants (methotrexate, n=3; azathioprine, n=2; mycophenolate mofetil, n=1), with no differences across groups that would be expected to influence the results.

There was heterogeneity in diagnoses, baseline disease activity and biomarker status between treatment groups (table 1). A higher proportion of patients in the enpatoran 100 mg two times per day group had CLE (44% vs 17%) and high IFN-GS Scores (89% vs 17%) compared with the placebo group. The proportion of patients with SCLE and DLE varied across groups: placebo, SCLE 17% (n=1); 100 mg two times per day, DLE 44% (n=4); 150 mg two times per day, SCLE 33% (n=1) and DLE 33% (n=1). All other groups had no patients with CLE. Median (Q1 to Q3) SLEDAI-2K Scores for those with an SLE diagnosis ranged from 6.0 (6.0 to 6.0) in the enpatoran 150 mg two times per day group to 20.0 (14.0 to 24.0) in the enpatoran 25 mg two times per day group, indicating moderate to severe disease activity. Musculoskeletal (arthritis) and skin manifestations were the most prevalent in patients with SLE based on SLEDAI-2K subscore domain scores at the screening visit (online supplemental table 2). Median (Q1 to Q3) CLASI-A Scores for patients with SLE and CLE ranged from 6.0 (5.0 to 10.0) in the enpatoran 50 mg two times per day group to 18.0 (1.0 to 19.0) in the enpatoran 150 mg two times per day group, with a broad range of scores in several of the groups.

Primary endpoint

Safety

Eight (42%) patients receiving enpatoran across the four dose groups and two (33%) receiving placebo reported at least one treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) by week 12 (table 2). The incidence of TEAEs did not increase with dose or treatment duration, with only one additional patient in the 100 mg two times per day and 150 mg two times per day groups reporting at least one TEAE between weeks 12 and 24. The most common TEAEs were gastrointestinal disorders and infections (online supplemental tables 3 and 4).

All TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity (grade 1/2), other than one grade 4 TEAE of worsening lymphopenia in the enpatoran 25 mg two times per day group, which was considered unrelated to treatment by the investigator. Three (12%) patients experienced at least one TEAE that was considered treatment related; these were dry mouth (100 mg two times per day, n=1), nausea (100 mg two times per day, n=1) and upper respiratory tract infection (150 mg two times per day, n=1; table 2). All treatment-related TEAEs occurred by week 12. Two (8%) patients receiving enpatoran had a TEAE that led to temporary discontinuation of treatment by week 12 (25 mg two times per day, n=1; 150 mg two times per day, n=1) and one patient in the placebo group temporarily discontinued treatment due to a TEAE between weeks 12 and 24. This includes a TEAE of upper respiratory tract infection in the 150 mg two times per day group that was considered treatment related.

There were no deaths, serious TEAEs, adverse events of special interest or dose-limiting toxicities during the study, and no changes in ECG or EEG parameters of clinical concern over time.

Secondary endpoints

Pharmacokinetics

Enpatoran plasma concentrations and PK parameters increased in an approximately dose-proportional manner with increasing dose from 25 mg to 150 mg two times per day (figure 2A). The median time to peak concentration was between 1 hour and 2 hours postdose, and the apparent half-life of enpatoran was 6–10 hours across dose levels. After 100 mg two times per day dosing on day 29, the geometric mean accumulation, based on the maximum observed concentration (Cmax) and trough concentration, was 1.33 and 1.86, respectively. Further, the geometric mean Cmax was 188 ng/mL and area under the concentration–time curve from dosing time to 12 hours postdose (AUC0-12h) was 1450 hours × ng/mL.

Clinical response

Preliminary evaluation of clinical response focused on the enpatoran 100 mg two times per day group due to the larger sample size and longer treatment duration than the other enpatoran groups. Median SLEDAI-2K Score, PGA Score and 28-joint count were similar between the placebo (n=6) and enpatoran 100 mg two times per day (n=9) groups in patients with SLE at baseline (table 1). At week 24, a numerically greater reduction from baseline in median SLEDAI-2K Score was observed with enpatoran 100 mg two times per day (median: −6; Q1 to Q3: −10 to −2) compared with placebo (median: −1; Q1 to Q3: −2 to 0; figure 3A). Similar results were observed for PGA Score and the number of tender joints, with respective median (Q1 to Q3) changes from baseline of −1.5 (−1.6 to −0.7) and −8 (−10 to −6) with enpatoran 100 mg two times per day versus −0.4 (−0.5 to −0.3) and −2 (−5 to −1) with placebo (figure 3B,C).

CLASI-A Scores decreased from baseline to the end of treatment in all groups, including placebo. At week 24, median (Q1 to Q3) change from baseline was −7.0 (−11.0 to −5.0) in the placebo group and −6.0 (−18.0 to −5.0) in the enpatoran 100 mg two times per day group (online supplemental figure 2). However, interpretation of CLASI-A Scores with enpatoran versus placebo was limited by the wide range of CLASI-A Scores at baseline in the enpatoran 100 mg two times per day arm (median: 12; min: 4; max: 29) and the unbalanced distribution of patients with SLE and CLE. In the placebo group, there was one patient (17%) with CLE (SCLE subtype) and five patients (83%) with SLE compared with four patients (44%) with CLE (all DLE subtype) and five patients (56%) with SLE in the enpatoran 100 mg group (table 1).

Exploratory endpoints

Pharmacodynamics

The proportion of patients with high IFN-GS Scores at baseline varied across groups, ranging from 17% in the placebo group to 100% in the enpatoran 50 mg two times per day and 150 mg two times per day groups (table 1). Mean (SD) IFN-GS Scores at baseline were −1.66 (1.98) in the placebo group and 1.24 (2.26), 1.50 (1.40), 1.07 (1.89) and 0.04 (0.52) in the enpatoran 25 mg, 50 mg, 100 mg and 150 mg two times per day groups, respectively. While IFN-GS Scores remained generally unchanged over time in the placebo group, most patients receiving enpatoran had strong, dose-dependent IFN-GS suppression (figure 2B). By week 2, all patients in the enpatoran 100 mg and 150 mg two times per day groups had low IFN-GS Scores, with mean (SD) scores of −2.18 (1.51) and −3.14 (0.97), respectively. Maximal IFN-GS suppression was observed by week 4; mean (SD) change from baseline was −3.26 (1.84) in the enpatoran 100 mg two times per day group and −3.77 (0.79) in the enpatoran 150 mg two times per day group, compared with 0.16 (1.60) in the placebo group. This was sustained to week 24 in both enpatoran groups (100 mg two times per day: −3.70±1.87; 150 mg two times per day: −3.54±1.04; placebo: 1.00±0.95). At week 26, 2 weeks following treatment withdrawal, IFN-GS Scores increased in both enpatoran groups, almost to baseline levels.

Consistent with the IFN-GS data, baseline levels of serum IFN-α were highly variable across groups; mean (SD) IFN-α levels ranged from 0.13 (0.18) pg/mL in the placebo group to 25.02 (31.93) pg/mL in the enpatoran 50 mg two times per day group. Despite this variability, substantial suppression of IFN-α was observed, which increased with enpatoran dose. Mean (SD) per cent change from baseline at week 4 was 30% (112%) with placebo, −78% (35%) with enpatoran 100 mg two times per day, and −86% (5%) with enpatoran 150 mg two times per day. This level of suppression was sustained through 24 weeks.

Discussion

In this study, patients with active SLE or active CLE (SCLE and DLE subtypes) on standard of care therapy received placebo or enpatoran two times per day at doses of 25 mg, 50 mg, 100 mg and 150 mg. The primary goal was to investigate the safety of enpatoran in patients with active SLE and CLE. In line with previous clinical studies,21 23 25 enpatoran was well tolerated and demonstrated favourable safety and PK profiles. Despite the small number of patients, the results suggest that the incidence and severity of TEAEs did not increase with increasing dose or treatment duration up to 150 mg two times per day for 24 weeks. Further, the fact that no safety concerns were observed despite high proportions of patients using antimalarials and systemic corticosteroids is reassuring given that several agents are often used concomitantly in the management of lupus. Enpatoran PK appeared dose proportional, with maximum plasma concentration reached rapidly and an apparent half-life of 6–10 hours.

TLR7/8 inhibitors are a novel class of drugs in development for a range of autoimmune diseases. Enpatoran is a first-in-class, orally administered dual TLR7/8 inhibitor that may modulate innate and adaptive immune processes central to lupus pathophysiology.20 21 In preclinical studies, enpatoran suppressed disease development in mouse models of lupus that are not driven by TLR7/8, suggesting it has a broad mechanism of action that may benefit a wide range of patients.20 Further, enpatoran increased the effectiveness of glucocorticoids in a preclinical inflammatory setting, which may allow reduction or tapering of steroid dose, addressing a significant unmet need in lupus management.4 24

This small exploratory trial provided an initial evaluation of clinical response with enpatoran in a heterogeneous setting, as per the study design. Encouraging results were observed compared with placebo across measures of global disease activity (SLEDAK-2K and PGA) and joint pain (28-joint count). The IFN pathway is dysregulated in many patients with lupus and overexpression of IFN-regulated genes has been associated with more severe disease in patients with SLE, SCLE and DLE.26 27 In an exploratory analysis of IFN-GS Scores and serum IFN-α levels, rapid reductions were observed with enpatoran treatment in a dose-dependent manner and sustained to the end of treatment. Two weeks following enpatoran withdrawal, IFN-GS Scores returned close to baseline levels, likely due to the reversibility of enpatoran binding to TLR7/8 and supporting the concept that high type I IFN in lupus is driven by TLR7/8.

Taken together, the clinical response and biomarker results from this study may support the therapeutic targeting of TLR7/8 for lupus. Enpatoran has a broader mechanism of action than current biologics for SLE, targeting B cells, IFN and other key drivers of lupus, which may translate to clinical significance.20 Further, the oral administration and potential for glucocorticoid dose tapering may bring additional benefits to patients.21 24 The phase II WILLOW Study (NCT05162586) will assess the efficacy and safety of enpatoran in a larger population of patients with SLE and CLE, evaluate the efficacy of enpatoran in glucocorticoid sparing and define the optimal therapeutic dose regimen for future studies.

The findings from this exploratory study must be considered preliminary due to several limitations. First, the sample size was small. It therefore was not possible to conclusively determine if there was any relationship between enpatoran dose and treatment duration with adverse effects. Second, the study included only white patients, which impacts the generalisability of the findings. Third, heterogeneity of the patient population limited interpretation of clinical response and biomarker results, particularly the prespecified secondary endpoint of change in CLASI-A Scores. Finally, the cohorts are not randomised by design, which can introduce bias when comparing dose levels.

In conclusion, in the first clinical trial in patients with lupus, enpatoran was well tolerated up to the maximum evaluated dose of 150 mg two times per day for 24 weeks and demonstrated favourable safety and PK profiles. The preliminary evaluation of clinical response and biomarkers suggests a pharmacological effect and supports the continued evaluation of enpatoran for patients with lupus in the phase II WILLOW Study.
