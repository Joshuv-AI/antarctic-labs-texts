Background

Pulmonary Alveolar Proteinosis (PAP) is a diffuse lung disease characterized by the “crazy paving” pattern on chest CT scan and accumulation of lipoproteinaceous material in the alveoli. While in most cases, PAP is associated with autoantibodies against granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), PAP could occur secondary to infection, immune deficiency or other environmental factors. Bacillus megaterium is a Gram-positive, aerobic, spore-forming, and rod-shaped bacterium widely distributed in the environment. It has been used in agriculture due to its ability to decompose organic phosphorus compounds. Generally, B. megaterium is known to be non-pathogenic or, at least, of very low virulence. Here we describe a rare case of secondary PAP caused by B. megaterium infection.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old female patient was referred with intermittent cough and shortness of breath presented half a year ago. She had a history of hypertension and coronary artery disease. She sought medical attention at local hospital, where routine blood tests and sputum pathogen screening were all negative. Chest CT showed multifocal patchy ground-glass opacities in both lungs and bilateral pleural thickening. She went through twice bronchoscopy examination. Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) showed opaque and milky-white appearance. BALF-mNGS suggested infections with Haemophilus influenzae (sequence count 12559) and Klebsiella oxytoca (sequence count 1287), with no evidence of fungal or virus infection. She was subsequently treated with Methylprednisolone for a week and Cefoperazone-Sulbactam plus Voriconazole for half a month, yet no significant improvement was observed.

On examination, the patient was afebrile, and presented with the following vital signs: temperature, 36 °C; heart rate, 96 beats/min; respirations, 18 breaths/min; BP, 124/68 mmHg; and oxygen saturation, 94% on room air. Bilateral end-expiratory wheezes and crackles were present on lung auscultation at lower lung fields. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable.

The routine laboratory tests showed elevated inflammatory markers, including a CRP level of 101.87 mg/L and ESR level of 74 ml/h. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) reached 541U/L and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) was 4.38 mmol/L. Two tumor markers were slightly elevated (CEA 30.8 ng/mL and Cyfra21-1 14 ng/mL, respectively). Tests for serum antibodies revealed positive antinuclear antibody (titer 1:80) and negative anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies to myeloperoxidase and proteinase-3. Arterial blood gas indicated lower PaO2 of 65 mmHg and higher alveolar-arterial gradient of 41.3 mmHg. Other routine blood tests including complete blood count, biochemistry, urinalysis, HIV test, C3 and C4 levels were normal. Pulmonary function tests (PFT) demonstrated restrictive ventilatory impairment and severe diffusion limitation with total lung capacity 56% predicted and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide 35% predicted. High resolution chest CT showed “crazy-paving” pattern symmetrically in bilateral lung fields that consisted of septal line thickening superimposed on areas of ground glass opacity. Localized consolidation could be found on the left upper lobe (Fig. 1).

A, B, and C Radiologic images pulmonary alveolar proteinosis. High-resolution CT image shows a “crazy-paving” pattern that consists of septal line thickening superimposed on areas of ground glass opacity and atypical localized consolidation on the left upper lobe

Due to the atypical presence of the left upper lobe, percutaneous CT-guided needle biopsy was performed for definitive diagnosis. The histologic result suggested granular eosinophilic material and macrophages filled in airspaces. Special staining suggested weakly positive Periodic acid-Schiff-diastase stain and Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining, and negative mucicarmine mucin stain (Fig. 2). No granulomas were observed. Bacterial culture finally indicated the presence of B. megaterium (bacterial count 2 +).

A and B Histologic images of pulmonary alveolar proteinosis. A Filled alveolar spaces by granular and proteinaceous material (hematoxylin and eosin stain; original magnification, × 200). B Periodic acid-Schiff highlights the proteinaceous material (original magnification, × 100)

Based on her CT scan, histologic result and bacterial culture, the diagnosis of PAP was confirmed. The patient was treated with clindamycin intravenously for eight days and switched to amoxicillin-clavulanate orally. GM-CSF antibody level was further tested and reached borderline significant (4.5 μg/ml, > 4 μg/ml is considered as autoimmune PAP). Considering the relatively low level of GM-CSF antibody and acceptable oxygen saturation, GM-CSF therapy and Whole-lung lavage (WLL) were postponed. The patient has been taken amoxicillin-clavulanate 0.375 g ter in die since then and her situation remained stable. The follow-up chest CT a month later showed significant improvement (Fig. 3).

Discussion and conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first adult PAP secondary to B. megaterium infection described in the medical literature. Previous literature has reported few cases of B. megaterium infection in certain sites including the cornea, soft tissue of the skin, brain, and pleura [1]. Most occurred in immunocompromised hosts with triggers like medical surgery or trauma. All patients could be completely cured with properly prolonged antimicrobial treatment.

PAP is a diffuse lung disease characterized by the accumulation of lipoproteinaceous material in the alveoli and dysfunction of alveolar macrophages. It occurs in about 7 people per million in the general population. PAP can be classified into different types according to the pathogenetic mechanism. Primary PAP is caused by the disruption of GM-CSF signaling and could be further divided into autoimmune and hereditary form. Secondary PAP results from various underlying conditions including malignancies and infections. Congenital PAP is caused by mutations in genes involved in surfactant production.

Common symptoms of PAP include shortness of breath, cough and chest pain, while up to a third of patients can be asymptomatic. Crackles might be present but are not universal. Antibodies to GM-CSF can be identified in almost all autoimmune PAP, both in the serum and BALF. Other potential serological markers including LDH, CEA, Cyfra21-1 have been investigated as surrogates of disease severity and progression, but clinical availability, sensitivity and specificity remain unknown. PFT typically reveal restrictive impairment and reduction of diffusing capacity. High resolution CT scan usually presents “crazy-paving” pattern characterized by diffuse, ground-glass opacities with septal thickening and subpleural sparing. BALF demonstrates opaque, milky appearance. Histologic evaluation shows granular eosinophilic material filling alveolar spaces that is positive for PAS staining. The diagnosis of PAP is made by specific CT scan, BALF and histologic patterns. For the treatment of PAP, besides oxygen therapy and smoking cessation, some patients with mild symptoms may experience spontaneous remission. Inhaled GM-CSF therapy was particularly aimed at autoimmune PAP. Treatment of underlying disorders should be considered in patients with secondary causes. Whole-lung lavage (WLL) is the cornerstone of PAP therapy when patients have severe hypoxemia or intrapulmonary shunt and no remission was achieved by treatments mentioned above [2, 3].

In the setting of chronic infection, treatment of infection alone may improve patients’ condition. Patients with PAP are also at increased risk of secondary infection with common and opportunistic pathogens. Due to the marginally elevated GM-CSF antibody, this could be a case of autoimmune PAP with secondary infection. A previous study showed the commonly reported opportunistic infections in patients with PAP included nocardiosis, mycobacteria and fungi [4]. B. megaterium infection was seldom reported. Antimicrobial or antifungal therapy together with WLL should be considered. In either case, once infection is confirmed, antimicrobial therapy is recommended and improvement could be expected.

In conclusion, PAP should be considered in apparently immunocompetent patients who present with an opportunistic infection like B. megaterium. PAP can be a focal histologic finding, which may not always be detected on BAL or transbronchial biopsy. Apart from mNGS, traditional method like percutaneous needle biopsy or surgical biopsies should not be neglected.
