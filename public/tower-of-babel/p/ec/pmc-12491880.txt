Introduction

Adnexal masses discovered during pregnancy present unique diagnostic and management challenges, particularly when found incidentally during cesarean delivery. Most ovarian lesions encountered in this setting are benign and asymptomatic, with management decisions often made intraoperatively. Among benign tumors, mucinous cystadenomas account for approximately 15–25 % of ovarian neoplasms and are typically discovered during routine imaging or surgery.

On the other hand, ovarian stromal Leydig cell hyperplasia is a rare histological entity characterized by clusters of androgen-producing Leydig cells within the ovarian stroma. While typically associated with hyperandrogenic symptoms, its presentation in pregnancy—especially in the absence of virilization—is uncommon and poorly understood.

The coexistence of mucinous cystadenoma and stromal Leydig cell hyperplasia is extremely rare, with only a handful of such cases documented in the literature. The hormonal environment of pregnancy, particularly the elevated levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), may play a role in the development or progression of such stromal changes. However, the pathophysiological relationship remains speculative.

In this report, we present an unusual case of an asymptomatic pregnant woman in whom this dual pathology was discovered incidentally during cesarean section. The case highlights the importance of intraoperative vigilance, the role of histopathological evaluation, and the potential influence of pregnancy-related hormonal factors in rare ovarian pathology. This case has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [11].

Case presentation

A 21-year-old woman, gravida 2 para 1, with no significant past medical history, presented at 38 + 4 weeks gestation for elective cesarean section due to a previous lower segment cesarean delivery. She reported no abdominal pain, distension, or symptoms suggestive of hyperandrogenism such as hirsutism, acne, or voice changes during pregnancy. Her antenatal course was uncomplicated. Physical examination on admission showed a healthy appearing woman with stable vital signs (blood pressure 112/70 mmHg, heart rate 84 bpm, afebrile). Abdominal examination was consistent with term pregnancy without palpable adnexal masses. Routine prenatal ultrasounds performed earlier in pregnancy did not identify adnexal pathology, likely due to obscuration by the gravid uterus.

During the cesarean section, performed under spinal anesthesia by a senior obstetric consultant, an incidental left ovarian cyst was identified. The cyst measured approximately 7 × 5 cm, was unilocular with a smooth external surface, and showed no signs of torsion, rupture, or malignancy. A cystectomy was performed, carefully shelling out the cyst while preserving normal ovarian parenchyma. The uterus and contralateral right ovary were inspected and appeared normal, with no cysts or surface abnormalities. No salpingo-oophorectomy was performed, as ovarian preservation was prioritized in this young patient.

Histopathological examination of the excised mass revealed a benign mucinous cystadenoma with stromal Leydig cell hyperplasia. There was no atypia or evidence of malignant transformation. The patient showed no clinical signs of virilization or hyperandrogenism. Serum androgen levels were not measured, as there were no clinical indications and hormonal assessments during pregnancy may be misleading.

Postoperatively, the patient had an uneventful recovery. She was discharged on postoperative day two in stable condition and began exclusive breastfeeding. No additional interventions were required, and follow-up was planned through routine postpartum visits.

The recommended next steps in management after delivery included routine postpartum follow-up with clinical review and pelvic ultrasound at 6–12 weeks to assess for recurrence or new adnexal pathology. As the cyst was benign and completely excised, no additional treatment was required.

Discussion

This case highlights the rare coexistence of a mucinous cystadenoma and stromal Leydig cell hyperplasia in a pregnant patient, discovered incidentally during cesarean section. While mucinous cystadenomas are among the more common benign epithelial ovarian tumors [1], the simultaneous presence of stromal Leydig cell hyperplasia—particularly in an asymptomatic pregnant woman—is extraordinarily rare.

Adnexal masses are occasionally encountered during pregnancy and cesarean delivery, with incidence rates ranging from 0.1 % to 2.4 % [2,12]. Most are benign and asymptomatic, but intraoperative findings may necessitate immediate decision-making regarding surgical management. Management is guided by size, appearance, and gestational age, with intraoperative decisions made on a case-by-case basis. In our patient, the cyst appeared benign and well-circumscribed; therefore, cystectomy was performed to prevent future complications and enable definitive histopathological diagnosis [3].

Mucinous cystadenomas are multilocular cystic tumors lined by mucin-secreting epithelium. They constitute approximately 15–25 % of all ovarian neoplasms and are often asymptomatic, discovered incidentally during imaging or surgery [1].

Histopathological analysis of the excised mass revealed a benign mucinous cystadenoma, lined by mucinous columnar epithelium, with underlying proliferation of polygonal stromal cells arranged in nests and cords. These cells displayed round nuclei and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, characteristic of Leydig cells. Immunohistochemical staining showed strong cytoplasmic and nuclear positivity for Calretinin, confirming the diagnosis of stromal Leydig cell hyperplasia (Fig. A, Fig. B, Fig. C, Fig. D). The absence of atypia, mitotic figures, or architectural complexity ruled out neoplastic transformation or steroid cell tumor [[4], [5], [6]]. This distinction is critical, as morphologically bland stromal proliferations may mimic neoplasms, and immunohistochemistry plays a key role in confirming a benign hyperplastic process [13].

Leydig cells are normally found in small numbers in the ovarian hilum and are responsible for androgen production. Hyperplasia of these cells in the ovarian stroma is rare and may be hormonally driven. During pregnancy, elevated levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)—which mimics luteinizing hormone (LH)—may stimulate proliferation of Leydig cells within hormonally responsive tissues [8]. This provides a plausible explanation for the stromal changes observed in our case.

Despite the histological presence of Leydig cells, the patient displayed no clinical signs of androgen excess such as hirsutism, acne, or deepening of the voice. Several factors may account for this: 1) insufficient androgen production by the hyperplastic cells; 2) elevated levels of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) during pregnancy binding circulating androgens; or 3) local hormone production with minimal systemic effect [7]. Therefore, serum androgen levels were not assessed postoperatively.

Only a handful of cases describing mucinous cystadenomas with stromal Leydig cell hyperplasia or luteinization have been reported. The earliest known example was published by Pascal and Grecco in 1988, who described a similar lesion discovered during cesarean section [9]. Since then, very few comparable cases have been documented, making this an exceptionally rare histopathological finding [10].

This case emphasizes the importance of intraoperative vigilance and the value of histopathological evaluation—including immunohistochemistry—when incidental adnexal masses are encountered during pregnancy. Accurate pathological diagnosis is essential not only for ruling out malignancy but also for understanding the potential hormonal dynamics of ovarian stromal changes in the gravid state.

Conclusion

This case underscores three key clinical lessons. First, obstetric surgeons must maintain a high index of suspicion for adnexal pathology during cesarean delivery, and careful intraoperative evaluation can facilitate timely and safe management of incidental ovarian masses. Second, pregnancy-related hormonal changes—particularly elevated hCG—may play a role in stimulating stromal elements such as Leydig cells within otherwise benign neoplasms, even in the absence of systemic symptoms. Third, thorough histopathological examination, including immunohistochemistry, is essential to accurately diagnose rare ovarian lesions, inform follow-up, and avoid misclassification of benign hyperplasia as a neoplastic process. This case contributes to the sparse literature on ovarian stromal changes during pregnancy and highlights the need for increased awareness and reporting of such findings.
