Investigation of nano-particle effects on cold storage performance using finite element modeling
Authors: Khalid H Almitani, Ali Basem, Hussein A Z Al-bonsrulah, Mashhour A Alazwari, Nidal H Abu-Hamdeh, Ahmad H Milyani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work examines the optimization of additive materials to enhance the solidification rate in a cold energy storage system equipped with a finned enclosure. Nanometer-scale particles were dispersed into the water-based medium to promote faster freezing. The system performance was evaluated by numerically investigating different nanoparticle volume fractions and particle diameters to assess their effects on the solidification process. The governing equations were solved using the Galerkin finite element approach, and the numerical predictions showed strong agreement with available experimental observations. An adaptive meshing strategy was applied to accurately resolve the transient characteristics of the freezing front. In contrast to earlier investigations that primarily examined the influence of nanoparticle concentration or storage unit configuration, the present work provides a systematic numerical assessment of the combined influence of nanoparticle diameter and concentration on the solidification characteristics of a finned cold energy storage system. An adaptive Galerkin finite element framework is employed to accurately capture the transient evolution of the freezing front and identify optimal nanoparticle conditions for enhanced thermal storage performance. The results indicate that selecting an optimal nanoparticle size significantly improves freezing performance, with a 20% increase in the solidification rate. Furthermore, the dispersing nanoparticles shortened the total freezing duration by about 41.23%, highlighting their effectiveness in enhancing the thermal performance of the storage system.Keywords: Freezing, Cold energy, Galerkin simulation, Solid front, Solidification, Conduction, Finned enclosureSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The need to cut emissions of greenhouse gases to lessen the consequences of climate change is now widely acknowledged. Cutting energy use significantly is necessary to achieve the objective of being carbon neutral by 2050, which aims to slow down the rise in global temperatures1,2. Energy-saving measures are desperately needed, especially when it comes to the energy consumption of buildings and transportation in many European nations, as a result of the ongoing conflict in Ukraine, growing energy prices, and supply issues3,4. Since the building industry consumes the greatest amount of energy, it is imperative to renovate buildings completely, improve how effectively energy systems operate, and make use of solar power, even when it is sporadic5,6. The findings of this evaluation suggest that the usage of PCM-filled cuboid storage cavities in buildings for solar thermal systems has not received enough research attention7,8. The scarcity of industrial solutions might also be attributed to this research deficiency. In order to permit direct heat transmission between the two heat exchangers and maybe even simultaneous charging and discharge (the home hot water system and the solar loop), creative solutions must be developed9,10. Nanoparticles are widely utilized in various usages to improve the efficiency of thermal units10,11. Over the past decade, numerous numerical techniques have been developed for modeling these systems12,13. Sheikholeslami et al.14 explored the combination of NEPCM with a solar panel, in the existence of a nanoparticle-coated glass. They illustrated an 11.2% increase in electrical power with a TiO2 coating. Additionally, they used a mixture of ZnO and paraffin in the storage tank. According to studies by Rathod and Banerjee15, an annular fin added to a vertically oriented double-tube heat exchanger shortened the time PCM required to melt by 25% and to solidify by 44%. While external triangular fins showed a substantially bigger 15% improvement over longitudinal fins, internal triangle fins offered an 11% improvement over the latter. Mohammed et al.16 investigated the heat discharge characteristics of air-based heat exchangers integrated with foam-enhanced PCM systems. They reported that the utilizing metal foam significantly improved the discharge capability of the system, with about a 116% increase in performance at the same flow rate.

Li et al.17 analyzed the phase change behavior of a paraffin-based system and evaluated the effect of periodic flipping. They reported that the enhancement associated with flipping is more considerable when the solid fraction during the freezing process remains below 50%. Sheikholeslami18 scrutinized the enhancement of solar panel performance by incorporating different fin configurations into a paraffin-based thermal storage enclosure. His study found that the highest efficiency was achieved with short-base arrow fins. Chaichan and Kazem19 looked at the integration of PCM with concentrators and distillers and observed that it can enhance these devices’ functioning. The results showed improvements in heating efficiency of 112.86%, parabolic concentrator efficiency of 63.49%, and system output of 306.54%. Abhat et al.20 scrutinized a work on a solar unit using a finned container filled with PCM. The buoyancy effects were significantly reduced by the aluminum tubes, which were radially dispersed throughout the PCM inside a container and constructed as finned tubes. Sheikholeslami and Al-Jammasi21 conducted a numerical analysis to assess the effect of a parabolic reflector on a thermal storage unit integrated with a U-tube collector. Their results showed that employing hybrid nanoparticle-enhanced paraffin together with the reflector significantly improved the phase change process, with the liquid fraction increasing by a factor of 2.31. Abdelgaied et al.‘s experiment22 used PCM positioned underneath the absorber in a distiller. They found that output of distiller boosts about90% with integration of paraffin. Also, wicking a planar liner into a conical shape and employing a nanomaterial have demonstrated promising outputs. Xu et al.23 have introduced a novel model that combines evolutionary techniques for optimization the geometry and boost the charging rate of PCM. It was looked at how PCM melting characteristics were affected by ambient convection. Based on optimization, it was shown that the ideal fin length should be attained together with a slight downward slope if fin thickness was unlimited. Zarma et al.24 investigated the application of nano-enhanced PCM to improve the thermal regulation and performance of concentrated photovoltaic systems. They reported that the PCM containing nanoparticles provided the optimum thermal performance among the investigated cases. Tunçbilek et al.25 evaluated the application of paraffin-based PCM containing aluminum oxide nanoparticles in building ventilation systems. The findings showed that the incorporation of nanoparticles did not always improve thermal performance, as higher nanoparticle concentrations resulted in reduced energy-saving efficiency. Using the Galerkin method, Shafee et al.26 examined the solidification of a PCM combined with metal foam. He revealed that the presence of metal foam considerably improved thermal transport within the PCM domain and reduced the required solidification time by nearly 80%.

The study explores the efficiency of a cold energy storage system enhanced with nanoparticles, focusing on optimizing the size and concentration of these additives to improve efficiency. By incorporating nanoparticles into the storage medium, this research addresses a serious gap in existing literature regarding the enhancement of thermal energy storage systems. Previous research has extensively investigated the use of PCMs and their combination into energy storage units. Present work contributes to filling this gap by systematically analyzing the impacts of various nano-powder diameters (dp) and concentrations (ϕ) on the freezing. The novelty of this work lies in its application of a homogeneous mixture assumption for nanomaterials, simplifying the modeling of complex interactions and focusing on key performance metrics. Additionally, the study utilizes advanced techniques such as the Galerkin method with an implicit scheme and innovative grid generation to enhance the accuracy of simulations. These methodological advancements enable a more precise evaluation of nanoparticle effects on the cold storage system. The results offer valuable insights into selecting optimal nanoparticle characteristics to improve thermal energy storage performance and support the development of more efficient energy management strategies.

Solidification through a finned enclosure

This article explores the treatment of a cold energy storage unit enhanced with nanoparticles (see Fig. 1). The study focuses on optimizing the performance of the storage system by varying two key parameters: the (dp) and (ϕ). These factors are treated as variables in the simulation process to determine their effects on solidification. To model these effects, the properties of the nanoparticles are based on a homogeneous mixture assumption. The conventional momentum equation, which accounts for velocity effects, is excluded from the solidification process due to its negligible impact. Consequently, the model is simplified to include only two primary equations that incorporate unsteady terms to describe the thermal behavior of the system27.

Two scalar quantities—temperature (T) and solid fraction (S)—were monitored for convergence during the simulation, as indicated in Eqs. (1) and (2). The convergence criteria were specified as follows: the residual for temperature was less than 1 × 10− 6, and the residual for solid fraction was less than 1 × 10− 5. These criteria were selected to ensure the accuracy of the results while maintaining computational efficiency. FELX PDE software was utilized in this study for the numerical simulations. The simulations were conducted on a Dell laptop with the following general specifications: 4-core processor, 8GB RAM, Windows 10 operating system, and 512GB SSD storage. These specifications were sufficient to perform the simulations and obtain the results within the required time frame. The value of 125 s represents a reference point for the contour plots at different stages of the freezing, rather than the time step. The time step was dynamically varied between 0.0001 and 0.06 s, with automatic adjustments made by the software as the simulation progressed to ensure both stability and accuracy. The total solidification time was 375 s, and three time stages—125s, 250s, and 375s—were selected to illustrate the progression of the solidification process. These stages were chosen to highlight key phases of the process while maintaining computational efficiency.

The Galerkin method (FLEX PDE software), combined with an implicit technique, is employed for numerical modeling. Additionally, a novel grid generation technique is used to adapt the mesh density dynamically, ensuring better resolution of the solid front as it movements. Current technique enhances the correctness of the simulation by aligning the grid more closely with the evolving phase front. The Galerkin method was first applied to cold storage units by Sheikholeslami27, establishing a benchmark that has been widely referenced in subsequent research. His pioneering work demonstrated the efficacy of this approach in analyzing complex thermal processes in cold storage units. The chief aim of current study is to find the best values of factors that lead to the fastest solidification process. The study concludes by presenting the most effective configuration based on simulation results, highlighting the improvements in performance achieved through the optimized use of nanoparticles.

The Galerkin finite element method was selected for solving the governing equations because of its suitability for transient phase change problems involving complex geometries and moving solid-liquid interfaces. Unlike methods based on fixed grids, the finite element approach provides high flexibility in mesh generation and allows local refinement in regions with steep thermal gradients. This capability is particularly important in the present study, where the solidification interface changes continuously with time. The FLEXPDE platform was employed due to its capability in solving nonlinear partial differential equations using finite element formulations, together with its automatic mesh generation and adaptive refinement features. These characteristics enable efficient computational treatment of the phase change problem while maintaining numerical accuracy. Therefore, the adopted numerical framework provides an effective approach for analyzing nanoparticle-enhanced cold energy storage systems.

Results and discussion

In the present study, the thermal performance of a nanoparticle-enhanced cold energy storage system is comprehensively investigated. The optimization of the diameter (dp) and concentration (ϕ), has been a central focus. The formulation used for nanomaterial properties assumes a homogeneous mixture, simplifying the complex interactions within the nanofluid by assuming uniform dispersion. Due to the negligible impact of velocity, the momentum equation has been excluded from the governing equations for the solidification process. Consequently, the model retains two primary equations that include unsteady state terms. The simulations identify the most effective configurations for nanoparticle size and concentration, highlighting the optimal case that demonstrates substantial developments in cold energy storage productivity. This work underscores the potential of nanoparticle-enhanced systems to advance thermal energy storage.

Figure 2 portrays the mesh configuration for a specific case. The grid style evolves over time, adapting to the fact that areas near the solidification front require a higher density of mesh elements to accurately capture the changes. This adaptive approach ensures that the mesh resolution is finer where it is most needed, enhancing the precision of the simulation. The validation of the model is portrayed in Fig. 3 (a), where the outputs are compared against data of Ref28. The low error margin in the comparison confirms the high accuracy of the current model. For this verification, the same numerical methods were applied to geometries analogous to those used in Ref28., ensuring consistency and reliability. Also, outputs have been compared for another experimental article29(see Fig. 3(b)). Given the successful validation, the model is deemed suitable for investigating new geometries and assessing the effects of incorporating nanopowders. This verification process is crucial as it establishes the credibility of the simulation framework, validating its capability to accurately model complex thermal processes. By confirming the model’s accuracy and reliability, it can now be confidently applied to explore the efficacy of various nanopowder dispersions on the productivity of cold storage systems.

Figures 4 and 5, and 6 portray how the addition of nanopowders enhances the freezing. As additives are mixed with water, the thermal conductivity, which is crucial for solidification, is significantly improved. This increase in conduction results in a more efficient freezing. The addition of nanoparticles generates a stronger thermal driving force, accelerating the movement of the ice front through the solution. As a result, the entire domain reaches lower temperatures more rapidly due to the increased volume of the solid phase. Consequently, the decrement in the water liquid over time leads to a decrease in latent heat, as evidenced by the energy curve (E). For the base case using only water, the time need to achieve full solidification is 633.62 s. However, this time decreases with the increasing concentration of nanopowders (ϕ). This demonstrates that higher concentrations of additives facilitate faster freezing, making the process more efficient.

Figures 7 and 8, and 9 illustrate the efficacy of various nano-powder sizes on the unit’s productivity. Among the three powder sizes evaluated, the nano-powder with a diameter of 40 nm exhibits the best productivity. The distribution of isotherms suggests a conduction-dominated heat transfer regime, evidenced by the alignment of the temperature contours with the system’s geometric boundaries. The initial solidification occurs in the regions adjacent to the elliptic wall and the fins, highlighting the effective role of these structural features. The solid fraction curve (S) for nanoparticles with a diameter of 40 nm shows a steeper slope compared to the others. Conversely, the nanopowder with a diameter of 50 nm results in the lowest slope on the solid fraction curve. The energy profile reveals that the unit with 50 nm nanoparticles has the highest energy content, which decreases over time. The most efficient freezing process is achieved using nanoparticles with a diameter of 40 nm, where the process is completed in just 372.33 s. This demonstrates that the 40 nm nanopowder size significantly enhances the speed of solidification compared to the other sizes tested.

Figure 10 displays the freezing times associated with various levels of (dp) and (ϕ). The most rapid solidification is achieved with nanopowders of diameter 40 nm and concentration ϕ = 0.04, requiring only 372.32 s to reach full solidification. This combination of parameters leads to reduction in freezing time, about 41.23% faster compared to the baseline scenario. When the nanopowder diameter is increased to 50 nm, the freezing time substantially increases, varying between 25.69% and 49.22% longer, depending on the concentration of the additives. This indicates that larger nanopowder sizes negatively impact the efficiency of freezing. Conversely, when the nano-powder diameter is optimized to 40 nm, growing ϕ from 0.02 to 0.04 leads to increase freezing about 19.44%. Notably, the impact of increasing concentration on freezing time is significantly more pronounced for the 40 nm nanopowder sizes, being approximately five times more effective than for the 50 nm size. These outputs underscore the meaning of both nanopowder size and concentration in optimizing the freezing process, with a 40 nm diameter and a higher concentration proving to be the most effective in reducing freezing time.

Conclusion

This study thoroughly examines the performance of a cold energy storage system enhanced with nanoparticles, focusing on improving the size and concentration of these additives. The simulation involved varying (dp) and (ϕ) to assess their influence on system efficiency. By assuming a homogeneous mixture for nanomaterial properties, the complexity of interactions within the nanofluid was simplified, allowing for a clearer analysis of performance. The governing equations for solidification were adjusted by excluding the momentum equation, due to the minimal influence of velocity. Thus, the model primarily involved two unsteady-state equations. The Galerkin technique, combined with an implicit technique, was engaged to solve these equations, while an innovative grid generation approach was used to adaptively refine the mesh around the ice front, enhancing correctness. The results highlight the most effective configurations for nanoparticle size and concentration, showing significant improvements in cold energy storage efficiency. The initial solidification occurs near the elliptic wall and the fins, as evidenced by the contours of the isotherms, which reveal a dominant conduction effect. The isotherms align with the geometric shapes of the container. Among the three nanoparticle sizes investigated, the intermediate size of 40 nm demonstrates superior performance compared to the others. The influence of concentration (ϕ) for a nanoparticle size of 50 nm is approximately five times less effective than for a 40 nm size. This suggests that the optimized size significantly enhances solidification. As the amount of ϕ increases, a stronger driving force is generated. Specifically, increasing ϕ from 0.02 to 0.04 for the optimized nanoparticle size results in decreasing freezing time by around 19.44%. When dp is increased to the 40 nm level, the freezing time declines about 11.7% to 19.98%, depending on the concentration of the additives. The solidification curve (S) for the 40 nm particles exhibits a steeper slope, indicating a more efficient freezing process, whereas the 50 nm particles show the least favorable performance. The reduction in the liquid phase over time correlates with a decrease in latent heat, as reflected in the energy curve (E). The most efficient solidification process is achieved with a concentration of 0.04 and a nanoparticle size of 40 nm, requiring 372.32 s to reach full solidification. This combination of parameters results in a significant deline in freezing time about 41.23%.

Although the present study provides valuable insights into the role of nanoparticle size and concentration in enhancing the solidification performance of a finned cold energy storage system, further investigations are recommended to broaden the applicability of the proposed approach. Future work should focus on experimental validation of the numerical predictions and evaluation of the long-term stability of nanoparticle-enhanced storage media. In addition, the effects of different nanoparticle materials, shapes, and hybrid nanostructures should be investigated to identify more efficient additives for cold energy storage applications. A comprehensive optimization framework considering nanoparticle concentration, thermal enhancement, and potential viscosity penalties would also be beneficial. Finally, extending the proposed model to larger-scale storage units and practical operating conditions can provide further insight into the feasibility of implementing nanoparticle-enhanced cold thermal energy storage systems in real-world applications.
