Transcatheter Closure of a Congenital Gerbode Defect in a Child
Authors: Ana Lucia Madrid-Marin, Ernesto L. Vallejo-Mondragón, Antonio J. Madrid Pinilla, Martin Elias Arrieta Mendoza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Gerbode defect is a rare left ventricular–to–right atrial communication (<1% of congenital heart defects). Percutaneous closure is a well-established alternative to surgery, although pediatric evidence remains limited.Case SummaryA 12-year-old girl with a neonatal-onset holosystolic murmur was lost to follow-up at age 3 after a canceled surgical repair; she returned to care 9 years later after remaining asymptomatic. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed a 4-mm type II congenital Gerbode defect—a perimembranous ventricular septal defect with anomalous tricuspid chordal insertion causing indirect left-to-right shunting. She successfully underwent transcatheter closure using a 4-mm muscular ventricular septal defect occluder.DiscussionThis case demonstrates safety and feasibility of off-label occluder use in pediatric type II Gerbode defects, particularly when cultural beliefs preclude open-heart surgery.Take-Home MessagesTranscatheter closure is safe and effective in selected pediatric Gerbode defects. Detailed echocardiographic assessment is essential for characterization, procedural guidance, and misdiagnosis avoidance.

History of Presentation

A 12-year-old girl from rural Colombia presented to our institution for the closure of a Gerbode defect. A holosystolic murmur was first detected at 21 days of life during a hospitalization for respiratory distress, as documented in contemporaneous neonatal records obtained from the referring institution. This led to an early diagnosis and hydrochlorothiazide was initiated for hemodynamic repercussions. Surgical correction was planned for age 3; however, it was canceled because of another respiratory event. Following this, her parents abandoned conventional medical follow-up, relying instead on traditional medicine (plants and rituals) for the next 9 years.

Despite the lack of intervention, she remained completely asymptomatic, without dyspnea, fatigue, cyanosis, or exercise intolerance, and regularly played soccer. At age 12, health care personnel convinced the mother to return to pediatric cardiology. On physical examination, she was alert and well-appearing with normal vital signs. Auscultation revealed a grade III/VI holosystolic murmur best heard at the left lower sternal border. She was classified as NYHA functional class I.

Past Medical History

Her medical history was significant only for the early diagnosis of the Gerbode defect and a recent prescription of hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg twice daily. She had no prior surgeries and no syndromic features.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the presentation of a holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border and left ventricular dilatation, the differential diagnosis included a perimembranous ventricular septal defect (VSD), severe tricuspid regurgitation in the setting of pulmonary hypertension, and an atrioventricular canal defect.

Investigations

Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated a 4-mm perimembranous VSD associated with an anomalous insertion of the tricuspid valve chordae tendineae, resulting in incomplete valve closure and an indirect left-to-right shunt from the left ventricle across the defective tricuspid valve into the right atrium, consistent with a type II (indirect) congenital Gerbode defect. Color Doppler confirmed a left-to-right shunting with resultant right atrial dilatation and secondary enlargement of the left atrium and left ventricle (Figure 1, Video 1) but overall contractility was normal, and the valves were morphologically and functionally intact. Right and left heart catheterization was performed via a transfemoral approach. Management

Despite the patient's asymptomatic clinical status at presentation, echocardiographic evaluation demonstrated significant left ventricular volume overload with dilated left heart cavities, and a high transseptal gradient on color Doppler confirmed normal pulmonary artery pressures. Combined echocardiographic, radiological, and clinical evidence of pulmonary hyperflow supported the hemodynamic significance of the shunt. These findings were collectively considered sufficient to indicate defect closure without formal pulmonary hemodynamic assessment or a trial of pulmonary vasodilator therapy.

The favorable anatomy and parental refusal of open-heart surgery (associated with their indigenous cultural beliefs) guided the decision toward transcatheter closure. Under general anesthesia and real-time intraoperative TEE and fluoroscopic guidance, the left ventricle was catheterized via the defect. A guidewire was advanced from the left ventricle to the pulmonary artery, and an arteriovenous loop was established (Figure 3, Video 3).Figure 3Fluoroscopic Imaging Demonstrating Established Arteriovenous Loop Technique

Device selection was based on the specific anatomical characteristics of this type II Gerbode defect. The muscular VSD occluder was selected over alternative devices based on the defect's specific anatomy: its symmetric disc design was appropriate given the absence of aortic valve proximity; its longer waist adequately fills the tunnel-like tract created by the partial tricuspid septal closure—which shorter-waist devices cannot reliably occlude. The VSD muscular occluder was preferred above the duct occluder II, reported in some Gerbode defect cases due to its lower profile, because its elongated retention disc could become entrapped within the chordae tendineae of the tricuspid septal leaflet and because the defect diameter was at the upper limit for safe duct occluder II deployment. Considering all, the muscular VSD occluder was a more appropriate and secure choice.

Based on the angiographic and echocardiographic measurements, the 4-mm VSD occluder was selected. The left disc was positioned on the left ventricular aspect of the defect, and the right disc was deployed into the right atrial aspect (Figure 4, Video 4).Figure 4Postdeployment Left Ventriculography Confirming Complete Occlusion of the Left Ventricular–to–Right Atrial Communication

Outcome and Follow-Up

Final intraoperative TEE and left ventriculography confirmed complete occlusion of the shunt with no residual flow. There were no aortic insufficiency, tricuspid valve compromise, or arrhythmias. The patient was transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit for routine monitoring with favorable recovery. A transthoracic echocardiogram the following day confirmed a successfully positioned device without residual flow, normal biventricular contractility, and normalized left ventricular dimensions (left ventricular internal diameter in diastole 40 mm, ejection fraction 65%, and fractional shortening 35%).

She was discharged on postoperative day 2 on aspirin 100 mg daily and amoxicillin for endocarditis prophylaxis during future dental procedures. At the 3-month follow-up, she remained completely asymptomatic in NYHA functional class I. Echocardiography confirmed a stable device position, complete closure of the shunt, and preserved tricuspid and aortic valvular function.

Discussion

The Gerbode defect is a rare congenital cardiac anomaly, first surgically repaired by Frank Gerbode in 1958,1 accounting for <1% of congenital heart diseases.2 In the type II congenital variant, the shunt flows directly from the left ventricle into the right atrium via the defective tricuspid valve, bypassing the right ventricle entirely. This anatomy circumvents the influence of elevated neonatal pulmonary vascular resistance on shunt magnitude, explaining the presence of a holosystolic murmur from as early as 21 days of life, as documented in neonatal records in our patient. The resulting marked increase in left ventricular preload similarly explains the early initiation of diuretic therapy. Because of the high-velocity left ventricular–to–right atrial jet, this defect is frequently misinterpreted on transthoracic echocardiography as tricuspid regurgitation with severe pulmonary hypertension, leading to a misdiagnosis rate of approximately 14.5%.2 In our case, the diagnosis was correctly established early in life, and the 9-year delay in treatment was strictly due to the parents' loss to follow-up and reliance on traditional medicine. Spontaneous closure is rare (∼7% of cases),2 necessitating definitive intervention to prevent progressive right ventricular volume overload, heart failure, and infective endocarditis.

Historically, surgical closure via median sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass has been the gold standard. However, transcatheter closure has become a well-established alternative to surgery, with recent pediatric series demonstrating feasibility across device types and anatomical subtypes.3, 4, 5 Evidence remains limited to small series and case reports, underscoring the value of additional pediatric cases with detailed anatomical characterization.3,5 In settings in which cultural beliefs preclude open-heart surgery, transcatheter closure becomes not merely an alternative to reduce operative morbidity but also increases medical acceptance among rural populations who might otherwise abandon treatment entirely.

Because no specific device is currently manufactured purposely for Gerbode defects, the primary literature regarding transcatheter closure emphasizes the importance of detailed echocardiographic imaging for device sizing and complication avoidance, as complications including complete heart block, device embolization, and device-induced hemolysis have been reported.2,6 Although transthoracic echocardiography may suffice diagnostically in pediatric patients with adequate acoustic windows, TEE was necessary in this case because of suboptimal transthoracic window quality, providing superior anatomical definition and safer intraprocedural guidance.2 The anatomical characteristics of this type II defect favored the VSD occluder, whose symmetric discs and longer waist ensured stable anchoring without valvular interference, consistent with prior reports of muscular VSD occluders in similar anatomical settings.4 The successful deployment in this patient adds valuable pediatric data to the evidence supporting transcatheter closure in children with favorable anatomy and especially as an alternative when parental or cultural barriers preclude open-heart surgery.

Conclusions

Percutaneous closure of a congenital Gerbode defect using a VSD occluder is a safe and effective alternative to surgical repair in carefully selected pediatric patients. Echocardiographic assessment is essential for avoiding misdiagnosis, characterizing the defect, and providing intraprocedural guidance. When anatomy is favorable, the transcatheter approach offers excellent outcomes, complete shunt occlusion, and avoidance of open-heart surgery morbidity.Take-Home Messages•When cultural beliefs or parental fears preclude traditional open-heart surgery, transcatheter closure offers a crucial, life-saving, and acceptable alternative to median sternotomy in selected pediatric patients with a Gerbode defect.•Because no specific device is manufactured for Gerbode defects, using off-label devices like a muscular VSD occluder requires precise echocardiographic guidance to effectively occlude the shunt and avoid major injuries.
