Targeted solutions to bridge the gap between sexes in the use of automated external defibrillators
Authors: Sonali Munot, Carolina Malta Hansen
Published: 2026

The critical importance of prompt bystander response, including early defibrillation, in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) has been well established.1, 2, 3 Yet, evidence from multiple observational and registry studies indicates that females experiencing OHCA receive significantly lower bystander intervention compared to males, especially in public locations.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 This includes reduced rates of OHCA recognition, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), use of automated external defibrillator (AED), and timeliness in shock delivery, highlighting critical gaps in pre-hospital response and the need for targeted strategies to address the sex disparities. This is important as the use of an AED by a bystander before paramedic arrival has a significant impact on survival and functional neurological outcomes.13, 14 Reviews of several studies suggest women are around 20% less likely to have AED pads applied compared with men.15

AEDs must be applied to bare skin with adjustment or removal of clothing as needed to ensure pad placement and good contact.16 Pads are to be placed in the standard anterolateral positions (right upper chest below the clavicle and left lateral chest below the armpit) while adjusting clothing and breast tissue only if it interferes with pad placement. Current guidelines do not suggest unfastening or removal of the bra, unless necessary for correct pad application, and there is little evidence to suggest that an underwire can cause harm during AED use.17 It is important to convey these messages to the public, given the reluctance or delays in pad application, which may be linked to these factors. Surveys and qualitative data consistently highlight bystander concerns related to exposing the female chest, perceived frailty of females, fear of accusations of sexual assault, misconceptions that breasts or bras may interfere with pad placement or defibrillation, as key deterrents.18, 19, 20 This also points to the importance of public awareness and education to highlight protection (Good Samaritan laws) for laypersons. In most jurisdictions with a PAD program, these laws protect lay responders who act in good faith during an emergency.21 This lends itself to the important role of education and of initiating dialogue around cardiac arrest in females while increasing the visibility of images that reinforce this knowledge.

In this issue of Resuscitation Plus, Fijačko et al. present results from a study that aimed to evaluate whether instructional pictograms on AEDs, on AED packages, and on the defibrillation pads include both sexes.22 Between May and October 2025, the investigators contacted companies, manufacturers, distributors, and their professional networks, as well as local sources, to obtain AEDs, packaging, and defibrillation pads for the original photography. Assessment of 54 AEDs found that the pictogram instructions predominantly featured males (90.7%), with only one AED displaying both sexes and one other depicting only a female figure. Similarly, lower representation of female images was observed across 44 packages (only 6.8%) and defibrillation pads (2 of 44).22 The authors highlight an overwhelmingly higher male representation, noting that this likely reinforces the observed sex bias. They urge manufacturers and peak bodies to promote more balanced visual representations to ensure equitable messaging is conveyed to the lay public.

The authors acknowledged the study limitation that they were unable to examine all available products, as not all companies/manufacturers accepted the invitation to participate in the study. Further, they did not include manual or training devices in their analysis. However, it seems unlikely that the gender visuals represented on training devices will be dissimilar to the ones on real devices.

Overall, it is important for the community to understand that from a clinical and device perspective, AED indications and effectiveness are the same irrespective of sex or gender. Using diverse visual representations in CPR and first-aid training courses creates opportunities to spark dialogue and engagement around inclusive bystander response. Gender/sex and even racial diversity depictions are also limited in educational and training videos, with a recent review reporting that only 1% videos featured female manikins.23 Along with these measures, raising awareness and explicitly reinforcing the protection provided by Good Samaritan laws will likely reduce hesitation in using AEDs on women. Collectively, such targeted measures are urgently needed to change the current narrative and address this critical gap.
