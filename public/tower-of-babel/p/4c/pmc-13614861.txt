AI and transfusion medicine in Europe: why public leadership cannot wait
Authors: Massimo La Raja, Vincenzo De Angelis, Giovanni Camisasca, Luis Larrea
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Transfusion medicine still lacks validated, large-scale artificial intelligence (AI) tools, while other medical sectors, such as imaging, pathology and cardiology, have absorbed AI into routine practice. The applications most relevant to blood services –inventory forecasting, donor selection, hemovigilance and prescription decision support– are repeatedly shown to be feasible, yet none has reached validated deployment on a large scale. AI needs and regulatory responsibilities vary by setting. Blood establishments, which own donor and blood component data, bear the primary regulatory obligations and in the European Union (EU), these are defined under the Substances of Human Origin (SoHO) regulation1 (EU 2024/1938). Hospital transfusion services, by contrast, engage with AI primarily through clinical decision support for transfusion prescribing. The relative delay in the adoption of AI tools is caused more by structural factors than technological ones, stemming from the distinctive market landscape of blood services. Blood is a gift, not a commodity and under SoHO-regulation any compensation to donors must follow the principle of financial neutrality. Demand of blood components is driven by clinical indications following guidelines based on evidence that, within a Patient Blood Management framework, are increasingly restrictive. Additionally labile components expire within weeks or even days and the entire vein-to-vein chain is governed by EU and national regulation enforced by competent authorities. These features remove three main conditions on which private investment depends: a scalable market, premium pricing, and proprietary training data. A small, fragmented customer base (roughly 280 services in Italy in 20 regions, 17 autonomous-community systems in Spain, some 1,200 blood establishments across Europe), fixed-price public tenders, data held exclusively by public or not-for profit institutions, multi-year implementation, and a dual regulatory burden (CE conformity assessment under the AI Act2 and the Medical Device Regulation (EU) 2017/745 (MDR), authorization/validation under the SoHO Regulation) are likely to make the commercial investment less attractive and less viable. The advanced analytics and clinical decision-support functionalities commercialized by leading North American blood bank information systems are not natively architected to fulfil the end-to-end donor-to-component traceability and governance requirements mandated under the EU regulatory framework.

That the obstacle is not the algorithm is shown largely by scattered experiences in the public sector also in Europe. In Spain, the Blood and Tissue Bank (Banc de Sang i Teixits)3 and the Hemotic (Olite, Spain) platform have brought machine-learning demand forecasting into routine use; a multicentre European study across seven blood establishments and 64 million donations confirmed that long-term forecasting at scale is feasible4. In Italy, the Italian National Blood Center (Centro Nazionale Sangue) questionnaire-digitization project created the first interoperable national donor selection dataset5, the prerequisite for any AI model. Comparable initiatives in Finland, Denmark, and the Netherlands, together with the pan-European PETRA infrastructure and the EBA Blood Byte Circle, demonstrate that donor data can be systematically digitized and shared as a public health asset. A further concrete step in this direction is the EDQM/EC Project for a Minimum Activity Data Set for Blood, which establishes common data-quality.

Since the barriers hampering AI development in transfusion medicine are a direct consequence of the public-service design of blood systems market forces alone will not remove them. Public blood services, which own the data, understand clinical priorities, and ensure institutional continuity, are uniquely positioned to lead this transformation. The SoHO Coordination Board can standardize data-quality protocols, enable mutual recognition of validations, and commission open-licence tools through Horizon Europe and its successor Framework Programme. Accordingly, national authorities should standardize and share their data, support development through open calls with open-licence requirements, and train clinicians to oversee and govern AI-driven systems.

A limited set of priority use cases would give this leadership early focus: platelet inventory forecasting, donor eligibility and deferral prediction, hemovigilance signal detection, and transfusion decision support are some examples.

Technical feasibility is well established; the remaining challenge is large-scale validated, interoperable and regulatorily compliant implementation. The regulatory architecture, for the first time, allows this to be addressed at European scale. We are confident that the public institutions responsible for Europe’s blood services are willing to lead.
