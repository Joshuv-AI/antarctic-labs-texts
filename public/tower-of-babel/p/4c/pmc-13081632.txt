Ceramic-reinforced 3D-printed attachments for clear aligner therapy: a laboratory comparison with conventional attachments
Authors: Xiaowei Wu, Haotian Gao, Qingchen Qiao, Xiaowei Li, Ping Ma, Ning Ding, Zeqing Zhao, Yuxing Bai, Dongyu Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundsClear aligner therapy (CAT) depends on accurately fabricated and mechanically reliable attachments to optimize aligner retention and force delivery. Conventional attachments, produced via manual resin filling, are prone to operator variability, prolonged chair-side procedures, and inconsistent bonding outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of preformed 3D-printed attachments as a standardized alternative to conventional resin-based attachments.MethodsFour types of attachment specimens were prepared: a ceramic-reinforced 3D-printed resin (Bego VarseoSmile CrownPlus, BVSC), a universal nano-hybrid restorative resin (Z350), a flowable restorative resin (Z350L), and a common 3D-printed resin (CPR). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Raman spectroscopy were used to characterize surface morphology and degree of conversion. Dimensional accuracy, water sorption, mechanical properties (elastic modulus, yield strength, bonding strength, wear resistance), and cytotoxicity were evaluated.ResultsBoth 3D-printed resins demonstrated significantly higher dimensional accuracy and degree of conversion than conventional restorative resins (P < 0.05). The BVSC group exhibited the best overall performance, showing superior dimensional stability, yield strength, bonding strength, and wear resistance compared with all other groups (P < 0.05). No significant differences in cytotoxicity were observed among all tested materials (P > 0.05).ConclusionsPreformed attachments fabricated using ceramic-reinforced 3D-printed polymers demonstrated high manufacturing precision, favorable mechanical performance, and good biocompatibility. These findings support their feasibility as a standardized and reliable alternative to manually fabricated composite attachments in clear aligner therapy.

Introduction

With the accelerating integration of digital technologies in dentistry, digital orthodontics has become a major theme in modern clinical and research practice. Among these advancements, clear aligner therapy (CAT) has gained increasing popularity due to its aesthetic appeal, patient comfort, and convenience of oral hygiene maintenance [1, 2]. To enhance biomechanical control, particularly for torque expression and rotational control, composite attachments are widely employed in CAT. These small, bonded auxiliaries enhance aligner fit and allow for more precise control of force systems [3, 4].

Traditionally, attachments are fabricated chairside using manually filled light-cured composite resins such as flowable resin, universal nanohybrid composite, or resin-modified glass ionomer [5]. However, this process is highly operator-dependent, time-consuming, and prone to variability in shape, size, and placement. Such inconsistencies like attachment flash can compromise both the esthetics and efficacy of orthodontic treatment [6].

In recent years, additive manufacturing—specifically 3D printing—has emerged as a promising alternative for producing orthodontic components [7]. This digital manufacturing workflow offers several notable advantages [8]: (1) increased design freedom and structural customization, supporting the fabrication of specialized configurations such as 3D-printed buccal buttons, power arms and preformed cantilevers—which provide enhanced capability in managing complex clinical cases; (2) broad compatibility with diverse printable materials, offering the potential to incorporate antibacterial, shape-memory, or other advanced features; (3) reduced technical sensitivity and minimized operator-dependent variability; (4) significantly shorter chairside time by eliminating resin filling, along with lower material consumption as no resin overflow; and (5) enhanced dimensional accuracy, detail reproducibility, and structural stability due to the highly controlled additive manufacturing process (Fig. 1).

However, existing studies on 3D-printed attachments have primarily focused on manufacturing feasibility, such as optimizing material formulations [9], post-processing parameters [10], or transfer workflows [11] to improve geometric accuracy [12] or biological safety [13]. In contrast, systematic comparisons between 3D-printed attachment materials and clinically established composite resins remain limited, particularly with respect to key performance dimensions under intraoral conditions, including dimensional stability in humid and thermally fluctuating environments, mechanical reliability (including failure and fracture behavior), wear resistance, and biocompatibility.

Building upon this foundation, the present study aims to evaluate the feasibility of using ceramic-reinforced 3D-printed attachments as a standardized alternative to conventionally fabricated ones. Comparative in vitro evaluations were conducted to assess the surface morphology, dimensional accuracy, water absorption, mechanical properties, and cytotoxicity of 3D-printed attachments versus commonly used clinical composites. Together, these assessments provide a comprehensive analysis to inform the clinical potential of high-performance, digitally manufactured orthodontic attachments in CAT.

Materials and methods

Experimental grouping and material classification

To investigate the differences of in vitro performance between ceramic-reinforced polymer attachments fabricated via 3D printing and clinically used conventional at-tachments, this study categorized the samples into four groups according to their material composition: BVSC (Bego VarseoSmile Crownplus, 3D-printed ceramic-reinforced resin), Z350 (3 M Filtek™ Z350 XT Universal Restorative), Z350L (3 M Filtek™ Z350 XT flowable resin), and CPR (Common Transparent Resins for 3D Printing), The specific compositions and contents are shown in the table below (Table 1). For the evaluations of dimensional accuracy, water absorption, elastic modulus, yield strength, and shear bond strength, 10 specimens were prepared per group. For all other tests, 5 specimens were prepared per group (Fig. 2).

Specimen fabrication

For the 3D-printed materials (BVSC and CPR), the pre-designed STL models (rec-tangular specimens, 25 × 2 × 2 mm, and disc-shaped specimens, ⌀14 × 2 mm) were first imported into the BEGO CAMcreator software for processing, with the printing direction set to align with the long axis and necessary supports added. The files were then transferred to the BEGO Varseo XS 3D printer, and VarseoSmile Crownplus A2 resin was used as the printing material. After printing, the supports were removed, and the specimens were immersed in 95% ethanol (Innokai, China) and ultrasonically cleaned for 15 min to eliminate uncured resin monomers. Finally, a 30-min post-curing process was performed to ensure complete polymerization and mechanical stability.

For the conventional resin materials (Z350 and Z350L), a cylindrical platform (⌀100 × 20 mm) was designed using CAD software. Rectangular and disc-shaped specimens, as described above, were arranged on the platform and the master mold was fabricated via 3D printing. The specimen template was then created by thermoforming Fusion AM thermoplastic sheets (1.0 mm thickness, RAYSHAPE, Beijing, China) using a vacuum forming device (Erkoform-3D, Erkodent, Pfalzgrafenweiler, Germany) (Fig. 3A). Dental composite resins were subsequently filled or injected into the template and polymerized for 40 s using a light-curing unit (iLED, Woodpecker, Guilin, China). The light-curing unit was operated under clinically relevant parameters, with a blue-light wavelength range of 430–480 nm and an intensity of approximately 1000–1200 mW/cm², to ensure adequate polymerization. After curing, the specimens were carefully removed and subjected to finishing and polishing procedures using sandpapers to ensure dimensional consistency and surface uniformity across all samples (Fig. 3B).

Material characterization

Surface morphology and particle size distribution analysis

Specimens were mounted on aluminum SEM stubs using double-sided conductive carbon tape, and a thin gold coating of approximately 5 nm was applied using a sputter coater to enhance conductivity. Surface morphology was observed with a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, S-4800, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) under high-vacuum mode, with an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV and a working distance of 6 mm. The acquired images were processed using ImageJ software (Version 1.8.0_112, 64-bit, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to adjust contrast and brightness and to enhance particle boundary clarity. The maximum diameter of each particle was measured along its boundary using the line tool, with 1–2 measurements per particle. All measurement data were exported into Origin 2024 (Version 10.1, OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA) for statistical analysis. Particle size distribution histograms were generated, mean size, range, and standard deviation were calculated, and Gaussian curve fitting was performed to evaluate the uniformity of particle dispersion.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) analysis

Prior to AFM analysis, the specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in deionized water for 5 min to remove potential surface contaminants and then air-dried at room temperature. Surface topography was characterized using an atomic force microscope (Dimension Icon, Bruker, Santa Barbara, CA, USA) operated in tapping mode to minimize tip-induced deformation of the polymer surface. For each specimen, multiple representative areas were randomly selected and scanned over an area of 2.0 × 2.0 μm². Environmental conditions were carefully controlled throughout the measurements to avoid interference from external vibration or fluctuations in temperature and humidity. The acquired three-dimensional topographical data were processed using Bruker NanoScope Analysis software. After plane correction, surface roughness parameters—including the arithmetic mean roughness (Ra), root mean square roughness (Rq), and maximum peak-to-valley height (Rmax)—were calculated. At least three different regions were analyzed for each specimen, and the averaged values were used for subsequent statistical analysis.

Degree of Conversion (DC) analysis

To measure the degree of double bond conversion (DC), Raman spectra were rec-orded for both the unpolymerized resin (R₀) and the polymerized specimens (R), using Raman spectrometer (LabRAM HR Evolution, HORIBA Scientific, Kyoto, Japan). A diode laser was used to irradiate the specimens through a 50× microscope objective. The laser wavelength was set to 785 nm, with a spectral resolution of 1 cm⁻¹. The laser power was adjusted to 100%, with an exposure time of 10 s per scan, and each measurement was accumulated over 10 scans. Raman spectra were collected in the range of 1500–2000 cm⁻¹. The spectral data were subsequently analyzed using curve-fitting software (Origin, OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). Characteristic peaks at 1608 cm⁻¹ (aromatic C–C reference peak) and 1635 cm⁻¹ (C = C stretching vibration peak) were analyzed to calculate the DC. Dimensional accuracy and water absorption stability

Dimensional accuracy measurement

Dimensional accuracy was assessed by measuring the length, width, and thickness of 10 rectangular specimens in each group using a digital caliper (Super Caliper; Mitutoyo). Each dimension was measured three times per specimen. The measured dimensions were compared with the original CAD designed values, and the mean percentage error (MPE) was calculated to evaluate the manufacturing precision of the specimens.

Water absorption test

Under standardized laboratory conditions (room temperature ~ 23 °C, relative hu-midity ~ 50%), the initial properties of the rectangular specimens were measured prior to testing. The dry mass (W₀, in mg, accurate to 0.1 mg) was determined using an analytical balance (BSA124S, Sartorius, USA). The specimen volume (V₀, in mm³, accurate to 0.1 mm³) was calculated by measuring length, width, and height with a digital caliper (Mitutoyo, Japan; accuracy 0.01 mm). The specimens were then immersed in a 37 °C water bath and retrieved at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 168 h (7 d). After each interval, specimens were gently blotted dry with non-woven gauze, and their mass (W₁–₅) was recorded using the same method. Water absorption (W, µg/mm³) was calculated according to the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{W}\;=\;(\boldsymbol{W}_{n}\;-\;\boldsymbol{W}_{0})\;\times\;\mathit{1000}\;/\;\boldsymbol{V}_{0},$$\end{document}

where Wn denotes the mass of the specimen measured at each specified time point.

Mechanical performance evaluation

Three-point bending test

All rectangular specimens were mounted on a universal testing machine (AG-IS 500 N, SHIMADZU Corporation, Japan) for three-point bending tests to evaluate their elastic modulus and yield strength. The test parameters were set with a support span of 15 mm and a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min. Yield strength (Re) was calculated as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{Re}=\boldsymbol{Fe/So,}$$\end{document}

where Fe represents the constant load at yield (N), and S₀ denotes the initial cross-sectional area of the specimen (mm²).

Fracture mode analysis

After completion of the mechanical tests, fracture mode analysis was performed on the specimens. The fractured samples were first examined macroscopically and immediately documented by photography. Particular attention was paid to the fracture surface characteristics, including the morphology of fracture edges, crack propagation paths, and the presence or absence of evident plastic deformation. The observed fracture features were then analyzed in conjunction with the corresponding load–displacement curves to characterize the failure behavior of different materials under mechanical loading.

Shear bond strength testing

Specially designed rectangular attachments (base area: 6.5 mm²) were bonded to the central region of the labial surface of freshly extracted human premolars (Approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Stomatological Hospital, Capital Medical University (NO. CMUSH-IRB-KJ-PJ-2022-43)). Prior to bonding, the enamel surface was etched with 37% phosphoric acid etchant (3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) for 30 s, rinsed with deionized water, and gently air-dried, followed by the application of a light-curing adhesive (Single Bond Universal Adhesive, 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA). Light curing was performed using a blue light-curing unit (wavelength: 430–480 nm; light intensity: 1000–1200 mW/cm²) for 20 s. To simulate intraoral conditions, all bonded specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h after a 15-minute setting period before debonding tests. The specimens were then mounted on a universal testing machine (AG-IS 500 N, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), and a shear force was applied parallel to the bonding interface at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min. The shear bond strength (P, MPa) was calculated using the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\boldsymbol{P=F/S,}$$\end{document}

where F represents the peak force at failure (N) and S denotes the actual contact area between the attachment base and the enamel surface (mm²).

Adhesive Remnant Index (ARI) evaluation

Immediately after attachment debonding, the enamel surfaces were examined to assess adhesive remnants. The residual adhesive on the enamel surface was observed under a stereomicroscope (Leica M205 C, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) at 10× magnification, and the adhesive remnant index (ARI) was recorded for each specimen. The ARI scores were defined as follows:ARI 0: no visible adhesive remaining on the enamel surface;ARI 1: less than 50% of adhesive remaining on the enamel surface;ARI 2: more than 50% of adhesive remaining on the enamel surface;ARI 3: almost all adhesive remaining on the enamel surface.

Wear resistance testing

Wear resistance was evaluated using a UMT multifunctional tribometer (Bruker, USA) operated in a ball-on-flat, linear reciprocating mode. The counter-body consisted of a 6-mm alumina (Al₂O₃) ceramic ball, and all tests were performed in artificial saliva at 37 °C. The normal load was set at 50 N, with an 8 mm stroke length, a reciprocation frequency of 1.6 Hz, and a total test duration of 30 min. After testing, the worn areas of the disc-shaped specimens were examined using a 3D white-light interferometric profiler (Contour GT-K, Bruker, USA). Measurements were conducted in vertical scanning interferometry (VSI) mode with a 1.0 × 1.0 mm² scanning area and a vertical resolution of approximately 1 nm. Raw surface topography data were processed with the instrument’s built-in software (Vision64, Bruker, USA) for tilt correction and Gaussian filtering, and the maximum wear depth within the worn region was recorded as the wear metric.

Biocompatibility assessment

The cytotoxicity of each material group was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay on human oral keratinocytes (HOKs). HOK cells were seeded into the lower chambers of 24-well Transwell plates (24-well, 0.4 μm pore size; Jet Biofil®, Guangzhou, China) at a density of 0.5 × 10⁵ cells/well. Once the cells reached approximately 70% confluence, disc-shaped specimens from each group were placed into the upper chambers of the Transwell inserts to establish indirect contact co-culture. After 24 h of incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 working solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere protected from light. Subsequently, the optical density (OD) of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Statistics analysis

All experimental data were subjected to descriptive statistical analysis and processed using GraphPad Prism version 9.3. For data conforming to a normal distribution, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare intergroup differences, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Material characterization

Surface morphology and particle size distribution

Based on SEM analysis of surface morphology (Fig. 4) and particle size distribution (Fig. 5), the BVSC group exhibited an average particle size of 1.39 ± 0.34 μm, while the CPR group measured at 1.29 ± 0.38 μm. Both values were significantly smaller than those observed in the conventional composite resin groups—Z350 (1.80 ± 0.90 μm) and Z350L (1.52 ± 0.51 μm) (P < 0.05). Moreover, the particle distribution in the BVSC and CPR groups appeared more uniform, consistent with their optimized photosensitivity for 3D printing applications. Notably, fewer voids and surface defects were observed in the 3D-printed groups, indicating improved homogeneity and structural integrity.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

As shown in Fig. 6, The 3D-printed groups exhibited significantly lower surface roughness at the nanoscale compared with the conventional composite resin groups, indicating a smoother and more homogeneous surface profile. Among them, the BVSC group showed the smoothest surface, with an arithmetic mean roughness (Ra) of 4.11 nm, a root mean square roughness (Rq) of 5.50 nm, and a maximum peak-to-valley height (Rmax) of only 50.3 nm (Table 2). The CPR group presented slightly higher surface roughness than the BVSC group (Ra = 16.5 nm, Rq = 21.0 nm, Rmax = 157 nm), but its values remained markedly lower than those of the conventional composite materials. In contrast, the Z350L and Z350 groups exhibited substantially higher surface roughness, with Ra values of 24.6 nm and 50.2 nm, respectively, accompanied by more pronounced peak-to-valley height variations (Rmax reaching up to 413 nm in the Z350 group), reflecting a highly heterogeneous surface morphology with pronounced nanoscale topographical fluctuations.

Dimensional accuracy and water absorption stability

Dimensional accuracy

As illustrated in Fig. 8, no statistically significant differences were observed among the four groups with respect to length and height accuracy (P > 0.05). For width, the 3D-printed groups (101.20 ± 1.57% and 101.60 ± 1.34%) exhibited markedly better accuracy than both the Z350 solid resin group (122.90 ± 5.36%) and the Z350 flowable resin group (113.30 ± 2.49%), with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). No significant difference was found between the two 3D-printed groups (P > 0.05). Additionally, the Z350L group showed slightly better width accuracy than the Z350 group (P < 0.05).

Water absorption progressively increased over time across all experimental groups. Among them, BVSC group exhibited the lowest water uptake at 168 h, with a value of 4.58 ± 2.77 µg/mm³. Mechanical properties

Three-point bending test

In terms of elastic modulus, the BVSC group exhibited a value of 6.24 ± 0.39 GPa, which was significantly lower than that of the Z350 group (14.01 ± 1.68 GPa, P < 0.05), but notably higher than both the Z350L group (4.29 ± 1.99 GPa, P < 0.05) and the CPR group (2.68 ± 0.58 GPa, P < 0.05). Regarding yield strength, the BVSC group demonstrated the highest performance (156.40 ± 10.18 MPa), significantly surpassing Z350 (75.81 ± 12.14 MPa), Z350L (52.60 ± 6.04 MPa), and CPR groups (16.10 ± 3.97 MPa) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 11A-B).

Fracture mode

Figure 11C presents the representative fracture morphologies of the specimens after yield strength testing, together with the corresponding stress–strain curves. Macroscopic observation revealed that all specimens fractured abruptly upon reaching the yield point, with localized fracture regions and well-defined fracture surfaces. No evident signs of plastic tearing or ductile deformation were observed, indicating a predominantly brittle fracture behavior. No fundamental differences in fracture mode were identified among the different materials, as all groups exhibited brittle fracture as the primary failure mechanism.

The stress–strain curves further support these observations (Fig. 11D). All materials showed an approximately linear elastic response during the initial loading stage, followed by a rapid stress drop after reaching the yield strength, without the presence of a distinct yield plateau or ductile deformation region. This stress–strain behavior suggests that elastic deformation dominated the loading process, and failure occurred through rapid fracture rather than progressive plastic deformation.

Adhesive Remnant Index (ARI)

Analysis of the adhesive remnant index (ARI) after debonding revealed distinct differences among the material groups with respect to failure location and the extent of adhesive remaining on the enamel surface (Fig. 12B–C). The BVSC group predominantly exhibited ARI scores of 2–3, indicating a relatively greater amount of residual adhesive after debonding. The Z350 group showed overall higher ARI scores, with an ARI score of 3 being the most prevalent, suggesting that the majority of the adhesive remained on the enamel surface following attachment removal. In contrast, the Z350L group was mainly characterized by ARI scores of 1–2, reflecting minimal adhesive residue on the enamel surface. Notably, the CPR group was overwhelmingly dominated by an ARI score of 0–1, indicating that debonding primarily occurred at the enamel–adhesive interface with limited adhesive retention.

Material characterization

Based on SEM analysis, the 3D-printed groups (BVSC and CPR) exhibited significantly smaller average particle sizes compared with the conventional composite resin groups (Z350 and Z350L), with a more uniform particle distribution. This refined and homogeneous microstructure enhances the material’s photosensitivity, making it particularly suitable for 3D printing applications that demand high curing quality [14]. Further atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis revealed consistent findings. Three-dimensional surface topography maps showed that the 3D-printed materials exhibited relatively lower surface height variations with uniformly distributed peak–valley features, whereas the conventional composite resins displayed more pronounced nanoscale roughness and heterogeneous surface topographical characteristics.

The degree of C = C bond conversion (DC) is as well critical for mechanical performance and biocompatibility. The BVSC group exhibited higher DC values than conventional composites, suggesting improved polymerization efficiency. This enhancement may stem from nano-ceramic fillers that promote light scattering and heat conduction, facilitating deeper and more uniform curing [15]. However, excessive filler content or poor dispersion can impede light transmission, reducing curing depth. Thus, optimizing filler characteristics and curing conditions is essential to achieve high DC without compromising material integrity [16]. The cytotoxicity results confirmed that ceramic-reinforced 3D-printed materials possess excellent biocompatibility, comparable with other groups.

Dimensional accuracy and water absorption stability

Attachments are critical for achieving retention and force delivery in clear aligner therapy, making geometric precision essential for clinical success. 3D printing, particularly digital light processing (DLP), offers high resolution (25–100 μm) suitable for reproducing complex designs [17–19]. In this study, the ceramic-reinforced polymer maintained high printing accuracy, with uniformly distributed fillers that did not compromise resolution. Compared with manually filled composite attachments, the 3D-printed attachments demonstrated superior dimensional fidelity and detail reproduction.

Given the humid and warm intraoral environment, attachment materials must maintain dimensional stability. Conventional Z350 resin, composed of hydrophilic methacrylate monomers (e.g. Bis-GMA, TEGDMA), tends to absorb water and swell. In contrast, 3D-printed resins polymerized via high-precision DLP systems achieve higher conversion rates with fewer residual polar groups, reducing water uptake. Additionally, hydrophobic fillers and optimized photo initiators reduce water channels formed by agglomerated fillers. In this study, the BVSC group demonstrated the lowest water sorption and highest dimensional stability across all evaluated metrics, including water absorption and dimensional stability (length, width, and height). These results highlight the ceramic-reinforced resin’s structural integrity in humid conditions, supporting its clinical applicability for long-term use in CAT.

Mechanical performance

The elastic modulus and yield strength respectively reflect a material’s resistance to deformation and its ability to withstand fracture under mechanical stress. In this study, the ceramic-reinforced resin demonstrated significantly superior performance in both parameters compared with conventional 3D printing resins and flowable composites.

The highest elastic modulus and stiffness were demonstrated in Z350 group, which may be due to the rigid polymer backbones and dense cross-linking of Z350 resin, limiting chain mobility and reduce plastic deformation. Prior research confirms a strong positive correlation between filler content and elastic modulus, with well-dispersed fillers enhancing mechanical strength [20]. However, in the scenario of CAT, chewing pressure, insertion and removal of aligners may cause micro strain and stress within the attachments, which means that high elastic modulus and stiffness may lead to excessive stress transmitted from the attachments to the teeth. Therefore, moderate stiffness of BVSC attachments may soften the force transmitted to teeth compared with conventional attachments.

In terms of yield strength, the BVSC group exhibited the highest yield strength, indicating superior resistance to fracture under sudden or excessive occlusal loads. The ceramic fillers within the material were uniformly distributed, serving as microstructural barriers to crack propagation through deflection and arrest mechanisms [21, 22]. Additionally, the layer-by-layer deposition and directional curing in 3D printing may create a fiber-like architecture perpendicular to the loading axis, mimicking a fiber-reinforced effect that improves rigidity and deformation resistance [23].

To further elucidate the failure behavior under three-point bending, fracture mode analysis was performed on the tested specimens. Macroscopic observations and stress–displacement curves consistently showed a linear elastic response followed by abrupt failure, indicating predominantly brittle fracture behavior, which is characteristic of methacrylate-based composites under flexural loading [24]. Stereomicroscopic examination revealed relatively smooth fracture surfaces across all groups, with cracks rapidly traversing the cross-section and no evident ductile deformation. Although the Z350 group exhibited the highest elastic modulus, its fracture behavior remained dominated by brittle failure, indicating that increased stiffness alone did not alter the intrinsic fracture mechanism. By comparison, BVSC achieved improved yield strength while maintaining a moderate elastic modulus, highlighting the importance of regulating crack propagation behavior rather than stiffness alone in the mechanical optimization of clear aligner attachment materials [25].

For shear bond strength, the 3D-printed ceramic-reinforced resin attachments also demonstrated favorable performance. This can be primarily attributed to the incorporation of nanoscale ceramic fillers in the resin matrix, which enhances chemical bonding and facilitates the establishment of stable micromechanical interlocking [5]. In addition, the ceramic fillers also improve light scattering pathways, resulting in more uniform and efficient deep polymerization, thereby further enhancing bond strength. Previous studies have similarly shown that the addition of 15 wt% zirconia (ZrO₂) ceramic filler to 3D-printed resins can increase shear bond strength by approximately 40–50%, with the predominant failure mode shifting from adhesive failure to mixed or cohesive failure [26].

Based on the adhesive remnant index (ARI) analysis after debonding, the materials exhibited differentiated failure behaviors under shear loading [27]. The ARI distributions of the BVSC and Z350 groups were predominantly in the range of ARI 2–3, while still retaining a proportion of ARI 1, with a higher proportion observed in the Z350 group. This distribution indicates that failure in these two groups mainly occurred through mixed-mode failure, in which cracks propagated across both the enamel–adhesive interface and within the adhesive layer [28]. Such a failure pattern suggests that, while achieving relatively high shear bond strength, these attachments maintained a balanced mechanical load transfer between the bonding interface and the material bulk [29]. In contrast, the Z350L and CPR groups showed a higher proportion of ARI 0–1, indicating that failure predominantly occurred at the enamel–adhesive interface and was characterized by adhesive failure [30]. This observation is consistent with their lower shear bond strength, suggesting that insufficient interfacial bonding strength facilitated rapid crack propagation along the interface, ultimately leading to attachment debonding.

Regarding wear performance, the ceramic-reinforced material demonstrated superior resistance during the early testing phase and remained comparable with the Z350 group in later stages, consistently outperforming other groups throughout the test duration. This advantage may the silanization treatment of the ceramic fillers in BVSC, which establishes stronger chemical bonding with the methacrylate-based matrix, thereby promoting more uniform filler distribution and more efficient stress transfer. Although Z350 also contains silanized fillers with a higher filler load, the presence of nanocluster aggregation and potentially incomplete interfacial bonding may lead to the initiation of microcracks [31, 32]. which promotes uniform internal stress distribution and enhances fatigue and erosion resistance. Furthermore, the DLP printing method contributed to a higher degree of conversion, resulting in a densely cross-linked polymer network with fewer residual monomers. Such a compact polymer structure can significantly enhance wear resistance, even when the filler content is not particularly high [33, 34]. Nonetheless, limitations inherent to the polymer matrix—such as molecular flexibility and poor interfacial reinforcement—can lead to stress concentration zones and microcrack initiation, ultimately affecting long-term wear resistance and mechanical durability [35].

Biocompatibility

During long-term intraoral application, the biosafety and biological responses of orthodontic attachments are governed by multiple interrelated factors, including surface chemical composition, micro- and nanoscale topography, filler–matrix interfacial stability, and the presence of residual leachable components [36]. Guerrero-Gironés et al. systematically compared the in vitro biocompatibility of various 3D-printed and conventional dental resins and demonstrated that resin formulation and post-processing protocols are critical determinants of cellular responses [37]. This finding is particularly relevant to ceramic-reinforced printed materials, as filler type and silanization treatment may modulate cell behavior by altering surface characteristics and residual monomer release. Consistent with this framework, the present biocompatibility results indicate that the selected ceramic–resin composition exhibits favorable cytocompatibility.

Beyond material composition, the manufacturing route itself represents an independent variable influencing biological performance. Bürgers et al. reported that orthodontic materials fabricated by 3D printing, milling, and conventional methods induced distinct cellular responses, highlighting the biological relevance of process-dependent microstructural and surface features [38]. Similarly, Rodríguez-Lozano et al. demonstrated that 3Y-TZP zirconia produced by lithography-based additive manufacturing and conventional milling elicited different cellular behaviors despite identical chemical compositions [39]. Comparative evaluation of cellular responses associated with different attachment fabrication routes therefore warrants further investigation.

Overall, increasing evidence suggests that post-printing cleaning and post-curing conditions directly regulate biocompatibility by influencing the degree of conversion and residual monomer content. Xu et al. showed that prolonged post-rinsing significantly reduced cytotoxicity in 3D-printed orthodontic splint resins [40], while Bayarsaikhan et al. reported that post-curing temperature and duration markedly affected the biological performance of printed dental resins [41]. These findings are consistent with our preliminary experiments and underpin the selection of a 30 min post-curing protocol in the present study. Recent systematic reviews further emphasize that deviations from manufacturer-recommended post-processing procedures may substantially compromise biocompatibility, underscoring the need for standardized post-processing strategies [42].

Outlook

This study conducted a comprehensive in vitro assessment of ceramic-reinforced 3D-printed attachment materials, evaluating key parameters such as dimensional accuracy, mechanical properties, surface morphology, and cytocompatibility. Relative to conventional clinical materials, the ceramic-reinforced group consistently exhibited superior performance. These findings support the feasibility of integrating such materials into clinical workflows. Furthermore, incorporating advanced attachment architectures—such as beveled margins, dual-force control zones, and interlocking geometries—may further enhance biomechanical precision and support complex orthodontic movements, including extrusion, de-rotation, and molar mesialization, etc.

Certain limitations should also be acknowledged. As an in vitro study, this work does not account for long-term intraoral performance, aging resistance, or interactions with the oral microbiome. Future investigations using in vivo models or preclinical simulations are warranted to validate the durability, biostability, and functional behavior of these materials under real-world clinical conditions. Such studies will be critical to bridge the gap between experimental findings and clinical translation.

Conclusions

This study confirms the promising potential of ceramic-reinforced polymer materials for fabricating preformed clear aligner attachments via 3D printing. The tested material exhibited superior dimensional fidelity, low water absorption, excellent structural stability, and high yield strength, maintaining their intended geometry even under moist oral conditions. Additionally, their favorable cytocompatibility supports their safety for intraoral use. These results provide a robust foundation for the clinical translation of 3D-printed, ceramic-reinforced attachments, offering a standardized and efficient alternative to conventionally bonded composite resins in clear aligner therapy.
