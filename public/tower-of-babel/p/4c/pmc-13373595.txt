All-inorganic layered pseudo-halide perovskites with enhanced photovoltaic performance
Authors: Waqar Ahmad, Ata Ur Rahman, Ihsan Ullah, Najah Alwadie, Imad Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, two-dimensional (2D) layered halide perovskites have emerged as promising photovoltaic (PV) candidates since they are more stable and have favorable electronic properties. In the present study, we used first-principles calculations based on density functional theory (DFT) to investigate the all-inorganic 2D pseudo-halide perovskites Cs2PbPs2XY (Ps = SCN and SeCN and X/Y = I and Br). The 3D crystal structure of these compounds is composed of 2D perovskite layers, which serve as quantum wells for electrons and holes, and 1D spacer slabs, which form an energy barrier and trap carriers in the wells. The incorporation of a pseudo-ion reduces not only the quantum confinement but also the binding energy, which enhances the PV response. These compounds have direct band gaps in the energy range of 2.28–2.51 eV. Among the studied compounds, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2 has a suitable band gap of 2.28 eV, a small binding energy (Eb) of 114 meV, a reduced interlayer distance (d) of 1.88 Å, and effective light absorption in the visible spectra for solar cell applications. These optical characterization outcomes represent its potential in optoelectronics, including light-emitting diodes and photo-detectors. The simulated device structure FTO/SnO2/Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2/Spiro-MeOTAD/Cu was optimized to evaluate its PV performance. SnO2 was used as the ETL, and Spiro-MeOTAD as the HTL. The optimal state of the device exhibited an open-circuit voltage (Voc) of 1.91 V, a fill factor (FF) of 91.2, a short-circuit density (Jsc) of 10.2 mA cm−2, and a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 18.12%. This work presents a robust foundation for future studies on all-inorganic layered pseudo-halide perovskites, therefore making it possible to design improved PV devices through both theoretical and experimental approaches.

Introduction

Two-dimensional (2D) halide perovskites have gained immense interest recently due to their diverse applications, particularly in photovoltaics and optoelectronics.1 These materials have a layered hybrid organic–inorganic halide perovskite structure in which atomically thin layers of inorganic slabs comprise corner-sharing BX6 (B = Pb, Sn, Ge, Si, Bi, Sb, and Cd and X = Cl, Br, and I) octahedra and are spaced apart by large organic or inorganic spacer cations (Ma, Fa, Cs, and Rb). This dimensional reduction provides natural quantum-well structures with a high level of quantum and dielectric confinement with large exciton binding energies, tunable band gaps, and greater environmental stability relative to their 3D analogues.2,3 Structurally, 2D-layered perovskites normally crystallize in either Ruddlesden–Popper (RP) or Dion–Jacobson (DJ) phases, depending on the spacer cation between the perovskite slabs. RP phases are composed of monovalent organic cations, which separate perovskite layers by van der Waals forces, whereas DJ phases consist of divalent bridging spacers connecting adjacent slabs by hydrogen bonds to increase structural rigidity and charge mobility.4,5 The variation in Pb–X bond length, octahedral tilting, dielectric confinement, and layer orientation plays a critical role in regulating band dispersion, carrier mobility, and exciton dynamics, and thus, structural engineering is central to optimizing device performance.6,7

The RP phase is characterized by the general formula (A′)2An−1PbnX3n+1, in which the thickness of the layer (n) determines the shift from purely 2D to quasi-3D electronic behavior.5,8 Due to their exceptional optoelectronic properties, 2D lead halide perovskites have driven fast evolution in photovoltaics. Their organic spacers are hydrophobic, which enhances resistance to moisture and inhibits ion movement, thus raising long-term stability in devices. Compositional and interfacial engineering have been shown to enhance open-circuit voltage and endurance of high-efficiency solar cells based on RP structures.9,10 Moreover, dimensional engineering approaches (multilayer 2D halide perovskites) allow improved carrier confinement and passivation of defects, which enhances power conversion efficiency (PCE) further.8,11

In addition to solar cells, 2D lead-based perovskites have outstanding luminescence characteristics such as high photoluminescence quantum yield, narrow emission bandwidth, and high exciton recombination at room temperature. These characteristics are promising for applications in light-emitting diodes (LEDs), lasers, and photo-detectors.12 In layered structures, strong light-matter interactions and excitonic effects have been shown to produce efficient perovskite LEDs and amplified spontaneous emissions.13 Their wide band absorption and tolerance to high radiation levels also make them useful for X-ray detection and other high-tech photonic devices.14,15 So far, most 2D halide perovskites have been demonstrated with organic cations at the A-site having a large size, where an organic spacer and an alternated perovskite sheet were created due to the excess size of the A-site cation that overcame the tolerance range of the ABX3 perovskite structure.16,17 The length of the A-site cation can be controlled to reduce the exciton binding energy, or the lead-halide framework can be changed to 2D [Pb(X)4(Y)2], and another halide can be introduced at the axial positions to create a new structure. This breaking of connectivity through corners reduces the separation between neighboring perovskite layers. Compared to A-site-substituted layered perovskites, which have large interlayer distances (5.32–17.58 Å), the interlayer distances in X-site-substituted 2D perovskites are significantly smaller (1.33–3.10 Å).18,19 For this reason, the exciton binding energies of these 2D halide perovskites are much smaller than the exciton binding energies of A-site-substituted 2D halide perovskites.20

In addition to cation engineering at the A-site, there is an alternative way to create layer halide perovskites via direct X-site anion effects in 3D ABX3, such as pseudo-halide perovskites, in which the SCN− anion partially replaces the halide anion.21,22 Because of the asymmetric geometry and the halogen-like valence electronic configuration of the SCN− anion, the octahedron [Pb(SCN)2X4]4− loses the corner-sharing ability, and therefore results in a layered structure, with the pseudo (SCN−) halide acting as an axial atomic coordination frame in the octahedron. Theoretically, this type of layered halide perovskite has a significantly smaller interlayer (quantum barrier) spacing than that of ordinary layered perovskites composed of large A-site cations, making it a remarkable category of layered semiconducting materials worth further study.22 Numerous recent works have reported promising characteristics of the pseudo-halide (MA)2Pb(SCN)2I2 compared to the parent 3D MAPbI3.22,23 Younts et al.24 found the long-lived triplet exciton in this layered (MA)2Pb(SCN)2I2, demonstrating its potential in optoelectronic applications. Nevertheless, this layered material has low ambient thermal and moisture stability due to the MASCN low-temperature volatilization, which hinders its practical implementation to a great extent.23,25

The breakthrough synthesis of the fully inorganic pseudo-layered halide perovskite, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, is conducted using an antisolvent-assisted spin-coating method.26 This compound has a higher structural stability and environmental resistance than Cs2PbI2Cl2.27 Liao et al.20 used a solution-processing approach to synthesize the inorganic pseudo-halide 2D perovskite, Cs2Pb(SCN)2Br2. The material thus obtained has a lower interlayer spacing (∼1.69 Å) and an even smaller exciton binding energy (∼160 meV) in 2D (monolayer) pseudo-halide perovskites, comparable to bulky counterparts (multiple layers). It is worth noting that an excess concentration of Pb(SCN)2 is necessary during synthesis to discourage the formation of a 3D structure.20 The close interaction of perovskite layers enabled Pb–X–Pb bonding between the adjacent perovskite sheets and allowed structural reconfiguration.20 Based on this strategy, Xing et al.28 prepared a similar inorganic 2D pseudo-halide perovskite, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2Br2, through the same solution-processing technique. Ganose et al.22 investigated the organic pseudo-halide perovskite MA2Pb(SeCN)2Br2 utilizing density functional theory (DFT) calculations. We have previously investigated the inorganic pseudo-layered (2D) halide perovskite Cs2Pb(SCN)2Br2 using DFT calculations.29

We recognize that the presence of lead (Pb) in the understudied compounds Cs2PbPs2XY (Ps = SCN and SeCN and X/Y = I and Br) gives rise to inherent toxicity and environmental concerns, similar to those of traditional Pb-based halide perovskites. It is worth noting, however, that the main purpose of this research is not to trap lead, but rather to alter the anion framework by incorporating the pseudo-halide (SCN−/SeCN−) to modify the structural stability, electronic structure, and optoelectronic properties. Thiocyanate and selenocyanate anions fill a dual purpose in this case; strong directional interactions between lead and the (S/Se and N) anion lead to chemical and structural stability that is not observed in the standard 3D lead halide perovskites, and the presence of the (S/Se and N) anion enables better moisture and thermal stability.20 For this reason, the pseudo-halide engineering approach has the potential to develop more stable systems derived from perovskites, which may have more promising operational lifetimes. In terms of materials design, this work is part of the overall goal of developing families of perovskites with compositional tunability of properties and improved stability.23,25 In particular, the quasi-2D pseudo-halide systems studied provide structural robustness and desirable electronic/optical properties that could be relevant to the future development of longer-lived PV absorbers, as well as to the partial replacement of lead or other dimensional engineering in future studies.

To the best of our knowledge, no data are available on most of the physical properties of Cs2PbPs2XY (Ps = SCN and SeCN and X/Y = I and Br). These materials provide improved optoelectronic stability and performance, which places pseudo-halide-based 2D perovskites as an attractive class of materials for futuristic device applications, particularly related to photovoltaics and optoelectronics. In the present work, first-principles calculations were performed to investigate the intrinsic characteristics of Cs2PbPs2XY (Ps = SCN and SeCN and X/Y = I and Br). The work is particularly focused on their electronic structures, density of states, optical properties, and photovoltaic potentials.

Theory and calculations

All calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP)30,31 based on DFT. The projector augmented-wave (PAW) method was employed to account for interactions between core and valence electrons.32 For the 84-atom unit cell of Cs2PbPs2XY (Ps = SCN and SeCN and X/Y = I and Br), a Γ-centered k-point mesh of 6 × 6 × 2 was used to ensure total energy convergence within 1 meV per atom. In self-consistent calculations, a plane-wave cutoff energy of 400 eV was used, and geometry optimization was done with a plane-wave cutoff energy of 520 eV to reduce Pulay stress errors.33

Geometry optimizations were considered complete when all atomic forces were below 10 meVÅ−1. The PBEsol exchange–correlation functional was employed, which is a modified version of the PBE functional,34,35 and is known for its accurate geometry optimizations compared to other exchange–correlation approximations.36,37 In order to obtain precise predictions of electronic properties, especially the band gap, we used the Heyd–Scuseria–Ernzerhof (HSE06) hybrid functional, which is known to resolve the band gap underestimation that is linked to the GGA functional.38,39 The HSE06 functional has shown reliability in describing band structures and density of states in similar systems.40 The computation of dielectric properties was done by density functional perturbation theory (DFPT) employing the PBEsol functional using the same dense 6 × 6 × 2 k-point mesh.41 The estimates of photovoltaic performance were based on the Solar Cell Capacitance Simulator-1D (SCAPS-1D) code, widely adopted and commonly used to simulate the characteristics of a solar cell by solving semiconductor equations, including the Poisson, continuity, and drift-diffusion equations with illumination conditions. This combined computational technique offers a very detailed view of structural as well as electronic properties, and it is methodologically consistent with existing standards in the field.42

Results and discussions

Structural properties

The Ruddlesden–Popper (RP) inorganic layered pseudo-halide perovskites Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr, and Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr crystallize in a K2NiF4-type orthorhombic lattice with the space group Pmmn (59).28,43 A complete set of cell parameters, inter-atomic distances, and atomic coordinates was optimized by selecting the GGA-PBEsol functional, with experimental values of lattice geometry and cell dimensions to characterize their ground–state properties. The optimized unit cell structure is shown in Fig. 1. The figure shows that the Cs2Pb(Ps)2XY compounds adopt a layered structure made of 2D [PbPs2XY]n2n− (Ps = SCN and SeCN and X/Y = I and Br) octahedral layers and a spacer cation Cs+ layer, and the 2D sheets are linked by the corner-sharing Pb-centered octahedral. The halogen atoms (Br− or I− or mixed) are placed in the equatorial plane of Pb, with the two SCN− or SeCN− ligands positioned opposite to each other on the axial positions in a near-planar trans-arrangement. In the Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr crystal structures, charge-compensating cesium ions Cs+ are placed in the interstitial regions between the neighboring octahedral slabs, whereas the parent (undoped) Cs2PbX4 crystal has four halide ions in the equatorial corners and two in the axial directions.45 The combination of deformed (octahedral elongation caused by pseudo-halide SCN− or SeCN−) octahedra and the intercalated spacers forms the monolayer RP crystal structure. The perovskite sheets are 2D, making the sheets quantum wells in which charge carriers move, and the spacer layer acts as a potential barrier that restricts motion in the vertical direction.46 Structural parameters, such as lattice constants, bulk moduli, and cell volumes, were then determined by fitting the total energy versus volume profiles using the Birch–Murnaghan equation of state.44 The calculated lattice constants, cell volumes, and tolerance factors are presented in Table 1, along with comparisons to the reported experimental and theoretical results. These structural parameters agree closely with the reported experimental results20,28 and remain in line with earlier computed estimates for Cs2Pb(SCN)2Br2, Cs2PbBr4, and Cs2PbI2Br2.29,45 The total volume of Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2 exceeds that of Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr, Cs2PbI2Br2, and Cs2PbBr4 since the SeCN− ion is larger than SCN− and Br− but smaller than I−. The size of the anions is as follows, I− > SeCN− > SCN− > Br−, due to which they follow the decreasing volume pattern: Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr, Cs2PbI2Br2, and Cs2PbBr4. The inter-atomic distances between Cs–Br, Cs–I, Pb–I, and Pb–Br are presented in Table 2, which follows the same pattern, a trend that matches the relative ionic radii of I−, SeCN−, SCN−, and Br−. The interlayer separation distance d(1.88, 1.83, 1.79, and 1.76 Å, respectively) is also slightly larger than the value measured for the hybrid (CH3NH3)2Pb(SCN)2I2, where d = 1.33 Å, even though Cs is smaller than CH3NH3. The result agrees well with earlier reports.21 This apparently paradoxical action is probably an indication of the number of extra hydrogen bonds between the methylammonium groups and the tetrahedral groups of a lead halide in the layered material.21 The shorter interlayer spacing in Cs2PbPs2XY (Ps = SCN and SeCN and X/Y = I and Br) compared to typical 2D halide perovskites brings the quantum wells and their confining barriers closer together, resulting in a decrease in the binding energy, unlike the typical 2D halide perovskites, where the binding energy increases with decreasing confinement. This decrease is due to a more distorted structure and complex ion (SeCN and SCN) configuration compared to the 2D halide.47 Besides this, the weakly hydrated nature of the thiocyanate and selenocyanate ions, which is due to the sp-hybridization of nitrogen, makes it hard to occupy the tetrahedral cage that water prefers.48 Although this feature slows moisture attack, prolonged exposure will eventually weaken the structure by de-chelating SCN− and SeCN− and allowing conversion to the thicker three-dimensional phase, CsPbBr3.20

To quantify the crystal stability of Cs2PbPs2XY (Ps = SCN and SeCN and X/Y = I and Br), we computed the tolerance factor, τ, and the octahedral factor, µ, using the conventional Goldschmidt tolerance factor based on ionic radii values.49,50 The geometry underlying the persistence of this layered pseudo-halide architecture can be explained by that approach.12In these two equations, r′, rx, rA and rB, denote the ionic radii of selenocyanate, thiocyanate, (SeCN and SCN), anion halide (I and Br), spacer cation Cs and divalent cation Pb, respectively. The octahedral and tolerance factor values of the compound are given in Table 1. The tolerance factor values are less than 1, and the octahedral values are near 0.53; this behavior is in agreement with the 2D character observed in standard RP perovskites.45 The tolerance and octahedral factor values of the studied compounds are associated with the characteristics of the low-dimensional hybrid frameworks. In the case of Cs2PbBr4 and Cs2PbI2Br2, the tolerance factors of 1.008 and 1.009 line up closely and suggest tetragonal order. The corresponding octahedral factors are 0.607 and 0.572, both of which fall well (0.414 < µ < 0.732) within the region of well-known stability boundaries of octahedral coordination.45

Electronic properties

For efficient photovoltaic and optoelectronic devices, knowledge of the material's band gap nature (direct or indirect band gap), the precise energy value of that gap, and the atomic orbitals contributing to the formation of the valence and conduction bands is essential. The band structures of the 2DRP pseudo-halide perovskites Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2 and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr were therefore computed from fully relaxed crystal geometries using the GGA-PBE and HSE06 exchange–correlation functional and are shown in Fig. 2. The figure demonstrates direct band gap nature of these compounds, the extremes of both valence band maximum (VBM) and conduction band minimum (CBM) for Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2 aligned at the ZU symmetry point of the Brillouin zone while for Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr, the extremes are aligned at the RT symmetry point. The energy widths of the gaps for Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr predicted using the PBE exchange–correlation functional, are approximately 1.69, 1.71, 1.76, and 1.82 eV, whereas the HSE06 approach gives larger values of around 2.28, 2.30, 2.43, and 2.51 eV, respectively. The sizeable direct band gap renders the materials attractive to a variety of applications, such as optoelectronic devices, ultrathin transistor arrays, UV-visible remote-sensing detectors, X-ray imaging, transparent photovoltaics, surface-passivated perovskite solar cells, and photo memory elements.28 A summary of both experimental measurements and calculation results derived using the PBE and HSE06 functionals is provided in Table 3 to present a direct comparison. Not surprisingly, the PBE results for Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr fall short of the theoretical gap calculated for MA2Pb(SeCN)2Br2, MA2Pb(SCN)2Br2, MA2Pb(SeCN)2I2 and are also smaller than the experimental measured values for Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SCN)2(I2/3Br1/3)2, Cs2Pb(SCN)2(I1/2Br1/2)2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2Br2; in contrast, the HSE06 estimate aligns much more closely with the latter.20,21,26 To put this into perspective, Table 3 provides the band gap values for Cs2PbBr4 and Cs2PbI2Br2 from our previous work, calculated using both the GGA and mBJ models. Band gap values obtained from the GGA functional are in excellent agreement with one another, and the computed band gaps for Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr closely match those of Cs2PbBr4 and Cs2PbI2Br2 when evaluated with the HSE06 hybrid functional and the mBJ approach, respectively.

Both sets of curves confirm that the compounds behave as semiconductors. The valence band maximum (VBM), ranging from −2.8 to 0 eV, is mainly contributed by halide p orbitals and determines the hole mass, with contributions from the Pb-6s, Se/S-3p, and N-2p orbitals. These orbitals are close to the Fermi energy level, and thus a small amount of energy can affect hole transport. A core valence band, ranging from −5 to −3 eV, also includes Se-p, S-p, I-p, N-p, Br-p, and N-s states. The conduction band minimum (CBM) is dominated by Pb-p with minor contributions of Pb-s, halogen p, and the p orbital of the thiocyanate/selenocyanate ligand, which determines electron mass. In the conduction band maximum, Cs-s, N-p, C-p, Se-p, and S-p states play a leading role.

Smaller effective masses imply higher charge carrier mobility because electrical conductivity (σ) varies inversely with effective mass m*.51 The values of the effective masses of the electron and hole are comparable to those of the organic counterparts40 and to our previously reported values.45 The effective masses of the holes of these materials are concentrated in proximity, a phenomenon that would be recognized by the similarity of their valence band curvatures.

One of the most popular models for the calculation of exciton binding energies is the Mott–Wannier (MW) model, which is particularly useful for bulk materials. Other models, including the hydrogenic model, are derived from it. Hydrogenic or fractional-dimensional models are more suitable for true 2D materials, where the binding energy is underestimated by the MW model.52,53 However, the 2D semiconductor composition in layered perovskites does not exist in the 3D perovskite form, and therefore, Eb,3D does not exist, which is the basis for the hydrogenic model.20 A previous study of Cs2PbI2Cl2 has reported binding energy using the MW model. The pressure coefficient of the dielectric constant was calculated and found to rise from 3.97 to 4.36, which was attributed to a decrease in the interlayer spacing from 3.10 Å to 2.74 Å, leading to a reduction in the exciton binding energy from 133 meV to 78 meV.54 The present materials are quasi-2D, not true 2D materials, and have much shorter interlayer distances (1.69–1.88 Å) than those of typical 2D materials (4–6 Å). Thus, the MW model is still applicable, and we used the MW theory53 for the estimation of the exciton binding energy (Eb), which determines exciton dynamics, diffusion, and major optical characteristics.3Large static direction-averaged dielectric constants (ε1(0)) and lower exciton mass (µ) result in a small Eb (revealing high charge carrier mobility). The calculated values are 114, 121, 130, and, 141 meV for Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IB, respectively, and are listed in Table 3. The table reveals that these values are lower than the experimental value of 160 meV reported for Cs2Pb(SCN)2Br2.20 The underestimated value may be attributed to the narrower band gaps of iodine-based pseudo-halide perovskites compared to those of Br-based perovskites. The table further shows the comparison of the calculated values of binding energies with our previously reported theoretical values for Cs2Pb(SCN)2Br2, Cs2PbBr4, and Cs2PbI2Br2.29,45 The binding energies of Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr lie in the suitable range for solar cell applications.55

Optical properties

Optoelectronic properties are important in materials engineering, photonics, and the integration of electronic devices, especially in developing photovoltaic (PV) cells, optical sensors, and lasers. Characterizations of materials are based on dielectric functions (ε1(ω) and ε2(ω)), absorption coefficient (α(ω)), and refractive index (n(ω)), all of which can be described as the interaction of incident light with electronic states involved in the interband transitions between the valence band and the conduction band. Since optical parameters are not only dependent on the electronic structure but also on the chemical composition of a material and its crystal symmetry, these optical parameters have to be calculated for each candidate material. For the orthorhombic pseudo-halide perovskites Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr, the required coefficients have been calculated from the Kramers–Kronig analysis coupled with the Drude model,56 and the resulting trends are summarized in Fig. 5–8.

The real part of the static dielectric constant ε1(ω) is made up of two components, the electronic ε1e(ω) and the ionic ε1i(ω). Weakly bound excitons extended over various unit cells can be screened by the lattice, while strongly bound localized excitons cannot. While there are more sophisticated expressions for the resulting radius-dependent interpolation of the dielectric constant between ε1e(ω) and ε1e(ω) + ε1i(ω), commonly, ε1e(ω) + ε1i(ω) is used for weakly bound excitons (such as narrow gap semiconductors), ε1e is used for strongly bound excitons, and an ad hoc intermediate value is used for intermediately strongly bound excitons.57 For these anisotropic 2D pseudo-halide perovskites, both the parallel ε∥ and perpendicular (ε⊥) and direction-averaged dielectric constant (ε1(0)) are reflected in Fig. 5. Since Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2 and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr are structurally anisotropic, the behavior of ε1(ω) varies significantly with the direction of polarization with respect to coordinate axes. Along the z-axis, inorganic spacer cations constrain the electronic response and suppress the magnitude of ε1(ω), whereas for electric fields along the xy plane from lower frequency, a maximum between 2.28–4.9 eV, then falls off because of direct inter-band excitations. In the optical visible region, ε1(ω) attains a maximum between 2.28–4.9 eV, with the negative values between 5.0–6 eV, reflecting plasmon excitation (Ep), which is a metallic phenomenon of a material58 resulting in the total reflection of photons.

The imaginary part of the dielectric function ε2(ω) quantifies the ability of a substance to absorb light, i.e., to have photons induce an excitation of electrons in the valence band to the conduction band. In Fig. 6, the beginning of observable absorption is comparable to the size of the electronic band gap. Intense absorption spans the visible and ultraviolet ranges, occurring at 2.28–6 eV for Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2 and Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, 2.43–6 eV for Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and 2.51–6 eV for Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr. This broad absorption suggests that these materials are promising for LEDs, photo-detectors and solar cells. The major peaks are attributed to thiocyanate SCN, selenocyanate SeCN, and bromide I–p states transitioning to the Pb-p conduction states, with minor peaks between 6.49 and 7 eV due to a shift to mixed Pb-p and Cs-d states.

Light propagation is controlled by the refractive index (n(ω)), with larger values associated with smaller band gaps. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the highest n(ω) = 3.2 is in the visible-UV region, and it is much higher than that of the MAPbI3-layered perovskite (2.61).59 The optical absorption spectrum in Fig. 8 shows that Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, compared to other materials such as Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr, possesses enhanced light-harvesting performance throughout the visible-UV spectrum of technological interest.

These advantageous optical observables encourage the calculation of PV parameters. Such important characteristics, including open-circuit voltage (Voc), short-circuit density (Jsc), fill factor (FF), and overall PCE, are obtained with the help of SCAPS-1D,42 a popular drift-diffusion model. The n–i–p architecture shown in Fig. 9 has the fluorinated tin oxide (FTO) at the top, followed by an electron transport layer (ETL), the Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2 absorber, a hole transport layer (HTL), and copper (Cu) or gold (Au) as a metallic back contact, which was tried (but gold is costly to work with). SnO2 usually can serve as an ETL because alternative materials like TiO2 or ZnO suffer from either limited conductivity or unstable interface chemistry. Its large band gap (3.6 eV) and high electron mobility reduce hysteresis and curb photo-induced corrosion. SnO2 was considered as an ETL due to its large band gap (∼3.6 eV), high electron mobility, optical transparency, and band alignment with the Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2 absorber layer. The energy band alignment of the FTO/SnO2/Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2/Spiro-OMeTAD heterostructure was designed based on the electron affinity and band gap data of the individual layers, as reported in the literature. The SnO2/absorber interface ensures the efficient extraction of electrons while avoiding interfacial recombination, whereas the valence band alignment with Spiro-OMeTAD ensures efficient hole transport and selective carrier extraction, as shown in Fig. 10.60,61 Cu2O, if carefully synthesized, remains a green, economical HTL and adds the needed p-type transport while limiting heavy-metal content. Due to this limitation, the optimized Spiro-MeOTAD is used as the HTL, which gives high efficiency compared to Cu2O.62,63

The thickness of the layers is the key to device performance, efficiency of materials, and the total cost of production. A well-matched absorber thickness permits one to collect the charge effectively by balancing light absorption and recombination losses.68,69 A simulation with a composition of x = 0.4 was conducted with the aim of exploring the optimum range of thicknesses of Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2. The absorber depth was varied in a systematic manner between 300 nm and 800 nm during the simulation, as shown in Fig. 13. Voc, Jsc, FF, and PCE increase gradually with an increase in thickness. The absorber is made to grow in thickness and gradually more and more long-wavelength photons are absorbed, and this energy is transferred into electron–hole pairs due to the enormous absorption coefficient of the material, which is greater than > 104 cm−1. This observation agrees with Beer's law that the internal carrier density increases exponentially with the depth of the layer,70 but also suggests that at some point, the incremental increase in carrier density makes less contribution to overall device behavior than the parasitic contributions do. Below a critical limit of about 200 nm, there is a gradual decrease in all primary values; under-thick layers cannot absorb the complete photon spectrum, excess charge carriers will recombine at the back interface, and limit the voltage and current levels. Therefore, the growth in efficiency above this point indicates that a practical value of around 800 nm thickness is adequate to capture almost all incident light but with minimal losses, thereby facilitating stable power conversion above 18.12%.

Conclusions

First-principles calculations were used to investigate the crystal structure, electronic properties, and light-matter interactions of layered RP pseudo-halide perovskites Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2, Cs2Pb(SeCN)2IBr, Cs2Pb(SCN)2I2, and Cs2Pb(SCN)2IBr. All calculations were based on fully relaxed unit cells in order to obtain self-consistent band structures, electronic, and optical properties. In these materials, 2D-layered perovskites behave as quantum wells, trapping carriers, while the anionic inter-layers (spacer) act as potential barriers. The octahedral and tolerance factors confirm that these materials are stable in an orthorhombic crystal arrangement. Low exciton binding energies, small carrier effective masses, and direct band gaps are exhibited by these materials, which make them promising candidates for use in optoelectronic and photovoltaic applications. The band gaps calculated with HSE06 are in good agreement with the literature values and with related available experimental data. The optical coefficients, such as the complex dielectric functions, refractive index, and absorption, exhibit a prominent band gap dependence. Particularly, the highest refractive index is 3.2, and absorption is found in the visible to ultraviolet energy range (2.28–4.5 eV). Analysis of the quantum efficiency shows good performance in the visible region and partially in the ultraviolet range of the electromagnetic spectrum. The Cs2Pb(SeCN)2I2 compound has good PV characteristics with a short-circuit current density of 10.2 mA cm−2 and a fill factor of 91.2%, which results in a maximum power conversion efficiency of 18.12%. This performance, along with an appropriate band gap and improved stability, places these RP pseudo-halide perovskites as replacements for conventional 3D halide perovskites in optoelectronic, PV, as well as light-emitting applications.
