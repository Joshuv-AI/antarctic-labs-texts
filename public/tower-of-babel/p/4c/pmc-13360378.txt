Awareness of the Mediterranean Diet and its Perceived Effect on Cardiovascular Health in the Western Region, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdullah M. Alharbi, Mohammed S. Alsaedi, Mutaz S. Alharbi, Faisal F. Almutairi, Adeeb M. Aloufi, Azzam M. Alharbi, Rakan D. Alsaedi, Hatim F. Alhejaili, Motasem M. Alyamani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjective:To increase awareness of mediterranean diet (MD) and its perceived effects on cardiovascular health, enhance knowledge and inform public health efforts aimed at improving the population's health outcomes of. The MD comprises heart-healthy eating patterns high in vegetables, legumes, nuts and seeds, and fruits.Methods:This cross-sectional study involved adult participants who were living in the Western region of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The data were collected online through social media platforms, then cleaned, coded, and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 21 to generate key insights.Results:A total of 390 participants were included in the study. The study noted gender differences in the awareness of core MD components, with females showing better knowledge than males (p = 0.009). Compared to other age groups, participants aged 18–29 years demonstrated significantly higher awareness of the diet (p = 0.008) and cardiovascular risk factors (p = 0.009). Common foods cited as good sources of dietary fiber include oats (302, 48.5%) and legumes (199, 31.9%).Conclusion:The study demonstrated a lack of awareness of the MD among the participants, but female and young adults demonstrated better awareness of the diet and its perceived effects on cardiovascular health. The research identified critical deficiencies in knowledge and awareness regarding the importance of MD in cardiovascular health. These findings call for greater public education and awareness regarding healthy lifestyle and dietary choices, aimed at encouraging healthy practices and enhancing overall wellbeing.

Introduction

Mediterranean diet (MD) emphasizes the intake of fresh, minimally processed, plant-derived foods, primarily including fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and various other plant-based items [1,2]. This dietary approach intentionally limits the intake of dairy products, red and processed meats, and sugars while allowing for moderate consumption of fish and poultry [3]. Research by Martínez-González et al. [4] indicates that following the MD plays a substantial role in enhancing cardiovascular health by lowering significant risk factors like cholesterol levels, hypertension, and obesity.

Research shows that the MD not only prevents overall cardiovascular events but also significantly reduces susceptibility to specific cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) like acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and hypertension [5]. These benefits are not necessarily associated with any particular component of the MD; rather, the benefits come from a holistic interaction of the different components that make up the MD.

Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) has experienced significant socioeconomic growth over recent decades, resulting in substantial changes to lifestyles. As a result, the prevalence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) among the Saudi population has reached unprecedented levels, making it 1 of the primary causes of death in the nation [6]. A systematic review of research conducted in KSA identifies 4 key factors contributing to the heightened risk of CVD: physical inactivity, obesity, poor dietary habits, and smoking [7]. In parallel with this rapid socioeconomic advancement, there has been a notable rise in the intake of processed foods that are high in calories, fats, and sugars [7]. Fast foods have progressively replaced the traditional local diet that characterized Saudi food. Research has consistently advocated consumption of the MD which helps people attain maximum nutritional benefits while minimizing caloric intake [8,9].

Despite the health benefits associated with the MD, little research has assessed the Saudi population's knowledge and awareness of the MD and its associated health benefits. Given the distinct cultural context of dietary habits in KSA, and the fact that the MD originates from countries bordering the Mediterranean Sea rather than from traditional Saudi cuisine, it is important to examine the extent to which modern-day Saudis are aware of this dietary pattern and its connection to cardiovascular health. Thus, this study seeks to assess the knowledge and awareness of the MD and its potential cardiovascular health benefits among adults in the Western Region of KSA. The results of this study will help to inform public health policy, particularly with regards to cardiovascular health.

Method

This research, conducted between November 2024 and December 2025, utilized a cross-sectional design conducted among the adult population in the Western region of KSA. An online survey was distributed among the target population, which included individuals aged 18 years and above. Exclusion criteria were age under 18 years, living outside the specified region, and age over 65 years.

The study included a total of 390 adult residents from the Western region. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. The sample size was calculated based on an estimated population of 5,000,000, a hypothesized outcome frequency of 50%, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence level, yielding a minimum required sample of 385 participants. To account for potential incomplete responses, a total of 390 participants were included in the final analysis.

Data was collected using the Mediterranean Diet Nutrition Knowledge Questionnaire (Med-NKQ), a pre-validated, self-administered tool. The original Med-NKQ is a 20-item instrument that was developed and validated to assess awareness of the MD and its effect on cardiovascular health in an Australian population [10]. For this study, the questionnaire was culturally and linguistically adapted for the Saudi context. This process involved forward translation from English to Arabic by a professional translator, followed by a review and validation of the translated version by a panel of experts, including a professor of English language and nutrition specialists, to ensure conceptual accuracy, clarity, and cultural appropriateness. A pilot study involving 40 participants (10% of the total sample) was conducted to assess feasibility, clarity, and internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha) of the adapted Arabic instrument. The finalized Arabic questionnaire was developed using an online Google forms platform and distributed electronically to participants across the major cities of the Western region.

After collecting the data, the information was input into a Microsoft excel spreadsheet for initial cleaning. This process involved removing duplicate entries, dealing with unusual data points, and excluding responses that were incomplete. Once the data was cleaned, it was coded and transferred into Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 21, for further analysis. A Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to check if the data followed a normal distribution. For descriptive statistics, categorical data was shown as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). To examine the relationships between socio-demographic factors (independent variables) and knowledge scores regarding cardiovascular risk factors and core MD foods (dependent variables), non-parametric tests were used. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied to compare 2 groups, such as gender-based, while the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for comparisons among more than 2 groups, such as age, city, income, and education. To categorize participants into low, moderate, and high awareness levels of MD, the following procedure was applied. First, a total knowledge score was calculated for each participant. The arithmetic mean of the total scores was 31.4 (SD = 5.3). Using the mean and standard deviation, the distribution was transformed into 3 ordinal levels: low awareness was defined as a score below the mean minus 1 standard deviation (≤26); high awareness was defined as a score above the mean plus 1 standard deviation (≥37); and moderate awareness was defined as scores falling between these 2 thresholds (27–36). Subsequently, the total awareness variable was recorded into these 3 categories. To examine associations between socio-demographic characteristics (gender, age, city, monthly income, educational level) and these 3 awareness levels (low, moderate, high), a Chi-square test was used, as it is appropriate for comparing categorical and ordinal descriptive variables. A p - value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study received ethical approval from the appropriate Institutional Review Board before it began. Each participant provided their consent electronically before completing the questionnaire. Everyone was free to choose whether to take part, and there were no rewards given. The privacy and anonymity of all participants were carefully protected during the entire research process, and the information gathered was used only for academic purposes.

Results

The study included a total of 390 participants (Table 1). Most respondents were males (67.9%) and within the age group of 18–29 years (60.5%). The majority resided in Al-Madina Al-Munawwarah (50.2%), while smaller proportions were from Jeddah (19.2%), Taif (10.3%), Yanbu (10.3%), and Makkah Al-Mukarramah (9.7%). A large proportion held a bachelor's degree (65.9%), followed by those with secondary education (25.1%); only a small percentage had only primary or intermediate education. More than half of the participants reported a monthly income of less than 5000 Saudi Riyals (SAR) (51%).

Survey results indicated that 48.5% of participants (n = 302) correctly identified oats as a good source of dietary fiber, and 31.9% (n = 199) correctly identified legumes. Additionally, nearly one-quarter of the participants correctly identified smoked salmon (142, 28.3%) as containing high sodium. About half, 187 (47.9%) of the participants correctly identified pasta with tomato sauce as a food reflecting the concept of MD for heart health, but only 76 participants (19.5%) were aware that the diet reduces the risk of heart attack by 30%.

Fewer than a third of participants (26.0%, n = 171) were aware of the role of extra virgin olive oil as a core component. More respondents correctly identified leafy green vegetables (45.6%, n = 282) and unsalted nuts (35.6%, n = 220). Nearly one-third 205 (35%) of the participants correctly reported grilling as the most common cooking method in the MD. When asked to identify common flavors in the MD, participants most frequently gave correct answers for lemon (40.4%, n = 280), oregano (27.4%, n = 190), and garlic (25.7%, n = 178). A majority (304, 77.9%) were aware that water is the beverage most often consumed in the MD; and more than half (199, 51%) knew that vegetable and lentil soup with whole grain bread and olive oil is an excellent meal choice for heart health. Only 142 participants (36.4%) knew the most suitable diet for breakfast. The results showed a significant statistical relationship between age and awareness of CVD risk factors (p = 0.009). Participants aged 18–29 years demonstrated better general awareness of CVD risk factors than other age groups. Other socio-demographic variables were not statistically significant (Table 4).

Discussion

The rising burden of CVD in KSA, driven by shifting lifestyles and dietary patterns, creates a need for preventive nutritional strategies like the MD [11,12]. A critical challenge is that, while the need for such interventions is growing, awareness of the MD among the adult population in Western, KSA remains low. Our findings show that the majority of participants demonstrated low to moderate awareness of the diet, a trend consistent with low adherence rates reported in KSA [13]. This gap shows that we must develop clear and targeted awareness campaigns that present the MD not as a foreign concept, but as a flexible and beneficial pattern that can include local foods and flavors.

The demographic patterns within our data offer crucial insights for designing these targeted awareness campaigns. The significantly higher awareness among young adults (aged 18–29) suggests that educational institutions are a key channel for sharing information. This group, often more exposed to global health trends and nutritional information in academic settings, can be leveraged as early adopters and peer influencers. Furthermore, the observed gender and geographical disparities—with females and residents of Taif city demonstrating better knowledge—highlight the role of social and cultural exposure. Taif's status as a tourist hub may facilitate a broad exchange of culinary practices, suggesting that an effective strategy could be promoting the MD through a lens of cultural variety, rather than health alone. A particularly revealing finding was the high MD awareness among lower-income groups, which may reflect the high proportion of students in our sample. This is a key opportunity; public health messaging should actively counter the idea that MD is expensive. Campaigns can focus on its affordable core components—like legumes, whole grains, and seasonal vegetables—and provide practical, budget-friendly cooking guides. Partnering with universities to offer workshops and affordable MD-based meals in cafeterias could directly reach this engaged but often budget-conscious group.

Beyond general awareness, our study identified specific knowledge deficits that need addressing. Although participants showed a basic understanding of certain heart-healthy concepts, considerable uncertainty existed about the core components of the MD, such as low recognition of extra virgin olive oil as a cornerstone of the diet. Healthcare providers can play a central role in improving this. During routine check-ups, doctors and nurses can give simple, clear advice about the proven benefits of the MD, such as its potential to reduce heart attack risk. Making this connection between the diet and a powerful health outcome can motivate people to learn more.

Most participants expressed uncertainty regarding basic questions about the MD, reflecting a widespread lack of knowledge about its health benefits. The findings strongly indicate a need for enhanced public awareness. By using targeted education programs, correcting misconceptions, empowering healthcare providers to give simple advice, and highlighting the diet's proven benefits, we can begin to close this knowledge gap. Enhancing understanding of the MD is crucial for promoting health dietary habits and lifestyle choices, which are essential for improving health outcomes of the population in KSA.

Study limitations

Firstly, the cross-sectional design permitted the examination of relationships among the variables, yet it fell short of establishing causal links. Secondly, the reliance on a self-administered online survey for data collection may have introduced bias, as there was no method to verify the accuracy of participants’ responses. Additionally, utilizing convenience sampling through social media platforms could limit the generalizability of the results, especially since those with greater digital literacy, internet access, and a heightened interest in health-related matters may be overrepresented. Finally, as the research focused solely on the Western region of KSA, its findings may not be relevant to other populations.

In conclusion, the study demonstrated a lack of awareness of the MD among the participants, but female and young adults demonstrated better awareness of the diet and its perceived effects on cardiovascular health. The research identified critical deficiencies in knowledge and awareness regarding the importance of MD in cardiovascular health. While some participants demonstrated knowledge of specific heart-healthy practices, overall understanding of the MD as a comprehensive dietary pattern remained limited. These findings call for greater public education and awareness regarding healthy lifestyle and dietary choices, aimed at encouraging healthy practices and enhancing overall wellbeing.
