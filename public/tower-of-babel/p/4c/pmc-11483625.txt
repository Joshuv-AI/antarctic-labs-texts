Treatment of oesophageal perforation with through-the-scope clips post-high-power short-duration atrial fibrillation ablation: case report
Authors: Henriette Zimmeck, Thomas Deneke, Ulrich Halm, Markus Zachäus, Sotirios Nedios
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrio-oesophageal fistula following percutaneous catheter ablation is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication. This case report highlights the advantages of a less invasive treatment for covered oesophageal perforation.Case summaryA 66-year-old male patient underwent catheter ablation with high-power short-duration radiofrequency for symptomatic (EHRA III, tachy-cardiomyopathy) persistent atrial fibrillation (AF). Post-procedural routine endoscopic examination revealed a thermal oesophageal lesion. Progression of the lesions in early follow-up examinations led to a CT scan that showed a covered perforation. Antibiotic coverage and parenteral nutrition were initiated. The patient was transferred to a multidisciplinary centre for evaluation of invasive treatments. Endoscopically, the lesions were slowly progressing, while retaining their endoluminal borders. Application of five through-the-scope clips resulted in temporary shielding that was replaced with two further clips 3 days later. This allowed for internal oesophageal healing and prevention of mediastinal infections. Due to recurrence of the AF, a repeat ablation with pulsed field was performed. Follow-up endoscopic examination after one year revealed no relevant lesions. Sinus rhythm and slight odynophagia persisted.DiscussionThis case underlines the importance of less invasive treatments for oesophageal lesions after catheter ablation, where endoluminal borders are preserved and approximation of the wound-margins allows for tissue repair. Although, this kind of treatment requires timely and thorough investigations as endoscopy and CT scan to exclude (peri-)cardiac involvement.

Introduction

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) with catheter ablation is the cornerstone of invasive treatment for atrial fibrillation (AF). However, this includes some risk for complications. Especially oesophageal injury remains a rare but significant and potentially fatal risk for patients undergoing temperature-based ablation. While oesophageal lesions are common, ranging from 10% to 15% after high-power short-duration (HPSD) ablation, only deep lesions progress to fistula formations that usually occur weeks after the procedure and carry a high mortality risk (55%–80%).1–4 Depending on the lesion extension, there are different therapeutic options from conservative treatment using proton-pump inhibitors (PPI) until oesophageal stenting or surgical treatment.3–6 While early surgical repair offers the best outcomes with a 33% mortality rate compared to oesophageal stenting (68%) or conservative care (97%), surgical intervention still carries risks that might be avoided with minimally invasive, non-surgical treatments.7 This case report demonstrates a minimal invasive approach for a covered perforation by using through-the-scope (TTS) metal clips.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old man with persistent AF, obesity, hyperlipoproteinaemia, and known tachy-cardiomyopathy (left ventricular ejection fraction 45% during AF and 65% during sinus rhythm) catheter ablation with HPSD radiofrequency (50 W, max. 10 s) under general anaesthesia. As part of routine post-operative diagnostics, despite the lack of symptoms, endoscopic examination was performed 1 day after the ablation. A 5 mm fibrin-covered thermal lesion was documented at 30 cm from the dentition (Figure 1). The patient received parenteral nutrition via a central venous catheter. Antibiotic shielding with meropenem (3 × 1 g/day) and caspofungin (50 mg/day) as well as high-dose PPI (40 mg/day) were given. Mild odynophagia was observed a few days after ablation. Vital signs were stable at all times and the patient developed no fever, while remaining at the hospital with no new symptoms. Due to progression in follow-up endoscopic examinations, a CT scan was performed 10 days after the initial procedure. An air-filled pocket formation protruding beyond the outer oesophageal contour as part of a covered perforation was revealed. The patient was transferred to a specialized cardiac centre with an affiliated cardiac surgery and gastroenterology department. A new endoscopic examination (two weeks post-ablation) revealed a deep thermal lesion with a diameter of 12 mm without any suspicion of fistula. Due to slow progression without evidence of pericardial or cardiac involvement, the lesion was treated by endoscopic clipping closure using five TTS clips. A follow-up examination 3 days later revealed a spontaneous descent of a central clip with a small dehiscence. Closure was achieved with two more clips (Figure 2). The oral diet was switched from parenteral to liquid and mash food during the hospital stay. Antibiotic shielding (as above) and high-dose PPI were stopped 14 days after the first clipping procedure. Oral anticoagulation was restarted 2 days after clipping. The patient switched to a normal diet 4 days after discharge. Mild odynophagia persisted, but a new endoscopy one year after the procedure showed slight reddening of the mucosa without relevant lesions. After one year, AF recurred and a pulsed field ablation was performed. Since then, the patient has remained AF-free.

Discussion

This case underlines the relevance of interdisciplinary collaboration in patients with oesophageal thermal lesions. Furthermore, it demonstrates the importance of early diagnostic and treatment—especially the value of routine post-procedural gastroscopy. Therefore, procedures should be performed at centres with experience and multidisciplinary expertise whenever possible. Oesophageal lesions occur relatively frequently with the use of temperature-based AF ablation procedures (10%–15%).3,4 Only in rare cases oesophageal fistula formation does occur, usually weeks after ablation.5 This indicates a highly complex pathophysiology that requires individual assessment and evaluation. A clear pathophysiological connection between the rather harmless thermal lesions and the sometimes-devastating oesophageal fistulas has not yet been established. The possible pathogenic mechanism consists of thermal lesions leading to microvascular obstructions in the oesophageal mucosa, which cause punctate necrosis and the development of mediastinitis. Peristaltic movement could precipitate deeper lesions and result in a fistula connecting the oesophageal lumen to the mediastinum, and in worse cases to the pericardium (Figure 3).4,5

The extent of dilatation of the atrium in atrial fibrillation patients and the resulting larger contact area between the atrium and the oesophagus may also be important in this regard. Some data also point to the involvement of gastro-oesophageal reflux in this process.3 Metal clip treatment is intended to ensure optimal adhesion of the wound edges and thus enable secondary wound healing.

Reduction of risk for oesophageal thermal lesions is possible by using non-temperature-based ablation techniques such as pulsed field ablation.8 By using electrical pulses to cause nonthermal irreversible electroporation and induce cardiac cell death, thermally mediated complications are prevented.9 Therefor, this procedure was chosen for second ablation in the present case.

The pathophysiological considerations and the risk of complications of more invasive treatments like oesophageal stenting, surgery, and drainage support the use of primarily less invasive methods. Complications of surgical procedures include bleeding, infections as well as general anaesthesia associated risks. Chest pain, oesophageal stenosis, and stent dislocation are complications described for oesophageal stenting.4–6,10,11 In targeted minimally invasive, non-surgical care, lower periprocedural risk and presumably faster recovery are expected. However, current data do not yet allow for a standardized selection process for respective less invasive treatment options, with surgical treatment being proven as the safest, as well as the most invasive treatment strategy.1 To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report of clipping for covered oesophageal perforation. In the present case, early diagnosis and limited expansion of the lesion were determining for the choice of treatment. However, this method should be considered exclusively for patients with isolated covered perforation without pericardiac or atrial involvement as consequences of misjudgement can be life-threatening.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates the advantages of a less invasive treatment for covered oesophageal perforations or lesions after catheter ablation for AF. The use of TTS metal clips allowed for minimally invasive, non-surgical care with consequently lower periprocedural risk and presumably faster recovery. Pre-procedural CT scan and close follow-ups in such cases are recommended for a safe and effective treatment. However, the described method should be considered only for patients with covered perforations without pericardial involvement. Early diagnosis through post-interventional routine endoscopic examination after PVI was crucial for the management decisions in this case. Further research is warranted to optimize procedural strategies in patients with an oesophageal lesion or perforation.
