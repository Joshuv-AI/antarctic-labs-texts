Early MRCP surveillance for recurrent primary sclerosing cholangitis after liver transplantation: a prospective imaging study
Authors: Emil Bik, Anna Stadnik, Karolina M. Wronka, Maciej Wójcicki, Joanna Raszeja-Wyszomirska, Piotr Milkiewicz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The true incidence and early course of recurrent primary sclerosing cholangitis (rPSC) after liver transplantation (LT) remain uncertain, largely because most studies rely on clinical/biochemical features which trigger imaging. We prospectively evaluated rPSC incidence, risk factors, and outcomes in all adult PSC transplant recipients (January 2017 - September 2024) using a predefined magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) surveillance protocol at 1 and 3 years. rPSC was diagnosed by Mayo criteria, and advanced rPSC was defined by therapeutic endoscopic intervention, retransplantation, or death. Among 119 recipients, 105 were eligible for follow-up and 102 completed at least the first scheduled MRCP. Recurrent disease occurred in 50 of 102 recipients (49.0%), including 31 (30.4%) already detected at the first imaging. During a median follow-up of 56 months, advanced rPSC developed in 15/102 patients (14.7%). Ulcerative colitis, de novo post-transplant inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and post-transplant IBD flare were associated with recurrence. Independent predictors of advanced recurrence were male sex, cytomegalovirus viremia, and lower donor body mass index, whereas cytomegalovirus donor-recipient mismatch was protective. Cytomegalovirus viremia was observed in 9 of 102 patients. Kaplan–Meier analyses showed faster progression to advanced recurrence in patients with cytomegalovirus viremia than in cytomegalovirus-negative recipients. Protocol-driven MRCP surveillance demonstrates that rPSC is frequent and often detectable early after liver transplantation, identifying a potential therapeutic window before clinical manifestations occur. Distinct risk profiles may suggest two phenotypes: an IBD-driven and a cytomegalovirus-associated form.

Introduction

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a chronic, progressive cholestatic liver disease characterized by inflammation and fibrosis of the intra- and extrahepatic bile ducts, ultimately leading to cirrhosis, liver failure, and the need for liver transplantation (LT)1–4. Although LT is the only curative option for end-stage PSC, disease recurrence in the graft (recurrent PSC - rPSC), remains one of the major long-term complications5.

The reported incidence of rPSC varies widely, ranging from 10% to over 50% of recipients6. Such variation reflects substantial methodological differences among studies: differences in follow-up duration, heterogeneity in imaging protocols, and lack of standardized diagnostic criteria7,8.

Importantly, the timing and frequency of magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) have a direct impact on rPSC detection rates. In many cohorts, imaging was performed only when clinically indicated, leading to potential underestimation of subclinical disease9–12. By contrast, prospective studies with predefined imaging protocols may show higher recurrence rates, suggesting that the true incidence of rPSC may have been underestimated6.

Another critical issue is the heterogeneous clinical presentation of rPSC. While some patients present with radiological abnormalities without biochemical or clinical progression, others rapidly develop graft dysfunction, require endoscopic interventions, or progress to retransplantation6,8. This heterogeneity complicates both the interpretation of epidemiological data and the identification of risk factors. Reported associations include male sex, younger age at LT, concomitant inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) activity, cytomegalovirus (CMV) viremia, and donor-related factors, although findings have been inconsistent13–17.

Taken together, existing literature underscores two major gaps: [1] the true incidence of rPSC remains unclear due to heterogeneous study designs and inconsistent imaging strategies; and [2] there is limited understanding of which patients develop clinically significant disease. Prospective MRCP surveillance cohorts provide a unique opportunity to address these uncertainties by capturing both symptomatic and subclinical recurrence and evaluating their clinical relevance. In this context, early identification of subclinical disease may allow closer surveillance and optimization of post-transplant management and could be particularly relevant in the context of emerging antifibrotic and disease-modifying therapies.

The present study therefore aimed to assess the incidence, risk factors, and outcomes of rPSC in a prospective single-center cohort of PSC transplant recipients undergoing routine MRCP surveillance at predefined time points.

Results

Between January 2017 and September 2024, 119 adult patients underwent LT for PSC at our center. Fourteen patients were not eligible for analysis due to events occurring before the first scheduled MRCP: 9 underwent re-LT and 5 died. None of these early events were attributable to rPSC. The remaining 105 patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria and entered follow-up.

During the observation period, 3 patients did not attend any of the scheduled MRCP examinations and were therefore not evaluable. The final study cohort comprised 102 patients. A detailed flow diagram of patient selection and follow-up is shown in Fig. 1.

All patients underwent the first scheduled MRCP according to the predefined surveillance schedule at a median of 14 months after transplantation (IQR: 12–17). Of these, 21 patients had only the first MRCP available, as the 3-year follow-up period required for the second examination had not yet been reached. The remaining 81 patients completed both scheduled MRCP examinations, with a median time to the second MRCP of 37 months (IQR: 30–45).

Baseline characteristics

The main characteristics of the 102 patients included in the study are given in Table 1. The median age at LT was 35.5 years (IQR 29.0–42.0) and 65 (63.7%) were male. PSC/AIH variant was present in 25 patients (24.5%). The median duration of PSC before LT was 6.3 years (IQR 3.9–9.3). At the time of transplantation, cirrhosis was present in 66 patients (64.7%), and cholangiocarcinoma was identified in 9 patients (8.8%).

Donor age had a median of 42.5 years (IQR 28.0–53.0). Donor sex was male in 65 (63.7%) cases. Biliary reconstruction was performed as Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy in 75 patients (73.5%) and duct-to-duct anastomosis in 27 recipients (26.5%). CMV viremia after LT was detected in 9 (8.8%) patients.

IBD-related features and colectomy

Regarding inflammatory bowel disease, ulcerative colitis was diagnosed in 74 patients, Crohn’s disease in 7, unclassified IBD in 4, and no IBD in 15. Data on IBD status were unavailable for two additional recipients. IBD had been diagnosed before LT in 75 patients and after LT in 10.

Regarding IBD therapy, the most intensive treatment ever received included: 5-ASA in 42 (41.2%), systemic steroids in 7 (6.9%), azathioprine in 10 (9.8%), combined systemic steroids and azathioprine in 6 (5.9%), biologics in 13 (12.7%), surgery in 5 (4.9%), and no IBD-directed treatment in 19 (18.6%).

Colectomy was performed before LT in 2 (2.0%) patients, with a median interval between colectomy and transplantation of 7.37 years (IQR 5.16–9.58). Post-transplant colectomy was performed in 3 (2.9%) patients, with a median interval of 2.11 years (IQR 1.35–2.28).

Recurrence of PSC

Overall rPSC occurred in 50/102 (49.0%) recipients. Radiological recurrence was documented at the first and second MRCPs in 31/102 (30.4%) and 47/81 (58.0%) patients, respectively. Advanced rPSC occurred in 15/102 (14.7%) recipients (1 death, 1 death after reLT, 3 successful reLT, 10 ERCPs). The median time to advanced rPSC was 59 months (IQR 34–72).

Factors associated with rPSC

Baseline clinical and biological data of patients with and without rPSC are given in Table 2. In univariable analysis, ulcerative colitis was significantly more prevalent among patients with rPSC (83.3% vs. 65.4%, p = 0.041). De novo IBD diagnosed after liver transplantation was also more frequent in the rPSC group (16.7% vs. 3.9%, p = 0.045), and post-transplant IBD flare occurred more commonly in patients with rPSC (50.0% vs. 28.9%, p = 0.030). No significant differences were observed in recipient age, donor age, donor BMI, biliary anastomosis type, PSC/AIH variant, cirrhosis or cholangiocarcinoma at LT, liver biochemistry at PSC diagnosis or at the time of liver transplant.

Multivariable modeling for rPSC was not performed because of clear collinearity and expected interactions among the univariable risk markers (UC diagnosis, de novo IBD, and post-LT UC flare).

Factors associated with advanced rPSC

The median follow-up for the assessment of advanced rPSC (clinically significant recurrence leading to ERCP, retransplantation, or death) was 56 months (IQR 31–74).

Baseline clinical and biological data of patients with and without advanced rPSC are given in Table 3. Advanced rPSC was significantly more frequent in male recipients (93.3% vs. 58.6%, p = 0.010) and in those with detectable CMV viremia (26.7% vs. 5.7%, p = 0.025). Donor BMI was lower among patients with advanced rPSC with a trend toward association (23.1 vs. 25.2 kg/m², p = 0.063). A CMV donor–recipient serostatus mismatch (R–/D+) tended to be less common in the advanced rPSC group (6.7% vs. 32.2%, p = 0.060). The duct-to-duct biliary anastomosis and pre-transplant CMV IgG seropositivity showed a trend toward higher risk (p = 0.055 and p = 0.088, respectively). None of the baseline biochemical parameters reached statistical significance for advanced rPSC.

Discussion

Recurrent PSC after liver transplantation remains a challenging and incompletely understood complication. In previous reports, recurrence rates have varied widely, ranging from 10% to more than 50% of recipients depending on study design, follow-up length, and diagnostic approach6,9,18,19. Our prospective surveillance cohort refines the understanding of rPSC timing and frequency after liver transplantation. Approximately half of recipients developed cholangiographic features consistent with rPSC, while about one in seven of all recipients progressed to advanced disease requiring ERCP, retransplantation, or resulting in death during follow-up. Most recurrences were detected at the first scheduled MRCP, approximately one year post-transplant, indicating that pathogenic mechanisms begin early after LT. In contrast, large national cohorts relying on symptom- or laboratory-triggered imaging report a much later detection, with cumulative incidences reaching about 50% only after 20–25 years of follow-up9. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in surveillance intensity rather than disease biology, as scheduled MRCP captures radiologic changes that may remain clinically and biochemically silent for years before manifesting overt disease.

The high proportion of such biochemically silent recurrence in our cohort underscores the limitations of enzyme-based surveillance alone and supports the value of protocol-driven MRCP follow-up after LT. In the evolving therapeutic landscape, early detection of recurrent or progressive ductal changes may define a crucial intervention window, as several antifibrotic and disease-modifying agents for PSC including norursodeoxycholic acid, FXR agonists (cilofexor, tropifexor), and PPAR agonists (bezafibrate, elafibranor) have shown promising antifibrotic and anticholestatic activity in recent trials20,21. Together, these findings provide both epidemiologic insight and a rationale for integrating structured post-transplant MRCP surveillance into the design of future interventional studies. However, the introduction of a protocol-based MRCP surveillance strategy in routine clinical practice after transplantation may be limited by cost and organizational constraints. Although current EASL guidelines6 for PSC recommend annual MRCP in the context of oncologic surveillance, the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of structured post-transplant imaging protocols should be carefully evaluated before widespread implementation.

Collectively, these observations highlight that recurrent PSC is not only common but also heterogeneous in its clinical course. To better capture clinically meaningful endpoints, we introduced the term ‘advanced rPSC’ to distinguish radiologic recurrence with limited or indolent bile duct changes from cases progressing to clinically significant complications. This definition integrates the occurrence of ERCP-requiring strictures, retransplantation, or PSC-related death as a composite outcome, reflecting the threshold at which recurrent biliary injury translates into graft dysfunction. By separating early radiologic findings from advanced clinical disease, this concept provides a more nuanced framework for analyzing post-transplant cholangiopathy and its predictors. The distinction between early and advanced recurrence highlights the heterogeneity of post-transplant biliary injury and suggests that different mechanisms may underlie disease initiation and progression. Among these, CMV infection and intestinal inflammation emerged as the main clinical correlates in our cohort.

The strong independent association between post-transplant CMV viremia and advanced rPSC observed in our cohort adds biologic plausibility to the hypothesis that viral infection may contribute to graft injury. Prior work has linked CMV infection with endothelial dysfunction, microvascular inflammation, and biliary complications after LT13,16,22,23. Our data suggest that CMV-mediated biliary epithelial injury may drive progression from radiologic to clinically significant recurrence, while the protective effect of donor–recipient serostatus mismatch (R–/D+) likely reflects intensified antiviral prophylaxis. Given these observations, it can be hypothesized that prolonging and extending CMV prophylaxis to all PSC transplant recipients might be beneficial, although this requires confirmation in randomized controlled trials. Importantly, this CMV-driven pattern appears biologically and clinically distinct from the inflammatory, IBD-associated subtype of recurrence discussed below, suggesting heterogeneity in pathogenic mechanisms.

Inflammatory bowel disease activity also appeared to modulate recurrence risk. Post-LT IBD flare and ulcerative colitis, particularly when newly diagnosed after transplantation, were associated with rPSC but not with advanced disease, implying that mucosal inflammation may trigger early biliary injury without necessarily dictating rapid progression to graft failure. This pattern aligns with the hypothesis of a gut–liver immune axis in PSC and rPSC17,24–26. From a clinical standpoint, maintaining IBD remission after transplantation becomes critically important, not only to prevent intestinal morbidity but also as a potential strategy to mitigate the risk of recurrent biliary inflammation and rPSC. Optimal long-term IBD management including early therapeutic escalation and multidisciplinary follow-up should therefore be considered a cornerstone of post-transplant care in PSC recipients.

Taken together, these findings may point toward the existence of two clinically and biologically distinct phenotypes of recurrent PSC after liver transplantation. The first, IBD-driven subtype, appears linked to intestinal immune activation and IBD flares. It likely occurs more frequently, but follows a slower and more indolent course, which may eventually culminate in biliary interventions, retransplantation, or graft failure over longer follow-up. This pattern is consistent with the concept of a gut–liver axis, in which intestinal inflammation, increased permeability, and dysbiosis promote aberrant trafficking of gut-primed lymphocytes to the liver and sustained cholangiocyte-directed immune injury17,26. The second phenotype appears to be related to CMV viremia and is characterized by accelerated biliary injury and likely more aggressive progression toward advanced disease, potentially mediated by direct epithelial and endothelial injury, microvascular inflammation, and amplification of local immune responses22,23. Our median follow-up of 56 months may have been too short to observe the full trajectory of the IBD-related form, but the contrast between these patterns supports the concept that rPSC is a biologically heterogeneous condition with distinct initiating triggers and clinical behaviors. No phenotype-specific imaging differences were formally assessed in this study, and we cannot conclude that these subtypes are associated with distinct MRCP patterns. The use of automated image analysis or artificial intelligence-based approaches may help enable a more detailed and objective characterization of biliary imaging features in future studies.

The biochemical profile of rPSC also provided prognostic insight. In this cohort, UDCA exposure during the first post-transplant year was not associated with differences in biochemical cholestasis among patients with radiologic rPSC. Although a substantial proportion of radiologic recurrences occurred with normal ALP and GGT values, biochemical cholestasis at the time of the first MRCP was strongly associated with faster progression to advanced rPSC in landmark analysis. This suggests that while serum cholestatic enzymes may not identify all early recurrences, their elevation reflects a stage of ongoing bile-duct injury with high fibrotic potential. Consequently, integrating biochemical parameters into risk stratification, alongside systematic imaging, may help recognize patients at imminent risk of clinically significant progression.

Another noteworthy finding was the identification of male sex as an independent risk factor for advanced rPSC. The association between male sex and a higher risk of rPSC observed in our study is consistent with previous reports13. Sex-related differences in immune regulation, hormonal milieu, and bile duct vulnerability have been proposed as modulators of cholangiocyte function and may influence the development of immune-mediated cholangiopathies27. Lower donor BMI was also independently associated with a higher risk of advanced rPSC in our cohort. Data specifically linking donor BMI to PSC recurrence are scarce, whereas meta-analytic summaries emphasize other factors (e.g. donor age) rather than BMI per se7. Mechanistically, smaller-caliber arterial and biliary structures in low-BMI donors could increase susceptibility to ischemic biliary injury through size/flow mismatch, a possible driver of post-transplant cholangiopathy28.

This study has several limitations. It represents a single-center experience, which may limit generalizability, and the number of CMV-positive recipients, particularly those without IBD, was small, restricting power for subgroup and interaction analyses. In addition, all MRCP examinations were evaluated by a single radiologist, and interobserver variability could not be assessed. However, this approach ensured consistency of image evaluation across the cohort. Nonetheless, all patients were enrolled in a uniform surveillance protocol with standardized imaging intervals and blinded radiologic assessment, minimizing selection and detection bias. External validation in larger, multicenter cohorts will be required to confirm these associations and to refine the proposed disease model.

Taken together, our findings support the hypothesis that recurrent PSC after liver transplantation encompasses at least two clinical trajectories: a more common, inflammation-driven course linked to IBD activity, and a less frequent but rapidly progressive, CMV-associated form. While our data suggest distinct mechanistic pathways, it remains possible that CMV primarily acts as an accelerator within the inflammation-driven phenotype rather than defining a completely separate entity. Large, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these subtypes and to determine whether CMV acts as a phenotypic driver or an accelerating cofactor in rPSC after liver transplantation. Identifying these trajectories early could ultimately help tailor surveillance intensity and preventive strategies including optimized CMV prophylaxis, strict IBD control, and timely antifibrotic interventions to individual risk profiles.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This is a single-center prospective observational cohort study conducted at the Department of Hepatology, Transplantology and Internal Medicine, Medical University of Warsaw. All adult patients who underwent liver transplantation (LT) for primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) between January 2017 and September 2024 were enrolled and followed according to a pre-defined routine MRCP surveillance protocol.

Patients were eligible if they had a follow-up of at least 90 days after LT and attended scheduled magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) examinations. Patients with shorter follow-up (e.g., early death or retransplantation) or without scheduled MRCPs were excluded.

Diagnosis of PSC and transplantation procedure

The diagnosis of PSC prior to LT was established according to the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) guidelines6 and confirmed by histopathological examination of the explanted liver.

All grafts were obtained from deceased brain-dead donors (DBD). The initial immunosuppressive regimen consisted of basiliximab administered on days 0 and 4 as induction therapy, followed by mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) or azathioprine (AZA) starting on day 1, and tacrolimus (Tac) introduced on day 5 as maintenance immunosuppression. Corticosteroids were administered during reperfusion and from day 1 post-transplantation, with tapering and planned withdrawal within 3 months. In patients with PSC/autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) variant, long-term prednisone (5 mg daily) was maintained. All CMV-seronegative recipients received antiviral prophylaxis for 100 days after transplantation.

Follow-up and MRCP imaging protocol

Scheduled MRCP examinations were planned at approximately 1 and 3 years post-transplant, with variations in timing according to radiology scheduling constraints. MRCP images were assessed by a single radiologist with extensive experience in hepatobiliary imaging. MRCP findings were interpreted blinded to biochemical results to avoid diagnostic bias.

All MRCP examinations were performed using a 1.5T MR scanner (Siemens Avanto, Erlangen, Germany) with a standardized institutional protocol. The protocol included heavily T2-weighted turbo spin echo sequences: 3D TSE acquisitions in oblique coronal and axial planes, 2D HASTE sequences in the coronal plane, and single-slab radial MRCP. Multiplanar reconstructions using maximum intensity projection (MIP) were performed for T2-weighted 3D TSE sequences.

Recurrent PSC (rPSC) was diagnosed according to the Mayo criteria5, defined as:

Clinical assessments

Follow-up visits were scheduled every 2–8 weeks during the first post-transplant year, and subsequently every 3–12 months. At each visit, clinical symptoms, laboratory results, and complications were assessed. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) was performed for therapeutic indications when clinically required. Consideration for re-transplantation was made in cases of graft failure or advanced recurrent disease.

Laboratory data were collected at four time points: retrospectively at the time of PSC diagnosis, on the day of LT, and within 3 months of the two scheduled MRCP examinations (at 1 and 3 years after LT).

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) activity was assessed clinically and by routine colonoscopies according to international guidelines.

Variables collected

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, donor-related, histopathological, and post-transplant treatment data were collected. Serum ALP and GGT were measured at each MRCP time point; the institutional reference limits were ≤ 120 U/L and ≤ 50 U/L, respectively. CMV viremia was assessed by quantitative PCR (qPCR) for CMV DNA in plasma. Histopathology reports of the explanted liver were reviewed.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as median with interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using the chi-square or Fisher exact tests, and continuous variables using the Mann–Whitney U test. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis were considered candidates for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. To avoid model overfitting and instability, biologically overlapping or collinear variables were not included simultaneously in the final model. For overall rPSC, multivariable analysis was not performed due to biological overlap between the significant predictors identified in univariable analyses. In survival analyses, Kaplan-Meier estimates and Cox regression models were applied. Time-to-event analyses for biochemical features were landmarked at the date of the first MRCP to assess predictors of advanced rPSC and to minimize immortal time bias. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).
