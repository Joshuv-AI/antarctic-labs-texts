Acute and preventive medical treatment of cluster headache in Taiwan: A narrative review
Authors: Fu-Chi Yang, Chia-Lin Tsai, Guan-Yu Lin, Chun-Pai Yang, Wei-Ta Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Cluster headache (CH) is a debilitating neurological disorder characterized by severe, unilateral pain, and ipsilateral autonomic symptoms. Chronic CH is exceedingly rare in Taiwan, constituting approximately 1% of all CH cases. This narrative review provides an up-to-date overview of the acute and preventive treatment strategies for CH in Taiwan, focusing on currently available pharmacological options in the country. The treatment approach for CH in Taiwan involves a stepwise strategy. High-flow oxygen and triptan nasal sprays are the mainstays of acute treatment, providing rapid relief, and good tolerability. Transitional treatments, such as oral steroids and suboccipital steroid injections, serve as a crucial bridge between acute and long-term preventive therapies, offering temporary relief while minimizing side effects through a carefully limited duration. For preventive treatment, verapamil is the first-line option, with lithium and topiramate being the second-line alternatives. Among the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) monoclonal antibodies, galcanezumab has demonstrated efficacy in the prevention of episodic CH. Preventive treatments are personalized to individual patients, starting with low doses and close monitoring for adverse effects. Neuromodulatory therapies, such as noninvasive vagus nerve stimulation, show promise for chronic and refractory CH but have limited availability in Taiwan. In conclusion, despite the availability of various acute and preventive treatment options, unmet needs in the management of CH in Taiwan remain. In particular, increased awareness and education among healthcare professionals to improve the diagnosis and management of CH in Taiwan should be implemented.

1. INTRODUCTION

Cluster headache (CH) is a debilitating condition characterized by intense, unilateral pain, often accompanied by autonomic symptoms.1,2 Because the prevalence and clinical presentation of CH can vary, understanding the distinction between its two main forms, episodic and chronic, is essential for effective management. Episodic CH, the more prevalent form, is defined by recurring headache attacks lasting from 7 days to 1 year, interspersed with pain-free intervals of 3 months or longer. In contrast, chronic CH manifests without significant remission periods, with sufferers experiencing attacks that persist for over a year or allowing only brief remission periods of <3 months. In Taiwan, chronic CH is exceptionally rare, affecting only about 1% of all CH patients; however, studies from Western countries have reported a higher prevalence of chronic CH, ranging from 10% to 15% of all CH cases.2,3 This marked difference in the prevalence of chronic CH between countries underscores the need for the treatment of CH in Taiwan, which primarily addresses the episodic CH.

The treatment focus in this context is multifaceted: acute management aims to rapidly alleviate the severe pain of attacks through prompt intervention, while preventive treatment strategies are designed to reduce the frequency and intensity of these attacks. High-flow oxygen inhalation and triptan nasal sprays are highlighted as primary acute treatments. Preventive treatments include pharmacological interventions such as verapamil, lithium, and topiramate, tailored to the patient’s clinical profile. While the pathophysiological understanding of CH, including trigeminal autonomic reflexes, provides a foundation for current treatment strategies, the role of genetic factors in guiding treatment selection and predicting efficacy has not been clarified.4 Recent genome-wide studies have linked CH to smoking as a causal risk, uncovered shared genetic risks with migraine and risk-taking behavior, and identified genetic differences between European and East Asian populations.4,5 In addition, CH shows strong associations with circadian rhythms, which have implications for treatment timing and strategies.6,7 However, the direct implications of these genetic findings on personalized treatment approaches are yet to be fully elucidated.

This narrative review aimed to delineate acute and preventive treatment strategies for CH, focusing on the clinical approach used in Taiwan. To this end, we used a grading system commonly used in clinical guidelines. Evidence strength was categorized from grade A to C, with grade A representing the highest level of evidence, typically derived from multiple well-conducted randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Grade B indicates moderate evidence, supported by at least one RCT or several robust observational studies, while grade C signifies evidence from case series, expert opinions, or studies with inconsistent results. Furthermore, we provide clinical recommendations for each treatment, stratified into five groups, ranging from I to V. Recommendations in group I are strongly supported by high-quality evidence and demonstrate excellent efficacy-to-safety ratios. Group II encompasses treatments with moderate evidence and favorable risk-benefit balances. Group III includes options endorsed by expert consensus or limited evidence, applicable when first-line treatments are not effective or available. Group IV consists of treatments with potential advantages but notable risks or insufficient evidence, and group V comprises treatments discouraged due to poor efficacy or adverse risk-benefit profiles.

By integrating this evidence grading and recommendation categorization, our narrative review aims to provide a comprehensive and methodologically sound overview of the available treatments for CH in Taiwan. This review also incorporated evidence from international guidelines8–10 and recent research findings. Such structured approach empowers clinicians to make informed treatment decisions, guided by the most current and reliable evidence, alongside expert consensus.

2. TREATMENT

CH management encompasses acute treatments, transitional and maintenance prophylactic medications, and device therapy.

3. ACUTE TREATMENT

3.1. Oxygen

Oxygen therapy, using pure oxygen inhalation, is an established acute treatment for CH. Its use was first introduced by Horton in the 1950s.11 The efficacy of oxygen therapy depends on the flow rate, with high-flow oxygen (>10 L/min) being more effective than low-flow oxygen (<10 L/min).12 In 1981, a case series showed that 82% of patients reported relief within 15 minutes of receiving oxygen at a flow rate of 7 L/min.13 In 2009, an RCT confirmed these findings, demonstrating a 78% relief rate at an oxygen flow rate of 12 L/min14 (grade A, group I). The superiority of high- over low-flow oxygen in the acute treatment of CH has been consistently demonstrated in clinical studies. A randomized, double-blind, crossover study by Cohen et al14 found that high-flow oxygen (12 L/min) was significantly more effective than low-flow oxygen (7 L/min) in reducing pain intensity and achieving pain-free status within 15 minutes of treatment onset. The mechanism underlying the efficacy of high-flow oxygen in CH is thought to involve the inhibition of trigeminal nerve activation and subsequent reduction of neurogenic inflammation.15 Extended inhalation times or the use of various delivery methods can help prevent potential rebound headaches.16,17 It is worth noting that hyperbaric oxygen therapy has shown mixed results in the treatment of CH.18–20

3.2. Triptans

Triptans, which serve as serotonin 1B/1D receptor agonists, are another important class of acute treatments for CH. In Taiwan, sumatriptan and rizatriptan are available in oral tablet formulations, while sumatriptan is also available as a nasal spray. However, subcutaneous sumatriptan injection and zolmitriptan nasal spray are not currently approved for use in Taiwan. In 1993, an RCT demonstrated the efficacy of subcutaneous sumatriptan, with 75% of individuals experiencing relief within 15 minutes of receiving a 6 mg dose21 (grade A, group I). In 2006, an RCT found that intranasal zolmitriptan (5 mg) could effectively relieve CH in 42% of users within 30 minutes,22 while intranasal sumatriptan has also shown promising results23 (grade A, group I for zolmitriptan and grade B, group II for sumatriptan).

In Taiwanese clinical studies, 80% of individuals reported relief from CH attacks after taking 50 mg of oral sumatriptan,24 whereas another study found that only 42.3% of individuals experienced relief with oral sumatriptan25 (grade C, group II). Given the brief duration of CH attacks, intranasal sumatriptan has emerged as a preferred acute treatment option in Taiwan. However, triptans should be used with caution due to potential dose-dependent adverse effects, such as “triptan sensations” resulting from vasoconstriction.1 These effects make triptans unsuitable for certain patient groups, including those with cardiovascular conditions or during pregnancy.26

3.3. Ergot derivatives

Ergot derivatives, such as ergotamine and dihydroergotamine (DHE), act on 5-hydroxytryptamine (ie, serotonin) 1B/1D receptors and various neurotransmitter systems.27 Ergotamine was first introduced as a treatment for acute CH by Harris.28 In 1948, clinical observations showed its effectiveness, with oral combinations providing relief in 71% of patients with CH.29 Moreover, a case series in 1981 revealed that sublingual formulations resulted in a relief in 70% of patients within 15 minutes13 (grade C, group III). DHE, in both inhaled and intravenous forms, has also shown promise in the treatment of acute CH.30–33 However, multiple formulations of DHE are not available in Taiwan, and definitive trials for ergot derivatives in CH are still lacking. Common side effects of DHE include nausea, vomiting, dizziness, and muscle cramps.34 Moreover, it is essential to consider these and other potential side effects of DHE, particularly in patients with unknown cardiovascular risks. For example, DHE can cause vasoconstriction, which may lead to serious cardiovascular complications such as myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral ischemia.27 Patients with a history of cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, or multiple cardiovascular risk factors should either use DHE with caution or avoid it.35

3.4. Analgesics

Analgesics, such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and fixed drug combinations, have not proven significantly effective for the acute treatment of CH (grade C, group V). In 1996, clinical observations showed that only 21% of patients reported relief with oral analgesics.36 However, in 2010, a case series suggested that the NSAID indomethacin provided some relief in a patient with CH.37 Given the limited evidence and poor efficacy, analgesics are not recommended as first-line acute treatments for CH.

3.5. Octreotide

Octreotide, a synthetic somatostatin analog, has shown potential in providing relief for acute CH. An RCT in 2004 demonstrated that a 100-μg subcutaneous dose of octreotide relieved headaches in 52% of patients within 30 min38 (Tables 1 and 2). However, this formulation has not been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of acute CH, which limits its clinical application.

4. PREVENTIVE MEASURES

4.1. Transitional prophylaxis

Transitional prophylaxis is an interim therapy aimed at quickly minimizing CH attacks and initiating more effective, sustained preventive treatments. This strategy is usually combined with maintenance prophylaxis, thus giving it the “transitional” label.

4.1.1. Steroids

Steroids have been a cornerstone in the transitional management of CH for over three decades, valued for their rapid effectiveness and ease of administration. They serve as an essential bridge until more sustainable preventive treatments become available. The clinical practice routine typically involves initiating treatment with the administration of oral prednisone at dosages ranging from 10 to 80 mg/d, which has been shown to significantly reduce or even stop acute CH episodes.39,40 While the starting dose can be as low as 10 mg daily, clinical guidelines typically suggest a range that can go up to 60 to 80 mg daily (1 mg/kg/d), especially during the initial phase of treatment. However, care must be taken during the tapering process, because abrupt discontinuation can lead to rebound headaches, a phenomenon observed in a significant proportion of patients. For acute management, a regimen of oral prednisolone, sometimes combined with prophylactic agents like verapamil, has proven effective in reducing attack frequency early in the treatment course (as demonstrated in a multicenter, double-blind, RCT).41 To mitigate the risk of side effects, including weight gain and hormonal alterations, steroids should be administered judiciously. The recommended approach is to start with a full therapeutic dose, then taper down gradually over 2 to 3 weeks. It is essential to avoid treatment durations exceeding 4 weeks to prevent complications such as femoral head necrosis.

Although the detailed mechanisms by which corticosteroids alleviate CH symptoms are not fully understood, their primary action is believed to involve anti-inflammatory effects and modulation of the hypothalamic and autonomic pathways. In Taiwan, prednisone is commonly used, which is metabolized to prednisolone in the liver. Both forms have shown similar efficacy in the treatment of CH.10 This practical guidance focuses on the strategic use of steroids in CH management, aligning with the broader aim of providing clinicians with actionable, evidence-based therapy recommendations.

4.1.2. Suboccipital steroid injection

Suboccipital steroid injections, a procedure involving the administration of steroids near the occipital nerve, serve as a transitory measure bridging the gap until longer-term CH management strategies can be implemented. While these injections are effective, akin to oral steroids, they are associated with a reduced risk of systemic side effects. This technique aims to interrupt the neurologic pathways between the occipital nerve and the cervical trigeminal complex, potentially inhibiting the trigeminal autonomic reflex implicated in CH.

The American Headache Society has accorded suboccipital steroid injections the distinction of a class A evidence-supported preventive treatment for CH; however, it is important to note that the therapeutic effects of suboccipital steroid injections are generally temporary, lasting only for a few weeks to months.42,43 This temporary nature is an inherent characteristic of most bridging therapies, which are designed to provide rapid relief while long-term preventive treatments are being established. Clinical evidence derived from a double-blind placebo-controlled study involving 23 patients with CH revealed that 85% of participants reported no headaches in the week following the injection, with a subset remaining headache-free for up to 1 month.44 In another randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial involving 43 patients, those treated with suboccipital steroid injections saw a significant reduction in daily attack frequency compared with those in the placebo group, indicating the treatment’s efficacy in managing CH symptoms.45

While suboccipital injections primarily cause localized effects such as neck pain or non-CH-related headaches at the injection site, they are not entirely free from systemic side effects. These can include transient alterations in endocrine function, mood changes, or other systemic effects, although they are rare. Clinicians should be aware of these potential effects and monitor patients accordingly. In terms of administration details, one typical formulation used includes 0.5 mL of 2% xylocaine combined with 2.5 mL of steroids, incorporating long-acting betamethasone dipropionate and fast-acting betamethasone disodium phosphate. The injection is typically performed in the suboccipital triangle, targeting the area between the painful mastoid and the occipital bone.44 A precise technique ensures the drug’s efficacy and minimizes complications, with the needle insertion varying in direction to ensure adequate distribution of the medication.

4.2. Maintenance prophylaxis

Maintenance prophylactic treatments, which work concurrently with acute and transitional therapies, aim to reduce the attack frequency, intensity, and duration of CH episodes. These treatments can taper off and stop if the patient remains attack-free for 2 weeks, suggesting an end to the cluster period. The restart protocol for individuals with yearly CH attacks is unclear. However, an early intervention strategy, supplemented by prophylaxis during predictable cluster periods, may ease patient anxiety and severe headache-associated discomfort.

5. ORAL MEDICATIONS

5.1. Verapamil

Verapamil is the preferred choice for prophylactic treatment of both episodic and chronic CH. It is well-tolerated with few adverse effects and can be combined with short-term prophylactics such as prednisolone. The recommended starting dose of verapamil is 80 mg three times daily, which can be gradually increased by 80 mg every 10 to 14 days until either a maintenance dose of 240 to 480 mg daily is reached, or side effects become intolerable. In some cases, higher doses of up to 960 mg daily may be necessary to achieve optimal efficacy.46 Studies have shown that verapamil treatment significantly reduces attack frequency in patients with both episodic and chronic CH.47,48 In one double-blind study, 80% of patients with episodic CH experienced an 80% reduction in headache frequency, which was significantly better than that in the placebo group.49 The study found verapamil to be as effective as lithium but with fewer adverse effects (grade A, group I). In Taiwan, 81% of patients with episodic CH treated with verapamil reported relief within 2 months.10

Verapamil can cause adverse effects related to atrioventricular node conduction, such as prolonged PR intervals, bradycardia, and atrioventricular block. These complications may occur within 5 months of treatment initiation. Before initiating verapamil therapy, a baseline electrocardiogram (EKG) should be obtained to assess any preexisting cardiac conduction abnormalities. EKG monitoring should be repeated during dose escalation, particularly when reaching higher doses (≥480 mg daily), to detect any potential cardiac adverse effects such as PR interval prolongation or heart block.46 Other potential adverse effects include hypotension, constipation, nausea, fatigue, dizziness, and edema. Monitoring blood pressure, heart rate, and liver function is essential, particularly during dose adjustments. Long-term use requires close monitoring for adverse effects to prevent complications.

5.2. Lithium

Lithium, a medication primarily used for treating seasonal affective disorder and bipolar disorder, has also been investigated as a preventive therapy for CH due to the periodic nature of the condition. In an analysis of 20 clinical studies, lithium demonstrated a response rate of 78% for chronic CH and 63% for episodic CH, where response was defined as a reduction in attack frequency of at least 50%.50

In an RCT comparing lithium with verapamil, both treatments were found to be superior to placebo in reducing attack frequency and duration in patients with chronic CH.51 However, another double-blind placebo-controlled trial focusing on episodic CH revealed no significant advantage of lithium over placebo in terms of remission rate, defined as the complete cessation of CH attacks during the treatment period52 (grade B, group II).

The typical therapeutic dosage of lithium for CH prevention ranges from 600 to 1200 mg/d, with more pronounced effects observed in patients with chronic CH compared with those with episodic CH. It is important to note that lithium has a narrow therapeutic index, with a target serum concentration range of 0.6 to 1.2 mM/L, necessitating careful monitoring and dose adjustments to maintain efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.

Common side effects associated with lithium therapy include nausea, tremors, blurred vision, and elevated white blood cell counts. In cases of lithium overdose, patients may experience confusion, seizures, and skin peeling. Long-term use of lithium requires regular monitoring for potential complications such as hypothyroidism and kidney dysfunction.

5.3. Melatonin

Melatonin, a hormone produced by the pineal gland, has been investigated as a prophylactic treatment for CH. Patients with CH frequently exhibit a marked reduction in their melatonin levels during cluster episodes. One double-blind pilot study found that melatonin (10 mg) improved CH symptoms compared to placebo.53 However, another study showed mixed results, highlighting the need for further research on the prophylactic efficacy of melatonin in CH54 (grade B, group III).

5.4. Antiepileptic drugs

The activation of cervical trigeminal complex neurons, influenced by the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) type A receptor, is a potential mechanism in the pathophysiology of CH. This has led to the exploration of GABA-related antiepileptic drugs for the treatment of CH.

5.5. Topiramate

Open-label studies on topiramate, at doses ranging from 25 to 200 mg/d, have shown varying results. Some studies have reported that up to 90% of participants experienced a cessation of their cluster period, while another study found that only 21% of patients achieved a reduction in headache frequency of more than 50%.55,56 A Taiwanese study suggested that doses up to 400 mg/d could be beneficial for patients with chronic CH who are resistant to lower doses57 (grade C, group II). Adverse effects of topiramate include dizziness, fatigue, altered taste, and memory impairment. Less common but severe effects include kidney stones and secondary narrow-angle glaucoma.

5.6. Valproic acid and derivatives

A retrospective analysis of 49 patients treated with divalproex sodium (500-1500 mg/d), either as a monotherapy or as an adjunctive treatment, found that 73% of patients showed improvement.58 In contrast, a double-blind study using sodium valproate (1000-2000 mg/d) revealed that 50% of patients experienced reduced headaches, but 62% of those on placebo showed similar outcomes.59 The lower response observed in the latter study may be attributed to the natural cessation of headaches at the end of a cluster period (grade C, group IV). Common adverse effects include hair loss, tremors, and liver abnormalities. Rare but severe issues such as pancreatitis are also a concern.

5.7. CGRP monoclonal antibodies

In 2019, galcanezumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), received an FDA approval for the prevention of episodic CH. The approved regimen involves monthly subcutaneous injections of 300 mg of galcanezumab. In an RCT involving 106 participants with episodic CH, the galcanezumab treatment group experienced a significant reduction in weekly attack frequency compared to the placebo group. The mean reduction in weekly attacks was 8.7 in the galcanezumab group vs 5.2 in the placebo group. Furthermore, 71% of patients treated with galcanezumab reported a reduction in headache frequency of more than 50%, compared to 53% in the placebo group60 (grade B, group II). However, a phase 3, randomized, placebo-controlled study found that galcanezumab was not effective in reducing the frequency of attacks in patients with cCH compared to placebo over a 3-month double-blind treatment period.61 In addition to galcanezumab, other CGRP monoclonal antibodies have been investigated for the prevention of CH. Fremanezumab, another CGRP antibody, was studied in a phase 3 trial for the prevention of chronic CHs (NCT03107052). However, this trial was discontinued due to insufficient interim efficacy results, highlighting the challenges in developing effective therapies for this subtype of CH.

The current state of evidence suggests that CGRP monoclonal antibodies, particularly galcanezumab, show promise in the prevention of episodic CHs. However, their efficacy in treating chronic CH remains less clear, with some studies yielding inconclusive or negative results. Further research is needed to identify specific subgroups of CH patients who may benefit from CGRP monoclonal antibody therapy and clarify the role of these agents in the overall management of the condition.

5.8. Onabotulinum toxin A

Two clinical trials have investigated the use of botulinum toxin type A (BTA) in patients with CH. A phase 1/2 study assessed the safety of BTA injected into the otic ganglion located in the infratemporal fossa in patients with refractory chronic CH with frequent weekly attacks. Among the 10 participants, one group received 25 IU of BTA, while another group received 12.5 IU. Two-month follow-up examinations indicated its safety, but no significant reduction in headache frequency was observed.62 A phase 3 study by the Norwegian University of Science and Technology is currently examining whether a 25-IU BTA injection into the sphenopalatine ganglion (SPG) can decrease attack frequency in patients with regular weekly chronic CH attacks (NCT03944876). This multicenter, randomized trial aims to enroll 112 participants to assess the use of a precise injection technique and anticipates results by September 2025. Previous observational studies on chronic CHs have shown promising results with BTA. However, no standard exists for dose, site, or frequency of administration.63 Comprehensive randomized trials remain necessary to evaluate the efficacy of BTA (grade C, group III).

6. NEUROMODULATORY THERAPY

Neuromodulatory therapies, such as noninvasive vagus nerve stimulation (nVNS), SPG stimulation, occipital nerve stimulation (ONS), and deep brain stimulation (DBS), have emerged as promising treatment options for patients with CH, particularly those with chronic or refractory forms of the disorder.

The nVNS has been investigated as a preventive treatment for both episodic and chronic CH. The ACT1 study, a randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled trial, demonstrated the efficacy of nVNS in the acute treatment of eCH attacks.64 However, the ACT2 study, which evaluated nVNS for the acute treatment of attacks in patients with cCH, did not show a significant difference between nVNS and sham stimulation.65 In terms of preventive efficacy, the PREVA study, a randomized, controlled trial, found that nVNS in combination with standard of care resulted in a significant reduction in the number of CH attacks per week compared to standard of care alone in patients with cCH.66 However, it is important to note that this study did not include a sham stimulation arm, and further research is needed to establish the preventive efficacy of nVNS in cCH. While nVNS has shown promise as a preventive treatment for eCH, its efficacy in cCH remains uncertain. Further well-designed, randomized, sham-controlled trials are necessary to clarify the role of nVNS in the prevention of cCH attacks.

SPG stimulation is another neuromodulatory technique that has garnered attention in the management of chronic CHs. It has been shown to be effective in both the acute and preventive treatment of chronic CH,67 offering a targeted approach to modulating the trigeminal autonomic reflex pathway involved in CH pathophysiology. While SPG stimulation requires a surgical implantation procedure, the long-term benefits and ability to provide on-demand stimulation have made it a valuable option for patients with refractory chronic CHs. However, it is important to note that the manufacturer of the device used for SPG stimulation has filed for bankruptcy, leading to unavailability of this device worldwide for over 5 years. Currently, its future availability remains uncertain.

ONS and hypothalamic DBS are additional neuromodulatory therapies that have been explored in the management of refractory chronic CHs. ONS involves the implantation of electrodes into the occipital region to modulate the neural circuitry of the trigeminal-cervical complex, an anatomical structure that has been implicated as playing a pivotal role in the complex pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CHs.68 Hypothalamic DBS, a more invasive procedure, targets the posterior hypothalamic region, which has been shown to be hyperactive in patients with CH.69 While both ONS and DBS have shown promising results in the treatment of refractory chronic CH, their invasive nature and potential surgical risks have generally limited their use in patients who have failed to respond to other treatments.

Given the limited prevalence of chronic CH and the effectiveness of oral pharmacological treatments in managing episodic CH, the use of neuromodulatory therapies in Taiwan remains limited. Future research and advancements in these technologies may expand their applicability, particularly in challenging and refractory CH cases.

In conclusion, in Taiwan, high-flow oxygen and triptan nasal sprays are the primary treatments for acute CH, while oral steroids and occipital nerve steroid injections serve as transitional measures. For ongoing prophylaxis, verapamil is the first-line treatment, with lithium, topiramate, and CGRP monoclonal antibodies used as second-line options. Prophylactic regimens should be individualized for each patient, starting with low doses and closely monitoring for adverse effects to ensure optimal treatment outcomes. Oral medications are effective for most Taiwanese patients, particularly those with episodic CH. Neuromodulatory therapies, such as nVNS, await government approval, and surgical treatments, such as SPG stimulation, show promise but are limited by the low prevalence of chronic CH in Taiwan. As research and clinical experience advance, the management of CH in Taiwan will continue to evolve, aiming to improve patient outcomes and their quality of life.
