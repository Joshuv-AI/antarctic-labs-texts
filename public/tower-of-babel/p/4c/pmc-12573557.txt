Advances in diagnosis and management of pleural mesothelioma: the Danish clinical guidelines
Authors: Vasiliki Panou, Jens Benn Sørensen, Jesper Ravn, Eric Santoni-Rugiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPleural mesothelioma (PM) is a rare, aggressive malignancy of the pleura. The PM diagnosis is challenging, both in terms of imaging, sampling of representative biopsies and pathological examination, and the treatment options are limited. The main aim of this article is to summarize the most recent advances in the diagnosis and management of PM as presented in the Danish clinical guidelines for PM. An expert task force consisting of a lung physician, a pathologist, an oncologist, and a thoracic surgeon conducted an ad hoc literature search during November 2023-July 2024, reviewed the evidence and developed the current clinical guidelines after reaching consensus. The article appraises the radiological modalities and biopsy techniques that are employed in PM diagnostics, provides a comprehensive overview of the evidence behind the PM histopathological and cytological diagnosis, and asserts the state of the art and novel developments of oncological, multimodal, and palliative PM treatment and follow-up that are portrayed in the Danish clinical guidelines.KEYWORDS: Pleural mesothelioma, diagnosis, clinical guidelines, pathology, surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, follow-up

Introduction

Pleural mesothelioma (PM) is an aggressive, mesothelium-derived, asbestos-related malignancy of the pleura [1]. In Denmark, there are approximately 129–139 cases annually with a crude incidence rate of 4.2 for men and 0.76 for women (data from NORDCAN and the Danish Cancer Register, Nordcan 2.0 (iarc.fr)). The main etiological agent for PM is asbestos, which explains the different incidence rates between men and women, as men in Denmark were traditionally employed in asbestos-related occupations [2]. All types of asbestos fibers are considered carcinogenetic, there is no lower safe threshold of asbestos exposure, and the mean latency of PM development is approximately 40 years [2–4]. Thus, PM is much more frequent in older patients with an average age at diagnosis of >70 years in most Western countries. Pathogenic germline variants in tumor-suppressor genes (TSGs) and DNA repair genes may also predispose to PM [5–7]. They are reported in 12% of PM patients (up to 16% considering likely pathogenic variants too) and they are associated with longer survival and younger age [5–9]. The TSG BRCA-Associated Protein 1 (BAP1) is the most frequently affected by tumor-predisposing germline mutations (7% of PM patients) [8,9]. Other rare risk factors for PM are the previously utilized radiographic contrast medium Thorotrast, thoracic ionizing radiation and the asbestos-like mineral erionite [10–12].

Despite the rarity of the disease, in the last years there have been advances in the diagnosis and treatment of PM. The main aim of this manuscript is to summarize the most recent developments aiming to provide the clinicians with evidence-based and up-to-date recommendations for the diagnosis and management of PM with a special focus on the Danish perspective. The present clinical guidelines are adopted by the Danish Lung Cancer Group as national guidelines for the treatment and management of PM.

Materials and methods

A task force consisting of a lung physician, a pathologist, an oncologist and a thoracic surgeon, experts in the diagnosis and management of PM was appointed by the Danish Lung Cancer Group in order to update the clinical guidelines. The task force members conducted a literature search, discussed the evidence and formulated the recommendations. The literature search was conducted ad hoc during November 2023–July 2024; hence, there is no mutual research strategy. All members of the task force reviewed the evidence and consensus was reached after taking into consideration the quality of the evidence, feasibility, acceptability, potential adverse events, and complications as well as cost and benefit. The Danish Group for the Diseases of the Pleura and the Danish Respiratory Society have endorsed the clinical guidelines. Based on extensive literature research, the following questions were answered:

Diagnosis

Which radiological modalities should be used in the diagnostic workup of PM? What are the most appropriate biopsy techniques for PM? Are there prognostic biomarkers that are validated for clinical use? Should patients with PM be assessed for asbestos exposure?

Pathology

What are the criteria for diagnosing PM on histological samples and distinguishing PM from other pleural cancers or from benign reactive pleural changes? How may cytology be used for the diagnosis of PM? Can histopathological grading of PM be used prognostically? What are the implications for diagnosing mesothelioma in situ?

Treatment

What is the oncological treatment of PM patients? Are there options for surgery for PM patients? What are the recommendations for follow-up for patients that undergo treatment?

Results and discussion

Diagnosis

Imaging

The clinical manifestation of PM is often non-specific and most frequently includes pleural dyspnea, thoracic pain, weight loss and pleural effusion [13,14]. Thus, the clinical manifestation can neither confirm nor disprove the diagnosis. The first-line imaging modality is typically chest radiography, as it is widely available. However, pleural thickening that is typical for PM may not always appear in chest radiography, depending on the size, location, and morphology [15]. When there is clinical suspicion of PM, Computed Tomography (CT) with iodinated intravenous contrast of the thorax and upper abdomen should be the imaging modality of choice due to a sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 81% for pleural malignancy [16,17]. Multiplanar reconstructions and high-resolution slice thickness (1–3 mm) should be included when possible [18]. Pleural effusion, pleural nodules, pleural thickening >1 cm, thickening of the interlobar septa, involvement of the mediastinal pleura, a widespread tumor that surrounds the entire lung, chest wall and rib invasion are typical but not pathognomonic signs of PM [15] (Figure 1). The absence of the above radiological signs cannot exclude PM, as the negative predictive value of CT is up to 65% [19]. On the other hand, pleural calcification and pleural plaques are considered benign changes [15]. Thoracic ultrasound (TUS) has a sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 90% for diagnosing malignant pleural disease and is superior to CT in detecting chest wall invasion [20–22]. Signs indicative of malignancy include the presence of nodules in the diaphragm and parietal pleura, thickening of the parietal pleura >10 mm and of the diaphragm >7 mm, liver metastases, and inability to resolve diaphragmatic layers [22]. TUS can provide real-time guidance during transthoracic pleural biopsies and visualize the course of the intercostal artery; hence, improving the diagnostic accuracy and reducing the risk of potential complications [17,23]. In addition, the use of TUS prior to medical thoracoscopy (MT) can confirm the induction of pneumothorax in the absence of pleural effusion and detect extensive septations that may hinder the procedure [24,25]. F-Fluorodeoxyglycose Positron Emission Tomography (FDG PET) has a sensitivity of 81–95% and specificity of 74–82% for malignant pleural disease, but the literature is diverse regarding its utility in PM diagnosis [26–29]. PM will typically appear with high FDG-uptake, but there are cases of PM with low or no FDG-uptake, which may lead to false-negative findings [15] (Figure 1). On the contrary, prior talcum pleurodesis and inflammatory conditions of the pleura, such as tuberculosis, will appear with high FDG-uptake and may lead to false-positive diagnoses [15]. FDG PET may be useful for excluding the presence of extra-thoracic disease in cases, where surgical treatment is considered [30]. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is superior to CT in revealing invasion of the diaphragm and chest wall/endothoracic fascia (69% versus 46% and 82% versus 55%, respectively) [31–33]. However, MRI is a costly imaging modality not always well-tolerated by patients, requires prolonged image acquisition time, has low sensitivity in nodal staging, and is prone to artefacts due to cardiac and respiratory motion. As a result, MRI is not routinely used in the diagnostic workup of PM [23,31].

Pleural biopsies and pleural effusion

Thoracoscopic pleural biopsy is the diagnostic reference standard for the histopathological PM diagnosis and can be performed under local MT or general anesthesia and single lung respiration by video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). The thoracoscopic approach allows the visualization of the pleura, acquisition of large biopsies, and management of the pleural effusion either by drainage, pleurodesis or indwelling pleural catheter placement [34]. Both MT and VATS have high sensitivity of 91–96% and 91–95%, respectively, and a low complication rate of 2–8% for MT and 1–15% for VATS [35–37]. Either the presence of a moderate pleural effusion or the induction of pneumothorax using a Boutin needle is necessary to perform MT, and the use of TUS prior to the procedure is recommended [38,39]. MT is less invasive, less costly and requires shorter hospital stay compared to VATS [37,39]. However, VATS should be chosen over MT in cases where the access to the pleural cavity is limited, for example, due to inadequate pneumothorax induction or adhesions and in cases where surgical interventions are required, such as pleurectomy or decortication. Percutaneous pleural biopsies are gaining ground in PM diagnosis in the recent years and can be performed under CT- or TUS-guidance. Closed pleural biopsies using Abram’s or Cope’s needle are not recommended due to low diagnostic sensitivity for malignant pleural disease of no more than 50% and complication rate of up to 15% [40–42]. TUS-guided pleural biopsies have a diagnostic yield of up to 84% and a complication rate of 3%, while the diagnostic yield and complication rate for CT-guided pleural biopsies are 93% and 7%, respectively [43]. However, the diagnostic yield of TUS- and CT-guided biopsies is lower for pleural lesions <10 mm and <5 mm, respectively, and overall these two techniques have lower sensitivity (77% and 86%, respectively), when it comes to tissue for molecular analyses [44]. Biopsy of mediastinal lymph nodes is not performed routinely in the diagnostic workup of PM due to a diagnostic sensitivity of <30% but should be considered prior to surgery as part of a multimodal approach [45,46].

The diagnostics of PM by cytological analysis of pleural effusions may be difficult or inconclusive, as in these specimens, differentiation of PM from reactive mesothelial proliferations or PM subtyping may be quite challenging or even impossible (see below). Thus, depending on circumstances, the sensitivity reportedly varies between 16% and 73% [47]. Yet in patients unable to tolerate pleural biopsies because of poor performance status, very advanced disease or other reasons, cytological analysis can be the technique that confirms the mesothelial origin of malignancy and allows the diagnosis of PM suggested by clinical and radiological data [47–49].

To summarize, CT of thorax and upper abdomen has a central role in the diagnostic workup for PM, but valuable information may also be acquired through TUS, FDG, PET, and, in some cases, MRI. In most cases, pleural biopsies are needed to provide a definite diagnosis for PM, unless patient frailty or preferences contraindicate a biopsy. Thoracoscopic pleural biopsies are often the procedure-of-choice for the PM diagnosis, but image-guided percutaneous biopsies may also be used in cases with significant pleural thickening and preference for minimal invasive procedure. Several parameters may affect the choice of biopsy procedure, including the morphology of the pleura by imaging, local availability and patient needs, preferences and frailty.

Pathology

Histopathological types

In 99% of cases, PM grows diffusely in the pleura, in the remaining 1% is a localized mass histologically indistinguishable from diffuse PM, but with much better outcome [50,51]. Over 60% of PMs are of epithelioid type (EPM), approximately 10% sarcomatoid (SPM) and the 25–30% biphasic (BPM; i.e., mixed epithelioid-sarcomatoid). The diagnosis of BPM in surgical resections requires that each component represents at least 10% of tumor tissue, while in biopsies the mere presence of each component, regardless of its percentage, is sufficient [50,52]. The three types correlate with the sensitivity to chemotherapy, relapse rate after pleural surgery, and overall survival (OS) with EPM being the most favorable type. Thus, it is important to name the percentage of each component in diagnostic biopsies from BPMs, as tumors with more sarcomatoid histology have worse prognosis. Moreover, in most specialized centers, the operability of PM is restricted to patients with EPM or BPM with no more than 50% sarcomatoid component [50–54]. The histopathological types are also relevant for immunotherapy, as in Denmark it is approved as first line treatment only for non-epithelioid PM.

Cytological investigations

The differential diagnosis of PM on cytological samples is challenging. High cellularity, large 3D clusters and manifest cell atypia may be indicative of PM, yet PMs with low cellularity and mild atypia in effusions may be observed [50]. Pleural cytology cannot demonstrate the most certain sign of malignancy for mesothelial cells, i.e., their invasion into the deep pleural stroma and subpleural fat tissue. Furthermore, the sensitivity of pleural cytology is particularly low for SPM or BPM with dominant sarcomatoid component, as the sarcomatoid cells do not shed into the effusion, which also makes impossible the distinction between EPM and BPM. Finally, it can be difficult to distinguish benign mesothelial cells in a florid reactive mesothelial proliferation (RMP) from tumor cells in an EPM, as RMP may mimic PM by displaying high cellularity, cytologic atypia, and several mitoses [50].

On the other hand, in presence of strong imaging-based suspicion of PM, conventional smears can be supplemented with cell-blocks that allow to detect the possible loss of the BAP1 tumor-suppressor protein by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and/or the homozygote deletion of the CDKN2A TSG by fluorescence in-situ hybridization (FISH), which are PM-associated molecular alterations never observed in RMP.

Architectural growth patterns & morphological variants of clinical significance

Each histopathological type of PM can exhibit different growth patterns and morphological variants, which have prognostic significance. For EPM, the micropapillary and the solid architectural patterns as well as the occurrence of necrosis and/or bizarre giant tumor cells with anaplastic nuclei (”pleomorphic” variant) are associated with reduced OS [50,52]. ‘Desmoplastic’ PM (DPM) is a SPM variant characterized by scattered, unremarkable spindle cells chaotically arranged in fascicles and expansile nodules in a dense collagenized stroma. It is associated with a worse prognosis and is particularly difficult to diagnose, as it resembles reactive fibrosis and pleural plaques [50,52]. Elements favoring the diagnosis of DPM are unequivocal deep invasion into fat tissue or lung parenchyma, necrotic areas and/or the co-presence of areas of conventional mesothelioma tissue. Thus, it can only be diagnosed with certainty on large pleural specimens obtained by VATS or extensive pleurectomy, when ≥50% of tumor tissue displays a desmoplastic pattern, whereas the term ‘PM with desmoplastic features’ should be used with small biopsies containing PM tissue with this histological aspect [50,52]. The ‘transitional’ PM consists of cells with mixed epithelioid and sarcomatoid features, but based on its poor prognosis and molecular profile is classified as a variant of SPM [50,53].

Cytomorphological grading of PM

Grading of EPM based on nuclear grade of tumor cells (I to III according to the sum of nuclear atypia score and mitotic count score) and presence/absence of tumor necrosis is also strongly advocated for by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Mesothelioma Interest Group (IMIG), both in biopsies and surgical resections, as it strongly correlates with prognosis and is expected to have therapeutic relevance [50,52,54–56]. A consensus was reached regarding a two-tier grading system that classifies EPM as low-grade (nuclear grade I or II without necrosis) or high-grade (nuclear grade II with necrosis or nuclear grade III) [50,52,54–56]. There is no strong evidence for grading non-epithelioid PM, although the Ki-67 proliferation index has been suggested as prognostic and predictive parameter for chemotherapy in all PM types [56].

Distinguishing PM from benign reactive pleural changes & from other cancer types

All initial PM diagnoses should be confirmed by experienced mesothelioma pathologists, as there can be significant interobserver variation, especially with BPM [51,52,57]. Invasive growth of mesothelium-derived cells is an essential diagnostic criterion. PM needs to be differentiated from RMP, which can be supported by ancillary investigations related to the most common somatic genomic alterations that result in inactivation of the TSGs BAP1 and CDKN2A [49,52,53]. Indeed, the loss of nuclear BAP1 expression in mesothelial cells as assessed by IHC (as a surrogate for BAP1 loss-of-function mutation, truncating fusion, or deletion) and/or the homozygous deletion of CDKN2A identifiable by FISH are specific for PM, as they are not observed in benign proliferations [50,51]. Yet, their sensitivity is suboptimal, as only 70% of EPMs and no more than 20% of SPMs are BAP1-negative by IHC, while 40–70% of EPMs/BPMs and 90% of SMPs harbor CDKN2A-deletion [49,52]. Consequently, positive BAP1 expression or lack of CDKN2A-deletion in invasively growing mesothelial cells does not exclude the diagnosis of PM. For practical reasons, the loss of cytoplasmatic methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (MTAP) expression detectable in mesothelial cells by IHC can be used as a surrogate for FISH-detected CDKN2A-deletion, as the MTAP gene is co-localized on chromosome 9p21 and most often co-deleted with CDKN2A [50,51,58–62] (Figures 2 and 3).

Deletions or inactivating mutations of the NF2 TSG are other common somatic alterations in PM [60,61]. They can be detected by FISH and DNA-sequencing, respectively, yet they are not routinely employed as biomarkers, because they are late and often heterogeneous events in tumor tissue [50,63,64]. Recently, IHC-detected loss of expression of Merlin, the product of the NF2 TSG, was proposed as additional surrogate marker in the differential diagnosis between PM and RMP but full implementation awaits further validation [50,65,66]. Importantly, an up to 100% concordance of BAP1 and MTAP expression in cytologic samples from pleural effusions and corresponding histopathologic specimens has been shown, indicating the utility of these biomarkers in the differential diagnosis of PM from RMP by cytology [67]. In this regard, a well-known challenge encountered routinely by thoracic pathologists is that the positivity of MTAP immunostaining is never very strong (Figure 2), thus the WHO and IMIG strongly recommend interpreting the MTAP immunostaining in presence of an appropriate positive control [68,69].

Deletion of CDKN2A and loss of MTAP expression correlate with shorter OS of PM patients, while loss of nuclear BAP1 expression is associated with more favorable prognosis [50,61]. This reflects in large part the better prognosis and response to platinum-based chemotherapy of PM patients with germline BAP1 mutations, who are younger and often affected by low-grade EPM and the familiar BAP1 tumor predisposition syndrome [5,6,70,71]. For these reasons, genetic germline testing and counseling for PM patients, especially the younger ones, has been proposed by the IMIG [50,71]. Interestingly, recent data seem to suggest that also in older patients without germline tumor predisposition, BAP1 loss by IHC correlates with better response to platinum-based chemotherapy or to immunotherapy [5,72].

The most frequent differential diagnosis for PM is pleural metastasis/spreading from other cancer types, especially pulmonary adenocarcinoma. For this distinction, IHC panels should be utilized, including: A) 2–3 of the most mesothelium-specific markers, such as calretinin (Figure 2), Wilms tumor-1 (WT1), and podoplanin/D2-40, and B) 2 or more markers tailored to (adeno)carcinoma, melanoma, sarcoma or lymphoma, depending on the observed tumor histology and clinico-radiological differential diagnoses, as extensively reviewed elsewhere [50–52].

A pan-cytokeratin (pan-CK) immunostaining is an important biomarker for diagnosing SPM, which being poorly differentiated, is very often negative for one or more mesothelial marker(s), but is diffusely positive for pan-CK in 95% of the cases [50–52]. Consequently, observing pan-CK-positive spindle cells invading the subpleural fat confirms the diagnosis of SPM vs. reactive fibrosis. SPM needs to be distinguished from sarcomas and pulmonary sarcomatoid carcinoma, which are also diffusely positive for pan-CK. The correlation with clinico-radiological data, IHC for specific sarcoma- or carcinoma-markers and GATA3 (positive in most SPMs, but not in sarcomatoid carcinoma) as well as molecular findings may be used for the distinction [50,52].

Mesothelioma in situ, a new diagnostic challenge

The current WHO classification of pleural tumors includes a new diagnostic category, called ‘mesothelioma in situ (MIS)’, defined as ”preinvasive single-layer proliferation of neoplastic mesothelial cells on the pleural surface” [52]. Its diagnosis requires thorough multidisciplinary information and discussion, is currently only applicable as precursor lesion for PMs with an epithelioid component, and is limited to surgical specimens or large VATS biopsies [50,53]. The essential criteria for this diagnosis are: 1) Persistent unexplained pleural effusion; 2) No thoracoscopic/imaging evidence of pleural tumor; 3) Histologic observation of one single layer of (more or less) atypical mesothelial cells covering the pleural surface without sign of invasion and 4) showing loss of BAP1 and/or MTAP expression by IHC and/or homozygous CDKN2A deletion by FISH, as a sign that they are neoplastic; 5) Multidisciplinary discussion of diagnosis [50,52]. In this respect, anamnestic information regarding possible asbestos exposure, thoracic radiation or genetic predisposition may be helpful.

MIS has been associated with somatic BAP1-mutation/deletion (or more rarely CDKN2A-deletion) [73]. The pathological differential diagnoses of MIS are invasive PM, benign RMP, and well- differentiated papillary mesothelial tumor (WDPMT). Importantly, WDPMT, as opposed to MIS, does not show loss of BAP1 or MTAP by IHC or CDKN2A deletion by FISH [50,52]. However, cases of WDPMT transforming after years to PM through loss of BAP1 or rare cases of MIS with lack of BAP1, CDKN2A or MTAP alterations have been observed [50,74,75]. IMIG has suggested to describe the cytological samples from cases with relapsing pleural effusions containing atypical BAP1-negative or CDKN2A-deleted mesothelial and lacking radiological or thoracoscopic evidence of tumor, as representing at least MIS without excluding the possibility of PM [76].

Consensus regarding the treatment of patients with MIS is still missing. Most of the specialized PM centers only implement close follow-up, however single cases treated with surgery or local/systemic chemotherapy aimed at preventing the development of PM, have been reported [76]. Currently, it is not clear how to predict whether and when a MIS might progress to PM.

To summarize, among the three types, EPM is the most frequent, most sensitive to chemotherapy, and associated with best prognosis. WHO recommends that all the architectural growth patterns and variants of each type of PM be reported in both biopsies and pleurectomy specimens given their prognostic value [50–52]. PM may be difficult to diagnose by pleural cytology as deep invasion is not assessable by this method, RMP may imitate EPM, and sarcomatoid cells in BPM/SPM do not shed into pleural effusions. Yet, IHC-detected loss of BAP1 expression and/or FISH-identified CDKN2A deletion in PM cells may differentiate them from benign mesothelial proliferations [50,77,78]. Deep invasion into pleural stroma and subpleural fat is an essential diagnostic criterion for PM. The distinction from RMP can be supported by demonstrating loss of nuclear BAP1 expression by IHC, CDKN2A deletion by FISH and/or MTAP expression by IHC in mesothelial cells. Combining the three markers in a panel, in which also immunohistochemical demonstration of Merlin/NF2 loss can be added, may significantly increase the diagnostic sensitivity. BAP1 loss correlates with better prognosis, particularly in younger individuals with germline BAP1 mutations. Recent data that need further validation indicate that BAP1 loss may also correlate with stronger effect of chemotherapy and immunotherapy in PM patients. For distinguishing PM from other primary or metastatic cancer types of the pleura, immunohistochemical panels including two-three PM-specific markers and different markers tailored to other cancers are used. However, it should be considered that SPM can often be negative for mesothelial markers and only positive for pan-CK staining. Lastly, MIS is a preinvasive single-layer proliferation of neoplastic (BAP1-negative and/or CDKN2A-deleted) mesothelial cells on the pleural surface of patients with persistent unexplained pleural effusion and no imaging/thoracoscopic sign of PM. In these cases, finding BAP1-negative or CDKN2A-deleted mesothelial cells in pleural cytology should be regarded as indicative for at least MIS, but with PM not excludable by cytology alone. These cases should be discussed in a multidisciplinary setting [50,72].

Prognostic factors

The most well-described positive prognostic factors for PM are early disease (stage I or II), epithelioid subtype, good performance status and germline mutations [5,79]. Histological and cytomorphological prognostic factors recommended by the WHO are described above. Other prognostic factors described in the literature are chest pain, weight loss, dyspnea, anemia, leukocytosis, and thrombocytosis [79]. Several prognostic scores have been proposed to assess PM patients prognosis, including the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) score, the Cancer and Leukemia Group B (CALGB) score, the Brims score, the modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS), the LENT score (when pleural effusion is present) and the PROMISE score [80–85]. Furthermore, studies have suggested potential prognostic and predictive biomarkers for PM, such as BAP1 and PD-L1 [70,86]. However, none of the above scores and biomarkers have been validated for use in the clinic, and there is need for prospective validation. In this respect, two important recently published studies have described younger patients with germline BAP1 mutations presenting with low-grade multifocal mesothelioma and indolent disease, though with risk of developing cancers also in other organs, as part of the BAP1 cancer syndrome [87,88].

Assessment of asbestos exposure

The main cause of PM is asbestos exposure; thus, thorough evaluation of the exposure by occupational physicians is recommended, especially considering the national compensation schemes [89]. Both occupational, domestic, and environmental asbestos exposure may cause PM [90,91]. In Denmark, only PM cases with previous occupational, and, to a less extent, domestic asbestos exposure are entitled to compensation.

Follow-up

For PM patients that undergo systemic oncological treatment, it is advisable to evaluate the response to treatment by CT thorax, preferably using the mesothelioma modified Response Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumours (mRECIST) criteria [92,93]. For patients that have received curative multimodal treatment with chemotherapy and surgery, it is recommended that CT thorax is performed every three-six months to evaluate remission.

Treatment

Oncological treatment of inoperable PM

First-line treatment

The mainstay treatment for patients with unresectable PM has, since 2003, been pemetrexed in combination with cisplatin due to the results of the first-line EMPHACIS study [94]. It was subsequently shown that carboplatin plus pemetrexed may be used if cisplatin is contraindicated or toxicities develop. A large, expanded access program revealed similar efficacy of the carboplatin regimen compared to cisplatin plus pemetrexed but with improved tolerability [95].

The MAPS trial showed 2.7 months longer OS using the combination of the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitor bevacizumab together with platinum plus pemetrexed [96]. The bevacizumab regimen is, however, not approved by regulatory health authorities for patients with unresectable PM. The LUME-Meso trial explored the angiogenesis inhibitor Nintedanib, targeting several receptors involved in angiogenesis. The trial found no difference in progression-free survival (PFS) [97].

Immunotherapy combinations have recently revealed activity in first-line treatment of PM. Nivolumab, a fully human anti-PD-1 antibody, and ipilimumab, a fully human anti-CTLA-4 antibody, have been employed in the CheckMate-743 Phase III trial, in which nivolumab plus ipilimumab exhibited a median OS increase of 4 months compared to pemetrexed combined with either cisplatin or carboplatin [98]. Interestingly, the histopathological subtype had a considerable impact, as median OS for nivolumab + ipilimumab vs. chemotherapy differed considerably between the epithelioid (median OS: 18.7 vs. 16.5 months) and non-epithelioid (median OS: 18.1 vs. 8.8 months) histology [98]. Nivolumab plus ipilimumab is now approved as a first-line treatment by the Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency. In Denmark, it is the preferred choice for first line treatment in unresectable patients with non-epithelioid histology.

Second-line treatment

Hitherto, the evidence for second-line treatment of relapsed PM and improvement in OS as compared to placebo has been limited. Hence, entry to clinical trials may be considered, depending on local availability. A randomized trial comparing vinorelbine against best supportive care revealed improved PFS but without significant OS benefit [99]. Another randomized trial compared pemetrexed monotherapy to best supportive care in relapsed PM and showed response rate to pemetrexed of 18.7% without significant OS benefit [100].

More recently, several second-line immune-targeted trials have been reported. Most of these have expectedly recruited patients treated with platinum and pemetrexed doublet, making future deployment in an era of first-line immune-checkpoint inhibitors potentially challenging, especially in the non-epitheloid subtype. Early phase II trials using single-agent PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors revealed response rates ranging from 8% to 29% [101]. However, the phase II MAPS2 trial reported higher response rates for ipilimumab and nivolumab (28%) than for nivolumab alone (19%), respectively, and median OS of 15.9 and 11.9 months [101]. The PROMISE-Meso trial compared single-agent chemotherapy (either vinorelbine or gemcitabine) against pembrolizumab but found no OS benefit for pembrolizumab despite a superior response rate [102].

The phase III CONFIRM trial displayed superior PFS and OS of nivolumab monotherapy as compared to placebo (PFS 3.0 vs. 1.8 months; OS 10.2 vs. 6.9 months) [103]. The randomized phase II RAMES study compared gemcitabine with or without ramucirumab, an anti-vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 antibody, and reported significantly improved OS with the combination (median OS 13.8 and 7.5 months, respectively) [104].

In conclusion, the current most widely used and best documented first-line chemotherapy for unresectable PM remains platinum together with pemetrexed. This regimen is also widely used as adjuvant or neoadjuvant treatment for resectable cases. Immunotherapy with a combination of PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibition, such as with nivolumab and ipilimumab, has greatly improved results for non-epitheloid subtypes and appears equal to chemotherapy in the epitheloid subtype. Further treatment improvements are eagerly awaited. Concerning the second-line setting, chemotherapy single agents have yielded modest response rate without OS benefit, while nivolumab proved significant survival benefit against placebo. Further trials in the search for more active agents and combinations are needed. So far, targeted treatments for PM have been unsuccessful, in large part due to this cancer type mainly being driven by alterations in TSGs, yet early-phase clinical trials may hold promise for the future [105].

Multimodal treatment

Surgery together with neoadjuvant/adjuvant platinum-pemetrexed combination-chemotherapy can be implemented as curative-intended treatment in selected cases (based on patient’s age below 75 year, PS 0–1, preserved cardiac and contralateral pulmonary function, tumor stage I-IIIA, and epithelioid or biphasic histology with <50% sarcomatoid component) [106]. The surgical operation is a lung-preserving pleurectomy/decortication (PD) or extended pleurectomy/decortication (EPD) with resection of ipsilateral pericardium and diaphragm if needed (Figure 4). In contrast, extrapleural pneumonectomy (EPP; en-bloc removal of the whole lung, visceral-parietal pleura, pericardium and ipsilateral diaphragm) does not provide a survival advantage and is more mutilating and frequently associated with postoperative complications as compared to PD/EPD. Thus, PD/EPD is the first-choice surgical procedure [107]. Surgery as monotherapy can be considered as de-bulking procedure in patients, who cannot tolerate chemotherapy; however, no survival benefit has been shown. The effect of multimodal treatment has been evaluated in two insightful studies but has been challenged by the MARS 2 study and needs further validation by new randomized controlled trials with adequate sample size [108–110]. Currently, PM surgery within a multimodality protocol is carried out in the Danish specialized, high-volume center at Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen University Hospital according to the recommendations of BTS and ERS/ESTS/EACTS/ESTRO guidelines. Based on the above-mentioned selection criteria, every year on average 25 out of the approximately 140 yearly cases of PM diagnosed in Denmark are operated at Rigshospitalet.

Other palliative treatment

Palliative radiotherapy can be considered as pain-relieving procedure in patients with pain extension matching the underlying disease process. Prophylactic radiotherapy against tumor spread into biopsy tracts after invasive procedures has shown no significant effect and therefore is not recommended [107]. PM patients with vena cava superior syndrome respond poorly to radiotherapy and can in selected cases be relieved by placing a stent in the vena cava superior. Pleurodesis and/or ad hoc drainage by an indwelling pleural catheter (IPC) can be attempted as palliation of dyspnea due to large volumes of relapsing pleural effusions. The efficacy of the two procedures has been shown to be comparable and not inferior to VATS pleurectomy, which on the other hand has more frequent complications [111]. The most frequently used material for pleurodesis is talc instilled thoracoscopically or by drain. With more extensive tumor involvement of the visceral pleura and lung ‘entrapment’ pleurodesis is less likely to succeed and IPC should be considered instead.
