A roadmap for systematic humanization of a chimeric antigen receptor: preclinical validation of a humanized CD22 scFv as a model
Authors: Narcís Fernández, Paolo Petazzi, Vladimir Mulens-Arias, Laura García Pérez, Pablo Menéndez, Víctor M Díaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Structure-guided complementarity-determining region (CDR) grafting preserves CD22 antigen binding.•Computational screening mitigates instability and glycosylation risks.•Humanized chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) maintain cytotoxicity and signaling capacity.•In vivo efficacy comparable with murine CD22 CAR constructs.•Pipeline enables clinically translatable low-immunogenic CAR design.

Immunotherapy has generated unprecedented expectations in cancer treatment, particularly for hematologic malignancies. Among these approaches, adoptive cellular immunotherapy based on the genetic engineering of human T cells with chimeric antigen receptors (CARs)—synthetic receptors that redirect T cells against tumor-associated surface antigens—has shown remarkable clinical success. CAR T-cell therapy has proven especially effective in refractory or relapsed B-cell malignancies, including B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) and B-cell lymphomas with CD19-directed CAR T cells, as well as multiple myeloma with BCMA-directed CAR T cells. These therapies have achieved early complete responses (CRs) in nearly 85% of patients, and around one half of the patients remain in CR 12 months after treatment [1,2].

CARs are synthetic biological products, and the joining regions between their different domains can give rise to newly generated immunogenic peptides. In CARs of murine origin, the single-chain variable fragment (scFv) is typically derived from mouse monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), raising well-founded concerns regarding potential immunogenicity. Murine-derived sequences may elicit host immune responses, including both humoral and cellular mechanisms, potentially leading to rejection of the CAR T cells [3,4]. Such immune recognition may compromise the efficacy of subsequent CAR T-cell infusions, sometimes resulting in premature loss or reduced persistence of the infused cells [5]. For example, in a study of autologous CD20 CAR T-cell therapy in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), 2 of 4 patients developed a cellular immune response against the vector-encoded neomycin phosphotransferase, likely contributing to early CAR T-cell clearance [6]. Similarly, in patients with renal cell carcinoma treated with anticarbonic anhydrase IX (CAIX) CAR T cells, humoral and cellular immune responses were observed in 7 and 9 of 12 patients, respectively, which adversely affected CAR T-cell persistence and led to early clearance [7]. Clinical studies of CD19-directed CAR T cells using murine scFvs have also identified immunogenicity as a significant concern in subsets of patients, with poorer responses noted in those receiving repeated infusions [8]. To address these issues, humanized or fully human CAR constructs have been developed. Humanized anti-CD19 CAR T cells have demonstrated potent anti-leukemia activity and durable remissions in patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) B-ALL, including those who previously received murine CAR T-cell therapy [9,10]. Importantly, the use of humanized CARs has been shown to improve efficacy in patients who relapsed after prior treatment with murine-based CD19 CAR T cells [11]. Therefore, the development of humanized CAR constructs represents a rational and effective strategy to mitigate immune-mediated rejection and enhance the therapeutic potential of CAR T-cell therapy.

In the context of B-ALL and B-cell lymphoma, although CAR T-cell therapies have achieved CR rates of nearly 85% in treated patients, approximately 60% inevitably relapse, primarily due to poor CAR T-cell persistence or the emergence of CD19− B-ALL clones [12, 13, 14]. Therapeutic options for patients who relapse with CD19-negative disease remain extremely limited. This clinical challenge has driven the development of novel strategies aimed at enhancing CAR T-cell efficacy and durability, including the design of scFvs targeting alternative antigens, optimization of costimulatory domains, and the exploration of different effector cell platforms.

Like CD19, CD22 is expressed in a B-cell lineage-restricted manner. Its presence on CD34⁺CD19⁻ B-cell progenitors may contribute to phenotypic escape in patients with B-ALL undergoing CD19-targeted immunotherapy [15]. CD22-directed CAR T cells represent a compelling complementary strategy to existing CD19-targeted therapies for 2 main reasons: they offer an alternative therapeutic option for relapses characterized by CD19⁻ or CD19dim expression, as CD22 expression is generally retained in such cases; and they enable targeting of early prevariable-diversity-joining rearrangement (pre-VDJ) CD19⁻ B-lineage progenitors that may evade CD19 CAR T-cell therapy. Indeed, CD22 has been explored in clinical trials using anti-CD22 antibodies conjugated to immunotoxins, and several CD22-specific CAR constructs have demonstrated clinical remission rates of approximately 70% of patients with R/R B-ALL, including those naïve to or refractory after CD19 CAR T-cell therapy [16, 17, 18].

We have recently developed and characterized a novel CD22-CAR, which targets a membrane-distal epitope of human CD22 and demonstrated potent efficacy at eliminating primary B-ALL cells [19]. To denote its murine origin, we refer to the construct as m-CD22.7-CAR and its corresponding scFv as m-CD22.7 throughout the manuscript. The m-CD22.7-CAR is a second-generation CAR comprising a CD8α transmembrane domain (TM), a 4−1BB costimulatory domain, and a CD3ζ signaling domain. This construct specifically recognizes and eliminates B-ALL cells in vitro and demonstrates potent antileukemic activity in vivo in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models of B-ALL. Furthermore, when incorporated into a bispecific tandem CAR alongside an anti-CD19 scFv or in T-cells secreting CD19 T-cell engagers, the construct exhibits enhanced antileukemic efficacy and specificity [20,21].

Given these encouraging results of CD22-directed CAR-T and the need to preclude CAR T cells from being rejected by the patient’s immune system, we aimed to develop humanized versions of the m-CD22.7-CAR and conduct a preclinical validation in parallel with the murine counterpart. Various strategies have been established for antibody humanization [22, 23, 24]. Here, we followed a systematic protocol using structure-assisted grafting of the murine complementary-determining regions (CDRs), followed by identification of potential liabilities as well as assessment of structural stability. The resulting humanized (h) CD22 CAR T cells (designated h-CD22.7-CAR) were subsequently validated through both in vitro and in vivo studies. In vitro analyses included the assessment of CD22-binding affinity in untransduced T cells and in T cells transduced with either murine or humanized CAR constructs, alongside assessment of CAR T-cell expansion. Furthermore, antigen specificity and cytotoxic activity were examined across a range of cell lines, PDX-expanded cells, and in vivo xenograft models. Across all assays, the h-CD22.7-CAR demonstrated comparable performance to its murine counterpart in terms of antigen recognition, specificity, and cytotoxic potency.

METHODS

Humanization Protocol

The humanization protocol is comprised of a set of defined steps described below.

Searching human frameworks

Human frameworks were selected through sequence similarity searches in the International Immunogenetics Information System (IMGT) database [25]. Sequence identity was calculated excluding the CDRs, as these hypervariable regions are intentionally replaced during grafting. The human framework sequences exhibiting the highest identity to the murine scFv framework regions were then chosen as acceptor scaffolds for CDR grafting.

Structural modeling of murine and human scFv

Structural modeling was performed using the Multiple Mapping Method with Multiple Templates (M4T), a comparative modeling approach that integrates multiple templates and alternative sequence alignments to enhance the accuracy and reliability of the resulting structural models [26].

Grafting of CDRs

Grafting involved not only the loop regions themselves but also the flanking residues, commonly referred to as Vernier regions or stems [27], which cradle and stabilize the loops. Studies of loop structure classification have consistently shown that the conformation of CDR loops is strongly influenced by these flanking residues [28, 29, 30].

In silico prediction of immunogenicity

A comparative in silico analysis of the murine CD22.7 scFv (m-CD22.7) and its humanized variants was performed using multiple artificial neural network (ANN) tools to assess their potential immunogenicity. Specifically, the prediction of potential major histocompatibility complex class 1 and II (MHC-I and MHC-II) binders was carried out using the following resources: NetMHCcons, an ANN-based tool for predicting MHC-I binders [31]; NetMHCstabpan, which predicts the stability of MHC-I-peptide complexes [32]; and NetMHCIIpan, an ANN for predicting MHC-II binders [33].

Identification of potential liabilities in humanized sequences

The resulting humanized sequences were further analyzed to identify potential sequence liabilities that could compromise scFv structure or function. Previous studies on antibody humanization have shown that sequence analysis can reliably predict such liabilities [34,35]. A total of 11 distinct liabilities were screened in the humanized sequences using the ABodyBuilder pipeline [36,37], including: the presence of N-linked glycosylation sites [38], unpaired cysteine residues [39], methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp) oxidation [40], asparagine deamination and aspartate isomerization [41], lysine glycation [40], conversion of N-terminal glutamate to pyroglutamate [42], integrin binding motifs [43,44], CD11c/CD18 binding motifs [45] and propensity for fragmentation [46]. It is worth mentioning that if the liability(ies) map onto the CDRs, then this is accepted, as changes in the CDRs can lead to loss of specificity and/or binding to the antigen.

Assessment of stability

The final step of the humanization process involved a structure-based assessment of scFv stability. Two complementary methods were used for this purpose: the InterfaceAnalyzer tool from the Rosetta suite [47] and the Split-Statistical Potentials Server (SPServer) [48]. InterfaceAnalyzer evaluates the scFv interface, providing metrics such as binding energy, interface surface area, and PackStat values [47,48]. In parallel, SPServer, which is based on statistical potential, was used to assess both the overall scFv stability and the interface energy [48]. Values obtained for the humanized scFvs were compared with those of the murine scFv to determine the structural changes induced by the humanization process.

Preclinical Validation of Humanized scFv(s)

CAR design and vectors, lentiviral production, and T-cells transduction

The original m-CD22.7-CAR has been previously described (Velasco-Hernandez et al. [19], 2020). Humanized versions of CD22-specific scFvs were cloned into a pCCL lentiviral backbone containing a human CD8α hinge and TM, human 4-1BB and CD3ζ endodomains (second-generation CAR), and a T2A-GFP cassette.

CAR-expressing viral particles, pseudotyped with VSV-G, were generated using cotransfection of HEK 293T cells with the pCCL vector and the packaging plasmids pMD2-VSVG, pMDLg/pRRE, and pRSV-Rev using polyethylenimine (PEI; Polysciences). Viral supernatants were collected at 48 and 72 hours after transfection and concentrated using ultracentrifugation.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from buffy coats of healthy donors via Ficoll-Hypaque gradient centrifugation (GE Healthcare). Buffy coats were obtained from the Barcelona Blood and Tissue Bank under Institutional Review Board approval (HCB/2018/0030). T cells were activated for 2 days using plate-bound anti-CD3 (OKT3) and anti-CD28 (CD28.2) antibodies (BD Biosciences) and subsequently transduced with the CAR-expressing lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in the presence of interleukin-7 (IL-7) and IL-15 (10 ng/mL; Miltenyi Biotec). CAR-transduced T cells were expanded in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco/Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma), penicillin-streptomycin, and IL-7/IL-15 (10 ng/mL) for up to 10 days. Surface expression of CD22-CAR was monitored using flow cytometry.

Cell Lines

The wild-type B-ALL cell lines SEM and NALM6, as well as CD19-knockout (KO) and CD22-KO SEM cells and Luciferase (Luc+)/GFP-expressing NALM6 cells (NALM6-Luc+), were obtained and cultured as previously described [19,20].

In Vitro Cytotoxicity Assays and Cytokine Release Determination

In vitro cytotoxicity and activation assays were performed by incubating target cells with either CD22-CAR T cells or untransduced (UT) T cells at various effector-to-target (E:T) ratios for the indicated time periods. Target cell lines were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and penicillin-streptomycin. CAR T cell-mediated cytotoxicity was determined by measuring the number of residual live target cells (7-aminoactinomycin D⁻; 7-AAD⁻) at each time point and E:T ratio. Absolute cell counts were obtained using BD TruCount absolute count tubes (BD Biosciences).

Cytokine production (IL-2, tumor necrosis factor α [TNFα], and interferon γ [IFNγ]) was quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using BD OptEIA Human ELISA kits (BD Biosciences) on supernatants harvested 24–48 hours after T-cell exposure, using a 1:1 E:T ratio. CD22-CAR expression was confirmed using GFP fluorescence, combined with incubation with human recombinant CD22-His (rCD22-His; ThermoFisher Scientific) followed by anti-His-APC antibody (J095G46; BioLegend).

T-cell activation was assessed 48 hours after PBMC plating using surface staining with CD3-PE (UCHT1; BD Biosciences), CD25-APC (M-A251; BD Biosciences), and CD69-VioBlue (REA824; Miltenyi Biotec). In vitro identification of T cells and target cells was performed using the following human antibodies: CD3-PE (UCHT1), CD22-APC (HIB22), CD19-BV421 (HIB19), CD10-PECy7 (HI10a), CD13-PECy7 (WM15, for SEM cells), or CD33-APC (WM53; BD Biosciences). Dead cells were excluded using 7-AAD staining. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted on a FACSCanto II cytometer using FACSDiva software (BD Biosciences).

In vivo xenograft models for B-ALL and CAR T cells

In vivo studies were performed using 12-week-old NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice (The Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) maintained under pathogen-free conditions. All procedures were approved by the local ethics committee (HRH-17–0029 P1).

NALM6-Luc+ cells (0.5 × 106) were transplanted intrabone marrow into sublethally irradiated (2 Gy) NSG mice, followed 3 days later by intravenous infusion of 2 × 106 of CD22-CARTs derived from healthy PBMCs. Tumor burden was monitored at the indicated time points by bioluminescence using the Xenogen in vivo imaging system (IVIS) 50 (Perkin Elmer). When MOCK-treated animals were fully leukemic, animals were euthanized and analyzed using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for leukemic burden and CAR-T persistence. Bone marrow (BM) and peripheral blood (PB) were FACS analyzed for human chimerism at sacrifice. Cells were stained with the following antibodies: HLA-ABC-PE (G46-2.6), CD45-BV510 (HI30), CD3-PerCP (SK7), CD22-APC, CD19-BV421, and CD10-PECy7 (BD Biosciences). After a 30-minute incubation, cells were lysed and fixed using BD FACSLysing Solution (BD Biosciences). Data were acquired on a FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed using FACSDiva software.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with n representing independent donors (≥ 3 per experiment). Differences between groups at a single time point were assessed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Experiments with several time points or E:T ratios were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc correction when appropriate. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1), and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.005 (**), and p < 0.001 (***).

RESULTS

Selection of Human Framework and Overall Description of the Humanization Process

The overall humanization workflow is summarized in Figure 1. The humanization process begins with the identification of the most suitable human framework regions onto which the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the murine scFv are to be grafted. The murine sequence of the scFv m-CD22.7 [19] was used to query the database of IgG (http://www.imgt.org/). The search returned a list of human immunoglobulin (Ig) genes sorted using sequence identity and coverage, selecting the human sequences with both the highest sequence identity for the heavy and light chains. For the heavy chain, we selected the allele IGHV4-31*02 and IGHJ5*01, which shared a 71.7% sequence identity and a total of 23 nonconserved substitutions excluding the CDRs (Figure 2A and Supplementary Figure E1). For the light chain, we selected the alleles IGKV2-30*02 and IGKJ4*01. The sequence identity was higher, at 82%, with a total of 12 nonconserved substitutions without considering the CDRs (Figure 2B and Supplementary Material).

Structural Modeling and CDRs Grafting

Once the most suitable human framework has been identified, the next step involves the structural modeling of both the murine and humanized scFv constructs (Figure 1). The m-CD22.7 sequence was modeled using templates from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/): 6BZW [49] for the heavy chain and 3SGD [50] for the light chain. For the human framework, the heavy chain was modeled using 4HJJ [51] and the light chain using 6NOU [52].

The murine and human scFv structures were structurally superimposed to identify the most suitable regions for splicing the CDRs from the murine scFv onto the human framework (Figure 1). This structural alignment allowed the identification of adjacent regions to the CDRs that exhibit the highest structural similarity. At these positions, the polypeptide main-chain conformation was most closely matched, ensuring that CDR grafting introduced minimal perturbations to the overall structure. The structural alignment revealed, as expected, a high similarity in the regular secondary structure elements, with the main differences localized to the loop regions (Figure 3). The heavy chain exhibited greater structural variation, particularly in H3 (Figure 3B). This is consistent with the known observation that the H3 CDR has the highest sequence and structural diversity among all CDRs and plays a central role in antigen specificity [53,54]. In m-CD22.7, the H3 CDR is slightly longer than its human counterpart and shows low sequence identity (Figure 2, Figure 3), suggesting an important role in antigen binding and specificity. In contrast, the light chain showed higher structural similarity to the human framework, consistent with the higher sequence conservation between the murine and human scFv (Figure 3C).

Beyond the CDRs, special attention was given to nonconserved residues located in other potentially important regions of the scFv that may influence its structural integrity or functional performance. These residues included substitutions at the interface between the variable heavy (VH) and variable light (VL) domains, fully buried positions, or regions structurally adjacent to CDRs that are not part of the Vernier regions. In such cases, the decision to preserve the murine residue or replace it with the corresponding human residue was made individually, considering the local structural constraints and microenvironment. To introduce flexibility in the humanization process, two versions of each humanized scFv were typically generated: a “strict” humanization, in which the CDRs, Vernier residues, and selected nonconserved residues critical for structure or function were retained from the murine sequence; and a “relaxed” version, in which some of the nonconserved residues included in the strict design were reverted to the corresponding human residues.

Based on the structural superposition, the CDRs H1, H2, and H3 of the heavy chain and L1, L2, and L3 of the light chain were grafted according to the residue boundaries listed in Table 1 (numbering as in Figure 2). Additionally, several nonconserved residues in the human scaffold were reverted to the murine counterpart in both the heavy and light chains (Figure 2). These included K39, N44, I67, Y78, T88, and S112 in the heavy chain and T7, T10, L41, and K50 in the light chain (residue numbering refers to the murine and humanized sequences and not the human scaffold). These residues were selected based on their potential structural or functional significance. For example, K39 in the heavy chain forms extensive contacts with the PGQS48 region in the light chain at the VH/VL interface, a region conserved in both human and murine frameworks; thus, preserving this residue was important structurally. The combination of the grafted CDRs and these selected residue changes constitutes the strict humanized version (h1) (Figure 2 and Methods).

To derive the relaxed humanization variant, hereafter referred to as h2, we identified several positions in the heavy chain to revert to the human sequence (Figure 2). These include NK44, I67, TE88, and S112. The structural context of these changes is illustrated in Figure 4. The first change, NK44KG, was reverted because of its structural location in the scFv. These residues are close to K39 and lie at the VH/VL interface (Figure 4A). Although the KG residues are conserved across the top human alleles identified using IMGT, the substitution of K with G represents a significant physicochemical change. To mitigate potential structural issues at the interface, this position was reverted to the human residue in the h2 variant. The second change, I67V, involves a residue buried in the core (Figure 4B). Although this is a conservative substitution, the side chain of isoleucine (I) is slightly larger than that of valine (V). To maintain optimal packing, the residue was reverted to a slightly smaller valine. The third change, TE88AA, is structurally adjacent to NK44KG (Figure 4C) and was similarly reverted. Finally, S112L represents a conserved substitution. However, leucine (L) is more prevalent among human sequences at this position, so the change was reverted in h2 (Figure 4D).

Screening for Liabilities and Stability

The final step before experimental validation involved screening the sequences for potential liabilities and posttranslational modification (PTM) changes that could affect protein function (Figure 1). Among the 11 liabilities assessed, only 3 were detected in both the murine and humanized variants (h1 and h2) (Table 3). These corresponded to potential liabilities associated with Met and Trp oxidation [40] and aspartic acid (Asp) isomerization [41]. Most of the identified liabilities were located within the CDRs or the adjacent stem regions, except for aspartate residues at positions 72 and 82 of the heavy chain and methionine at position 4 of the light chain, which were conserved across both murine and human sequences. Therefore, although liabilities were present, they were either intrinsic to the functional regions (CDRs or stems) or fully conserved between the murine and humanized scFv.

Finally, we evaluated the stability of the different scFv variants using structure-based energy calculations. The murine, h1, and h2 variants were first analyzed using SPServer. The calculated energy scores were comparable, with h2 exhibiting the most favorable value: −17.90, −18.88, and −21.06 for the murine, h1, and h2 scFv, respectively. This analysis was complemented with the Interface Analyzer from the Rosetta suite (Table 4). The overall binding energies were similar across all variants, with h2 displaying the most favorable value, corresponding to a slightly smaller interface surface. The PackStat values, which assess interface compactness, were also highly comparable among all variants (Table 4).

In Vitro and In Vivo Validation

The murine and both h1 and h2 CD22 scFvs were cloned into the clinically validated pCCL-Ef1a-based second-generation CAR lentiviral backbone, including a T2A-eGFP reporter cassette (Figure 5A). Primary T cells were successfully transduced, and similar CAR expression was detected using recombinant CD22 (rCD22) that correlated with eGFP signal (Figure 5B). Humanized CD22 CAR T cells showed a slightly, although nonstatistically significant, diminished expansion compared with mCD22.7 CAR T cells (Figure 5C) and comparable CD4:CD8 CAR T cell rates (not shown).

The CD22⁺ B-ALL cell lines NALM6 and SEM, along with the CD22⁻ Jurkat T-ALL cell line and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-edited CD22 knocked-out SEM cells, were used to evaluate the in vitro cytotoxicity and specificity of CD22 CAR T cells (Figure 6, Figure 7). Cytotoxic activity was initially measured in 24–48-hour cocultures of UT or CAR T cells with target cells at various effector-to-target (E:T) ratios. All CAR T-cell constructs (murine, h1, and h2) demonstrated comparably robust cytotoxic activity against NALM6 cells (Figure 6A) and SEM cells (Figure 7A, upper). Notably, this cytotoxic effect was antigen-specific, as no significant target cell death was observed following coculture with JURKAT cells (Figure 6B) or with CD22-knockout SEM cells (Figure 7A, below). Only low-level, nonspecific cytotoxicity was observed for h2-CD22.7 against SEM KO cells, and this occurred only at high E:T ratios and extended incubation (48 h) (Figure 7A). Cytotoxicity correlated well with pro-inflammatory cytokine release (Figures 6C, 7B). More importantly, cytotoxicity data generated with cell lines were fully reproduced with CD22low and CD22high B-ALL PDX (PDX-50 and PDX-265 are CD22low; PDX-1370 is CD22high) (Figure 8).

DISCUSSION

The development of CAR T-cell therapies has significantly improved outcomes for patients with R/R B-cell malignancies [1,55]. However, CAR constructs utilizing murine-derived scFvs pose a critical challenge due to their immunogenicity. The host immune system can recognize murine sequences, leading to the generation of anti-CAR antibodies and T-cell-mediated rejection, which ultimately reduces CAR T persistence and limits therapeutic efficacy [3,4,56]. This concern is specially pertinent in patients undergoing repeated CAR T-cell infusions, as pre-existing humoral and cellular immunity against murine-derived epitopes can result in accelerated clearance of infused CAR T cells, thereby compromising the efficacy of subsequent treatments [5,8]. Moreover, it was reported that the onset of anaphylaxis and cardiac arrest occurred within minutes of a third infusion of autologous messenger RNA (mRNA)-based mesothelin-targeting CAR T cells in one patient, an event attributed by the investigators to the presence of CAR-specific IgE antibodies [57].

To overcome these limitations, humanized CAR constructs have been engineered to reduce immunogenicity and enhance in vivo persistence, while preserving or improving antitumor efficacy [9,10]. In this study, we used a systematic, structure-assisted approach to humanize the previously reported murine CD22.7-CAR, which targets a membrane-distal epitope of the CD22 antigen [19, 20, 21]. The humanization strategy entailed grafting the CDRs of the murine scFv onto selected human immunoglobulin frameworks while ensuring structural integrity, antigen-binding affinity, and functional stability. By integrating in silico analyses to predict potential structural and functional liabilities, we strategically minimized framework modifications that could compromise CAR expression, folding, or antigen specificity—an essential consideration in antibody humanization [22,23].

Our preclinical validation confirmed that the humanized CD22-CAR versions preserved the antigen specificity, cytotoxicity, and in vivo antitumor efficacy of its murine counterpart. These findings align with previous studies highlighting the efficacy of humanized CAR T-cell therapies, particularly in scenarios where murine CAR constructs exhibited hampered effectiveness due to immune-mediated rejection [11]. The potential clinical benefit of our humanized CAR is especially pertinent in the context of the high relapse rate associated with CD19-targeted therapies, wherein CD22-directed CAR T cells have emerged as a promising salvage strategy [12,13]. The diminished immunogenicity of the CD22 CAR humanized construct -as indicated using in silico analyses showing fewer predicted MHC I/II-associated peptides—may enhance the durability of clinical responses, especially in patients who relapse following prior CD19-CAR T-cell therapy, improving persistence. Whether the observed reduction in immunogenic peptide content within humanized CD22-targeting CAR constructs significantly enhances CAR T-cell persistence and sustained B-ALL control remains to be determined. Definitive insights will require results from clinical trials comparing murine and humanized CD22 CAR T cells, specifically evaluating cellular and humoral immune responses against the CAR constructs and their correlation with CAR T-cell persistence.

Moreover, this study reinforces the accumulating evidence supporting CD22 as a clinically relevant and therapeutically actionable target for B-ALL [20]. The successful humanization of CD22.7-CAR offers a viable alternative to murine CD22 CARs and underscores the broader applicability of this structure-guided humanization pipeline to the development of other CAR constructs. By preserving the functional integrity of CAR T cells, although minimizing immunogenicity, our humanization approach contributes to the broader effort of optimizing CAR designs for durable and effective clinical application.

Future studies will be required to assess the persistence and immunogenicity of humanized CD22-CAR T cells in clinical settings. Moreover, continued optimization of CAR architecture, such as refinement of costimulatory domains or integration of novel engineering strategies, may further enhance therapeutic efficacy. Collectively, our findings support the potential of humanized CD22-CARs as next-generation therapeutic candidates for R/R-B-ALL and establish a robust framework for advancing the clinical translation of CAR T-cell therapies targeting alternative antigens.
