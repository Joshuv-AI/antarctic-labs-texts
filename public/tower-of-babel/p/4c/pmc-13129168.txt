Modeling the Public Health Impact of Introducing 4CMenB Vaccination for Infants in the Brazilian National Immunization Program
Authors: Thatiana Pinto, Zeki Kocaata, Ana Medina, Igor Sampietri, Graziela Bernardino, Justyna Zawieja, Yaneth Gil-Rojas, Anna Tytuła, Maria Gabriela Graña, Elise Kuylen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInvasive meningococcal disease (IMD) caused by meningococcal serogroup B (MenB) is a rare but serious illness with potentially life-threatening outcomes, particularly in infants. In Brazil, IMD is endemic, with the highest incidence in infants and most cases being due to MenB. The four-component MenB vaccine (4CMenB), with potential cross-protection against other meningococcal serogroups, is approved in Brazil for active immunization of individuals aged 2 months to 50 years. This modeling study assessed the public health impact on all age groups in Brazil of introducing routine vaccination with the 4CMenB vaccine in infants aged < 1 year.MethodsA static multigenerational multicohort model was developed using a discrete-time Markov approach over a 100-year time horizon. The model incorporates vaccine effectiveness, coverage, and waning rates to estimate the decrease in IMD incidence under the vaccination strategy (2 + 1 schedule at ages 3, 5, and 12 months) evaluated vs. no vaccination. Annual incidence rates of IMD by age group and serogroup between 2007 and 2023, excluding the COVID-19 pandemic period, were retrieved and adjusted for underreporting. Annual IMD-related deaths were calculated from age-specific case fatality rates.ResultsAssuming 69% cross-protection against serogroup W (MenW), the 4CMenB vaccination of infants would avert 63,038 (− 21%) IMD cases caused by MenB and MenW, and 11,301 (− 20%) deaths in all age groups. Introduction

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) is a serious health condition, with a potentially fatal outcome, caused by the bacterium Neisseria meningitidis. Twelve serogroups of N. meningitidis have been identified, and six of them (MenA, MenB, MenC, MenW, MenX, and MenY) are responsible for the majority of IMD cases worldwide [1, 2]. The pathogen can enter the bloodstream, potentially causing life-threatening conditions such as meningitis and septicemia [3, 4]. While IMD is relatively rare, infants and young children are at the greatest risk of contracting this disease, followed by adolescents and young adults, making it a major public health concern [5]. Up to 20% of individuals who survive acute IMD may experience long-term sequelae, such as physical disabilities, neurological disabilities, and psychological or behavioral impairments, that can persist for many years following the infection [6].

IMD is endemic in Brazil, with periodic outbreaks occurring [7, 8]. The incidence of the disease exhibits an unpredictable pattern that varies geographically, with infants experiencing the highest incidence rates [9]. Surveillance data indicate that N. meningitidis is an important cause of bacterial meningitis in Brazil. Although the disease is rare, its severe impact on children and adolescents makes it a public health concern [5]. Brazilian data from 2024 show incidence rate of 2.76/100,000 inhabitants in children under 1 year of age, versus an incidence of 0.38/100,000 inhabitants considering all age groups in the same period [10].

The distribution of serogroups that contribute to IMD epidemiology varies globally, shaped by geographic location and vaccination strategies. In Brazil, MenB has become the leading cause of IMD, overtaking MenC in several regions. In 2024, MenB accounted for 55.9% of IMD cases, increasing to 63% in 2025. In infants aged < 1 year, MenB was responsible for 73% of cases in 2024, increasing to 79% in 2025, with an incidence rate of 1.23 cases per 100,000, more than nine-fold higher than in the general population over all age groups [10].

While the global average case fatality rate for IMD is approximately 10%, Brazil consistently reports higher rates, with an overall case fatality rate of 22% and 28.4% in infants in 2024. MenB case fatality rates have exhibited a concerning upward trend, increasing from 11.1% in 2020 to 25% in 2023, with further increases observed during outbreaks [11]. In addition, underreporting remains a major challenge, as diagnostic limitations, such as the use of antibiotics prior to specimen collection, often limit pathogen identification and result in cases being misclassified as meningitis of unknown origin [11].

Vaccines to prevent IMD are available and have been incorporated into national immunization programs (NIPs) in many countries [12, 13]. The introduction of the meningococcal serogroup C conjugate vaccine (MenC vaccine) into Brazil’s NIP in 2010 has led to a significant reduction in serogroup C IMD, particularly among children under 5 years of age [14]. However, despite these successes, the proportion of IMD cases caused by serogroup B has been increasing. Data from 2010 to 2020 indicate that while serogroup C initially remained predominant, its proportion decreased from 80% in 2010 to 47% in 2020 [15].

To date, the four-component meningitis B vaccine (4CMenB; Bexsero, GSK) has only been implemented in Brazil in the private sector; in 2015, it received marketing authorization for active immunization against MenB in individuals aged 2 months to 50 years [16]. Evidence from real-world use in the UK and local prescribing information in Brazil suggest 4CMenB also provides cross-protection against serogroup W [16]. Furthermore, the vaccine has been prioritized for high-risk populations and in response to outbreaks [17, 18].

The objective of this modeling study was to estimate the potential public health impact of introducing routine infant immunization with the 4CMenB vaccine in Brazil, using a 2 + 1 dosing schedule, compared with no MenB vaccination.

Methods

Model

A static multigenerational multicohort model was developed to simulate the epidemiological patterns of IMD caused by serogroups B and W (Fig. 1). This static model was developed using a discrete-time Markov approach. The model includes a starting cohort aged 0 to 99 years based on population data for Brazil [19]. Each year, a new birth cohort enters the model, and existing individuals age until natural mortality or the end of the analysis period. The model captures demographic changes and incorporates vaccine effectiveness, coverage, and waning rates to estimate the decrease in IMD incidence under the evaluated vaccination strategy. Annual IMD-related deaths are calculated from age-specific case fatality rates.

Target Population

The population eligible for vaccination is the birth cohort of each year. Infants aged < 1 year exhibit a significant peak in IMD incidence in Brazil, making them a high-priority group for vaccination [20].

Intervention and Comparator

The intervention we evaluated was 4CMenB vaccination in infants. This vaccine is indicated for active immunization against IMD caused by N. meningitidis serogroup B, with potential cross-protection against serogroup W [16, 21]. MenB vaccination in infants with a 2 + 1 4CMenB schedule (at 3, 5, and 12 months of age) was compared with no vaccination [19].

Time Horizon

A 100-year time horizon was used to evaluate the long-term impacts of the vaccination strategy, in alignment with Brazil’s National Committee for Health Technology Incorporation (CONITEC) guidelines. CONITEC recommends using patients’ life expectancy as the preferred time horizon for chronic conditions or diseases with long-term outcomes, unless a different duration is clearly justified [22].

Demographic and Epidemiological Data

The model incorporates age-specific population data and mortality rates from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics [19]. Annual incidence rates of IMD by age group and serogroup are based on data from the Sistema de Informação de Agravos de Notificação, averaged from 2007 to 2023, excluding the COVID-19 pandemic years 2020–2022 [10]. The pandemic years were excluded from the calculation of the average incidence of invasive meningococcal disease in Brazil as the incidence during this period does not accurately reflect the usual epidemiological patterns in the country. This deviation is primarily attributed to the implementation of non-pharmacological measures, such as social distancing, lockdowns, and reduced mobility, which significantly impacted the transmission dynamics and reporting of infectious diseases. The age groups analyzed included infants aged < 1 year, toddlers aged 1–4 years (individually by year and as a combined group), children (5–14 years), adolescents (15–19 years), and adults (≥ 20 years). Annual incidence was calculated by dividing the number of cases by the population size and multiplying by 100,000. In the absence of vaccination, MenB and MenW incidences were assumed to remain constant (Table 1).

IMD-related mortality was determined using the case fatality rate (CFR), which represents the probability of death from acute IMD as a percentage of individuals with IMD, varying by age. It was assumed that deaths from IMD occur within the same calendar year as the corresponding acute cases. The model also assumes no excess mortality among survivors of acute IMD who develop life-long sequelae [27].

Adjustments were made for underreporting. In the SINAN system, bacterial meningitis is classified by etiology: meningococcal meningitis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, other bacteria, and undetermined etiology. In the publicly available database, cases due to “other bacteria” and those of “undetermined etiology” are aggregated and cannot be separated. Following Itria [28], we assumed that the proportion of cases for which etiology has been determined is similar in all regions to that of São Paulo, which has better reporting than most regions.

Based on this, the underreporting was estimated to be 83.4%.

Additionally, even in cases where N. meningitidis is identified as the causative pathogen, the specific serogroup may not always be reported. This represents an additional factor contributing to the underreporting of N. meningitidis serogroup B (MenB) cases within the country. Therefore, as the serogroup was not specified for some reported cases (approximately 50.0% of cases per year), these cases were distributed among serogroups, assuming they had the same serogroup distribution as the data regarding cases for which serogroup information was available.

Vaccination Parameters

The model requires vaccine effectiveness estimates for all targeted N. meningitidis serogroup strains, and for each dose in the intervention strategy. Key inputs include effectiveness against a specific serogroup, vaccination coverage per dose, and the average duration of protection.

The model incorporated dose-specific estimates for 4CMenB vaccine effectiveness against IMD caused by MenB, based on real-world evidence from the implementation of MenB vaccination in the UK. Effectiveness estimates were 33.5% (95% BCI 12.4–49.7) after dose 1, 78.7% (95% BCI 71.5–84.5) after dose 2, and 80.1% (95% BCI 70.3–86.7) after the full three-dose course [23].

4CMenB includes antigens that are common to multiple meningococcal serogroups, rather than being unique to MenB. It is anticipated that 4CMenB provides some degree of cross-protection against non-B serogroups. In the model, cross-protection was incorporated by applying the estimated efficacy of the MenB vaccine against non-B serogroups. For the present analysis, cross-protection against MenW after the complete schedule was taken into account, with a 69.0% reduction in MenW cases (95% CI 33.5–78.8) [21], based on real-world evidence from UK and consistent with 4CMenB prescribing information in Brazil [16] (Table 1).

Vaccination coverage for dose 1 and dose 2 was assumed to be 87.9%, and 82.8% for dose 3, based on the average MenC vaccine coverage for the previous 10 years (2013–2022) in Brazil [26] (Table 1).

Vaccine waning was modeled as an exponential decay equal to the inverse of the average duration of protection (41 months). This method aligns with the standard practice for modeling vaccine waning in economic evaluations of MenB vaccination [29–33].

Public Health Impact Outcomes

The numbers of IMD cases and deaths avoided were calculated for the total population and stratified by age group, with stratifications for each year of age within the 1–4 years age group and by serogroup.

Utility

Age-specific utilities were used (1) to recalibrate age-independent IMD-associated utility reductions toward the age-dependent baseline utilities and (2) to calculate, combined with the natural mortality data, the quality-adjusted life expectancy foregone at the age of premature death caused by acute IMD. In the calendar year when a patient experiences an acute case of IMD, utility losses are assigned to survivors based on whether they develop sequelae. Utility reductions for acute disease with sequela were applied beyond the sequela-related utility losses in the first year (Table 2).

Quality-Adjusted Life Year (QALY) Losses

To estimate health outcomes, the model includes the average age-specific utility of the general population. It also incorporates short-term adjustments to baseline utilities in cases of acute IMD and long-term adjustment to baseline utilities in patients with life-long IMD sequelae. The wider impact that IMD can have on family members and caregivers, such as emotional or psychological distress, was taken into account by a multiplicative factor of 1.5 based on cross-sectional survey of 1218 family networks affected by meningitis [39]. A 5.0% discount rate was assumed, based on health economic guidelines for Brazil [43].

Results

Impact of Vaccination on IMD Cases

The model estimated that vaccination with 4CMenB would prevent 63,038 IMD cases caused by serogroups B and W across all age groups, leading to an overall reduction of 21.2% (Fig. 2a). This prediction was based on a total of 212,583,750 individuals entering the model and 637,880,063 vaccine doses administered in the vaccinated cohort.

Reduction in IMD cases due to 4CMenB vaccination, over a 100-year time horizon. a In all age groups, b against MenB serogroup, and c against MenW serogroup, among infants and toddlers aged ≤ 4 years. *We assumed MenW protection only after the three-dose course (with the third dose at 12 months of age). 4CMenB four-component MenB vaccine, MenB N. meningitidis serogroup B, MenW N. meningitidis serogroup W, IMD invasive meningococcal disease

The greatest impact of 4CMenB against serogroup B was observed among children aged 1 year (60% reduction) (Fig. 2b). In infants aged < 1 year, the reduction was 43.1% (n = 21,408). When aggregating the data for toddlers aged 1–4 years, the reduction was 47.2% (n = 29,850) (Supplementary Fig. S1).

IMD cases due to MenW were predicted to decrease in the vaccinated cohort vs. no vaccination due to cross-protection by 4CMenB against serogroup W (Fig. 2c). MenW cases in the 1–4-year age group would be reduced by 41.6% (n = 5612) due to cross-protection from the 4CMenB vaccine. As we assumed cross-protection against MenW only after completion of the full three-dose schedule at 12 months of age, no impact on MenW cases in the < 1-year age group was observed in our model. The impact of 4CMenB on reducing cases of MenW disease was greatest among children aged 1 year (51.6% reduction; n = 2667; Fig. 2c).

In age groups older than 4 years (children, adolescents, and adults), the model also projected a decline in IMD cases, although the reduction was less pronounced than that observed in infants (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Impact of Vaccination on IMD-Related Deaths

The model also predicted the impact of 4CMenB vaccination on IMD-related deaths. The number of IMD-related deaths across all age groups was predicted to be reduced by 20% when routine vaccination was in place, compared with no vaccination (Fig. 3a).

a Reduction in IMD-related deaths due to 4CMenB vaccination, over a 100-year time horizon, b against MenB serogroup, and c against MenW serogroup, among infants and toddlers aged ≤ 4 years. *We assumed MenW protection only after the three-dose course (with the third dose at 12 months of age). 4CMenB four-component MenB vaccine, MenB N. meningitidis serogroup B, MenW N. meningitidis serogroup W, IMD invasive meningococcal disease

4CMenB had the greatest impact on MenB-related deaths among children aged 1 year (59.9%; n = 2752). In infants aged < 1 year, the reduction was 43.1% (n = 3335) (Fig. 3b). When aggregating the data for toddlers aged 1–4 years, the reduction was 47.2% (n = 5970) (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Protection against death caused by serogroup W infection was also considered, with the most prominent reduction in MenW-related deaths (51.6%; n = 505) observed in the 1-year age group, followed by 41.6% (n = 306), 33.6% (n = 119), and 27.2% (n = 132) among children aged 2, 3, and 4 years, respectively (Fig. 3c).

Again, a similar pattern was observed for IMD-related deaths, with projected reductions in individuals aged more than 4 years being smaller than those estimated for younger children (Supplementary Fig. S2).

QALY Losses

According to the model, the 4CMenB infant vaccination program in Brazil can result in a gain of 138,737 QALYs compared to no vaccination, by avoiding cases of devastating health conditions caused by acute IMD, including life-long sequelae and death.

Discussion

IMD caused by MenB is a rare but serious illness with potentially life-threatening outcomes, particularly among infants. MenB IMD remains an unmet medical need in Brazil. To assess the potential public health impact in Brazil of routine infant immunization with the 4CMenB vaccine, a static model was developed to explore the effect of a 2 + 1 dosing schedule at 3, 5, and 12 months of age [16].

The modeling study estimated that introducing routine infant immunization with the 4CMenB vaccine in Brazil using a 2 + 1 schedule would significantly reduce the burden of IMD, particularly IMD due to serogroups B and W. Over a 100-year time horizon, vaccination was projected to prevent approximately 63,000 IMD cases and reduce IMD-related deaths by 20.1%. The greatest benefit was observed among infants and young children, in whom disease incidence and fatality rates are highest. Specifically, MenB-related IMD cases and deaths were predicted to decrease by 43–60% in children aged 0–4 years. Cross-protection against serogroup W further contributed to a notable reduction in cases and deaths in young children. The vaccination program was also associated with a gain of nearly 139,000 QALYs, demonstrating substantial public health benefit from preventing acute illness, long-term sequelae, and premature death.

4CMenB is licensed for various age groups in more than 50 countries worldwide, including Australia, Brazil, Canada, Chile, Uruguay, the USA, and several countries in Europe [44]. In 2015, the vaccine was licensed in Brazil [45]. In Latin America and the Caribbean, 4CMenB has been introduced in NIPs. Chile began routine vaccination in July 2023, with doses administered at 2, 4, and 18 months of age [46–48].

This study is among the first to assess the potential impact of routine 4CMenB vaccination in Brazil, specifically focusing on its implications for public health. A static multigenerational multicohort model using a discrete-time Markov approach was used to simulate the epidemiological dynamics of IMD caused by serogroups B and W in Brazil. A key strength of the model lies in its capacity to project long-term public health outcomes across multiple birth cohorts, offering a structured assessment of the potential impact of introducing 4CMenB routine vaccination. Importantly, vaccine-specific parameters, such as its effectiveness against MenB IMD and the average duration of protection, were informed by robust real-world evidence from post-implementation studies in countries where 4CMenB has been introduced into the national immunization program. While this model does not capture indirect effects, such as herd protection, this limitation is less critical in the context of a meningococcal protein vaccine such as 4CMenB, which is primarily characterized by direct protection rather than by an effect on transmission.

The model used IMD incidence data averaged over the 2007–2023 period (excluding the COVID-19 pandemic years). However, the incidence of IMD is unpredictable and subject to regional and temporal fluctuations, so a considerable degree of uncertainty remains in estimating the disease burden. To address limitations in surveillance data quality, an underreporting rate of 83.4% was applied. This adjustment accounts for persistent challenges in detecting and reporting IMD cases in Brazil. Specifically, difficulties in collecting diagnostic material and the frequent use of antibiotics before specimen collection often prevent identification of the causative agent. Consequently, many cases are classified as meningitis of unknown origin, contributing to an underestimation of the true burden of IMD, despite it being a notifiable disease. Regarding disease burden metrics, QALYs were selected as the primary outcome measure in this analysis because they capture both survival and long-term health-related quality-of-life impacts through the use of health state utilities. This approach allows a more sensitive representation of the severe and lifelong sequelae associated with IMD. While disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) are widely used for population-level burden assessments, they rely on standardized disability weights that may not fully reflect the heterogeneity and long-term consequences of IMD [49]. DALYs are therefore acknowledged as a complementary metric that could be explored in future analyses.

While adolescents represent an additional population at increased risk of invasive meningococcal disease and play an important role in meningococcal epidemiology, including a contribution to transmission and requiring targeted vaccination strategies (ACWY and B) to reduce transmission [50, 51], the present analysis was intentionally focused on infants. This reflects the current epidemiological situation in Brazil, where infants experience the highest incidence and case fatality rates and therefore remain the primary public health priority for prevention. Nonetheless, adolescent vaccination represents an important complementary strategy for population-level IMD control. In this context, MenABCWY vaccination was not currently assessed in the model, as such vaccines are not currently included in Brazil’s public health immunization program. However, we recognize the potential future utility of MenABCWY vaccines and this could be a possible pathway for broader adolescent protection should national policy evolve.

Importantly, 4CMenB vaccination is primarily characterized by direct protection of the vaccinated individual, as protein-based MenB vaccines have not been shown to reliably reduce nasopharyngeal carriage or interrupt transmission. Therefore, exclusion of carriage and herd effects from our model was intentional and scientifically justified. The absence of herd protection does not underestimate the impact of 4CMenB itself; rather, it reflects the biological distinction between protein-based MenB vaccines and conjugate MenACWY vaccines, which can reduce carriage and contribute to indirect protection.

In light of the limited evidence that 4CMenB reduces meningococcal carriage and transmission, additional strategies to ensure direct protection of young children warrant consideration [52]. In particular, a catch-up vaccination program for children older than 12 months could provide direct protection to the 1–4-year age group, who otherwise benefit more slowly from an infant-only strategy. As catch-up vaccination was not simulated in the present model, the projected near-term impact in this age group is likely conservative. Furthermore, national surveillance data indicate continued circulation of both serogroups B and W in Brazil, with MenW contributing meaningfully to IMD burden in young children [11]. Within this epidemiological context, incorporation of a quadrivalent conjugate MenACWY vaccine in infancy could help address the residual MenW disease burden observed in children under 12 months and complement protection provided by 4CMenB [50]. Consistent with approved prescribing information, cross-protection against MenW from 4CMenB was assumed only after completion of the full vaccination schedule, which contributes to the early-life MenW gap observed in the model outputs.

This study had some limitations. The model did not assume any carriage or transmission protection from 4CMenB. Additionally, deaths with no specified cause may also be linked to IMD, particularly in instances where individuals face barriers to accessing medical care and postmortem investigations are not conducted. Despite being a notifiable disease, these gaps in reporting and diagnosis contribute to the underestimation of the true impact of IMD in Brazil. Some specific input data were not available for the Brazilian context, necessitating the use of data from other countries, such as estimates of utility losses due to IMD sequelae. Vaccine effectiveness and waning against serogroup W were extrapolated based on available data for MenB vaccines, using estimates consistent with the approved national product label and real-world evidence. This reliance on external data introduces uncertainty regarding the transferability and applicability of these parameters to the Brazilian population. The estimated impact of 4CMenB in this study may be conservative. A cross-protection level of 69.0% against MenW following the three-dose schedule was applied, based on the available evidence and consistent with the approved national product label [16]. However, this may underestimate the broader effect of the vaccine. Emerging data suggest that 4CMenB may confer partial protection against additional serogroups beyond B and W, including C and Y, through shared subcapsular antigens. These broader immunological effects have been documented in immunogenicity and observational studies, but were not fully accounted for in the present model. As a result, the overall public health benefit of incorporating 4CMenB into the infant immunization schedule may be greater than projected, particularly in settings where multiple serogroups co-circulate [21, 53, 54].

Finally, while the model provides useful projections, the absence of formal sensitivity analyses limits the exploration of how uncertainty in key parameters might influence the results. Conducting sensitivity analyses could offer important insights into the robustness of the findings, helping to identify which assumptions most affect the projected impact and thereby enhancing confidence in the conclusions drawn from the model.

The inclusion of 4CMenB in national programs highlights the importance of considering decision-making factors. These include the burden and severity of disease, societal impact, and the need for rapid response to outbreaks or transmission clusters [55]. Such contextual and strategic considerations have also been emphasized in previous vaccine assessments.

Conclusion

This modeling study indicates that introducing the 4CMenB vaccine in infants in Brazil could substantially reduce the burden of IMD, reducing both morbidity and mortality. These findings offer valuable insights for decision-makers, highlighting the potential public health benefits of incorporating 4CMenB vaccination into the NIP for infants in Brazil.
