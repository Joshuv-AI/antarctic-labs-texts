Anticancer effects of folic acid-functionalized covalent organic framework containing doxorubicin on SW480 colon cancer cells: a promising tool for drug targeted delivery
Authors: Razie Ezati, Behrooz Johari, Jaber Yousefi Seyf, Yousef Mortazavi, Mehdi Azizi, Hadi Samadian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Colorectal cancer is one of the deadliest forms of gastrointestinal cancer, with conventional treatments often facing significant limitations. As a result, new approaches, particularly in targeted drug delivery, have shown great promise. In this study, the COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier was developed, where covalent organic frameworks (COFs) were functionalized with folic acid (FA) and then loaded with Doxorubicin (DOX). The as-synthesized COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier was characterized using different techniques. To assess its anticancer effectiveness, MTT, flow cytometry, and scratch assays were conducted on SW480 and HUVEC cells to examine cell viability, cellular uptake, cell cycle progression, apoptosis, and cell migration, respectively. The obtained results demonstrated that the COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier was efficiently internalized by cancer cells and showed significantly higher cytotoxicity compared to other synthesized nanocarrier groups and free DOX drug. Moreover, the COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier caused cell cycle arrest, induced apoptosis, and inhibited cell migration at lower doses than the free DOX drug. Altogether, these findings suggest that the COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier is an effective and promising drug delivery system for DOX in colorectal cancer, potentially enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of DOX drug while minimizing side effects through targeted delivery. Further investigation is required to assess their efficacy in vivo and discover potential clinical applications.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent types of cancer, and statistics indicate that it is the second deadliest cancer globally, with a higher incidence in developed countries. CRC usually develops from polyps, with mutations converting adenomatous polyps into adenocarcinomas over time. These mutations impact proto-oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes, and delayed diagnosis can result in advanced, hard-to-treat stages and a poor prognosis [1–3]. Conventional treatments for cancer, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy, face several challenges. Although they can be effective, they have limitations. Chemotherapy, for instance, often harms healthy cells and can result in drug resistance, which may lead to treatment failure [4, 5].

Doxorubicin (DOX) effectively treats various cancers, including CRC [6]. It can be used alone or in combination with other drugs, and it works through mechanisms such as damaging the DNA of cancer cells, interfering with cellular respiration, and inducing apoptosis, leading to effective tumor suppression [7]. However, despite its effectiveness, side effects such as digestive disorders, hematopoietic interference, and cardiac toxicity limit its use [8, 9]. Extensive research has been conducted to improve the targeted delivery of this drug to cancer cells while reducing its toxicity to healthy cells [10].

Covalent Organic Frameworks (COFs) are emerging as promising candidates for developing therapeutic platforms based on DOX drug [11, 12]. Recent advancements in drug delivery have showcased the potential of nanostructures derived from COFs. While COFs have been around since 2005, it’s only in the past decade that researchers have begun to investigate their applications in biomedicine [13]. These structures are particularly well-suited for targeted drug delivery in cancer treatment due to their highly porous and crystalline structure, which provides a large surface area and high porosity [14]. Their design can be easily modified and functionalized, and they demonstrate excellent chemical and thermal stability, making them a promising new platform for the drug delivery of anticancer agents [15]. For example, Liu et al. introduced a DOX-loaded COF structure using a simple one-pot synthesis, which showed promising release behavior and therapeutic potential [16]. Another study by Wang et al. demonstrated a fluorescent COF capable of releasing its drug payload in response to environmental stimuli [12]. Moreover, Benyettou et al. developed a COF system that included magnetic nanoparticles, allowing it to be used not only for drug delivery but also for MRI imaging and heat-based cancer treatment. This study shows how COFs can be adapted for multiple roles in cancer therapy [17]. Although the therapeutic potential of COFs has been well demonstrated, their application in targeted drug delivery-especially using folic acid as a guiding ligand-remains underexplored.

Folic acid has gained considerable attention as a targeting molecule because it binds strongly to folate receptors, which are present in high numbers on the surface of many cancer cells, including those in colorectal tumors. This specific interaction allows folic acid-modified nanocarriers to get into cancer cells more easily, reducing their uptake by healthy tissues. While folic acid has been widely applied in polymer- and lipid-based drug delivery systems [18, 19] due to its strong affinity for folate receptors on cancer cells, its conjugation with covalent organic frameworks for targeted therapy has been comparatively rare and is still an emerging area of research.

In this study, a COF-based targeted structure was developed for CRC. The COF was functionalized with folic acid to target the overexpressed folate receptors on the surface of cancer cells. After loading the folic acid functionalized COF with DOX, its effectiveness was evaluated in SW480 cancer cells and normal HUVEC cells. To achieve an effective delivery system, it was crucial for the structure to enter cancer cells and disintegrate in the acidic microenvironment of endosomes and lysosomes, releasing DOX inside the cells while preventing drug release at the physiological pH of the body. To accomplish this, a COF with an imine bond was synthesized with 2,6-diformylpyridine (DFP) and 1,3,5-tris (4-aminophenyl) benzene (TAPB). The resulting COF was then functionalized with folic acid, using EDC/NHS conjugation chemistry. Therefore, this study introduces a novel and straightforward approach to the delivery of DOX using a folic acid functionalized COF. To the best of our knowledge, the folic acid-conjugated COF structure has not been previously utilized for DOX drug delivery in colorectal cancer (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

Folic acid was purchased from Merck. DFP and TAPB were obtained from TCI in 100 mg quantities. Doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX·HCl) was kindly provided by Actoverco Pharmaceuticals. Fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA), Bovine serum albumin (BSA) (A4612), DMEM high glucose (Gibco, USA), EDC (25952-53-8), NHS (24500), Penicillin-streptomycin (P4333), MTT (57360-69-7), and trypsin-EDTA (T3924) were provided by Sigma-Aldrich Co. (USA). DMSO, and acetone were purchased from Emertat Chimi (Iran). Annexin-PI kit was purchased from Sigma (USA). PBS was prepared in the laboratory. All other chemicals and solvents were of the highest commercial grade. The SW480 (CCL-228) and HUVEC (CRL-1730) cell lines were procured from the Pasteur Institute of Tehran, Iran.

Covalent organic framework (COF) synthesis

To synthesize the COF, 2,6-diformylpyridine (DFP, 36 mg, 0.27 mmol, 9 equivalents) and 1,3,5-tris(4-aminophenyl) benzene (TAPB, 63.24 mg, 0.18 mmol, 6 equivalents) were dissolved in 9 mL of 1,4-dioxane. A catalytic amount of acetic acid was added to initiate the reaction [17]. The reaction mixture was heated to 100 °C in an oil bath and refluxed under vigorous stirring for 1 h. The obtained COF was filtered and washed several times with 1,4-dioxane, water, and acetone, and the presence of DFP and TAPB was checked with a UV spectrophotometer. Finally, the resulting yellow COF powder was dried in a vacuum oven at 70 °C for 24 h.

Conjugation of COFs with folic acid (FA)

To conjugate the COFs with FA, 5 mg of folic acid was dissolved in 50 mL of water and sonicated for 20 min. Subsequently, 5 mg of EDC (1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride) and NHS (N-hydroxysuccinimide) were dissolved separately in 5 mL of water and added to the folic acid solution, followed by sonication for an additional 20 min [19]. Subsequently, 50 mg of COF powder was dispersed in 20 mL of water and sonicated for 20 min. The COF solution was then added to the folic acid-EDC/NHS mixture and stirred at 700 rpm for 24 h at room temperature, protected from light. The resulting mixture was filtered, washed with 0.3 M sodium carbonate solution, ethanol, and water, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 50 °C to obtain the COF-FA conjugate (COF-FA nanocarrier).

Loading doxorubicin (DOX) into the COF-FA conjugate

To increase the loading efficiency of Doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX·HCl) into the COF-FA nanocarrier, the former was first converted into its free base form. Fifteen mg of DOX·HCl was dissolved in water, and then triethylamine (TEA) was added in order to cause a color change (to convert DOX.HCl into DOX). Dichloromethane (DCM) was added to the resulting solution and poured into a separatory funnel to cause the formation of bilayers [20]. The lower DCM phase containing the free base of DOX was separated, and DCM was exchanged with DI water. The solution was adjusted to a neutral pH of 7.0. To synthesize the COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier, the DOX solution was added to the COF-FA nanocarrier in a one-to-four ratio. The mixture was then adjusted to a neutral pH and shaken for 72 h to induce COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier formation. The DOX-loaded COF was then separated by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The collected product was washed thoroughly three times with PBS solution to remove any adsorbed DOX on the nanocarrier. To assess the extent of DOX loading, the supernatant from the centrifuged solution was carefully collected and analyzed with a UV-vis spectrophotometer. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at the specific wavelength of DOX (480 nm), corresponding to its characteristic absorbance peak. A DOX calibration curve was generated at a wavelength of 480 nm to calculate the loading efficiency (LE) and loading capacity (LC). The Drug Loading Efficiency (DLE, %) and Drug Loading Capacity (DLC) were calculated by comparing the amount of drug loaded in the nanocarrier with a standard calibration curve. Characterization of the synthesized nanocarrier

The synthesized COF in the current study was characterized using various analytical techniques. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted using a Perkin-Elmer Spectrum RXI FT-IR spectrometer, employing the KBr tablet method. The morphology of the samples was examined using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopes with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FE-SEM/EDX) with a TESCAN MIRA III instrument and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM Philips EM 208 S). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms and the corresponding pore-size distribution were analyzed with a BET BELSORP Mini II analyzer at 77 K. The specific surface area was calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. To assess the crystallinity of the samples, powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis was performed using a Philips PW1730. The chemical composition of the COF was also investigated by XPS BESTEC (EA 10) measurement. UV-vis spectra were measured using an X AvaSpec-2048 TEC spectrometer. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were conducted with a Horiba SZ-100 to determine the ζ-potential of the nanoparticle.

In vitro COF stability assay

The stability of the COF nanocarrier was assessed under various pH conditions to evaluate its degradation and long-term stability. The COF was incubated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 to simulate physiological conditions. In addition, the stability of the COF was tested under acidic conditions by incubating it in PBS at pH 5.0 (the pH of the solutions was adjusted using a 1 M HCl solution) to mimic the acidic environment of cancer cells or other acidic microenvironments. For the pH stability tests, samples of the COF were prepared and incubated at pH 5, 6, and 7.4 for a defined period. UV-visible spectroscopy was used to monitor any changes in the COF’s absorption profile, which could indicate degradation or structural instability over time. To evaluate the stability over an extended period, the COF was incubated at pH 7.4 for up to 1, 3, and 6 months, with UV-visible spectra recorded at regular intervals. These measurements allowed for assessing any gradual COF stability changes and potential degradation over time.

DOX drug release assay

To investigate the pH-responsive drug release behavior of DOX from the COF-FA@DOX, a specific amount of this nanocarrier was dispersed in 10 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solutions at pH 7.4, which simulates physiological conditions, and at pH 5 and 6, representing the acidic environment of cancer cell lysosomes. The samples were incubated at a constant temperature of 37 °C to mimic the human body’s temperature. The release of DOX was monitored over time by withdrawing 1 mL aliquots of the release medium at predetermined intervals (0, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54, 64, 70, and 80 h). Each time, the sampled volume was immediately replaced with an equal amount of fresh PBS buffer to maintain constant volume and ensure continuous drug release. The released drug was quantified using UV-vis spectroscopy. The cumulative drug release was calculated by comparing the amount of DOX in the supernatant to the initial drug loading, allowing for the assessment of the release profile at both physiological and acidic pH conditions.

Hemocompatibility assay

A total of 2 mL of human blood was obtained from a healthy donor and collected in tubes coated with EDTA to prevent coagulation [21]. The blood was then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to separate the plasma, which was discarded. The remaining red blood cell (RBC) pellet was washed by adding phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by pipetting to resuspend the cells. After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and this washing procedure was repeated five times to remove plasma components and other contaminants thoroughly. Following the final wash, 20 mL of PBS was added to the RBC pellet, and the cells were resuspended by pipetting. For sample preparation, various concentrations of the test compound (1, 5, 15, 20, 30, 50, 100, and 250 µg/mL) were prepared in PBS. A total of 0.8 mL of each sample was mixed with 0.2 mL of the washed RBC solution in separate tubes. Negative control (PBS) and positive control (Triton X-100) were also prepared by mixing the respective solutions with the blood. All tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to allow interaction between the blood cells and the test compounds. Following incubation, the tubes were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min, and 100 µL of the supernatant from each tube was carefully transferred into the wells of a 96-well plate. Hemolysis was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 540 nm using an ELISA reader. All tests were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Cell culture procedure

Both cell lines (SW480 and HUVEC) were cultured in DMEM/high glucose medium containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Cultures were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Cell viability assay

To evaluate the cell viability percentage after various treatments using the MTT test, SW480 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h (37 °C and 5% CO2). Then, the cells were treated with nanocarriers (COF, COF@DOX, COF-FA@DOX, and the DOX drug) at different concentrations of 0.5, 2.5, 7.5, 10, and 15 µg/mL. 24 and 48 h post-treatment, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for approximately 4 hours. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO (150 µL per well), and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate ELISA reader. Finally, the IC50 of the samples was determined [22].

Nanocarrier cellular uptake assay

The cell lines with different expressions of folate receptors, such as SW480 (high expression) and HUVEC (low expression), were seeded in a separate 12-well plate with 500 µL complete medium and were allowed to grow overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The SW480 cell groups with no treatment (Ctrl) and the cell groups treated with 500 µL of optimum medium containing COF (15 µg/mL) were used as a negative control. Cell groups were treated with COF@DOX (15 µg/mL) and COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier (2.5, 10, 15 µg/mL based on DOX drug) as a positive control. The HUVEC cell group was treated with 500 µL of optimum medium containing 15 µg/mL of COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier. 24 h post-treatment, the cells were washed with PBS solution, then trypsinized and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min. The cells were suspended in 500 µL of PBS solution, and flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD Biosciences flow cytometer (San Jose, CA, USA), and the data were analyzed using FlowJo v7 software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA) [23].

Cell cycle assay

SW480 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with COF, COF@DOX, COF-FA@DOX, and DOX drug at a 7.5 µg/mL concentration. 48 h post-treatment, the treated cell groups were trypsinized and centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 3 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 50 µL of PBS, and the cells were fixed using 70% ethanol. The fixed cells were centrifuged to discard the ethanol. Following, the cells were treated with 1 mL of propidium iodide (PI) master mix solution containing 40 µL of PI, 10 µL of RNase, and 950 µL of PBS and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Finally, data were read using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), and the results were analyzed using FlowJo v.7 software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR). The data obtained are reported as the percentage of the cell population in each phase of the cell cycle [24].

Apoptosis assay

SW480 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 6 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. Then, the cells were treated with COF, COF@DOX, COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers, and DOX drug at a concentration of 7.5 µg/mL. 48 h post-treatment, the treated cell groups were harvested and washed with PBS. Following, the cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Finally, data were read using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), and the results were analyzed with FlowJo v.7 software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR) [24].

Scratch assay

To investigate the ability to inhibit cell migration (scratch repair), SW480 cells were cultured in a 12-well plate at a density of 4 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h. When the cells reached a confluence of 70%, a scratch was made on the cells in a specific area of the bottom of the plate using a sterile tip. The supernatant, along with the detached cells, was discarded. The cells were treated with various nanocarriers, including COF, COF@DOX, COF-FA@DOX, and the DOX drug at a 2.5 µg/mL treatment concentration. Finally, microscopic images of the scratched area were captured at 0 to 96 h and analyzed using Image J (v1.52; NIH) software [25]. Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied using GraphPad Prism version 6 to conduct statistical analysis. All values are presented as mean ± SD. Significance levels were defined as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Each assay was repeated at least three times.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of COF nanocarrier

COF was formed from the relatively straightforward reaction between DFP and TAPB. In this reaction, a characteristic yellow substance was rapidly formed in the test tube, which had almost the same appearance and properties until the end of the reaction. However, as the reaction continued, changes occurred in the level of chemical bonds. Therefore, refluxing for 1 h was necessary to ensure the formation of imine bonds.

The Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra of the starting materials (TAPB and DFP) and the final COF confirmed the successful synthesis of the imine-linked structure. As shown in Fig. 2A, the spectrum of TAPB, the presence of N-H stretching vibrations was evident in the range of 3200–3400 cm− 1, while C = C stretching vibrations in aromatic rings appeared between 1400 and 1650 cm− 1, also 3442, 3360, 1620, 1593, 1516, 1283, and 827 wavenumbers are attributed to the TAPB [26, 27]. In the spectrum of DFP, a broad peak at 1714 cm− 1 indicated the presence of C = O stretching vibrations, while aromatic C-H stretching was observed at 2800–3100 cm− 1 [28]. The FT-IR spectrum of the final COF revealed the formation of a strong characteristic peak for C = N bonds at 1606 cm− 1, confirming the successful condensation reaction between TAPB and DFP. Furthermore, the disappearance of the C = O stretching peak (~ 1714 cm− 1) and N-H stretching peaks (3200–3400 cm− 1) from the starting materials confirms the efficient formation of the COF structure. These findings demonstrate the efficiency of the condensation reaction in creating a stable, porous imine-linked COF framework [17, 28].

The crystalline structures of the COF were investigated using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis. The XRD pattern of the synthesized COF sample is shown in Fig. 2B. The diffraction peaks observed at 2θ = 4° (110 plane) and 2θ = 24° (003 plane) confirm the crystalline nature of the COF material. The peak at 4° corresponds to the (110) reflection, characteristic of the ordered framework structure [29]. This low-angle reflection indicates the presence of a well-organized porous network with a regular lattice structure. Additionally, the peak at 24° is attributed to the (003) plane, which arises from π-π stacking interactions between adjacent layers. This peak provides evidence of the layered structure and strong interlayer interactions, which are essential for maintaining the stability and functionality of the COF [17].

The effectiveness of nanocarriers in drug delivery applications is highly influenced by their porosity and surface area. In Fig. 2C, D, the adsorption and desorption isotherms of nitrogen gas and the pore size distribution are shown, respectively. The synthesized COF exhibits type II isotherms with Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas of 765.6 m²/g and narrow pore size distributions, featuring an average pore width of 1.8 nm and a calculated pore volume of 0.621 cm³/g. The type II isotherm observed in nitrogen adsorption-desorption analysis is characteristic of mesoporous materials, confirming the formation of a porous structure in COF [30]. The high surface area and significant pore volume further emphasize the porous nature of the materials, which is an important feature for potential applications such as adsorption, separation, and catalysis.

The chemical composition of COF was further analyzed using XPS measurements. As shown in Fig. 3A, there are obvious peaks at 285, 400, and 532 electron volts (eV), which represent C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s, respectively, and prove that Carbon (C), Oxygen (O), and Nitrogen (N) elements are present in the structure and ultimately prove the formation of COF. The element percentage analysis showed that C, with 88.80%, has the highest amount in the COF structure. Also, O 6.82% and N 4.44% constitute the COF elements. Figure 3B, C, and D show the high-resolution spectra of the sample in a deconvoluted form. XPS analysis provides valuable data on COF’s elemental composition and chemical state. The significant carbon content in the data is a key feature of COFs, highlighting their organic composition. The observed carbon and nitrogen levels can be explained by the incorporation of imine bonds within the framework and the functional groups originating from the robust residues of the starting materials. This confirms the COF’s successful formation and expected structure [31].

Folic acid (FA) functionalization of COF nanocarrier

The ability to specifically target certain cells significantly impacts the effectiveness of drug delivery. To study the effect of modification, COF was functionalized with FA through the EDC/NHS method, establishing ligand-receptor interactions. With these standard couplings, the bond formation between the carboxylic acid group of folic acid and the amine groups present in COF was easily achieved.

FTIR spectroscopy provides valuable information for evaluating the interaction of folic acid and COF (Fig. 4A). In the COF-Folic acid spectrum, the appearance of folic acid-related carbonyl and amide bonds (1680 cm− 1) [32], along with the broadening of the O-H/N-H stretching region (3000–3500 cm− 1), indicates strong hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between COF and folic acid [33]. Additionally, slight shifts in the imine (C = N) band suggest electronic interactions within the modified structure. These spectral changes confirm the successful incorporation of folic acid, improving COF biocompatibility and targeting ability for biomedical applications.

DOX loading into COF-FA nanocarrier

To explore the potential capabilities of the designed system, the chemotherapeutic agent DOX was successfully incorporated into the COF-FA nanocarrier to achieve COF-FA@DOX. After 72 h of loading, the color change of the COF-FA nanocarrier from yellow to red was clearly visible. This is the first indication of successful drug loading in the COF-FA nanocarrier. The formed COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier was transferred to filter paper and washed several times with lukewarm water until it was ensured that the unloaded DOX was completely washed away. Based on the calculations, the loading efficiency and loading capacity were 96% and 25%, respectively.

Figure 4B shows a strong absorption band in the 3200–3500 cm− 1 range, corresponding to the O-H and N-H stretching vibrations of the hydroxyl and amine groups present in DOX [34]. Additionally, strong bands between 1600 and 1700 cm− 1 indicate the presence of C = O (carbonyl) stretching, a characteristic functional group in the DOX drug [34]. In the COF-FA@DOX spectrum, significant shifts and new absorption bands further confirm the interaction between COF and DOX [12]. The appearance of a distinct peak around 1650 cm− 1, corresponding to C = O stretching, verifies the successful DOX loading. These spectral changes indicate strong intermolecular interactions, likely through hydrogen bonding and π-π interactions, ensuring stable drug incorporation.

The particle size distribution of COF, COF-FA, and COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier, from DLS analysis, is presented in Fig. 4C. The COF shows an average particle size of 151.5 nm. This size is consistent with the size obtained through TEM, and particles of this size are desirable for biomedical research [35]. Functionalization of COF with folic acid has slightly increased the size, and the particle size has reached 171.2 nm. Despite this size increase, the particle size is still in the desired range for biomedical applications, while the functionalization of the particles has affected the zeta potential and increased the stability of the particles. This modification likely prevents aggregation, resulting in a more homogeneously distributed suspension suitable for biomedical applications [35]. In contrast, the incorporation of doxorubicin (DOX) into the COF-FA framework leads to an increased particle size of 193.4 nm, consistent with drug molecules’ successful loading. Also, considering the hydrodynamic diameter, this size is close to that obtained through TEM. This increase in particle size may be due to the high hydrophilicity of DOX, causing agglomeration of the particles. In other words, the formation of hydrogen bonds between the particles as a result of interactions among the different groups in DOX leads to the aggregation of COF-FA@DOX particles, thus forming larger particles with a broader size distribution [36].

The zeta potential analysis reveals significant changes in the surface charge of the COF-based nano platform after functionalization with folic acid and drug loading with doxorubicin. The COF exhibits a zeta potential of -7.94 mV, indicating a slightly negative charge, which is characteristic of its chemical structure (Fig. 4D). Upon modification with folic acid, the zeta potential drastically shifts to -61.10 mV, demonstrating successful conjugation. This drastic change in zeta potential can be attributed to the anionic functional groups present in folic acid, such as carboxyl and hydroxyl groups, which are exposed on the particle surface after conjugation. These negatively charged groups alter the surface charge distribution, leading to a highly negative zeta potential. The high negative charge enhances electrostatic repulsion between the particles, preventing aggregation and improving colloidal stability. Following the loading of DOX, as shown in Fig. 4D, the zeta potential changes to -16.6 mV, suggesting that hydrogen bonding plays a more dominant role than electrostatic interactions. This shift suggests successful drug loading. It should be noted that nanoparticles with neutral and negative zeta potentials are more desirable for systemic applications. These nanoparticles have a lower risk of immune response and are less likely to be off-target al.ong the way [37].

The synthesized COFs were thoroughly characterized, as illustrated in Figs. 5 and 6. The Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) image of COFs demonstrates the formation of uneven slices consisting of tiny, flake-like 2D structures (Fig. 5A, B). A closer analysis of the structures using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals a nanoparticle morphology characterized by layered 2D shapes (Fig. 6A, B) with an average size of approximately 126 nm and 170 nm for COF and COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers, respectively (Fig. 6C, D). In this structure, the darker areas indicate the overlapping and stacking of multiple layers on top of each other. It is important to note that the functionalization of the COF with folic acid and the loading of doxorubicin did not induce any morphological changes in the material (Figs. 5B and 6B).

EDX was employed to determine the elemental composition of the synthesized materials. The EDX analysis was conducted using FESEM. The EDX spectra were collected at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. The acquisition time for each spectrum was set to 60 s to ensure a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio. The elemental analysis was performed over a range of 0–20 keV, allowing for the detection of elements. The results indicated the presence of C, N, and O with corresponding weight percentages of 69.74%, 17.34%, and 12.92%, respectively (Fig. 5C). This analysis provided insights into the homogeneity and composition of the material, which is crucial for understanding its properties and potential applications. The percentage of elements obtained in EDX is almost consistent with the percentage of elements determined in XPS, thus confirming the formation of the COF structure. The EDX was also performed with the aforementioned settings for COF-FA@DOX (Fig. 5D), and based on the data, it is revealed that in the COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier, the percentage of C, N, and O elements was 74.40%, 10.16%, and 15.44%, respectively, which indicates tangible changes in the COF structure.

pH-triggered structural instability of COF nanocarrier

An effective delivery system should exhibit high stability in a physiological environment while being capable of releasing its payload at the therapeutic site in response to pH changes. Consequently, the stability of COF was evaluated under pH conditions representative of those encountered by the delivery system. pH 5.0 was used to simulate the acidic conditions of intracellular compartments such as lysosomes and endosomes, whereas pH 6.0 represents the mildly acidic extracellular tumor microenvironment and pH 7.4 reflects the physiological conditions of normal tissues and blood. UV-vis data indicate a change in the COF structure at acidic pHs of 5.0 and 6.0. As can be seen in Fig. 7A, the COF structure does not change at physiological pH even after 6 months, while at acidic pH, changes are noticeable after 24 h. The pH-dependent structural instability of COF offers one of the desirable properties of this nanostructure in drug delivery, which was confirmed by UV-vis spectroscopic data. In particular, a significant change in the spectral profile was observed at acidic pH (5.0 and 6.0) compared to physiological pH. This change, characterized by a shift in existing peaks and the appearance of new peaks, indicates a disruption of the conjugated π-electron system in the COF structure. One important reason for this disruption could be the hydrolysis of imine bonds under acidic conditions, which destroys the extended 2D network and the formation of smaller fragments or oligomers [38].

Drug release study

The drug release profile of the as-synthesized nanocarrier was evaluated in vitro under pH conditions of 7.4, 6.0, and 5.0, with the results monitored using UV-vis spectroscopy and presented in Fig. 7B. A comparison of cumulative release profiles at three pHs of 7.4, 6.0, and 5.0 indicates that COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers are fully pH-responsive. Although a small amount of DOX is released at physiological pH, the highest drug release occurred in the first 24 h at pH 5. At this pH, up to 70% of the drug is released from COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers in the first 24 h, and release monitoring up to 60 h indicates relative stability of the nano platform and no significant release of DOX after 24 h (Fig. 7B).

Hemolysis assessment of COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers

The biocompatibility of the nano platform was evaluated through red blood cell hemolysis testing to ensure biological safety in cancer treatment. Usually, in examining hemolytic effects on red blood cells, hemolysis of less than 5% is ignored, and according to this criterion, it was shown that COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers with different amounts of DOX do not have hemolytic effects on red blood cells (Fig. 7C). The hemolysis test results demonstrate the excellent biocompatibility of COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers. The hemolysis level observed at all tested concentrations of DOX-loaded nanoplatform was consistently below 5%. This finding is very important because it indicates that COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers do not cause significant damage to red blood cells, which is an important parameter for the introduction of a drug nanocarrier in systemic applications [39].

DOX-loaded nanocarriers reduce cell viability

The standard MTT assay was employed to investigate the in vitro biocompatibility of COF@DOX-FA on SW480 cells 24 and 48 h after treatment. As depicted in Fig. 8, COF demonstrated no significant cytotoxicity in all used concentrations in both 24 and 48-hour investigations, confirming its good biocompatibility. Free DOX drug exhibited dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity, with cell viability significantly reduced (below 50%) at 24 h at concentrations ≥ 10 µg/mL, and further reduced (below 30%) at 48 h. COF@DOX nanocarriers showed moderate cytotoxicity at 24 h, with a significant enhancement in toxicity observed at 48 h, where cell viability dropped by over 50% at concentrations ≥ 7.5 µg/mL. The most profound cytotoxicity was observed in the cell group treated with COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier, which demonstrated a significant reduction in cell viability, dropping below 20% at higher concentrations at 48 h. The obtained results 24 h post-treatment with 0.5, 2.5, 7.5, 10, and 15 µg/mL of various nanocarriers showed that the IC50 values for the DOX drug, COF@DOX, and COF-FA@DOX-treated groups are 13.33 ± 0.163, 7.80 ± 1.92, and 11.04 ± 0.85 µg/mL, respectively. At 48 h post-treatment, the IC50 values for the DOX drug, COF@DOX, and COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers are 4.409 ± 0.59, 3.84 ± 0.39, and 3.79 ± 0.36 µg/mL, respectively.

DOX-loaded nanocarriers arrest the cell cycle in the sub-G1 phase

A flow cytometry assay assessed the alteration in cell cycle progression 48 h post-treatment with COF, free DOX drug, COF@DOX, and COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers. In the control (Ctrl) group, most cells were in the G1 phase (61.08%) and a minimal sub-G1 fraction (4.47%). COF@DOX nanocarriers led to more pronounced effects; the percentage of the cell population in the G1 phase reduced to 31.22%, the S phase increased to 32.05%, and the sub-G1 population rose significantly to 26.24%. Altogether, there was a significant arrest in the treated cells with COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers compared to other groups under the same conditions in the sub-G1 phase.

Discussion

In this study, we succeeded in synthesizing a new covalent organic framework functionalized with folic acid and showed its application in pH-responsive drug delivery for cancer therapy. Our results revealed that the synthesized COF had pH-responsive drug release behavior, good porosity, and moderate crystallinity. The synthesized COF from TAPB-DFP is not a good crystalline material as compared to other published COFs [40, 41]. This is due to the non-planar core structure, which has rotationally misaligned phenyl rings [17]. A similar observation has been reported for imine-linked COFs, where increased structural planarity is usually associated with better crystallinity [42, 43].

Here, the COFs were functionalized with folic acid to allow specific targeting of cancer cells overexpressing folate receptors. Functionalization with folic acid promoted specific cellular uptake of the nanocarriers through folate receptor-mediated endocytosis, consistent with a previous report by Yue et al. [44]. In their study, the folate-targeted COFs showed an enhanced cellular uptake in folate receptor-positive MCF-7 cells. In contrast, the folate receptor-negative A549 cells were less responsive to the FCOF. It was noted that folate receptor-mediated endocytosis enhanced the delivery of FA-functionalized COF by cell membrane folate receptors. This report is consistent with our results, which indicated folate receptors could play a role in the high uptake of COF carriers in cancer cells.

In our study, we first tried to load the water-soluble form of doxorubicin hydrochloride (DOX·HCl), a common chemotherapeutic drug. However, since the framework was largely hydrophobic and completely organic, loading DOX·HCl directly was ineffective. We therefore converted DOX·HCl into the hydrophobic free base form of DOX, using triethylamine [20]. This resulted in better molecular compatibility with the COF matrix, allowing for successful loading.

In addition, since loading efficiency is highly related to the pH value of the external environment according to other COF-based drug delivery reports [45], the loading was conducted at neutral pH (7.4) to maintain the structure stability of COF and drug as well as form beneficial interactions like π–π stacking and hydrogen bonds for stable drug loading [39]. Successful loading was also proved by various methods. The distinct peak in FTIR spectra (specifically, C = O stretching vibration at ~ 1650 cm-1), broad OH/NH band, and spectral shift confirmed the interaction between DOX and COF. The increase in particle size (151.5 → 193.4 nm), the decrease in zeta potential determined by DLS, and the change in FTIR spectra also indicated that the drug was stably anchored to the framework. The change in the physicochemical properties was attributed to the successful encapsulation and stabilization of DOX into COF-FA, which agrees with similar reports on other COFs as drug delivery systems [16, 46].

The pH-dependent release pattern of DOX from COF-FA@DOX demonstrated that these nanocarriers are a pH-responsive drug delivery system. The relatively rapid release of up to 70% of the loaded DOX within the first 24 h at acidic pH (PBS, pH 5.0, 37 °C) is a promising observation. This rapid release profile suggests that COF-FA@DOX can effectively deliver its drug cargo to the target site in a relatively short time frame. The mechanisms of drug release in response to pH are of great importance, which in the case of COF-FA@DOX may be one of the following: (a) protonation-induced swelling: protonation of certain groups in the COF-FA@DOX structure at acidic pH can lead to nanocarrier swelling, increasing the pore size and facilitating faster drug release [47]. (b) Hydrolysis of bonds: as previously discussed, acidic environments may cause hydrolysis of specific bonds in the COF structure, leading to degradation of the framework and release of the loaded DOX drug. (c) Drug interactions: changes in the pH environment can alter the interactions between DOX and the COF-FA@DOX matrix, weakening drug-nanocarrier interactions and facilitating drug release [48]. Such targeted release minimizes systemic exposure and enhances tumor-site drug availability, affirming the suitability of our COF-FA@DOX system for selective chemotherapeutic intervention.

In the present study, COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers exhibited the most pronounced cytotoxic effects across both time intervals. Significant reductions in cell viability were observed even at low concentrations (0.5 and 2.5 µg/mL). After 48 h, this formulation achieved the lowest cell viability among all tested groups, particularly at higher concentrations, where viability dropped below 20%. The superior performance of COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers can be attributed to folic acid-mediated active targeting. Folic acid binds to folate receptors overexpressed on cancer cells, enhancing cellular uptake and improving drug delivery specificity [49]. Moreover, free DOX drug exhibited dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity; these results align with the well-established efficacy of DOX drug as a chemotherapeutic agent; however, the rapid and nonspecific uptake of free DOX drug into cells contributes to its systemic toxicity, limiting its therapeutic window in clinical applications [50]. In contrast, COF@DOX nanocarriers exhibited sustained drug release, ensuring prolonged exposure of cancer cells to DOX drug while reducing early-phase toxicity. This controlled release enhances the therapeutic efficacy of the DOX drug, provides a distinct advantage over the free DOX drug, and successfully modulates drug release kinetics, avoiding the initial burst release often observed with free drugs [51].

In our study, the enhanced cellular uptake of COF@DOX nanocarrier compared with free DOX drugs indicates that the drug-loaded nanocarrier exhibits enhanced efficiency as a carrier for the delivery of the drug to the cells [12]. The significant cellular uptake of COF-FA@DOX noted the role of folic acid (FA) in effectively targeting cancer cells that express high levels of folate receptors (FRs) [52]. Additionally, due to low-level FR expression in HUVEC cells [53], the uptake rate of COF-FA@DOX nanocarrier (15 µg/mL) in them was found to be 43.4%. Uptake results from SW480 cells indicate that the attachment of FA to COF@DOX facilitates the uptake of these conjugates into FR-positive cells through an FR-mediated endocytic mechanism [54].

The cell cycle analysis demonstrated minimal effects in the control and COF groups, which aligns with their biocompatibility and lack of inherent cytotoxicity [17]. Free DOX drug induced significant cell cycle disruption (S phase arrest), causing DNA damage and apoptosis [55]. However, its non-specific distribution and systemic toxicity limit its therapeutic potential, highlighting the need for more efficient drug delivery systems. The cell cycle arrest in the S phase induced by COF@DOX nanocarriers aligns with the well-documented mechanism of doxorubicin, which interferes with topoisomerase II and inhibits DNA replication [55]. Additionally, the significant increase in the sub-G1 population following treatment with COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers indicates the induction of apoptosis, a finding supported by similar studies on receptor-targeted nanocarrier systems [56].

The apoptosis analysis highlighted the superior therapeutic efficacy of the functionalized COF nano platform. COF alone exhibited negligible apoptosis, emphasizing its biocompatibility and suitability for drug delivery without intrinsic toxicity. Free DOX drug induced significant apoptosis, but its non-specific toxicity led to higher necrosis rates, limiting its clinical application [50]. COF@DOX nanocarriers enhanced apoptosis induction by providing sustained DOX release, reducing early-phase toxicity, and improving therapeutic efficacy. The COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers demonstrated the highest apoptosis induction, attributed to the selective targeting of folate receptor-expressing cancer cells, which enabled more efficient drug delivery and minimized off-target toxicity [39, 44]. This selective targeting ensures improved therapeutic outcomes and better safety profiles, positioning COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers as a promising platform for targeted cancer therapy.

The present study emphasizes the importance of targeted drug delivery in preventing cancer cell migration and metastasis. COF alone showed minimal cell migration inhibition, emphasizing the need for drug loading or surface modification to enhance its therapeutic effects. Free DOX drug exhibited moderate migration inhibition due to its cytotoxic effects, but its non-specific distribution limits its potential to reduce metastasis effectively [50]. COF@DOX nanocarriers showed improved results, with a ~ 70% inhibition of migration due to controlled release and sustained drug exposure [12]. The most significant inhibition (~ 90%) was observed in the cell group treated with the COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers, owing to the selective targeting of folate receptor-positive cancer cells. This targeted approach enhances drug delivery and minimizes off-target effects, highlighting the potential of COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers in preventing metastasis and improving cancer therapy outcomes.

Conclusion

We developed a folic acid-modified COF nanocarrier that shows strong potential for targeted delivery of doxorubicin. The porous COF structure allowed for high drug loading, and folic acid functionalization helped direct the system toward cancer cells with overexpressed folate receptors (FRs). The addition of the DOX drug in the resulting COF-FA resulted in an efficient loading capacity. Interestingly, the release behavior was clearly pH-sensitive: drug release remained low at physiological pH but increased significantly under acidic conditions, which is promising for tumor-specific delivery. The in vitro biocompatibility results of the synthesized COF-FA@DOX nanocarriers also proved that they could be safe to use, as they show no appreciable hemolysis effects. The obtained results from MTT and flow cytometry assay on SW480 (FR+) and HUVEC (FR−) cells clearly show the effect of nanocarrier internalization, and their cytotoxic nature can be assigned to folic acid-mediated FRs targeting. It is also observed that in addition to showing significant antitumor activity, the COF-FA@DOX was able to induce apoptosis and cell cycle arrest, and also inhibited cell migration, which may be beneficial in reducing metastasis. Although further in vivo work is still needed, our findings suggest that this platform holds real promise for future targeted cancer treatments.
