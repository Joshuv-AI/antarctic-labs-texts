The application of design thinking in physical activity research: a protocol for a scoping review
Authors: Elise Rivera, Vivian Romero, Samuel Cassar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the many health benefits, global levels of physical activity remain insufficient. Traditional approaches to promoting physical activity have shown limited success, indicating the need for innovative strategies. Human-centred design thinking (HCDT) is a creative problem-solving and solution generation approach that offers a novel method for developing tailored interventions by engaging communities and understanding their specific contexts. However, it is unclear to what extent HCDT is being applied in physical activity research. The purpose of this protocol is to specify how a scoping review will be conducted to identify, explore, and map the application of HCDT in physical activity research.MethodsThe scoping review protocol will follow Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework, encompassing five stages: identifying the research question; identifying relevant studies; selecting eligible studies; charting the data; and collating, summarising, and reporting the results. The scoping review will follow the reporting guidance in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses protocols (PRIMSA-P) and the PRIMSA-P checklist for reporting scoping reviews. The database literature search will be conducted using PubMed, SportDiscus, PsychINFO, CINAHL, Embase, MEDLINE, ProQuest, SCOPUS, and Web of Science. The inclusion criteria include: qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods studies or protocol papers; full-text availability; published after 2016 and in English; and studies with explicit use of HCDT. Two reviewers will screen titles, abstracts, and full texts for inclusion. Two reviewers will extract the data from included studies using a data extraction table. Findings from the studies will be aggregated to summarise recurring themes via a narrative report.DiscussionIt is unknown how HCDT is currently being applied in physical activity research, despite its proven potential to address complex health challenges and offer innovative solutions. This scoping review will provide an overview of the current utilisation of HCDT, such as which HCDT approaches and activities are applied most often and where HCDT is being used for physical activity (i.e. populations, geographies). Background

Physical activity has been considered a “best buy" in public health [1] given that physical inactivity is estimated to be linked to 5.3 million annual deaths and is a modifiable risk factor for chronic diseases [2, 3]. Despite its benefits, most of the world’s population is insufficiently active [4, 5]. Given these low population levels of physical activity worldwide, there have been urgent calls to improve physical activity across the lifespan through research targeting surveillance; correlates and determinants; and intervention and policy development, implementation, and evaluation [6, 7]. However, several initiatives targeting physical activity promotion at a population level have demonstrated limited success, often due to complex factors with varying impact on settings and individuals, lack of end-users’ involvement beyond consultation, one-size-fits-all approaches, and reliance on top-down approaches [8, 9]. Thus, there have been calls to better consider specific needs, experiences, and perspectives of communities and their context through bottom-up approaches by actively engaging with them through community-based participatory research (CBPR) [10]. This health research paradigm centres on close collaboration between researchers and the community to understand community priorities, active involvement of the community throughout the research process, designing strategies with the community that suit their needs, community empowerment, and working in partnership with communities through equal decision-making and power sharing [11, 12]. In addition to the need for processes that are community-focused and that involve engaging actively with communities (i.e. engaging with stakeholders to problem solve at all stages of a project from defining a problem through to the end stages) [13], there is a need for greater innovation to develop solutions that target the complexities of insufficient physical activity through approaches that are more novel and creative. One such CBPR tool that can support this is human-centred design thinking (HCDT).

HCDT is an innovative problem-solving process that leverages insights concerning the experiences, perspectives, and needs of users of experiences, services, or interventions to generate best-fit, meaningful, and context-specific solutions through iterative phases involving empathy, clear problem definition, creative ideation, rapid prototyping, and testing [14–16]. HCDT also entails working through a range of design activities through non-linear phases of inspiration, ideation, and implementation to problem frame and generate solutions [14]. HCDT is distinct from co-creation, co-design, and co-production in that these processes usually involve researcher-community partnerships with stakeholders/community during solution development, whereas HCDT focuses more on problem definition and solution design that utilises empathy-driven processes to ensure that end-users’ needs, contexts, and desires are met [17].

While not yet widespread [18], HCDT is gaining traction in health research and public health. For example, there are several review papers on the topic, including a scoping review of 21 studies concerning the application and context of HCDT in global health, which found that HCDT is commonly being used for disease management, health systems, infectious disease prevention, primary prevention, and health behaviours/health education [19]. Of the identified studies that used HCDT for health behaviours, it was applied for contraception use, lifestyle interventions, hand hygiene, and nutrition [19]. Physical activity was not targeted, although it could link to the healthy lifestyle interventions [19]. Additionally, a narrative review of 82 studies concerning the application of HCDT in health research found that design methods have often been used in combination with traditional qualitative and quantitative methods to understand the research context/problem definition and solution development [20]. However, health research is broad and, as of yet, there has not been a focus on physical activity research specifically. A further two reviews examining the use of HCDT approaches in digital health interventions have indicated its widespread use in mobile health or digital health [21, 22]. These reviews found that HCDT was useful for tailoring mHealth content, understanding user experience and needs, meaningful involvement of stakeholders, supporting co-creation and/or CBPR [21, 22], fostering active collaboration with communities, and enhancing usability of solutions (e.g. app) either overall or specifically concerning mental health [21, 22]. However, the mobile and digital health interventions were not limited to physical activity. Taken together, the use of HCDT in the context of physical activity research remains unknown, and a research gap exists concerning how HCDT is being utilised and applied in the public health domain of physical activity (e.g. to develop policies, interventions).

Therefore, the aim of this scoping review is to identify, explore, and map the existing literature concerning the utilisation and application of HCDT in physical activity research. It is anticipated that the results of the scoping review will (1) confirm which HCDT framework (Stanford d.School, Double Diamond, IDEO, etc.), phases (e.g. empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test), and activities (e.g. empathy interview, 5 Why’s, quick ideas, round robin brainstorming, concept pitch) are used most frequently; and (2) indicate the populations, geographic locations, and topical areas (e.g. physical activity and the built environment, active living interventions, physical activity policy) in which HCDT is being applied.

Methodology

This protocol is for a scoping review of the literature reporting on the application of HCDT in physical activity research. Scoping reviews are useful for mapping bodies of literature regarding context, location, and discipline [23], rendering this a suitable method for addressing our aim. This scoping review protocol has been registered within the Open Science Framework platform (registration ID: 10.17605/OSF.IO/F94PZ). Additionally, the scoping review protocol follows the reporting guidance in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses protocols (PRIMSA-P) [24] and the PRIMSA-P checklist for reporting scoping reviews (PRIMSA-ScR) [25], and these checklists can be found in Additional files 1–2. The proposed scoping review will be guided by Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework for scoping studies [26] and methodological guidance for scoping reviews by Levac and colleagues [27]. In line with these frameworks, the following steps will be performed: (1) identification of the research question; (2) identification of relevant studies; (3) selection of eligible studies; (4) charting the data; (5) collation, summarisation, and reporting of the results. A quality appraisal of included studies will not be conducted as this review aims to identify all research activities in the field concerning the application of HCDT in physical activity research.

Identifying relevant studies

The literature search will be conducted using the following electronic databases to ensure rigour and comprehensiveness: PubMed, SportDiscus, PsychINFO, CINAHL, Embase, MEDLINE, ProQuest, SCOPUS, Web of Science. A preliminary search of the literature was conducted in MEDLINE, searching article titles, abstracts, and full-text articles to inform the development of the final search strategy. Guidance from a university librarian on the final search strategy will be sought to best scope the literature. This will include seeking suggestions and verification concerning appropriate syntax and the adaptation of search strategies across databases.

Eligible articles will be uploaded into and screened within the Covidence software. This program will be used to identify and remove all duplicate articles and to screen titles, abstracts, and full-text articles. The title and abstract screening process will first be piloted with a double screening of all coauthors for 10% of retrieved articles (10% by ER, 10% by VR, and 10% by SC). Using kappa statistic, interrater reliability will be calculated for the title and abstract screening. The official title/abstract screening process will commence when an agreement rate of 75% or higher is achieved by ER, VR, and SC based on the pre-determined eligibility criteria in congruence with the research questions. If any articles are identified where it is unclear whether they should be included based on the initial state of title/abstract reviewing, the study will be included for full-text review to ensure its full consideration before reaching an inclusion or exclusion decision. Excluded citations will be discussed among all authors to reach consensus. Next, the full-text screening of the included articles will be independently performed by two authors. All attempts will be made to ensure the full-text articles are acquired, such as consulting the university librarian, searching the web, and contacting the author(s) if necessary. The reviewers will meet regularly at multiple stages of the screening process to discuss any discrepancies that arise. Lack of consensus and significant discrepancies between two reviewers will be rectified through discussion to reach consensus. The first author (ER) will arbitrate any discrepancies if there is still no agreement. The degree of agreement between reviewers will be determined and reported. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses protocols (PRIMSA-P) [24] and the PRIMSA-P checklist for reporting scoping reviews (PRIMSA-ScR) will be used to guide the screening process (see Additional files 1–2) [25]. The selection of studies to be included in the scoping review is expected to take approximately 6 to 8 weeks to complete, and the estimated study screening timeframe is April-May 2026.

Charting the data

A data charting form (Table 4) will be used to chart and electronically capture relevant information for each included study. The data charting form draws on aspects of O’Cathain and colleagues’ taxonomy of approaches for intervention development in health, given that intervention development is a key aspect of physical activity research [32]. The data extraction form will also draw on guidelines for comprehensive reporting of HCDT [33]. The data charting will be performed independently by two reviewers, who will discuss the results and frequently and continuously update the data charting form. Any disagreements will be resolved between the two reviewers through discussion or decided by a third reviewer. It is estimated that the data extraction process will take approximately 8 weeks, and the estimated study screening timeframe is September–October 2026.

Collating, summarising, and reporting the results

A scoping review is a suitable methodology for mapping the concepts of a research discipline and identifying the main sources and types of available evidence [23]. Consequently, the findings from this scoping review will offer an overview of how HCDT is being applied in physical activity research. We will aggregate the findings of the included studies to summarise and highlight recurring themes. The authors will summarise the extracted data (refer to Table 4) concerning the research question and sub-questions, aligning it with the overall study purpose through a narrative report. Additionally, results will also be organised into categories (e.g. frameworks applied, HCDT phases, HCDT methods/activities, areas of physical activity where HCDT is being applied, examples of HCDT applied for problem framing, examples of HCDT applied to develop solutions, populations and geographic areas where HCDT is applied, use of HCDT as a stand-alone approach or with other approaches). The broader implications of the results for practice, policy, and research will be discussed. It is estimated that the data collation, summarising, and reporting process will take approximately 8 to 10 weeks, and the estimated study screening timeframe is October–December 2026.

Discussion

The scoping review aims to identify, explore, and map the application of HCDT in physical activity research. It will provide an overview of the “state of HCDT in physical activity”, as well as highlight knowledge gaps regarding how HCDT is being used in physical activity research. Additionally, the scoping review will identify which context, frameworks, phases, methods, and activities of HCDT have been used in physical activity research and for what purpose (e.g. to problem define and/or generate solutions). The review will also indicate the domains of physical activity in which HCDT has been used (e.g. active recreation, active transport), population groups, and geographic areas where HCDT has been most frequently used. Collating the published literature on the application of HCDT in physical activity research, this review will provide methodological clarity and suggest new HCDT approaches to addressing the issue of physical inactivity. The inclusion of communities in the design of research has been a strong focus for the physical activity sector for many years, and HCDT approaches provide opportunities for innovation in research, policy, and practice. By identifying effective HCDT strategies and highlighting areas for further investigation, this review aims to enhance the development of innovative, context-specific solutions using HCDT that can improve global physical activity levels and overall public health outcomes.

A limitation of this review is the potential exclusion of studies that incorporate HCDT principles without explicitly stating so (e.g. directly stating the design methods, HCDT framework) due to the search terms and keywords used and exclusion criteria applied. However, the scoping review aims to focus specifically on the application of HCDT in physical activity research, so studies not explicitly mentioning HCDT (or related concepts) will be intentionally excluded in accordance with the study aim. Another limitation may be the exclusion of relevant studies that relate to physical activity but more loosely (e.g. focus only on informal sport without making links to physical activity) and were therefore excluded by the keywords and MeSH terms used in the database searches. However, to mitigate this, we used similar search terms, keywords, and MeSH terms used in other scoping reviews focused specifically on physical activity overall [34, 35]. Additionally, the quality of the studies will not be assessed, so the reliability of the extracted data from the included studies cannot be evaluated. Lastly, the review may miss relevant studies due to the language bias of only including articles published in English.
