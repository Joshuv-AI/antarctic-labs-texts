Effect of ketamine intervention on hemodynamic responses in patients with treatment-resistant depression
Authors: Guan-Jie She, Wei-Chi Li, Chun-Hsiang Chou, Li-Kai Cheng, Tung-Ping Su, Cheng-Ta Li, Wei-Chen Lin, Pei-Chi Tu, Shih-Jen Tsai, Ya-Mei Bai, Mu-Hong Chen, Li-Fen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence shows that ketamine, an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, can produce rapid anti-suicidal effects in treatment-resistant depression, yet its underlying neuropharmacological mechanisms remain elusive. The present study aimed at investigating the therapeutic effect of ketamine infusion on suicidal ideation by examining hemodynamic response changes in resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) data. We analyzed data from clinical trials involving 45 patients with treatment-resistant depression, including either ketamine (N = 21) or midazolam (N = 24). T1-weighted images and rsfMRI data were collected before and three days after treatment. Three parameters, including response height (RH), time-to-peak (TTP), and full-width at half-maximum (FWHM), were extracted. Results showed that ketamine significantly altered TTP and FWHM in the bilateral olfactory cortex and right inferior parietal gyrus. Changes in TTP and FWHM within the thalamus and superior frontal gyrus were associated with improvements in suicidal ideation. Additionally, ketamine responders exhibited reduced TTP in the left precuneus. These findings suggest that ketamine induces region-specific HRF modulations in brain regions implicated in sensory-cognitive integration, mood regulation, and cognitive control. This supports a broader model in which ketamine’s neuropharmacological effects may be understood through the lens of neurovascular coupling dynamics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51314-3.Subject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Many patients with treatment-resistant depression (TRD) suffer from suicidal ideation, which marks the transition from suicidal thoughts to suicide attempts1. Numerous clinical trials have provided robust evidence supporting the therapeutic effects of ketamine in alleviating suicidal ideation in patients with TRD2–7. Despite the proven efficacy of its therapeutic effects, the underlying neural mechanisms for these outcomes have yet to be fully elucidated8–10.

Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) is a well-established neuroimaging technique for investigating the effects of pharmacological interventions on TRD. Many studies have explored ketamine-induced changes in brain activation patterns and functional connectivity (FC)11,12. In our previous research13, ketamine was shown to modulate FC in prefrontal cortex (PFC)-related circuits and reduce the risk of suicidal ideation. Recent studies on patients with major depressive disorders have shown that ketamine infusion modulates FC disruptions between limbic regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex, hippocampus and amygdala. It has also been linked to key resting-state networks, such as the default mode network (DMN)14,15. These findings suggest that the anti-suicidal effect of ketamine may be attributed to the rebalancing and resynchronizing of various brain networks. fMRI research has yielded valuable insights into ketamine-induced changes in macro-scale brain networks; however, explaining its lasting therapeutic effects despite rapid biological elimination requires in-depth analysis of the underlying neural mechanisms at the micro-scale16,17.

Previous studies have shown that the pharmacological effects of ketamine are closely associated with NMDA receptor antagonism, with corresponding effects on cerebral vascular function and multiple neurotransmitter systems18,19. Vascular function and neurotransmitter systems play critical roles in the neurovascular coupling that links blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) signals to underlying neural activity20–22. This coupling is influenced by various neural and vascular factors, including cerebral blood flow (CBF), cerebral metabolic rate of oxygenation (CMRO2), vasodilation, and neurotransmitter concentrations23–25. The complex process of neurovascular coupling can be described using a mathematical model known as the hemodynamic response function (HRF), which is characterized by three primary morphological parameters: response height (RH), time to peak (TTP) and full-width at half-maximum (FWHM)26–28. Each parameter reflects specific aspects of the underlying neural mechanisms. RH, indicating the amplitude of the HRF, is primarily influenced by the magnitude of neural activation, similar to the conventional fMRI BOLD signal29. TTP captures the latency to peak which reflect the efficiency of the underlying neural response. FWHM indicates the overall duration of the response, indicating the sustainability of the neural response29. Analysis of these parameters makes it possible to capture the dynamics of neural activation and neurovascular coupling at the micro-scale29,30.

Our aim in this study was to investigate the effect of ketamine intervention on neurovascular coupling in order to elucidate the neuropharmacological mechanism(s) underlying the effects of ketamine on suicidal ideation. This study was based on two main hypotheses: (1) Ketamine can induce changes in the process of neurovascular coupling, as reflected by changes in HRF, particularly in NMDA-receptor dependent brain regions; and (2) Alterations in HRF are associated with a reduced likelihood of suicidal ideation in patients with TRD (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study sought to clarify the effects of ketamine on neurovascular coupling by examining changes in HRF morphological parameters. Our results revealed that ketamine was associated with altered TTP and FWHM in the left olfactory cortex and right inferior parietal cortex. TTP reflects the timing of the cerebral blood flow response, whereas FWHM reflects the duration of the hemodynamic response after neural activation27. Together, these parameters capture neural efficiency and sustainability, both of which are important temporal aspects of the neurovascular response29. Among ketamine responders, the altered TTP observed in the left precuneus suggests a ketamine-associated change in the timing of neurovascular coupling in specific brain regions. In the overall ketamine subgroup, similar RH changes across regions suggest coordinated effects across distributed brain networks. This interpretation is in line with pharmacological fMRI studies showing that ketamine can produce region-specific changes in whole-brain functional organization and connectivity31–33, and that these effects differ from those seen under midazolam treatment34.

Interpreting network-level hemodynamic patterns requires an understanding of the relationship between HRF-based metrics and conventional measures of Functional Connectivity (FC). Although RH is not a direct measure of functional connectivity, alterations in HRF morphology may reflect neurovascular processes that shape FC estimates. Hemodynamic responses reflect the integrated output of neurovascular coupling, whereas variability in HRF timing and shape reflects differences in the translation of neuronal activity into vascular signals35. Accordingly, HRF parameters characterize the temporal dynamics of neurovascular coupling, including response latency and duration, which can systematically bias or modulate correlation-based FC measures derived from BOLD signals36. Note that hemodynamic responses are shaped by the local vascular architecture and the specific vascular compartment that is sampled.

HRF profiles vary across locations and vascular compartments, with more sustained responses in the central parenchyma and earlier return to baseline in more distant arteries36. These regional differences often manifest in BOLD-based FC measures. Because BOLD time series represent neural activity convolved with region-specific HRFs, inter-regional differences in HRF timing often appear as relative BOLD delays and bias zero-lag correlation estimates of FC37. Unmodeled HRF variability may therefore generate spurious positive or negative connections. This means that HRF-based metrics should be viewed as upstream complements or confounders of conventional BOLD-derived FC measures37.

A quantitative model of human neurovascular coupling based on mechanisms identified in animal studies supports this interpretation by showing that the temporal shape of the hemodynamic response depends on pathway-specific neuronal and glial signaling rather than on global changes in cerebral blood flow alone38. Glutamatergic pyramidal neuron activity is closely tied to NMDA receptor function, which can influence the timing and duration of vascular responses through vasoactive signaling and astrocyte-mediated pathways. In this context, HRF latency and width may be particularly sensitive to changes in glutamatergic neurovascular coupling, providing a physiological rationale for using HRF-based metrics to examine ketamine-related effects.

The observed changes in HRF are likely related to NMDA receptor antagonism and a ketamine-induced downstream glutamatergic cascade, including enhanced glutamate release and AMPA receptor activation19,39. Alterations of this type have been shown to influence neurovascular coupling by shifting the balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters. Glutamate plays a key role in regulating cerebral blood flow by stimulating the production of nitric oxide (NO), a potent vasodilator. Moreover, glutamate-mediated signaling in astrocytes triggers the release of vasoactive molecules, such as prostaglandins and epoxyeicosatrienoic acids, further promoting the adjustment of blood flow in response to neural activity40.

Other sedative or anxiolytic agents have been shown to alter arousal and inhibitory tone, with corresponding effects on cerebral hemodynamics41. Sedation has been associated with systematic changes in resting-state fMRI measures, including both local and global network properties41, which commonly appear as shifts in overall hemodynamic magnitude rather than pathway-specific temporal changes in neurovascular coupling. Moreover, a growing body of evidence suggests that changes in consciousness and anesthetic state can also affect neurovascular coupling, producing measurable changes in the timing and shape of the hemodynamic response estimated from resting-state BOLD signals42. These effects may occur in conjunction with reductions in task-evoked CBF and BOLD responses, as well as altered CBF-BOLD coupling under volatile anesthesia43. This makes it more difficult to interpret fMRI findings when conclusions rely on a single hemodynamic measure. Overall, these studies indicate that altered consciousness can influence fMRI signals through broad but agent-dependent effects on hemodynamics and neurovascular coupling. In this context, the temporal HRF changes observed after ketamine infusion may reflect more than a generic sedative effect. This interpretation is supported by comparative mouse studies showing that different anesthetic regimens produce distinct BOLD response dynamics and hemodynamic profiles, with ketamine-based anesthesia yielding more prolonged responses than regimens such as dexmedetomidine/isoflurane44.

Ketamine interventions were shown to reduce TTP and increase FWHM in the olfactory cortex, indicating neural responses with reduced latency and sustained duration. Numerous researchers have reported that the olfactory cortex plays a critical role in sensory processing and emotional regulation45–48. Its connection to the limbic system, including the amygdala, hippocampus, and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), likely contribute to its involvement in emotional regulation48,49. We observed positive correlations in HRF parameter changes between the olfactory cortex and mood-related brain regions, particularly for RH. Considering that RH reflects the magnitude of neural activation, these coordinated changes with the limbic system support a possible role for the olfactory cortex in mood regulation. We also detected positive correlations in TTP and FWHM between the olfactory cortex and ACC, further supporting a functional association between these regions, consistent with previous studies50.

Modulation of the neurovascular unit through NMDA receptor antagonism may help explain the pharmacological effects of ketamine observed in the olfactory cortex. NMDA receptor dependent long-term potentiation is important for learning and synaptic plasticity in the olfactory cortex and has been linked to its sensory processing and integrative functions51. It is possible that ketamine affects these processes, with downstream consequences for olfactory cortex activity and its functional relationships with emotion-related regions such as the amygdala and anterior cingulate cortex43. Within this framework, the decreased TTP and increased FWHM observed in the olfactory cortex may indicate ketamine-related alterations in neurovascular and synaptic response dynamics.

We observed a ketamine-induced decrease in FWHM in the inferior parietal cortex. Previous neuroimaging studies have reported structural abnormalities in this region in individuals with suicidal ideation (altered gray matter volume and cortical thickness), and have linked these abnormalities to perseverative thinking, impaired executive function, and disrupted metacognitive processing52–55. Although the relationship between these structural findings and HRF changes remains uncertain, it is possible that such abnormalities are associated with altered neurovascular coupling through disrupted neuronal, glial, and vascular interactions56. Within this context, the decrease in FWHM in the current study may reflect ketamine-related changes in local neurovascular response dynamics, possibly indicating a hemodynamic response of shorter duration. This interpretation is broadly consistent with previous [18 F]-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET findings showing increased glucose metabolic activity in inferior parietal cortex after ketamine infusion, which has been taken to suggest re-engagement of cognitive control networks57.

The mechanism by which ketamine infusion affects the inferior parietal cortex has yet to be fully elucidated. Recent studies have implicated this region in the pharmacological effects of ketamine. The inferior parietal cortex is rich in NMDA receptors, which are involved in synaptic plasticity and neurovascular signaling58,59. It is therefore plausible that ketamine, through NMDA receptor antagonism, influences local neurovascular and synaptic response dynamics in this region. Such changes may be relevant to the restoration of dysfunctional neural circuits along with improvements in cognitive control and flexibility, which could in turn help to reduce the rigid maladaptive thinking commonly observed in individuals with suicidal ideation60.

Note that the direction of FWHM change in the inferior parietal cortex was opposite to that observed in the olfactory cortex. Regional variation in HRF characteristics is well documented, and previous studies have suggested that these variations are related to differences in local processing demands61. Accordingly, our finding of region-specific directional changes in FWHM is consistent with the broader literature. The increased FWHM observed in the olfactory cortex and thalamus may reflect more prolonged local response dynamics in regions involved in sensory processing and integration. By contrast, the decreased FWHM observed in the inferior parietal cortex may indicate a shorter local response duration in a region associated with cognitive control. Together, these findings suggest that ketamine affects HRF dynamics in a region-specific manner rather than producing a uniform effect across the brain.

Previous studies have highlighted the importance of thalamo-cortical pathways in emotion regulation, cognitive control, and suicidal behavior62–65. In the current study, lower CSSRS scores were associated with decreased TTP and increased FWHM in the thalamus as well as the medial and orbital superior frontal gyrus. These findings suggest that neurovascular changes within this circuit are associated with suicidal ideation. This interpretation is consistent with prior work showing that thalamic dysfunction is linked to emotional dysregulation in individuals at risk of suicide66–68. The superior frontal gyrus has likewise been implicated in suicide-related clinical features. Chen et al. reported that baseline hypoconnectivity between the bilateral superior frontal gyrus and the striatum is predictive of symptom improvement after ketamine infusion in cases of treatment-resistant depression69. Huang et al. reported that lower structural integrity in the superior frontal gyrus was associated with increased impulsivity in individuals with bipolar disorder and suicide attempts70.

Our results revealed positive correlations in RH changes between the thalamus and superior frontal gyrus, suggesting coordinated alterations within the thalamo-cortical circuit. Because RH reflects the magnitude of the underlying hemodynamic response, it is possible that these findings indicate linked changes in regional response dynamics rather than isolated effects in single areas. This interpretation is broadly consistent with current views that adaptive cognition depends on the flexible modulation of neural activity63. In this context, We posit that ketamine-related changes in neurovascular coupling within the thalamo-cortical circuit may contribute to improved mood regulation and cognitive control, providing a possible neurophysiological basis for its anti-suicidal effects.

The observed effects of ketamine on the thalamo-cortical circuit may be related to NMDA receptor antagonism, which is the primary pharmacological action of ketamine71. The relatively high density of NMDA receptors in the thalamus72 suggests that this region is particularly sensitive to ketamine stimulation. Direct evidence for NMDA receptor involvement in the superior frontal gyrus is more limited. It is therefore plausible that some of the effects observed in the superior frontal gyrus reflect downstream consequences of ketamine-related changes in thalamic function. Within this context, the thalamus may represent an additional pathway, alongside the olfactory-related pathway, through which ketamine influences sensory, cognitive, and affective processing. This interpretation is broadly consistent with previous work showing that the cognitive effects of NMDA receptor antagonists involve the thalamus and its cortical projections73.

Ketamine responders showed decreased TTP in the left precuneus, a key node of the default mode network, critically involved in self-referential processing and episodic memory74–76. This region has also been implicated in depression and suicidal ideation, and altered connectivity of the precuneus has been linked to cognitive and emotional dysregulation68,77. In this context, the decreased TTP observed after ketamine infusion may indicate altered local hemodynamic response timing in a region relevant to self-related cognition. Such changes may be relevant to the therapeutic effects of ketamine, particularly in processes that are often disrupted in individuals with suicidal ideation.

To assess the robustness of this responder-based finding under a more conventional definition, we repeated the analysis requiring a CSSRS reduction of at least 50%. This definition may better capture clinically meaningful improvements in partial responders and may also reveal treatment-related patterns not detected when response is defined as a CSSRS score of zero. Under this criterion, the precuneus interaction became marginal, whereas significant interactions emerged in the left medial superior frontal gyrus, right superior parietal gyrus, and left thalamus (Supplementary Table S3). Although these regions were not identified in the original responder-based interaction analysis, the medial superior frontal gyrus and thalamus also showed convergent involvement in the ΔTTP-ΔCSSRS and RH correlation analyses, while the superior parietal finding remained anatomically consistent with the broader parietal involvement observed in our ketamine-related HRF results. Taken together, these findings support a distributed network-level interpretation rather than an effect confined to a single region.

Recent reviews have reported that the efficacy of ketamine for suicidal ideation ranges from 30% to 60%78,79. Our findings were broadly consistent with this range, with an observed response rate of 38%. Several factors may contribute to variability in ketamine response. One important consideration is the concomitant use of other medications. For ethical reasons, participants in the current study continued their existing treatment for treatment-resistant depression. Benzodiazepines have been reported to attenuate the antidepressant effects of ketamine80,81, and both benzodiazepines and some mood stabilizers may interact with ketamine through arousal regulation, glutamatergic signaling, and/or neurovascular coupling82. It is possible that such interactions influenced both the observed magnitude and temporal characteristics of the hemodynamic responses. Although we used an active control to reduce nonspecific pharmacological and procedural effects, both ketamine and midazolam possess sedative properties that could have affected arousal state and contributed to inter-individual variability in HRF parameters. It is also possible that treatment outcomes were influenced by heterogeneity among patients with treatment-resistant depression, including differences in symptom severity, illness duration, and comorbidities83. Taken together, these factors should be considered when interpreting variability in both clinical response and HRF findings.

This study was subject to several limitations, which should be considered in the interpretation of our findings. First, some ROIs showed significant effects in the uncorrected analysis, while the findings did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, indicating that the evidence for region-specific HRF alterations was limited. The fact that the observed effect sizes ranged from 0.311 to 0.556 suggests that the effects of ketamine on neurovascular coupling were modest and accompanied by substantial inter-individual variability associated with concomitant medication use and the inclusion of an active control. Larger studies and alternative analytic approaches will be required to clarify whether pharmacological interventions reliably modulate HRF dynamics.

Second, the three widely used HRF parameters addressed in this study (response height, time-to-peak, and full width at half maximum) proved to be informative; however, they cannot capture the full complexity of hemodynamic response dynamics. Future studies examining a broader range of HRF features may provide a more complete characterization of ketamine-related hemodynamic and metabolic changes. For example, the initial dip has been linked to transient increases in cerebral metabolic rate following neural activation30,84, whereas measures such as onset latency and post-stimulus undershoot amplitude may reflect vascular compliance and delayed neurovascular coupling processes85.

Third, neurovascular changes were assessed only at Day 3 after a single ketamine infusion, thereby limiting our ability to draw conclusions pertaining to the time course of the observed HRF alterations. Clinical studies have shown that ketamine can produce antidepressant effects within hours, with symptom improvement peaking within 24 h and gradually diminishing over the following week5. The HRF changes observed at Day 3 may therefore reflect sustained circuit-level effects beyond the acute psychoactive phase, and possibly early downstream neurobiological processes86. However, the present design was unable to distinguish transient neurovascular responses from early markers of longer-term adaptation. Longitudinal studies with extended follow-up are required.

Fourth, the absence of a healthy control group limited our ability to separate baseline disease-related neurovascular abnormalities from the general pharmacological effects of ketamine on cerebral hemodynamics. Although our use of an active control helped to account for nonspecific pharmacological and procedural effects, both ketamine and midazolam have been shown to alter arousal state. Moreover, participants continued the concomitant use of prescribed medications (mood stabilizers and benzodiazepines) throughout the study. Thus, sedation-related effects and medication interactions may have contributed to the observed HRF changes and reduced the magnitude of ketamine-related effects. The inclusion of healthy controls together with clinical comparator groups would help clarify the specificity of these hemodynamic findings.

Finally, the use of the AAL atlas may have limited sensitivity to spatially localized HRF variation. A finer parcellation strategy may be better suited to detecting regional heterogeneity in HRF dynamics. Future studies should therefore examine the impact of spatial resolution on HRF estimation more directly.

This investigation into ketamine-induced HRF parameter alterations provides important insights into the pharmacological mechanisms underlying its anti-suicidal effects. Recent studies have shown that ketamine enhances brain network integrity in patients with TRD and suicidal ideation, particularly in the thalamus and DMN region87. Our results support the view that ketamine-related HRF changes involve a distributed set of regions rather than a single isolated site. Temporal HRF patterns revealed prolonged responses in the olfactory cortex and shorter responses in the inferior parietal cortex, reflecting region-specific effects in areas involved in emotional sensory processing and cognitive control. The thalamus, which serves as an important relay between sensory and cortical systems, also showed temporal HRF changes associated with improvement in CSSRS scores. Together, these findings suggest that ketamine-related neurovascular effects may involve multiple interacting pathways, including but not limited to the olfactory pathway. Taken together, these findings support a network-level interpretation in which region-specific changes in neurovascular timing contribute to the therapeutic effects of ketamine on suicide symptoms. Reduced suicidal tendencies may be associated with more flexible integration of sensory, cognitive, and emotional processing, arising from ketamine-induced changes in the temporal dynamics of neurovascular responses across distributed brain networks.

Methods

Subjects and study procedure

This study is part of a larger clinical trial detailed in a previous article88. We collected and analyzed data related to TRD patients exhibiting varying levels of suicidal ideation. TRD was defined as major depressive disorder with poor treatment response to antidepressants administrated at an adequate dosage and treatment duration. Exclusion criteria included comorbidities with other axis I psychiatric disorders, major illness (e.g., cancer or epilepsy), current pregnancy, and intense risk of suicide. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either a single infusion of 0.5 mg/kg ketamine or 0.045 mg/kg midazolam. All participants completed self-assessment questionnaires and underwent MRI scanning immediately prior to infusion (baseline) and on Day 3 post-infusion. The Montgomery-Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD 17), and Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (CSSRS) were used to assess the psychological state and suicidal severity. Ketamine responders were classified as patients with CSSRS scores that decreased to zero after treatment. The robustness of our findings to the operational definition of treatment response was assessed by performing supplementary analysis using a conventional responder criterion, defined as a ≥ 50% reduction in CSSRS score following treatment. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent. This study was registered with UMIN Clinical Trials Registry as UMIN000033916.

MRI image acquisition

Participants underwent resting-state functional and high-resolution T1-weighted structural MR imaging using a 3-Tesla PET/MR scanner (Signa, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, USA) at Taipei Veterans General Hospital. Resting-state functional MR images (rsfMRI) were acquired from single-shot echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequences with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2500 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, 45 contiguous slices, 200 volumes, 5 dummy scans, voxel size = 3.4 × 3.4 × 3.6 mm3,. High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were acquired with a three-dimensional fast spoiled gradient echo (3D-FSPGR) sequence using the following parameters: TR = 6.9 ms, TE = 2.5 ms, flip angle = 12°, 176 contiguous slices, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Head cushions and earplugs were provided to reduce head motion and noise; however the patient remained awake during the scans.

Image preprocessing

The CONN functional connectivity toolbox89 was used for the preprocessing of rsfMRI data. Individual fMRI scans were co-registered to the corresponding T1-weighted images prior to slice-timing correction, realignment, and normalization to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template. The Artifact Detection Tools (ART)-based scrubbing procedure was employed to deal with head movement-related artifacts, and the threshold for outlier detection was set to the 97th percentile of a normative sample for both frame-wise displacement and global signal changes, with volumes exceeding these thresholds identified as outliers and modeled as nuisance regressors. Signal contributions from white matter, cerebrospinal fluid, and six motion parameters were regressed out. Finally, the rsfMRI data were band-pass filtered (0.008–0.09 Hz) and spatial smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 6 mm to prepare the data for HRF analysis.

HRF estimation and parameter extraction

HRF estimation was performed using the rsHRF toolbox, based on a blind deconvolution approach developed for resting-state fMRI by Wu et al.90. This method is based on the premise that spontaneous BOLD signal fluctuations during rest can be interpreted as responses to underlying non-random spontaneous neural events. Spontaneous neural events were modeled as pseudo-events using a point-process framework following the detection of relatively large amplitude fluctuations in BOLD signals, defined as peaks exceeding a threshold of mean + standard deviation. These neural events were then convolved with a canonical HRF model that included a temporal derivative, thereby allowing modest variation in HRF shape across individuals and regions. For each region, the HRF that best fit the observed BOLD time series (defined as the one with the smallest residual error) was selected as the estimated HRF. The latent neural event time series was then reconstructed from the estimated HRF and the observed BOLD signal using Wiener deconvolution. A first-order autoregressive model (AR(1)) was applied to account for unmodeled neural activity. The brain was parcellated using the automated anatomical labeling (AAL) atlas91, which divides the cerebrum into 90 regions. Within each region, voxel-wise BOLD signals were averaged to obtain a representative regional BOLD time series, and HRF estimation was performed on these regional mean signals. We then extracted three morphological parameters from the estimated HRFs, the response height (RH), time-to-peak (TTP) and full-width at half-maximum (FWHM). The parameters were then utilized for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

For demographic data, independent t-tests were used for continuous variables, and chi-square test was used for nominal variables to compare the intervention groups (ketamine vs. midazolam) and ketamine responsiveness (responders vs. non-responders). For clinical questionnaire scores and HRF parameters, repeated-measures two-way ANOVAs were performed with time (baseline vs. Day 3) and intervention group (ketamine vs. midazolam) as independent variables, and questionnaire scores and HRF measures (RH, TTP, and FWHM) as dependent variables. To identify HRF changes specific to ketamine responders, a separate repeated-measures two-way ANOVA was conducted within the ketamine group, with time and ketamine responsiveness (responder vs. non-responder) as independent variables. Associations between HRF parameters and suicidal ideation were assessed using Pearson correlation analyses between changes in HRF parameters and changes in CSSRS scores. Correlation coefficients in the ketamine and midazolam groups were compared using Fisher’s r-to-z tests implemented using the cocor package in R92. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Because this study was exploratory and statistical power was limited by sample size, inter-individual variability, concomitant medication use, and the use of an active sham control, findings that reached an uncorrected threshold of p < 0.05 are also reported to facilitate the interpretation of potentially meaningful effects. In addition, Cohen’s d was calculated for all ANOVA results to quantify effect size.

Finally, to investigate how regions with significant HRF alterations relate to broader brain dynamics, we performed Pearson correlation analyses between HRF parameters in brain regions that survived previous statistical analysis and other brain regions. A significance threshold of p < 0.005 with Bonferroni correction was applied.
