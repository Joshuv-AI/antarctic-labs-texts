SOVA is observed in approximately 0.09% of the general population, and rupture occurs in only about 35% of these cases[2,3]. Despite its rarity, the condition carries substantial risk, especially when rupture leads to acute left-to-right shunting, volume overload, and heart failure. Epidemiological trends suggest a higher prevalence of SOVA in Eastern populations compared to Western ones[4].

Most SOVAs remain asymptomatic until rupture, at which point clinical manifestations vary based on the site of rupture and the chamber involved[5]. Among the known associations, the co-occurrence of ruptured SOVA with ventricular septal defects (VSDs), particularly membranous VSDs, is of notable concern. This combination can significantly complicate diagnosis and management due to overlapping hemodynamic disturbances and structural complexities[4].

Here, we present a rare case of a ruptured right sinus of Valsalva rupture into the right ventricle, accompanied by a supracristal VSD in a 64-year-old female patient, which was successfully managed through surgical intervention. This case underscores the complex management of a sinus of Valsalva rupture in conjunction with a congenital heart defect.

This case report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE criteria[6].

Case presentation

We present the case of a 64-year-old patient that presented to the Emergency Department (ED) of our institute with acute onset Dyspnea and was found to have a ruptured right sinus of Valsalva aneurysm into the right ventricle, along with an associated membranous VSD. She had complaints of mild dyspnea for 2 years with varying levels of severity, increasing with exertion, but had not undergone evaluation.

The patient was a known case of VSD since childhood but the parents had refused to get it treated due to a lack of symptoms and a host of other personal reasons. Our patient also had a history of total thyroidectomy and was on thyroxine. Two days prior to presentation, she developed sudden shortness of breath that partially resolved with rest. The next morning, she visited another institute, where an Echocardiogram revealed a reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) of 35%, a dilated left atrium, suspected supracristal VSD, and a ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm. Although urgent admission was advised, she left against medical advice and later presented to our institute.

On arrival at the Cardiac Emergency Room, her vital signs were mostly normal with an elevated systolic blood pressure of 145/69 mm Hg, and an oxygen saturation of 97% on 2 liters of oxygen via nasal cannula. Physical examination revealed bilateral equal air entry and a grade III pansystolic murmur. Peripheral pulses were well felt, and there were signs of heart failure including peripheral edema and jugular venous distension was present.

Repeat transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated significant left ventricular dilation with an LV internal end-diastolic dimension of 59 mm (normal: 38–52 mm), LV internal end-systolic dimension of 41 mm (normal: 20–35 mm), and interventricular septum thickness of 8 mm. The left atrium was severely dilated with a volume index of 61 ml/m2 (normal: <34 ml/m2). The aortic root measured 35 mm. Left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) stroke volume was reduced at 28.0 cm3. Importantly, color Doppler imaging confirmed a left-to-right shunt across the membranous ventricular septum and a ruptured right sinus of Valsalva at the Sinotubular junction, into the right ventricle. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure was estimated at 48 mm Hg, indicating moderate pulmonary hypertension.

She was admitted to the Cardiac Care Unit for optimization and further evaluation. She underwent a left heart catheterization (LHC) which was normal and pre-operative preparation for surgical repair of both the ruptured sinus and the VSD. The patient underwent repair of the ruptured sinus of Valsalva and closure of the VSD using a double-patch technique. Following median sternotomy and bicaval cannulation, the aorta was cross-clamped, and an aortotomy was performed. Antegrade ostial cardioplegia was administered through the right and left coronary ostia.

Intraoperative inspection revealed a large ruptured sinus of Valsalva arising from the right coronary sinus and extending into the right ventricle. A sizable subaortic VSD was also identified, located just beneath a thickened and calcified right coronary cusp, while the remaining aortic valve leaflets appeared normal (Fig. 1). The VSD and the ruptured sinus of Valsalva were closed separately, utilizing Dacron and bovine pericardial patches, respectively, secured with interrupted Ethibond pledgetted sutures (Fig. 2). Static water testing of the aortic valve demonstrated excellent leaflet coaptation, with no evidence of regurgitation. The patient was weaned off cardiopulmonary bypass uneventfully. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography confirmed the integrity of the repair, showing no residual shunt, no aortic regurgitation, and preserved biventricular function.

The postoperative course was smooth. The patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout recovery, with no arrhythmias or low-output syndrome, and was successfully discharged in good condition after 6 days of hospital stay.

On early follow-up transthoracic echocardiography performed at 1 month, there was no recurrence of the ruptured sinus of Valsalva, no residual VSD shunt, and no aortic regurgitation. Left ventricular systolic function remained preserved, confirming the durability of the repair.

Discussion

Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SOVA) is a significant and rare cardiac anomaly, accounting for approximately 0.1–3.5% of congenital heart defects. It is characterized by localized dilation of the aortic root between the aortic valve annulus and the sinotubular junction. The etiology is predominantly congenital, resulting from incomplete fusion of the aortic media with the annulus fibrosus, although acquired forms have been reported, secondary to conditions like infective endocarditis, trauma, atherosclerosis, and connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome[1,2]. Our case was a ruptured SOVA, which, in itself, is rare as just over a third of all SOVA’s rupture and this case was also seen to be associated with a VSD.

The uniqueness of this case lies in the convergence of several uncommon and high-risk features. These include a markedly delayed age at presentation, the coexistence of a membranous VSD, and the presence of a rare cardiac pathology that progressed to an even rarer and potentially catastrophic complication. Together with the inherent diagnostic complexity posed by overlapping clinical and echocardiographic findings, these factors clearly distinguish this case from the majority of reports available in the literature[5].

Notably, our patient presented at a significantly older age than the mean age of onset typically described for ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysms, which is approximately 40 years. Presentation in advanced age is uncommon and may reflect prolonged compensatory mechanisms or delayed diagnosis, ultimately predisposing to acute decompensation. Furthermore, the requirement for emergency surgical intervention in an elderly patient substantially increases perioperative risk due to reduced physiological reserve and a higher burden of comorbidities[7,8].

Furthermore, previous literature has shown there to be an interesting correlation between SOVAs and VSDs- an association seen in our patient. Approximately 30-50% of SOVA cases are associated with a VSD, which is thought to facilitate aneurysms because the ventricular septum and the right and noncoronary cusps share an embryonic origin and are congenitally weak in patients with VSD, which leads to weakened walls that are easily involved in aneurysm formation[9]. The VSD seen in our patient was identified just below the right coronary cusp of the Aortic valve, which is a typical location for Supracristal VSDs that can cause prolapse of the aortic cusp and regurgitation if left untreated. The surgical intervention in such patients will require placement of a patch, which was done in our patient[10,11].

The main form of VSD that is associated with RSOVA is the supracristal VSD, which is found in up to 90% of cases involving the Right Coronary Sinus. While there may be other genetic and environmental predispositions that lead to this association, there is a higher prevalence of this association in Asian populations, which may explain the higher incidence of ruptures in Asian populations, which serves as a possible hypothesis regarding the significantly higher rates of RSOVA’s in Asian populations as compared to others[12,13].

While many patients with SOVA remain asymptomatic, the risk of aneurysmal rupture into adjacent cardiac chambers presents a serious clinical concern. The rupture incidence remains low, with only 0.09% of the general population affected, and around 35% of SOVA cases progressing to rupture over time[3,4] . The clinical presentation of a ruptured SOVA largely depends on the site of rupture. One in four patients will present with acute symptoms of dyspnea, shortness of breath, chest pain and fatigue as seen in our patient. The severity of the shunt, its location and presence of associated lesions will determine the severity at time of presentation. The right sinus of Valsalva is most frequently involved, with it typically rupturing into the right ventricle or right atrium, leading to the formation of a continuous left-to-right shunt. This pathophysiology leads to acute right ventricular volume overload, a higher pulmonary circulation and may progress to left sided dysfunction. In our case, the rupture occurred into the right ventricle which is consistent with literature that states a higher probability (60-90%) of ruptures into the right sided chambers[14,15]. A VSD, as seen in our case, will exacerbate the left to right shunting, pulmonary hypertension and heart failure symptoms which will also cause more severe symptoms as evidenced in our patient’s progressive dyspnea and left ventricular dilation.

The diagnosis of a ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm is challenging due to a host of reasons. Firstly, the rarity of the condition alongside its non-specific symptoms often causes a misdiagnosis of Pulmonary Embolism and other pulmonary complications. Secondly, in patients like ours, distinguishing flow abnormalities due to ruptured SOVA from those caused by a VSD can be challenging. Furthermore, the continuous murmur of a ruptured SOVA contrasts with the pansystolic murmur typical of an isolated VSD. Therefore, alongside using Echocardiography – both transthoracic and transesophageal – which remains the gold standard for diagnosing SOVA and its complications, Color Doppler imaging is essential for identifying abnormal flow patterns and diagnosing the severity of shunting. In a recent study of 36 patients, “all had surgical confirmation of the anatomic defects. The location of the SVA was correctly determined in 97% of patients, and the associated presence of a VSD was correctly determined in 79% of the studies”[16].Therefore, concluding the viability of using colored Doppler in such cases. Moreover, advanced imaging modalities, including cardiac CT and MRI, provide complementary information, especially in complex cases or for surgical planning[17,18].

Managing such a rare and severe condition under emergent circumstances further compounded the surgical and anesthetic challenges. Despite these risks, timely recognition and decisive surgical management were pivotal in achieving a favorable outcome. This case therefore underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for complex congenital cardiac anomalies even in older patients, as well as the critical role of prompt multidisciplinary intervention in optimizing outcomes

Management of ruptured SOVA requires prompt surgical intervention. It is accepted that all aneurysms, whether ruptured or unruptured, should be operated upon. Surgical techniques typically involve patch repair of the aneurysmal rupture site, along with concurrent closure of any associated VSD. Several series have demonstrated excellent surgical outcomes, with significant improvements in symptoms and cardiac function post-repair[3,4]. In recent years, percutaneous closure techniques using occluder devices have emerged as alternatives in selected cases, offering less invasive options for high-risk surgical candidates. However, open surgical repair remains the gold standard, especially in the presence of complex anatomy or concomitant lesions like VSD[19].

Prognosis following successful surgical repair is generally favorable, with low recurrence rates and normalization of cardiac hemodynamics. Nonetheless, long-term follow-up with echocardiography is advised to monitor for residual shunts, aneurysm recurrence, or valvular complications[20].

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for SOVA in patients presenting with unexplained continuous murmurs and heart failure symptoms, particularly in regions with higher prevalence. Timely diagnosis and definitive surgical management are crucial for preventing adverse outcomes.
