Is Burn Center Admission Necessary After Home Oxygen Ignition Injury?
Authors: Hope E Werenski, Anju Saraswat, James H Holmes, John K Bailey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccidental ignition of home oxygen does not mandate emergent intubation, but due to the thermal component of the injury, patients are initially directed toward burn units. To identify potential benefit associated with admission to an American Burn Association (ABA)-verified burn center, the local registry was queried from January 2016 to May 2022. Charts were inspected for additional data related to the pattern of the patient’s injuries, comorbidities, and hospital course. We compared patients admitted to the burn service with those primarily cared for by non-burn services. A total of 48 adult patients were admitted with burn injuries associated with home oxygen use. Of the 28 patients intubated on admission, 19 were managed by the burn service, and 9 by non-burn services. There were no differences in ventilator days, ICU days, total length of stay, or mortality. Of the 20 patients admitted without intubation, the burn service managed 7, and non-burn services managed 13. These 2 groups had no identified differences in ICU days, total length of stay, or mortality. This single-center review found no outcome differences between patients cared for in an ABA-verified burn center and those managed by non-burn services following home oxygen-related burn injury versus non-burn services for home oxygen ignition injury. In addition, most intubated patients required only short-term ventilation, suggesting intubation may often be avoidable in this population.Keywords: oxygen-related burns, smoking and oxygen use, facial burns, inhalation injury

INTRODUCTION

Since the 1970s, home oxygen therapy has been available for the supportive treatment of patients with chronic lung disease.1 However, one of the known risks of home oxygen therapy, particularly for patients who smoke, is the fire risk associated with ignition sources near home oxygen. It is estimated that over 1000 injuries occur annually due to home oxygen ignition accidents.2 As home oxygen prescriptions have risen, so too have burn injuries from accidental ignition.3

Ignition sources such as sparks or open flames near a patient’s oxygen cannula or face mask may result in an injury pattern of flash burn to the face, neck, or upper body (Figure 1).4 While patients may be intubated for suspected inhalation injury and airway control, the typical injury pattern associated with the accidental ignition of home oxygen does not mandate emergent intubation. However, due to the thermal component of the injury, patients are initially directed toward admission to burn units.

At Atrium Health Wake Forest Baptist Medical Center (AHWFB), patients with this injury pattern may be admitted to either the American Burn Association (ABA)-verified burn center or non-burn services (NBSs) such as the medical intensive care unit. The recent stress applied to the healthcare system by COVID-19 has highlighted the potential benefit of flexibility in the disposition of injured patients. Therefore, we aimed to determine if there is a demonstrable benefit from admission to an ABA-verified burn center for patients presenting with home oxygen ignition injuries.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a single-institution retrospective review of our institution’s ABA-verified burn center registry. We included all adult patients admitted to AHWFB with burn injuries due to the ignition of home oxygen between January 2016 and May 2022. Patients were identified using our registry (Trauma One, Version 4.21, Lancet Technology, Inc.) with mechanism search term “home oxygen.” Patients with burns unrelated to home oxygen or with no burn injury were excluded. Individual patient charts were inspected for discrete data points related to the general descriptor of the patients’ injuries, comorbidities, and hospital course.

The number of patient comorbidities was reported as a composite variable, which included prior myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, prior cerebrovascular accident, dementia, chronic pulmonary disease, rheumatologic disease, peptic ulcer disease, liver disease, diabetes, moderate to severe renal disease, cancer without metastasis, leukemia, lymphoma, metastatic solid tumor, and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). Patients intubated at or near the time of admission included those intubated in the field, in the emergency department, or at an outside hospital. Admitting NBSs included hospitalist, medicine, nephrology, neurology, and trauma services.

We compared patients admitted to the burn service (BS) with those primarily cared for by NBS teams. We compared categorical data with the chi-squared test and continuous data with independent samples t-tests. Categorical data were reported as frequency and percent. Continuous data were reported as mean with standard deviation. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

This study was approved by our local institutional review board. The institutional review board also approved waiver of consent, given the retrospective nature of the study.

RESULTS

From January 2016 to May 2022, 48 adult patients with burn injuries associated with home oxygen use were admitted to our institution. All study patients required home oxygen therapy for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease with an average baseline home oxygen requirement of 3.25 L/min. Of the 48 admissions for home oxygen ignition injuries, ignition by cigarette smoking accounted for many accidents (85.4%), followed by open flame (12.5%) and unknown source of ignition (2.1%). The patients were divided into those intubated at or near the time of admission and those managed without intubation.

The 28 study patients intubated at or near the time of admission were managed by the BS (n = 19) or NBSs (n = 9). Intubated patients cared for by the BS were older (BS 70.4 y vs NBS 61.4 y, P = .02). However, sex, race, and mean number of comorbidities were similar between BS and NBS patients (Table 1). There was no statistically significant difference in mean percentage of total body surface area (% TBSA) (BS 5.5% vs NBS 1.6%, P = .27) or mean % TBSA of full-thickness burns (BS 2.0% vs 0.1%, P = .22). Burns were limited to the face in 52.6% of intubated patients on the BS and 66.7% on NBS (Table 2). There was no difference in burn distribution between BS and NBS cohorts. Complications while intubated included pleural effusion (n = 1) and pneumonia (n = 2) on the BS and pneumonia (n = 1) on NBS. The BS performed 2 bronchoscopies, and NBS performed 4. No tracheotomies were performed on either service. The mean number of ventilator days was similar (BS 2.0 vs NBS 3.8, P = .92), with 16 BS patients and 4 NBS patients extubated in 2 days or less. There were no statistically significant differences in mean intensive care unit (ICU) days (BS 6.5 vs NBS 6.0, P = .88), total length of stay (LOS) (BS 9.8 days vs NBS 12.1 days, P = .63) or in-hospital mortality (BS 21.1% vs NBS 0.0%, P = .071) between intubated patients on the BS and NBS (Table 2).

Of the 20 patients admitted without intubation, the BS managed 7 of these patients, and NBSs managed 13. Age, sex, race, and mean number of comorbidities were similar between BS and NBS patients (Table 3). Patients managed without intubation admitted to the BS had greater mean % TBSA burns (BS 9.0% vs NBS 1.4%, P = .04), but mean % TBSA full-thickness burns were similar (BS 5.6% vs 0.0%, P = .10). Burns were limited to the face in 14.3% of non-intubated patients on the BS and 84.6% on NBS (Table 4). There were no statistically significant differences in mean ICU days (BS 6.6 vs NBS 1.3, P = .10), total LOS (BS 10.6 days vs NBS 5.7, P = .30), or in-hospital mortality (BS 0.0% vs NBS 0.0%) between patients managed without intubation on the BS and NBS (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

With the increased prescription of home oxygen therapy in recent decades, there has been a synchronous increase in burn injuries secondary to the ignition of home oxygen. Although the typical injury pattern associated with the accidental ignition of home oxygen does not mandate emergent intubation, many patients are initially directed toward admission to burn units due to the thermal component of the injury. Therefore, determining if these injured patients benefit from admission to an ABA-verified burn center may help facilitate hospital disposition.

In this single-institution study, we compared patients with home oxygen ignition injuries admitted to the BS with those primarily cared for by NBS teams. Of the patients intubated at or near the time of admission, there was no statistically significant difference in mean ventilator days, ICU days, total LOS, or in-hospital mortality between the BS and NBSs. Similarly, of the patients managed without intubation, there were no statistically significant differences in mean ICU days, total LOS, or in-hospital mortality between the BS and NBSs.

A relatively low number of verified burn centers in the US serve large geographic areas.5 As a result, patients are often assessed and triaged at local hospitals before being transported by ground or air to these larger centers for specialized burn care. The American Burn Association has designed guidelines to aid in burn center referral, which includes referral for patients with burns involving the face and suspected inhalation injury.6 Although many of the patients in this study fit within these referral guidelines, the similar outcomes between the burn center and NBSs at our institution highlight the potential to care for these home oxygen ignition injuries at institutions without designated burn centers.

The COVID-19 pandemic has stressed the importance of admission flexibility and resource allocation, which can be applied in various healthcare settings.7 Given the similar outcomes in care between BS and NBSs for these patients, there is the potential to consider bed availability when admitting patients with home oxygen ignition injuries, especially those with low % TBSA that do not require surgical intervention. In addition to in-hospital resource allocation, transport to institutions with ABA-verified burn centers can incur substantial costs to both patients and hospital systems.8 Besides the financial burden, caring for patients closer to home may also improve social factors such as family support and discharge planning.9

Although facial burns raise concern for inhalation injury, our data and prior literature suggest most of these cases do not involve the lower airway. Inhalation injury occurs by breathing in thermal or chemical irritants that damage the respiratory tract or lung parenchyma resulting in significant morbidity and mortality.10 Unnecessary intubation for airway protection without signs of respiratory distress is not uncommon, as singed facial or nasal hair may be interpreted as a possible inhalation injury.4 However, not all patients with burns to the face require intubation. The ignition of home oxygen typically results in a flash burn, often involving the face, without significant airway involvement that is more commonly seen in severe burns with associated smoke inhalation. In our study, approximately 60% of patients were intubated for suspected inhalation injury, with the majority requiring only brief periods of mechanical ventilation. In alignment with previous literature on home oxygen ignition injuries, most of these injured patients likely did not require intubation for true airway injury, arguing for the potential to avoid unnecessary intubations.4,11,12

The majority of patients prescribed home oxygen therapy have been long-time smokers.11 Despite warnings of the dangers while smoking on oxygen, up to 50% of home oxygen users continue to smoke.1,3,13 Prevention is key in decreasing the number of home oxygen ignition incidents leading to injury, death, and house fires. Current efforts include smoking cessation, oxygen tubing fire-breaks, and home risk assessments.2 However, more assertive warnings and prevention efforts are needed to decrease the prevalence of home oxygen ignition accidents.3

Our study is limited by its retrospective, single-center design and relatively small sample size, which increases the risk for type II error and restricts generalizability. As such, the reader is cautioned to view the findings with appropriate skepticism. However, this review is supportive of the potential to safely rely on other intensivists as a force multiplier. Larger, multi-institutional data are needed to validate these findings. In addition, this study did not include patients who died at the scene or local hospitals before transport to our institution. Furthermore, our study is limited by sample size. Although no significant differences in outcomes were noted between care provided by our ABA-verified burn center and that by NBSs at AHWFB, further evaluation is needed to confirm preliminary studies. In addition, the comparison of care to the burn center is that of a tertiary care center. While we believe care for patients with home oxygen ignition injuries is possible at primary hospitals, a future assessment of their comfort level to do so is needed.

Finally, it is recognized that of the unintended consequences associated with triaging select patients away from burn center admission. If consistently applied, this strategy could reduce capture of these cases in burn registries, thereby affecting national data quality. It could also diminish opportunities for prevention campaigns and outreach, raising the risk for an “out of mind” effect. While ABA verification requirements stipulate that at least 95% of patients with burn injuries must be admitted under burn center services, which helps safeguard against widespread underreporting, continued vigilance is needed to ensure that registry completeness and prevention efforts are not compromised. At the author’s center, all of these patients with burn injuries were captured for data and further burn prevention—regardless of the unit they were discharged from.

Our findings also raise the question of how these data may be used to develop a structured triage framework for referring facilities. Currently, admission decisions often involve nuanced discussions between referring providers and burn center staff, balancing clinical needs, reassurance, and resource stewardship. A standardized approach informed by data has the potential to reduce unnecessary transfers while maintaining patient safety and ensuring appropriate use of burn center resources.

CONCLUSIONS

These findings support more flexible triage and admission decisions for patients with home oxygen-related burns, particularly in settings with limited burn center capacity. This single-center review revealed no difference in outcomes between patients cared for in an ABA-verified burn center versus NBSs for home oxygen ignition injury. From a care perspective and resource utilization, the outcomes appear similar. Further, we note that patients required only brief periods of mechanical ventilation—suggesting the opportunity to avoid intubation in these patients. Prevention efforts, like those in the burn center, need to continue, and further studies must be done to confirm these preliminary findings.
