Impact of postpartum urinary and anal incontinence on patient-reported quality of life and risk of depressive disorder in women receiving primary and secondary care in the Netherlands: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Ellen Mooren, Anke G Posthumus, Anneke Steensma, Jacoba van der Kooy, Leonie Speksnijder
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore how urinary (UI) and anal incontinence (AI) affect various aspects of quality of life (QoL) and the risk of depression 6 months postpartum, using patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).DesignProspective cohort study.Participants and settingWomen who gave birth between 2020 and 2022 within a local obstetric collaborative network in the Netherlands, including 13 midwifery practices, 5 maternity care organisations at the primary care level, and 1 secondary teaching hospital.InterventionsProspectively collecting data by using the questionnaires of the International Consortium for Health Outcome Measures Pregnancy and Childbirth set at five moments during pregnancy and postpartum.Primary and secondary outcome measuresAssociations between UI, AI, QoL and likelihood of depression based on PROMs collected from questionnaire 6 months postpartum.ResultsOf the 663 women who completed the 6-month postpartum questionnaire, 79 women had severe UI, 71 had severe AI and 45 experienced both. These women reported significantly lower QoL as measured by PROMIS-10: 34 (IQR 30.5–38.5), 34 (IQR 30–39.5), and 32 (IQR 27–35), respectively, compared with a score of 37 (IQR 33–42) for the total population. Additionally, the positive rate for depression screening was notably higher among these groups, with rates of 6%, 10% and 18%, respectively, compared with 3% for the overall group.ConclusionsIn this observational cohort study, we found that severe UI and/or AI 6 months postpartum significantly impact QoL and increase the likelihood of depression, as indicated by PROMs. Our findings emphasise the importance of screening, evaluation and treatment of UI and/or AI to improve the QoL and reduce the risk of depressive disorders for postpartum women.

Introduction

In recent years, the integration of patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) into healthcare has gained increasing attention, aiming to enhance the quality of care at an individual level. PROMs and PREMs offer a unique perspective, shedding light on issues that are most relevant to patients, which are often overlooked by conventional clinical quality indicators.1 2 Acknowledging the significance of patient-centred care, the International Consortium for Health Outcome Measures (ICHOM) has developed indicator sets designed to prioritise such patient-centred outcomes.3 One of these sets, the Pregnancy and Childbirth questionnaire has been translated and validated for implementation in the Netherlands.46 This comprehensive tool collects clinical outcome data alongside PROMs and PREMs.7 8 Data is gathered at five points during pregnancy and postpartum, with pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) as one of the key outcomes.

PFD, encompassing conditions such as anal incontinence (AI) and urinary incontinence (UI), is a major concern during the postpartum period. Population-based studies report that 3%–4% of women experience new symptoms of AI after pregnancy, and up to 25% of first-time mothers suffer from faecal incontinence (FI) at 6 weeks postpartum. Yet, only 14% of symptomatic women seek medical care, indicating an underappreciation of the issue. Despite this, the emotional, physical, social and mental impact of these conditions is significant.913

Similarly, UI is prevalent postpartum, with rates reaching up to 30%.14 15 However, there is a gap between the prevalence of UI and-help seeking behaviour of women postpartum, as both healthcare professionals and women accept it as being a result of the delivery.16 Yet previous research has shown that postpartum UI is leading to considerable reductions in overall quality of life (QoL), affecting QoL domains including general health perception, physical limitations, daily life activities, psychological/emotional/mental and social aspects and sexuality.17

Mental health problems, like depression, are a prevalent condition in life with impact on QoL. The aetiology of mental health problems is multifactorial, including family history, exposure to stress, traumatic experiences, unfavourable socioeconomic conditions, lack of sleep and presence of medical comorbidities such as PFD.18 A metaanalysis by Molina et al showed that depression affect two in ten women with PFD.19

Despite the documented prevalence of PFD and its impact on QoL and risk of depression, these conditions remain insufficiently explored in the postpartum period.

Recognising this gap, our study aims to assess the impact of UI and AI on various dimensions of QoL and the likelihood of depression in women receiving primary and secondary care in the Netherlands, 6 months postpartum, using PROMs.

Methods

This was a prospective cohort study including women who gave birth between 2020 and 2022 within a local obstetric collaborative network (OCN) in the Netherlands. All these women were asked to answer the questionnaires of the ICHOM Pregnancy and Childbirth set, to collect PROMs and PREMs. The five questionnaires of this ICHOM set cover various domains relevant to patients. This study focuses on PFD, health-related QoL and likelihood of depression, which were explored in the 6-month postpartum questionnaire.

Overall study population and data collection

The OCN includes 13 midwifery practices, five maternity care organisations at the primary care level, and one secondary teaching hospital (the Amphia hospital). Each year, about 3500 women deliver in this OCN. During their initial booking visit, women were invited to participate in the ICHOM Pregnancy and Childbirth questionnaires as part of standard care. Informed consent to use their data for research purposes was obtained and registered in their electronic health record. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee (METC) of Erasmus Medical Centre, Rotterdam (MEC-2024–0509).

Women were invited to complete digital questionnaires of the ICHOM Pregnancy and Childbirth set that covered maternal health, including clinical outcome data (such as mortality and morbidity), PROMs (addressing aspects like mother-to-infant bonding, breastfeeding, mental health, healthcare-related QoL and PFD) and PREMs (evaluating satisfaction with care and healthcare responsiveness). The questionnaires were sent at five different time points; at 11 weeks of pregnancy, 28 weeks of pregnancy, the first week after birth, 6 weeks postpartum and 6 months postpartum (figure 1). The PROMs relevant to this study (PFD, QoL, likelihood of depression) are answered in the 6-month postpartum questionnaire. If necessary, a reminder was sent after 2 weeks. Women who completed at least one questionnaire were included. Those who delivered before 24 weeks were excluded. The questionnaire data were linked to clinical data (such as maternal age, gestational age, BMI, ethnicity, mode of delivery, perineal laceration and known risk factors for incontinence (asthma and inflammatory bowel disease) using anonymous identifiers. A sample size calculation estimated that a minimum of 370 participants was required, based on an estimated response rate of 20% from the approximately 10 000 deliveries during the study period, using a confidence level of 95%, margin of error of 0.05 and proportion of 0.5. We used a general formula for estimating a required sample size for proportions typically used for estimating proportions in cross-sectional studies. Although this is a cohort study, this approach was chosen due to the absence of specific prior data on expected outcome distributions in this cohort. The conservative estimate of 0.5 yields the maximum required sample size, ensuring sufficient power regardless of the true proportion.

PROMs of primary outcome

The five questionnaires of the ICHOM set (figure 1) cover various domains relevant to patients. This study focuses on PFD, health-related QoL and likelihood of depression 6 months after delivery, all assessed using internationally standardised questionnaires. The questionnaire on PFD begins with a screening question for UI and AI. Positive responses lead to further questions using the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Short Form (ICIQ-SF) for UI20 and the Wexner for AI.2123

The ICIQ-SF score ranges from 0 to 21, with 0 indicating no urine loss and 21 indicating complete UI. A score ≥6 is considered abnormal. The questionnaire distinguishes between stress urinary incontinence (SUI) and urge urinary incontinence (UUI). SUI is identified by leakage during coughing, sneezing or physical activity, while UUI refers to leakage before reaching the toilet.

The Wexner score ranges from 0 (perfect AI) to 20 (total AI). According to ICHOM, an abnormal Wexner score is defined as a score of 1 or higher on specific questions (online supplemental appendix 1). Since this cutoff does not differentiate mild and severe AI in our data, we used an alternative scoring system, the St Mark’s Incontinence Score (SIMS). In the SIMS, FI is defined as a score of 1 or higher for liquid or solid stool incontinence, and flatus incontinence is defined by a score of 2 or higher for difficulty controlling gas. This scoring system is also possible in the Wexner score and therefore used in our analyses.

The PROMIS-10 questionnaire assesses QoL across 10 questions covering physical health, physical functioning, general mental health, emotional distress, satisfaction with social activities and relationships, ability to carry out usual social activities and roles, pain, fatigue and overall QoL. Scores range from 10 to 50,24 with a score of 19 or lower and/or a pain score of 3 or higher considered abnormal (online supplemental appendix 1).

The Patient Health Questionnaire two score (PHQ-2) is a screening score for likelihood of depression, with scores ranging from 0 to 6. A score of ≥3 suggests a higher likelihood of major depressive disorder.25 If this cutoff is reached, further assessment is conducted using the Edinburgh postnatal depression scale (EPDS), which ranges from 0 to 30. An abnormal EPDS score is defined as a total score of≥10 and/or a score of 2 or 3 in one subquestion (question 10).26

For each domain, an alert score was defined based on existing literature or, if not available, determined by a national multidisciplinary project team, guided by expert opinion (online supplemental appendix 1).

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics were used for patient characteristics and clinical outcomes for all women who filled out at least one questionnaire. The response rate is described for each questionnaire. Our primary endpoints (UI, AI, QoL, likelihood of depression) were extracted from questionnaire 5 (completed at 6 months postpartum). Baseline characteristics were provided for the women who completed this questionnaire. Continuous variables were categorised for clinical interpretation, and statistical significance was analysed using the chi-square test. Comparison of baseline characteristics between responders and non-responders to questionnaire 5 was performed to examine potential selection bias. For variables with significant differences in baseline characteristics, additionally, a univariate logistic regression was performed for the primary outcome measures.

We reported the frequencies of UI, AI and a combination of both at 6 months postpartum. Additionally, we described the means and SD for ICIQ-SF and/or Wexner scores, along with the frequencies of abnormal ICIQ-SF and Wexner scores. Normality of PROMIS-10 scores and EPDS was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and the Shapiro–Wilk tests. Normally distributed data were analysed using Student’s t test, while skewed data were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The PHQ-2 score was dichotomised using a cutoff score of 3. Frequencies and percentages were reported, and the chi-square tests were used for analysis. Relative risks for positive PHQ-2 scores were calculated by dividing the proportion of women with high scores in each symptom group (UI, AI or both) by the overall proportion in the total population. The threshold for significance was p<0.05 for all analyses. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 28.0.1.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL, ISA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this research.

Results

Study population

During the study period, 11 828 women delivered in the OCN, and all women were invited to complete the ICHOM questionnaires. Of these, 2889 women (24%) filled out at least one questionnaire and were included in this study. Of these women, 663 (23%) filled out questionnaire 5 (6 months postpartum). The response rates for questionnaire 1 to 4 were 73%, 74%, 50% and 37%, respectively, among the 2889 women included (figure 2). Of these women, 311 (11%) responded to all five questionnaires. Table 1 shows baseline characteristics. The population that completed questionnaire five differed statistically from those who did not respond to questionnaire five in several ways: there were more Caucasian women, more women delivered with a midwife (either in an outpatient clinic or at home), different distributions regarding duration of second stage of labour, different mode of delivery (a lower rate of caesarean section) and a higher episiotomy rate. Women with an episiotomy had this in 39% of responders and 35% of non-responders due to instrumental delivery. In univariate analysis, there was no significant effect on the primary outcome measures for these five characteristics.

PROM for UI at 6 months postpartum

The questions about UI were answered by 645 women (97.0%) (table 2). 180 (27.9%) experienced UI at 6 months postpartum. The mean ICIQ-SF score was 6.1 (SD 3.4) and 79 women (12.2%) had an abnormal ICIQ-SF score, indicating severe UI. Symptoms associated with SUI were experienced by 158 women (87.7% of women with UI), 34 women (21.5%) had symptoms for UUI, and 22 women (12.2%) had a combination of SUI and UUI.

PROM for AI at 6 months postpartum

Of the 577 women (87.0%) who answered the questions about AI, 124 women (21.5%) experienced AI (table 2). Among these women, 115 (19.9%) had flatal incontinence, 16 women (2.8%) had FI for solid or liquid stool and eight women (1.4%) reported both. The mean Wexner score was 2.3 (SD 1.5), with 71 scores (57.3%) classified as abnormal. Additionally, 45 (7.8%) women experienced a combination of UI and AI, of which 17 women (2.9%) had both an abnormal ICIQ-SF and an abnormal Wexner score.

Quality of life at 6 months postpartum

All women answered the questions about QoL. The median PROMIS-10 score was 37 (IQR 33–42). Three women (0.5%) had an abnormal PROMIS-10 score (table 2).

Women with severe symptoms of UI had a median score of 34 (IQR 31–39) compared with women without an abnormal ICIQ-SF score, median 37 (IQR 33–42) (p<0.001) (figure 3). Women with severe symptoms of AI had a median score of 34 (IQR 30–40), which is significantly lower than women without an abnormal Wexner score (median of 37 (IQR 33–41)) (p<0.001) (figure 4). Women with both severe UI and AI had a median score of 32 (IQR 27–35), compared with 37 (IQR 32, 25–41) for women without this combination of UI and AI (p<0.001) (figure 5).

Likelihood of depression at 6 months postpartum

The response rate for the PHQ-2 was 96%. Overall, 22 women (3.3%) had an abnormal score (>3), suggesting likely depression (table 2). The incidence of high PHQ-2 scores was significantly increased among those with UI, AI and a combination of both, at 6.3%, 9.9% and 18%, respectively. This corresponds to a relative risk of 1.9, 3.0 and 5.3 compared with the overall risk for high PHQ-2 score. The incidence and relative risk of high PHQ-2 scores across groups is shown in table 3 and visualised in figure 6. For women who scored high on the screening question, the EPDS was also administered. The median EPDS score did not differ statistically between the subgroups.

Discussion

Main findings

In this observational cohort study, we found that severe symptoms of UI and/or AI 6 months postpartum significantly negatively affect health-related QoL. Furthermore, the presence of UI and/or AI significantly increases the likelihood of depression.

Our findings regarding the incidences of UI and AI were consistent with the literature, which reports incidences of 27% for UI and 19% for AI.915 The reported PROMIS-10 scores were comparable to those found by Depla et al,5 who observed a median score of 37 at 6 months postpartum. However, to our knowledge, the impact of UI and AI on postpartum QoL based on PROMs has not been researched.

6 months postpartum, a study of Handa et al found that both FI and UI symptoms significantly diminished overall health ratings and negatively impacted various dimensions of QoL measured with the Medical Outcomes Study Short Form Health Survey. The authors emphasised the importance of routinely screening for AI and UI in postpartum women and providing evaluation and treatment for those affected.15

Our results revealed lower QoL (PROMIS-10) scores and higher likelihood of depression (PHQ-2) scores in women with severe UI and/or AI, highlighting the significant impact of these symptoms on maternal well-being 6 months postpartum.

Interpretation

These findings underscore the need for further research and targeted interventions. Although the differences in median score for PROMIS-10 were small, the clinical relevance of these findings warrants further investigation. A systematic review of Terwee et al concluded that small changes in PROMIS scores (minimal important change (MIC)) can be clinically important. The systematic review showed that MIC values for PROMIS-10 scores vary widely and depend on the study population. For example, MICs tend to be higher in patients undergoing surgery than for non-surgical interventions. However, there is currently insufficient evidence to define MIC thresholds specifically for postpartum women.27 As such, while the observed difference is statistically significant, we cannot confirm whether it exceeds a clinically meaningful threshold for this specific population. Moreover, only three women in our cohort had an abnormal PROMIS-10 score, limiting the statistical power to detect meaningful group differences in QoL. PHQ-2 scores showed a more pronounced difference. However, interpretation should take into account that the PHQ-2 is a screening tool and absolute numbers of women with abnormal scores (n=22) were as low. We should therefore be cautious in interpreting this result as clinically significant at the population level.

The ICHOM Pregnancy and Childbirth set, developed and standardised both nationally and internationally, evaluates PROMs and PREMs considered relevant to patients, including PFD. Our findings underscore the importance of effective screening, evaluation and treatment of UI and/or AI to enhance the QoL and risk for depression for postpartum women. While the data from ICHOM questionnaires at 6 months postpartum are currently mostly utilised at a meso-level for research purposes and benchmarking, evaluating individual-level impacts in the future is essential for actionable insights and personalised care improvements. Our insights emphasise the importance of routinely assessing UI and AI postpartum, a practice often overlooked, and prioritising prompt treatment and support due to their profound impact. Treatment and support are crucial, as not all cases may require active intervention, but guidance is essential for managing these conditions effectively. Furthermore, integrating evaluations of UI and AI with assessments of depression and QoL is crucial for tailored intervention strategies where needed. In conclusion, addressing UI and/or AI comprehensively postpartum is essential for improving both the QoL and likelihood of depression outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has a number of strengths and limitations that merit discussion. A major strength of our study is the inclusion of a large prospective cohort of women, enhancing the reliability and generalisability of our results. We used standardised validated instruments for patient-reported outcome measures. Despite a substantial loss to follow-up, with 23% of the initial cohort completing the 6-month postpartum questionnaire, this group is representative, with minor differences in baseline characteristics compared with women who did not respond to the 6 month postpartum questionnaire.

A decreasing response rate from pregnancy to the postpartum period is a common trend in most research studies, and boosting these numbers can be challenging. For example, the response rate at 6 months postpartum in the study of Delpla et al was around 2%.4 The ICHOM workgroup selected the 6-month endpoint as a pragmatic compromise; while important outcomes may not emerge until later or can still recover over time, response rates for patient questionnaires decrease over time, making a later endpoint less feasible.4 28 29 Nevertheless, it is crucial to continue encouraging patients to complete these questionnaires over time, as these outcomes have the potential to drive improvements in quality of care. The low response rate 6 months after delivery causes the absolute numbers of certain outcome measures (eg, abnormal PROMIS-10 and PHQ-2 score) to be low, with potential great impact on results and statistical analysis. A low response rate also raises concerns about selection bias with potential underreporting as also reported in other studies.5 30

We found a significant difference in ethnicity in baseline characteristics, with a higher rate of Caucasian women responding to questionnaire 5, which may limit the generalisability of our finding to a more diverse population. This comparison of baseline characteristics between responders and non-responders serves as a sensitivity analysis, allowing us to assess potential selection bias.

The minor differences in baseline characteristics may have influenced our results. It is possible that the lower caesarean section rate influenced the PFD, since caesarean section has a protective effect.31 A higher episiotomy rate and longer duration of second stage might suggest more difficult vaginal deliveries and are therefore associated with a higher risk of PFD.32 33 The significant difference in place of birth, with less hospital deliveries, presumably reflects the lower caesarean section rate.

In addition to the response rate and the limited follow-up time, we cannot exclude the possibility of unaccounted confounding factors influencing the relationship between incontinence and QoL and likelihood of depression, such as a traumatic delivery.

Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insight into the relationship between postpartum PFD and maternal well-being.

Conclusion

Our findings underscore a high prevalence of UI and AI among postpartum women, significantly impacting their QoL and risk of depression at 6 months after delivery. As the severity of incontinence increases, there is a noticeable decline in both aspects, highlighting the urgent need for targeted interventions to enhance the well-being of these women. Healthcare providers, policymakers and researchers must collaborate to implement effective screening, evaluation and treatment protocols to collectively take action to address these challenges and enhance overall maternal healthcare.
