Machine learning-driven development of a novel unfolded protein response-related gene signature for predicting lung adenocarcinoma patient prognosis
Authors: Rui Jiao, Chengyang Wu, Tao Zhang, Hanyu Yan, Weidong He, Zhaoyang Wang, Xiaolong Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common lung cancer histological subtype. Although the unfolded protein response (UPR) has been linked to various human diseases, its role in LUAD remains unclear. To identify UPR-related genes, we applied various methods, including weighted gene co-expression network analysis, differential expression analysis, and multivariate Cox regression. Ten machine learning algorithms were used to construct a UPR-related signature (UPRRS), which was validated using multiple public LUAD datasets. The UPRRS was integrated into a nomogram used in clinical practice for prognosis prediction. We also evaluated predicted drug sensitivity patterns across different risk subgroups. We identified 33 UPR-associated hub genes. A UPRRS was developed through systematic evaluation of 101 machine-learning combinations, exhibiting stable prognostic performance across multiple cohorts. Integration of the UPRRS into a nomogram facilitated the construction of a quantitative prognostic model. Significant differences in biological processes and tumor microenvironment immune cell infiltration were observed between the high- and low-risk UPRRS groups. All five UPRRS genes (ALDH2, FKBP4, KLF4, LAIR1, SIDT2) were validated at the protein level in LUAD cell lines, and FKBP4 was further confirmed by IHC in clinical tissues. Functional experiments showed that FKBP4 knockdown inhibited proliferation, migration, and invasion of A549 and H1975 cells, supporting a potential role for FKBP4 in LUAD progression. Our UPRRS provides a promising tool for prognostic stratification and may offer additional insights into tumor immune microenvironment characterization and therapeutic response prediction in LUAD. Introduction

Lung cancer is the primary contributor to cancer-associated mortality, responsible for approximately 18% of all global cancer-related deaths [1]. Among the various histological types, lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 50% of all lung cancer cases [2]. Despite advances in diagnostic and treatment methods, the lung cancer patient survival rate remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate between 10 and 20% [3, 4]. Targeted therapies and immunotherapies are among the most effective treatments for patients who are diagnosed with advanced-stage lung cancer. However, only a small proportion of patients can benefit from these therapies because of the significant heterogeneity and rapid development of therapeutic resistance associated with lung cancer [5, 6]. Therefore, the identification of new biomarkers with both high specificity and sensitivity is critical for the specific diagnosis, personalized treatment, and reliable prognosis for LUAD patients.

The unfolded protein response (UPR), a crucial signaling mechanism, serves to monitor and maintain the stability and integrity of the proteins within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). This process is active when there is an accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins in the ER [7, 8]. In mammalian cells, the UPR is controlled through three essential ER-resident proteins: activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), and protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) [9]. While these proteins typically remain inactive, they become activated if proteins in the ER are unfolded or not folded properly. When activated, they can then regulate downstream signaling cascades and UPR effectors, thereby reducing the load on the ER. Tumor cells, which often experience rapid growth, are exposed to both internal and external stressors that can compromise protein equilibrium [10].

The UPR is inextricably linked to the development of a variety of human diseases, including neurological diseases, cardiovascular disorders, and cancer [11]. Notably, UPR activation is crucial in fostering drug resistance among cancer cells, highlighting its significance as a primary focus in cancer therapeutic development [12]. ATF6, IRE1, and PERK can regulate the transition between survival-promoting signaling that promotes cell death and are linked to multiple features of cancer [13]. Moreover, UPR-targeted approaches in cancer immunotherapy are becoming increasingly prominent [14]. In clinical settings, the UPR has shown promise in predicting outcomes for acute myeloid leukemia patients [15]. UPR-related markers have also been established for skin melanoma and liver cancer to forecast patient survival and treatment-related outcomes [16, 17]. Thus, the UPR stands out as a promising therapeutic target for various tumor types. A better understanding of UPR signals in oncology and investigating the potential advantages of UPR-targeting therapies may contribute to improving the effectiveness of cancer treatments.

Notably, similar prognostic signatures based on UPR-related pathways have been developed for other cancer types: for instance, a novel UPR-related signature for predicting acute myeloid leukemia (AML) prognosis [18], UPR-associated prognostic signatures for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [19], and novel UPR-related gene signatures for bladder cancer outcome prediction [20]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, UPR-related molecular subtypes have also been linked to the immunogenic landscape and risk stratification [21], while UPR targeting has demonstrated potential in BRAF-mutant colorectal cancer with poor prognosis [22]. However, these models were developed in other cancer types and may not fully capture the interplay between tumor-intrinsic stress responses and the immune microenvironment in LUAD.

Although numerous UPR-related genes have been recognized as biomarkers that can affect tumor progression, no multigene signature has been linked to the UPR for predicting LUAD patient prognosis. To address this gap, we developed a prognostic model incorporating UPR-related genes and established a nomogram for risk assessment. Moreover, we performed expression and functional validation of FKBP4 to explore its potential role in LUAD progression.

Methods

Data acquisition and analysis

For analyzing LUAD data, transcriptomic information and pertinent clinical data of LUAD patients were obtained via The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Cases with deficient or missing clinicopathological information were excluded, leading to the establishment of a refined cohort consisting of 444 TCGA-LUAD individuals. For further mRNA expression analysis, the GSE13213 and GSE72094 datasets were obtained directly from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository. These datasets offer prognostic details for 117 and 398 patients, respectively, which ensured a sufficient sample size. Somatic mutation data in MAF format were likewise retrieved from TCGA. The UPR-related genes were retrieved from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). Batch correction was conducted via the ComBat function from the ‘sva’ R package [23]. Notably, the TCGA dataset was not merged with GEO datasets before batch correction. The preprocessing workflow was performed sequentially as follows:TCGA RNA-seq FPKM expression data from LUAD patients were processed independently with log2 transformation, and ComBat correction was not applied to this cohort;Each GEO microarray dataset was normalized individually, merged into one unified expression matrix, log2-transformed, and then corrected for intra-platform batch effects using ComBat;The batch-corrected pooled GEO matrix was split into two separate validation cohorts, GSE13213 and GSE72094. All prognostic models were trained solely on the TCGA subset and validated on the separated ComBat-adjusted GEO cohorts.

Processing and acquisition of data for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

Here, scRNA-seq results collected from 25 LUAD patients were obtained via Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN) [24]. Data analysis was conducted using the ‘Seurat’ package [25]. For the quality control screening process, cells with mitochondrial gene percentages greater than 20% were discarded, while low-quality and sparsely expressed genes were removed before downstream analysis. The 2,000 most variable genes were subsequently selected for downstream analysis. These quality control and preprocessing procedures were implemented according to previous LUAD scRNA-seq studies [26, 27]. The ‘Harmony’ tool [28]was used to adjust for the effects of batching across the 25 samples. Cell clusters were identified using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions with a resolution of 0.5, then visualized using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Specific gene expression levels were used to characterize the various cell types. The ‘AUCell’ tool was used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) values of the UPR gene set in each cell.

Hub gene identification

Univariate Cox regression analysis was employed to identify important prognostic genes in the TCGA-LUAD dataset. To investigate gene expression differences between normal and cancer samples, the ‘limma’ package [28] was used for RNA differential expression analysis with the TCGA data. Significant differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the following: adjusted P-value < 0.05 and Log2 fold change (FC) > 1. The final set of UPR-related genes was identified by combining the DEGs and those with prognostic value, as well as those identified by Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA). This intersection strategy was applied to enhance model robustness and reproducibility, and reduce false-positive signals in subsequent modeling analysis. While it is relatively stringent to avoid false positives, this design prioritizes the stability, reliability, and reproducibility of the prognostic model, which is crucial for clinical application.

Development and validation of a UPR-related signature (UPRRS)

To avoid unstable and biased performance estimation from a single random split, the TCGA cohort was randomly partitioned into training and testing sets 10 independent times at a 7:3 ratio. The following 10 machine learning algorithms were employed: LASSO regression, Ridge regression, and elastic net using the ‘glmnet’ package [29]; CoxBoost [30]; random survival forest (RSF) [31]; partial least squares for Cox (plsRcox) [32]; supervised principal components (SuperPC) [33]; generalized boosted regression model (GBM) [34]; and survival support vector machines (survival-SVM) [35]. We constructed all 101 possible combinations of these 10 algorithms, including both single-model and pairwise hybrid combinations of feature selection and survival models. We provided an explicit description of the composition logic for the 101 algorithm combinations: the pipeline was built upon 10 base algorithms following a systematic two-stage framework, covering all possible single-model implementations and all pairwise feature-selection-plus-modeling combinations. Exhaustive enumeration was applied to avoid confirmation bias. The 101 pipelines are composed of 10 independent single-algorithm models plus 91 unique two-stage hybrid pipelines, summing to 101 total model combinations. The Elastic Net α grid ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 was used to cover the full regularization spectrum, and three Stepwise Cox directions were adopted for comprehensive variable screening. Hyperparameters were optimized instead of directly using package default configurations. Custom hyperparameter search grids were predefined for each algorithm, and tenfold cross-validation was used to select optimal parameter values on the TCGA training cohort for fair cross-model comparison. For computationally intensive algorithms (RSF, GBM, survival-SVM), we adopted standardized parameters widely validated in survival analysis literatures to balance computational load and comparability. Complete hyperparameter settings, search ranges and tuning strategies for all base algorithms are fully documented in Supplementary Table S1-1. The complete list of all 101 single-algorithm and two-stage hybrid pipelines, with separated feature selection and survival modeling components for each pipeline, is summarized in Supplementary Table S1-2. For two-stage hybrid pipelines combining feature screening and downstream survival modeling, all feature selection and hyperparameter optimization steps were restricted to training folds only to eliminate validation data leakage. This strategy allowed systematic evaluation of feature selection-model combinations to identify the most stable prognostic pipeline. The C-index values for all 101 models across training, test, and external validation cohorts are presented in Fig. 2A for comprehensive performance comparison. Model performance was first evaluated across TCGA training, TCGA testing, and external validation cohorts (GSE13213 and GSE72094) using the concordance index (C-index). To ensure that external datasets did not influence model selection, we implemented a two-stage evaluation strategy: primary ranking was based on multi-cohort performance following Liu et al. [36], while a separate TCGA-only sensitivity analysis was independently conducted after model selection, in which all 101 models were re-ranked using only TCGA training and testing cohorts. This sensitivity analysis was used solely to assess ranking stability and was not involved in model selection (Fig. S2). The LASSO + Stepwise Cox model was selected as the final prognostic model because it ranked among the top-performing pipelines across all evaluated cohorts and showed stable ranking performance in the TCGA-only sensitivity analysis (Fig. S2).

Notably, the optimal model emerged as the combination of LASSO regression and Stepwise Cox regression, achieving an average C-index of 0.645 across cohorts and demonstrating robust predictive performance. The LASSO regression analysis was performed using the ‘glmnet’ R package [29] with tenfold cross-validation to minimize partial likelihood deviation and avoid overfitting (Fig. 3B, C). This step reduced the 33 hub genes to seven genes with non-zero coefficients. Subsequently, Stepwise Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to further refine the gene set, ultimately retaining five core genes (ALDH2, FKBP4, KLF4, LAIR1, SIDT2) for UPRRS construction (Fig. 3D). The regression coefficients of these five genes were as follows: ALDH2 (−4.089 × 10–5), FKBP4 (1.077 × 10–4), KLF4 (2.745 × 10–4), LAIR1 (−3.474 × 10–4), and SIDT2 (−3.431 × 10–4). The UPRRS risk score for each patient was calculated as the linear combination of the expression level of each core gene multiplied by its corresponding regression coefficient.

The TCGA training and testing datasets, along with the GSE13213 and GSE72094 cohorts, were divided into low-risk and high-risk groups. The median cutoff was derived solely from the training set and then applied to the test and external validation sets. All preprocessing steps, including normalization and scaling, were performed exclusively within the training cohort. The derived parameters were applied to testing and external validation cohorts to prevent training data leakage, while external cohorts were not involved in model parameter estimation or model fitting. Risk scores were calculated using coefficients derived solely from the training dataset. To determine whether there was a significant gap in overall survival (OS) between these groups, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed using the ‘survminer’ R package [37], with the log-rank test employed to assess statistical significance (P < 0.05). The timeROC package [38] was used to perform receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis evaluating the UPRRS predictive power for LUAD overall survival.

Cohort-specific C-index and corresponding standard error (SE) were calculated via the survival R package under Cox regression. Time-dependent AUC for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS was estimated with the IPCW method. Two-sided 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for both metrics were generated by normal approximation. We additionally computed the overall mean and inter-cohort standard deviation of C-index across all four cohorts; all relevant quantitative performance metrics are compiled in Supplementary Table 2.

Comparison with published LUAD prognostic signatures

We systematically screened published LUAD prognostic signatures related to pyroptosis, cuproptosis, hypoxia, ferroptosis, autophagy and tumor immunity. Three eligible signatures were retained (Supplementary Table 3 footnote). Signatures with ≥ 50% gene overlap with UPRRS were excluded to reduce redundant comparisons. Original gene lists of included signatures were refitted with multivariate Cox regression on the TCGA-LUAD training cohort to remove analytical bias. C-index across four cohorts (TCGA Training, TCGA Testing, GSE13213, GSE72094) was calculated to compare prognostic discriminatory ability, following a previously described benchmark pipeline [36]. Partial gene sets failed full matching to Affymetrix arrays in two GEO datasets due to platform differences, which hindered complete cross-model performance assessment.

Development of a predictive nomogram using clinical parameters and the UPRRS

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were carried out using the TCGA database to examine if the UPRRS could help independently predict LUAD patient survival outcomes. Numerous factors, such as gender, age, tumor size (T), lymph node condition (N), metastatic dispersal (M), stage of disease progression, and UPRRS risk factors, were combined to develop a predictive nomogram. The nomogram was developed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 repetitions. The precision of the nomogram was assessed using calibration analysis and C-index analysis. Cases with missing clinicopathological information were excluded with a missing rate < 5%, and complete-case analysis was applied to minimize bias.

Enrichment analysis

To understand the various relevant biological functions and pathways in the high-risk and low-risk groups, Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome enrichment analyses were performed using the R program ‘clusterProfiler’ [39]. The KEGG database systematically integrates associative information on genes, pathways, and diseases, serving as a core tool for deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying biological processes [40, 41]. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to uncover the potential regulation mechanisms that may be associated with the UPRRS. The genes were ordered using their log2 fold change values, which were computed via differential expression analysis with the ‘limma’ package [28]. All differential expression and enrichment analyses were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). An adjusted P-value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Immune cell infiltration and immune checkpoint expression analyses

To examine the association between the UPRRS and immune cell milieu within the lung tumor microenvironment (TME), the ESTIMATE algorithm was used. Furthermore, the CIBERSORT algorithm (LM22 signature, 1000 permutations) was utilized to assess relative proportions of 22 immune cell types. To confirm the CIBERSORT results, an XCell algorithm was performed as well. The connection between risks and immune checkpoints was demonstrated through application of the ‘ggstatsplot’ package [42].

Prediction of potential chemotherapeutic drugs

We incorporated RNA-seq data with information on drug sensitivity taken from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database to determine the differences in reaction in patients who are classified as UPRRS-high and -low. Drug sensitivity scores for each patient were estimated using the ‘oncoPredict’ package [43].

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from two LUAD cell lines, A549 and H1975, and an epithelial normal bronchial model, HBE, using a cell-based RNA isolation kit (Solarbio R3901). The RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara DRR037A). Next, qRT-PCR assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Solarbio R9303) with a LightCycler 96 real-time fluorescent Quantitative PCR System (Roche, 5,815,916,001). The data were analyzed using the comparative cycle threshold (Ct) method. The Ct values of each gene were normalized using the GAPDH Ct values. The relevant primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Materials 1.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue samples from tumors and their corresponding non-cancerous counterparts were procured from a group of 10 LUAD patients. These individuals were diagnosed and treated at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Tangdu Hospital of Fourth Military Medical University (Xi’an, China). For patient data, please refer to Supplementary Material 2. Tissue sections were processed with standard deparaffinization, antigen retrieval and endogenous peroxidase inactivation. After blocking, the sections were incubated with primary antibody against FKBP4 (PA5-19,057, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a dilution of 1:1200. Phosphate buffered saline was used to prepare negative controls. The sections were then incubated with biotinylated secondary antibody and HRP-conjugated streptavidin, followed by DAB staining and hematoxylin counterstaining. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, mounted and examined under light microscopy.

Cell culture and lentivirus infection

Human normal bronchial epithelial (HBE) and lung adenocarcinoma (A549, H1975) cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were passaged with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA (Solarbio) at 80–90% confluence, and logarithmic-phase cells were used for experiments.

Lentiviral sh-FKBP4 and sh-NC (Obio Technology, Shanghai) were used to infect A549 and H1975 cells (30–50% confluence) with 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich). 48 h after infection, cells were selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 7–10 days to establish stable knockdown lines. Knockdown efficiency was verified by Western blot.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from HBE, A549 and H1975 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein quantification was conducted via BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal protein loads were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: ALDH2 (1:1000, #78,856, CST), FKBP4 (1:1000, #PA5-19,057, Thermo Fisher Scientific), KLF4 (1:1000, #55,004–1-AP, Proteintech), LAIR1 (1:1000, #25,289, CST), SIDT2 (1:1000, #21,544–1-AP, Proteintech), GRP78 (1:1000, #PA5-34,941, Thermo Fisher Scientific), CHOP (1:1000, #MA5-50,465, Thermo Fisher Scientific), ATF4 (1:1000, #PA5-72,620, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and VINCULIN (1:2000, Servicebio). Membranes were further incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, Servicebio) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an ECL kit (Millipore) and captured with a Bio-Rad chemiluminescence imaging system.

CCK-8 cellular viability assay

Stable cells were seeded in 96-well plates (3 × 103 cells/well) with 100 μL complete DMEM. CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, Cat No. C0038) was added at 0, 24, 48 and 72 h, and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. OD value at 450 nm was measured by a microplate reader (Bio-Rad). The experiment was repeated three times independently with six technical replicates each.

Wound healing assay

Stable cells were seeded in 6-well plates to 90–100% confluence, and a straight scratch was made with a 200 μL pipette tip. After washing with PBS (Solarbio), cells were cultured in serum-free DMEM. Wound images were captured at 0, 24 and 48 h under an inverted microscope (Olympus, 100 ×). The wound healing rate was calculated accordingly.

EdU incorporation assay

The BeyoClick™ EdU-594 Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Beyotime, Cat No. C0078S) was used to detect cell proliferation according to the manufacturer's directions. Stable sh-FKBP4 and sh-NC cells were seeded in 24-well plates, incubated with 50 μM EdU solution for 2 h at 37 °C, then fixed, permeabilized, and stained with Apollo dye and DAPI. EdU-positive cells were counted under a Leica confocal microscope (200 ×) and calculated as percentages of total DAPI-stained cells.

Transwell invasion assay

24-well Transwell chambers (8.0 μm pore, Corning Costar, Cat No. 3422) pre-coated with Matrigel (1:8, BD Biosciences) were used. Stable cells (1 × 105/200 μL, serum-free DMEM) were seeded in the upper chamber, and 600 μL complete DMEM (10% FBS) was added to the lower chamber. After 48 h incubation, non-invaded cells were wiped off, and invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio), and photographed (Olympus, 100 ×). Three random fields were counted per group.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise stated, in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.0. Differences between sh-FKBP4 and sh-NC groups were compared by two-tailed Student’s t-test. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001 were considered statistically significant.

Results

To evaluate the UPR activity across different cell types, we calculated the AUC value of the UPR gene set for each cell using the ‘AUCell’ tool. The t-SNE plot (Fig. 1C) shows the spatial distribution of the AUC values, with immune cell clusters (such as Macrophage and DC) displaying intense signal. A violin plot was used to further quantify this difference. Macrophage and DC exhibited significantly higher AUC values compared with the other cell types, indicating enhanced UPR activity in these immune cell types (Fig. 1D).

Subsequently, the ‘hdWGCNA’ package was applied to identify UPR-related genes. A soft power threshold of 7 was determined to construct a stable co-expression network (Fig. 1E), which yielded eight distinct modules. Correlation analysis between the module eigengenes and AUC values revealed that the green module had the strongest association with UPR activity (Fig. 1F), leading to the selection of the 881 genes from this module as the UPR-related genes for further analysis.

The volcano plot (Fig. 1G) shows the genes with differential expression patterns between normal and malignant lung tissues within the TCGA-LUAD bulk RNA-seq data. These genes were identified by logFC > 1 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05. After univariate Cox regression analysis, 499 genes with prognostic significance were found in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. By intersecting the 881 genes from the green module, the DEGs from TCGA-LUAD, and the genes with prognostic significance identified by univariate Cox regression, we obtained 33 UPR-related candidate genes (Fig. 1H).

Integrative UPRRS construction

When aiming to construct the UPRRS, we systematically evaluated 101 combinations of machine learning algorithms for model construction. These were specifically tailored to evaluate the 33 crucial genes previously identified. The TCGA-LUAD dataset was carefully divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. For the training set, we crafted 101 predictive models, leveraging the tenfold cross-validation method. Simultaneously, we calculated the C-index for all validation and training sets, which are clearly shown in Fig. 2A. The optimal model was the combination of LASSO and Stepwise Cox regression. This model achieved a stable average C-index value of 0.645 across validation cohorts, which effectively indicated its favorable predictive ability. LASSO Cox regression identified seven prognostic genes by minimizing partial likelihood deviance (Fig. 2B, C). Genes with non-zero coefficients identified by LASSO were further refined using Stepwise Cox regression, ultimately yielding five signature genes for the final UPRRS model (Fig. 2D). Based on the original cross-cohort ranking framework, the LASSO + Stepwise Cox model was selected as the final UPRRS model. To assess the robustness of this selection strategy, a TCGA-only sensitivity ranking analysis was subsequently conducted. Evaluation of the UPRRS model

The prognostic performance of the UPRRS was evaluated using ROC analysis. The 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year AUC values revealed its predictive potential across different cohorts. As shown in Fig. 3A-D, these respective values were 0.725, 0.662, and 0.636 for the training cohort; 0.659, 0.664, and 0.628 for the testing cohort; 0.794, 0.663, and 0.631 for GSE13213; and 0.587, 0.636, and 0.579 for GSE72094. The risk score for each patient was then estimated using the expression levels of the five most important genes. These levels were weighted by the coefficients of regression within the UPRRS model. Using the median cutoff value derived from the training cohort, patients were classified into low-risk and high-risk groups. Across the training, testing, GSE13213, and GSE72094 cohorts, the high-risk patients had significantly shorter OS compared with those categorized as low-risk (log-rank test, P < 0.05, Fig. 3E-H). Furthermore, higher risk scores were associated with increased mortality across all cohorts (Fig. 3I-L). We further quantified model performance using cohort-specific C-index values with corresponding standard errors (SE) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Time-dependent AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival were also calculated with 95% CI using the IPCW method. In addition, the overall mean C-index and inter-cohort standard deviation were calculated to summarize cross-cohort stability. Multi-signature benchmark

To objectively quantify the predictive performance of UPRRS against previously reported LUAD prognostic signatures, we performed standardized head-to-head comparisons across all four cohorts using identical analytical workflows (Supplementary Table 3). Three published single-pathway signatures were enrolled for parallel evaluation, corresponding to a pyroptosis-related signature [44], an autophagy-related signature [45], and an immune-related signature [46]. All competing signatures were refitted on the same TCGA training cohort to eliminate methodological bias. In this benchmark analysis, UPRRS yielded the highest mean C-index (0.622) among all compared signatures. C-index values calculated for individual cohorts of each model are summarized in Supplementary Table 3.

Development and evaluation of the nomogram survival model

To assess the accuracy of using the UPRRS for LUAD patient survival prediction, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed for OS using data from the TCGA-LUAD dataset (Fig. 4A, B). The results revealed that the UPRRS emerged as a highly significant risk factor for OS in both cases (hazard ratio > 1, P < 0.001). UPRRS immune characteristics

To characterize immune cell infiltration patterns in LUAD samples, the ESTIMATE algorithm was applied. This enabled precise quantification of immune scores, stromal scores, ESTIMATE scores, and tumor purity across the UPRRS risk stratification groups. Notably, the low-risk UPRRS group exhibited significantly higher ESTIMATE, immune, and stromal scores, as illustrated in Fig. 6A-C. Subsequently, the XCELL algorithm was used to comprehensively investigate the correlations between UPRRS-associated genes and diverse immune cell subsets. Our findings revealed that LAIR1 expression patterns were strongly correlated with the abundance of multiple immune cell populations, whereas FKBP4 displayed negative associations with M2 macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts, and several other cell types (Fig. 6D). To further delineate the differences in immune cell infiltration between the high- and low-risk UPRRS groups, the CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to estimate the specific immune cell proportions in each sample (Fig. 6E). In-depth analysis demonstrated a marked enrichment of resting mast cells in the low-risk group. Moreover, except for CD276, the expression levels of immune checkpoint molecules were significantly higher in the low-risk UPRRS group than in the high-risk group (Fig. 6F). Together, these results describe the immune landscape differences associated with the UPRRS in LUAD. Drug response targeting the UPRRS

To comprehensively investigate the association between our established model and drug sensitivity, we analyzed the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of a broad panel of compounds in LUAD samples using the GDSC database. As shown in Fig. S1 A-H, IC50 values of various drugs correlated with UPRRS status. Notably, Camptothecin, Irinotecan, Gemcitabine, Taselisib, and Mitoxantrone exhibited elevated IC50 values in the high-risk UPRRS group, suggesting differential drug response patterns between risk groups. This finding may provide insights for future personalized treatment strategies in LUAD.

However, these in silico results suggest a potential association between UPRRS-related genes and drug sensitivity. Experimental validation, such as in vitro drug viability assays, is required to confirm these observations. Therefore, conclusions regarding therapeutic sensitivity or resistance remain preliminary and should be interpreted with caution.

Western blot analysis generally confirmed the protein expression trends of the five genes observed at the mRNA level (Fig. 7F). FKBP4 protein was markedly increased in LUAD cells, while KLF4 protein was decreased, consistent with its well‑documented role as a tumor suppressor in lung adenocarcinoma. The other three genes (ALDH2, LAIR1, SIDT2) also showed low protein expression, in line with their status as protective genes in our UPRRS model.

Interestingly, despite the reduced expression of KLF4 in LUAD cells, multivariate Cox regression identified higher KLF4 expression as being associated with poorer prognosis in patient cohorts. This apparent inconsistency may be attributed to the complex, context‑dependent roles of KLF4 in the tumor microenvironment and UPR signaling network, rather than its cell‑autonomous function as a tumor suppressor in vitro.

Given that FKBP4 was the only gene consistently and prominently upregulated at both mRNA and protein levels in LUAD cells and served as a key high-risk factor in our five‑gene UPRRS, we selected FKBP4 for further functional validation to explore its biological role in LUAD progression.

Stable knockdown of FKBP4 was established in A549 and H1975 cells and verified by Western blot (Fig. 7G). Functional assays revealed that FKBP4 knockdown decreased overall cellular metabolic viability as measured by CCK-8 assay, and EdU incorporation assay further verified the direct inhibitory effect on cell proliferation (Fig. 7H, I). Moreover, silencing FKBP4 markedly inhibited cell migration and invasion in wound healing and Transwell invasion assays, respectively (Fig. 7J, K).

In clinical LUAD tissues, IHC further confirmed that FKBP4 protein expression was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent non‑tumor tissues (Fig. 7L).

Collectively, these data suggest that FKBP4 may contribute to oncogenic phenotypes in LUAD cells. Considering FKBP4 is one hub gene of our UPR-related prognostic signature, we further used western blot to explore the potential correlation between FKBP4 and UPR pathway activity. Figure 7G displays the protein levels of GRP78, ATF4 and CHOP in FKBP4-knockdown A549 and H1975 cells. Depletion of FKBP4 led to synchronous downregulation of these three canonical UPR markers in both cell lines, which suggests an association between FKBP4 expression and basal UPR signaling activity at the cellular level. This observation provides preliminary cellular evidence supporting the bioinformatic association between FKBP4 and the UPR pathway derived from UPRRS analysis. It should be noted that these coordinated expression changes only reflect correlation rather than definitive causal regulation, and detailed mechanistic exploration will be addressed in follow-up dedicated research.

Discussion

The 5-year survival rate for LUAD has improved significantly in recent years, driven by the advent of novel chemotherapeutic agents, targeted therapies, and immunotherapies [47]. Despite this progress, LUAD remains a leading cause of cancer-associated morbidity and mortality worldwide. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy, demonstrating notable advantages in both efficacy and safety and thus bringing renewed hope to the clinical management of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [48]. In this context, prognostic models integrating molecular features, immune microenvironment characteristics, and therapeutic response-related information may improve risk stratification and individualized treatment in LUAD [49]. Increasing evidence suggests that the UPR is involved in tumor progression, immune regulation, and therapeutic resistance across multiple cancer types [50]. However, there remains a marked paucity of validated biomarkers linked to UPR function in LUAD. In this context, we investigated the prognostic significance and potential clinical implications of a UPR-related risk signature in LUAD.

To further evaluate the predictive performance of our UPR-related prognostic signature, we compared our model with several recently published machine learning-based prognostic signatures for lung adenocarcinoma, including immune-related, intratumor heterogeneity-associated, and immune evasion-related models [26, 51, 52]. Our UPRRS achieved an average C-index of 0.645 and maintained comparable time-dependent AUC values across multiple independent cohorts, suggesting relatively stable prognostic performance.

We additionally conducted standardized head-to-head benchmarking to quantitatively compare the predictive performance of UPRRS under unified analytical criteria against three single-pathway prognostic signatures corresponding to pyroptosis [44], autophagy [45], and tumor immunity [46] (Supplementary Table 3). All competing signatures were refitted on our identical TCGA training dataset to ensure fair comparison, and UPRRS exhibited more robust prognostic discriminatory ability across all four enrolled cohorts. Unlike several previously reported signatures derived primarily from transcriptomic screening, our signature was developed based on genes associated with the UPR stress response. We further performed in vitro functional experiments to explore the association between FKBP4 and UPR signaling. We also recognize inherent limitations of this cross-signature comparison: several genes from published signatures could not be fully matched to probes on the Affymetrix microarrays of the two GEO cohorts, which restricts complete head-to-head performance evaluation across all candidate models.

Consistent with several previously published LUAD prognostic models [26, 51, 52], the predictive performance of our signature gradually declined with prolonged follow-up, with relatively better performance observed for short-term survival prediction. This phenomenon is common in transcriptome-based prognostic models and may reflect the increasing influence of complex clinical factors and dynamic tumor microenvironment changes on long-term survival outcomes. Therefore, our model may be more applicable for early prognostic evaluation and risk stratification in LUAD patients.

In contrast, our UPRRS integrates tumor-intrinsic UPR stress adaptation and immune microenvironment remodeling through five core genes identified from single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses. Compared with many previously reported LUAD prognostic signatures primarily based on bulk transcriptomic analyses, our study additionally incorporated single-cell transcriptomics, hdWGCNA-based UPR activity analysis, machine learning framework screening, and experimental validation. These complementary analyses may provide additional perspectives for understanding LUAD biological heterogeneity and UPR-related features.

To further explore the biological relevance of our signature, we validated core signature genes at mRNA and protein levels in LUAD cell lines and clinical specimens. Functional experiments targeting hub gene FKBP4 revealed its critical roles in cell proliferation and migration, and FKBP4 knockdown triggered coordinated expression changes of key UPR proteins, offering preliminary cellular evidence for the bioinformatic link between FKBP4 and UPR signaling. It remains unclear whether FKBP4 directly modulates the PERK-ATF4-CHOP cascade or indirectly affects UPR activity through broader cellular stress responses. Collectively, these findings support the biological relevance of UPRRS, whereas precise causal mechanisms connecting FKBP4 to UPR signaling and tumor malignant phenotypes remain unelucidated and await deeper mechanistic exploration.

Previous work has demonstrated that the UPR exerts a profound influence on the prostate cancer immune microenvironment, highlighting its potential as an innovative and promising targeted therapeutic strategy [53]. Building on this evidence, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the intricate relationship between the UPRRS and immune microenvironment in LUAD, along with its regulatory effects on tumor immunity. Our findings revealed that patients in the high-risk UPRRS group exhibited significantly lower immune scores, a phenotype that may account for their poorer clinical outcomes. In contrast, the low-risk group exhibited elevated immune checkpoint expression, pointing to distinct immunotherapy-relevant biological features across the two risk subgroups. These results reveal correlations between UPRRS and tumor immune landscape, but any inference regarding immunotherapy response remains exploratory without independent immunotherapy-treated patient cohorts for validation.

LUAD is characterized by extensive molecular heterogeneity, distinct progression patterns, and diverse biological behaviors, which pose considerable challenges for clinicians in the clinical management of affected patients [54]. Hence, optimizing personalized therapeutic approaches for LUAD is of utmost importance. Our research further explored the association between the UPRRS and predicted therapeutic sensitivity patterns in LUAD. Intriguingly, patients with higher UPRRS scores exhibited higher predicted IC50 values for several conventional chemotherapeutic agents, while potentially remaining sensitive to certain FDA-approved targeted compounds for NSCLC treatment. Compared with conventional prognostic signatures, our UPRRS specifically focuses on UPR-related pathways associated with tumor stress adaptation and therapeutic resistance, which may provide additional insights into treatment response prediction. This feature complements the predictive scope of signatures centered on metabolism or immune function. We separated preprocessing of TCGA RNA-seq and GEO microarray datasets to avoid cross-platform training-validation leakage from joint ComBat correction; cross-platform validation without unified batch correction provides a conservative test of the signature’s generalizability. In addition, a TCGA-only sensitivity ranking analysis demonstrated that the final selected model maintained comparable performance after exclusion of external validation cohorts from the ranking procedure, supporting the robustness of the model-selection strategy.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the UPRRS is associated with therapeutic response heterogeneity in LUAD and may aid prognostic evaluation and risk stratification. However, additional mechanistic research and independent clinical validation are needed to support its clinical translation.
