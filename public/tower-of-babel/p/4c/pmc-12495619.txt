DNA N6-methyladenine and ABCA1 methylation associations with subclinical coronary atherosclerosis in people with HIV
Authors: Jose I Bernardino, Regina Dalmau, Diego Rodriguez-Torres, Gabriel Saiz-Medrano, María Fernandez-Velilla, Inmaculada Pinilla, Javier Rodriguez-Centeno, Andrés Esteban-Cantos, María Jiménez-González, Tatiana Mata, Carmen Ramos, Berta Rodés, Miriam Estébanez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractABCA1 methylation and N6-methyladenine DNA have been identified as epigenetic markers in coronary artery disease. We performed a cross-sectional exploratory study to analyze whether global DNA methylation and ABCA1 promoter methylation in monocytes are associated with coronary atherosclerosis in asymptomatic HIV individuals without cardiovascular disease. Coronary atherosclerosis was defined as a coronary plaque in at least one coronary segment. We included 27 individuals, 15 of whom had coronary atherosclerosis. ABCA1 DNA methylation was higher in participants with coronary atherosclerosis, with statistically significant differences at specific loci related to the activator protein-2 (AP-2) binding site. There were no differences in global levels of 5-methylcytosine and N6-methyladenine DNA between individuals with or without coronary atherosclerosis. ABCA1 methylation showed moderate correlations with both the Leaman score and coronary plaque volume, whereas global DNA N6-methyladenine had a negative moderate correlation with the segment severity score and LDL cholesterol. Among participants with coronary atherosclerosis, we found a strong negative correlation between ABCA1 methylation and total atheroma plaque volume, with certain loci (CpG 9 and 12) exhibiting very strong associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-025-01977-y.Keywords: ABCA1 methylation, N6-methyladenine, Coronary atherosclerosis, HIV

Introduction

People with HIV (PWH) have an increased cardiovascular risk compared with the general population and an elevated risk for subclinical atherosclerosis [1–3]. Cardiovascular risk equations explain 70–80% of the variability of cardiovascular risk and attempts to improve this predictive ability by adding new plasma biomarkers have led to only modest improvements in discrimination and risk prediction [4].

Atherosclerosis is a disease with multiple epigenetic mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, histone acetylation, and microRNAs contributing to atheroma plaque formation [5]. Studies on the association between DNA methylation, coronary artery disease and incident cardiovascular disease have shown mixed results but most of them have shown a promising role of DNA methylation and epigenetic markers as biomarkers of aging and cardiovascular disease [6].

ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) is an integral membrane protein that mediates cholesterol transfer from peripheral tissues to the liver and intestine in reverse cholesterol transport which is critical in the synthesis of HDL cholesterol and preventing the development of atherosclerosis. HIV infection impairs reverse cholesterol transport [7]. This process is mainly mediated by monocytes and macrophages. Monocyte and macrophages are key contributors to inflammation during atherosclerosis [8]. HIV infection impairs reverse cholesterol transport and reduces ABCA1-dependent efflux capacity, and this effect is only partially reversed after antiretroviral treatment initiation [9, 10].

A previous study has shown that differential methylation within the ABCA1 promoter region contributes to variability in plasma HDL and atherosclerotic lesions [11]. ABCA1 and ABCG1 hypermethylation in epicardial adipose tissue is associated with coronary artery disease [12]. ABCA1 methylation has also been associated with aging biomarkers and the development of coronary heart disease in males [13].

DNA N6-methyladenine (6 mA) is an easy-to-measure epigenetic marker that plays a role in a wide range of medical conditions, and recent reports suggest it is also related to cardiovascular disease [14, 15]. In a recent report including 207 individuals with carotid atherosclerosis, leukocyte levels of 6 mA were inversely correlated with carotid plaque size and thickness and were associated with carotid plaque progression [16].

In this exploratory study, we assessed the association between epigenetic markers (ABCA1 methylation and global 5mC and 6 mA), coronary artery disease and coronary plaques in a small group of virologically controlled PWH with subclinical atherosclerosis.

Methods

Study design and clinical assessments

This was a cross-sectional exploratory study including PWH > 45 years with two or more cardiovascular risk factors on antiretroviral therapy with HIV-RNA < 50 copies/ml for at least 6 months. All participants were asymptomatic regarding cardiac symptoms. We excluded participants with established clinical cardiovascular disease or chronic kidney disease. Cardiovascular risk was assessed using the SCORE-2 risk. All participants signed a written informed consent for inclusion, and the study was approved by the ethics committee of hospital Universitario La Paz and Hospital Central de la Defensa Gómez Ulla (8/22, 16.02.2022). All participants underwent a structural clinical history and physical examination and a 9-h fasting blood test was performed for lipids. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from fresh whole blood by density gradient centrifugation and were stored to viability in liquid nitrogen until analysis. Computed coronary tomography angiography (CCTA) was performed with 180- and 320-slice CT scanners according to a standardized protocol of the Society of Cardiovascular CT guidelines. Coronary plaque volume and composition were quantified using a semi-automatic plaque analysis software (Sure Plaque Vitrea. Canon). All CCTA scans were centrally read at Hospital Universitario La Paz. Coronary atherosclerosis was defined as the presence of at least one coronary plaque in any arterial segment. All data were collected in a codified electronic spreadsheet using REDCap software.

Quantification of global DNA 5-methylcytosine and N6-methyladenine

Genomic DNA was extracted from whole peripheral blood samples using QIAmp DNA Blood (Qiagen, Germany). Global DNA 6 mA and 5mC levels were measured using the MethylFlash 6 mA DNA Methylation ELISA Kit (Colorimetric) and MethylFlash Global DNA Methylation (5mC) ELISA Easy Kit (Colorimetric), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Epigentek, NY, USA). Each sample was analyzed in duplicate.

ABCA1 promoter DNA methylation and ABCA1 mRNA levels in monocytes

Monocytes were isolated from frozen PBMCs using the EasySep™ Human CD14 Positive Selection Kit II (StemCell, Canada). Genomic DNA and mRNA were extracted from monocytes using an AllPrep DNA/RNA/protein mini kit (Qiagen, Germany).

To assess the DNA methylation levels of the ABCA1 promoter, extracted genomic DNA was converted with sodium bisulfite using the EZ DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, CA, USA). PCR was used to amplify the converted DNA in the region spanning the ABCA1 proximal promoter using modified primers (ABCA1-F4 5’ AGTTTAGGTTTTTGAAAGGAAAT 3’ and ABCA1-R7 5’ AAACCTATACTCTCCCTCCTCCCC 3’). The PCR products were then analyzed using next-generation sequencing (NGS) on a MiSeq sequencer (Illumina, CA, USA). Briefly, PCR amplicons underwent a second PCR of 13 cycles performed with Q5® Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) in the presence of 400 nM of primers with barcodes of the Access Array Barcode Library for Illumina Sequencers (Fluidigm). The finally obtained amplicons were validated and quantified by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer using DNA7500 chips, and an equimolecular pool of these amplicons was purified with AMPure-XP Beads (Beckman coulter) to eliminate primers/dimers and titrated by quantitative PCR using the “Kapa-SYBR FAST qPCR kit forLightCycler480” and a reference standard for quantification. The pool of amplicons was denatured prior to be seeded on a flowcell at a density of 10 pM, where clusters were formed and sequenced using a “MiSeq Reagent Kit v3”, in a 2 × 300 pair-end sequencing run on a MiSeq sequencer. The sequences were analyzed, filtered (only uniquely mapped reads with mapping quality ≥ 90% were retained for downstream analysis) and aligned using the Geneious R11 software. We achieved an average sequencing depth of ~ 80,000 × per amplicon. Overall, 16 CpG dinucleotides were analyzed (Fig. 1).

To assess ABCA1 mRNA levels, cDNA was generated from the total RNA in five separate reverse transcription reactions using random primers included in the GoScript Reverse Transcriptase Mix (Promega, WI, USA). The resulting cDNAs were pooled for qPCR analysis using TaqMan® technology. Equal amounts of cDNA were run in duplicate and amplified in a 20 μL reaction containing 10 μL of 2 × TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermofisher, MA, USA) in a QuantStudio1 thermal cycler. Primers and TaqMan probes were included in the TaqMan gene expression assays by Thermofisher (ABCA1: Hs01059118_m1, FAM). Each sample was calibrated to the GAPDH housekeeping gene (Hs0275991_g1-VIC). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the study participants were compared descriptively according to their coronary atherosclerosis status. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) as they did not follow a normal distribution. Qualitative variables were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the χ2 Pearson or Fisher test, as appropriate, for qualitative parameters and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. An association analysis was performed using Spearman’s correlation. Statistical analysis was completed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

In this study, we included 27 participants. The characteristics are listed in Table 1. The median age was 57 years. The majority were male (81.5%) and Caucasian (85.2%). All participants had suppressed viral replication with a median duration of antiretroviral treatment of 8 years. The median CD4 count was 659 cells/μL. Regarding traditional risk factors, 23 of the 27 participants had dyslipidemia, of whom 20 were on statin therapy. More than half of the patients were hypertensive and approximately one-third had diabetes mellitus. One-quarter of the participants were active smokers. The median cardiovascular risk was 6%.

Fifteen (55%) participants had coronary atherosclerosis. The main differences between those with or without coronary atherosclerosis were the presence of dyslipidemia (100% vs. 66.7%; p = 0.06) and more participants receiving statins in the coronary atherosclerosis group (93% vs. 50%; p = 0.03). The duration of exposure to protease inhibitors (PIs) was slightly longer in the coronary atherosclerosis group (p = 0.09), as none of the participants in the comparison group had received PIs.

Of the 15 participants with atheromatous plaques, non-calcified plaques were observed in at least one segment in 11 (73%). More than half of the patients (53.3%) presented with mild stenosis (< 50%), and only three exhibited severe coronary artery disease with stenosis greater than 70%. Nine patients (60%) had vulnerable plaques (defined as positive remodeling, low-attenuation plaque, or spotty calcification). The median Leaman score was 6.8, and ten participants (66.7%) had a Leaman score greater than 5. The mean coronary artery calcium (CAC) score of all participants was 125.8. Ten participants (37%) had a CAC score of 0, and four (14.8%) had a CAC score greater than 400. CCTA findings are summarized in Supplemental Table 1.

We did not find associations between any of the epigenetic markers and cardiovascular risk factors, except for a significant correlation between ABCA1 methylation and serum glucose (rho = 0.41; p = 0.03), and 6 mA with total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol (rho = − 0.44; p = 0.008 and rho = − 0.40; p = 0.04, respectively) (Supplemental Table 2).

We performed analyses to explore whether any of the epigenetic variables were associated with the presence of coronary artery disease. There were no statistically significant differences in global blood DNA methylation (5mC and 6 mA), ABCA1 promoter methylation, and ABCA1 mRNA expression between the groups (Table 1). Detailed analysis of ABCA1 methylation from CpG 1 to 16 revealed that there was a trend toward higher methylation at all studied CpGs in the CAD group (Supplemental Table 3). The median percentage of methylation at locus 9 and 16 of ABCA1 was significantly higher in the group of participants with coronary arteriosclerosis [(14.81 vs. 12.52, p = 0.04), and (2.45 vs.1.60, p = 0.04), respectively].

We observed a trend toward a moderate correlation between global ABCA1 methylation and the Leaman score (rho = 0.37, p = 0.06) and between 6 mA DNA methylation and the segment severity score (rho = − 0 .33, p = 0.09).

We then performed further analysis to determine if there was any relationship between these epigenetic biomarkers and plaque volume and morphology. We found a strong negative correlation between global methylation of ABCA1 and total atheroma plaque volume in the group of participants with coronary arteriosclerosis (rho = − 0.55; p = 0.034) (Fig. 2A). Of note, stronger and statistically significant negative correlations were observed between methylation at loci 9 and 12 and total plaque volume in individuals with coronary atherosclerosis (rho = -0.74; p = 0.001, and rho = -0.77; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B and C).

Discussion

This exploratory analysis identified a potential association between ABCA1 DNA methylation in monocytes and N6-methyladenine DNA in blood with two distinct measures of coronary plaque burden---the Leaman score and the segment severity score---in asymptomatic PWH. While no significant differences were observed in global ABCA1 DNA methylation between individuals with and without coronary atherosclerosis, some CpG positions within the promoter region exhibited methylation differences. Additionally, ABCA1 methylation was significantly associated with total plaque volume in participants with coronary atherosclerosis.

Recently, epigenome-wide association studies have demonstrated the role of DNA methylation in the pathogenesis of acute coronary syndromes [17]. Additionally, some authors have created a methylation risk score that improves prediction of acute coronary syndromes over classical risk factors [6].

Previous studies have demonstrated that global 6 mA in blood is reduced in individuals with hypertension, and 6 mA levels are recovered after blood pressure control with pharmacological treatment [15]. In another study of 207 individuals with carotid atherosclerosis, 6 mA decreased with increasing carotid plaque thickness. Serum LDL, and 6 mA levels were associated with the progression of carotid plaque [16]. In our study, 6 mA DNA levels in blood were negatively correlated with the segment severity score, a measure of coronary plaque burden, and LDL and total cholesterol. Our results are in line with previous publications and suggest a possible role for 6 mA DNA levels as a biomarker for atherosclerosis in HIV individuals.

Other studies have demonstrated higher ABCA1 methylation levels in subjects with carotid atherosclerosis [18], coronary heart disease [11, 13], and acute coronary syndrome [19]. None of these studies included PWH or analyzed monocytes. In our study, monocyte ABCA1 methylation was moderately correlated with the Leaman score, a validated marker of coronary atherosclerotic plaque burden, and some loci in ABCA1 were differentially methylated and independently associated with coronary atherosclerosis. Among the four most differentially methylated positions between patients with and without atheromatosis, one lies within the activator protein-2 (AP-2) binding site in the ABCA1 proximal promoter, whereas the other three are located between 6 and 17 nucleotides upstream of the AP-2 binding site (− 323 to − 293 bp region). Moreover, the two CpG positions showing the strongest correlation with plaque thickness are located immediately upstream and downstream of the AP-2 binding site (at − 312 bp and − 293 bp, respectively). AP-2, a transcription factor, negatively regulates ABCA1 expression [20]. Although further experiments are needed to confirm this effect in our patients, other studies have shown that methylation of the promoter region containing the AP-2 binding site may inhibit AP-2 binding and reduce transcriptional activity [21]. In our study, monocyte ABCA1 methylation was negatively correlated with total plaque volume. Infante et al. have previously shown in a cross-sectional study including 65 participants with CHD and 30 controls an association between ABCA1 gene expression in PBMCs, coronary stenosis and total plaque volume [22]. In this study, the authors proposed that ABCA1 could help identify high-risk plaques, as was related to non-calcified plaque composition. In our study, we could not find any associations with high-risk plaques, first due to the small sample size and because more than 60% of patients in our study had high-risk plaques on CCTA. We did not find a relationship between ABCA1 methylation and ABCA1 expression, but this has also been reported in other studies [11].

Global 5mC and 6 mA methylation were measured in whole blood and ABCA1 methylation was assessed in monocytes. We believe that measuring ABCA1 in monocytes is most relevant when focusing on cardiovascular disease and atherosclerosis because monocytes and macrophages are key players in foam cell formation, thereby contributing to atheroma plaque formation. On the contrary, global 5mC and 6 mA have been measured from whole blood samples as a proxy of global methylation (5mC) and a possible biomarker in blood for atherosclerosis progression (6 mA). Most studies published in the literature have been conducted using total blood samples. Our findings provide novel insights into the potential epigenetic mechanisms contributing to the increased cardiovascular risk observed in PWH. In this population, the reverse cholesterol transport mediated by monocytes/macrophages—a key process in lipid homeostasis and atheroprotection—is known to be impaired, potentially accelerating the development of coronary artery disease. In this context, the higher ABCA1 promoter methylation detected in individuals with subclinical atherosclerosis suggests a potential downregulation of cholesterol efflux pathways, given the central role of ABCA1 in mediating cholesterol export to high-density lipoproteins (HDL). The observed correlations between ABCA1 methylation, coronary plaque burden, and atherosclerosis severity scores suggest that epigenetic modifications in lipid-regulating genes may represent a critical link between chronic HIV infection, macrophage dysfunction, and atherogenesis.

This study was limited by its exploratory design, small sample size, and lack of a control group without HIV infection. Additionally, we did not collect data on diet, exercise, or environmental factors that also influence DNA methylation. The lack of a control group precludes us from addressing the potential effect on ABCA1 methylation of HIV or antiretroviral therapy. Nevertheless, this cross-sectional study set the hypothesis that will be tested in a larger prospective study of PWH and HIV-negative controls.

In conclusion, in this small exploratory study, ABCA1 methylation and 6 mA was associated with coronary artery disease in PWH, although further studies are needed to clarify its impact on gene expression and confirm the link to clinical coronary artery disease. These new epigenetic biomarkers could help identify individuals with a higher coronary plaque burden and add value to the stratification risk of classical cardiovascular equation tools.
