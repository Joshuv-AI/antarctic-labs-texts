The application value of intraoperative lateral spreading response monitoring during microvascular decompression in patients with primary hemifacial spasm
Authors: Jun Yang, Xiaocui Yang, Hanjie Liu, Jiajia Liu, Ke Li, Jiawei Shi, Yingzhun Liang, Yu Song, Xing Fan, Hui Qiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe application value of intraoperative lateral spreading response (LSR) during microvascular decompression (MVD) is always disputed. The current study aimed to explore the predictive value of intraoperative LSR monitoring for the long-term outcome in patients with primary hemifacial spasm (pHFS).MethodsThe data from 312 pHFS patients were retrospectively reviewed. The zygomatic LSR (ZYG-LSR) and mandibular LSR (MAN-LSR) monitoring were performed during surgery. The correlations of ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR disappearances with patients’ long-term outcomes (one year after surgery) were retrospectively investigated. Consequently, binary logistic regression analysis was applied to explore their predictive value. Finally, the implications of their combined utilization for predicting the long-term outcome were explored.ResultsPatients with either persistent ZYG-LSR or MAN-LSR exhibited a higher incidence of spasms one year after surgery (p < 0.001). Persistent ZYG-LSR (odds ratio 7.721, p < 0.001) and MAN-LSR (odds ratio 10.729, p < 0.001) were both identified as independent predictive factors for an unfavorable long-term outcome. Taking both ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR into consideration, patients with simultaneously disappeared two waves had the highest long-term recovery rate (97.2%), followed by patients with either persisted wave (76.5%) and patients with simultaneously persisted two waves (27.3%). The differences among all pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (p < 0.001 for all).ConclusionThe current study confirmed the application value of intraoperative LSR monitoring during MVD in patients with pHFS. In addition, the clinical significance of two-branch LSR monitoring was also described. The findings can provide important insights for optimizing the application of intraoperative LSR monitoring in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03293-w.Keywords: Lateral spreading response (LSR),; Microvascular decompression (MVD),; Hemifacial spasm (HFS), Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM)

Introduction

Hemifacial spasm (HFS) is a peripheral neurological disorder characterized by painless, involuntary unilateral clonic or tonic contractions of the facial muscles. It is typically induced by irritation or compression of the facial nerve and can be categorized into primary and secondary types. Primary HFS (pHFS) is generally caused by a benign vascular compression on the root of the facial nerve, while secondary HFS is often attributed to clear nonvascular etiologies like tumors, aneurysms, or multiple sclerosis. Due to the significantly lower prevalence of secondary HFS compared to pHFS, the latter is more of a concern for neurologists and neurosurgeons [1–3].

Although HFS is generally considered benign, frequent spasms can disrupt regular facial movements, impairing essential functions like speaking, chewing, and swallowing. The unpredictable and visible nature of HFS can seriously affect the patient’s daily life and social interactions, and lead to feelings of self-consciousness or embarrassment. Accordingly, effective treatment is particularly crucial for HFS patients to improve their physical functionality and emotional health. Medication and botulinum toxin treatments are common conservative therapeutic options for HFS, and botulinum toxin injections, in particular, have shown notable efficacy in alleviating spasms [4, 5]. However, both medication and botulinum toxin treatments require long-term management and are accompanied by potential temporary effects and adverse events. For patients who exhibit inadequate responses to conservative therapies or experience severe side effects, surgical treatment can provide them with the prospect of a definitive and lasting resolution.

Microvascular decompression (MVD) is widely acknowledged as the most effective surgical intervention for pHFS [6]. Similar to conservative treatment, surgical treatment is also surrounded by risks of many complications, such as hearing loss or facial paralysis.Since the 1980s, various intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) modalities, such as electromyography, facial motor evoked potential, and F-wave, have been applied to MVD and have greatly improved its safety [6–8]. Among various IONM modalities used during MVD, the lateral spreading response (LSR), also known as abnormal muscle response, is particularly special. LSR is an abnormal electrical activity elicited by stimulating one facial nerve branch and subsequently recorded in muscles innervated by other branches. If the responsible blood vessels were confirmed and decompressed from the facial nerve, LSR should disappear. Overall, the clinical significance of LSR for MVD is to ensure the effectiveness rather than the safety of the surgery [9–12]. However, the application value of intraoperative LSR monitoring has been controversial over the years. Some studies have suggested that the disappearance of LSR at the end of MVD does not affect the long-term outcome of pHFS patients [13–15]. In the current study, we assessed the application value of intraoperative LSR monitoring during MVD and aimed to provide new clinical evidence in this field.

Methods

Ethical approval

It was conducted in strict adherence to the World Medical Association’s Helsinki Declaration, ensuring ethical standards in biomedical research involving human subjects. The current study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (No. KY 2020–096-01). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

Patient population

The data from patients who underwent MVD for pHFS at the Department of Neurosurgery, Beijing Tiantan Hospital, from November 2020 to December 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) with a final diagnosis of pHFS; (2) revealed the LSR on preoperative facial electromyography. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) with insufficient demographic and clinical data; (2) failed to elicit LSR intraoperatively (details are presented in the section “Intraoperative LSR Monitoring”); (3) with insufficient follow-up data (the recovery status at one year after surgery could not be obtained due to loss to follow-up or other contributing factors). A total of 312 patients were ultimately enrolled in the study. The demographic and clinical data were retrieved from the medical records, the electrophysiological data were collected from the institutional database, and the follow-up data were obtained through clinic visits and telephone interviews.

Anesthesia and surgical procedures

Combined intravenous-inhalation anesthesia was applied to all patients. Intravenous propofol, sufentanil, and atracurium were administered for anesthesia induction. Anesthesia was induced by intravenous propofol, sufentanil, and atracurium. All patients received a combination of intravenous and inhalation anesthesia. Anesthesia was maintained with a continuous infusion of propofol and remifentanil, with the minimum alveolar concentration of sevoflurane controlled at 0.5. Muscle relaxants were omitted during the surgery except for induction.

All surgeries were performed via retrosigmoid approach under an endoscope. The patient was positioned laterally, and a transverse incision was made near the external auditory canal. Subsequently, the scalp flap was elevated, and a drill was used to create a small bone window. The dura mater was incised, and cerebrospinal fluid was slowly released from the cerebellopontine angle cistern. The endoscope was inserted through the operative field, and the facial nerve and the responsible blood vessels were identified. The vessels were carefully dissected away from the nerve, and a Teflon pad was interposed between them to form a buffer. After the surgeon confirmed sufficient decompression, the dura and the craniotomy defect were closed adequately in layers, followed by the scalp.

Intraoperative LSR monitoring

Intraoperative LSR monitoring was conducted using the Nicolet IONM system (Nicolet Biomedical, Inc., Madison, Wisconsin, USA), with disposable hypodermic needle electrodes applied for stimulation and recording. For zygomatic LSR (ZYG-LSR) monitoring, the anode was positioned at the midpoint along the line connecting the tragus and the lateral canthus, with the needle electrode inserted perpendicularly into the skin to ensure precise stimulation of the zygomatic branch of the facial nerve. The cathode was placed 2 cm medial to the anode. The active recording electrode was positioned over the mentalis muscle, with the reference electrode located 2 cm laterally. For mandibular LSR (MAN-LSR) monitoring, the anode was placed along the mandibular margin, with the cathode positioned 2 cm laterally. The active recording electrode was placed on the orbicularis oculi muscle beneath the pupil, and the reference electrode was located 2 cm lateral to this point. Stimulation was delivered in a constant-current mode with the following parameters: a duration of 0.3 ms, a frequency of 1.1 Hz, and an intensity of 10–30 mA. The recording system was configured with a band-pass filter set at 10–1500 Hz. Cases in which neither ZYG-LSR nor MAN-LSR could be elicited during intraoperative monitoring were excluded from the final analysis in accordance with the predefined exclusion criteria.

All monitoring procedures were conducted by a certified neurophysiologist with at least five years of experience in intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, in close coordination with the surgical team. The baseline waveform was obtained following cerebrospinal fluid release, and stimulation recordings were conducted every 2–5 min during routine phases of the procedure. During critical surgical steps, such as the dissection of the offending vessel from the facial nerve or the insertion of a Teflon pad, recordings are obtained at least once per minute or more frequently as needed. Immediately after each stimulation, LSR responses were compared with the baseline waveform, with results promptly communicated to the surgical team.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (Version 24.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA), and the statistical graphics were plotted using Sangerbox (http://www.sangerbox.com/). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies or percentages, and continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation (SD). Group comparisons were performed using the Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, Student’s t-test, or nonparametric tests as appropriate. Binary logistic regression analysis was applied to further explore the predictive value of intraoperative LSR monitoring for patients’ long-term outcomes (one year after surgery). The odds ratio (OR) and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were employed to assess the predictive significance of the variables included in the model. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

The mean age and male–female ratio of the cohort were 50.46 ± 10.08 years and 202:110, respectively, consistent with the established epidemiological characteristics of HFS. One week after surgery, 63 patients were still experiencing spasms, and 14 of them remained symptomatic one year after surgery. Moreover, among those patients who showed favorable outcomes one week after surgery, 27 cases experienced HFS recurrence within one year . The flow diagram is presented in Fig. 1. The baseline characteristics were then compared between patients with spasms and those without one year after surgery. The results are shown in Table 1. It seemed that patients with older age or who visited for recurrence were more likely to have a poor prognosis (p = 0.013 and p < 0.001, respectively). No significant difference was demonstrated in the remaining characteristics between the two groups. Due to the limited number of patients visited for spasm recurrence (only five), the relevant result should be treated cautiously.

The prognostic value of LSR monitoring

ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR were successfully elicited in 301 and 283 cases, respectively. Patients with persistent ZYG-LSR (positives) exhibited a higher incidence of spasms than those with disappeared ZYG-LSR (negatives) at both one week (38.00 vs. 17.13%, Chi-square test, p < 0.001) and one year (54.00 vs. 5.58%, Chi-square test, p < 0.001) after surgery (Fig. 2A). Similar results were also observed in the comparison between patients with persistent and disappeared MAN-LSR (41.67 vs. 15.79% at one week after surgery, 55.56 vs. 4.05% at one year after surgery; Chi-square test, p < 0.001 for both), and the results are shown in Fig. 2B. In addition, delayed relief (with spasm one week postoperatively and recovered within one year) was observed in 49 patients, but no significant correlation was found between delayed relief and intraoperative LSR status.

ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR were both recorded in 272 cases. The data from these patients were used to build a binary logistic regression model to identify predictive factors of clinical outcomes one year after surgery. The result is presented in Fig. 3. Persistent ZYG-LSR (OR 7.721, 95% CI 2.671–22.319, p < 0.001) and MAN-LSR (OR 10. 279, 95% CI 3.529–29.938, p < 0.001) were both identified as significant and independent predictors for an unfavorable long-term outcome.

In addition, delayed relief (with spasm one week postoperatively and recovered within one year) was observed in 49 patients. These cases, along with those experiencing one-year recurrence, constitute two subgroups exhibiting discordance between short-term and long-term clinical outcomes. The detailed clinical characteristics of these two subgroups are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Overall, the long-term prognosis of the majority of patients in both subgroups was consistent with the intraoperative LSR monitoring findings.

The significance of two-branch LSR monitoring

The 272 patients with available ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR could be subdivided into three groups according to the different situations of the two LSRs at the end of surgery: group A (216 cases, both ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR disappeared), group B (34 cases, either one disappeared, the other persisted), and group C (22 cases, both ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR persisted). We compared the incidences of long-term spasms among the three groups, and the results were statistically significant between each pair (Chi-square test, p < 0.001 for all, Fig. 4). Patients in group C showed the highest incidence of spasms (72.7%) one year after surgery, followed by group B (23.5%) and group A (2.8%).

Discussion

Early in the 1980 s, the disappearance of LSR in MVD was noted as a potential indicator of effective facial nerve decompression in pHFS patients. By the 2000 s, LSR monitoring became widely adopted in MVD procedures. However, the debate over the clinical significance of LSR monitoring has persisted since then, with even the first relevant clinical study of the new century raising questions about its prognostic value [16]. Over the past two decades, numerous studies have suggested that the disappearance of LSR during MVD showed little predictive value for the long-term outcome [15, 17–19]. These studies propose that as long as the follow-up period extends beyond one year, most patients can get spasm relief, regardless of intraoperative LSR status. At the same time, there is also considerable research that supports the predictive value of LSR monitoring for the long-term outcome [20–22].

In the current study, we aimed to explore the predictive value of intraoperative LSR monitoring for the long-term outcome of patients with pHFS. Guided by previous literature, we selected one year as the minimum follow-up period. The recovery rate in our cohort one year after surgery was 86.9%. This recovery rate appears lower than that reported in the previous studies, likely due to our conservative definition of the cured category, which did not include patients with mild episodic symptoms as cured. We adopted two-branch LSR monitoring during the surgery. Patients who exhibited either ZYG-LSR or MAN-LSR disappearances at the end of MVD had an increased likelihood of achieving favorable short-term and long-term outcomes. Moreover, persistent ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR were recognized as independent risk factors for long-term spasms. Collectively, our findings validated the prognostic significance of intraoperative LSR status concerning the long-term outcome in patients with pHFS.

In light of the confirmed prognostic significance of ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR, we further investigated the implications of their combined utilization for predicting patient outcomes. We observed that the intraoperative statuses of ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR were not always consistent in clinical practice, which led us to investigate how intraoperative monitoring could inform prognostic predictions when one response was absent while the other was present. The results indicated that the application of two-branch LSR monitoring could enable more accurate stratification of patient outcomes.. Patients with disappeared ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR at the end of MVD exhibited an exceptionally high recovery rate of 97.2% one year after surgery. However, if either ZYG-LSR or MAN-LSR remained, the long-term recovery rate for those patients dropped significantly to below 80%. In cases where both ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR persisted, there was over a 70% chance that the patient would experience long-term spasms after surgery. The aforementioned findings first quantified the potential efficacy of two-branch LSR monitoring in predicting the long-term outcome of pHFS patients who underwent MVD, which is instrumental in enhancing the management of patient expectations regarding treatment outcomes and represents a significant innovation within the scope of the current study.

With the assistance of two-branch monitoring, neurosurgeons can make better adjustments to their surgical strategy during MVD. For patients with disappeared ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR, it can be considered that the surgical purpose has been achieved. Neurosurgeons can minimize unnecessary procedures, thereby conserving surgical time and reducing the risk of certain complications. However, in cases where an “early disappearance” of LSRs is observed during cerebrospinal fluid release, a thorough evaluation and management of any suspected responsible vessels within the operative field should still be performed. For those with persistent ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR, neurosurgeons should re-examine their operations, evaluate whether the pad has shifted or whether there are other responsible vessels that remain undetected, and consider whether additional adequate decompression of the facial nerve is necessary. If both LSR waveforms remain unchanged after the aforementioned procedures are carried out, the surgical intervention should be deemed complete, and neurosurgeons are advised to inform patients again postoperatively regarding the potential for residual symptoms or delayed recurrence. As for patients with only one LSR remaining, their handling may rely more on the experiences of neurosurgeons, but these patients can generally be expected to have a relatively favorable long-term prognosis.

We observed that compared to single-branch LSR monitoring, dual-branch LSR monitoring typically prolongs the surgical procedure by 3 to 5 min due to electrode placement and baseline recording. However, this additional time can be compensated by guiding intraoperative decision-making and reducing unnecessary exploration. Considering the balance between sensitivity and practicality, we are more inclined to recommend the more sensitive dual-branch LSR monitoring. The use of standardized equipment and trained personnel ensures minimal disruption to surgical workflow. And two-branch monitoring requires no additional specialized equipment beyond standard IONM systems, making it feasible for most neurosurgical centers with existing IONM capabilities. Moreover, a recent study shows that even for patients who have previously received botulinum toxin injections, two-branch LSR monitoring is still feasible for their surgical treatment [23].

Here comes a new question. Can multi-branch LSR monitoring provide increased support for neurosurgeons and neurophysiologists? A study published in 2020 highlighted the use of four-branch monitoring during MVD for pHFS, demonstrating that this new method can effectively improve the positive detection rate [24]. However, from our point of view, the potential increase in the application value of multi-branch LSR may be somewhat limited. According to our results, two-branch LSR monitoring can already provide a reasonably accurate indication of a favorable long-term outcome under the premise that both LSRs have disappeared. In contrast, multi-branch LSR monitoring may increase the complexity of intraoperative interpretation and instead confuse neurophysiologists and neurosurgeons. Moreover, the positive detection rate of two-branch monitoring has demonstrated satisfactory performance. Perhaps in the future, we will design randomized controlled clinical trials to compare the application value of two- and multi-branch LSR monitoring in patients who underwent MVD.

Although fully acknowledging the application value of intraoperative LSR monitoring, we also need to recognize its limitations. First, LSR may not always be elicited intraoperatively, even if its presence is confirmed by preoperative electromyography. The application of two-branch monitoring can effectively alleviate this problem. Second, delayed relief can result in false-positive cases. The delayed relief can be understood through the classical hypothesis concerning the mechanism of pHFS, which proposes that pHFS is not solely induced by the mechanical pulsations of the responsible vessels against the facial nerve but also arises from demyelination of the nerve and/or increased activity in the facial motor nucleus caused by neurovascular compression. Considering that delayed relief is inevitable, but patients will ultimately have a good long-term outcome, it does not warrant excessive concern. Notably, two latest publications suggested that the polyphasic morphology of LSR induced by temporal branch stimulation can predict delayed relief [25, 26]. We need to verify this finding in our future research. Third, the presence of false-negative cases. For other commonly used IONM modalities, false negatives often correlate with dysfunction observed right after surgery and are primarily induced by technical issues. Nevertheless, HFS may recur due to many postoperative reasons, the most important of which is the displacement of the pad. As a result, it is challenging to ascertain whether the false negative of LSR is a “true” one. Lastly, technical factors can also play a role, as they can be influenced by the diversity in IONM equipment and systems and variations in standardized procedures and practices for intraoperative LSR monitoring across different medical centers. Recognizing these limitations can help us better interpret intraoperative LSR changes and design future research.

This study has several limitations that should be noted. First, standardized spasticity severity scales were not utilized in the current study, which may limit the objectivity and comprehensiveness of our outcome assessments. Accordingly, we recommend that future research incorporate validated assessment tools to improve the rigor and interpretability of outcome evaluations. Moreover, the single-center design and retrospective nature of this study may introduce selection bias, potentially affecting the generalizability of our findings. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to further validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

In the current study, we confirmed the application value of intraoperative ZYG-LSR and MAN-LSR monitoring during MVD in patients with pHFS. In addition, we quantitatively described the clinical significance of two-branch LSR monitoring. The findings can aid neurophysiologists and neurosurgeons in interpreting intraoperative LSR changes more effectively, ultimately improving surgical strategy adjustment and patient expectation management.
