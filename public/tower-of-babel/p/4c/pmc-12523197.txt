Association of Hormonal Status With Sarcopenia, Frailty and Outcome in Patients With Decompensated Cirrhosis
Authors: Magdalini Adamantou, Eleni Pergantina, Aikaterini Kamiliou, Nikolaos Rachiotis, Vasileios Lekakis, Lampros Chrysavgis, George Papatheodoridis, Anna Angelousi, Evangelos Cholongitas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSarcopenia and frailty have been associated with hormonal dysregulation in non‐cirrhotic populations. The aim of the study was to evaluate hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal and gonadal (HPA‐HPG) axes as well as growth hormone/insulin‐like growth factor‐1 (GH/IGF‐1) levels in patients with cirrhosis and their correlation with reduced muscle mass and frailty.MethodsOne hundred twenty‐four patients with decompensated cirrhosis were prospectively included. Clinical and laboratory variables, including hormonal assessment, were evaluated. MELD‐Na and Child‐Turcotte‐Pugh scores were calculated. Muscle mass was estimated through mid‐arm muscle circumference and dual energy X‐ray absorptiometry. Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) and Liver Frailty Index (LFI) were also calculated.ResultsAmong the studied hormones, low DHEAS levels were the only independent factor associated with abnormal SPPB and LFI [Odds ratio (OR): 1.11, p = 0.019 and OR: 0.97, p = 0.005 respectively]. Separate analysis on the basis of gender revealed that DHEAS was independently associated with abnormal SPPB and LFI only in men. Δ4 androstenedione [Hazard Ratio (HR): 0.64, p = 0.038] and MELD‐Na score (HR: 1.108, p = 0.0057) were the only prognostic factors independently associated with mortality. MELD‐Na was significantly associated with the outcome in both male and female patients (HR: 1.052, p = 0.04 and HR: 1.22, p = 0.009, respectively), whereas Δ4 androstenedione was significantly associated only in men (HR: 0.47, p = 0.01).ConclusionsOur study is the first to evaluate HPA‐HPG and GH/IGF‐1 dysregulation in the same cohort of patients with decompensated cirrhosis. DHEAS was the only hormone correlated with frailty parameters and physical function, whereas low Δ4 androstenedione was associated with adverse outcomes in men.

Summary Reduction of muscle mass/physical function and frailty are common in patients with decompensated cirrhosis, whereas hormonal changes are precipitating factors for these conditions.For the first time, we found that low Δ4 androstenedione was an independent prognostic factor associated with adverse outcomes in the total cohort and in men.In addition, low DHEAS levels were independently associated with abnormal Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) and Liver Frailty Index (LFI) in the total cohort and in men.

Introduction

Frailty is the clinical state of multiple system impairment, which leads to increased vulnerability to stressors because of the decline of physiologic reserves [1]. Reduced physical function is an important part of frailty, referring to a progressive decrease in muscle strength and/or function, which may adversely affect the ability to perform daily activities [1, 2]. Sarcopenia, a syndrome characterised by progressive and generalised loss of muscle mass, has been evaluated as a risk factor and an important component of physical frailty [2]. Although it was first introduced as a medical term in 1989 in order to describe the age‐related decrease in muscle mass [1, 2], it is currently used, as well, to define the loss of muscle mass related to systemic diseases.

In patients with cirrhosis, the definition of the aforementioned terms has a more operational character and refers to sarcopenia as loss of muscle mass, whereas physical frailty is considered the clinical presentation of impaired muscle contractile function instead of the generalized term of frailty [3]. The prevalence of frailty and sarcopenia is relatively high in patients with cirrhosis [4, 5], whereas both conditions are associated with increased risk for liver‐related complications and adverse outcomes before and after liver transplantation (LT) [4, 5, 6]. Although the exact mechanisms implicated in their pathogenesis have not been elucidated yet, several common factors have been proposed to contribute independently or synergistically, including malnutrition, elevated myostatin levels, mitochondrial dysfunction, endotoxemia with hyperinflammatory state and hormonal dysregulations [7, 8]. Even though the latter have been associated with the development of sarcopenia and frailty in several extra‐hepatic chronic diseases [9, 10, 11], only a few studies have evaluated this association in patients with cirrhosis to date.

In fact, the hypothalamic–pituitary‐gonadal (HPG) axis is the most studied hormonal system concerning sarcopenia and frailty in cirrhosis. HPG axis impairment has been related to gonadal dysfunction and ultimately low serum testosterone levels, notably in male patients [12], which are particularly correlated with sarcopenia, frailty, increased rate of hepatic decompensation and mortality [12]. Despite the fact that the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis is also known to be affected and adrenal insufficiency is frequent in cirrhosis [13], there is only one recent study in the literature evaluating the association of cortisol with frailty in a cohort of patients with end‐stage liver disease, showing that the frail group had lower cortisol levels compared to the not frail [14]. On the other hand, no study has ever assessed sarcopenia in this setting. In addition, regarding the adrenal steroids, lower levels of dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) have been associated with worse outcomes in patients with cirrhosis and septic shock [15], whereas there is only one study [16] examining physical function in cirrhosis in which DHEAS levels were measured in a small group of patients but not tested for significance comparing sarcopenic and non‐sarcopenic patients. Δ4 androstenedione has never been evaluated in the setting of sarcopenia and frailty in patients with cirrhosis, but low Δ4 androstenedione serum levels have been associated with more advanced stages of liver disease [17]. Finally, dysregulation of growth hormone (GH) and insulin growth factor (IGF)‐1 axis has also been found to be associated with sarcopenia and frailty in patients with cirrhosis [18, 19]. Nevertheless, to our knowledge up to the present, there is no study evaluating the changes of all three axes (HPG, HPA and GH‐IGF‐1) and their association with sarcopenia and frailty in the same cohort of patients with cirrhosis, as well as their impact on prognosis.

Thus, the aim of our study was to evaluate for the first time the HPG, HPA and GH‐IGF‐1 axes and their association with low muscle mass/strength, frailty and prognosis of patients with decompensated cirrhosis, including both genders and various etiologies of liver disease.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

In this prospective cohort study, all consecutive adult patients (≥ 18 years old) with decompensated cirrhosis visiting our liver clinics for pre‐LT waitlist evaluation from January 2020 to December 2023 were enrolled. Diagnosis of cirrhosis was based on liver biopsy or a combination of clinical and radiological findings. Decompensation was defined by the presence of any of the following: ascites, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), variceal bleeding or non‐obstructive jaundice. Patients on medications containing steroids in any form (e.g., tablets, inhalers and ointments) or hormonal replacement were excluded. Other exclusion criteria were overt HE, acute bleeding or infection during the last month, need for combined kidney and liver transplantation, previous LT, pregnancy and infection with human immunodeficiency virus. Patients had no variceal bleeding, HE or infection at baseline and during the last month before their enrollment. The patients' follow‐up continued until August 2024 or until death or LT occurrence.

The study was conducted in Laiko General Hospital, a tertiary hospital located in Athens, Greece, and was approved by the ethics committee of the local Institutional Review Board. All personal data were protected according to the 2017 Declaration of Helsinki [20]. Written informed consent was collected from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study.

Clinical Data and Laboratory Measurements

Medical history was obtained from every patient enrolled in the study, including demographic and clinical characteristics such as age, sex, cause of cirrhosis, previous history of cirrhosis complications, presence of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), comorbidities and current medications. The whole of the women included in the study was postmenopausal. Anthropometric data were also collected, including patients' height and morning weight measured with a calibrated height and weighing scale on the day of the baseline visit for the calculation of body mass index (BMI).

On the same day, morning blood samples were collected from each patient preceded by at least 15 min of rest in a quiet setting and overnight fasting. Full blood count, serum creatinine, albumin, total protein, electrolytes, liver function tests including aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, gamma glutamyltransferase, as well as clotting profile with international normalised ratio (INR) and fibrinogen were measured. On that day, the severity of liver disease was determined by calculating the Model for end‐stage liver disease (MELD)‐sodium (MELD‐Na) [21] and the Child–Pugh (CTP) [22] scores.

Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) levels were measured with chemiluminescent assay (Liaison, DiaSorin), serum cortisol (SC), DHEAS, sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG), estradiol (E2) and total testosterone (sTesto) with electrochemiluminescence (Combass e801 Roche). Δ4 androstenedione, 17‐OH‐progesterone (17‐OHP), IGF‐1 and GH, as well as a 24‐h urine cortisol collection, were measured with radioimmunoassay. Free testosterone (fTesto) was calculated indirectly using the Vermeulen formula [23]. All biochemical functional tests were performed in the morning of patients' evaluation in our clinic between 8.00 and 8.30 am in the same laboratory.

Evaluation of Muscle Mass

On the day of hormonal evaluation, every subject was evaluated for sarcopenia findings. Assessment of muscle mass was based on mid‐arm muscle circumference (MAMC) and dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DEXA). The former was calculated with the evaluation of mid‐upper arm circumference (MAC). MAC was measured on the dominant arm, at the midpoint between the tip of the shoulder (acromion) and the tip of the elbow (olecranon), with the patient standing, using a flexible but inelastic measuring tape [24, 25], whereas triceps skin fold (TSF) was measured at the same point of the arm parallel to the horizontal axis, posteriorly, using a Lange skinfold caliper [24]. MAC and TSF were measured by the same operator, and they were then used for MAMC calculation through the MAC (cm) − 3.14 × TSF (cm) equation [26]. Recently proposed MAMC cutoff points ≤ 21.5 cm for women and ≤ 24.2 cm for men were used to define the presence of moderate/severe muscle mass depletion in patients with cirrhosis [25].

Regarding DEXA, we calculated Appendicular Skeletal Muscle Mass (ASM) as an indicator of muscle quantity using the manufacturers' standard protocols [Horizon W (S/N 300472M)]. ASM was then adjusted to body size using height squared in order to calculate appendicular skeletal mass index (ASM/height [2], ASMI), whereas cutoff < 7.0 kg/m2 for men and < 5.5 kg/m2 for women, as suggested by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP2), defined the presence of depletion of skeletal muscle mass [2].

Evaluation of Physical Function and Frailty

On the same day, muscle strength was assessed by measuring handgrip strength of the dominant hand with a calibrated pneumatic handheld dynamometer, and the average of three measurements was used [27]. Patients remained seated during the measurement with their forearms placed on armrests. In addition, the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) was calculated using gait speed, the chair stand test and a balance test. We used the cutoff < 10 points as an indicator of poor physical performance on the basis of literature data focused on cirrhosis [1]. Finally, patients' frailty status was assessed and categorised according to the Liver Frailty Index (LFI) [27]. LFI is based on gender, the handgrip strength of the dominant hand, the time needed for 5 chair stands and a balance test. Values > 4.4 define frail patients, whereas scores < 3.2 define robust. Patients with scores between 3.2 and 4.4 are characterised as pre‐frail [1, 28].

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables with normal distribution were described by mean ± standard deviation (SD) and with abnormal distribution by median (ranges), whereas their comparisons between groups were made by Student's t‐test and Mann–Whitney U‐test, respectively. Qualitative variables were presented by absolute (N) and relative (%) frequencies, and their ratios were compared by corrected chi‐square or two‐sided Fisher's exact test. Multivariate analysis was performed by stepwise logistic regression models including all variables with significant associations (p < 0.05) in the univariate analyses. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were also performed after adjusting for confounders. The area under a receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was used for the evaluation of the discriminative ability of the independent variables [29]. Patients' survival was calculated by Kaplan–Meier curves, and its associations were assessed using the log‐rank test. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed in the context of competing risks, with death as the primary outcome and LT as the competing event. These analyses were conducted using the Fine–Gray sub‐distribution hazards model to estimate the effect of covariates on the cumulative incidence of death while accounting for the competing risk of LT [30]. Factors with p < 0.05 in the univariate Cox regression analysis were entered into the multivariate model. Multivariate competing risk analyses were further performed after adjusting for confounders. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Whenever two or more hormones from the same axis were significant in univariate analysis, every multivariate analysis was performed adjusting for hormonal variables implicated in the same endocrine axis on the basis of both statistical significance in univariate analysis and biological plausibility, regardless of whether they were significant in univariate analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 29.0, IBM) and appropriate R packages within R software (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 131 patients were evaluated, but seven were excluded because of steroid administration (n = 2) and incomplete hormonal profile and/or unavailable DEXA result (n = 5). Thus, 124 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the study [85 (68.5%) males, mean age 55.6 ± 10.3 years]. Their baseline characteristics are shown in Tables 1, 2. The median follow‐up was 15 (range: 4–56) months. The mean MELD‐Na and CTP scores were 15.4 ± 6.6 and 7.6 ± 2.4, respectively. Alcohol was the main cause of cirrhosis (n = 48, 38.7%), followed by cholestatic liver diseases [primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) and primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)] (n = 23, 18.5%). Past medical history of variceal bleeding, HE, or spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) was documented in 26.6%, 27.4%, and 14.5% of patients, respectively. The hormonal profile, skeletal muscle mass and physical function/frailty characteristics are shown in Table 2. In total, 78 (62.9%) and 68 (54.8%) patients had low MAMC and ASMI, respectively; 37 (29.8%) had poor physical performance (i.e., SPPB < 10), whereas 45 (36.2%) were frail.

Characteristics of Patients With or Without Reduced Muscle Mass

MAMC

Our cohort was divided into two groups: group 1 (n = 78) included patients with abnormal MAMC (i.e., ≤ 21.5 cm and ≤ 24.2 cm for women and men, respectively), and group 2 (n = 46) included patients with normal MAMC. Patients in group 1, compared to those in group 2, had significantly more severe liver disease (i.e., higher MELD‐Na score: 16.2 ± 7 vs. 13.8 ± 6, p = 0.03) associated with higher mean creatinine (0.99 ± 0.42 vs. 0.8 ± 0.17 mg/dL, p = 0.013). Otherwise, all the other variables were not statistically different between the two groups (Table 3). The findings were similar when men and women were evaluated separately.

ASMI

On the basis of ASMI measurements from DEXA scan, patients were divided into two groups: group 1 (n = 68) included patients with reduced muscle mass (i.e., abnormal ASMI: men < 7.0 kg/m2, women < 5.5 kg/m2) and group 2 (n = 56) included patients with normal muscle mass according to gender‐based cutoffs. Patients in group 1, compared to those in group 2, had significantly higher MELD‐Na score (16.2 ± 6.38 vs. 14.1 ± 6.78, p = 0.04) associated with higher mean creatinine levels (1.03 ± 0.44 vs. 0.78 ± 0.22 mg/dL, p = 0.015); otherwise, all the other variables were similar between the two groups (Table 4). Characteristics of Patients on the Basis of Physical Function and Frailty

SPPB

Patients with abnormal SPPB test (< 10, n = 37), compared to patients with normal SPPB test (≥ 10, n = 87), had higher CTP score (8.36 vs. 7.34, p = 0.038), but lower median levels of DHEAS (22.8 vs. 36.45 μg/dL, p = 0.011), 17‐OHP (0.96 vs. 1.2, p = 0.021), and IGF‐1 (34.7 vs. 38, p = 0.024) (Table 5). After adjusting for MELD‐Na, Δ4 androstenedione, and sTesto, DHEAS was also independently associated with abnormal SPPB (OR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.002–1.34, p = 0.027). In addition, DHEAS remained an independent factor (OR: 1.018, 95% CI: 1.001–1.035, p = 0.035) when 17‐OHP was excluded from multivariate analysis, whereas 17‐OHP was not independently associated with SPPB when DHEAS was excluded. The comparison for men and women separately is presented in Table S1. Interestingly, the findings of multivariate analysis in the total cohort for DHEAS were confirmed only in men (OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.018–1.21, p = 0.02; after adjustment, OR: 1.019, 95% CI: 1.002–1.036, p = 0.032), whereas IGF‐1 (OR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.13, p = 0.03) was the only factor independently associated with SPPB in women.

LFI

Since handgrip strength is a component of LFI calculation, no further evaluation was performed on the basis of this variable. Instead, our cohort was divided into two groups: frail patients (LFI > 4.4, n = 45) and robust and prefrail patients (LFI ≤ 4.4, n = 79). Frail patients, compared to robust and prefrail, had older age (59.7 vs. 52.6, p < 0.001), a more frequent history of diabetes mellitus (31.1% vs. 15.1%, p = 0.03), and higher mean creatinine (1.13 vs. 0.8 mg/dL, p = 0.04), but significantly lower median levels of sTesto (1.4 vs. 2.3 ng/mL, p = 0.01), DHEAS (23.4 vs. 27.5 μg/dL, p = 0.03) and GH (4.9 vs. 8.4 ng/mL, p = 0.012) (Table 6). In multivariate logistic regression analysis, DHEAS was the only factor independently associated with frailty (OR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99, p = 0.005), but it had low discriminative ability (AUC: 0.63, 95% CI: 0.58–0.69). After adjusting for MELD‐Na, Δ4 androstenedione, and 17‐OHP, DHEAS was also independently associated with frailty (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, p = 0.015). In addition, DHEAS remained an independent factor (OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, p = 0.019) when sTesto was excluded from the multivariate analysis, whereas sTesto was not independently associated with frailty when DHEAS was excluded. The comparison for men and women separately is presented in Table S2. The findings of the multivariate analysis in the total cohort for DHEAS were confirmed only in men (OR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.98, p = 0.01; after adjustment, OR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99, p = 0.046), whereas GH (OR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.91–0.98, p = 0.035) was the only factor independently associated with LFI in women.

Factors Associated With the Outcome

During follow‐up [15 months, range (4–56)], 35 (28.2%) patients died and 22 (17.7%) patients underwent LT, whereas 67 (54%) were alive at the end of follow‐up. Univariate Cox regression analysis showed that MELD‐Na score [Hazard Ratio (HR): 1.12, p < 0.001], CTP score [HR: 1.36, p < 0.001], Δ4 androstenedione [HR: 0.62, p = 0.021], and SHBG [HR: 0.98, p < 0.001] were factors associated with the outcome (Table 7). Multivariate Cox regression in the presence of competing risks, including all the variables that were significant in univariate analysis, showed that MELD‐Na score [HR: 1.108, 95% CI (1.045–1.174), p = 0.0057] and Δ4 androstenedione [HR: 0.64, 95% CI (0.42–0.97), p = 0.038] were the only factors independently associated with mortality. Patients with Δ4‐androstenedione < 1.13 ng/mL (i.e., the median value in our cohort), compared to those with ≥ 1.13 ng/mL, had worse outcomes (log rank 5.42, p = 0.02) (Figure 1).

To explore potential sex‐dependent associations, we performed stratified analyses by sex. Discussion

This is the first study in the literature in which the impact of HPG, HPA and GH‐IGF‐1 axes on sarcopenia, physical function/frailty and outcome was evaluated in the same cohort of patients with cirrhosis. Notably, specific hormones were independent factors for poor physical function/frailty (DHEAS) and worse prognosis (Δ4 androstenedione). In addition, the MELD‐Na score was found to be an independent factor significantly associated with the presence of reduced muscle mass (i.e., low MAMC and ASMI) and worse outcome.

Alterations of the liver‐muscle axis are well known in cirrhosis [3]. HPG axis impairment and gonadal dysfunction also seem to cause hypogonadism in cirrhotic male patients [31]. Testosterone is an anabolic hormone that is normally synthesised under LH stimulation with a negative feedback loop, by 95% in the testes in men, with a small proportion in the adrenal glands, whereas in women, it is synthesised mainly in the ovaries and the rest in the adrenal glands as well. STesto is bound with high affinity to SHBG (44%–60%) and albumin (~55%), whereas a small percentage (1%–4%) refers to fTesto which is not measured directly in clinical practice [12]. The SHBG serum concentration is usually high in patients with cirrhosis, leading to even lower fTesto levels [7]. Portosystemic shunts cause decreased catabolism of oestrogens by the liver and elevated peripheral aromatization of androgens to oestrogens lead to elevated oestrogen levels, prohibited LH secretion, further SHBG production, and lower fTesto serum levels [32]. On the basis of literature data, decreased sTesto levels because of suppressed HPG axis [31, 33, 34] have been associated with increased morbidity and mortality in patients with end‐stage liver disease [12, 34]. However, regarding muscle strength and frailty, there is only one study by Sinclair et al. [35] showing that lower sTesto levels were correlated with dynapenia in a group of 95 male patients with cirrhosis, whereas testosterone was characterised as a significant predictor of frailty in univariate but not in multivariate analysis in a recent study by Ha et al. [36]. Similarly, in our study, lower sTesto levels were significantly associated with frailty only in univariate analysis, but this was not confirmed in multivariate analysis. In addition, although testosterone has been associated with sarcopenia in cirrhosis [32, 37, 38, 39] and its exogenous administration has been shown to improve skeletal muscle mass even after adjusting for confounding factors [12], sTesto was not associated with reduced muscle mass in our patients. This finding could be attributed to the methods used for muscle mass quantification (ASMI using DEXA and MAMC), since previous studies [32, 37, 38, 39] were based on quantification through computed tomography scan (CT scan). In fact, measurement of the L3 vertebrae psoas muscle using the CT scan is considered a more objective method compared to MAMC, as it is not influenced by interobserver variability [1], whereas, compared to DEXA scan, it is not affected by fluid retention [40].

Regarding adrenal androgens, DHEAS is produced by 17‐OHP and it is considered the main adrenal androgen and precursor for testosterone production through steroidogenesis in the adrenal glands. In fact, the majority of DHEAS is produced in the adrenal glands, whereas a small amount is synthesised in the gonads. DHEAS can be converted to dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), a desulfated form, which is then converted to Δ4 androstenedione and lastly testosterone [41]. Thus, testosterone production is closely linked with 17‐OHP, DHEAS and Δ4 androstenedione. These adrenal precursor forms of testosterone do not bind to SHBG and have a higher serum concentration compared to testosterone [42]. In our cohort, we evaluated—for the first time to our knowledge—not only testosterone (total and its free proportionate), but also its precursors 17‐OHP, DHEAS and Δ4 androstenedione in patients with decompensated cirrhosis and found that DHEAS, but not sTesto, was the only factor independently associated with SPPB test and frailty. These findings were also confirmed in men when we divided patients according to gender. In patients with liver disease, DHEAS has only been studied in the setting of acute on chronic liver failure, where lower DHEAS levels were associated with adverse outcomes in patients with cirrhosis and septic shock [15], as well as with advanced fibrosis in patients with chronic hepatitis C [43]. Thus, this is the first study reporting a strong association between low DHEAS levels and abnormal SPPB/LFI in patients with decompensated cirrhosis.

Moreover, we found out that in the total cohort, Δ4 androstenedione together with MELD‐Na were independently associated with mortality. Separate analysis according to patients' gender revealed that in men, Δ4 androstenedione and sTesto were significantly associated with the outcome when we excluded each hormone from another in the analysis because of their shared pathway in steroidogenesis [41]. In women, only MELD‐Na was associated with the outcome. Interestingly, in literature, only testosterone has been evaluated in the setting of cirrhosis regarding patients' outcome, and previous studies in men are in accordance with our findings [12]. Regarding Δ4 androstenedione and its effect on prognosis, there are no available studies in patients with cirrhosis, although lower Δ4 androstenedione levels have been associated with advanced liver disease stage in male patients [17]. The discrepancy in our findings between genders could be attributed to the relatively small number of female patients included in our study.

Compared to previous studies [16, 18, 19] evaluating GH‐IGF‐1 changes in patients with cirrhosis, our study assessed the whole endocrinological axis instead of focusing on GH and IGF‐1 only. In general, impaired muscle protein synthesis and increased myostatin levels are associated with GH resistance and reduced IGF‐1 levels in patients with cirrhosis [44, 45]. The latter has been shown to be a predictive factor of the outcome of patients since it has been correlated with a higher risk of further decompensation, acute‐on‐chronic liver failure, and mortality related to liver disease in a recent study [46]. Although GH supplementation has been shown to improve parameters of muscle mass, strength, and frailty in patients with decompensated cirrhosis [47], GH levels have only been evaluated in one study, in which there was no association with sarcopenia and frailty [18]. Similarly, in our study, lower median GH levels were associated with frailty only in univariate analysis, whereas GH was the only factor associated with frailty in women but not in men. IGF‐1, on the other hand, has been correlated with sarcopenia parameters in at least two studies [18, 19], and with frailty and physical function in only one [18] of two relevant studies [16, 18]. Interestingly, in our study, IGF‐1 levels were associated with the SPPB test only in univariate analysis in the total cohort and represented the only factor independently associated with SPPB in women.

Considering the differences between men and women, our findings suggest that there might be alterations in hormonal factors related to frailty and physical performance between genders in the cirrhosis setting. Male patients were more prone to the impact of HPG axis deficiencies on muscle function and frailty, whereas these disturbances were associated mainly with alteration of the GH‐IGF‐1 axis in female patients. This could be attributed to the fact that women have lower baseline testosterone levels [48]; thus, fluctuations of sTesto and precursor forms probably will not have as massive an effect as in men. However, the small number of women in our cohort might statistically affect our results; thus, a larger number of female participants is needed to better evaluate these associations.

To date, in patients with cirrhosis, testosterone supplementation has been the main pharmacologic intervention because of its anabolic effect on muscle mass [12]. Recently, GH administration led to an increase in skeletal muscle index, MAMC, handgrip strength and LFI improvement without serious adverse events in patients with decompensated cirrhosis, but no improvement in the 12‐month survival was noted [47]. DHEAS and Δ4 androstenedione, on the other hand, have never been evaluated so far as possible pharmacologic interventions in the setting of cirrhosis.

We acknowledge that there are some limitations, including that our study is a single‐centre one. Furthermore, we measured muscle mass with a DEXA scan instead of the evaluation of L3 muscle mass area with the CT scan (which is considered the gold standard method) [40] although we did use ASMI for muscle quantification, an index that is not influenced by ascites presentation since it is based only on appendicular lean mass measurements, making the results more accurate [8, 49]. DEXA scan was one of the main methods we used because of less radiation exposure for the patient, compared to CT scan, and given the fact that it is generally a less expensive technique [1, 8] with results similar to those of the CT scan on the basis of previous studies [50]. In our study, we also provided results on the basis of muscle mass quantification through the calculation of MAMC using the cutoff points suggested in the literature [25]. These cutoffs were evaluated in a Brazilian population; thus, they might not be the optimal choice for measurements in other populations, and secondly, they were not validated compared to a gold standard method. Finally, our study is an observational one; thus, our findings might be influenced by factors we did not assess, making it difficult to establish causality.

In conclusion, this is the first study in the literature evaluating HPG, HPA and GH‐IGF‐1 axes in the setting of sarcopenia, physical function/frailty and outcome in patients with decompensated cirrhosis, irrespective of gender or aetiology of the liver disease. Low DHEAS levels were correlated with abnormal SPPB and LFI in men, whereas low Δ4 androstenedione was associated with the adverse outcome in the total cohort, as well as in men, but not in women. Our findings could contribute to the optimization of the accuracy of prognosis and the effective management of these patients in everyday clinical practice.
