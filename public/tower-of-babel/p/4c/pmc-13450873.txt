When Dysphagia Is Not Psychogenic: An Atypical Presentation of Schatzki Ring in a Patient With Schizophrenia
Authors: Ngozi T Akueme, Adedeji O Adenusi, Nader Daoud, Mombien Guerrier, Muhammad Hasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Schatzki rings are benign distal esophageal rings that are often asymptomatic but may cause intermittent solid-food dysphagia or acute food bolus impaction. Symptoms are classically localized to the lower chest; however, atypical throat-localized symptoms may obscure the diagnosis, particularly in patients with psychiatric comorbidity. We report the case of a 74-year-old woman with schizophrenia, gastroesophageal reflux disease, chronic kidney disease stage 3, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia who presented with three days of globus sensation and progressive inability to swallow. Physical examination revealed salivary pooling without a visible oropharyngeal mass or mucosal lesion. A barium swallow study was attempted but could not be completed because the patient was unable to swallow the contrast. Given concern for esophageal obstruction, esophagogastroduodenoscopy was performed, revealing a 4-5 cm inspissated food bolus impacted at the gastroesophageal junction. The bolus was fragmented with a Roth net and completely removed endoscopically. A Schatzki ring was identified at the gastroesophageal junction and was traversed with moderate resistance. A 3 cm hiatal hernia and mild gastritis were also noted. The patient had complete symptom resolution after endoscopic therapy and was started on pantoprazole and scheduled for outpatient follow-up with interventional gastroenterology for esophageal dilatation. This case highlights the importance of avoiding premature attribution of dysphagia to functional or psychiatric causes. Inability to tolerate oral intake, salivary pooling, and food bolus impaction are obstructive features that should prompt urgent endoscopic evaluation.

Introduction

A Schatzki ring, also known as a lower esophageal ring, is a thin, symmetric, circumferential narrowing of the distal esophagus located at or near the gastroesophageal junction. It is commonly classified as a benign B-type esophageal ring and is composed predominantly of mucosa and submucosa [1,2]. Although many Schatzki rings are asymptomatic, symptomatic rings are an important structural cause of intermittent solid-food dysphagia and food bolus impaction [2,3].

The classic presentation is episodic, non-progressive dysphagia to solids, often described as food getting stuck in the lower chest after ingestion of meat or bread. This presentation is sometimes referred to as steakhouse syndrome [2,4]. Ring diameter is clinically relevant because narrower rings are more likely to produce symptoms; rings less than 13 mm are usually symptomatic, whereas rings greater than 20 mm are rarely symptomatic [2]. Schatzki rings are frequently associated with gastroesophageal reflux disease and hiatal hernia, although the causal nature of these associations remains incompletely understood [2,3,5,6].

Atypical presentations may delay recognition, particularly when symptoms are localized to the throat rather than the lower chest. In patients with psychiatric comorbidity, throat discomfort, globus sensation, and difficulty swallowing may be misattributed to functional or psychogenic etiologies. We present a case of a Schatzki ring in a 74-year-old woman with schizophrenia who presented with globus sensation, salivary pooling, inability to swallow, and food bolus impaction. The case emphasizes the need to recognize obstructive features and avoid diagnostic anchoring.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old woman with a medical history of hypertension, schizophrenia, chronic kidney disease stage 3, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, osteoporosis, and gastroesophageal reflux disease presented with a three-day history of globus sensation and progressive inability to swallow. She described a persistent sensation of a lump in her throat and reported using her finger to remove retained food from her throat after attempting to eat. This was her first episode of this nature.

She denied odynophagia, oral rash, oral ulcers, unintentional weight loss, chest pain, abdominal pain, and constipation. Although she had a known history of gastroesophageal reflux disease, she denied active reflux symptoms during the preceding year. She had no known medication allergies.

On presentation, her vital signs were normal. Her weight was 69.4 kg, and her body mass index was 26.2 kg/m2. Physical examination was notable for pooling of saliva in the oral cavity. There were no oral ulcers, mucosal lesions, or visible oropharyngeal masses. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable.

A barium swallow study was initially requested; however, the patient was unable to swallow the contrast material. Because of acute inability to swallow, salivary pooling, and failure to tolerate oral contrast, there was concern for a high-grade obstructive process. Gastroenterology was consulted, and the patient was kept nil per os. Otolaryngologic and psychiatric etiologies were considered because of throat-localized symptoms and psychiatric comorbidity; however, the obstructive features appropriately shifted evaluation toward endoscopy. On day 2 of admission, esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed a large, approximately 4-5 cm, inspissated, coalesced food bolus impacted at the gastroesophageal junction. The food bolus was fragmented using a Roth net, and complete endoscopic removal was achieved. A Schatzki ring was identified at the gastroesophageal junction and was traversed with an adult gastroscope, although moderate resistance was encountered, suggesting partial luminal obstruction. A 3-cm hiatal hernia was also noted. Mild gastritis was seen and biopsied. The duodenal bulb and second portion of the duodenum appeared normal. Esophageal biopsies were deferred during the index procedure because the EGD was performed urgently to relieve the impacted food bolus, and additional mucosal manipulation was avoided in the setting of acute impaction with local edema and mucosal trauma. Following the procedure, the patient was transferred back to the medical floor in stable condition. She was placed on a pureed diet for 24 hours, continued on her home medications, and started on pantoprazole 40 mg orally daily for eight weeks. She reported complete resolution of symptoms. Outpatient follow-up with gastroenterology and advanced endoscopy was recommended for further management, including consideration of definitive endoscopic therapy for the Schatzki ring.

Surgical pathology from the gastric biopsy demonstrated benign gastric mucosa with mild chronic nonspecific inflammation. Giemsa staining showed no definite Helicobacter pylori organisms. The submitted esophageal foreign body specimen consisted of food material, confirming food bolus impaction.

Discussion

This case illustrates an atypical presentation of Schatzki ring in which the predominant symptom was globus sensation rather than classic lower-chest solid-food dysphagia. Schatzki rings are commonly silent but may become clinically significant when luminal narrowing causes intermittent solid-food dysphagia or acute food-bolus impaction [2,3]. The patient’s throat-localized symptoms, psychiatric comorbidity, and absence of active reflux symptoms created potential for diagnostic anchoring. However, salivary pooling and inability to tolerate oral intake were important obstructive features that supported urgent endoscopic evaluation.

Globus sensation is often approached as an otolaryngologic, functional, or psychiatric complaint, particularly when patients describe a lump in the throat rather than food sticking in the chest. This distinction is clinically important because globus alone is generally not equivalent to true dysphagia. In this case, the patient had more than a globus sensation; she had progressive inability to swallow, retained food, salivary pooling, and inability to complete a contrast swallow. These features suggested impaired esophageal clearance and ultimately correlated with a large food bolus impacted at the lower esophagus.

Food bolus impaction requires emergent endoscopic evaluation when patients have complete esophageal obstruction or are unable to handle secretions. The American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy recommends emergent removal of esophageal food bolus impactions in patients with evidence of complete esophageal obstruction and advises avoiding contrast radiographic examinations before removal of foreign objects [7]. The World Society of Emergency Surgery similarly recommends emergency endoscopy for complete esophageal obstruction and urgent endoscopy within 24 hours for esophageal foreign bodies without complete obstruction [8]. In this patient, failure to swallow oral contrast, together with salivary pooling and inability to tolerate oral intake, supported concern for high-grade obstruction and the need for direct endoscopic evaluation.

In patients with chronic psychiatric illness, symptom reporting may be affected by communication barriers, altered perception of bodily symptoms, medication effects, or clinician bias; therefore, objective findings such as salivary pooling, inability to tolerate oral intake, and failed contrast swallowing should be prioritized when evaluating possible esophageal obstruction [9,10].

The differential diagnosis of new-onset dysphagia includes esophageal malignancy, peptic stricture, eosinophilic esophagitis, reflux esophagitis, pill-induced esophagitis, infectious esophagitis, esophageal webs, diverticula, extrinsic compression, and motility disorders such as achalasia [2]. Progressive dysphagia from solids to liquids, unintentional weight loss, anemia, or constitutional symptoms should raise concern for malignancy. In contrast, acute food impaction in the setting of a distal esophageal ring supports a structural obstructive process. Eosinophilic esophagitis remains an important consideration in patients presenting with food bolus impaction, even when a Schatzki ring is identified. United Kingdom guidelines on esophageal dilation recommend considering distal, mid, and proximal esophageal biopsies to exclude eosinophilic esophagitis in symptomatic Schatzki rings [11]. Esophageal biopsies were not documented in the available pathology from the index procedure; therefore, eosinophilic esophagitis could not be fully excluded and should be considered during follow-up endoscopy.

Management of symptomatic Schatzki ring is directed toward relief of obstruction and prevention of recurrence. Acute food bolus impaction requires endoscopic therapy, including removal, fragmentation, or advancement of the bolus when clinically appropriate. Definitive therapy for a symptomatic Schatzki ring typically involves endoscopic dilation using bougie or balloon techniques. Guidelines recommend a single graded dilation session to a relatively large diameter, usually 16-20 mm, for dysphagia related to Schatzki rings [11]. Proton pump inhibitor therapy after dilation is recommended because it reduces the risk of symptomatic relapse [11]. In this case, acute endoscopic therapy relieved the obstruction, and the patient was started on pantoprazole. Definitive dilation was deferred to outpatient advanced endoscopy follow-up, so the long-term impact on recurrence prevention is not yet known.

The association between Schatzki ring, hiatal hernia, and gastroesophageal reflux disease has been reported in prior studies, although the direction and mechanism of this relationship remain debated [2,3,5,6]. This patient had a known history of gastroesophageal reflux disease and a 3 cm hiatal hernia on index EGD, supporting the commonly observed coexistence of these conditions. Notably, the absence of active reflux symptoms did not exclude reflux-associated anatomy or distal esophageal pathology.

Conclusions

A Schatzki ring is a common but often underrecognized structural cause of intermittent solid-food dysphagia and food bolus impaction. Although patients classically describe food sticking in the lower chest, atypical presentations, such as a globus sensation and apparent throat obstruction, can occur. In patients with psychiatric comorbidity, clinicians should avoid premature attribution of dysphagia to functional or psychogenic causes when objective obstructive features are present.

This case demonstrates that salivary pooling, inability to tolerate oral intake, and acute-onset dysphagia are important warning signs of esophageal obstruction. EGD is both diagnostic and therapeutic when food bolus impaction is suspected. Early recognition, appropriate endoscopic management, acid suppression, and gastroenterology follow-up for definitive therapy are important for relieving symptoms, preventing recurrence, and avoiding diagnostic delay.
