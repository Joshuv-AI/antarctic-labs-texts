Interpretable machine learning model for predicting the risk of dysphagia in patients after cardiac surgery
Authors: Xuan Ren, Junchen Fan, Huajun Wang, Xiangdi Hu, Baizhi Chen, Man Zuo, Yelin Shen, Aoxiang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDysphagia is recognized as one of the most common severe complications following cardiac surgery, with the potential to result in adverse clinical outcomes, including aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, and prolonged hospitalization. Early identification of patients at high risk for postoperative dysphagia is critical for implementing targeted preventive interventions and improving clinical outcomes. However, traditional risk assessment approaches have inherent limitations in accurately predicting this complication. Accordingly, this study was designed to develop and validate a machine learning-based risk prediction model for the accurate identification of patients at high risk for developing dysphagia following cardiac surgery.MethodsA prospective observational cohort study design was used to recruit 573 patients undergoing cardiac surgery at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital between September 2024 and July 2025. Predictor variables included patient demographic characteristics, preoperative risk factors, intraoperative procedural variables, and clinical postoperative indicators. Eight distinct machine learning models were developed for comparison: logistic regression (LR), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), categorical boosting (CatBoost), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector machines (SVM), naive Bayes (NB), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and gradient boosting machine (GBM). The complete dataset was randomly divided into a 7:3 training-to-test split for model development and validation, respectively. Model performance was evaluated across multiple metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. Model calibration performance was assessed using calibration curves, while clinical utility was evaluated through decision curve analysis (DCA). The optimal-performing model was then interpreted and visualized using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to enhance model interpretability for clinical implementation.ResultsThe incidence of postoperative dysphagia among the study cohort was 23.03%. Among all evaluated machine learning models, the GBM demonstrated superior overall performance, achieving a test set AUC of 0.851 (95% CI: 0.781–0.908). This performance was statistically significantly better than that observed with all other models evaluated in the study. The key predictive factors identified by the model included patient age, diabetes, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, tracheal intubation time, gastric intubation time, and sedative drug use duration. Additionally, the GBM model demonstrated excellent calibration performance and high clinical utility, indicating significant potential for integration into routine clinical practice to enable accurate, individualized patient risk assessment.ConclusionThe machine learning model developed in this study demonstrated the ability to accurately identify patients at high risk for postoperative dysphagia, providing a robust foundation for implementing targeted preventive interventions in clinical practice. Integration of this validated machine learning model into standard clinical workflows holds significant promise for reducing the incidence of dysphagia-related complications and improving overall postoperative patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03554-3.Keywords: Cardiac surgery, Dysphagia, Intensive care, Machine learning, Predictive models

Introduction

Dysphagia is defined as a clinical symptom characterized by the inability to safely and effectively transport food or liquids from the mouth to the stomach due to structural or functional defects in the jaw, lips, tongue, soft palate, pharynx, larynx, or esophagus. The occurrence of dysphagia is frequently associated with underlying diseases, medical interventions, or iatrogenic factors, including tracheal intubation [1, 2]. Dysphagia is considered a common and significant complication among patients who have undergone cardiac surgery. The incidence of dysphagia varies considerably based on factors, including intubation duration, with reported ranges from 5.6% to 67.5% [3]. Although the pathogenesis of dysphagia in this population remains not completely understood, prolonged tracheal intubation is widely recognized as a fundamental risk factor. The pathophysiological process of dysphagia involves multiple overlapping factors, which may include mechanical injury to the oropharynx and larynx [4, 5], impaired sensory and cognitive function [6, 7] and neurogenic muscle weakness [8–10].

Following cardiac surgery, postoperative dysphagia may result in severe adverse outcomes, potentially leading to serious complications, such as aspiration pneumonia and tracheoesophageal fistula [11]. Delayed oral feeding and malnutrition in patients may also result, thereby prolonging hospital stays and increasing mortality risks [12, 13]. Such factors significantly impair postoperative recovery and overall quality of life [14, 15]. For families of patients, this translates into significantly escalated treatment costs [16]; for public health systems, such complications also exacerbate the overall economic burden of healthcare [6], rendering them critical issues that require urgent resolution in the management of perioperative cardiac surgery.

Cardiac surgery, as a fundamental treatment for cardiovascular diseases, has experienced increasingly widespread clinical application due to an aging population and the rising incidence of cardiovascular diseases [17]. According to the American College of Surgeons’ Adult Cardiac Surgery Database (ACSD) data for 2023 [18], the global annual volume of cardiac surgeries has exceeded 9 million cases. The White Paper on Cardiac and Great Vessel Surgery and Extracorporeal Circulation, published by the Chinese Society of Extracorporeal Circulation (ChSECC), indicates that the total number of such surgeries nationwide in 2024 was approximately 360,000 [19]. These procedures are characterized by high complexity and risk, often requiring median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) support. Prolonged intubation, significant blood loss, and tissue trauma are endured by patients during surgery, with persistent systemic stress experienced postoperatively. Consequently, the risk of complications, including dysphagia, is substantially increased [20]. A prospective cohort study involving patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) confirmed that the average postoperative intubation duration was 27.05 h, with 30.5% of patients requiring intubation for over 24 h after admission to the intensive care unit [21]. This finding further emphasizes the elevated risk of postoperative dysphagia among patients undergoing cardiac surgery.

Timely and systematic screening for dysphagia, coupled with targeted interventions, has been demonstrated to effectively reduce postoperative complication rates while enhancing patient clinical outcomes and subjective experiences [22, 23]. Nevertheless, current clinical practice still lacks precise and efficient tools for risk prediction. Existing studies utilizing traditional logistic regression methods for constructing predictive models [24, 25] are constrained by limitations in variable coverage and face difficulties in capturing complex nonlinear associations, resulting in restricted predictive accuracy and clinical applicability. Furthermore, prior studies have not comprehensively identified the risk factors associated with postoperative dysphagia in cardiac surgery. A comprehensive integration of multidimensional information, such as patient baseline status, surgical procedures, and postoperative management, has not been achieved, making it challenging to fulfill clinical demands for individualized risk assessment and precise intervention.

Therefore, this study first conducts a systematic review of existing research progress and limitations through a comprehensive literature analysis. Subsequently, a systematic literature screening is conducted to identify risk predictors, ensuring a more comprehensive inclusion of risk factors. Multiple machine learning algorithm models are then constructed and compared for validation to identify the risk prediction model for postoperative dysphagia in cardiac surgery that demonstrates optimal predictive performance. This model not only maximizes predictive accuracy but also quantifies individual disease risk while identifying core, interpretable influencing factors. It provides clinicians with scientific evidence to identify high-risk populations and develop personalized intervention strategies that target modifiable risk factors. Ultimately, this study aims to reduce the incidence of postoperative dysphagia following cardiac surgery, improve patient survival rates and quality of life, and alleviate the burden on healthcare systems and socioeconomic pressures.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

A convenience sampling strategy was employed in this study to recruit patients undergoing cardiac surgery at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, Guangzhou, China, between September 2024 and July 2025. A total of 573 eligible patients were ultimately enrolled in the study (Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria included: (1) adult patients (≥ 18 years of age) who underwent cardiac surgery without pre-existing dysphagia; (2) successful endotracheal extubation performed in accordance with treating physician orders. Exclusion criteria included: (1) any medical condition that could potentially affect swallowing function, including vocal cord dysfunction, neuromuscular disorders, oropharyngeal or esophageal malignancies, prior neck surgery, or gastrointestinal disease that precluded oral intake; (2) in-hospital death before extubation; (3) performance of tracheostomy before extubation; (4) presence of psychiatric illness, impaired consciousness, or inability to cooperate with study procedures. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (KY2024-657-02). Clinical trial number: not applicable. All participants or their legal guardians have provided informed consent.

With a minimum training sample size of 384 cases, the validation group size was estimated to consist of approximately 165 cases according to the 7:3 ratio, resulting in a total required sample size of 549 cases. Ultimately, this study enrolled 573 cases, thereby satisfying the required sample size criteria.

Research tools

Inclusion of variables

Through a systematic literature review and under the guidance of domain experts, we extracted a comprehensive set of 59 perioperative clinical variables. These comprised: (1) demographic and preoperative characteristics: age, sex, body-mass index (BMI), smoking, drinking, documented comorbidities (including diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, cerebrovascular disease, heart disease, surgical history, previous cardiac surgery, atrial fibrillation, unstable angina, stable angina, myocardial infarction and dyslipidaemia), perioperative stroke, perioperative sepsis, NYHA cardiac functional class, preoperative albumin, preoperative CREA, left-ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), activities of daily living (ADL) score, NNIS classification, ASA and Mallampati airway score. (2) Intraoperative variables: urgency, surgical procedure type, operative time, intraoperative bleeding, intraoperative blood transfusion, intraoperative infusion volume, anaesthesia time, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) usage, CPB time, aortic cross-clamp (ACC) time, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) time, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support, intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) usage, number of intraoperative defibrillations, difficulty with intubation, endotracheal tube model, intraoperative transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE), number of TEE, TEE time. (3) Postoperative variables: use of sedative drugs, sedative drug use duration, ciprofol Injection, dexmedetomidine, remimazolam, remifentanil, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, occurrence of delirium, gastric intubation, gastric intubation time, and tracheal intubation time. In this study, both sedative drug use duration and gastric intubation time were defined as the cumulative duration from the initiation of endotracheal intubation to the completion of postoperative extubation.

Evaluation of swallowing disorders

The Standardized Swallowing Assessment (SSA) was first developed and validated by Ellul et al. [26] in 1996 as a standardized instrument for the systematic evaluation of deglutitive function. The assessment is conducted in three sequential stages: (1) preliminary evaluation of patient consciousness level, head and trunk control, respiratory status, lip closure, soft palate mobility, laryngeal function, gag reflex, and voluntary cough; (2) performance of a water-swallow test with three discrete 5-mL boluses, during which laryngeal excursion, repetitive swallowing, coughing, stridor, and immediate post-swallow laryngeal competence are carefully observed; and (3) administration of an additional 60 mL of water if no abnormalities are detected in stage 2, while monitoring for successful test completion and any occurrence of cough or stridor. The presence of any deviation from normal assessment parameters at any stage is classified as a positive SSA result, whereas successful completion of all stages without adverse events indicates intact swallowing function. In this study, dysphagia screening using the SSA was performed four hours after endotracheal extubation in patients who had undergone cardiac surgery. The evaluation was conducted by the researcher themselves.

Statistical methods

IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27.0 software was used for all descriptive statistical analyses. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, with intergroup comparisons performed using the independent samples t-test. Continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution were presented as median (interquartile range), with intergroup comparisons performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages, %), with intergroup comparisons performed using Pearson’s chi-square tests. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

To ensure high data quality, screening for missing data was performed as an initial step. Variables with a missing value rate exceeding 20% were excluded to reduce potential bias associated with severe data incompleteness. For the remaining variables with minor missing data, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was used to handle missing values. After completing data preprocessing, the full dataset was randomly divided into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio for model construction and validation, respectively. Variable selection was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method, implemented using the glmnet package in R version 4.4.1. Ten-fold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ₁ₛₑ), and variables corresponding to this optimal parameter were retained as the final features incorporated into the predictive model.

Eight machine learning algorithm models were constructed using the training set, including logistic regression (LR), adaptive boosting (AdaBoost), categorical boosting (CatBoost), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine (SVM), naive Bayes (NB), k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and gradient boosting machine (GBM). For each algorithm, five-fold cross-validation was used to tune model parameters and determine optimal parameter combinations, which enhanced overall model stability. Model performance was evaluated on the test set using the following metrics: area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. Decision curve analysis (DCA) and calibration curves were generated to further evaluate the clinical utility of the developed models.

Model interpretability was enhanced through the use of SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, which is based on cooperative game theory principles. This method quantifies the marginal contribution of each feature to individual sample prediction outcomes by calculating SHAP values. Both global visualizations (feature contribution rankings) and local visualizations (single-sample feature impact) are generated, which improves the overall interpretability of the predictive model.

Lasso regression for variable selection

The initial study dataset included 59 clinical variables. To develop a robust risk prediction model, Lasso regression was used for initial variable selection. Ten-fold cross-validation was utilized to determine the optimal regularization parameter (λ) for the Lasso regression model. λ₁ₛₑ (penalty coefficient corresponding to the standard error method) was used as the selection criterion. This regularization parameter balances model complexity and predictive performance, effectively reducing the number of retained variables while preserving clinically meaningful predictors. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, the Lasso regression model identified 8 variables as optimal predictors when the regularization parameter λ was set to 0.0532. These variables included patient age, diabetes, chronic lung disease, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, tracheal intubation time, gastric intubation time, and sedative drug use duration.

Comparison of ROC-AUC across machine-learning models

In terms of discriminative capability, the GBM model achieved the highest recall (0.940) and F1 score (0.893) among all evaluated models. The GBM model demonstrated greater performance balance compared to the second-best performing KNN model (recall = 0.940, F1 = 0.880) and exhibited a significant performance advantage over the XGBoost model (recall = 0.816, F1 = 0.633). The GBM model’s accuracy (0.825) approached optimal classification levels, being only slightly lower than that of the NB model (0.842), while its AUC value of 0.851 also ranked among the top-performing models. Collectively, these results demonstrate the comprehensive performance superiority of the GBM model. Model calibration performance for the GBM model was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow (HL) test and calibration curves. The Brier score was additionally calculated to assess consistency between predicted probabilities and actual clinical outcomes. The HL test revealed no statistically significant discrepancy between predicted probabilities and actual observed probabilities (χ²=11.421, P = 0.1790 > 0.05), indicating good model calibration. A Brier score of 0.124 (range: 0-0.25) further confirmed the high accuracy of the GBM model’s probability predictions. The calibration curve (Fig. 4) demonstrates that the GBM model’s predicted probabilities were closest to the perfect calibration diagonal, nearly overlapping within the moderate-risk range (0.3–0.7). This indicates that the model provides highly reliable predictions across different risk levels. From a clinical utility perspective, decision curve analysis (Fig. 5) demonstrates that the GBM model consistently ranks among the top-performing models in terms of net benefit across most risk threshold intervals. This demonstrates superior practical clinical decision-making value compared to other models, including AdaBoost and SVM. In summary, the GBM model demonstrates outstanding performance across three key domains: positive sample recognition capability, overall classification effectiveness, and practical clinical utility. This establishes the GBM model as the optimal predictive model for this study.

Model interpretation

To visually demonstrate how each variable influences the incidence of postoperative dysphagia, SHAP analysis was used to interpret the GBM model’s predictive outputs. The SHAP feature importance bar plot (Fig. 6) quantifies the mean absolute SHAP values for each variable. Sedative drug use duration was identified as the most influential predictor, followed by age, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, tracheal intubation time, chronic kidney disease, gastric intubation time, and chronic lung disease.

SHAP bar plot for variable ranking in the GBM model

The SHAP summary plot (Fig. 7) further visualizes the relationship between feature values and corresponding SHAP values. For sedative drug use duration, higher durations generally correlate with increased SHAP values. This indicates that longer sedative drug use duration during intubation is associated with an increased risk of developing postoperative dysphagia. Similarly, for the patient age feature, SHAP values demonstrate an upward trend with increasing age. This suggests that elderly patients have an increased risk of developing postoperative dysphagia. Among other features, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, prolonged tracheal intubation, chronic kidney disease, and extended gastric intubation placement all shift the model prediction toward a higher probability of developing postoperative dysphagia.

SHAP summary plot of variables in the GBM model

To demonstrate the model’s interpretability at the individual patient level, a representative patient case (Fig. 8) was selected for analysis. Key patient characteristics included age 67 years and a sedation duration of 5 h. SHAP analysis revealed that patient age contributed positively to the risk prediction value, increasing the predicted risk, while sedation duration contributed negatively, decreasing the predicted risk. After integrating all model features, the final predicted risk value was below the baseline average, indicating a low probability of developing postoperative dysphagia for this patient.

SHAP force plot

Collectively, these SHAP-derived interpretations not only validate the clinical plausibility of the GBM model but also suggest potential clinical strategies to mitigate the risk of postoperative dysphagia following cardiac surgery. These strategies include reducing perioperative sedative exposure, streamlining operative workflows, and implementing enhanced post-operative surveillance for elderly patients.

Discussion

Postoperative dysphagia is one of the most frequently encountered perioperative complications across surgical specialties. It not only elevates the risk of adverse events, including aspiration pneumonia and malnutrition, but also extends patients’ hospital stays and places an increased strain on healthcare resource allocation. Therefore, the development of an accurate, clinically actionable predictive model for postoperative dysphagia is critical to the early clinical identification of high-risk patients and the formulation of personalized intervention strategies. The predictive performance of eight machine learning algorithms was systematically compared using prospective cohort data from 573 patients in this study. The observed incidence of dysphagia was 23.03%, which aligns with the 5.6% to 67.5% range reported by Skoretz et al. [3], thereby validating the representativeness of the study sample. Among the eight candidate machine learning models evaluated, the GBM model exhibited the most favorable overall performance, attaining a test set AUC of 0.851 (95% CI 0.781–0.908), which falls within the high-accuracy range reported in similar clinical prediction studies. Additionally, the GBM model’s Recall (0.940) and F1 score (0.893) were significantly higher than those of all other candidate models. With respect to the model’s probabilistic prediction accuracy, results from the HL test and Brier score analysis revealed no statistically significant discrepancy between the predicted probabilities generated by the GBM model and the actual incidence of dysphagia observed among study participants. These findings confirm that the GBM model exhibits not only superior classification performance but also the capacity to reliably quantify individual patient risk levels with high precision. Subsequent decision curve analysis was performed to further validate the clinical utility of the GBM model. The GBM model’s net benefit consistently ranked first among all evaluated models and was significantly higher than that achieved by the AdaBoost, SVM, and other candidate models. These results suggest that leveraging the GBM model to inform clinical decision-making across varying risk thresholds can consistently generate greater tangible clinical benefits for patients.

When compared to existing postoperative dysphagia risk assessment models in cardiac surgery [24, 25], this machine learning–based study achieves notable improvements and distinct advantages in predictive performance, variable dimensions, and clinical utility. First, the study demonstrates superior predictive performance relative to previously developed models. Traditional logistic regression was employed in previous studies for model construction, an approach known to possess inherent limitations in capturing complex variable relationships. The GBM algorithm was utilized in this study to capture complex associations among variables, resulting in AUC and recall values that were significantly higher than those attained by all other evaluated models. The probabilistic prediction accuracy of the GBM model is better aligned with the clinical demand for highly precise patient risk assessment. Second, the study encompasses a more comprehensive variable system, thereby overcoming the limitation of previous models that focused on single-dimensional risk factors. Risk factors spanning the entire clinical process, including patient baseline status, surgical procedures, and postoperative management, were incorporated into the model, which better reflects the complex interplay of multiple contributing factors observed in clinical practice. Third, the GBM model offers greater practical clinical value when compared to alternative prediction models. Predictive logic was visualized using SHAP analysis, and the model’s computational cost was found to remain manageable, thereby facilitating broader implementation in clinical practice settings.

SHAP analysis was employed to elucidate the mechanistic contribution of several key predictors to the development of postoperative dysphagia. Total sedative drug use duration was identified as the most impactful predictor of postoperative dysphagia. Prolonged sedation was found to elevate the risk of aspiration pneumonia through three primary pathways: (1) suppression of central and peripheral neural reflexes, thereby attenuating pharyngeal sensation and motor function [10, 27]; (2) extended endotracheal intubation, which heightens the likelihood of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury and disuse atrophy of the pharyngeal musculature [5, 28]; and (3) induction of ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW), further compromising the function of swallowing-related muscle groups [29, 30]. The specific pharmacological mechanisms and potential synergistic effects of the sedative drugs utilized in this study, including ciprofol injection and dexmedetomidine, among others, warrant further rigorous investigation.

Patient age was identified as another key predictive factor for postoperative dysphagia. The positive predictive contribution of age becomes increasingly significant as the patient’s age advances. Previous studies indicate [31] that when age is divided into ten-year intervals, the incidence of post-extubation dysphagia gradually increases following age 30, rising markedly after age 55, with statistically significant differences observed across age groups. Research conducted by Barker et al. [2] demonstrates that for every ten-year increase in age among cardiac surgery patients, the probability of developing postoperative dysphagia doubles. This phenomenon is attributed to age-related degenerative declines in pharyngeal muscle strength and coordination, impaired neural regulation, reduced pharyngeal sensation, and the frequent presence of multiple comorbidities among elderly patients, all of which collectively elevate the risk of developing postoperative dysphagia [9, 28].

Additionally, variables including diabetes, atrial fibrillation, and tracheal intubation time were found to contribute significantly to the development of postoperative dysphagia, with their underlying mechanisms closely aligned with clinical pathophysiological processes. Diabetic patients, who are chronically exposed to hyperglycemia, are predisposed to peripheral neuropathy. This neuropathic damage can impair pharyngeal sensation, thereby elevating the risk of aspiration and subsequent dysphagia [32]. Atrial fibrillation, a prevalent cardiac arrhythmia, results in impaired cardiac output, leading to reduced systemic tissue perfusion. This compromised perfusion leads to insufficient blood supply to the craniocervical region and oropharynx, thereby impairing normal physiological functions of these structures [33]. Previous studies indicate [34] that patients with diminished preoperative physiological reserve and functional capacity exhibit a higher likelihood of developing postoperative dysphagia. Prolonged endotracheal intubation duration was identified as an independent predictor of postoperative dysphagia [2, 35–37]. Direct mechanical friction and sustained compression of the pharyngeal mucosa during endotracheal intubation induce mucosal edema, which progresses to local tissue apoptosis and necrosis [14]. Concurrent airway irritation precipitates chronic inflammatory responses, which further compromise the structural and physiological integrity of swallowing function [35]. Research indicates [14] that patients who undergo endotracheal intubation for durations exceeding 48 h exhibit a postoperative dysphagia incidence rate of 51.2%.

In contrast, the duration of gastric intubation time and chronic lung disease were found to contribute relatively minimally to study outcomes. The proportion of study participants who did not require nasogastric tube placement was 56.54%. When nasogastric tubes were placed, they were predominantly utilized for short durations (most ≤ 3 days). Short-term gastric intubation time may cause minimal mechanical irritation and mucosal damage to the pharyngeal mucosa, thereby reducing persistent suppression of the swallowing reflex and preventing pharyngeal muscle atrophy that is associated with prolonged tube retention. Patients with chronic lung disease were hypothesized to have benefited from standardized perioperative respiratory optimization management protocols, which improve pulmonary reserve capacity through structured respiratory training, thereby attenuating this factor’s contribution to postoperative dysphagia. These findings further suggest that targeted perioperative interventions can effectively mitigate the impact of specific potential risk factors, thereby providing an evidence-based reference for developing individualized patient management strategies in clinical practice settings.

All variables retained in the final model are routine perioperative monitoring parameters that do not incur additional clinical or financial costs, ensuring feasibility for implementation across tertiary care centers, community health centers, and resource-limited clinical settings. When coupled with SHAP-based individualized explanation capabilities, the model can be operationalized across three complementary clinical domains. First, preoperatively, the model identifies high-risk patients—characterized by advanced age, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, or extended sedation requirements—thereby enabling proactive planning of early extubation protocols and sedation-sparing management strategies. Second, postoperatively, dynamic recalibration can be performed using daily updates of sedation duration and gastric intubation time, allowing for serial patient risk re-stratification and implementation of graded clinical interventions. Third, all post-extubation patients should undergo a standardized swallowing screening assessment within 4 h following extubation. Low-risk patients who successfully pass this assessment may gradually transition to normal oral feeding. For moderate-to-high-risk patients, a video fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) may be performed on an as-needed basis to characterize the specific type of swallowing disorder (e.g., pharyngeal retention, laryngeal penetration). This targeted assessment approach aims to facilitate timely referral to a speech-language pathologist (SLP) for swallowing rehabilitation interventions, thereby preventing aspiration and associated aspiration pneumonia. Additionally, a standardized multidisciplinary team (MDT) care pathway should be established, involving specialists from cardiac surgery, anesthesiology, critical care medicine, rehabilitation medicine, and clinical nutrition to provide seamless coordinated management throughout the entire patient journey from preoperative assessment through postoperative recovery.

This investigation is limited by its single-center, observational study design; although the sample size is substantial, potential selection bias cannot be entirely excluded. Furthermore, the absence of external validation limits the generalizability of the model’s performance findings, necessitating additional research for further corroboration. Future research should establish multi-center collaborative research efforts to expand the sample size and conduct comprehensive external validation studies. Through the integration of dynamic time-series data, including daily sedation scores and serial inflammatory biomarkers, future research will develop an online, continuously updatable risk-prediction tool. Detailed dose-response analyses of individual sedative agents are warranted to generate precise clinical evidence for the development and implementation of targeted intervention strategies. Concurrently, a comprehensive health economic evaluation, encompassing direct medical costs, patient-reported quality of life outcomes, and complication rates, will be conducted to quantify the multidimensional clinical and economic benefits of the model.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the GBM-based machine learning model developed in this study reliably identifies cardiac surgery patients at heightened risk for developing postoperative dysphagia, while SHAP-derived explanatory insights provide clinicians with transparent, actionable clinical decision support. The model demonstrates good discriminative performance and practical clinical applicability, the model is anticipated to facilitate early recognition of high-risk patients and implementation of targeted interventions, thereby attenuating dysphagia-related clinical complications, improving long-term patient prognosis and health-related quality of life, and reducing overall healthcare expenditures. These attributes underscore the model’s substantial potential for broad clinical implementation and widespread dissemination across diverse healthcare settings.
