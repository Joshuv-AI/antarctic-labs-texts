Lattice radiation therapy for canine sinonasal carcinoma: feasibility study of spatially fractionated radiotherapy using volumetric modulated arc therapy
Authors: Patricia Gualtieri, Amber Prebble, Tiffany Martin, Robert J Griffin, Mary-Keara Boss, Del Leary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLattice radiotherapy (LRT) administers spatially fractionated doses to a tumor volume with the intent to induce beneficial anti-tumor host responses and avoid normal tissue damage. This study investigated feasibility of LRT for canine sinonasal carcinoma (SC) using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), aiming to achieve the following criteria: (1) place ≥ 3 vertices of high dose “peak” regions within the gross target volume (GTV) separated by low dose “valley” regions; (2) achieve a 3:1 and 5:1 peak-to-valley dose ratios (PVDR); (3) meet organ at risk (OAR) dose constraints; (4) meet quality assurance (QA) standards. A secondary aim was to assess the impact of dose calculation algorithms and grid size on dosimetry. The peak vertices were placed manually as 5 mm 3D spheres within the GTV. Treatment plans were initially optimized and calculated on Eclipse using Acuros with 2.5 mm grid size and underwent QA using Varian’s Portal Dosimetry (3 mm/3%). Plans were then recalculated without re-optimization comparing Acuros XB (AXB) and anisotropic analytical algorithm (AAA) with variable grid sizes, to compare doses estimated for peak and valley lattice target volume and OARs.ResultsTwenty plans were generated from 10 image sets from dogs with SC. In all cases, (1) placement of ≥ 3 vertices was possible, with a median center-to-center distance of 30 mm (range: 26–41 mm); (2) achieved both 3:1 and 5:1 PDVR; (3) plans met OAR constraints; (4) all plans passed QA with gamma index > 95%. When comparing calculation algorithms with a consistent grid size, AAA overestimated the dose in both peak and valley regions compared to AXB. When using AXB, increasing the dose calculation grid size from 1.0 mm to 2.0 mm and 2.5 mm led to underestimation of the lattice metrics and overestimation of the valley dose, ultimately resulting in a reduced calculated PVDR. These deviations were not detected on standard clinical QA with portal dosimetry.ConclusionVMAT LRT planning and delivery is feasible for canine SC. This data suggests that the predicted—and consequently, reported—lattice target metrics can vary depending on specific treatment planning software settings. Further investigations could define the most appropriate QA approach for high-resolution LRT plans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05030-1.

Background

Lattice radiation therapy (LRT) is a form of spatially fractionated radiation therapy (SFRT). SFRT implies a non-homogenous dose distribution delivered to a bulky tumor, in the form of alternating volumes of high-dose “peak” and low-dose “valley” regions [1–5]. The aim of this radiotherapy technique is to achieve a positive tumor response while maintaining a low toxicity profile [2, 4]. The mechanisms of action of SFRT are hypothesized to be associated with a differential response in terms of vascular effects, bystander effects [6, 7], and tumor microenvironment changes including anti-tumor immunity [8–11], observed between peak and valley regions [1, 2, 12].

The concept of spatial fractionation in the 2-dimentional (2D) form of GRID therapy was first introduced in 1909 by the German radiologist Alban Köhler to spare normal tissue from high dose superficial kilovoltage therapy [13]. This technique continued to be used in the 1950 s to treat deep-seated tumors and avoid excessive toxicity to the skin with orthovoltage technique [14, 15], however it was abandoned after the introduction of megavoltage therapy which penetrates deeper and spares skin dose. In the 1990 s, this approach acquired new interest in the context of treating locally advanced tumors resistant to standard fractionated radiation therapy by using high peak to valley dose ratios [5, 16]. The advent of modern linear accelerators equipped with multi-leaf collimators and capable of intensity modulation allowed the creation of 3-dimensional (3D) configuration of spatially fractionated treatment plans [17]. The name “lattice” originates from the geometric array design of the high-dose vertices within the gross target volume (GTV), separated by a set distance to create peak-to-valley dose ratio (PVDR) similar to GRID therapy [17]. Typically the vertices are 0.5–1.5 cm in diameter, with a separation range of 2 to 6 cm center-to-center distance [4, 5]. Through intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), the 3D-lattice plans can control the positioning of peak vertices within the GTV, thereby allowing safe dose escalation, delivery of ablative radiation doses and minimizing normal tissue toxicity [3, 18].

LRT has been applied in human medicine for treatment of different tumor types [19–21], such as lung [22, 23], gynecologic [24], and head and neck cancers [25], including sinonasal tumors [26]. This approach is still widely variable in human medicine in terms of patient selection, lattice array design, optimal peak-to-valley dose ratio and lattice volume, dose prescription and planning, in combination with sequential conventional or hypofractionated radiotherapy and systemic therapies, and is currently in need of standardization efforts [5, 20]. In terms of quality assurance for modulated LRT plans, standard clinical methods have been used and reported [3, 17, 27]. The clinical intent of LRT can vary depending on case selection, with most LRT cases aiming at dose boosting, or tumor “radiation-debulking” for palliation of clinical signs for locally advanced tumors [12]. The use of SFRT to mediate and improve anti-tumor immune responses has been reported and investigated in both pre-clinical models [9, 28, 29] and human clinical settings [11, 30, 31].

In this context, there is the opportunity to investigate immune, vascular, and clinical effects of SFRT/LRT in a large animal, naturally occurring cancer model. While GRID radiotherapy has been reported for canine sarcoma [32], the use of LRT has not yet been described in veterinary medicine. Human LRT techniques and outcomes may not be directly translated to canine patients, due to anatomical differences (e.g., smaller nasal cavities, variations in tissue density) and unique dosimetric challenges in veterinary patients. We have studied tumor- and immune-associated radiation effects in canine head and neck cancer following treatment with stereotactic body radiotherapy, with an interest in canine sinonasal tumors [33–37]. We have also reported on dosimetric differences between collapsed cone convolution and Monte Carlo algorithms for radiation therapy planning of canine sinonasal tumors [38]. With this study, we have embarked on a research effort to investigate the radiation-associated effects of LRT through the treatment of canine sinonasal carcinoma. Canine sinonasal carcinoma is a common tumor type treated in veterinary radiation oncology [39–42]. Before advancing to a planned canine SC LRT clinical trial, we aimed to explore LRT radiotherapy methods using a linear accelerator and planning software clinically available in veterinary medicine. This study investigates the technical feasibility of Lattice RT (LRT) for canine sinonasal carcinoma using volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), with the goals to achieve the following criteria: (1) place ≥ 3 vertices of high dose “peak” regions within the gross target volume (GTV) separated by low dose “valley” regions; (2) achieve a 3:1 and 5:1 PVDRs (54 Gy peak/18Gy valley and 75 Gy peak/15 Gy valley, respectively; with each PDVR being a 3-fraction protocols); (3) meet our standard organ at risk (OAR) dose constraints for 3-fraction plans; (4) meet quality assurance (QA) standards for patient specific modulated plans. A secondary aim was to investigate the dosimetry effects observed when using different algorithms and grid size settings on the planning software.

Methods

Case selection, contouring and planning

Computed tomography (CT) scans from client-owned dogs with histologically confirmed sinonasal carcinomas were retrospectively included in a pilot in-silico study, to capture a spectrum of possible clinical presentations of the specific tumor type. Patient information recorded included breed, weight, longest tumor diameter and tumor volume as calculated in Eclipse after contouring. This information was tested for normality with Shapiro-Wilk test and reported in mean ± standard deviation (SD). Client consent was previously obtained for general anesthesia and advanced imaging procedures as part of a sinonasal carcinoma clinical trial (IACUC #6179). Images were acquired in 2 mm slices, before and after iodinated contrast IV injection, with patients positioned in a CT simulation set up for radiation planning purposes, as previously described [43]. Varian Eclipse Treatment Planning System (Version 15.5) was used for contouring and inverse treatment planning (Varian Medical Systems, Inc. Palo Alto, CA). Both pre- and post-contrast images were used for identification and contouring the gross tumor volume (GTV) and organs at risk (OARs).

Lattice target placement and planning for all cases was conducted by a single author (P.G.) Peak vertices were manually placed as 3D spheres measuring 0.5 cm in diameter within the GTV (Fig. 1). The goal was to position the peak spheres central to the GTV and as far as possible from the organs at risks (skin, oral mucosa, brain, eyes) in all directions and planes, aiming to achieve a center-to-center distance of 3 cm, as measured on the sagittal plane. The volumetric sum of the “Peak_Vertices” was manually generated using a Boolean operation in Varian’s Contouring application and was defined as Vertices Tumor Volume “VTV”. Similar sized spheres were inserted evenly between peak dose spheres for valley dose prescription purposes, defined as “Valley_Vertices” region. Ring structures measuring 5 mm around the peak vertices and GTV were created to aid optimization of the lattice plans and obtain the desired steep dose drop-off. The difference in the center-to-center distance between peaks according to number of peak vertices placed was calculated with a one-way ANOVA. The authors arbitrarily selected a minimum of three vertices to achieve dose heterogeneity, which has been linked to biological effectiveness [44]. However, this should not be considered a strict requirement; in human medicine, a lattice is typically defined as having at least two boost regions (“vertices”) within GTV [45]. No PTV expansion was applied for the purpose of the lattice plan creation. To investigate the impact of delivery uncertainties in multifractionated LRT and the possible need for a PTV expansion, additional experiments were conducted both in silico and with a phantom, as described in more detail below.

Sagittal view of a pre-contrast CT scan of a canine sinonasal tumor used for contouring. Peaks (red circles) were manually placed at a median distance of 3 cm. Similar spheres (light blue spheres) were inserted between peak dose spheres for valley dose prescription purposes

In alignment with two completed phase II clinical trials using a five-fraction LRT protocol (20 Gy valley/66.7 Gy peak) for human cancer patients (NCT04553471, NCT04133415) [3], this study proposes a comparable three-fraction LRT protocol (18 Gy valley/55 Gy peak) for treating canine sinonasal carcinoma (SC), based on biologically equivalent dose calculations (assuming α/β = 10 for radio-response of sinonasal carcinoma cells). The dose prescriptions 75 Gy peak/15 Gy valley for 5:1 PVDR over three fractions for the technical exercise in this feasibility study, to evaluate the practicality of achieving extreme dose escalation and meeting OAR dose constraints.

Inverse planning for LRT was performed with 6MV photons and Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy (VMAT) via RapidArc. Beams were arranged in a coplanar fashion and modulated using a 120 Millenium multileaf collimator (MLC), with the finer inner MLC equalling 5 mm, and sliding-window technique. Three full arcs per plan were used to allow high degree of freedom for the software optimization to modulate the dose. In the optimization window, the settings for structure resolution were switched from the default 2.5 mm to fine 1.25 mm. At the time of study initiation, the institutional default settings on Eclipse were Acuros XB (AXB) as the calculation algorithm and a 2.5 mm calculation grid size [46, 47]. The dose prescription in the software was set to the valley prescription, in order for the relative hotspot to reflect the PVDR.

Lattice plan evaluation

Plans were evaluated with the goals of 1) ≥ 50% of VTV receiving 100% of lattice dose prescription (54 Gy for the 3:1 PVDR and 75 Gy for 5:1 PVDR plans); 2) ≥ 99% of the GTV receiving 100% of valley dose prescription (18 Gy for the 3:1 PVDR and 15 Gy for 5:1 PVDR plans); 3) the mean dose of the Valley_Vertices region to approximate the valley dose prescription; 4) the relative hotspot to be located within the VTV; 5) obtaining the desired PVDR on evaluation of the dose line profile on sagittal view and 6) achieving OAR dose constraints. The PVDR was defined as the maximum point dose relative to the prescribed valley dose (with GTV coverage) and was manually confirmed on the sagittal plane between the VTV and adjacent valley region using a dose–line profile. Lattice plan parameters recorded included: PVDR, the estimated volume receiving lattice dose prescription, Lattice Dose: GTV volume ratio (%), Maximum dose to the VTV, dose covering 99%, 95% and 50% of the VTV, Maximum, Mean and Minimum dose to the Valley_Vertices, Maximum (GTVMax), minimum (GTVmin), mean (GTVmean), as well as 99% (GTV99%) and 50% (GTV50%) of dose covering the GTV. Plan deliverability and quality assurance (QA) was evaluated via the standard clinical IMRT QA protocol at the authors’ institution using a Varian electronic portal imaging device (EPID) and gamma analysis on individual VMAT arcs. A passing QA score with a minimum of 95% gamma index for a 3 mm distance to agreement and a 3% absolute dose difference. Plans were delivered with a Varian Trilogy linear accelerator.

Grid size spacing and calculation algorithm effect in Silico

The achieved test plans were later recalculated without re-optimization, using different algorithms available in the treatment planning system at the authors’ institution (AXB vs. AAA) and different grid sizes (2.0 mm and 1.0 mm). Arc geometry, MLC sequence and monitor units were consistent across plans for the same patients. Each case had plans recalculated and evaluated using AXB 1.0 mm, AXB 2.0 mm, AXB 2.5 mm, AAA 1.0mmm, and AAA 2.0 mm for both 3:1 and 5:1 PVDR. At the authors’ institution, a 3% or greater difference in estimated dose delivery for target structures or OARs for a re-calculated plan is typically considered noteworthy in clinical practice.

The raw data observed from each software setting re-calculation was tested for normality with Shapiro-Wilk test. A one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s Multiple Comparison Test to compare the mean of each column with AXB 1.0 mm was used for parametric data, while Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s Multiple Comparison Test was used for non-parametric data. Significance was set a priori at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using commercial software Prism v10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Impact of positioning shifts and relationship to peak-to-valley dose ratio (PVDR) with fractionation

To get a better understanding of how multiple fractions can alter the intended PVDR, we conducted two different experiments. In the first experiment, an in-silico simulation overlapped sine waves to emulate how the peak to valley ratios can be altered due to the second sine wave being shifted along the x-axis. This is a 1D experiment to simplify the demonstration, however, the concept can be applied for two or three dimensions. This experiment demonstrates how the same LRT dose distribution with a prescribed PVDR can change the final PVDR when delivered over two different treatment fractions due to functional smearing of the intended spatial positions of the peaks and valleys. The amplitude of the sine wave was fixed to deliver a 5:1 peak-to-valley ratio, and the peaks were separated by 3 cm by setting the wavelength of the sine function to 3 cm (= 2π). (The Matlab source code is available upon request.) Here the first sine wave is the PVDR of a single fraction, and the second sine wave represents a second fraction that is shifted due to setup and anatomical variation. In our example, we used shifts of 1–4 mm. Mathematically, to achieve 1 mm and 4 mm shifts, the phase shift of the sine function was set to 0.2*pi/3 or 0.8*pi/3, respectively. Finally, the two sine waves were summed as a superposition to investigate the resultant PVDR over multiple fractions.

The second experiment used radiochromic film (EBT-3, Ashland, Bridgewater, NJ) to measure the resultant alteration of the PVDR dose after positional shifts in the phantom between fractions. In this experiment, we are looking at measurements rather than simulation over a 2D plane. Regarding the phantom, a canine-specific head and neck phantom (VetPhan), was modified to include a unilateral nasal tumor. In brief, the 3D printed phantom was created from a previous canine patient CT scan and printed simultaneously with two different materials that act as tissue and bone surrogates. The HUs of these materials match those of tissue and bone. The phantom is printed to allow insertion of various dosimeters or film for dose analysis. A unilateral nasal tumor was contoured and printed as part of the phantom after assigning tissue equivalent Hounsfield Units (HU = 0).

The nasal cavity and tumor were sectioned along a coronal plane, enabling the insertion of radiochromic film to measure dose delivery of an LRT plan. For this portion of the study, protocols consisting of a single fraction, three fractions, or five fractions, were delivered to two Peak_Vertices with the goal of achieving a total VTV dose prescription of 5 Gy, while the valley region was prescribed 1 Gy. This dose was selected based on the linear dose range of the film. The film was custom-cut to match the silhouette of the canine phantom and positioned between the dorsal (nasal) and ventral (maxillary) sections (Fig. 2).

(a) VetPhan 3D printed dosimetry phantom was altered by adding a coronal slice along the lattice sphere locations to measure the dosimetric delivered distribution of the (b) lattice plan developed in our treatment planning system. Measured over a single fraction, three fractions, and 5 fractions, (c) radiochromic film was acquired for each fractionated treatment plan with the same total dose delivered. The radiochromic films were (d) digitized and (e) a profile was drawn longitudinally across the peak vertices to measure the PVDR

To replicate a clinical scenario, a CT simulation was performed using a bite block, bite mold, and Vac-Lok cushion for cranial immobilization to replicate our standard workflow and setup, where daily on-board imaging allows for a 1–2 mm PTV [43]. The phantom was then placed onto the treatment couch, aligned, and imaged with cone-beam CT for patient positional matching. After each fraction was delivered, the phantom was removed from the couch, the immobilization equipment was dismantled, and the treatment couch was randomly shifted to initiate a new patient setup. The radiochromic film, one piece per plan, was left in place for all (1, 3, or 5) fractions of each plan and was subsequently analyzed to evaluate the effects of setup margin errors on dose distribution by measuring the flatness of the peaks.

To explore how larger setup errors and delivery uncertainties—and consequently, potentially larger PTV margins, as might be used at other institutions—affect dose distribution, the experiment was repeated with intentionally greater longitudinal shifts. Specifically, for the three-fraction protocol, after cone-beam CT imaging and treatment of the first fraction, shifts of ± 4 mm were applied during delivery of the remaining two fractions, without repositioning the phantom. For the five-fraction protocol, following OBI-based alignment and first fraction delivery, longitudinal shifts of ± 2 mm and ± 4 mm were introduced in the subsequent fractions.

Results

Case selection, contouring and planning

A total of 10 cases of histologically confirmed sinonasal carcinomas were included for the initial in-silico study. Breeds included were Border Collie (n = 2), Wirehaired Pointing Griffon, Portuguese Water Dog, Red Heeler, Labrador Retriever, Australian Shepherd, Brittany Spaniel; two patients were mixed breed dogs. The mean weight was 24.5 ± 5 kg. The mean longest tumor diameter measured 11.5 ± 2 cm and mean tumor volume measured 47.2 ± 19 cm3. Additional details can be found in Supplementary Material 1.

A total of 20 plans were created, a 3:1 PVDR and 5:1 PVDR for each dog. Three or more peak vertices were able to be placed within the GTV separated by valley vertices regions in all dogs. Figure 3 summarizes total number of vertices placed in canine sinonasal carcinoma patients. No more than 5 vertices per tumor were able to be placed in this study population. There was no difference in the center-to-center distance between peaks according to number of vertices placed (One-way ANOVA p = 0.99).

All (10/10) cases met criteria for both 3:1 and 5:1 peak-to-valley dose plans. For comparison, a standard SBRT dose color wash with a homogenous dose distribution is presented in Fig. 4B. Grid size spacing and calculation algorithm effect in Silico

For plan comparison purposes, the authors used AXB with the finest available grid size (1 mm) in the planning system as the most accurate representation of the dose delivery as a comparative baseline to other scenarios using AAA and/or larger grid spacing (or voxels), as it has been shown to provide near-Monte Carlo dose estimation accuracy in air-tissue interfaces [38, 46, 48]. When the original plans in AXB 2.5 mm were recalculated, notable dose deviations were noted in the target structures with the different software settings. The absolute and relative results are summarized in Supplementary Material 3. The relative dose deviation results (Figs. 6A-B) showed many parameters had greater than 3% differences, as indicated by the dashed line, compared to the predicted dose using AXB at a 1.0 mm grid setting.

Relative dosimetric deviations of Lattice plan parameters when compared to Acuros XB 1.0 mm for (A) 3:1 PVDR LRT plans and (B) 5:1 PVDR for 10 dogs with sinonasal carcinoma. Results presented in mean and standard deviation

For example, when evaluating the effect of dose calculation grid size on the AXB algorithm, increasing the grid from 1 mm to 2 mm in the 3:1 PVDR plans resulted in the following relative dose estimation changes: VTV metrics (VTVMax, VTV99%, VTV95%, VTV50%) were underestimated by −6.9 ± 1.9 to −8.5 ± 1.8%, while the valley region metrics (Valley_Vertices Max, Mean, Min) were overestimated by 2.9 ± 2.7 to 4.6 ± 2.1%. The GTV99% was overestimated by 2.9 ± 1.1% while GTVMean was underestimated by −0.6 ± 0.1%. When the grid size was further increased to 2.5 mm, these deviations were amplified: VTV metrics were underestimated by −11.3 ± 2.8% to −13.3 ± 2.7%, valley region metrics overestimated by 4.4 ± 4% to 8.5 ± 1.9%, GTV99% overestimated by 3.7 ± 1.7% and GTVMean underestimated by −1.5 ± 1.1%.

For the 5:1 PVDR plans, these trends were more pronounced: when grid size was switched from 1 mm to 2 mm, VTV metrics were underestimated by −8.9 ± 1.6% to −10.9 ± 1.5%, the valley region metrics were overestimated by 5.2 ± 1.6% to 6.5 ± 2.5%, the GTV99% was overestimated by 4.6 ± 2.8% and the GTVMean was underestimated by −0.7 ± 0.2%. With a 2.5 mm grid size, VTV metrics were underestimated by −14.6 ± 2.2% to −16.9 ± 2.2%, valley region metrics overestimated by 7.5 ± 1.8% to 11.3 ± 3.4%, GTV99% overestimated by 5.3 ± 1.4% and GTVMean underestimated by −1.3 ± 0.4%.

Notably, in this study model, the choice of grid size was able to directly impact the calculated PVDR. For example, a desired LRT specific PVDR of 3:1 (originally optimized and calculated plans using AXB 2.5 mm) can effectively be closer to 4:1, and the 5:1 ratio increased to approximately 6:1–7:1 when reducing the grid size to 1 mm, which is described as the relative VTVMax(%) in the Supplementary Material 3. Additionally, the mean Lattice Dose: GTV volume ratio had a over three-fold difference between the 2.5 mm grid size of 0.4 ± 0.3% (3:1 PVDR) and 0.2 ± 0.08% (5:1 PVDR) and the recalculated values 1.4 ± 0.6% (3:1 PVDR) and 1.4 ± 0.3% (5:1 PVDR) with finer 1 mm grid size. No differences in the geometrical location and spatial distribution of the peak vertices were observed during plan review.

When evaluating AAA compared to AXB at similar grid size, for the 3:1 PVDR plans, the estimation in VTV metrics (VTVMax, VTV99%, VTV95%, VTV50%) showed a relative overestimation of 1.1 ± 0.9 to 2.6 ± 0.9% for 1 mm grid size spacing and 2.2 ± 1.3 up to 4 ± 2% for the 2 mm grid size spacing. When evaluating the valley region metrics (Valley_VerticesMax, Mean, Min), the dose prediction was overestimated by 1.9 ± 1 to 3.1 ± 1.4% for the 1 mm grid size spacing and 0.6 ± 1.7% to 1.6 ± 1.2% for the 2 mm grid size spacing. For the 5:1 PVDR plans, VTV metrics were overestimated by 1.4 ± 0.5% to 3 ± 1.3% and the valley metrics by 2.6 ± 2.6% to 4.6 ± 4.5% for the 1 mm grid setting, while for the 2 mm grid spacing setting the overestimation for VTV metrics was in the range of 2.9 ± 1.1% to 5.2 ± 2.2% and for the valley metrics was 0.6 ± 1.6% up to 1.4 ± 1.8%.

In summary, in our simulations, we found that when comparing dose calculation algorithms with a consistent grid size, AAA overestimated both peak and valley doses compared to AXB. When using AXB, increasing the dose calculation grid size from 1.0 mm to 2.0 mm and 2.5 mm led to an underestimation of the VTV dose plan metrics and an overestimation of the valley dose. This combination—underestimating peak dose and overestimating valley dose—resulted in a reduced calculated absolute value of the peak-to-valley dose ratio. These deviations were further amplified when using higher dose 5:1 PVDR plans (Fig. 6). Statistical results from the data analysis are indicated in Fig. 6, and further details can be found in the Supplementary Material 4.

Impact of positioning shifts and relationship to peak-to-valley dose ratio (PVDR) with fractionation

Using a simple sine wave to simulate the peak and valleys of a LRT dose distribution, an in-silico experiment was carried out to predict how multiple fractions can alter the PVDR. This experiment was carried out using two different levels of precision, assuming shifts of 1 mm vs. 4 mm. Note that the PTV contains the level of precision found in the setup margin (SM) as described in the Methods section; notably, multiple fractions with a less precise treatment unit would include a larger PTV. The results of using a 1 mm shift found only a small change in the intended 5:1 PVDR, measuring 4.9:1 while a 4 mm shift found the PVDR was reduced to a 4:1.

Complimentary measurements using a 3D printed dosimetric dog head with radiochromic film placed along the coronal 2D plane within the nasal cavity found that shifting the phantom by approximately 1 mm (or less) altered the intended 5:1 ratio and yielded PVDRs of 4.33:1, 4.25:1, and 3.65:1 for a single fraction, 3 fractions, and 5 fractions, respectively. This correlates with simulated data where the PVDR decreases and continues to decrease with the number of fractions corresponding to increasing inter-fractional anatomical motion and setup error.

When considering a larger PTV, by introducing purposeful shifts along the longitudinal direction, we found that the PVDRs were further reduced from the intended 5:1 to 4.64:1, 3.17:1, and 3.8:1 for a single fraction, 3 fractions, and 5 fractions, respectively.

Discussion

This study is the first to investigate the feasibility of LRT for nasal tumors in veterinary patients. In this canine population, despite anatomical limitations and small tumor sizes, we successfully achieved a geometric lattice design that met the intended peak-to-valley dose ratios on a sagittal plane using a standard clinical linear accelerator in all cases. Additionally, the estimated OAR plan parameters were comparable to or lower than those reported for a 30 Gy three-fraction SBRT protocol for canine nasal tumors [42], even though the peak lattice dose in this technical exercise was 1.8 (54 Gy) to 2.5 (75 Gy) times higher than the SBRT prescription. This suggests that LRT plans, even with high peak prescriptions, are not only technically feasible but may be safe for clinical use with respect to OAR exposure. No notable differences were observed in the dose estimation of OAR plan metrics when recalculating with different calculation algorithms and grid sizes, which can be reassuring for clinical practitioners concerned for patient safety with ablative dose-escalation in LRT plans. This result should be more easy to achieve in LRT planning in larger treatment regions as well as larger patients.

While VMAT allows for highly precise targeting of tumors, including LRT [27], the accuracy of the estimated dose distribution depends on calculation algorithms and settings used, including field size and energy [46]. Accurate dose estimation is fundamental for treatment planning and reporting, especially in complex structures with heterogenous media, such as the lung or nasal cavity [38, 46]. For example, in dog nasal tumors, a collapsed cone convolution (CCC) algorithm, can overestimate target volume and OARs by 0.5-3% and underestimate the near-maximum dose to PTV at air-interface by 1.1%, when compared to Monte Carlo algorithm [38]. Our results suggest that algorithm choice and grid size is especially relevant for high-resolution lattice VMAT plans for sinonasal tumors.

In regard to calculation algorithm, in our study AAA had the tendency of over-estimating dosimetry compared to AXB of same grid size, which is not surprising. AXB is a Boltzmann equation solver algorithm known for its ability to accurately account for tissue heterogeneity in dose estimations, providing more realistic dose calculations, particularly in areas with prominent air-tissue and tissue-bone interfaces [46, 49]. The AAA algorithm uses convolution, superimposition for dose calculation that is fast and widely used [50], however it may not be able to provide dose estimation of media and tissue heterogeneity with the same level of precision. This has been shown for planning of tumors in different locations [46], such as lung [47, 51], esophageal [52] and prostate cancer [51, 53] in human medicine. The previously described differences between these two calculation algorithms at air-tissue interface [46, 47] are confirmed in our in-silico study in the canine nasal cavity.

In regard to grid size, various studies have been conducted to investigate the impact of grid size in different treatment sites. One paper empirically demonstrated that a grid with 2.5 mm spacing is sufficient to prevent dose errors larger than 1% with IMRT [54]. However, other clinical studies showed that the variation in grid sizes from 2 to 4 mm resulted in dose discrepancies of 2–4% for head and neck cancer [55] and variation in grid size from 1 to 2.5 mm resulted in differences up to 6.2–13% for spine VMAT stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) [56]. In our study, the most noticeable effects in dose estimation were observed within the vertices target parameters, rather than in the overall GTV dose or OAR parameters. This can be explained by the consideration that the OARs are located at a distance of least 6 mm from the peak vertices and therefore they are not as affected by calculation inaccuracies associated with high resolution peak areas. In terms of the GTV parameters such as the GTV 99%, GTV 50% and GTVmean, the dose estimation is averaged over a much larger volume than the peak dose areas (which represented < 2% of the GTV in all cases), therefore this estimation across a larger volume can result in loss of the impact of high dose deviations in this context.

In this study, the differences in estimation of both peak and valley vertices caused by the different grid sizes resulted in shifts in lattice specific plan metrics of relevance such as the PVDR, and the volume of the GTV receiving lattice prescription. This finding is especially relevant given the current debate on the optimal lattice plan parameters, including PVDR, the challenges in quality assurance in highly non-uniform dose patterns, and inconsistent reporting of algorithm settings for treatment planning used in clinical lattice scientific reports [5, 20, 27]. In LRT VMAT plans, the geometry of the lattice spheres confines the high-dose regions—and any potential dose deviations, which may be on the order of several Gray—within the tumor. As a result, the clinical and biological implications of these dosimetric variations on patient treatment and outcomes remain uncertain. The variations identified for canine tumors, characterized by tissue inhomogeneity and complex air-tissue-bone interphase, as well as small tumor and VTV size, may not be as pronounced for other anatomic sites or larger targets.

The degree of inter-fractional variability does remain important with delivering multiple fractions of lattice therapy. Regarding a PTV, a single fraction essentially has little dependence on the set-up error and anatomical inter-fractional changes that make up a PTV. This is due to the high dose peaks being well within the target delivered over a single fraction recalling that the PTV is made up of an internal margin (IM) which considers the anatomical reproducibility between fractions by adding a margin that takes into account uncertainties in size, shape, and position of the clinical target volume (CTV) within the patient while the setup margin (SM) considers the uncertainties in patient positioning and alignment of the therapeutic beams during the treatment planning, and through all treatment sessions [57]. The concept and size of the PTV does, therefore, does have a considerable influence to the overall dose distribution when delivering multiple fractions of lattice therapy. These margins may or may not be symmetrical in all three dimensions. Nonetheless, this hypothesis was tested in a simulation using a simple sine wave along one dimension and including a shift along the x axis for the PTV (Fig. 7). By averaging the resultant sine waves with the full span of the PTV, the blurring effect of the peak to valley ratio can be seen. This blurring effect was also confirmed in our radiochromic film measurements comparing single to multiple fractions in a dog head phantom. An implied limitation in the use of radiochromic film is that this approach captures only capture PVDR in a single plane, and the 3D dose distribution might differ from the 2D film results. Indeed, in a clinical setting the IM and SM that make up the PTV would span over three dimensions further smoothing the planned PVDR, however, this simple simulation can help realize the important effect of how the PTV margin and fractionation can alter the PVDR from the intended planned dose distribution. At this time, it is unclear how multi-fractionation and the associated blurring in SFRT and LRT specifically affects the biological response. The authors recognize the previously published hypotheses that the heterogenous dose distribution between the peaks and valleys as potentially the most important factor to stimulate a cancer killing response for LRT, as well as a high immunogenic response, with possible trigger for abscopal effect [58]. This is supported by pre-clinical models with different forms of SFRT, such as GRID, mini-beam RT and partial tumor irradiation, especially when combined with checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy [9, 10, 28, 29, 58–60]. However, one of the major knowledge gaps in SFRT is represented by the correlation of dosimetric parameters and treatment response [58]. In one pre-clinical study, tumor equivalent uniform dose (EUD), valley (minimum) dose, and percentage of the tumor directly irradiated – but not peak dose – were the dosimetric parameters that exhibited closest associations with response [61]. The findings of this study call into question the current clinical practice of using peak dose, therefore an exact peak to valley ratio, as the basis for SFRT prescription. Potentially, the peak to valley ratio may be less important than the range of the intermediate dose or other metrics of plan evaluation. This may also suggest that the blurring explored in the in-silico and phantom work for multi-fractionation LRT may be of relative clinical relevance.

Gaining a clear understanding of the dosimetry/biology dynamics is challenging due to the complexity of SFRT and LRT dosimetry, inconsistencies in reporting the use of treatment planning systems and calculation algorithm settings, and the practice in human medicine of following SFRT with a conventional course of radiation therapy. Nevertheless, these challenges present an opportunity to further explore the dosimetry-response relationship using clinical large animal models in veterinary medicine.

Quality assurance is considered a challenge for spatially fractionated radiotherapy plans, and LRT specifically [5, 27]. Calculation algorithms, delivery systems and quality assurance techniques that are used for standard clinical treatment with linear accelerators are often commissioned with the goal of estimating, delivering and verifying relatively large (> 3 cm) and uniform doses to the target(s). Newer systems that are designed for stereotactic treatment should be generally aligned for small peak vertices used in lattice therapy, typically ≥ 1 cm for human medicine [58]. However, in our veterinary patients with nasal tumors, using very small targets (~ 5 mm) may exceed current stereotactic commissioning and quality assurance standards, and may create a unique challenge in the limits for highly non-uniform and high-resolution targets. In our study, standard clinical QA with EPID and gamma analysis used for SBRT plans failed to capture the high-resolution dose estimation inaccuracies. This may be related to the mathematical model implied in the gamma analysis and/or three-dimensional nature of the plans. While some studies have been conducted on this matter [27], none address verification of < 1 cm lattice sphere/vertex plans. Further studies should investigate the effect of changing gamma analysis parameters to a finer resolution and/or using different QA approaches for LRT plans with such high resolution.

Beyond the uncertainties associated with high-resolution dose estimation, gamma analysis in portal dosimetry quality assurance does not account for inaccuracies in delivering a multi-fractionation regimen. LRT plans, particularly those with 5 mm peak vertices, require a stereotactic technique to ensure precise and reproducible ablative dose delivery. This relies on a rigid, reproducible patient setup and image-guided verification of patient positioning, especially in multi-fractionation treatments [43]. Further research is needed to assess the impact of multi-fractionation on LRT dosimetry and determine whether a PTV margin is necessary to account for setup errors and dose delivery uncertainties for specific patient immobilization approaches.

Limitations

This study is an in-silico feasibility analysis focused on a single tumor type in a small patient cohort, characterized by relatively uniform weight and tumor size. As such, the findings may not fully represent the broader spectrum of clinical scenarios encountered in veterinary medicine, particularly in canine patients, specifically when considering LRT for smaller patients or brachycephalic breeds. Moreover, the observed variations in dose estimations across different software settings may not extrapolate to larger lattice vertex designs, larger tumors, or anatomical sites with more homogeneous tissue density.

Conclusion

VMAT LRT planning and delivery is feasible for dogs with sinonasal carcinoma. This data suggests that the predicted—and consequently, reported—lattice target plan parameters can vary depending on specific treatment planning software settings, in this specific model. Information regarding calculation algorithm and grid size settings used in the planning software should be reported in LRT studies to allow for reproducibility of dosimetric results. This modality of therapy may require more investigation to define the optimal quality assurance verification methods for high-resolution plans. Multi-fraction delivery uncertainties may affect the peak-to-valley dose ratio; however, the relationship between dosimetric variations and biological response requires further investigation.

Building upon the results of this project, LRT is currently being investigated in a prospective clinical trial for canine sinonasal carcinomas, which includes serial analyses of tumor microenvironmental and systemic effects. We aim to contribute to the understanding of the clinical and biological impact of LRT through canine comparative oncology research, including efforts toward optimization of lattice dosimetry plan parameters.
