Alterations in mineral and biochemical traits of Artemisia haussknechtii Boiss. populations by soil and environmental factors
Authors: Milad Javanmard, Hassanali Naghdi Badi, Ali Mohammadi Torkashvand, Ali Mehrafarin, Babak Bahreininejad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtemisia haussknechtii, a native Iranian plant prized for its nutritional and ethnopharmacological benefits, was systematically evaluated across diverse environmental conditions in Iran. The findings highlight its significance as a rich source of essential minerals, with calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), potassium (K) and iron (Fe) levels spanning 16,850–24,060, 2509–4389, 6920–9250, and 1027–1469 mg/kg, respectively. The populations exhibited varying contents of total anthocyanin (TA), total flavonoid (TFC), and total phenolic (TPC), ranging from 5.60–10.42 Mmol/g FW, 10.87–17.89 mg QE/g DW, to 31.37–39.41 mg GAE/g DW. Factor analysis elucidated distinct diversity among populations, categorizing them into three groups. The first group, thriving in higher temperatures with minimal amounts of humidity and rainfall, displayed lower TPC, TFC, and TA along with elevated manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), and zinc (Zn) levels. The second group, occupying habitats with increased altitude and rainfall but reduced temperature, showcased maximal amounts of nitrogen (N), K, phosphorus (P), TA, TFC, and TPC, and minimal amounts of Cu, Mn, and Ca. The third group, found in regions marked by higher humidity, more sunshine hours, and higher soil CaCO3 levels, exhibited peak Ca, Mg, and Fe levels. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) corroborated these classifications, underscoring the impact of environmental conditions on A. haussknechtii’s mineral and biochemical composition. These findings highlight the significance of the mineral and antioxidant compounds in this plant species and their relationship to environmental and genetic factors. Introduction

Artemisia L. used worldwide as a flavoring, spice, and seasoning in the preparation of food, salads, tea, and beverages1. It holds numerous medicinal applications in traditional medicine, serving as an anti-spasmodic, anthelmintic, antibacterial, and anti-rheumatic remedy for conditions such as malaria, cancer, hepatitis, inflammatory conditions, liver diseases, infections, fever, and the reduction of menstrual pains, Also, recent studies indicate that the active compounds in Artemisia may help reduce liver toxicity, diabetes, oxidative stress and obesity2,3.

A. haussknechtii Boiss., a species in the genus Artemisia, is native to Iran. It contains compounds like 1/8-cineole, borneol, and camphor, which make it valuable in the pharmaceutical industry, as well as in food preparation and the production of beverages and flavors4.

Secondary metabolites are organic compounds produced by plants that can significantly impact human health. While genes influence the rate of secondary metabolite production, environmental factors, plant growth stage, handling practices, and processing conditions significantly affect their content and composition5. Phenolic compounds, among the most widespread and significant secondary compounds in plants, possess the capability to protect against oxidative stress and eliminate reactive oxygen species (ROS) due to the presence of one or more aromatic rings6. Polyphenols, including anthocyanins and other flavonoids, are produced by plants under abiotic stress, influencing biological diversity and crop production7.

It is also important to mention the significance of elements that plants require only 14 essential minerals, including N, P, sulfur (S), K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, Cu, Zn, molybdenum (Mo), chlorine (Cl), nickel (Ni), and boron (B). These essential elements are crucial not only for the health of plants but also for the human body, contributing to tissue maintenance, adjustment and symmetry of various bodily functions, as well as other physiological and biochemical processes8.

Mineral elements are transported to the transpiring leaf tissues through the xylem and are then absorbed from the apoplast by specific cell types. They circulate within the plant and are delivered to non-transpiring or xylem-deficient tissues through the phloem. K, Mg, N, P, and S are readily transported, while Fe, Zn, and Cu exhibit less mobility, and Mn and Ca are essentially immobile in the phloem9.

In Artemisia, the content of antioxidant compounds, such as TFC, TPC, and TA varies depending on genotype, extraction method, stage of growth, and environmental conditions10,11.

The content of elements in Artemisia is contingent on each species’ ability to absorb elements from the soil, the harvested plant part, and the environment12,13. Macroclimate variables, such as annual temperature and precipitation patterns, are likely to be the main drivers of variation in elemental composition along the geographic gradient. The soil environment plays a stronger role than climate in determining the stoichiometry of Artemisia species. The identity of species and their growth forms greatly impact plant stoichiometry. Even closely related species can have different responses to the same environmental gradient14.

Given the food, medicinal, and cosmetic value and uses of A. haussknechtii, it is imperative to analyze its nutritional and biochemical factors. In this study, various populations of A. haussknechtii were gathered from the natural habitats in the central and western regions of Iran, and their diversity was assessed concerning elements and phytochemical traits. We examined the nutritional value of various populations and the environmental and genetic factors that influence it.

Material and methods

Plant material

Seventeen populations of A. haussknechtii were collected during the flowering stage in September and October 2021. The collection areas (habitats) were situated in five central and western provinces of Iran (Fig. 1). The identity of the plants studied in this work was verified through voucher specimens with numbers (SFAHAN) 17,172 to 17,188, securely stored in the Herbarium of Isfahan Agricultural and Natural Resources Research and Education Center, Iran.

Extraction

Ten grams of crushed aerial parts of A. haussknechtii were extracted in 200 ml of methanol using a shaker for 24 h at room temperature. After extraction, the extracts were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min, and the resulting supernatants were analyzed. Filtration was performed using Whatman No. 2 filter paper. The solvent was then evaporated at its boiling point until all methanol was completely removed. The dried extract was then redissolved in methanol to a concentration of 4 mg/mL, and the solution was stored at – 20 °C until analysis15.

TPC

The total phenolic content of the aerial parts of the A. haussknechtii extract was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent with gallic acid as the standard16. A methanolic solution of the extract, with a concentration of 1 mg/mL, was used for the analysis.

The reaction mixture was prepared by combining 0.5 mL of the methanolic solution of the extract, 2.5 mL of 10% Folin-Ciocalteu’s reagent dissolved in water, and 2.5 mL of 7.5% NaHCO3. Simultaneously, a blank was prepared, containing 0.5 mL of methanol, 2.5 mL of 10% Folin-Ciocalteu’s reagent dissolved in water, and 2.5 mL of 7.5% NaHCO3. The samples were then incubated in a thermostat at 45ºC for 45 min. After the incubation in the dark, the absorbance versus the prepared blank was measured at 760 nm using a spectrophotometer apparatus of type Unico S-2100. The total phenolic content of the aerial parts was expressed as milligrams of gallic acid equivalent per gram of dry extract (mg GAE/g D) using the calibration curve with gallic acid.

TFC

Extraction was employed to measure the total flavonoid content through the aluminum chloride colorimetric assay, utilizing quercetin as the reference compound. The absorbance of the flavonoid-aluminum complex was measured against a blank at a wavelength of 420nm using a UV/Visible spectrophotometer Unico S-2100 (USA) after the reaction remained at room temperature for 60 min. In brief, 0.5 mL of each shoot extract (1:10 g/mL) in methanol was mixed separately with 1.5 mL of methanol, 0.1 mL of 10% aluminum chloride, 0.1 mL of 1 M potassium acetate, and 2.8 mL of distilled water. The calibration curve was established by preparing quercetin solutions at different concentrations ranging from 20 to 80 μg/mL in methanol17.

TA

To measure anthocyanin, 0.1 g of leaves of A. haussknechtii were weighed. The plant material was pulverized in a mortar with 5 mL of acidic methanol (methanol and hydrochloric acid in a ratio of 99:1), and then an additional 5 mL of acidic methanol was added. The resulting extract was kept in the dark for 24 h at room temperature, and subsequently, it was centrifuged for 10 min at a speed of 4000 rpm. The absorption intensity at the 550 nm wavelength was read using a spectrophotometer18.

The Beer-Lambert Law equation is represented as A = εbc, where A is the absorbance of an anthocyanin solution at a wavelength of 550 nm, ε is the molar absorption coefficient (M−1cm−1), b is the length of the path traversed by the light (cm), and c is the concentration of the solution (M).

Mineral elements

Sample preparation

First, the aerial parts were washed with distilled water, followed by a wash with 0.1 M hydrochloric acid, and then rinsed again with distilled water. The samples were then dried in oven at 70°C for 48 h 19.

Extraction

The samples were extracted and digested through dry burning combined with hydrochloric acid. To do this, 2 g of dried aerial parts of A. haussknechtii were placed in a porcelain crucible and heated in a furnace at 550°C for 4 h. The ash was moistened with distilled water, and then 10 mL of 2 M hydrochloric acid was added. The mixture in the crucible was filtered into a 100 mL volumetric flask, and the final extract was brought up to a total volume of 100 mL19.

To measure Ca and Mg in the extract, 1 mL of both the sample and control extracts was taken and diluted with distilled water in a 9:1 ratio. 0.25 mL of the diluted extract was transferred to a test tube using a micropipette, and 4.75 mL of a lanthanum nitrate solution (1 g per L of lanthanum) was added. This extract was analyzed for Ca absorption at a wavelength of 422.7 nm and Mg absorption at a wavelength of 285.2 nm, using an atomic absorption spectrometer20.

Micronutrients such as Fe, Mn, Zn, and Cu were measured using the atomic absorption method. The extraction of these micronutrients was performed through a digestion method combined with dry combustion and hydrochloric acid. Standard, control, and extract samples were vaporized using an acetylene-air blue flame. The absorption of Fe, Mn, Zn, and Cu was measured at wavelengths of 248.3, 289.5, 213.8, and 324.7 nm, respectively, with a CTA-2000 atomic absorption spectrometer20.

To determine the amount of K, 1 g of the aerial parts of A. haussknechtii was placed in a porcelain crucible. The crucibles were heated in a furnace at 550 °C. Once the crucibles cooled, 5 mm of 2N hydrochloric acid was added for each gram of aerial part powder, and the mixture was transferred to a 50 ml volumetric flask. The crucibles were washed multiple times with distilled water, and the volume was adjusted to 50 ml. The K in the extract was measured using a Jenway PFP7 flame photometer at a wavelength of 766.5 nm20.

To measure P using the calorimetric method, pour 5 cc of the digested extract solution into a 25 ml volumetric flask. Next, add 5 cc of ammonium molybdate-vanadate solution and fill the flask to the mark with distilled water. Finally, measure the absorbance at a wavelength of 470 nm using a spectrophotometer20.

Total N measurement using the the Kjeldal method involves oxidizing a powdered sample of the aerial parts of plant with concentrated sulfuric acid in the presence of a catalyst, resulting in the formation of ammonium sulfate and titrated with sulfuric acid20.

Soil characterization

Soil characterization, including pH, EC, O.C, S.P, gypsum, N, phosphorus availability (PAv.), potassium availability (KAv.), and soil texture, was measured following the methods outlined by Rayment and Lyons21.

Ash

In order to calculate the ash percentage of the aerial parts of A. haussknechtii, two grams of dry were placed in a muffle furnace set at a temperature of 600ºC for a duration of six hours. The weight of the sample was then recorded after cooling. The procedure outlined by22 was followed to determine the ash percentage.

Data analysis

We conducted the Bartlett’s test to assess the homogeneity of variances. The p-values for all 13 attributes were significantly higher than conventional significance levels (e.g., 0.05). This means the variances across the 17 populations are not significantly different, supporting the assumption of homogeneity of variance. Consequently, this justifies our methodology of employing a simple completely randomized design (CRD). The SAS software (version 9.4 TS level 1M3) was employed for analysis of variance (ANOVA), mean comparison, and correlation analysis. The Duncan multiple range test at a significance level of p ≤ 1% was used for comparing traits, and correlation analysis was conducted between edaphic and climatic characteristics.

For multivariate analysis, including clustering, factor analysis, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA), the JMP software (version 11.0.0) was utilized. Initially, cluster analysis was performed using the Ward method to classify the populations, and the method of FA identified traits with the most diversity and variance among populations. FA was conducted based on a principal component analysis of the studied traits. PCA was applied to evaluate soil diversity based on the measured soil characteristics in different regions.

Results

A. haussknechtii populations in different habitats displayed variations in mineral and biochemical traits. The N, P, and K levels in the studied populations ranged from 0.66–1.45%, 825–1135 mg/kg, and 6920–9250 mg/kg, respectively. Ca was the most abundant macro-element in A. haussknechtii, with levels ranging from 16,850 to 24,060 mg/kg. Mn was the second most abundant macro-element, with levels ranging from 2509 to 4389 mg/kg. Among the micro-elements, Fe exhibited the highest levels (Table 3).

Discussion

In our study, we found that population A2 had the highest N content at 1.45%, while population A4 had the lowest at 0.66% (Table 3). This variation was related to the content of O.C and N in the soil, with region A2 exhibiting the highest O.C and soil N, while A4 region was one of the lowest in these items (Table 2). Positive correlations were found between N in plants and soil N (0.56) and O.C (0.57) at a 5% significant probability (Table S1). A strong relationship exists between soil elements and the elemental composition of plants23. Soil O.C and soil N are crucial factors for soil fertility in ecosystems24. These features indicate soil quality and have an impact on agriculture and natural ecosystems25. Nitrogen fertilization and fixation, soil organic matter mineralization, and precipitation influence plant-available N. The immobilization of N can occur through the connection of organic material and the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio26.

Our findings showed that the highest P content in plants was observed in populations A12 and A13, while the lowest P content was in populations A10 and A8 (Table 3). There was a positive correlation between plant P and PAv at a significance level of 1% (Table S1). Soil P extraction has a correlation with plant P uptake over one growing season27. The correlation between plant P uptake and soil P concentration in the North Central Region of the United States was reported to range from 0.74 to 0.9428.

Plant P showed a positive correlation with S.P of the soil and the average annual rainfall, with correlation coefficients of 0.48 and 0.49 at a significance level of 5%, respectively (Table S1, S2). Additionally, there was a negative correlation between plant P and the average annual temperature and the average annual sunshine hours, with correlation coefficients of − 0.61 and − 0.60 at a significance level of 1%, respectively (Table S2). Populations of Achillea wilhelmsii from colder regions showed improved P uptake, likely due to the selection of pre-existing genetic variation for cold tolerance, which allows for larger plant sizes. This observation highlights how plants adapt to selection pressures, leading to trait divergence in response to environmental variability, this was consistent with our results29.

Plant nutrient relations are influenced by water availability30, light31 and temperature32. Water loss can decrease plant nutrient uptake by reducing both the diffusion of ions in the soil and mass flow through mineralization. The diffusion of P in the soil is more influenced by soil moisture compared to N, and the reduction in plant P is greater than that of plant N under conditions of drought stress33. The absorption and utilization of nutrients in plants are affected by light quality, intensity, and photoperiod31. Extreme temperatures potentially damage nutrient uptake proteins like PHT1-P uptake protein, leading to a decrease in P uptake32. Rising temperatures negatively influence soil available P in natural ecosystems worldwide34.

Soils with a higher percentage of clay tend to have higher S.P values35. The amount of plant P at the soil solution level is enhanced with higher clay content36. Water availability in soils can improve P absorption by increasing water intake by roots, water transport from roots to leaves, and stomatal opening37.

The highest K content was observed in population A13, while the lowest was in population A14, fluctuating between 6920 to 9250 mg/kg (Table 3). The results indicated a positive correlation between soil P availability and plant K (0.50), and a negative correlation between average annual temperature and plant K (− 0.54), both statistically significant at a 5% probability level (Table S1, S2).

Elevated temperatures have been shown to decrease nutrient uptake in different plants38. High temperatures can lead to a decline in root development and K absorption39. P has been reported to increase root length and root surface area in soybeans40. It appears that P uptake contributes to increased root extension, allowing roots to absorb more K in plants. Prolonged K deficiency inhibits root growth and disrupts the ratio between roots and shoots41.

The highest amount of Ca in plants was observed in population A10, while the lowest was in population A11 (Table 3). Additionally, the amount of CaCO3 in the soil of the A10 region was the highest, while A11 had negligible amounts. There was a negative correlation between plant Ca and soil P availability (− 0.58) (Table S1). In the soil of A8 and A10 regions, which showed the lowest amounts of P, N, K, and organic matter, it was indicative of nutrient-poor soils. Moreover, the A10 region had the highest amount of lime, categorizing it as a calcareous soil. A high amount of lime stabilizes P and reduces the availability of absorbable P. This also indicated a negative correlation between the available P for plants and the amount of lime in the soil42, which aligns with our findings. High CaCO3 and pH in calcareous soils lead to low P efficiency, reducing crop productivity and yields43,44. P precipitates as calcium phosphate in calcareous soils45.

The highest amount of Mn in plants was found in population A10, while the lowest was in population A12 (Table 3). There was a negative correlation between Mg and PAv. at a 5% probability level (− 0.54) (Table S1). One possible reason for this phenomenon is related to the forms of orthophosphate (Pi) in the soil (HPO42− and H2PO4−) that are readily absorbed by plants. Pi may react with Mg2+ to generate magnesium ammonium phosphate or magnesium phosphate, thereby converting P from a water-soluble form to a less soluble form46,47. A large amount of P in the soil can convert to complex molecules of Ca–P and Mg–P, and these forms are not readily available for absorption by plants46,48. Specifically, it transforms P into less soluble compounds (variscite, strengite, and apatite) of Mg and Ca in alkaline soil49.

In the population of A10, the highest Mn and the lowest K were observed. Additionally, in populations A12 and A3, the lowest amount of Mg and one of the highest amounts of K in plants were found (Table 3). It appears that there is a reverse relationship between Mg and K in A. haussknechtii populations. Mg uptake by plants is also limited by higher concentrations of soil K50. The availability of Mg can strongly decrease with increasing cations like K in the nutrient solution. Probably, the unique chemical properties of Mg create a special behavior for this element in the soil. Mg has a smaller ionic radius than K, but it has a larger hydrated radius51.

Our results showed that in micronutrients, the highest amount of Zn in A. haussknechtii was observed in population A17, while the lowest was in population A7. There was a negative correlation between Zn and environmental factors, specifically the average annual humidity (− 0.56) and the average annual rainfall (0.50), at a 5% probability level (Table S2). A study reported that excess water reduces Zn uptake in chamomile52, which is consistent with our findings.

The highest amounts of Fe were observed in population A13, while Cu and Mn were highest in population A16. Conversely, the lowest levels of Fe, Cu, and Mn were found in populations A17, A1, and A8, respectively. The absorption of micronutrients in plants is influenced by mineral concentrations in the soil, soil characteristics such as pH, O.C, texture, interactions between elements, weather factors, genotype, etc.53–55.

Genetic factors, along with the interaction between genetics and local environmental conditions, influence the concentration of elements in plants. Each accession possesses a unique genetic structure that impacts how minerals are absorbed and distributed56.

The range of TA differs from 5.6 to 10.42 Mmol/g FW in different populations of A. haussknechtii. Population A13 exhibited the highest amount of TA, whereas A17 presented the lowest (Table 3). The average annual temperature was negatively correlated with this trait at a 5% probability level (− 0.56) (Table S2). The highest amount of TFC was observed in population A5 (17.89 mg QE/g DW), while the lowest was in A17 (10.87 mg QE/g DW) (Table 3). A17 region had the lowest amounts of TA and TFC, which could be attributed to its highest average annual temperature among the studied regions (Table 1).

The stability of anthocyanins is influenced by factors such as temperature, light, pH, and more57. Anthocyanin biosynthesis is a branch of flavonoid metabolism that begins with 4-coumaroyl CoA from phenylalanine metabolism, and phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) is the major enzyme in this process58. Expression levels of anthocyanin synthesis genes are affected by temperature, with low temperatures increasing the activity of these genes59. High temperatures hinder anthocyanin biosynthesis by reducing the capacity of biosynthesis through the prevention of the molecular signaling cascade60. Additionally, heat leads to the degradation of anthocyanins by producing H2O2 and the oxidative activities of peroxidase (POD) in plant cells61.

The highest and lowest amounts of total phenolic content were observed in the population of A2, with 39.41 mg GAE/g DW, and A17, with 31.37 mg GAE/g DW, respectively (Table 3). Additionally, the TPC and TFC in A. haussknechtii extract were reported as 31.6 µg GAE/mg DW and 9.8 µg QE/mg DW, respectively10. The results showed that TPC was correlated with the altitude at a 5% probability (0.58) (Table S2). The amount of bioactive compounds, such as total phenolic content, depends on many internal and external factors, such as cultivar, altitude, growing site, light, radiation, temperature, soil characteristics, and pathogens62. The amount of phenolic and flavonoid compounds in A. vulgaris extracts increased at higher altitudes11. Higher altitudes have lower temperatures and higher UV-B radiation. Secondary metabolites like phenolic content, anthocyanins, and flavonoids are enhanced under high UV-B radiation and lower temperatures63.

Conclusions

In this study, the assessment of macro and microelements in A. haussknechtii revealed that the plant serves as a substantial reservoir of Ca, Mn, K, Fe and antioxidant compounds. Factors such as genetic variation among populations, environmental conditions, and genotype-by-environment (G × E) interactions significantly influence the quantities of mineral and biochemical compounds in A. haussknechtii populations. Analyzing populations of this plant across 17 regions in Iran highlighted the impact of soil, geography, and climate on their biochemical and mineral compositions.

Notably, the study uncovered intriguing findings: (1) the levels of TPC and TA, functioning as antioxidants, were influenced by altitude and temperature, respectively, and (2) the mineral compounds of the plant were responsive to soil characteristics and climatic conditions. In summary, this research demonstrated significant variability among populations concerning N, P, K, Zn, Cu and TA.
