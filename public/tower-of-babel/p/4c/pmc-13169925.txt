Acute mental health services use: a comparative study of utilization patterns among Nova Scotia’s rapid access and stabilization program (RASP) participants and a control group
Authors: Medard K Adu, Samuel Obeng Nkrumah, Belinda Agyapong, Ngozi Ezeanozie, Ejemai Eboreime, Gloria Obuobi-Donkor, Sanjana Sridharan, Jason Morrison, Bryanne Taylor, Monica MacKinnon, Mahmoud Awara, Wozney Lori, Vincent Israel Opoku Agyapong

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTimely access to psychiatric care remains a significant challenge in publicly funded mental health systems. In response, Nova Scotia launched the Rapid Access and Stabilization Program (RASP) to improve access, reduce wait times, and ease the burden on emergency mental health services.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of RASP on mental health service utilization by comparing one-year post-intake outcomes of RASP participants with a matched historical control group.MethodsUsing a controlled post-intake evaluation design approach, the study analyzed individuals enrolled in RASP between April 2023 and April 2024 and a Control group made up of a cohort of patients referred to the mental health and addiction program (MHAP) central intake services by primary health providers (PHP). Key outcomes included psychiatric inpatient admissions, Emergency Department (ED) visits, and mobile crisis service use. Quantitative data were analysed using descriptive statistics and the post-intake health services utilization variables were compared between RASP and the control group using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) after adjusting for baseline characteristics and potential confounders.ResultsRASP participants were slightly younger than controls (Mean age: 40.2 vs. 42.60 years). Although the RASP group showed lower mean utilization of several acute mental health services including the mean number of inpatient admissions (0.01 vs. 0.02), mean inpatient length of stay (0.25 days vs. 0.45 days), and mean number of mobile crisis calls (0.01 vs. 0.04) as well as proportion of unique patients admitted to psychiatric Inpatient units post-intake (0.07 vs. 1.60), differences were not statistically significant.ConclusionWhile findings did not reach statistical significance, trends suggest that RASP may help reduce acute service utilization. The control group showed nearly double the proportion of unique patients admitted to psychiatric inpatient units’ post-intake, the mean number of inpatient admissions and mean length of stay, and four times the mean number of crises calls. Introduction

Mental health services are integral to the overall health and well-being of individuals and communities. As the global burden of mental health disorders rises, healthcare systems face significant challenges in providing equitable, high-quality, and accessible mental health care [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that mental and behavioral disorders account for approximately 14% of the global burden of disease, with depression and anxiety disorders ranking among the leading contributors to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost worldwide [2].

In Canada, mental health disorders affect up to one in five people annually, with approximately 17% among older adults [3]. Recent data indicates 29.5% of older Canadians reported worse mental health post-pandemic, highlighting unique challenges in this age group, with demand for mental health services often exceeding system capacity [3]. Limited availability of timely and effective care can exacerbate mental health conditions, increase reliance on emergency services, and contribute to poorer long-term outcomes [4]. This growing burden underscores the urgency of addressing mental health needs through comprehensive and sustainable strategies. However, despite increasing recognition of the importance of mental health, services in many regions remain underfunded, stigmatized, and fragmented, requiring innovative and multifaceted approaches to overcome these barriers [5–7].

Mental health service utilization is a critical indicator of healthcare system effectiveness, reflecting the extent to which individuals access appropriate mental health care services. Addressing these gaps requires strengthening mental health infrastructure, integrating community-based interventions, and expanding early stabilization interventions at critical moments to decrease demand on more acute services. These programs are designed to provide timely psychiatric support, reduce emergency department visits, and improve overall mental health outcomes by facilitating more efficient care pathways. Enhancing service coordination and accessibility through targeted interventions will be crucial in improving mental health service utilization and reducing disparities in care [8].

Scaling up these early intervention, rapid access, and stabilization programs can be achieved by integrating them into stepped care models, enhancing community-based support, utilizing technology for outreach, and ensuring adequate funding and resources to expand service capacity and accessibility [8]. Evidence suggests that rapid-access mental health programs contribute to improved patient outcomes, decreased healthcare costs, and enhanced continuity of care [9, 10]. However, research evaluating their long-term impact on service utilization and cost-effectiveness remains limited, particularly in the Canadian context.

According to data collected by the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI), Canadians faced substantial delays in accessing community-based mental health counselling. During the period from April 1, 2020, to March 31, 2021 which coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic and placed unprecedented strain on mental health services. About half of Canadians waited an average of 22 days for their first scheduled counselling session, and approximately 10% waited nearly four months [11]. Although pandemic-related disruptions contributed to these prolonged wait times, they also highlighted long-standing system pressures and reinforced the need for innovative models that can improve timely access to care. Provincially, a similar trend is observed in Nova Scotia, where individuals with non-urgent mental health needs wait an average of 104 days for their first appointment with a clinician after referral [12], while urgent cases are typically seen within 0–13 days [13]. These persistent delays emphasize the importance of rapid-access initiatives such as the RASP in bridging care gaps and reducing the burden on acute mental health services.

Nova Scotia mental health services

Nova Scotia’s mental health services are publicly funded and administered by the Nova Scotia Health Authority (NSHA), with services organized across four administrative zones: Central, Eastern, Western, and Northern. The province faces significant challenges in mental health service delivery, including long wait times for psychiatric assessments, geographic disparities in care access, and high rates of emergency department utilization for mental health concerns [14]. The province has been working actively to address these mental health challenges through various services and technology-based health initiatives [12].

The provincial Mental Health and Addiction Program (MHAP) is delivered using the stepped care model, where a continuum of services and service providers are available to support people’s specific needs, from the least intensive interventions (health promotion, primary care, self-management, community care) to the most intensive treatment services (formal and specialized mental health and addictions care) for more complex needs [15].

To further expand capacity and access to mental health care, the province launched a novel mental health initiative for people in need of mental health care at various intensity levels without requiring ED visits or hospitalization. This new service is referred to as the Rapid Access and Stabilization Program (RASP). RASP is a pilot mental health initiative introduced to improve timely access and capacity within the mental health system. RASP provides expedited psychiatric assessments, treatment plan recommendations for primary care providers, and access to digital mental health supports, with the broader aim of reducing wait times for community mental health services and minimizing reliance on emergency and inpatient care. Referrals to RASP are made through the provincial Mental Health and Addictions central intake system, accessible to primary healthcare providers including general practitioners and nurse practitioners and allowing self-referrals with consent. Psychiatrists involved in RASP also provide telephone consultations to primary care providers to support ongoing patient management. Patients assessed through RASP may choose to enroll in the Text4Support program, which delivers daily supportive text messages based on cognitive-behavioral therapy principles [8].

The establishment of RASP at the Queen Elizabeth II (QEII) Health Sciences Centre in Halifax represents a strategic effort to address the barriers to mental health care by providing a structured, tier 3 model of psychiatric care within the Central Zone [8]. Despite its potential, the extent to which RASP influences service utilization patterns and access to mental health care requires further investigation.

Research gap

Despite growing interest in early intervention and rapid-access mental health programs, evidence remains limited regarding their real-world impact on acute mental health service utilization. Few studies have examined whether such programs reduce reliance on high-cost services including emergency department visits, mobile crisis involvement, and psychiatric hospitalizations within publicly funded health systems. Additionally, the literature provides insufficient data on how these programs influence service pathways during their early implementation phases, particularly in Canadian contexts where wait times and resource constraints remain substantial.

Another gap concerns the absence of empirical evaluations assessing whether rapid-access programs can meaningfully reduce system pressures, such as prolonged wait times or unnecessary acute care encounters. Furthermore, little is known about how sociodemographic characteristics shape patients’ engagement with and outcomes from stabilization-oriented services, leaving uncertainties about program equity and reach.

This study addresses these gaps by evaluating the early impact of Nova Scotia’s Rapid Access and Stabilization Program (RASP) on acute mental health service utilization and by examining how baseline characteristics may influence these patterns.

Study objectives

This study aimed to investigate whether attending RASP impacts patients’ health services utilization by evaluating data from the assisted population on the frequency of emergency department visits, Mobile crisis calls (In our context, mobile crisis services refer to a provincially operated mobile crisis team that provides telephone-based crisis support and, when necessary, in-person outreach by a multidisciplinary team), and Length of stay for mental health issues one year post-enrollment into the RASP compared to a historical Control group (which includes a cohort of patients referred to the MHAP central intake services by primary health care providers before the implementation of RASP).

Specifically, this research seeks to:

Compare the proportion of patients who utilized acute mental healthcare services after attending RASP and a matched control group 12 months post-intake to determine whether program engagement influences acute mental health services utilization.

Compare the proportion of patients who utilized acute mental healthcare services after attending RASP and a matched control group 12 months post-intake to determine whether program engagement influences acute mental health services utilization.

Compare the mean number of times acute mental healthcare services were utilized by RASP participants and a matched control group 12 months post-intake to determine whether program engagement influences acute mental health services utilization.

Compare the mean number of times acute mental healthcare services were utilized by RASP participants and a matched control group 12 months post-intake to determine whether program engagement influences acute mental health services utilization.

Compare the mean length of inpatient hospital stay between RASP participants and a matched control group 12 months post-intake to determine whether program engagement influences inpatient length of stay.

Compare the mean length of inpatient hospital stay between RASP participants and a matched control group 12 months post-intake to determine whether program engagement influences inpatient length of stay.

By determining the potential impact of RASP on acute mental health services utilization, this research seeks to inform targeted interventions to optimize care delivery and improve outcomes for this population. Additionally, the findings are expected to contribute to policy discussions on enhancing access and reducing disparities in mental healthcare for vulnerable groups in the province.

Method

Study setting

This study was conducted in the Central Zone (CZ) of the province of Nova Scotia in Canada. Adult mental health care in Nova Scotia is publicly funded and delivered by the Nova Scotia Health Authority through the Mental Health and Addictions Program, which serves residents across four administrative zones: Central, Eastern, Western, and Northern. RASP, a tier 3 model of care (A middle-to-higher level of mental health service delivery within a stepped-care framework, designed for individuals with moderate to severe mental health needs who require specialized psychiatric assessment, short-term stabilization, or focused therapeutic intervention but do not require inpatient hospitalization or intensive, long-term tertiary treatment (Tier 4)), serves patients within CZ and was established and implemented in April 2023 at the QEII Health Sciences Centre in Halifax, the provincial capital.

Study design

This evaluation study employed a quantitative and controlled post-intake comparative design. We assessed acute mental health service utilization within one year following RASP enrollment and compared outcomes to a historical control group that received standard mental health care before the RASP implementation.

Participants and data collection

All patients who attended RASP and consented to allow access to their health services utilization records had their health information about ED visits, mobile crisis visits, and inpatient mental health utilization extracted from provincial information systems at two points: one year before and one year after their assessment at RASP. The same data on healthcare utilization was extracted from historical records of patients referred to MHAP Central Intake Services by a PHP before RASP implementation (April 2022 to May 2023). These historical datasets constituted the study’s control group, enabling it to assess the health services utilization of participants’ post-RASP intake compared to the control group.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measures (health services utilization) included a comparison of the frequency/duration of high-cost mental health services utilization (i.e., proportion and/or mean number of mobile crisis visits, ED visits, hospital admissions, and cumulative length of stay) within one-year post-intake date for the patients at RASP and Control group (historical data).

Data cleaning and verification

Prior to the analysis, all data sets (RASP intake survey data and the linked provincial health service utilization records) were subjected to a thorough data cleaning and verification process to ensure data integrity and analytical reliability. All duplicate identifiers or intake dates were removed, and where applicable categorical variables were recoded to ensure uniformity across the data set. Likewise, the data extracted from provincial health service utilization records was also screened for logical consistency. All data cleaning, recoding, and preliminary descriptive analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics (Version 29). For reproducibility, a detailed log of all data transformations and verification procedures was kept in a secured file for future use whenever necessary.

Data analysis

Quantitative data analysis involved descriptive and inferential statistics using SPSS version 29 for Windows. Group comparability at baseline was first examined using descriptive statistics, t-tests, and chi-square analyses. The differences in acute health service utilization following intake were then assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables. The post-intake health services utilization variables were compared between RASP and the control group using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with the intervention condition as the independent variable, baseline and pre-intake variables as the covariates, and the post-intake outcomes as the dependent variable. In each case, checks were conducted to ensure no violation of the statistical assumptions of normality, linearity, homogeneity of variance, homogeneity of regression slopes, and reliable measurement of the covariate.

Ethics considerations

This study has been approved by the Research Ethics Board at Nova Scotia Health (REB file #1028254). Participants from RASP included in this study have all provided consent to access their health services utilization records. Ethics approval for exemption from requiring informed consent for all control group patients has been granted by the same ethics board approving the study.

Discussion

Compared to a matched control group, this study evaluated the early impact of RASP on acute mental health service utilization. The post-intake analyses showed no statistically significant differences in inpatient admissions, ED visits, mobile crisis use, or length of stay. However, the RASP group consistently demonstrated lower mean rates across these indicators, with shorter hospital stays, fewer admissions, and fewer crisis calls. These trends, though not statistically significant, suggest potential benefits of RASP in reducing acute service use.

While both groups were nearly comparable at baseline in terms of sex distribution and prior ED visitation, a small but statistically significant difference in mean age was observed, with individuals in the RASP group being slightly younger on average compared to those in the control group. Potentially, this age difference may reflect the design and make of the RASP model, which attracts mainly persons who are entering the mental health system for the first time and often younger adults in the early stages of illness. Literature shows that approximately 75% of all lifetimes mental health disorders begin before the age of 24, highlighting early adulthood as a critical period for early intervention and system engagement [16].

To account for this potential confounder, age was included as a covariate in all adjusted analyses of post-intake outcomes. After establishing baseline comparability, post-intake outcomes were analyzed using both categorical and continuous measures. Across all analyses, the findings revealed no statistically significant differences between the RASP and the control group on all measures (inpatient psychiatric admissions, ED visits, mobile crisis service use, or length of hospital stay).

This suggests that RASP, as established during the study period, may not have yielded immediate reductions in acute mental health service utilization compared to the standard care during its early phase of implementation. However, the small proportion of patients in each cohort utilizing acute care services post-intake may have likely impacted the study’s statistical power to detect meaningful differences between the groups. This finding is consistent with some previous evaluations of early intervention or stabilization programs, which suggest that such models may take a longer period to demonstrate measurable impacts on service utilization, such as reduced hospital admissions or lower healthcare costs [17–19]. Conversely, a study that sought to measure the service utilization impact of a shared care planning program found that even with a small sample size and short duration of the intervention, significant reductions in emergency department, inpatient admissions, and outpatient visits were observed [20].

Despite the lack of statistical significance, the RASP group showed numerically lower mean rates of inpatient admissions compared to the control group, with consistently small effect sizes. Similarly, the proportion of length of stay on admissions for individuals in the control group was approximately twice that observed in the RASP group. This pattern, although not statistically significant, suggests that RASP may have the potential to reduce acute mental health services use. Thus, with a larger sample size and more patients in each group utilizing acute mental health care services, the study might have had greater power to identify significant differences between the groups. These factors should be considered when interpreting the findings and evaluating the early impact of RASP. This finding is consistent with a previous case-control study of readmission rate. In that study, they found that the control group had a mean length of stay of 7.6 days and a readmission rate of 18.4%, compared to 3.4 days and 10.6% for the intervention group, indicating potential for significant differences with larger samples and longer study duration [21].

Furthermore, the proportion of ED visits post-intake was nearly identical between the RASP and control groups. This pattern also observed in the minimal and inconsistent use of mobile crisis services likely reflects broader systemic factors, such as the limited availability of alternative crisis response options and a continued reliance on acute care pathways, even among individuals who have had initial contact with stabilization programs.

Previous research has shown that without sustained community-based follow-up and comprehensive care coordination, brief interventions or rapid access services alone may not significantly reduce subsequent use of emergency mental health services [22].

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study lies in its use of a matched control group and the application of robust statistical controls (i.e., ANCOVA adjusting for age and prior ED visits), which enhance the internal validity of the findings. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the use of administrative data may underrepresent informal or community-based care utilization, which could provide a bigger picture of patient engagement. Secondly, it is important to note that only a limited set of demographic and pre-intake variables were available for this analysis. The reason is that participants enrolled in the RASP program had no prior documented mental health history. Individuals with such histories would not have qualified for RASP enrollment, as they would have already been captured by the existing mental health care system.

Thirdly, given the chronic nature of mental disorders, the follow-up window of one year may have been too short to capture the delayed impacts of early stabilization. Again, the absence of clinical outcome data (e.g., symptom severity, quality of life, or patient-reported satisfaction) limits the scope of evaluation to service-based outcomes. Furthermore, the small proportion of patients in both cohorts utilizing acute care services may have limited the statistical power of the study to detect meaningful differences between the groups. Despite these limitations, the findings from this study present important insights into the early implementation phase of the RASP model. While no significant changes in acute service utilization were observed within the timeframe assessed, the program holds value in enhancing timely access to psychiatric assessment and support. It is also important to acknowledge that the lack of significant findings may reflect a true absence of early impact, rather than solely methodological constraints. Thus, the results highlight the need for further research to determine whether rapid-access models such as RASP produce meaningful changes in service utilization or clinical outcomes over longer periods and in larger populations to better evaluate the program’s full impact.

Policy and practice implications

Although the findings indicate that RASP did not lead to immediate reductions in acute service utilization during its early implementation phase, the program’s structure still offers potential value by providing timely psychiatric assessment and short-term stabilization for individuals entering the mental health system. This function central to the program’s design addresses a known gap in traditional outpatient pathways, where wait times for initial psychiatric consultation can be substantial. While our study does not provide evidence to support broader system-level impacts at this stage, understanding RASP’s role in improving timely access can help inform future evaluations of rapid-access service models. Therefore, policymakers and service planners should consider integrating RASP-like programs within a stepped care framework, ensuring that patients receive the right level of support early in their care trajectory. Future program evaluations should incorporate long-term follow-up, clinical outcomes, and cost-effectiveness analyses to fully capture the value of rapid access interventions. Moreover, investment in community-based supports and post-assessment follow-up will be critical in maximizing the benefits of early stabilization programs like RASP.

Conclusion

This study evaluated differences in acute mental health service utilization between individuals enrolled in Nova Scotia’s Rapid Access and Stabilization Program in its early phase and a matched control group. The finding revealed that participants in the RASP group were slightly younger compared to the control group. However, post-intake analyses suggested no statistically significant differences in acute mental health services utilization between the two groups. While findings did not reach statistical significance, trends suggest that RASP may help reduce acute service use, with the control group showing nearly double the proportion of unique patients admitted to psychiatric Inpatient unit’s post-intake, the mean rate of inpatient admissions and mean length of stay, and four times the mean rate of crises calls. This signifies that with a larger sample size and more patients in each group utilizing acute mental health care services, the study could have more power to detect differences. While the findings do not demonstrate an immediate impact on mental health services utilization, they highlight the significance of considering a broader range of outcomes, such as clinical improvements, patient satisfaction, and care coordination, when evaluating the impact of rapid access models. Further study with larger samples and extended follow-up is recommended to fully assess RASP’s effectiveness and inform future policy.
