CD13 identifies S100A4+ immunosuppressive monocytes and predicts temozolomide benefit in glioblastoma
Authors: Dong Yu, Ruiyao Hu, Qing Zhu, Xiao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM) is an immunologically “cold” tumor in which S100A4-driven immunosuppressive myeloid cells promote progression and resistance. However, the specific immune subpopulation that intrinsically expresses S100A4 and the corresponding cell surface targets remain poorly defined.Materials and methodsS100A4 expression was profiled across immune subsets in healthy human bone marrow and peripheral blood by flow cytometry. Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and lethal primary brain malignancy in adults, with a median survival of merely 15 months despite maximal surgical resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide chemotherapy [1, 2]. The aggressive nature of GBM stems not only from its intrinsic invasive growth pattern but also from a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) that renders the tumor refractory to conventional treatments and emerging immunotherapies alike [3–5]. Unlike immunologically “hot” tumors such as melanoma, GBM is characterized as an “immune-cold” tumor, exhibiting sparse tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) alongside abundant immunosuppressive myeloid cells [3, 6–8]. This unique immune landscape has increasingly been recognized as a major barrier limiting the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade and adoptive cellular therapies in GBM patients [9–11]. Consequently, deciphering the cellular composition and molecular regulators of this immunosuppressive niche has become a critical priority for developing more effective therapeutic interventions.

The GBM TME harbors a remarkably heterogeneous array of myeloid cells, including brain-resident microglia, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), monocytes, dendritic cells, and granulocytes such as neutrophils [2, 12–14]. Among these, the monocyte and macrophage lineage and the granulocyte lineage exert distinct yet often complementary roles in sustaining tumor progression [15, 16]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which often adopt an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype, promote tumor growth through the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines, facilitation of angiogenesis, and suppression of effector T cell function [17, 18]. Meanwhile, tumor-infiltrating neutrophils (TINs), historically overlooked due to their short lifespan, have recently emerged as important mediators of immunosuppression in multiple cancer types [19, 20]. Notably, recent single-cell studies have revealed that naturally immunosuppressive neutrophils reside in human bone marrow, and that neutrophils and monocytes may share convergent transcriptional features under pathological conditions [21, 22]. However, the extent to which these lineages contribute to GBM progression, and whether distinct subsets within each lineage carry specific immunosuppressive functions, remains incompletely understood. This cellular complexity necessitates the identification of lineage-specific markers that can distinguish pathogenic myeloid populations from those with homeostatic or anti-tumor functions.

A seminal study by Abdelfattah et al. provided a comprehensive single-cell atlas of human glioma and infiltrating immune cells, identifying S100A4 as a regulator of immunosuppressive T cells and myeloid cells in GBM [23]. The authors demonstrated that S100A4 was highly expressed in pro-tumorigenic myeloid subsets, including MDSCs and immunosuppressive macrophages, and that deleting S100a4 in non-cancer cells was sufficient to reprogram the immune landscape and significantly prolong survival in mouse glioma models [23]. Importantly, five myeloid cell subtypes defined in this study were shown to be independent prognostic indicators of glioma patient survival. These findings nominated S100A4 as a promising immunotherapy target and demonstrated the utility of single-cell datasets for discovering therapeutic targets [23]. However, given the extensive heterogeneity of myeloid cells within the GBM TME, a key question remained unanswered: which specific myeloid subpopulation serves as the principal carrier of the S100A4 signal? Addressing this question is essential for translating the S100A4-targeting concept into a clinically viable strategy.

Motivated by these findings, we set out to systematically identify the immune cell subpopulations that intrinsically express S100A4 and to discover cell surface markers that could serve as surrogates for targeting this immunosuppressive compartment. Using flow cytometry, we profiled S100A4 expression across immune cell types in paired healthy human bone marrow and peripheral blood samples, revealing monocytes as the predominant S100A4 carriers. Through integrative analysis of the TCGA GBM dataset, we identified CD13 (encoded by ANPEP) as the cell surface marker most significantly correlated with S100A4 expression. We further validated this association across multiple independent datasets, including the HPA, Immgen and GEO datasets, and uncovered a potential mechanistic link between CD13, S100A4, and TGFβ signaling in GBM-infiltrating monocytes. Critically, we demonstrated that temozolomide confers a significant survival benefit specifically in CD13-high GBM patients using the GEPIA 3 platform. Overall, our study identifies CD13 as a surface marker for S100A4-high immunosuppressive monocytes and highlights the CD13-S100A4-TGFβ axis as a promising target for GBM immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Human samples

Bone marrow aspirates and peripheral blood were collected from healthy donors with informed consent, in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. Peripheral blood samples were also obtained from glioblastoma patients prior to surgical resection. All samples were processed within 12 h of collection to maintain cell viability. All procedures involving human participants were approved by the Ethics Committee of Suzhou Municipal Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Protocol # S-2026-148-K01).

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

Consistent with previous reports, single-cell suspensions from bone marrow and peripheral blood were prepared by red blood cell lysis to obtain total white blood cells (WBCs), including polymorphonuclear leukocytes [21, 24–26]. Single‑cell suspensions from bone marrow and peripheral blood were stained with fluorochrome‑conjugated antibodies against surface markers to identify major immune populations. The antibody panels included CD3, CD4, CD8, CD19, CD56, CD11b, CD14, CD15, HLA‑DR, CD163, and CD45 (full list in Supplementary Table 5). For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using a commercial kit, then incubated with an anti‑S100A4 antibody. CD13 surface expression was assessed by staining with an anti‑CD13 (WM15) antibody. Data were acquired on an NovoCyte flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo v10.9.

For cell sorting, peripheral blood mononuclear cells from glioblastoma patients were labeled with CD3, CD19, CD11b, and CD14 antibodies. Monocytes, non‑monocyte myeloid cells, and non‑myeloid immune cells were isolated using a FACSAria III cell sorter.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

RNA was extracted from sorted cell populations using TRIzol reagent, followed by chloroform‑isopropanol purification. cDNA was synthesized and quantitative real‑time PCR (qPCR) was performed with SYBR Green master mix on an ABI Prism Step‑One system. Relative expression of S100A4 and ANPEP was normalized to GAPDH and calculated by the 2ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences for S100A4, ANPEP, and GAPDH are listed in Supplementary Table 5.

TCGA and database analyses

Gene expression and clinical data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) GBM cohort were accessed via the GEPIA, GEPIA2, and GEPIA2021 platforms [27–29]. Pearson correlation analysis was used to identify genes whose expression correlated with S100A4. Differentially expressed genes were defined by log2 fold change > 1 and adjusted P < 0.05. Survival analysis was performed by Kaplan–Meier curves with the log‑rank test, stratifying patients by the median expression level of the gene of interest (high: above median; low: ≤ median). To assess robustness, we also repeated the analyses using alternative cutoffs (upper and lower quartiles), which yielded consistent results (data not shown). The Human Protein Atlas database was queried for expression of S100A4 and ANPEP across immune cell lineages and brain regions. Bulk RNA‑seq data of 28 human peripheral blood immune cell subsets (GSE107011) were analyzed to profile S100A4 and ANPEP expression [30]. Mouse immune expression data were obtained from the Immgen database (https://rstats.immgen.org/Skyline/skyline.html) [31]. Drug response analysis in GBM patients stratified by CD13 or S100A4 expression was conducted using the GEPIA 3 platform.

Single‑cell RNA‑seq data analysis

GSE131928 dataset. Single‑cell transcriptomic data of tumor‑infiltrating myeloid cells from GBM (GSE131928) were processed with Seurat v4.1.1 in R [23]. Cells with fewer than 180 detected genes or > 30% mitochondrial reads were filtered. Data were log‑normalized, scaled, and subjected to principal component analysis. Batch correction was performed using Harmony, and clustering was carried out with the Louvain algorithm. Myeloid cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker expression, such as CD14, CD68, CD163, S100A8, S100A9. Cluster 11 was identified as the monocyte population co‑expressing high levels of S100A4 and ANPEP. Differential gene expression among clusters was assessed by the Wilcoxon rank‑sum test (log fold change > 0.25, adjusted P < 0.05). UMAP plots and violin plots were generated to visualize marker expression. To infer functional associations, protein-protein interaction networks were constructed using the STRING database (high confidence score > 0.7), and correlation between CD13/S100A4 and immunosuppressive factors was evaluated by Pearson analysis.

GSE182109 dataset. Raw scRNA‑seq data from GSE182109 were processed for validation. Count matrices were loaded with ReadMtx(), and Seurat objects were created with CreateSeuratObject() (min.cells = 3, min.features = 200). After merging, quality control retained cells with 200–5,000 detected genes and < 15% mitochondrial reads. Data were normalized and variable features were selected by variance‑stabilizing transformation. Following scaling and PCA (30 dimensions), clusters were identified (dims 1–20, resolution 0.5) and visualized by UMAP.

Cluster markers were determined using FindAllMarkers() (only.pos = TRUE, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25). To assess immune identity, module scores were calculated with AddModuleScore() using curated gene sets: Monocyte (CD14, LYZ, VCAN, FCN1), M1 macrophage (CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11, STAT1), M2 macrophage (CD163, MRC1, MSR1, CCL18), Neutrophil (S100A8, S100A9, CSF3R, FCGR3B), and Treg (FOXP3, CTLA4, IL2RA, IKZF2). Pearson correlations between S100A4 (or ANPEP) expression and immune module scores were computed and visualized by bar plots. Co‑expression was further assessed by FeatureScatter and blended FeaturePlot.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 and R v4.1.3. Comparisons between two groups were made using a two‑tailed unpaired Student’s t‑test. Survival differences were evaluated by log‑rank test and Cox proportional hazards models. For transcriptomic data, adjusted P values were obtained via Benjamini‑Hochberg correction. Data are presented as mean ± SD, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

S100A4 is predominantly expressed in monocytes

Previous studies identified S100A4 as a marker of immunosuppressive, poor-prognosis myeloid cells in glioblastoma [23]; however, due to substantial myeloid heterogeneity, the specific cell subpopulation primarily expressing S100A4 remains unclear. Recent research had demonstrated that naturally immunosuppressive neutrophils reside in human bone marrow [32]. This inspired us to investigate which immune cell types, particularly myeloid populations, intrinsically express high levels of S100A4 in healthy human bone marrow and peripheral blood.

To address this, we collected bone marrow and peripheral blood samples from healthy donors and examined S100A4 expression across immune subpopulations. We characterized immune cell subpopulations in human bone marrow and peripheral blood by flow cytometry [33] (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Within live immune cells (E506−CD45+), we identified the following populations: non CD4 & CD8 T cells (CD3+CD4−CD8−), CD4+ T cells (CD3+CD4+CD8−), CD8+ T cells (CD3+CD4−CD8+), B cells (CD3−CD19+), NK cells (CD3−CD19−CD56+), monocytes (CD3−CD19−CD11b+CD14+), macrophages (CD3−CD19−HLA-DR+CD163+), and neutrophils (CD3−CD19−CD15+). Flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow revealed that the monocyte and macrophage lineage harbored a high proportion of S100A4+ cells and exhibited relatively high S100A4 mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 1A, B). Intriguingly, non-myeloid non CD4 & CD8 T cells and CD4+ T cells also displayed substantial S100A4 expression, whereas bone marrow B cells and neutrophils showed low S100A4 levels. We next focused on the S100A4+ population within human bone marrow immune cells, performed t-SNE dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis, and mapped individual immune cell subsets onto the t-SNE plot (Fig. 1C). The results revealed that S100A4⁺ immune cells constituted approximately 15% of total bone marrow immune cells, among which monocytes represented the dominant population (Fig. 1D); this predominance was particularly pronounced within the S100A4+ myeloid compartment. We similarly collected peripheral blood samples from healthy donors for flow cytometric analysis. The results revealed that peripheral blood monocytes also displayed the highest proportion of S100A4+ cells and the strongest S100A4 MFI (Fig. 1E, F). t-SNE dimensionality reduction and clustering further demonstrated that, within the peripheral blood S100A4+ compartment, monocytes remained the predominant subpopulation among S100A4+ myeloid cells, even when the more abundant lymphoid populations (T, B, and NK cells) were taken into account (Fig. 1G, H). Collectively, these findings indicate that, both in the bone marrow, an inherently immunosuppressive microenvironment, and in peripheral blood, S100A4+ myeloid cells primarily refer to monocytes. Given the relatively limited abundance of monocytes among total immune cells, this observation further highlights their role as the principal carriers of the S100A4+ myeloid signal and suggests a potential contribution to homeostatic immune regulation.

CD13 represents the most significantly correlated cell surface marker with S100A4+ cells in glioblastoma

Although the S100A4+ myeloid compartment is closely linked to poor prognosis in glioblastoma, S100A4 itself is an intracellular molecule, rendering it challenging to exploit for direct detection or therapeutic targeting. To address this limitation, we focused on identifying S100A4-associated cell surface markers that could serve as more accessible surrogates for monitoring and intervention.

Using the TCGA glioblastoma dataset, we systematically analyzed surface markers correlated with S100A4 expression, aiming to nominate candidates with translational potential as diagnostic or therapeutic targets. We first identified the top 10 genes most significantly correlated with S100A4 expression in GBM patients using the TCGA database (Fig. 2A). Among them, ANPEP, which encodes the cell surface protein CD13, drew our attention because its expression showed a significant positive correlation with S100A4 in both GBM tumor and peritumor tissues (Fig. 2B). Boxplot analysis further revealed that ANPEP levels were significantly elevated in GBM tumors (Fig. 2C). Principal component analysis based on S100A4 and ANPEP expression demonstrated substantial overlap between tumor and peritumor samples (Fig. 2D), suggesting similar S100A4/CD13 expression profiles and implying that S100A4/CD13-expressing myeloid cells may begin to shape the immunosuppressive microenvironment from the tumor margin. Analysis of the HPA database showed that S100A4 was highly expressed in the monocyte lineage, while CD13, although expressed in monocytes, was even more abundant in the granulocyte lineage (Fig. 2E). Using the GEPIA 2021 platform, we further observed widespread upregulation of S100A4 across multiple tumor-infiltrating immune cell types in GBM, most prominently in the monocyte and macrophage lineage (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Fig. 2). Taken together, these results indicate that S100A4+ myeloid cells in GBM tumor tissues are predominantly comprised of monocytes, and that CD13 represents a potential cell surface marker for this population. This finding not only provides a candidate molecule for monitoring immunosuppressive myeloid cells within tumors, but also lays a foundation for the development of future targeting strategies.

To explore whether the CD13-S100A4 co-expression pattern extends beyond GBM, we performed a pan‑cancer correlation analysis of ANPEP and S100A4 expression across all TCGA tumor types. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, a significant positive correlation was observed only in a subset of cancers, including GBM, HNSC, LUSC, SARC, THCA, and THYM. In most other tumor types, no significant association was detected. These results indicate that the CD13-S100A4 axis may not be universally conserved across all solid malignancies. Notably, S100A4 itself has been reported to display context‑dependent prognostic value in different cancer types. Nonetheless, the strongest correlation was found in GBM, and together with our flow cytometry validation in patient samples, our findings support the relevance of this axis specifically in the context of glioblastoma.

CD13 marks both monocytes and neutrophils, yet monocytes remain the principal S100A4+ carriers in glioblastoma

To validate CD13 as a surface marker capturing the S100A4+ myeloid compartment, we performed flow cytometry analysis of healthy human bone marrow and peripheral blood using the same gating strategy applied for S100A4 (Fig. 3A-H; see Supplementary Fig. 1A, B for gating). In contrast to the restricted expression pattern of S100A4, CD13 was broadly detected across myeloid lineages. In bone marrow, both monocytes/macrophages and neutrophils exhibited high levels of CD13, with neutrophils showing the strongest mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3A, B). t‑SNE analysis confirmed that CD13+ cells were abundant and distributed primarily within monocyte and granulocyte clusters (Fig. 3C, D). In peripheral blood, a similar pattern was observed: although the CD13+ compartment was dominated numerically by neutrophils due to their much higher abundance, monocytes also expressed CD13 at high levels on a per‑cell basis (Fig. 3E-H). Critically, we previously demonstrated that neutrophils do not express S100A4 (Fig. 1A, B). Therefore, despite their numerical advantage and high CD13 expression, neutrophils cannot account for the S100A4⁺ myeloid signal that we and others have linked to poor prognosis in GBM. Instead, the S100A4+ myeloid population is carried predominantly by monocytes, which co‑express CD13 at high levels. This uncoupling of CD13 and S100A4 in granulocytes positions the CD13+ monocyte subset as a functionally relevant surrogate for the S100A4⁺ immunosuppressive myeloid fraction. Given the established roles of S100A4 in promoting immune suppression and tumor progression, these findings suggest that CD13 could serve as an accessible cell surface target for monitoring and therapeutically depleting the pathogenic monocyte compartment in glioblastoma, without disrupting the numerically dominant but S100A4‑negative granulocyte population.

CD13⁺ monocytes represent the S100A4-high population in glioblastoma

To obtain a broader expression profile of CD13 and S100A4 across diverse immune cell types, we analyzed a bulk RNA-seq dataset (GSE107011) encompassing 28 finely defined human peripheral blood immune cell subsets. Consistent with the pattern observed in Fig. 2E, S100A4 was broadly expressed across multiple immune cell types, with particularly high levels in classical (C)-monocytes, intermediate (I)-monocytes, non-classical (NC)-monocytes, myeloid (m)-DCs, and neutrophils. In contrast, ANPEP exhibited a more restricted expression pattern and was predominantly detected in C-monocytes, I-monocytes, NC-monocytes, m-DCs, basophils, and neutrophils (Fig. 4A). This comprehensive analysis further supports the notion that the monocyte lineage serves as the principal carrier of the CD13/S100A4 double-positive signal.

We next validated these findings at the transcript level in GBM patients. After excluding T and B cells, we sorted peripheral blood monocytes, non-monocyte myeloid cells, and non-myeloid immune cells from glioblastoma patients (Fig. 4B). qPCR analysis revealed that monocytes expressed significantly higher levels of both ANPEP and S100A4 compared to the other two immune populations (Fig. 4C), confirming that peripheral blood monocytes from glioblastoma patients represent the predominant immune subset naturally co-expressing high levels of CD13 and S100A4.

We further extended our analysis to examine the brain-region distribution of CD13 and S100A4. According to the HPA dataset, S100A4 was widely distributed across multiple brain regions, whereas ANPEP was characteristically enriched in the thalamus (Fig. 4D). This regional enrichment suggests that the S100A4+ myeloid cells implicated in glioblastoma progression by Nourhan Abdelfattah et al. may preferentially localize to the thalamus, where they could potentially exert local immunomodulatory functions. This observation provides new insight into the regional immune heterogeneity of glioblastoma and may inform the spatial targeting of future therapeutic strategies.

S100A4+ monocytes exert immunosuppression via TGFβ signaling in glioblastoma

The above findings established human monocytes as the principal carriers of the S100A4+ myeloid signal and identified CD13 as a candidate surface marker for this population. To investigate whether this expression pattern is evolutionarily conserved, we analyzed the Immgen dataset (https://rstats.immgen.org/Skyline/skyline.html) encompassing mouse immune cell populations (Supplementary Tables 1 and Fig. 4). To quantitatively substantiate this observation, we extracted ANPEP expression data from the ImmGen Human Expression Data and Mouse Gene Skyline portals (Supplementary Fig. 5). In human peripheral blood immune cells, ANPEP was predominantly expressed in monocytes and dendritic cells, with the highest normalized expression level exceeding 2,000 (DESeq2‑normalized counts). In contrast, mouse immune cells showed detectable ANPEP expression primarily in peritoneal mast cells and dendritic cells, but the maximum expression level reached only approximately 350, substantially lower than in humans. This approximately six‑fold difference in peak expression levels between species confirms that mouse myeloid cells lack robust CD13 expression compared to their human counterparts. This species discordance suggests that the CD13/S100A4-defined immunosuppressive myeloid compartment in GBM may represent a human-specific immune phenomenon. Consequently, conventional mouse models are inherently limited in recapitulating the dynamics of CD13/S100A4+ myeloid cells within the glioblastoma microenvironment, and future functional and translational studies should consider this limitation when selecting model systems. Our study therefore did not pursue in vivo experiments in mice.

To explore the potential immunosuppressive mechanisms underlying CD13/S100A4-monocytes, we analyzed a single-cell transcriptomic dataset of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells in GBM (GSE131928). Based on UMAP dimensionality reduction and cluster-specific signature gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B), myeloid cells were classified into clusters 0–13, among which cluster 11 was annotated as monocytes (Fig. 5A). Violin plots showed that most clusters, except clusters 6 and 12, exhibited robust S100A4 expression, with cluster 11 displaying the highest levels (Fig. 5B). In striking contrast, ANPEP expression was almost exclusively restricted to cluster 11 (Fig. 5B), and UMAP feature mapping confirmed that cluster 11 was the sole myeloid subset co-expressing high levels of both ANPEP and S100A4 (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that CD13 specifically marks S100A4-high monocytes within the glioblastoma myeloid infiltrate. To quantitatively assess this co-expression, we calculated the percentage of double-positive cells within the S100A4⁺ monocyte population, which was 11.7% (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Among all myeloid cells, the vast majority (80.8%) were S100A4+/ANPEP−, 10.7% were double-positive, only 0.1% were ANPEP+/S100A4−, and 8.3% were double-negative (Supplementary Fig. 7B). These data demonstrate that nearly all CD13+ cells co-express S100A4, confirming CD13 as a specific surface surrogate for S100A4+ monocytes. Moreover, both S100A4 and ANPEP showed the strongest positive correlation with the monocyte lineage among all immune subsets (Supplementary Fig. 7C, D), indicating that CD13+ monocytes represent the key S100A4+ myeloid population detectable in both tumor tissue and peripheral blood, which holds significant translational potential for non-invasive monitoring and targeted therapy.

(E) Correlation heatmap between CD13/S100A4 expression and key immunosuppressive factors. TGFB1 shows the strongest positive correlation with both markers. We next examined the transcriptional similarity between cluster 11 and other myeloid subsets, as well as immunosuppressive Treg cells. Notably, cluster 11 simultaneously exhibited transcriptional features of both monocytes and neutrophils (Fig. 5D), which is highly consistent with previously reported monocyte-like precursors of granulocytes (MLPGs) and immature neutrophils [21, 22], suggesting that immunosuppressive neutrophils and monocytes may share convergent expression profiles. We then assessed the association of CD13/S100A4 signaling with key immunosuppressive factors. Both CD13 and S100A4 showed the strongest positive correlation with TGFB1 (Fig. 5E). STRING protein-protein interaction network analysis further identified TGFB1 as a critical node bridging CD13 and S100A4 (Fig. 5F), a finding corroborated by independent network analysis using the cluster 11 core signature gene set (Supplementary Fig. 4B, C). It should be noted that these analyses reveal correlative associations rather than causal relationships. Future mechanistic studies, such as CD13 blockade or inhibition of TGFβ signaling, are needed to determine the directionality of this regulatory axis. TGFβ is a canonical immunosuppressive cytokine closely linked to markers of immunosuppressive cells such as PMN-MDSCs and TAMs [34–37]. Overall, these findings suggest that in GBM-infiltrating CD13+ monocytes, the surface protein CD13 and the intracellular factor S100A4 are functionally interconnected through TGFB1 signaling, potentially establishing a TGFβ-dependent immunosuppressive axis. This highlights the CD13-TGFB1-S100A4 network as a promising therapeutic target for reprogramming the GBM immune microenvironment.

Temozolomide represents an effective therapeutic strategy for CD13-high glioblastoma

To evaluate the clinical relevance of elevated CD13 expression, we performed a pan-cancer analysis using TCGA data and found that high CD13 expression was significantly associated with an increased risk of poor prognosis (Fig. 6A). We then collected and compared peripheral blood samples from healthy donors and glioblastoma patients. The results revealed a significantly higher proportion of monocytes in the peripheral blood of glioblastoma patients relative to healthy individuals (Fig. 6B). This observation not only reinforces the central role of monocytes in the GBM immune microenvironment but also supports the feasibility of CD13/S100A4-monocytes as potential prognostic and treatment-monitoring biomarkers.

We next leveraged the latest GEPIA 3 platform to examine the drug responsiveness of GBM patients stratified by CD13/S100A4 expression. When comparing Group 1 (complete and partial response) and Group 2 (stable disease and progressive disease) (Supplementary Table 2), we observed only trends toward improvement for several drugs, without reaching statistical significance. We subsequently assessed the impact of individual drugs on the survival of GBM patients with high CD13 or S100A4 expression (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression level of CD13 or S100A4 (high: above median; low: ≤ median). Strikingly, temozolomide conferred a significant survival benefit specifically in patients with CD13-high glioblastoma (Fig. 6C), whereas comparable benefit was not observed in the S100A4-high subpopulation. Using alternative cutoffs, such as quartiles, yielded similar results, indicating the robustness of CD13’s predictive value.

In brief, these findings suggest that temozolomide may represent a promising therapeutic agent for targeting immunosuppressive myeloid cells, particularly monocytes, in glioblastoma patients. Its therapeutic benefit may be partly mediated by reducing the CD13-high, pro-tumorigenic monocyte population, thereby remodeling the immune microenvironment and ultimately extending patient survival.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that among diverse human immune cell populations, monocytes represent the principal carriers of the S100A4 signal, both in the intrinsically immunosuppressive bone marrow and in the peripheral blood of healthy individuals. By integrative multi-dataset analysis, we identified CD13 as the most significantly correlated cell surface marker with S100A4 expression in GBM. We further determined that GBM-infiltrating CD13+S100A4+ monocytes are transcriptionally positioned at the interface between the monocyte and neutrophil lineages, and that their immunosuppressive program may be orchestrated through a CD13-TGFβ-S100A4 axis. Finally, we provided clinical evidence that temozolomide effectively improves survival in CD13-high GBM patients (Fig. 7).

Our study addresses a critical gap in the S100A4 literature by pinpointing monocytes as the dominant S100A4-expressing myeloid subpopulation, thereby refining the concept of S100A4-targeted immunotherapy. The identification of CD13 as a cell surface surrogate is particularly significant because S100A4 is an intracellular molecule, limiting its utility for antibody-based detection or depletion. By demonstrating that CD13 specifically marks S100A4-high monocytes in GBM, we provide a clinically actionable target for monitoring and therapeutically manipulating this immunosuppressive population [38]. Furthermore, the species discordance we observed in CD13 expression between human and mouse emphasizes the need for human-relevant model systems, such as humanized mouse models, for future translational studies of the CD13-S100A4 axis. The observation that temozolomide benefits CD13‑high but not S100A4‑high GBM patients (Fig. 6C) suggests distinct biological roles. One possibility is that CD13 influences chemosensitivity via S100A4‑independent mechanisms, such as its aminopeptidase activity or an association with MGMT [39]. Alternatively, S100A4‑high patients may harbor additional resistance pathways, as S100A4 drives mesenchymal transition and stemness in GBM, which are well‑known drivers of therapy resistance [40, 41]. Further studies integrating these markers with MGMT status and molecular subtypes are warranted.

S100A4 is a member of the S100 family of calcium-binding proteins and functions both intracellularly and extracellularly to regulate diverse processes, including cell motility, invasion, stemness, and immune signaling [42, 43]. Its association with poor prognosis across multiple cancer types underscores its importance in tumor biology, yet its intracellular localization has hindered therapeutic exploitation. Our finding that CD13, a membrane-bound aminopeptidase, is the surface marker most tightly linked to S100A4 expression in GBM provides a critical translational bridge. While CD13 itself has been implicated in tumor cell invasion and angiogenesis, its functional role in immunosuppressive monocytes is less explored [44–47]. Our data suggest that CD13 and S100A4 are interconnected through TGFβ signaling, raising the possibility that CD13 may actively participate in shaping the immunomodulatory phenotype of these cells rather than simply serving as a passive marker.

Beyond their correlative association, the CD13+S100A4+ monocyte subset is likely to exert direct immunosuppressive effects on effector T cells through the TGFβ-centered axis. In line with this, a recent study demonstrated that TGFβ signaling in monocytic myeloid cells suppresses CD8+ T cell activity during tumor metastasis [48]. Moreover, S100A4 has been shown to shift CD4⁺ T cell differentiation toward a Th2-polarized, pro-tumorigenic phenotype while reducing the proportion of Th1 cells [49]. Consistent with these findings, GBM-derived immunosuppressive monocytes have been reported to impair T cell responses through soluble factors [50]. Thus, the CD13-TGFβ-S100A4 axis identified in our study may contribute to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by restraining cytotoxic T cell function, and targeting this axis represents a potential strategy to enhance antitumor immunity in GBM.

A notable observation from our analysis is that GBM-infiltrating S100A4+ monocytes (cluster 11 in the GSE131928 dataset) simultaneously exhibit transcriptional features of both monocytes and neutrophils. This aligns with recent reports describing MLPGs and immature neutrophils that share convergent expression profiles with immunosuppressive monocytes [22]. Indeed, recent research, including work by Wei Liu et al. has demonstrated that myelocyte- and metamyelocyte-stage neutrophils, which predominate in human bone marrow and tumors across multiple cancer types, possess potent immunosuppressive properties [21]. While we found that neutrophils do not express S100A4, the transcriptional similarity between immunosuppressive monocytes and neutrophils highlights a broader paradigm: distinct myeloid lineages may converge on shared immunosuppressive programs driven by common molecular hubs such as TGFβ [51]. This functional convergence, rather than lineage origin, may ultimately determine the immunosuppressive capacity of myeloid cells in the TME.

We acknowledge several limitations of the present study. First, our flow cytometric profiling of immune cell subsets was performed on healthy donor samples, and the protein-level validation of CD13/S100A4 co-expression was conducted on peripheral blood rather than tumor-infiltrating leukocytes from GBM patients. This was largely due to the difficulty of obtaining sufficient fresh primary tumor specimens, which is a common challenge in GBM research. However, our transcriptomic analyses across multiple GBM datasets consistently corroborated these findings in the tumor context. Second, the functional link between CD13, S100A4, and TGFβ signaling was inferred from correlation and network analyses rather than direct experimental perturbation; future mechanistic studies employing genetic or pharmacological intervention are warranted to establish causality. Third, the species specificity of CD13 expression limits the utility of conventional mouse models. Notably, emerging humanized mouse models that support human neutrophil and monocyte reconstitution, such as the NCG-Gfi1−/− human immune system (HIS) mouse model, may provide valuable platforms for preclinical testing of CD13-targeted therapies [21].

Although CD13 represents a promising surface marker for S100A4+ immunosuppressive monocytes in GBM, its broad expression on normal myeloid cells, particularly neutrophils (Fig. 3), raises legitimate concerns about on‑target/off‑tumor toxicity. Unselective CD13 targeting might impair neutrophil functions such as phagocytosis, chemotaxis, or reactive oxygen species production, potentially increasing infection risk or altering innate immune homeostasis. To mitigate this risk, several strategies can be considered. Local delivery approaches, such as intratumoral injection or convection‑enhanced delivery, can increase local drug bioavailability within the tumor microenvironment while limiting systemic dissemination and associated side effects [52]. This strategy has been successfully applied to deliver potent immunotherapy combinations intratumorally with complete tumor eradication and no systemic toxicity, even when the same combination was lethal when administered systemically [53]. Alternatively, antibody‑drug conjugates (ADCs) offer a targeted approach to selectively deliver cytotoxic payloads to CD13+ cells. Notably, several ADCs targeting CD13 have been developed and validated as tumor targets [52]. However, it is important to note that most ADC toxicities are driven by payload effects on non‑transformed tissues, and the presence of the target antigen in normal tissue may also contribute to certain adverse events [54]. Therefore, careful design of the ADC components (antibody, linker, and payload) is critical to minimize off‑target toxicity. In parallel, lipid nanoparticle (LNP)‑based RNA therapeutics have emerged as a safe and efficient platform for targeting myeloid cells in the tumor microenvironment. Studies have demonstrated that LNP‑encapsulated siRNA targeting immunosuppressive genes in tumor‑associated macrophages achieves potent antitumor immunity without systemic inflammation or tissue damage [55–57]. Moreover, recent work has identified a catalytically inactive form of CD13 on endothelial cells that is conformationally accessible for selective targeting by peptide‑protein conjugates, suggesting that isoform‑selective targeting could achieve tumor‑specific delivery while sparing CD13 expressed on normal myeloid cells [58]. Collectively, these emerging strategies, local delivery, ADC optimization, LNP‑based RNA interference, and isoform‑selective targeting, provide a roadmap for safely targeting CD13+ immunosuppressive monocytes in glioblastoma while minimizing potential toxicities to normal neutrophils. Preclinical evaluation of these approaches in humanized mouse models will be essential before clinical translation.

Beyond the specific case of CD13, it is well recognized that human and mouse macrophages differ considerably in their surface marker profiles, cytokine production, and responsiveness to polarizing signals. For example, iNOS is preferentially induced in mouse macrophages upon LPS stimulation, whereas human macrophages characteristically upregulate CCL20, CXCL13, and IL-7R [59]. Moreover, a comprehensive review by Monnier et al. highlighted that differences in the impact of survival and polarization factors, as well as in the cytokines produced and markers expressed, help explain why TAM‑targeting therapies that succeed in mouse models often yield less impressive results in clinical trials [59]. In the context of GBM, conventional mouse models using syngeneic tumor lines fail to fully recapitulate the human immune microenvironment, as they lack human‑specific myeloid cell phenotypes and interactions [60]. These interspecies discrepancies may therefore confound the translation of preclinical data, and caution is warranted when extrapolating findings from conventional mouse models to human GBM patients. Humanized mouse models reconstituted with human immune cells offer a more faithful platform for evaluating CD13‑based immunotherapies in future studies [61].

Building upon our findings, several research avenues merit exploration. Functional studies using CD13 blockade or conditional deletion in human monocyte models, combined with S100A4 knockdown, are needed to dissect the mechanistic contribution of each component to the immunosuppressive phenotype. In vivo evaluation of CD13-targeted therapies in humanized GBM models would address the translational potential while circumventing the species mismatch [62, 63]. Additionally, given the strong correlation between CD13/S100A4 and TGFβ signaling, combination strategies that co-target this axis alongside immune checkpoint inhibitors may produce synergistic effects. Finally, the potential of CD13 as a liquid biopsy biomarker for monitoring monocyte-mediated immunosuppression in GBM patients during treatment warrants clinical investigation [13, 38].

In summary, our study identifies monocytes as the principal S100A4-expressing myeloid subset, nominates CD13 as a targetable surface marker for this population, and positions the CD13-S100A4-TGFβ axis as a promising therapeutic vulnerability in glioblastoma.

Conclusion

This study establishes monocytes as the principal carriers of the S100A4 immunosuppressive signal in human bone marrow and peripheral blood, and identifies CD13 as a cell surface marker tightly linked to this pathogenic population in glioblastoma. We further reveal a CD13-TGFβ-S100A4 regulatory axis in GBM-infiltrating monocytes and demonstrate the clinical benefit of temozolomide in CD13-high patients. By providing an accessible surface target for monitoring and potentially depleting immunosuppressive monocytes, these findings offer a translational framework for advancing GBM immunotherapy.
