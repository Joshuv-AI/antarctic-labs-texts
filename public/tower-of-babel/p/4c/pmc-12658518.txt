What drives inequality in modern family planning use among urban women residents? A cross-sectional analysis using the inequality of opportunity framework in Uganda’s secondary cities
Authors: Rornald Muhumuza Kananura, Catherine Birabwa, Ronald Wasswa, Jacquellyn Nambi Ssanyu, Kharim Mwebaza Muluya, Sarah Namutamba, Moses Kyangwa, Felix Kizito, Othman Kakaire, Richard Mugahi, Peter Waiswa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionModern family planning (mFP) use is vital for improving women’s health and rights. Yet, within urban areas, growing inequalities in service access remain poorly understood. This study applies the inequality of opportunity framework to examine individual and contextual drivers of mFP use in Uganda’s emerging secondary cities.MethodsWe analysed cross-sectional survey data collected in November–December 2021 from 1023 women aged 15–49 in Iganga Municipality and Jinja City, Central-Eastern Uganda. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine associations between mFP use and individual-level predictors. The Shapley decomposition method was applied to a weighted logistic regression model to quantify the proportion of inequality attributable to unfair circumstances versus fair differences in effort (sexual activity).ResultsmFP prevalence was 45.6% (95% CI 41.8% to 49.4%), with 63% of users relying on long-acting reversible methods. The Shapley analysis findings indicated that 37.8% of the variation in predicted mFP use was explained by observed predictors. Of this, 90% was due to unfair circumstances: age (17%), parity (16%), employment (10%), religion (9.5%), authority (9%), place of residence (9%), wealth position (8%), education (7%) and marital status (5%). mFP use declined with increasing age but rose with parity, with the highest likelihood observed among women aged 20–30 years with three or more children. While mFP use generally increased with wealth and employment, among poor women, those who were employed, lived in non-slum areas or were educated were paradoxically less likely to use mFP.ConclusionInequality in mFP use in Uganda’s secondary cities stems from structural and demographic disadvantages beyond women’s control. Poorer women who were educated, employed or in non-slum areas were still less likely to use mFP, suggesting how pro-poor efforts may miss marginalised subgroups within advantaged categories. Addressing these gaps requires inclusive, intersectional strategies that reflect urban inequality and promote equitable access.Keywords: Community Health, Epidemiologic Factors, Health Services Accessibility, Public Health, Sociodemographic Factors

Introduction

Universal access to sexual and reproductive health is a crucial component of sustainable development.1 2 This includes empowering women to decide the number and when to have children, which contributes substantially to reducing maternal and child mortality and promoting economic growth.3–5 Available evidence has indicated how modern family planning (mFP) is critical in controlling the world’s total fertility rate and its consequences including climate change.2 Despite rising global demand for FP, many low- and middle-income countries—especially in sub-Saharan Africa—still face high unmet needs due to elevated fertility rates and low levels of women’s empowerment.6–8 Within these countries, significant disparities persist, with lower coverage observed among poorer, less-educated, rural and younger women.

To achieve United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals 3 and 5, particularly the target related to universal access to sexual and reproductive health and the promotion of gender equality, there is a critical need to understand and address inequalities in access to FP services to ensure that no one is left behind. While it has been assumed that urban dwellers have better access to health services, the urban population experiences multiple factors that inhibit them from accessing appropriate FP services.9–11 On the one hand, more often the urban healthcare system is dominated by the private health providers,11 who usually provide services at exorbitant prices that do not match the quality of services. As such, the poor may not have enough money to purchase appropriate FP products. On the other hand, the urban population is engaged in employment, most of which is inflexible to allow them access to health services because often, they work throughout the day and weekends without leave days.9 Such a kind of employment coupled with other competing family duties may hinder women from accessing appropriate FP services.9

Health inequity exists when people are unfairly deprived of the resources that are necessary to prevent them from undesirable conditions.9 12 The WHO commission on social determinants of health considers unfair differences within and between groups as social injustice.13 It is only through the equity lens that certain population segments can be observed if they are being deprived of the FP resources needed to avoid unwanted pregnancies.9 12 Additionally, understanding inequity in FP services helps policymakers to comprehend the capacity of the existing public health system in meeting the needs of the most vulnerable individuals.14 The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated the inequities in access to FP services, with women in lower socioeconomic class areas experiencing inadequacies in access services.15

With the rapid urbanisation in sub-Saharan Africa, efforts to achieve universal access to FP services require an approach that goes beyond urban–rural inequalities to understanding of intraurban disparities in mFP coverage and the identification of population segments that are being left behind. Applying the inequality of opportunity (IOp) framework, this study examines the role of socioeconomic, family and individual factors in shaping mFP use in Iganga Municipality and Jinja city, in Central Eastern Uganda. Uganda is a particularly relevant case due to its rapid urbanisation, marked not only by the expansion of Kampala city suburbs but also by the rise of newly gazetted cities and municipalities across the country.

Inequality of opportunity in the context of modern family planning services

Building on Roemer and Trannoy’s foundational work,16 the IOp framework posits that total observed inequality in outcomes, such as mFP use, can be decomposed into components attributable to individual effort, chance and structural conditions. The framework’s core premise is that not all inequality is ethically problematic: differences arising from individual effort or preferences may be acceptable, while those resulting from circumstances beyond an individual’s control are not. The IOp framework categorises determinants of inequality as either fair (individual choices) or unfair (externally imposed constraints).16–18 Checchi and Peragine distinguish between ethically non-offensive inequality (due to effort) and ethically offensive inequality (due to circumstances).17 In the context of FP, this study considers the socio-economic, place of residence, religion affiliation and demographic factors as unfair circumstances.16 17 From a rights-based perspective, we argue that women should be able to use FP services regardless of their socioeconomic status, place of residence, religious affiliation and age. Recent sexual activity is considered a fair effort, as it represents a circumstance that cannot be avoided by any woman of reproductive age.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This was a quantitative cross-sectional household survey that was carried out in Iganga Municipality and Jinja City in central eastern Uganda (Busoga region) from November to December 2021. Based on the 2022 Uganda Demographic and Health Survey (UDHS), Busoga’s total unmet need for FP was 26.2%, slightly above the national average of 24%, with 16.7% for birth spacing.19 Among sexually active unmarried women in the region, unmet need was substantially higher at 54.9%, including 42.6% for spacing.19

Sample size and sampling procedure

We applied lot quality assurance sampling (LQAS) data collection methodology. At the first stage of sampling, municipality divisions in the two districts were randomly selected (two in Iganga and three in Jinja). We then randomly selected five parishes in each division as LQAS supervision areas, where a fixed sample size of at least 20 households was allocated. Each parish had a sampling frame consisting of all villages with their respective population. This was followed by a random selection of 50% of the villages in each parish using a table of random numbers. The number of interviews in each parish was divided across selected villages.

According to Jinja City and Iganga Municipality statistical abstract reports, during the day, 40% of the population is non-resident, who reside in the bordering villages. To cater for such differences, we used a list of all the households and workplaces within the selected urban centres. In the process, a total of 1023 women aged 15–49 years were successfully enrolled for the study. The inclusion criteria were women of reproductive age group (15–49 years of age) who were residing and/or working in the study area. Of these, 206 women were interviewed from their workplace and therefore information specifically on the household characteristics such as household assets, housing structure, as well as water, hygiene and sanitation was not captured. The study not only excluded women who refused to consent but also those who had severe illness at the time of the survey. Details on the study design and sampling are indicated in the study protocol.20

Data collection

Data were collected using face-to-face interview structured questionnaire for each of the sampled households. 20 experienced and qualified research assistants (6 men and 14 women) were recruited and trained intensively for 7 days with the training covering the data collection tools as well as research and field work ethics. We collected information on women’s sociodemographic characteristics, marriage and sexual activity, prenatal and postnatal care, contraception use, child health, fertility (birth and pregnancy history), as well as working environment. The questionnaire was prepared in English and then translated to Lusoga; the primary language spoken in the study areas.

The questionnaire was initially designed in Microsoft Excel for compatibility with the Open Data Kit platform (online supplemental file 1), uploaded onto Android tablets via KoboCollect and administered electronically by the research assistants. The questionnaire had inbuilt validation checks for providing appropriate questions for specific participants and for providing warning messages in case of inconsistencies (online supplemental file 1). Prior to data collection, a pretest survey was conducted in villages that were not part of the sampled areas, and identified problems were corrected before the actual survey was done. Household and workplace geo-coordinates for all interviews were also taken. Four supervisors were selected, and the principal investigator supervised the entire data collection process.

Study variables and their measurements

mFP use was the dependent variable. This was defined as the use by a woman or her partner of at least one of the following methods: male or female sterilisation, injectable, intrauterine devices, contraceptive pills, implants, female or male condoms, standard-days method, lactational amenorrhoea (limited to women who were within 6 months postpartum, exclusively breastfeeding and had not resumed menstruation) and emergency contraception. We generated mFP use as a binary outcome which was coded 1 if a woman or her partner was currently using any of the above methods at the time of the survey and 0 if a woman was either using a traditional method or not using any method.

The independent variables included woman’s age, education level (no education/primary, secondary/higher), marital status (unmarried, married) and place of residence (landing site slum, mainland slum and other residential areas such as those for elites and businesspeople). Other variables were working status (not working, work throughout the year, work seasonally/part of the year and occasionally), religion (Catholic, Anglicans, Muslims and Pentecostal) and parity (0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5+, but treated as numerical within the model).

We also included wealth index, which was generated using tetrachoric factor analyses. We included items that are known to determine the household’s wealth status, including asset ownership such as television, bicycle, car and land; housing structure; and household hygiene and access to water sources. From this, wealth tertiles were generated with the first tertile representing 33.3% of poor households and the last tertile corresponding to 33.3% less poor households. FP decision-making authority was another variable where women were asked to state a person who usually decides for them when to use FP methods and the options were: woman alone, husband, jointly with partner and other people. The survey also asked women about their sexual activity and the time they last had sex with any man. This was a basis of generating the variable of sexual activity with categories of within 5 months, between 6 and 12 months and 1 year and above preceding the interview.

Data analysis

We started with a descriptive summary of all study variables using weighted and unweighted proportions. Bivariate logistic regression models were fitted to assess unadjusted associations between mFP use and key covariates, reporting ORs with 95% CIs. Variables with a p value less than 0.2 in the bivariate analysis, along with those theoretically established as determinants of mFP use, were included in the multivariate binary logistic regression model for further analysis. We used sampling weights in all models to account for complex survey design. To evaluate the relative importance of each predictor in shaping the probability of mFP use, we employed Shapley value decomposition, a model-agnostic method derived from cooperative game theory. This approach calculates the average marginal contribution of each variable to the model’s predicted outcome by considering all possible combinations of predictors. All analyses were conducted in R (V.4.5.0), using the glm() function for logistic regression and the iml package for Shapley value decomposition.

Ethical declarations

Before data collection, we sought administrative clearance from the leaders of Jinja city and Iganga municipality. Informed consent was obtained from all study participants and participants were made aware of their right to withdraw from the interviews at any stage. Married participants aged 15–19 years were deemed emancipated, thereby allowing them to directly provide consent for their participation. For unmarried adolescents, a two-step consent process was implemented. Initially, informed consent was obtained through the household head (parent/guardian), followed by individual consent from the participants for their participation in the interviews. Details of the research ethics approval and registration are provided in the Ethics Approval Statement section.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

The majority resided in slum areas, with 43% living in mainland slums and 21% in slums located along landing sites (online supplemental table 1). Most participants were married (73%), had attained secondary or higher education (60%) and had been sexually active within the 5 months preceding the interview (92%) (online supplemental table 1). Close to 40% of women were in the third wealth quartile and nearly half (52%) were employed year-round (online supplemental table 1).

Distribution of mFP use

The prevalence of mFP was 45.6% (95% CI 41.8% to 49.4%) of which 63% were using long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARC), 36% short-term methods and close to 1% permanent methods (sterilisation) (online supplemental figure 1). mFP use increased sharply among those with more recent sexual activity and was lower (13%) among women who had sexual intercourse more than a year ago (figure 1A). Among women using mFP, sexual activity patterns varied by marital status (figure 1B). Among married mFP users, 96.3% had been sexually active within the 5 months preceding the interview. In contrast, only 64% of unmarried mFP users had engaged in sexual activity during the same period (figure 1B).

Inequality in modern family planning use by individual factors

After running the multivariable logistic regression (table 1), we applied Shapley decomposition to obtain the contribution of each factor. The receiver operating characteristic curve demonstrated moderate discriminatory performance, with an area under the curve of 0.69 (online supplemental figure 2). The total mean absolute Shapley contribution of all predictors was 0.378, indicating that, on average, the predictors jointly shifted individual predicted probabilities of modern contraceptive use by 37.8 percentage points from the mean prediction. Among the ten predictors assessed, calculated age (16.6%) and parity (16.2%) contributed the most, followed by sexual activity status (10.1%), employment status (9.9%) and religious affiliation (9.5%) (figure 2). Marital status had the lowest relative contribution (4.9%). Considering sexual activity status as a fair effort and all other variables as unfair circumstances (figure 2), 89.9% of the model’s predictive power is attributable to IOp, indicating that much of the predicted variation in contraceptive use is shaped by factors beyond individual control.

Association of individual and contextual factors with mFP use

The likelihood of using mFP decreased with increasing age (figure 3A) and increased with parity (figure 3B). Specifically, each additional year of age was associated with a 4% reduction in the likelihood of mFP use (adjusted OR (aOR) 0.96, 95% CI 0.95 to 0.97), whereas each additional birth was associated with a 16% increment in mFP use (aOR 1.16, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.20) (table 1). The interaction between age and parity further revealed that the likelihood of mFP use was highest among women aged 20–30 years who had at least three children (figure 3D).

In terms of education, women with at least secondary education level were 23% less likely to use mFP (aOR 0.77, 95% CI 0.72 to 0.82) compared with those with primary level education or none (table 1). When an interaction between education and wealth status was introduced, the likelihood of mFP use was lower among the women with at least secondary level education who belonged to households with lower wealth tertile (figure 4A). Considering employment status, relative to women not working, the odds of using mFP were 36% higher among those employed year-round (aOR 1.36, 95% CI 1.27 to 1.46 and 73% higher (aOR 1.73, 95% CI 1.58 to 1.89) among those working part of the year (table 1). Introducing an interaction between employment status and wealth category revealed that women in the lowest wealth group who worked either throughout the year or part of the year were significantly less likely to use mFP methods (figure 4B).

Turning to decision-making authority, compared with women who made FP decisions independently, the odds of using modern FP were significantly higher among those whose husbands were the main decision-makers (aOR 1.75, 95% CI 1.48 to 2.06), and among those whose decisions were made by other people (aOR 1.34, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.57). Additionally, the interaction between decision-making authority and wealth status revealed that women in higher wealth tertiles who reported making decisions about mFP use independently or jointly with their husbands were less likely to use modern methods compared with those whose decisions were influenced by their partners or others (figure 4B).

Compared with unmarried women, married women had significantly lower odds of using modern FP methods (aOR 0.85; 95% CI 0.78 to 0.93) (table 1). Regarding sexual activity, the odds of mFP use declined as the time since last intercourse increased. Women whose last sexual activity occurred within the past 5 months (aOR 3.75, 95% CI 3.19 to 4.42) or between 6 and 12 months ago (aOR 2.03, 95% CI 1.66 to 2.48) were significantly more likely to use mFP methods compared with those whose last activity was over a year ago (table 1).

Discussion

This study examined the individual-level determinants of mFP use in emerging urban areas of Uganda, with a particular focus on understanding the inequality in mFP use. Overall, 46% of women were using mFP, and among them, 62% relied on LARC methods. While our estimates exceed the national average coverage (33%), they align closely with the overall urban coverage (48%) reported in the 2022 UDHS.19 Applying the IOp framework, we found that 37.8% of the variation in mFP use was attributable to the full set of observed individual-level characteristics. Of this total, 10% was linked to recent sexual activity—a factor we considered as fair effort. The remaining 90% of the explained variance stemmed from unfair circumstances outside women’s control, including demographic, socioeconomic and structural dimensions.

Age and parity emerged as dominant contributors to inequality in mFP use, jointly accounting for approximately one-third of the total explained variation. Although the likelihood of mFP use decreased with increasing age, it rose consistently with higher parity, with usage peaking among women in their prime reproductive years who had multiple children. These patterns suggest that younger women are more inclined to adopt modern contraceptive methods, potentially due to the greater need for preventing unplanned pregnancies and birth spacing. Conversely, the high likelihood of mFP use among women with higher parity appears more motivated to use mFP could be driven by the desire to limit further childbearing. These findings underscore the need to tailor FP interventions to both life-course stage and fertility preferences, with particular attention to older, low-parity women who may be underserved, face unique barriers to access and use or have distinct fertility needs that may not be adequately addressed by existing contraceptive options. We recommend integrating FP interventions within existing healthcare delivery platforms, particularly maternal and child health (MCH) services, where many women first engage with the health system. However, we recognise that certain groups, such as those who have already completed their desired childbearing, may have limited or infrequent contact with MCH services.

Consistent with extensive literature from sub-Saharan Africa,21–28 the likelihood of mFP use increased with household wealth status and interacted with other factors to exacerbate inequalities across subgroups. Women in the lower wealth tertile were associated with financial constraints that impede access to mFP services. These constraints include both direct costs of contraceptive methods and indirect costs such as transportation, time away from income-generating activities and limited access to accurate health information. In urban settings, these barriers are further shaped by the service delivery landscape: public facilities, which typically offer free services, are often located far from informal settlements where the urban poor reside, while nearby private facilities may be more accessible but unaffordable.

Contrary to previous studies conducted in Uganda and across sub-Saharan Africa,29–31 women with secondary education or higher were less likely to use mFP compared with those with no formal or only primary education—a pattern also observed in a study on urban Indonesia.32 Moreover, our analysis of the interaction between education and wealth revealed a counterintuitive pattern: women with lower levels of education in the higher wealth tertiles were more likely to use mFP than their wealthier, more educated counterparts. Persistently low use despite socioeconomic advantage points to underlying structural or normative barriers. Community-based interventions often focus on less-educated or poorer women, increasing access for these groups. Conversely, wealthier, educated women—less often reached by such programmes—may face gaps in service engagement.

Furthermore, and consistent with previous studies,30 31 women who were employed were more likely to use mFP compared with those who were not working. However, this effect varied depending on household wealth position. Among wealthier women, those working occasionally had the highest likelihood of using mFP, even more than those employed throughout the year. In contrast, women from the poorest tertile were less likely to use mFP despite working throughout the year. Some scholars have emphasised that the type of employment matters in shaping access to reproductive health services, including FP.33 34 For example, jobs that allow for greater autonomy and flexibility may facilitate health-seeking behaviour, while those that are rigid or low-paying may constrain access. Additionally, women who are not working may have lower levels of autonomy and economic empowerment, which can reduce their ability to make independent decisions about contraceptive use.34 As such, for wealthier women, occasional employment may be associated with greater autonomy, access to information and flexible time to seek services. Among poorer women, however, structural barriers such as time poverty, cost and limited access to services may dampen the enabling effects of employment.

Turning to the role of place of residence within the urban space, our findings revealed important nuances in how urban residence and household wealth intersect to influence mFP use. Contrary to conventional assumptions, living in a slum was not uniformly associated with lower contraceptive uptake. In fact, poorer women residing in mainland slums were slightly more likely to use mFP than their counterparts in non-slum areas. Conversely, women in the lowest wealth tertile living in non-slum mainland areas had the lowest predicted mFP use. This group may represent a ‘hidden” underserved population—economically disadvantaged yet spatially removed from the focus of pro-poor interventions that typically prioritise slum settings. Residential variation in mFP use is influenced by various factors including the availability of and distance to service providers.

In our recent study, we found that many small clinics are concentrated in small towns within the mainland.35 Also, we noted that the mainland slums are close to the urban business centre and can easily access the services, while the mainland non-slum residential areas are apart from the urban business centres, and therefore, use of health services including FP may be hard for those with financial constraints. The findings challenge the conventional dichotomous categorisation of considering only slum dwellers. In reality, marginalised populations may also reside within non-slum neighbourhoods yet face hidden barriers to service access. As such, our study results underscore the complexity of health service utilisation in urban settings and point to the need for geographically nuanced interventions that address disparities across all residential areas—not only within slums but also among underserved groups in non-slum communities.

Furthermore, our findings suggest that reproductive decision-making autonomy does not uniformly translate into increased mFP use across socioeconomic groups. While women whose husbands or others were the main decision-makers exhibited significantly higher odds of mFP use, the association for those who reported joint decision-making was positive but not statistically significant. The interaction between reproductive decision-making autonomy and wealth status revealed important nuances. Among both poorer and wealthier women, those who perceived themselves as making FP decisions jointly with their partners were less likely to use mFP than those reporting independent decision-making. Interestingly, poorer women who relied on their husbands for decision-making were sometimes more likely to use mFP than those who reported autonomy—possibly reflecting supportive male partners or covert use. These patterns suggest that formal expressions of autonomy may not always reflect actual agency, especially in low-resource settings where sociocultural dynamics, economic dependence and gender norms mediate reproductive choices.

Note that in certain patriarchal or extended-family households, decision-makers (eg, husbands, elders or mothers-in-law) may actively support and facilitate contraceptive use.31 In these settings, reported lack of decision-making authority does not necessarily imply disempowerment; rather, it may signal reliance on pro-FP gatekeepers who enable access through instrumental or logistical support. Among wealthier households, even where women report limited autonomy, supportive partners or family members may mitigate structural barriers, such as cost or access, thereby sustaining higher levels of mFP use. Conversely, in poor households, even when autonomy is reported, the lack of enabling resources may limit actual use, suggesting that autonomy without support may not be sufficient for action. Interventions must therefore consider not just who makes the decision, but under what conditions and with what support such decisions are enacted.

Religious affiliation and the perceived importance of religion contributed 8.4% to the observed inequality in mFP use, highlighting the continued influence of sociocultural norms and faith-based beliefs on contraceptive behaviours in urban Uganda. Religion has long been recognised as a key determinant of health-seeking behaviours, including reproductive health decisions.36–38 In many religious communities, the use of modern contraception is perceived to conflict with doctrinal teachings. Attitudes toward mFP vary significantly across religious groups, with traditional Protestant churches often more accepting of contraceptive use, while the Catholic Church permits only natural methods and prohibits modern contraceptive methods. Islamic teachings and many traditional Christian denominations also emphasise pronatal values, viewing children as blessings from God, which can discourage contraceptive use. Such variations in religious doctrine and interpretation underscore the importance of engaging religious leaders as influential actors in shaping community norms around FP. Interventions that have involved faith leaders in FP advocacy—framed through culturally sensitive messaging and scriptural references from the Bible or Quran—have shown promising results in improving acceptance and uptake of mFP.36–38

Furthermore, the association between recent sexual intercourse and mFP use highlights the close alignment between reproductive exposure and contraceptive behaviour. Women who had not engaged in sexual activity for extended periods were significantly less likely to use modern contraception, suggesting that perceived or actual pregnancy risk strongly influences contraceptive decision-making. In contexts where contraception is not used, sexual activity significantly increases the risk of unintended pregnancy.39 In this study, approximately 4 in 10 unmarried women and 6 in 10 married women who reported sexual activity in the month preceding the survey were using mFP. These findings point to the need for expanding access to a broad method mix to meet the diverse fertility intentions of sexually active women. Although sexual activity was classified as a fair circumstance in our analysis, it is important to recognise that it can itself be shaped by structural inequalities, including gender norms, partner dominance and sexual coercion.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is the use of a large, representative dataset capturing diverse urban population groups, allowing for granular analysis of intraurban disparities in mFP use. The application of Shapley value decomposition within the IOp framework enabled a rigorous assessment of the relative contributions of individual and contextual determinants—an approach rarely applied in reproductive health research in sub-Saharan Africa.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Associations identified in the analysis should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Second, the reliance on self-reported data, particularly regarding sensitive subjects such as sexual activity and decision-making autonomy, may be affected by social desirability bias. While confidentiality measures were implemented during data collection, such biases remain possible. Third, approximately 59% of the inequality in mFP use remained unexplained by the measured factors in the model. This residual inequality likely reflects supply-side and demand-side barriers not captured in the survey data. On the supply side, potential contributors include limited availability of preferred contraceptive methods, periodic stock-outs, inadequate staffing and long distances to health facilities.40 On the demand side, entrenched social and cultural norms, stigma, misinformation and varying levels of autonomy and agency are likely to shape contraceptive behaviour in complex ways. These unmeasured barriers may disproportionately affect marginalised groups within urban settings and contribute to persistent inequities in mFP access.

Lastly, this study was conducted in newly gazetted cities and municipalities, and its findings may not be fully generalisable to larger cities. Nevertheless, many large city suburbs share similar characteristics with these emerging urban areas. Thus, the study provides a strong foundation for understanding the drivers of mFP disparities in Uganda and other countries experiencing similar urbanisation trends, while highlighting the need for more nuanced, mixed-methods research to capture the complexity of access barriers.

Conclusion

Our study provides new insights into the structural and contextual determinants of mFP use in emerging urban settings in Uganda. Using the IOp framework, we show that much of the variation in mFP use is unjust, shaped by socioeconomic status, residence and household decision-making—factors beyond women’s control. These findings call for a shift from individual-focused models to structural interventions that address intersecting inequalities in education, employment, wealth and gendered power dynamics.

One critical strategy is to integrate mFP into routine health system touchpoints that women already access, such as antenatal care, postnatal care, immunisation visits and outpatient services. Embedding FP counselling and provision within these commonly used MCH platforms can help normalise mFP use and reduce missed opportunities. Similarly, leveraging the routine reach of community health workers, by making FP part of their ongoing home visits and community-based outreaches, can extend services to under-represented or harder-to-reach groups. Outreach efforts must also go beyond the ‘usual’ disadvantaged populations to capture marginalised women in non-slum areas, informal workers and the urban poor in less visible settings.

Other service delivery models, such as workplace-based, digital and social platforms, and off-hour services, can be tested to assess how they can effectively meet the needs of women whose employment patterns or living conditions limit interaction with conventional health platforms. Lastly, gender-transformative approaches that enhance women’s agency and engage men and community influencers are essential to addressing deep-rooted social norms and promoting reproductive equity.
