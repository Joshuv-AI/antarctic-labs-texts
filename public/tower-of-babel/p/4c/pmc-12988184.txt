Evaluation of mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE gene markers for detection of enteric fever using real-time PCR
Authors: Samreen Arshad, Saima Younas, Muhammad Luqman Qadir, Nazim Hussain, Muhammad Zafar Saleem, Qurban Ali, Shiming Han, Daoud Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnteric fever, a systemic infection caused by Salmonella enterica serovars Typhi and Paratyphi, continue to pose a substantial public health concern, with an estimated 20 million cases annually. It is transmitted via the consumption of contaminated food or water and is a substantial public health issue in low- and middle-income nations, resulting in extended fever, stomach pain, and severe sequelae if not addressed. The clinical diagnosis of enteric fever is complicated by nonspecific symptoms, the low sensitivity and specificity of blood cultures and serological methods, and the restricted application of qPCR in routine diagnostics, partly due to the insufficient testing or validation of numerous gene targets across various Salmonella strains. The study was conducted to evaluate mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE gene markers for detection of enteric fever causing typhoidal Salmonella species using real-time PCR. Primers specific to the mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE genes were developed and assessed utilizing Primer3Plus, an oligo calculator, and NCBI BLAST. Conventional PCR with optimized thermal profiles was employed for amplification, succeeded by SYBR Green-based real-time PCR and gel electrophoresis to verify the size and specificity of the amplicons. Sanger sequencing of specific folE amplicons confirmed their identity and revealed conserved sequences among S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi. The mdh gene demonstrated significant genus-level specificity, whereas dld and tcfA exhibited diagnostic potential for typhoidal strains. Initial in-silico predictions suggested that folE was specific to S. Typhi, but it was also experimentally identified in S. Paratyphi isolates. Sanger sequencing validated significant sequence similarity and a synonymous SNP, demonstrating functional conservation of folE, which encodes GTP cyclohydrolase I, among typhoidal Salmonella. The folE gene sequences for S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi have been submitted to GenBank with accession numbers PV700621 and PV700622. Introduction

About 2500 Salmonella serotypes have been categorized into the subspecies enterica using the White–Kauffmann–Le Minor method1. The majority of serotypes can be broadly divided into typhoidal and nontyphoidal strains. The former group is the cause of enteric fever and includes S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi (typhoidal Salmonella), which have been detected in humans only, while the latter group includes strains that have been reported to infect both humans and animal species, causing a variety of illnesses, including salmonellosis, sepsis, and gastroenteritis, respectively2. Incidence of enteric fever is minimal in Australia, Europe, North America, New Zealand, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada, with the majority of cases originating from Pakistan or India via travel3,4. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that there are up to 21 million cases of enteric fever and 161,000 fatalities annually across the globe. Nonetheless, the true burden of the disease remains indeterminate, as this estimate was derived from a restricted number of surveillance studies employing current diagnostic methods5. In 2017, there were 14.3 million instances of typhoid and paratyphoid fevers worldwide6. Individuals from low-income nations, particularly in Southeast Asia, Africa, Pakistan, India, and Latin America, appear to be the most impacted by enteric fever6. Enteric fever is endemic in Pakistan, with an estimated frequency of 727,800 cases and 8,175 fatalities in 2017, alongside a rising trend of antibiotic-resistant enteric fever7. In Pakistan, S. Typhi is the predominant etiological agent of enteric fever, although S. Paratyphi A is the most prevalent among paratyphoid serovars, with a lower incidence. A study in Pakistan revealed that 93% of Salmonella-positive blood cultures were identified as S. Typhi, while only 7% were S. Paratyphi A, with no isolates of S. Paratyphi B or C discovered8. From 2016 to 2019, the incidence of enteric fever was documented at certain locations in Karachi, Pakistan. In total, 22% (2230/10,094) of enrolled patients tested positive for enteric fever, with 94% (2093/2230) of the isolates identified as Salmonella Typhi9. Health authorities in Pakistan have determined that between 2016 and 2020, over 22,354 instances of typhoid fever were documented. This calculation is employed to quantify the incidence rate of typhoid fever, yielding a ratio of approximately 15.5 per 1,000 individuals10. Globally, typhoid fever results in 269,000 fatalities from 26.9 million new cases annually11. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, there were 9.25 million typhoid fever illnesses and 110,000 fatalities in 2019, with South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa accounting for the majority of these cases12.

Alongside diagnostic difficulties linked to closely related Salmonella serovars, the transmission pathways and certain clinical manifestations are also common among these serovars. The elderly, children, immunocompromised individuals, and travelers are particularly vulnerable to enteric fever13,14. Salmonella serovars are disseminated via the fecal–oral pathway following the consumption of contaminated food and drink. The incubation period of enteric fever is roughly 8 to 14 days15, and the duration and severity of the illness are influenced by the bacterial strains, dosages, and host immunological responses16–19. Typhoid and paratyphoid fevers are clinically indistinguishable. Both can manifest with similar severity of sequelae, but typhoid fever is more common than paratyphoid fever in most endemic regions20–23. Clinical manifestations include elevated temperature, malaise, loss of appetite, headache, tachycardia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, gastrointestinal distress, and neurological sequelae are not exclusive to enteric fever24–27, complicating a diagnosis based only on clinical presentation. Infections caused by viruses (e.g., influenza, dengue), parasites (e.g., typhus, leishmaniasis, malaria), and certain bacteria (e.g., tuberculosis, brucellosis) prevalent in endemic regions may exhibit analogous symptoms28–32. The existing diagnostic methods are unable to accurately differentiate enteric fever from other conditions.

Prolonged diagnosis and commencement of antibiotic therapy may lead to significant clinical consequences and mortality33. Consequently, prompt and accurate laboratory identification of enteric fever is essential to diminish disease and death rates, as well as to limit the spread of the infection. The conventional diagnostic techniques, including blood or stool cultures and the Widal test, may be employed to identify the etiological agent of the infection. But these approaches possess numerous drawbacks. The WHO suggests bacterial isolation from blood or bone marrow for the conclusive diagnosis of enteric fever34. Nonetheless, due to its invasive characteristics, bone marrow aspirates are infrequently obtained, despite their culture demonstrating high sensitivity (about 90%) and relative resistance to past antibiotic usage in patients35. The blood culture methods yield compromised findings due to prior antibiotic administration and a diminished bacterial load, and they are time-consuming as well. Moreover, they only detect 45–70% of confirmed cases35–38. Serological tests are appropriate for point-of-care application, characterized by ease of use, speed, and cost-effectiveness compared to certain existing serological tests. Nonetheless, these tests sometimes exhibit cross-reactivity among antigens and demonstrate inadequate sensitivity and specificity, thereby leading to diagnostic misinterpretations39. The commonly employed serological assay in endemic areas, the Widal test, yields positive results solely in the initial phases of the disease40.

DNA-based diagnostic techniques, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), have demonstrated more sensitivity, specificity, and rapidity relative to traditional culture-based methods for diagnosing numerous infectious illnesses41–44. Specific genes and genetic loci used to identify Salmonella Typhi and Paratyphi include the tviA is uniquely associated with the Vi capsular operon, and the H1d flagellar gene (fliC-d) for S. Typhi, and the tyvelose epimerase gene (tyv) for S. Paratyphi A45. However, these loci have notable drawbacks: (i) some are absent in certain strains (e.g., Vi-negative S. Typhi), (ii) they can be horizontally transferred or lost, (iii) sequence similarity among typhoidal serovars can lead to cross-amplification, and (iv) they generally cannot differentiate all etiological agents of enteric fever from one another46. In contrast, the genes selected in this study (mdh, dld, folE, and tcfA) combine conserved housekeeping targets with serovar-specific polymorphisms and a typhoidal-restricted virulence factor. This panel enables robust detection and more reliable differentiation of S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi than previously used targets.

Real-time PCR, also known as Quantitative PCR (qPCR), has significantly enhanced the detection of Salmonella species, presenting significant advantages in comparison to conventional diagnostic methodologies such as culture, biochemical assays, and serological tests. While traditional culture techniques are often regarded as the gold standard, they are inherently time-intensive (typically necessitating 4–7 days for results). These can produce false negatives due to low bacterial numbers or antibiotic use before sample collection. In contrast, qPCR facilitates the rapid, sensitive, and specific identification of Salmonella DNA directly from clinical or food samples, frequently yielding results within a matter of hours. A recent investigation revealed that qPCR achieved a detection sensitivity exceeding 90% for Salmonella spp., in comparison to an approximate sensitivity of 70–80% associated with standard culture techniques47.

Biochemical identification is dependent upon the growth and metabolic activity of viable organisms, which can be compromised by the conditions and handling of the sample. Likewise, serological techniques may fail to differentiate between current and past infections. Similar antigens on the surface of these bacteria can lead to cross-reacting immune responses and false-positive or indeterminate findings in serological tests, including the Widal test48. Real-time PCR evades these constraints by permitting the detection of even non-viable bacteria, rendering it particularly advantageous in food safety assessments and outbreak investigations. The application of Real-time PCR for the detection of Salmonella represents an efficacious methodology that facilitates the identification of this pathogen across diverse sample types, encompassing both food samples and clinical specimens. Recent advancements have culminated in the creation of multiplex real-time PCR assays capable of simultaneously detecting and distinguishing multiple Salmonella serovars, which may offer enhanced efficacy and accuracy compared to existing detection techniques. These assays have exhibited high sensitivity and specificity, making them suitable for routine surveillance and outbreak investigations.

This study presents a straightforward approach to uncover novel DNA diagnostic markers specific to enteric fever by employing genome data from NCBI and BV-BRC databases and publicly accessible nucleic acid sequence alignment tools (BLASTn). This study aims to establish a real-time PCR assay for the identification of enteric fever by synthesizing primers that are specific to both the genus and serovars for the detection of S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi. Moreover, real-time PCR results are compared to the blood culture and biochemical-based reporting of the bacteria.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and storage

Salmonella enterica subspecies enterica serovar Typhi ATCC 700,931 strain, provided by CAMB’s diagnostic lab, was utilized as the reference strain. Presumptive bacterial strains were gathered from Jinnah Hospital in Lahore, such as clinically invasive Salmonella Typhi and Salmonella Paratyphi, Escherichia coli, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Enterococcus sp. To obtain a sufficient number of bacterial cells, blood samples from suspicious typhoid patients were collected in blood culture bottles and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) and MacConkey agar media plates were then streaked with bacterial cells grown in blood culture bottles. Colonies were inspected for distinctive morphology after 24-hour incubation of plates at 37 °C. On MacConkey agar, both S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi showed up as pale colonies that could not ferment lactose. On XLD, S. Paratyphi may exhibit less noticeable H₂S activity or even no darkening, whereas S. Typhi usually generates conspicuous black-centered red colonies due to robust H₂S production. To identify microorganisms, presumed colonies were collected and sent to the VITEK MS-MALDI-TOF (Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization Time-of-Flight) system. The culture plates were stored at 4 °C and re-streaked twice a month.

The experimental data collection followed all the rules and regulations at the Centre for Applied Molecular Biology (CAMB), University of the Punjab, Lahore, Pakistan. Permissions from the authorities were obtained as required. It has been confirmed that the bacterial samples were collected and used while complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation with appropriate permissions from authorities of the CAMB. It has also confirmed that informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s). The Ethical Review Committee of the CAMB gave the ethical approval to conduct the research work. This study was approved by institutional Review board Punjab university Lahore, Pakistan (Letter No.D/99/FIMS, Dated 13-9-2024).

Primer designing and validation- in silico approach

The whole-genome gene annotation of Salmonella Typhi and Salmonella Paratyphi was conducted using the BV-BRC platform49. Four candidate genes exhibiting differing specificity among Salmonella serovars were selected for primer designing for PCR detection of enteric fever. A common target for genus-level detection was chosen to be the mdh gene50,51, an extensively preserved virulence gene found in all Salmonella species. To improve discriminatory capability, primers were further synthesized for the dld gene and tcfA gene. The dld gene encodes the D-lactate dehydrogenase (DLD) enzyme. This enzyme is crucial for D-lactate metabolism and facilitates the conversion of D-lactate to pyruvate, a vital step in the bacterium’s metabolic pathways52. And the tcfA gene encodes a protein belonging to the autotransporter family, which is released by Gram-negative bacteria. These proteins facilitate the translocation of molecules through the bacterial outer membrane. It is also implicated in bacterial adherence to host tissues, which is necessary for the bacterium’s ability to colonize the host and commence infection53. For the precise identification of S. Typhi, the folE gene was also targeted, which encodes GTP cyclohydrolase I, an enzyme that plays a role in folate (vitamin B9) production. Folate is crucial for numerous physiological functions of bacteria, including DNA synthesis, cellular proliferation, and amino acid metabolism54.

The primers were designed according to the whole genome sequence of Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhi Ty2 (Accession number: AE014613.1) using primer3plus (https://www.primer3plus.com/), and primer properties such as melting temperature (Tm), GC content, hairpin formation, and self-complementarity were assessed using the Oligonucleotide Properties Calculator (https://www.biosyn.com/gizmo/tools/oligo/oligonucleotide%20properties%20calculator.htm). To prevent non-specific amplification during PCR, the specificity of the primers was initially assessed in silico using NCBI-BLAST software (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) and in silico PCR (http://insilico.ehu.es/PCR/) and AmplifX software. Genomic DNA extraction by heat lysis method

Heat lysis was used to extract bacterial genomic DNA from Salmonella colonies by slightly modifying the already reported method by Dashti, Ali A., et al.55. A microcentrifuge tube was filled with 500 µL of sterile distilled water, and a single, well-isolated bacterial colony was removed using a sterile loop from a fresh agar plate and mixed in distilled water. To ensure adequate mixing, the suspension was vortexed for 15 s. After that, the tube was heated for ten minutes at 85 °C in a heat block to lyse the bacterial cells and liberate the genomic DNA. After the heat treatment, the tube was allowed to sit at ambient temperature for three minutes before being centrifuged for three minutes at 5000 RPM in order to pellet the cell debris. Without disrupting the pellet, the crude DNA-containing supernatant was carefully moved to a fresh, clean microcentrifuge tube. The extracted DNA was used as the template for real-time qPCR assays and kept at -20 °C until needed.

Conventional PCR with gradient temperature optimization

Gradient PCR, from 52 to 60 °C, was performed on a conventional thermocycler using bacterial colonies to optimize the annealing temperature of the primers at which they give the best results. The product sizes of primers were 760 bp (mdh), 276 bp (folE), 663 bp (tcfA), and 992 bp (dld). The recipe used for the reaction mixture included 2.4µL of 25 mM MgCl2, 1 µL of 5U/µL Thermo Fisher Scientific™ Taq DNA Polymerase, 1 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 2 µL of 10X PCR buffer, 11.6 µL nuclease-free PCR water, template DNA (single colony), and 1 µL each of 10µM forward and reverse primers. SYBR™ green-based real-time PCR

Thermo Fisher Scientific™ SYBR™ Green format was used for real-time PCR for qualitative detection of mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE genes in the above-mentioned collected samples, and data processing was performed utilizing the AB Quant Gene Real-Time PCR System (SYSTAAQ Diagnostic Products). The PCR mixture (10 µl) included 2 µl of extracted DNA as the template, 1 µM of each forward and reverse primer, and 6 µl of Thermo Scientific ™ SYBR ™ Green qPCR Master Mix. The amplification program included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of PCR for 30 s at 95 °C for denaturation, 30 s at the optimized annealing temperature for each gene for primer-annealing, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min, concluding all amplification reactions. Every run contained S. Typhi ATCC 700,931 as the positive control and a non-template control lacking target DNA template. Fluorescence data were collected at the end of each extension phase. The assay was regarded as valid only when the negative control exhibited no fluorescence signal, and the positive control displayed amplification.

Evaluation of primer performance-sensitivity and specificity

To ascertain the specificity and sensitivity of PCR primers, each set was examined against a panel of gathered Salmonella and non-Salmonella species. To verify sensitivity, only the target gene should be amplified; to verify specificity, no amplification in the non-Salmonella strains should be detected.

Enteric fever detection profile

The in-silico PCR and NCBI blast showed the presence of the mdh gene in all strains of Salmonella except S. bongori and S. arizonae. While the dld gene was present in S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi A and C, it was absent in S. Paratyphi B and a few others. Along with a few other strains, S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi A possessed the tcfA gene, while S. Paratyphi B and C did not. The folE gene showed in silico PCR bands only in S. Typhi strains, hence considered specific for S. Typhi. Gel purification for Sanger sequencing

Two samples were sent for sanger sequencing by extracting DNA fragments from agarose gel using FavorPrep TM GEL/PCR Purification Mini Kit (Favorgen, Taiwan) (Cat. No: FAGPK 001) following the kit’s protocol. DNA bands of interest were removed and put into a microcentrifuge tube. About 500 µL of FADF Buffer was added to the gel followed by incubation at 55 °C for 10 min. The dissolved samples were then cooled to room temperature and poured onto FADF columns followed by centrifugation for 30 s at 11,000 × g. After washing the columns with 750 µL of ethanol-added Wash Buffer, they were centrifuged for 30 s at 11,000 × g and then again for three minutes at 18,000 × g to get rid of any remaining wash buffer. DNA was eluted by adding 50 µL of Elution Buffer to the membrane’s center. Purified DNA was recovered by centrifuging the column at 18,000 × g for one minute. Each purified DNA was then sent for Sanger sequencing.

Analysis of sequencing results

The chromatogram files derived from Sanger sequencing (.ab1 format) were initially accessed and evaluated with BioEdit software to assess the quality of the electropherograms. Regions of low quality, uncertain bases denoted by Ns, and background noise were manually excised from both ends of the chromatograms. The cleaned nucleotide sequences were analyzed via nucleotide BLAST (blastn), to determine the species and gene identity. The nucleotide sequences were translated into protein sequences using Expasy-Translate Tool (https://web.expasy.org/translate/) and CDS feature available on NCBI, followed by a protein BLAST (blastp) to obtain their functional annotation and assess their similarity with established protein sequences. The sequences of several genes were aligned with MEGA 12 software for phylogenetic analysis. The sequences were then submitted to NCBI via the BankIt submission portal.

Results

Gradient temperature optimization using conventional PCR

A thermal gradient PCR was conducted to optimize the annealing temperature for amplifying the mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE gene primers. Temperature intervals of 52 °C to 60 °C were examined. Distinct and precise amplification bands for the mdh gene, dld gene, tcfA gene, and folE gene, measuring ~ 760 bp, ~ 992 bp, ~ 663 bp, and ~ 276 bp, respectively, were seen across the gradient.

Validation of PCR conditions for the mdh gene amplification

The mdh gene amplified well at all the annealing temperatures tested between 52 and 60 °C, as seen from the persistence of clear bands at the expected product size of ~ 760 bp on the 1.5% agarose gel image (Fig. 1). This tells us that the primers used to amplify the mdh gene were effective and specific at a wide range of temperatures, implying the stability of the PCR conditions. This consistent amplification through all tested temperatures validates the optimal performance of the mdh primers under the 52 to 60 °C conditions and shows stability of the assay under the described PCR conditions.

Enhanced tcfA gene specificity at higher temperatures

Amplification of the tcfA gene was observed across annealing temperatures from 52 to 60 °C. In contrast, progressively higher temperatures produced clear, well-defined bands, reflecting improved primer specificity and amplification efficiency. These findings emphasize the importance of precise annealing-temperature control for reliable and targeted tcfA gene amplification.

Real-time PCR detection

Real-time PCR was performed to detect the presence of mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE genes. The amplification curves were examined to compare the collected S. Typhi samples with S. Paratyphi samples, non-Salmonella species samples, positive ATCC control strain, and no-template control (negative control). As 56.9 °C annealing temperature gave optimal amplification in gradient PCR, hence, this temperature was chosen for real time PCR for all genes. The cycling conditions are shown in (Fig. 5).

Diagnostic potential of the mdh gene for Salmonella

The real-time PCR assay was effectively adapted, targeting the mdh gene, a housekeeping gene of Salmonella enterica, utilizing SYBR Green chemistry. The amplification process comprised 35 cycles. Fluorescence detection graphs (Fig. 6A) exhibited consistent exponential amplification for all samples, including S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi, except samples 8 and 13 (both were S. Typhi). Threshold cycle (Ct) values ranged from 16.68 to 22.56 (Fig. 6C), signifying diversity in the copy number of the target template, showing the specificity of the test. The linearity of the amplification curves confirmed the lack of substantial technical variation. Agarose gel electrophoresis examination of all reactions confirmed the presence of the anticipated PCR product of ~ 760 bp (Fig. 6D), validating primer specificity. No amplification was observed in the non-Salmonella species. The mdh gene demonstrated consistent expression throughout the examined isolates, serving as a reliable internal control or a novel genetic marker for Salmonella identification.

Specific detection of dld gene in Salmonella and fluorescent artefacts in Enterococcus via qPCR

The existence of the dld gene in Salmonella and non-Salmonella isolates was verified using a real-time PCR that targeted the 992 bp of the gene. A strong amplification of the target DNA was demonstrated by the amplification curves (Fig. 7A), which displayed distinct and repeatable fluorescence signals with distinct exponential growth phases across samples. The assay’s specificity and lack of contamination were confirmed by the absence of amplification in the negative control. The Ct values varied from about 14.51 to 28.27 (Fig. 7B), which suggests that isolates differed in the quantity of the template. Agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 7C), which was used for post-PCR confirmation, confirmed that all positive samples had amplified the 992-bp anticipated fragment. The correlation between gel bands and real-time curves indicates the assay’s robustness. Four samples (one S. Typhi and three S. Paratyphi) showed no amplification in the predicted samples, which may indicate degradation of DNA, a low template copy number, or absence of this gene. Sal13 showed a lighter band in gel electrophoresis, while Ct real-time PCR showed no Ct value, indicating low template concentration. The dld gene exhibited negative results in all non-Salmonella species, except for Klebsiella pneumoniae and Enterococcus species, while Enterococcus species demonstrated fluorescence but were not identified in gel electrophoresis, suggesting the presence of nonspecific probe binding or fluorescent artifacts rather than true amplification. These observations underscore both the specificity of the assay for Salmonella and the need to interpret anomalous qPCR fluorescence with caution.

Real-time PCR confirms specific amplification of tcfA gene in typhoidal Salmonella

Real-time PCR of the tcfA gene amplified S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi DNA effectively in the tested samples, validating its presence. Amplification curves obtained with the SYBR Green assay (Fig. 8A) showed clear exponential fluorescence in S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi samples, whereas one biochemically reported S. Typhi isolate, one S. Paratyphi isolate, all non-Salmonella species, and the non-template control exhibited no amplification. The corresponding Ct values for positive samples ranged from 13.97 to 31.25 (Fig. 8B), while no signal was detected in non-Salmonella species or the negative control. Discussion

Enteric fever is prevalent in the majority of developing nations. The incidence is 14 million cases annually, resulting in approximately 130,000 to 200,000 fatalities56. Enteric fever is caused by S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi, with the majority of cases reported from South and Southeast Asia57,58. Salmonella infections in endemic areas can be identified using diagnostic approaches like serological testing and blood and bone marrow cultures, but their low sensitivity and invasiveness make diagnosis challenging59. It results in the majority of diagnoses being made clinically and antimicrobials given empirically. A quick, simple, and sensitive molecular method for identifying Salmonella species and differentiate Salmonella serovars is PCR. Various studies have employed conventional, multiplex, nested, and qPCR to detect Salmonella species by targeting multiple genes60–64. The goal of this study was to uncover novel DNA diagnostic markers specific to enteric fever using real-time PCR and to compare these molecular results to blood culture and biochemical-based identification of the bacteria. Four genes—mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE—were identified as prospective candidates for molecular diagnosis to accomplish this objective. SYBR Green chemistry was selected over TaqMan chemistry due to its cost-effectiveness, simplicity, and suitability for initial assay development and validation in resource-limited settings. While TaqMan chemistry provides higher specificity, and expensive. SYBR Green allowed us to efficiently screen and optimize primers. Specificity in our assay was ensured through rigorous primer design, melting curve analysis, and validation against non-target strains. Conventional PCR and SYBR Green-based real-time PCR were utilized to assess the diagnostic efficacy of these gene markers in identifying typhoidal Salmonella associated with enteric fever.

This study’s findings highlight the mdh gene’s diagnostic potential as a dependable genus-level genetic marker for identifying Salmonella species responsible for enteric fever. Real-time PCR employing SYBR Green chemistry successfully amplified the mdh gene in 13 of 15 Salmonella isolates, resulting in a positive rate of 86.7%. The elevated detection rate underscores the assay’s sensitivity and validates the use of mdh as a housekeeping gene for pathogen identification. The amplification curves exhibited exponential growth in the majority of examined isolates, with the exception of two S. Typhi samples, maybe attributable to template degradation or variance in primer binding sites. No amplification was detected in non-Salmonella species, hence supporting the assay’s specificity. The Ct values range (16.68–22.56) indicates variations in the target gene’s copy number among isolates, potentially reflecting differences in genomic DNA concentration or transcriptional activity. Notwithstanding this fluctuation, the uniform linearity of amplification curves signifies negligible technical error and validates the assay’s repeatability. Sukhnanand et al.65 discussed the mdh gene (malate dehydrogenase) as such a housekeeping gene for its crucial role in the metabolism in bacteria and its constant, constitutive expression in a wide range of conditions and cell types. In Salmonella enterica, the mdh gene encodes a protein which converts malate to oxaloacetate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, a major pathway for energy production. Apart from being used as a control, the mdh gene is proposed as a diagnostic marker for Salmonella as it is present in most serovars and absent in most non-Salmonella organisms. The findings of current study align well with the well-known role of mdh gene.

The study of the dld gene as a potential diagnostic marker for enteric fever shown reliable amplification in 11 of 15 Salmonella isolates, resulting in a positive rate of 73.3%. The lack of amplification in one S. Typhi and three S. Paratyphi isolates indicates potential reasons including gene absence, suboptimal template quality, or sequence variation at primer binding sites. In silico analysis revealed that the dld gene may be absent in Salmonella Paratyphi B, possibly explaining the negative results observed. This hypothesis supported one sample but the other three samples lack comprehensive support from other gene targets, such as tcfA and folE, which did not correspond with a Paratyphi B profile, underscoring the necessity for additional molecular typing or sequencing. The assay’s specificity was essentially confirmed, as amplification did not occur in the majority of non-Salmonella species. Klebsiella pneumoniae and Enterococcus species shown a certain level of amplification. Only K. pneumoniae generated a comparable 992 bp band on agarose gel, but Enterococcus exhibited modest fluorescence in real-time PCR but did not display discernible bands in gel electrophoresis which indicates the possibility of a non-specific amplification or primer-dimer formation. Faintly labelled non-target fluorescence signals may sometimes be detected with real-time PCR results due to incomplete primer binding or to the formation of secondary structures, especially in the SYBR Green assay, which non-specifically binds to any double-stranded DNA66. The lack of a clear band on a gel indicates that no large or specific amplicon was produced, validating the concept that the fluorescence seen did not represent true amplification of the dld gene. This discrepancy indicates that the application of melt curve analysis and gel verification is required to confirm qPCR results. In addition, it is possible that the fluorescent signals were generated by low-affinity, non-specific binding to the homologous sequences of Enterococcus, instead of stable binding, because nonspecific amplifications were not detectable by gel electrophoresis. Overall, the dld-based assay showed moderate sensitivity and specificity for Salmonella species, but false negatives and inadequate amplification suggest it’s more suitable as a supplementary target in multiplex PCR assays. Future research should involve sequencing negative isolates to elucidate strain categorization and investigate the impact of dld polymorphisms on amplification efficiency.

The tcfA assay produced robust findings, with 9 of 10 S. Typhi isolates (90%) and 4 S. Paratyphi isolates (100%) testing positive, culminating in an overall positivity rate of 86.7% (13/15) among typhoidal Salmonella. The results align with in silico study, confirming the presence of tcfA in S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi A, while it is absent in S. Paratyphi B and S. Paratyphi C. The solitary negative S. Typhi result may be attributed to sequence heterogeneity in the primer binding region, suboptimal template quality, or misidentification during biochemical assays. The genomes of the common broad-host-range serovars Typhimurium and Enteritidis do not contain the Typhi colonization factor (tcf), which belongs to the α fimbrial clade of the chaperone-usher fimbriae and is found in the typhoidal serovars Typhi, Paratyphi A, and Sendai. Additionally, Tcf’s homology to the cable (Cbl) pili of Burkholderia cepacia and the CS1 fimbria of human-specific enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli has suggested a role for tcf in the host specificity of typhoidal serovars to humans. In S. Typhi, the tcf locus includes six ORFs encoding the fimbria chaperone (tcfA), major fimbrial subunit (tcfB), the usher (tcfC) and the adhesin (tcfD). The tcfA gene is expressed at high salt concentrations, but its deletion did not impair bacterial adhesion or invasion of epithelial cells. Furthermore, tcfB antibodies were detected in the blood of patients with typhoid fever, suggesting that these fimbriae are produced and may play a role during infection67. Another study aligns with this, showing the presence of tcfA gene in S. Typhi, and S. Paratyphi A68. No amplification occurred in non-Salmonella species or the non-template control, hence affirming the assay’s specificity and absence of cross-contamination. The identity of the amplicon was confirmed using agarose gel electrophoresis, which exhibited distinct 663 bp bands in positive samples. Ct values varied from 13.97 to 31.25, indicating differences in template abundance among clinical isolates. The evident association between real-time PCR and gel electrophoresis further emphasises the reliability and specificity of the tcfA-based assay. Furthermore, experimental results revealed that the folE gene is not specific to S. Typhi, as amplification was also observed in S. Paratyphi isolates. folE was detected in 100% of S. Typhi (10/10) and 80% of S. Paratyphi (4/5) isolates. The amplification in S. Paratyphi isolates was unforeseen, as in silico PCR and NCBI BLAST analyses utilizing various computational techniques suggested that folE is unique to S. Typhi and lacking in S. Paratyphi, rendering it a viable biologically-restricted diagnostic marker. Nonetheless, the fluoresence observed in S. Paratyphi samples during the experimental validation suggests that the gene is clearly conserved among Salmonella enterica serovars. This observation is supported by our Sanger sequencing data from isolates Sal15_folE and Sal17_folE, which revealed a significant similarity in folE sequences to reference strains of S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi. Holt, K.E, et al. carried out comparisons of whole-genome sequences which indicated that the chromosomes of Paratyphi A and Typhi exhibit a significantly closer genetic relationship than those of other S. enterica serovars69. The mismatch between in silico predictions and experimental observations underscores the limitations of relying solely on in silico predictions. The BLAST results indicated that Sal15_folE exhibits 100% sequence identity and 99% query coverage with Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhi, corroborated by a highly significant E-value of 1e-119, affirming its restricted genetic affinity with S. Typhi. Sal17_folE demonstrated 100% sequence identity and 100% query coverage with Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Paratyphi A, yielding an E-value of 2e-117, thereby confirming its significant sequence homology with S. Paratyphi A. These findings experimentally confirm that the folE gene is significantly conserved among typhoidal Salmonella serovars, hence endorsing its efficacy as a universal molecular marker for their identification. The sequence alignment of folE gene segments from Sal15_folE and Sal17_folE demonstrated a 97% identity, with the 3% discrepancy resulting from initial misalignment and a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) at position 145 of the query sequence. This SNP features a guanine (G) in S. Typhi and an adenine (A) in the S. Paratyphi sequence. This nucleotide variation is a synonymous mutation70, meaning it does not change the encoded amino acid sequence of GTP cyclohydrolase I, therefore maintaining the protein’s structure and function. This discovery highlights the functional conservation of the folE gene in both isolates, despite slight nucleotide variations.

As indicated in (Table 4), molecular results largely aligned with biochemical identification, demonstrating potential of mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE gene markers for identifying typhoidal Salmonella responsible for enteric fever. Nonetheless, their restricted capacity to differentiate between S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi serovars diminishes their efficacy for comprehensive epidemiological monitoring and customized treatment. This underscores the necessity for more precise markers to enhance diagnosis accuracy and patient management. In the present study, the capability of the newly developed assay to differentiate typhoidal from non-typhoidal Salmonella was not directly assessed, as only typhoidal serovars were included during assay development and validation. No parallel testing with non-typhoidal serovars using either the gold standard or the newly developed method was performed. To robustly establish this discriminatory power in future work, a panel of well-characterized non-typhoidal Salmonella isolates should be analyzed in parallel with typhoidal strains using both the gold standard and the new assay, followed by comparative sensitivity/specificity analysis to quantify differentiation capability. These findings endorse the ongoing advancement of gene-based detection instruments, and additional study utilizing a wider array of clinical isolates is essential to corroborate these results across other geographies.

Conclusion

Enteric fever continues to pose a considerable health challenge in developing nations, primarily attributed to S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi. This study assessed four gene targets—mdh, dld, tcfA, and folE—as potential molecular markers for the detection of enteric fever-inducing typhoidal Salmonella utilizing SYBR Green-based real-time PCR. mdh exhibited significant genus-level specificity, whereas dld and tcfA revealed diagnostic potential for typhoidal strains, with tcfA being especially proficient in identifying S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi. A. folE was initially considered specific to S. Typhi, as per an in-silico study, but it was also identified in 80% of S. Paratyphi isolates experimentally. Sanger sequencing validated significant sequence similarity and a synonymous SNP, demonstrating functional conservation of folE among typhoidal Salmonella. The nucleotide sequences of the folE gene in S. Typhi and Paratyphi are available at GenBank under accession numbers PV700621 and PV700622, respectively. Despite limited serovar discrimination, the study demonstrates that molecular assays can provide rapid, sensitive screening for typhoidal Salmonella in routine diagnostics and research. With refined genetic targets, the same platform could enable precise serovar differentiation and high-throughput clinical testing.
