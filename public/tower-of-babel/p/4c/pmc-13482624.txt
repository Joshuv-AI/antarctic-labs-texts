Integrated metabolomic, cytotoxic, and computational investigation of Caralluma tuberculata extract in breast cancer cells
Authors: Amir Ali, Rodolfo Moreno-Castillo, Luis Cordova-Bahena, Carlos Martín Cerda-García-Rojas, Zia-ur-Rehman Mashwani, Edgar Hernández-Martínez, Juan Pedro Luna-Arias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Caralluma tuberculata, belonging to the family Apocynaceae, is widely used in folk medicine for the management of diseases such as diabetes and cancer, owing to its rich secondary metabolite profile. This study aimed to explore the metabolites of C. tuberculata and evaluate their anti-breast cancer activity using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), Liquid Chromatography–Electrospray Ionization Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC–ESI–MS/MS), and the MTT assay. HPLC and LC–ESI–MS/MS analyses revealed major bioactive peaks at 17–22 and 45–80 min, indicating a high diversity of phytoconstituents. Metabolomic analysis identified 60 phytochemicals, highlighting the plant as a rich source of compounds with potential pharmacological relevance. Moreover, the extract exhibited selective cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells (44% reduction in viability) while exerting comparatively lower effects on non-tumorigenic MCF-12F breast cells (22%), suggesting its potential as a source of bioactive compounds. To explore potential molecular targets associated with these effects, the identified metabolites were evaluated for their interactions with carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) using molecular docking. Among them, diosmetin and luteolin showed the strongest binding affinities and favorable safety profiles. Molecular dynamics simulations of these selected compounds further supported the stability of their interactions with CPT1A. These findings highlight diosmetin and luteolin as promising candidates for further investigation as potential modulators of CPT1A in breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57810-w.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among women worldwide, with an increasing global incidence and significant clinical heterogeneity1,2. It is classified into distinct molecular subtypes, including luminal (ER-positive), HER2-enriched, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), based on gene expression and receptor status. Among these, TNBC represents the most aggressive subtype, characterized by poor prognosis, high metastatic potential, and the absence of effective targeted therapies, thereby necessitating the development of novel and more selective treatment strategies3.

Recent advances in cancer biology have highlighted metabolic reprogramming as a hallmark of tumor progression. In particular, alterations in lipid metabolism, especially fatty acid oxidation (FAO), have gained attention for their role in supporting cancer cell survival, proliferation, and adaptation to metabolic stress4. CPT1A is a rate-limiting enzyme responsible for the transport of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria, where they undergo β-oxidation to generate energy5–7. Overexpression of CPT1A has been reported in several cancer types, including breast cancer, where it contributes to tumor growth, metabolic flexibility, and resistance to stress conditions8,9. Consequently, targeting FAO through inhibition of CPT1A has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy. However, known inhibitors such as etomoxir are associated with toxicity and off-target effects, limiting their clinical applicability10. This emphasizes the need to identify safer and more effective alternative inhibitors.

Natural products derived from medicinal plants have long served as a valuable source of therapeutic agents due to their structural diversity and biological activity. In recent years, there has been growing interest in exploring plant-derived metabolites as potential anticancer compounds with improved safety profiles. The genus Caralluma, belonging to the Apocynaceae family, is well known for its rich phytochemical composition, including flavonoids, phenolics, terpenoids, and glycosides, which exhibit a wide range of pharmacological activities11–13. Among these, Caralluma tuberculata has gained attention due to its traditional medicinal use and reported cytotoxic effects against various cancer cell lines, including breast cancer14,15. Previous studies have identified key bioactive compounds such as luteolin, kaempferol derivatives, and other phenolic constituents using LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis, suggesting their potential role in modulating cancer-related pathways16,17.

Advances in analytical technologies have significantly improved the ability to characterize complex plant metabolomes. Liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC–ESI–MS/MS) provides high sensitivity and selectivity for the identification of small molecules in complex biological matrices18,19. In parallel, computational approaches such as molecular docking and dynamics simulations have become vital tools in modern drug discovery. These techniques enable the prediction of protein–ligand interactions, assessment of binding affinity, and evaluation of structural stability, thereby accelerating the screening of potential therapeutic candidates in a cost-effective method20. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically investigate phytochemicals derived from C. tuberculata in the context of CPT1A inhibition, combining LC–ESI–MS/MS profiling with in vitro cytotoxicity assays and in silico approaches, including molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations.

A total of 60 phytochemicals were screened, among which luteolin and diosmetin demonstrated strong binding affinity toward the target protein. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations confirmed the stability of these protein–ligand complexes, supporting their potential as promising candidates for further development. These findings provide a foundation for the identification of plant derived inhibitors targeting metabolic pathways in breast cancer. However, further experimental validation is required to confirm their therapeutic efficacy and safety.

Materials and methods

Plant extract preparation

C. tuberculata was successfully cultivated from a greenhouse of PMAS Arid Agriculture University Rawalpindi, 46300, Pakistan under conditions simulating its native warm, semi-arid habitat. Daytime temperatures were maintained at 25–28 °C with moderate humidity (60–70% during initial acclimatization) using a digital thermometer hygrometer (Model: HTC-1). The plant was identified by botanist Professor Dr. Zia -ur-Rehman mashwani, Department of Botany, PMAS Arid Agriculture University Rawalpindi, Pakistan and collection was conducted without harming the natural population of the species by following the institutional ethical guidelines. The specimen was deposited in a publicly accessible herbarium Quaid e Azam University, Islamabad, 45320, Pakistan with Voucher Number RAW 103626. Three grams of dried shoot powder were treated with 50% methanol solution to obtain the crude extract. The extraction was carried out for one hour at 4 °C in the dark conditions with mechanical shaking at 1000 rpm (Thermolyne—Nuova II). The resultant mixture was then centrifuged ((Eppendorf 5810R Centrifuge) at 7000 rpm (8500×g) for 20 min at 4 °C to separate cell debris. A two mL aliquot of the supernatant was taken to determine its absorption spectra using UV/VIS spectrophotometry (NanoDrop 2000c Spectrophotometer) and the remaining extract was standardized to − 70 °C until use. To concentrate components, extracts were filtered through a Whatman filter paper: 1004 150 and then concentrated in a rotary evaporator (Büchi R-205V805, Flawil, Switzerland) under vacuum at 45 °C at the Laboratory 49, Department of Chemistry at Cinvestav (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Plant collection and crude extract preparation of Caralluma tuberculata.

High-performance liquid chromatography with diode array detection (HPLC–DAD) analysis of C. tuberculata organic extract

The HPLC analysis of the methanolic extract of C. tuberculata was conducted following the methodology of21 with minor modifications. Organic compounds were separated using a Phenomenex Kinetex XB-C18 100 Å, 150 mm in length × 2.1 mm in diameter at 35 °C using a Vanquish Flex UHPLC system (Thermo Scientific). The mobile phase used 0.1% de formic acid in degasified deionized distilled water (solvent A) and 0.1% de formic acid in 100% methanol (HPLC grade) as solvent B. The separation of the organic compounds was performed using a gradient elution program as follows. The run started at 0 min with 10% of phase B, maintaining this initial condition until 50 min, where phase B reached 50%. The gradient then continued to increase, reaching 100% phase B at 75 min, and this composition was maintained up to 95 min to ensure complete elution of retained compounds. Subsequently, the system was returned to initial conditions by decreasing phase B to 10% at 102 min, and this composition was kept constant until 110 min for column re-equilibration. The flow rate used was 0.6 mL min−1 and the sample volume (120 mL) was injected. A UV–VIS detector was used, scanning from 190 to 800 nm with a bandwidth of 2 nm.

Liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC/ESI–MS/MS) analysis of C. tuberculata methanolic extract

The metabolomic profiling of C. tuberculata methanolic extract was conducted using LC/ESI–MS/MS spectrometry with a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 120, a high-resolution Orbitrap mass spectrometer paired with liquid chromatography for small molecule analysis. The run time was set to 28 min, with the instrument functioning in both positive and negative ion modes. Heated Electrospray Ionization (HESI) was the ion source used, with a spray voltage of 3800 V for positive ions and 3000 Volts for negative ions. The ion transfer tube temperature was set at 350 °C, the vaporizer temperature used was 275 °C, the sheath gas flow was set at 40 arb and the auxiliary gas at 5 arb, while the sweep gas was set at 0 arb. The mass spectrometer has a maximum resolution of 120,000 and a scan range of mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) ratio of 100–1200. The maximum injection duration of 100 ms was the typical AGC target. Dynamic exclusion was defined with a mass tolerance of ± 10 176 ppm and an exclusion time of 30 s. For 30%, 50%, and 70% HCD levels, the collision energy was standardized. EASY-IC was used for lock mass correction during data collecting in profile mode, and mild trapping was used for accurate peak analysis. Finally, the organic compound identification was performed with the Thermo Scientific Compound Discoverer 3.3.

Cell viability MTT assay

For the MTT assay, methanol was completely removed from the extract (described in the rotary evaporation section above) to avoid solvent-related cytotoxicity in cell culture assays. After that, DMSO was used to re-dissolve the extract due to its biocompatibility and non-toxicity to cells. Moreover, DMSO can also help to maintain the chemical integrity of the extract and allows preparation of a stable and homogeneous stock solution22. The 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was used to assess cell viability in this investigation to assess cell viability. MDA-MB-231 and MCF-12F were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), a Global Bioresource Center. The MCF-12F and MDA-MB-231 cell lines were cultured and inoculated into 96-well plates, loading 10,000 cells per well in 100 μL of the respective culture medium. Under standard culture conditions, the cells were subsequently incubated for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were treated with plant extracts at 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 and 160 μg/mL, prepared in 0.5% DMSO. Untreated cells were used as the untreated control, whereas DMSO-treated cells were used as the vehicle (solvent) control. After the incubation period, the media was discarded from the wells, and added with 15 mg/mL MTT in PBS 1 × and incubated for four hours protected from light at 37 °C. Subsequently, Fformazan crystals were dissolved using 100 µL of acidified isopropanol per well and the optical density absorbance (O.D.) at 580 nm determined in an measured using an Epoch 2 microplate reader (BioTek, Agilent Technologies). Statistical analysis of cell proliferation was expressed as percentage of cell viability, calculated as (OD of treated cells/OD of untreated control cells) × 100, and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. Statistical differences among groups were evaluated using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess the effects of treatment and concentration, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post hoc analysis. Dose–response curves were generated using nonlinear regression analysis to determine the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) values.

Protein model analysis

The amino acid sequence of CPT1A was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database under the accession number NP_001427287.1, corresponding to carnitine O-palmitoyltransferase 1, liver isoform 1, from Homo sapiens. The three-dimensional structure of CPT1A was predicted using the AlphaFold Server 323, and the resulting model was saved in PDB format. The stereochemical quality of the predicted structure was evaluated using Ramachandran plot analysis via the Structure Assessment tool of Swiss-Model24. The validated model was subsequently used for molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations. For electrostatic analysis, the PDB file was converted into PQR format using PDB2PQR version 3.6.125. The electrostatic potential was calculated using the Adaptive Poisson–Boltzmann Solver (APBS) software version 3.4.1. Visualization of the protein surface was performed using UCSF Chimera26, with electrostatic potential values ranging from − 5 (red, negative potential) to + 5 (blue, positive potential) kcal/mol.

Molecular docking

The 60 compounds from previous analyses, along with etomoxir as a reference CPT1A inhibitor, were selected for molecular docking. All SDF files for small molecules were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Furthermore, molecular structure optimization for small molecules was done using Avogadro (http://avogadro.cc/). The MMFF94 force field (gMerck Molecular Force Field) was used to optimize geometry and save the molecules in MOL2 format for molecular docking.

The molecular docking was executed using AutoDock Vina 1.1.227, with a grid box centered at the binding-site coordinates. To ensure adequate conformational sampling, the exhaustiveness parameter was set to 64, and up to 10 binding modes were generated for each ligand. Docking poses were subsequently clustered based on structural similarity, and the representative pose from the most populated cluster was selected for further analysis. The results were visualized in PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0, Schrödinger, LLC).

Physicochemical properties of the fourteen Top Binders

The ADMETlab platform (http://admet.scbdd.com/) and open-source tools available at http://www.openmolecules.org were employed for an in silico assessment of physicochemical properties including drug score (DS), drug-likeness (DL), cLogP (lipophilicity), toxicity risk, and TPSA value. The compounds that met the requirements for zero toxicity, acceptable cLogP, MW, TPSA, and drug-likeness among the top 14 receptor binders were luteolin (PubChem CID 5280445) and diosmetin (PubChem CID 5281612), making them the best candidates for CPT1A targeting and used for molecular dynamics simulations.

Molecular dynamics simulations and binding free energy calculations

For molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the selected protein–ligand complexes, GROMACS 4.5.528 and the CHARMM36m force field29 were used. To construct the system, membrane-associated residues were removed, and residues 123–773 of the protein were used for modeling. The system was embedded in a cubic solvation box (95 Å × 95 Å × 95 Å) filled with TIP3P water molecules. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ counterions were incorporated to neutralize the system and attain a physiological ionic strength of 0.15 M. The steepest descent algorithm was employed for energy minimization, subsequently followed by equilibration within the NVT ensemble at 310.15°K. Using a Parrinello-Rahman barostat, the pressure was kept at 1.01325 bar. Trajectory snapshots were saved every 10 ps during 200 ns production MD simulations with a 2 fs integration timestep. A custom Python pipeline (https://github.com/AngelRuizMoreno/Scripts_Notebooks/blob/master/Scripts/plipMD_V3.1.py) that combines the MD Analysis30 and PLIP31 to obtain interaction profiles throughout the MD trajectories was used to analyze ligand–protein interactions. The interaction frequency data generated by this pipeline were subsequently visualized as heat maps using GraphPad Prism 8 software https://graphpad-prism.software.informer.com/8.0/. Finally, the gmmpbsa v1.6 package was used to implement the Molecular Mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area (MM/PBSA) method which provided thorough insights into the stability and energetics of the protein–ligand complexes33,34.

Results and discussion

Screening of phytochemicals from C. tuberculata extract using UV–Visible spectrophotometry

Plant-derived chemicals, such as phenolics, flavonoids, and other secondary metabolites, can be examined using ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy by observing specific absorption patterns34 The absorption within the visible spectrum directly affects the detected color of the involved chemicals. In the present study, a considerable absorbance was observed in the UV region between 200 and 250 nm in C. tuberculata, indicating the presence of aromatic compounds, flavonoids, phenolics, and other conjugated systems Further, the absorbance gradually drops from 250 to 300 nm, highlighting the existence of unsaturated and aromatic bioactive metabolites, while the absorbance values are nearly zero after 300 nm (Fig. 2). The relative absorption spectra and wavelength regions may provide important insights into the presence of secondary metabolites in C. tuberculata35Similarly, the existence of significant phytoconstituents was also documented in Caralluma edulis using the same technique36. However, for precise identification of compounds in C. tuberculata, we employed advanced analytical techniques, including HPLC and LC-ESI MS/MS.Fig. 2UV–visible spectrum of C. tuberculata extract.

Screening of phytochemicals from C. tuberculata extract using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

HPLC is a versatile method capable of analyzing biological samples for the isolation and identification of compounds present in a mixture37. Therefore, in this study, the extract of C. tuberculata was analyzed using HPLC, and several peaks were observed in the chromatogram, corresponding to various bioactive metabolites. Based on their retention time, peaks observed between 0 and 30 min represented polar compounds due to their high solubility in mobile phase. However, fewer peaks were observed between 10 and 40 min and moderately polar compounds were observed at the mid-range of 30 to 60 min. On the other hand, presence of secondary metabolites beyond 60 min may indicated non-polar compounds, which exhibit strong interaction with the stationary phase. The retention period and peak intensities suggest a diverse set of phytoconstituents and differential affinities to the stationary phase38. According to39, early eluting peaks within 5 min reveal the presence of highly polar compounds, due to hydrophilic nature. Similar reports were also observed in another species of genus Caralluma (C. fimbriata, C. umbellate, and C. attentuata, C. europaea and C. tuberculata) by using HPLC technique16,40. These bioactive metabolites in C. tuberculata received significant attention due to their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anticancer, and antidiabetic properties and may have potential applications in pharmacology41.

In addition, heat maps are also a prominent method of visualization in the metabolomics analysis, utilizing color intensity to display the relative abundance ion in each sample42. This study presents heat map analysis illustrating the metabolic profiling of C. tuberculata over time. This approach provides a comprehensive overview of compound distribution, suggesting that multiple metabolites elute at overlapping retention times. The depth and richness of the bioactive metabolites in the extract were assessed using broad spectrum of color. While Blue regions indicate low intensities and black regions suggest the absence or very low presence of compounds (Fig. 3). Several reports have documented the use of heat maps for visualization and classification of secondary metabolites in various plants including Bambusoideae, Acer negundo, Panax ginseng, and P. quinquefolius43–45,. In accordance with our current findings, this study provided a graphical representation of the main compounds in the sample and may serve as a potential source biomedical applications. However, LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis is required to individually identify each compound that may be utilized in the development of herbal drugs for breast cancer disease.Fig. 3High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) of C. tuberculata organic extract. Compound identifications of C. tuberculata through LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis

LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis of C. tuberculata extract enabled the putative identification of 60 metabolites belonging to diverse chemical classes, including flavonoids, phenolic acids, amino acids, fatty acids, alkaloids, and related secondary metabolites (Table S1; Fig. 4; Figure S1). Metabolite annotation was performed using retention time, mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and spectral database matching. Among the detected metabolites, flavonoids represented a major compound class, including luteolin, diosmetin, and kaempferol derivatives. Several phenolic acids, fatty acids, amino acid derivatives, and indole-related compounds were also identified. Previous studies have reported biological activities for many of these metabolite classes, including antioxidant and antiproliferative properties46–51. However, the present study does not establish direct biological contributions of individual metabolites to the observed cytotoxic effects. The coexistence of multiple phytochemical classes indicates substantial chemical diversity within the extract and may contribute collectively to the observed biological activity. Nevertheless, because the biological assays were conducted using a crude extract, the contribution of specific metabolites or potential synergistic interactions remains unresolved. Importantly, the current metabolomic analysis provides qualitative and putative metabolite identification only. Although high-resolution MS and spectral matching support reliable annotation, no authentic reference standards or quantitative validation methods were employed. Therefore, metabolite identities and relative abundances should be interpreted cautiously. Further targeted metabolomic analyses, compound isolation, and bioactivity-guided fractionation studies are necessary to confirm compound identities and determine the biological relevance of individual constituents.Fig. 4LCMS analysis of C. tuberculata organic extract.

Cell survivability (%) of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-12F cell line following exposure to C. tuberculata extracts

Cancer remains a major global health challenge, prompting continued interest in the identification of biologically active compounds from natural sources. Medicinal plants have long been investigated as potential sources of pharmacologically relevant molecules, including compounds with antiproliferative properties. Species of the genus Caralluma have attracted scientific attention because of their diverse phytochemical composition and reported biological activities in traditional medicine13. In the present study, treatment with C. tuberculata extract reduced cell viability in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-12F cell lines in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5a, b). The extract demonstrated greater cytotoxic effects in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells compared with the non-tumorigenic MCF-12F cell line. In MDA-MB-231 cells, viability decreased from approximately 100% in the untreated control group to below 20% at the highest tested concentration (160 µg/mL). In contrast, MCF-12F cells retained comparatively higher viability under similar treatment conditions. Dose–response analysis yielded IC₅₀ values of 19.24 µg/mL for MDA-MB-231 cells and 63.87 µg/mL for MCF-12F cells, indicating differential sensitivity between the tested cell lines. Although these findings suggest that the extract exerts stronger cytotoxic effects on the cancer cell model under the experimental conditions used, the current results should be interpreted cautiously because the biological evaluation was limited to a single breast cancer cell line and one non-tumorigenic cell line. Therefore, the present findings provide only preliminary evidence of differential cytotoxic activity and are insufficient to establish broad selectivity toward breast cancer cells. Furthermore, the present study evaluated a crude plant extract rather than isolated purified compounds. Consequently, the contribution of individual metabolites, including luteolin, diosmetin, or other identified phytochemicals, to the observed cytotoxic effects cannot be directly established. The biological activity observed may result from the combined or synergistic effects of multiple constituents present within the extract. Previous studies involving Caralluma species have similarly reported antiproliferative effects in different cancer cell models. For example, hydroethanolic extracts of C. europaea and its fractions demonstrated cytotoxic activity against MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 breast cancer cell lines16. Likewise, extracts of Caralluma acutangula exhibited antiproliferative activity against MCF-7 and HepG2 cells within tested concentration ranges52. Such findings collectively support the potential biological relevance of phytochemicals present in Caralluma species. While the LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis identified multiple metabolites previously associated with antiproliferative or antioxidant activities in the literature, the present study does not establish direct causal relationships between specific compounds and the observed cellular responses. Identification of active site and electrostatic potential of CPT1A

Carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) is a mitochondrial outer membrane enzyme involved in the transport of long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix for β-oxidation. CPT1A is a transmembrane protein composed of 773 amino acids, including two transmembrane segments (TM1: 48–73; TM2: 103–122), a short N-terminal regulatory domain (residues 1–47), and a large cytoplasmic catalytic domain (residues 123–773) containing the putative substrate-binding regions and catalytic residues53. The conformational properties of the N-terminal region have been associated with regulation by malonyl-CoA and modulation of enzyme activity. The electrostatic potential distribution of CPT1A was visualized to explore the surface charge characteristics of the modeled protein structure and to identify regions that may potentially contribute to ligand interaction. Surface electrostatic mapping provides structural information regarding the distribution of charged residues that may influence molecular recognition and docking orientation.

The electrostatic surface was visualized using a charge gradient ranging from negative (red) to positive (blue) potential values. Different rotational orientations of CPT1A demonstrated heterogeneous surface charge distribution across the catalytic domain and surrounding regions. Positively charged surface regions may contribute to interactions with negatively polarized ligand moieties, whereas negatively charged regions may facilitate polar or hydrogen-bonding interactions.

Although electrostatic mapping may help rationalize predicted docking orientations, these observations remain descriptive and computational in nature. The present analysis does not establish catalytic inhibition, binding specificity, or functional modulation of CPT1A. Therefore, the electrostatic visualization should be interpreted only as supportive structural information complementary to the molecular docking analysis. Additionally, the protein structure used in this study was derived from a predictive model, and membrane-associated regions were not included in the simulations, which may influence ligand interactions under physiological conditions54,55 (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Comparison of the Surface electrostatic potential of CPT1A. Virtual screening against CPT1A enzymes section

Molecular docking has emerged as a key technique in modern drug development, enabling scientists to precisely forecast how tiny compounds will connect to their target proteins56. This computational method offers a quick and economical way to search through large libraries of natural substances in search of safer and more effective treatments for breast cancer. For breast cancer, targeting Carnitine Palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), an essential modulator of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation, is a viable treatment approach. CPT1A facilitates the movement of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria, which helps cancer cells that are growing meet their higher energy needs. When this enzyme is inhibited, metabolic pathways may be disturbed, which could inhibit the growth and progression of tumors57. In order to target the CPT1A protein, a set of 60 bioactive chemicals that were identified in C. tuberculata underwent molecular docking study. The strongest affinity binding compounds were subsequently validated using high-resolution ESI–MS/MS extracted chromatogram analysis, as shown in Fig. 7. Leading candidates among the screened compounds were found to be diosmetin (PubChem CID: 5281612) and luteolin (PubChem CID: 5280445). Clustering analysis of docking poses revealed that the top-ranked binding modes of diosmetin and luteolin correspond to the most populated clusters. Similar clustering trends were observed across the screened compounds and are summarized in Table S2, while representative clustering matrices for selected compounds are shown in Figure S2. To provide a reference framework for interpreting docking scores, etomoxir, a known CPT1A inhibitor, was included in the docking analysis. The predicted binding affinity of etomoxir was − 6.7 kcal/mol, compared with − 8.9 and − 8.7 kcal/mol for diosmetin and luteolin, respectively. These results indicate that the phytochemicals exhibit more favorable predicted binding energies. Notably, all compounds bind within the same catalytic pocket, supporting the consistency of the predicted binding modes and their potential interaction with CPT1A. Consistent with earlier studies highlighting the affinity of natural flavonoids for this target in breast cancer situations, The docking analysis predicted favorable binding interactions between selected flavonoids and the CPT1A binding pocket58. Previous studies have reported that flavonoids such as diosmetin and luteolin may influence metabolic pathways associated with cancer progression, including pathways related to fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and cellular energy metabolism. In the present study, in silico analyses predicted favorable interactions of diosmetin and luteolin with CPT1A, a key enzyme involved in FAO. However, these observations are based solely on computational predictions and were not experimentally validated through functional or mechanistic assays. Therefore, any potential relationship between the identified compounds, CPT1A modulation, and altered ATP synthesis remains hypothetical and requires further investigation. Previous research has shown that diosmetin and luteolin exhibit anticancer properties in MDA-MB-231 cells, which are models of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). These natural bioactive metabolites exhibit a number of modes of action, such as apoptosis, mitochondrial malfunction, decreased cell viability, increased LDH release, blockage of phosphorylation pathways, activation of S-phase and G0/G1-phase cell cycle arrest, and more59–61. These findings suggest that targeting CPT1A-mediated fatty acid oxidation may represent a potential therapeutic strategy for metastatic TNBC to block FAO by targeting CPT1A. The potential of flavonoids produced from C. tuberculata as novel anticancer drugs is highlighted by their considerable inhibitory activity and harmless nature. Confirmation of their mechanistic functions requires additional clinical and experimental validations. Diosmetin and luteolin are thought to inhibit CPT1A, which may upset redox equilibrium and encourage apoptosis by altering mitochondrial membrane potential and raising intracellular ROS levels62. This suggests a sophisticated anti-metastatic strategy that involves reprogramming lipid metabolism and regulating oxidative stress, providing fresh insights into plant-based treatments for breast cancer. Both diosmetin and luteolin share the characteristic flavone scaffold formed by a fused bicyclic ring system and a distal aromatic ring. Their structures differ only in the methoxy or hydroxyl substituents on the distal ring. Our docking results indicate that both flavonoids display strong binding affinities for the CPT1A active site pocket by burying the distal aromatic ring while exposing the fused bicyclic system to the solvent (Fig. 8A,B). Interestingly, both diosmetin and luteolin form a pi–pi stacking interaction with Phe712 at the entrance of the pocket. They also establish hydrogen bonds with the side chain of His473 and the backbone of Asp477 and Ala591, covering the entire CPT1A pocket. In addition, several hydrophobic contacts with Tyr589, Asp477, Pro479, and Thr602 further stabilize their binding modes (Fig. 8C,D). Notably, diosmetin forms two additional hydrogen bonds, one with Ser685 at the entrance of the pocket and another with Met593 at the bottom (Fig. 8C). Structural comparison revealed that etomoxir occupies the same catalytic pocket and interacts with key residues involved in CPT1A activity, including His473 and Ala478. Notably, diosmetin and luteolin exhibited similar interaction patterns, reinforcing the consistency of the docking results and supporting the validity of the predicted binding modes. Finally, both flavonoids bind within the same pocket used by palmitoyl-CoA, the natural substrate of CPT1A. Notably, diosmetin and luteolin occupy only part of this pocket, whereas palmitoyl-CoA spans the entire binding cavity (Fig. 8E). The structure of CPT1A has been described as a membrane-associated enzyme that is crucial for the metabolism of long-chain fatty acids, with two transmembrane segments and distinct N- and C-terminal domains63. The functions of this enzyme depend on its active center, which is composed of residues His473 and the amino acid segment 589–602. This region facilitates the binding of CoA and carnitine and supports the transport of fatty acids into mitochondria. Our computational analysis suggests that both compounds may occupy regions associated with the CPT1A catalytic pocket by interacting with His473 and other essential amino acids that are involved during enzymatic activity. However, additional interactions that diosmetin showed with Thr602 and Ser685 may have improved its binding stability and may contribute to differences in predicted binding stability. Etomoxir was included only as a reference compound to contextualize docking poses and predicted binding energies. Although etomoxir has been associated with substantial harmful side effects, these naturally occurring flavonoids may represent potentially safer and potential candidates for future experimental investigation64. The pharmacokinetic characteristics of natural substances are essential to guarantee that the compounds not only show the ability to bind to the active site of the enzyme but also exhibit drug-like behavior65. They support the verification of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, and toxicity (ADME-Tox). Diosmetin and luteolin’s pharmacokinetics were analyzed in silico, and the results showed that they adhered to Lipinski’s rule of five, a crucial standard for determining drug-likeness (Table 2). Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed that diosmetin and luteolin did not provide any toxicity hazards, which is a crucial requirement for medication safety. Their ideal range of lipophilicity (cLogP) values of 2.47 and 1.86 suggests a well-balanced hydrophilic-lipophilic profile that promotes membrane permeability. With moderate water solubility as a drug-like entity, the molecule is deemed suitable for oral administration based on its solubility (logS) values of –2.87 and –3.82, as well as its drug-likeness score of 2.07 and 1.9 respectively. Below the crucial threshold of 140 Å2, the topological polar surface area (TPSA) values of 100.13 Å2 and 111.13 Å2 indicate excellent properties for oral bioavailability and cell membrane permeability. A thorough investigation of luteolin’s pharmacokinetic characteristics as an anticancer medication using an in silico method has been proposed as a promising therapeutic candidate in accordance with Lipinski’s rule of five (77; 78). Diosmetin and luteolin are attractive natural candidates for further research as therapeutic agents due to their interaction with CPT1A and meet important pharmacokinetic requirements. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are an efficient way to examine the stability and dynamic behavior of protein–ligand complexes across time. They offer vital details regarding molecular flexibility, conformational changes, and binding stability during ligand-target protein interactions66. The simulations improve the precision of drug design by identifying structural gaps that can be filled to produce more effective inhibitors and by enabling a more accurate evaluation of binding affinities. MD simulations were performed to evaluate the stability of the docking-derived complexes and to refine the predicted binding modes. The structural stability of the protein–ligand complexes was assessed by monitoring the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the protein backbone and ligand throughout the 200 ns simulations. Diosmetin showed an RMSD of 1.44 土 0.35 Å, indicating high stability throughout the simulation. In particular, minimal conformational deviations were observed during the second half of the simulation (Fig. 9A). On the other hand, luteolin showed an RMSD of 2.38 ± 0.40 Å, indicating a rearranging on its binding mode. However, the new conformation remained stable throughout the simulation (Fig. 9B). Interestingly, the methoxy group of diosmetin appears to contribute to ligand stabilization compared with the hydroxyl group of luteolin, which lacks this effect. In both complexes, CPT1A remained stable, with RMSD values of 1.97 ± 0.30 Å for CPT1A–diosmetin and 1.88 ± 0.36 Å for CPT1A–luteolin (Fig. 9A,B). Diosmetin showed higher stability in its interaction with CPT1A than luteolin, suggesting a comparatively stable predicted interaction during the simulation period. As a lead drug for targeting CPT1A in metabolic or therapeutic pathways, the results justify future experimental investigation. Similar stabilization patterns have been reported for flavonoids interacting with other protein targets. For example, luteolin has been shown to form stable complexes with MDM2 during MD simulations through hydrophobic interactions67. Likewise, diosmetin has demonstrated stable binding to CDKN2, driven by hydrogen bonding mediated by its hydroxyl groups68. In addition, the CPT1A–etomoxir complex exhibited an etomoxir RMSD of 2.7 ± 0.54 Å, indicating higher instability during the first 150 ns of the simulation, followed by stabilization at the end of the simulation. To assess individual amino acid contributions to binding, per-residue energy decomposition was performed. Phe712 emerged as the main contributor to the binding of both diosmetin and luteolin, in agreement with the docking results. For diosmetin, additional favorable contributions were observed from Asp477, Pro479, and Ala591, which further corroborate the docking predictions. Moreover, Glu603, Met648, and Ser687 also contributed favorably to diosmetin binding. In contrast, Arg595 showed the strongest unfavorable contribution, whereas Gln688 and Thr689 displayed weaker unfavorable effects (Fig. 9E).

For luteolin, Tyr589 contributed favorably, as predicted by docking, followed by additional favorable contributions from Gln688, Thr689, and Pro690. Conversely, His473 and Ser685 showed unfavorable contributions to luteolin binding (Fig. 9E). Notably, residues Gln688 and Thr689 displayed opposite energetic contributions for diosmetin and luteolin, indicating that subtle structural differences, such as methoxy versus hydroxyl substitution, significantly influence CPT1A binding affinities. Overall, these correlations suggest that natural flavonoids may potentially interact with residues associated with the CPT1A binding pocket by interfering with the hydrophobic pocket in a manner analogous to inactivating mutations71.

Finally, intermolecular interaction analysis revealed that Ala478, Ala591, Thr602, and Phe712 maintain high-frequency hydrophobic contacts with both diosmetin and luteolin, while Ser687 and Gln688 form persistent hydrogen bonds. In contrast, His473, Tyr589, and Gly713 played a key role in stabilizing the luteolin binding mode but not that of diosmetin, indicating that ligands with high structural similarity can adopt distinct binding modes within the CPT1A pocket (Fig. 9F).

Importantly, the present study does not provide direct experimental evidence of CPT1A inhibition or modulation. The proposed association between the identified phytochemicals and CPT1A is based exclusively on computational approaches, including molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations, and MM/PBSA calculations, which provide predictive models of potential protein–ligand interactions rather than direct evidence of biological inhibition or mechanistic causality. No enzymatic assays, fatty acid oxidation measurements, gene or protein expression analyses, mitochondrial functional assays, or metabolic validation experiments were performed. Furthermore, the CPT1A structure used in this study was derived from an AlphaFold predictive model rather than an experimentally resolved crystal structure, and membrane-associated regions were not included in the simulations, which may influence ligand binding under physiological conditions. Therefore, the involvement of CPT1A in the observed cytotoxic activity should be considered hypothetical and preliminary. The present findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating observations that require further validation through enzymatic assays, metabolic analyses, CPT1A expression studies, mitochondrial functional assays, and fatty acid oxidation experiments to determine the biological relevance of the predicted interactions under physiological conditions.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated metabolomic, cytotoxic, and computational investigation of C. tuberculata. LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis revealed a diverse phytochemical composition with potential biological relevance. The extract showed selective cytotoxic effects against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells compared with non-tumorigenic MCF-12F cells, indicating the presence of compounds that may contribute to anticancer activity. Computational analyses suggested that diosmetin and luteolin could potentially interact with CPT1A and exhibited stable binding behavior during molecular dynamics simulations. However, the present findings do not establish direct CPT1A inhibition or mechanistic involvement in the observed cytotoxic effects, as no functional validation experiments were performed. In addition, the biological evaluation was limited to one cancer cell line and one non-tumorigenic cell line using a crude extract; therefore, the observed cytotoxic effects should be considered preliminary. Accordingly, the computational observations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Additional biochemical, molecular, metabolic, and multi-cell-line validation studies are required to determine the biological relevance of CPT1A in relation to the identified compounds and the observed cellular responses.
