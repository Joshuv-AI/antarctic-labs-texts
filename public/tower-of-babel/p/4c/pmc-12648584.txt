The impact of comprehensive pharmaceutical care on medication safety in gynecologic oncology chemotherapy patients
Authors: Zhiqi Zhang, Zhuo Zhang, Qianxin Liu, Yan Zhang, Peng Jia, Lan Mi, Rui Lian, Fei Chen, Xiaocong Pang, Ying Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo evaluate the impact of our hospital's full-course pharmacy service on the safety and rationality of medication in patients with gynecologic malignancies undergoing chemotherapy.MethodsA retrospective cohort analysis was conducted on patients who underwent chemotherapy in the Gynecologic Oncology Ward at Peking University First Hospital between September 2022 and September 2024. Patients were divided into a control group, who received routine pharmaceutical care based on clinical medication prescription review, and the intervention group, who received comprehensive pharmaceutical care throughout their treatment. This included prescription review, medication education, drug consultation, and adverse reaction management. The incidence of severe adverse reactions, the appropriateness of leukopoietic treatment and monitoring, and the rate of unplanned hospital admissions were compared between the two groups.ResultsThe intervention group demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of severe nausea (6.1 % vs 11.4 %, P = 0.034), anorexia (3.3 % vs 9.3 %, P = 0.003), and leukopenia (9.4 % vs 15.7 %, P = 0.034) compared to the control group. Additionally, the appropriateness of leukopoietic treatment (93.5 % vs 85.0 %, P = 0.001) and monitoring (89.2 % vs 65.0 %, P < 0.001), as well as the rate of unexpected hospital admissions (2.2 % vs 6.4 %, P = 0.013), have shown significant improvement in the intervention group.ConclusionComprehensive pharmaceutical care has a positive impact on medication safety for gynecologic oncology patients, significantly reducing the incidence of severe adverse reactions and the rate of unplanned hospital admissions.

Introduction

Gynecologic malignancies seriously threaten women's health. In 2020, cervical, endometrial, and ovarian cancers were all among the top ten most commonly diagnosed cancers in women.1 As a primary treatment option, antineoplastic drugs help control tumour growth and metastasis, playing a crucial role in comprehensive management.2 However, their high toxicity and variable therapeutic effects raise concerns about drug safety. Adverse reactions may cause severe organ damage, long-term,3,4 or even permanent harm.5 They can also reduce treatment adherence6 and quality of life,3,7,8 sometimes leading to dose reduction or treatment discontinuation, which compromises outcomes. Therefore, effective monitoring and management of these side effects are essential.

The Hematology/Oncology Pharmacy Association (HOPA) recognizes oncology pharmacists as essential members of multidisciplinary teams, given their expertise in antineoplastic therapy and side-effect management.9 Studies confirm that these pharmacists improve medication safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness through interventions like patient education and prescription review.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 However, most research has not detailed the specific services provided or patient types involved. For example, only a few studies explore their role in managing post-chemotherapy neutropenia or adjusting treatments.15,16 Data is even scarcer on areas like the primary prevention use of bone marrow growth factors. Furthermore, existing studies predominantly focus on hematologic, genitourinary, and lung cancers,12, 13, 14 with very few addressing the distinct field of gynecologic oncology.17,18

Gynecologic oncology involves complex treatments that demand specialized pharmaceutical care. For cancers like ovarian and cervical cancer, regimens often combine extensive surgery, highly emetogenic chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapies.19 This complexity creates a high-risk medication environment, requiring pharmacists' expertise in managing drug interactions, dosing, and scheduling. Beyond these general challenges, gynecologic care carries unique, gender-specific burdens that profoundly affect patients' quality of life and psychosocial well-being.20 Treatment can impair reproductive function, requiring fertility discussions with young patients. It may also alter body image, induce premature menopause, and cause sexual dysfunction—creating distinct supportive care needs less common in other cancers. This complex interplay between aggressive disease management and its profound effects on female identity underscores the critical need for specialized, multidisciplinary support.

Within this context, the oncology pharmacist's role in ensuring medication safety is crucial. This study examines their direct interventions for key adverse reactions: proactively managing nausea/vomiting and myelosuppression, and ensuring proper use of growth factors. By adhering to supportive care guidelines, pharmacists help prevent treatment delays and unplanned hospitalizations, thereby securing therapy continuity. However, existing research on pharmacy services in gynecologic oncology remains limited and has primarily assessed outcomes such as medical costs, outpatient rates, or drug reconciliation. A significant evidence gap persists in quantifying how comprehensive pharmacist interventions prevent direct medication harm and improve safe medication use. This gap is critical because severe side effects may cause patients to delay or refuse chemotherapy, directly compromising treatment success.

In this study, comprehensive pharmaceutical care refers to a patient-centered model providing proactive, continuous support throughout chemotherapy, including during hospitalization and after discharge. This involves proactive management of adverse reactions, structured pre-chemotherapy education, and sustained post-discharge follow-up. In contrast, routine pharmaceutical care are defined as essential, reactive tasks like drug dispensing, basic prescription review, and responding to drug information requests, without structured clinical management.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of comprehensive pharmaceutical care on key medication safety outcomes in patients with gynecologic oncology who receive chemotherapy. By focusing on adverse reactions particularly relevant to this population and their treatment journey, we seek to fill a critical evidence gap and highlight the indispensable, specialized role of pharmacists in empowering women through their cancer care.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The adverse reaction data were collected from medication therapy follow-up records within our hospital's gynecologic oncology pharmacy service. At the start of chemotherapy, each patient received a structured ADR diary and was educated on how to record the type, onset, and duration of any adverse events. Clinical pharmacists then conducted proactive, structured follow-ups during each treatment cycle. In these sessions, they reviewed the diary with the patient, clinically assessed the severity of all reported ADRs, and documented their evaluations. Laboratory parameters, such as hematologic values, were cross-verified against the Electronic Medical Record (EMR). Similarly, the use of supportive medications was confirmed via discharge prescriptions or outpatient records in the EMR and then verified with patients during follow-up. Data were collected from September 2022 to September 2024. Due to the nature of the clinical intervention, blinding of patients, pharmacists, physicians, or outcome assessors was not feasible. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (Ethics Approval Number: 2024Y511–002).

Intervention measures

The control group received routine pharmaceutical care, which consisted of a chemotherapy order review by an oncology pharmacist to ensure regimen consistency with the intervention group. No further interventions were provided.

The intervention group received this routine pharmaceutical care plus comprehensive pharmaceutical care. For patients in their first cycle, pharmacists provided education on potential adverse reactions and their management. This included instructions on using antiemetics, scheduling blood tests, and administering growth factors like granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSFs). A QR code was also provided for remote pharmacist consultation. For patients in subsequent cycles, oncology pharmacists followed up on adverse reactions from the previous cycle, recording the duration of occurrence and assessing the severity of adverse reactions, which were reported to the physician. Severe, manageable adverse reactions and cases of myelosuppression were given focused management, with targeted recommendations for intervention. Additionally, further patient education was provided.

Data quality and handling of missing data

To ensure data integrity, all patient records were rigorously reviewed for completeness and consistency. Records with missing key outcome data were excluded from the analysis. Specifically, one patient from the intervention group was excluded due to incomplete follow-up diary entries regarding laboratory monitoring, which precluded the assessment of myelosuppression. No other exclusions were made in either group due to missing data or inconsistencies between the EMR and patient-reported information.

Observation indicators and methods

Incidence of severe adverse reactions

We primarily observed common adverse reactions in patients, including gastrointestinal reactions (e.g., nausea, vomiting, acid reflux, anorexia, diarrhoea, and constipation), neurotoxicity, pain, fatigue, and myelosuppression. Adverse reactions were evaluated strictly by clinical pharmacists in accordance with version 5.0 of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). Among these, neurotoxicity graded as 2–3 was classified as a severe adverse reaction. For other types of adverse reactions, severity was graded on a scale from 1 to 4: grades 1–2 were classified as mild adverse reactions, while grades 3–4 were classified as severe adverse reactions.

To ensure consistency and accuracy in CTCAE grading across different clinical pharmacists, regular inter-rater reliability checks were conducted. This involved periodic joint assessments of sample cases and discussions to reach a consensus on ambiguous gradings. Additionally, all pharmacists involved in the study received standardized training on the application of CTCAE v5.0 criteria prior to the commencement of data collection.

Rational use of G-CSF

The rational use of G-CSF was assessed based on three indicators: appropriate monitoring of complete blood counts (CBC), rate of appropriate indications for G-CSF use, and the rate of appropriate dosing and administration of G-CSF.

Appropriate CBC monitoring: Patients should undergo CBC tests 1–2 times per week to monitor white blood cell and neutrophil levels.21

Appropriate indications for G-CSF use: According to the NCCN Guidelines,22 appropriate indications for G-CSF include:1.Prophylaxis for patients receiving chemotherapy regimens with a high risk of febrile neutropenia (FN).2.Prophylaxis for patients receiving chemotherapy regimens with an intermediate risk of FN, accompanied by patient-specific risk factors.3.Prophylaxis or treatment for patients who have previously experienced FN or dose-limiting neutropenia.

Unplanned hospital admissions

Unplanned hospital admissions refer to instances where patients are admitted during chemotherapy for reasons unrelated to chemotherapy, medical exams, or surgery. The frequency and causes of unplanned admissions were recorded for each patient.

Data analysis methods

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed in R25 to balance the baseline characteristics between the intervention and control groups. The propensity score was estimated using group assignment as the dependent variable and pre-specified covariates: age, tumour type (ovarian, cervical, endometrial), tumour staging according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) system, chemotherapy cycle number, and chemotherapy regimen, with categories of taxane-based, anthracycline-based, platinum-based, or other regimens.

The initial patient cohort exhibited a sample size ratio of approximately 1:5 between the control (n = 164) and intervention (n = 741) groups. To respect this inherent data structure and maximize the use of available data, we pre-specified a 1:5 matching ratio for the propensity score analysis. This strategy aimed to enhance statistical power and precision while maintaining inter-group balance. Matching was performed using the nearest neighbor method with a caliper width of 0.02 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. The balance of covariates between the matched groups was assessed using the standardized mean differences (SMD). An SMD of less than 0.1 was considered to indicate a good balance. To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted post-hoc sensitivity analyses using matching ratios of 1:2 and 1:3 with an identical caliper width.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Quantitative data accords with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and was analyzed using independent sample t-test; quantitative data not accords with the normal distribution was expressed by median (interquartile range) and was analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Qualitative data were expressed as percentage, and were compared using the chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher's exact test as appropriate. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic data

A total of 1053 adverse reaction monitoring records were collected from the medication therapy follow-up database between September 2022 and September 2024. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 905 records were eligible for analysis. Comprehensive pharmaceutical care was implemented starting in October 2022. All records from September 2022 constituted the control group (n = 164). Records from October 2022 to September 2024 (the full operational phase of comprehensive pharmaceutical care services) formed the intervention group (n = 741). Unplanned hospitalizations

Unplanned hospitalizations were significantly less frequent in the intervention group (2.2 %, 11/489) than in the control group (6.4 %, 9/140) (OR 0.335, 95 %CI 0.136–0.825, P = 0.013). Control group causes: grade 4 myelosuppression (n = 3), abdominal pain (n = 2), and one case each of acute conjunctivitis, abdominal distension with intestinal obstruction, fever, and COVID-19. Intervention group causes: grade 4 myelosuppression (n = 2), abdominal pain (n = 2), and one case each of intestinal obstruction, chest pain, pulmonary embolism, vomiting, urinary tract infection, pulmonary inflammation, and severe hypotension.

Discussion

Gynecologic malignancies pose a significant threat to women's health. Although systemic therapies used in their treatment are effective, they often cause considerable adverse effects26.27 In this context, oncology pharmacists have become essential members of the multidisciplinary team. Studies have confirmed that oncology pharmacy services improve medication safety,28, 29, 30, 31 reduce the incidence of adverse reactions associated with antitumor therapy,32, 33, 34 enhance the efficacy of primary or adjuvant treatments,16,31,35, 36, 37 and promote rational drug use.38, 39, 40 Our findings further demonstrate that comprehensive pharmacy services can significantly improve medication safety indicators in gynecologic oncology patients receiving chemotherapy. We observed a reduction in the incidence of severe nausea, anorexia, and leukopenia, more rational use of G-CSF, and fewer unplanned hospitalizations.

Management of adverse reactions

Our results confirm that pharmacist-led interventions can significantly mitigate chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting.41, 42, 43 In our study, these structured interventions, which were grounded in guideline-based risk assessment and patient education, successfully reduced the incidence of severe nausea and controlled the rate of severe vomiting at 0.2 %. This finding is consistent with the established efficacy of such pharmacist-led models.44,45 The clinical significance of these reductions extends beyond mere symptom control. Effective management of nausea and vomiting is directly linked to improved nutritional intake, reduced dehydration risk, and better maintenance of physical strength and daily functioning, thereby substantially preserving patients' quality of life during treatment. Similarly, appetite loss is a common challenge during cancer therapy46, and its management often involves addressing contributing factors like nausea,47,48 this finding underscores the value of a holistic approach. Alleviating anorexia can help prevent cancer-related cachexia and support continued treatment tolerance. Furthermore, from an economic perspective, preventing these acute symptoms may reduce the need for rescue medications, unscheduled clinic visits, and potential hospitalizations, translating into tangible cost savings for the healthcare system.

In contrast, the overall incidence of severe acid reflux, diarrhoea, constipation, and stomatitis was low, with no significant differences between groups. Pharmacist interventions for chemotherapy-induced neurotoxicity were limited to lifestyle guidance and symptom follow-up, as this is a cumulative toxicity with no effective preventive medications.49 For worsening symptoms, pharmacists collaborated with physicians to discuss chemotherapy delays or dose reductions. Despite the high incidence of peripheral neuropathy, no differences were observed between groups, highlighting the need for future research into novel preventive and treatment strategies.

Bone marrow suppression

Chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression is a common dose-limiting toxicity, with neutropenia posing a risk for serious infections and treatment delays.50, 51, 52 Despite established guidelines, the real-world use of G-CSF is often characterized by underuse in high-risk settings and overuse in low-risk scenarios.53, 54, 55, 56, 57 In this study, pharmacist interventions directly addressed these challenges. Through risk assessment, patient education, and follow-up evaluation, the intervention significantly improved adherence to G-CSF guidelines, enhanced the rationality of G-CSF use and blood count monitoring, and reduced the incidence of severe leukopenia. No significant differences were observed between the two groups in the incidence of severe neutropenia, anaemia, or thrombocytopenia.

Beyond these immediate hematologic parameters, we interpret these findings as having critical implications for long-term hematologic recovery and treatment integrity. By ensuring appropriate primary and secondary G-CSF prophylaxis and reinforcing the importance of blood count monitoring, our intervention helps maintain chemotherapy dose intensity and minimizes treatment delays. This consistent management helps maintain chemotherapy dose intensity, minimizes treatment delays, and may reduce the risk of cumulative hematologic damage. Consequently, although the reduction in febrile neutropenia was not statistically significant in this cohort, our intervention is positioned to lower the incidence of this serious secondary complication.

Interpretation of non-significant findings and study limitations

Our study did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in several other adverse events, including neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, chemotherapy-induced peripheral neurotoxicity (CIPN), and gastrointestinal disturbances such as diarrhoea and constipation. Several factors may explain these findings. First, the pharmaceutical intervention was strategically prioritized to target the most common and acutely symptomatic toxicities (e.g., nausea and vomiting) that most significantly impact quality of life and treatment adherence. Second, the pathophysiology of certain toxicities like CIPN is complex and less amenable to preventive strategies alone, often requiring dose modification rather than supportive care. Finally, while the sample size was sufficient to detect differences in primary outcomes, it may have been underpowered to detect smaller-magnitude effects in less frequent events. These findings suggest that while comprehensive pharmaceutical care is highly effective for managing specific toxicities, its benefits may not extend equally to all chemotherapy-related adverse events.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and observational design cannot establish causality between the intervention and the outcomes. Although we saw improvements, they may be partly due to unmeasured factors. Second, documentation bias is possible. Adverse events might have been recorded differently in patient diaries compared to pharmacist reports, leading to inconsistent data. Third, the pharmacists were not blinded to the intervention. This may have biased their assessment of subjective symptoms like nausea and fatigue. Future trials could use blinded clinicians to independently evaluate these endpoints. Fourth, the sample size was limited by the available retrospective data, which was too small to reliably detect rare side effects or subtle differences. Future prospective, multi-center studies with a pre-specified power analysis are needed to confirm our findings and enhance their generalizability. Furthermore, such trials present a valuable opportunity to incorporate formal cost-effectiveness analyses, which would be crucial for justifying the broader implementation of this pharmaceutical care model. It is also relevant to consider the potential influence of the Hawthorne effect in this study. Patients in the intervention group received increased attention through pharmacist follow-up, which may have affected their reporting of symptoms. Nonetheless, we consider this heightened engagement and monitoring to be an integral aspect of the clinical care model itself, and thus its contribution to the outcomes is both inherent and difficult to disentangle from the direct effects of the pharmaceutical interventions.

Regarding generalizability, our model was implemented within a specific institutional context. The feasibility of replicating this intensive, pharmacist-driven model in settings with different staffing levels or higher pharmacist-to-patient ratios may vary. The core components, including risk assessment, patient education, and follow-up, are universally applicable. However, the intensity and frequency of these services might need tailoring based on available resources. Future implementation research should focus on identifying the essential elements of this care model that yield the greatest benefit, facilitating its adoption across diverse clinical environments.

Conclusion

In summary, the implementation of comprehensive oncology pharmacy services is associated with a significant reduction in the incidence of key adverse reactions, including nausea, anorexia, and leukopenia, in patients with gynecologic malignancies. These services can promote the rational use of G-CSFs, underscoring the potential value of integrating clinical pharmacists into the multidisciplinary oncology team to enhance patient care during chemotherapy. However, given the exploratory and retrospective nature of this work, these encouraging findings require validation through larger-scale, prospective, randomized controlled trials.

Future research should not only seek to confirm the efficacy of this model but also focus on identifying the core, scalable elements of these services. This will be essential for adapting and implementing the model across diverse oncology settings in China and globally, particularly in centers with varying levels of resources and staffing ratios. Ultimately, such efforts are crucial to determining the impact of comprehensive pharmacy care on long-term clinical outcomes, such as survival and cost-effectiveness, and to facilitating its broader adoption.
