Circulating Tumor DNA detection in cancer: a comprehensive overview of current detection methods and prospects
Authors: Ana Regina de Abreu, Ayla Wyninckx, Timon Vandamme, Ken Op de Beeck, Guy Van Camp, Marc Peeters, Pierre Laurent-Puig, Julien Taieb, Valerie Taly, Leonor Benhaim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractComplete oveview of ctDNA detection methods.The analysis of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has emerged as a major minimally invasive biomarker in oncology. Numerous methods exist for ctDNA detection and should be selected based on the specific oncological context. PCR-based methods are often preferred for their sensitivity and cost-effectiveness; however, they are limited to a narrower range of genes. In contrast, NGS-based methods enable comprehensive cancer genotyping and more efficient identification of actionable mutations. Moreover, the growing number of emerging approaches, such as third-­generation sequencing and fragmentomics, highlights the increasing technical complexity of ctDNA detection. Overall, this review provides insights into the advantages and limitations of various detection strategies that can help improve clinical care for patients.Keywords: liquid biopsy, ctDNA, cancer, detection, clinical application

This manuscript describes the importance of accurate circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) detection in our clinical practice, offering an in-depth review of both established and emerging ctDNA detection technologies. It explores their application across the continuum of cancer management, with particular attention to technical and practical considerations. As ctDNA plays an increasingly pivotal role in cancer diagnostics, this work provides crucial insights on the strengths and limitations of these methods to support clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide.1 Despite significant advancements in treatment, challenges persist. Early-stage cancers are often asymptomatic, and nearly half of all patients are diagnosed with metastatic stage, which underscores the limitations of current detection methods. Imaging techniques lack the required sensitivity for effective screening and minimal residual disease (MRD) detection. Hence, ongoing research focused on developing more sensitive and accessible biomarkers is essential.

Cancer evolves through the accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations, which can be used for tumor detection and characterization. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs), methylation alterations, copy number alterations (CNAs), and gene fusions can be detected in tumor DNA, typically obtained through a tissue biopsy. However, relying on a tumor biopsy has its limitations, as it may not fully capture intra- or inter-tumoral heterogeneity.

The concept of “liquid biopsy,” introduced in 2019, was originally associated with the analysis of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) within blood samples. It was later extended to include the analysis of various circulating components, such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), within various biological fluids.2–4 Although blood remains the most commonly analyzed fluid, other biofluids—including urine, saliva, cerebrospinal fluid, pleural fluid, and ascites effusion—are also of considerable interest.5,6

The detection of ctDNA offers multifaceted applications in oncology. Positioned for roles in screening, diagnosis, and treatment monitoring, ctDNA presents distinct advantages over traditional tissue biopsy.7 Unlike tissue biopsy, which evaluates only a small fraction of the tumor, ctDNA theoretically reflects the entire tumor genome, representing all (sub)clones of cancer cells.8 The concentration of ctDNA correlates with the localization of the primary tumor and overall tumor burden.5,9 It increases proportionally to tumor load and cancer stage, highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker. The short half-life of ctDNA (ranging from 16 minutes to 2.5 hours) further supports its value for real-time monitoring of treatment efficacy.10,11 During treatment, fluctuations in ctDNA concentration have shown a stronger correlation with treatment response compared to serum markers or imaging techniques.12 ctDNA enables dynamic assessment by capturing spatial and temporal tumor heterogeneity.5,13 It has demonstrated effectiveness in the early detection of treatment resistance.14–17 Patients with high ctDNA levels tend to have poorer survival outcomes than those with low or undetectable ctDNA levels.18,19 However, because ctDNA constitutes only a small fraction of total cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in plasma, its detection—particularly in early-stage cancers—requires highly sensitive and specific methods.7

The integration of liquid biopsy into clinical practice is increasing, driven by a growing number of clinical studies that generate a wide range of heterogeneous data.2,20 Much of this heterogeneity stems from variations in detection methods. The sensitivity and specificity of each method significantly influence data interpretation, contributing to discrepancies in ctDNA detection rates and its clinical relevance. This review aims to clarify the interpretation of these clinical studies by outlining the general methods used for ctDNA detection and highlighting their respective advantages and limitations.

Tumor-informed (non-agnostic) and tumor-naïve (agnostic) approaches

After its extraction from plasma, the identification of the ctDNA fraction within cfDNA relies on the detection of specific alterations. Two approaches exist for this purpose, which are particularly relevant in the context of MRD monitoring in early-stage cancers rather than comprehensive molecular profiling. The tumor-informed (non-agnostic) approach involves examining cfDNA for the same alteration identified in paired primary tumor tissue. The paired detection of an alteration in both tissue and blood confirms the presence of ctDNA within cfDNA. This approach requires a two-step process: initial characterization of the alterations in the primary tumor tissue, followed by targeted ctDNA analysis. Typically, different techniques are used for tissue and plasma due to differences in DNA quality and concentration. DNA from tumor biopsies is more abundant, allowing for a wide range of detection techniques with minimal sensitivity concerns. The preferred detection method, in this case, can identify as many alterations as possible. In contrast, plasma-derived cfDNA requires highly sensitive and specific techniques due to its lower yield. In general, the tumor-informed approach is highly sensitive and specific21 but it is time-consuming and requires personalized assay design for each detected alteration.22

Agnostic (tumor-naïve) approaches bypass the need for prior tumor tissue analysis and rely instead on detecting alterations frequently associated with specific cancer types. Indeed, some solid tumors are characterized by a high frequency of the same mutation. For example, 70%-95% of pancreatic cancers harbor KRAS mutations in codon 12 of exons 1 and 2, enabling direct plasma screening without prior tissue information. However, the absence of a detected mutation does not exclude the presence of ctDNA, but its detection can be confirmed with a limited turnaround time. Agnostic strategies can also detect other tumor-derived features, such as epigenetic signatures (eg, methylation patterns) and cfDNA fragment length profiles. These features can be assessed directly from plasma without requiring the assessment of tumor tissue. While agnostic methods are generally less sensitive than tumor-informed ones, their faster turnaround time, simplicity, and scalability make them valuable tools for MRD monitoring, early detection, and recurrence surveillance.

Both agnostic and non-agnostic approaches can be performed using one or a combination of several detection methods, which are detailed in the following chapters.

Interpreting ctDNA results and potential confounders

Early-stage cancer detection, MRD monitoring, and recurrence surveillance are known to be associated with lower ctDNA levels. Thus, the detection is more challenging.7 It becomes problematic when the sensitivity and specificity are affected by biological phenomena, such as clonal hematopoiesis (CH). Clonal hematopoiesis is part of the normal aging process, and involves the accumulation of hematopoietic clones harboring somatic mutations in cancer-associated genes, leading to clonal expansion of mutations in blood cells.23 These mutations present a source of biological background noise within the cfDNA signal. Thus, CH can be a significant confounding factor during ctDNA analysis, leading to false positives.24 This concern is even more pronounced in agnostic approaches, where tumor tissue is not analyzed, and any detected mutation is assumed to be derived from the tumor. Without paired tissue confirmation, the risk of incorrectly classifying these CH-associated mutations as tumor-associated mutations is substantially higher, potentially leading to inappropriate therapeutic decisions.

Strategies have been developed to address this challenge, including parallel sequencing of matched white blood cells to identify and filter out CH-related variants,25 which doubles the costs involved. In silico algorithms that utilize molecular and genomic features could also be employed to infer the origin of the cfDNA fragments.26

Clonal hematopoiesis filtering methods vary across platforms and are not always disclosed, acknowledging CH is essential to ensure the specificity and clinical validity of ctDNA assays.

General ctDNA detection techniques

Changes in ctDNA can be detected by quantitative and qualitative analyses of ctDNA. Quantitative analysis measures its abundance as an indicator of tumor burden and tumor progression. Qualitative analysis focuses on detecting specific alterations, revealing insights into treatment resistance. During treatment, a shift in the tumor’s genetic landscape could reflect its adaptation before progression. One notable example is the emergence of KRAS mutations in ctDNA during treatment with EGFR inhibitors, which confers progressive resistance in CRC.27 These escape mechanisms have also been observed with the BRCA1 methylation status in breast cancer,28 exon 19 deletions, and the L858R point mutation in EGFR in NSCLC.29 Instead of measuring the ctDNA abundance, understanding which alterations are present can provide critical information about treatment efficacy.

Next-generation sequencing

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) is a versatile method that allows high-throughput sequencing of DNA fragments. Various NGS platforms use different sequencing technologies but share the principle of automated parallel sequencing of millions of small DNA fragments. Afterward, bioinformatic analyses read and classify these sequences by mapping each read to the human reference genome.30 These so-called “high-throughput” or “next-generation” methods have revolutionized genomics by enabling cost-effective sequencing within a few days.

In the following paragraph, we will describe the main NGS methods, including Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) and Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS), that enable the detection of de novo mutations. In contrast, most platforms use methods to increase the sensitivity of ctDNA detection at low tumoral variant allele frequencies (VAFs). These methods include Safe-Sequencing System (Safe-SeqS), CAncer Personalized Profiling by deep Sequencing (CAPP-Seq), integrated Digital Error Suppression-enhanced CAPP-seq (iDES-enhanced CAPP-seq), and Base-Position Error Rate (BPER). They are mainly dedicated to identifying specific mutations.

NGS-based methods

The NGS technology is based on clonal amplification and “sequencing by synthesis” (SBS). During synthesis, the sequencing is associated with an automated DNA base identification. SBS uses deoxynucleotides that emit a unique fluorescence signal when they are incorporated into the strand being synthesized.31 The DNA bases are automatically identified as they are incorporated into the nucleic acid chain. Unlike the Sanger method, which sequences a single DNA fragment at a time, NGS operates in a massively parallel manner, sequencing millions of fragments simultaneously per run.31 However, errors may arise from mutations introduced during template preparation, preamplification steps, and instrument calibration. Other errors can be related to base misincorporation or base-calling errors.31

Whole genome sequencing

Whole genome sequencing provides a complete characterization of tumor genomes. It holds significant promise in uncovering rare mutations and identifying previously unnoticed oncogenes, encompassing the detection of CNAs, indels and point mutations. Nevertheless, its clinical application faces numerous challenges, such as quality assurance issues with sequencing errors ranging from 0.1% to 0.05%. It requires extensive data analysis and powerful bioinformatics support, raises ethical concerns, and demands a considerable investment of time and resources.

Currently, direct sequencing of cfDNA by WGS is theoretically feasible but remains expensive, limiting its widespread adoption. Moreover, the concentration of ctDNA in plasma samples is often too low at early stages or MRD, leading to a high risk of misleading interpretations. Consequently, WGS has seldom been employed for cfDNA analysis. However, recent advances in error-correction algorithms enable ultra-sensitive WGS,32,33 reducing error rates to 10−7-10−5 and improving the detection of alterations and analytical sensitivity in low-frequency ctDNA. Importantly, more accurate detection of CNAs in cfDNA is of high clinical importance, as CNAs often provide more actionable information than point mutations. For instance, in advanced prostate cancers that benefit from PARP inhibition, BRCA2 loss confers higher sensitivity to PARP inhibitors than BRCA2-mutated tumors.34

Whole exome sequencing

The principle of WES is to selectively sequence the protein-coding regions (exons), which constitute about 1% of the human genome. Compared to WGS, it allows for the cost-effective detection of numerous variants. The first step involves capturing the genomic regions of interest through targeted enrichment methods. Since the original description of the direct genomic selection (DGS) method in 2005,35 several enrichment strategies have been developed, including array-based capture and bead-labeled probes. After selection, the coding regions are amplified and sequenced.

Currently, direct sequencing of cfDNA by WES is theoretically possible but remains expensive. It could be used to analyze plasma samples with a high concentration of ctDNA, such as in metastatic settings. Whole exome sequencing is one of the favored options for the characterization of primary tumors with minimal risk of errors.

Whole genome bisulfite sequencing

Whole genome bisulfite sequencing is a leading method in epigenomics that enables the comprehensive mapping of DNA methylation across the entire genome. It involves treating genomic DNA with sodium bisulfite, which deaminates unmethylated cytosines into uracil while leaving the methylated cytosines unchanged. Subsequent high-throughput sequencing enables the identification of methylation patterns at the single-nucleotide level. Despite its effectiveness, WGBS requires substantial input DNA and involves complex data analysis, which can be challenging due to DNA degradation during bisulfite treatment and potential amplification biases.36

Given the low ctDNA concentration within plasma samples, WGBS cannot be directly applied to cfDNA analysis. However, it remains a viable tool for characterizing primary tumors before employing more targeted techniques on plasma.

NGS-based targeted methods: optimization for low-input samples

Modified NGS approaches are used for sequencing a genomic region multiple times, thereby improving the detection rate by reducing errors and thus needing less input DNA. These methods are specifically designed for detecting rare clonal types. The presented examples include Safe-SeqS, CAPP-Seq, iDES-enhanced CAPP-Seq, and BPER, all of which exhibit detection limits below 0.1%.37

Safe-sequencing system

The Safe-SeqS uses unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) assigned to each DNA template, which reduces putative sequencing errors by at least 70-fold. It demonstrates high sensitivity, up to 98%, for detecting tumor mutations.31,38 Advantages include screening for known and rare variants (SNVs, indels, and CNVs), automated analysis with reduced sequencing errors, and low DNA input. However, it is expensive and requires extensive data analysis and robust bioinformatics support.

CAncer Personalized Profiling by deep Sequencing

Developed by Newman et al., CAPP-Seq involves designing “selectors” consisting of biotinylated DNA oligonucleotides that target mutated regions in the cancer of interest.39 Hybridization of ctDNA with these “selector probes” enables subsequent sequencing for the detection and quantification of mutations. The technique demonstrates a low background error rate, yielding a detection sensitivity of 0.01% for ctDNA within cfDNA. Challenges include DNA input amount, potential sample cross-contamination, allelic bias, and potential errors in capture reagents, Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR), or sequencing.

To address these limitations, Newman et al. developed the “integrated digital error suppression”-(iDES)-enhanced CAPP-seq. This method combines in-silico elimination of highly stereotypical background artefacts with a molecular barcoding strategy.40 The templates are amplified and tagged using four barcodes. Next, a computational approach for “background polishing” models position-specific errors, enabling error suppression in independent samples.

Base position error rates

Base position error rates uses an algorithm that corrects the interpretation of mutations detected by sequencing and quantifies substitution errors attributable to sample handling, library preparation, enrichment PCR, and sequencing. The position-error rate (PER) is independently quantified in a set of negative controls (including mismatches, deletions, and insertions) at each base position. The minor allele frequency (MAF) is then calculated for each plasma sample and position. A true mutation is defined when the MAF is significantly higher than the PER. A binomial test compares the observed mutation frequency in ctDNA with the BPER. This algorithm improves the sensitivity of rare mutation detection in plasma to 0.1%.41

Third-generation sequencing

Third-generation sequencing offers an improvement over NGS in its ability to cover long reads (10 kb instead of 600 bp) and complex genomic regions.42,43 Two associated methods are Singe Molecule Real-Time (SMRT) and Nanopore sequencing.42 The detection of long cfDNA fragments in the plasma of cancer patients using SMRT and Nanopore sequencing has broken new ground.44,45 Furthermore, Nanopore sequencing has successfully been used to detect short cfDNA fragments (100-150 bp) from the plasma of cancer patients.46,47 The main strength, particularly in cancer diagnostics, lies in its capability to directly detect DNA methylation without the need for chemical or enzymatic treatment in real-time. This enables immediate analysis of genetic (SNVs, splice variants, gene fusions, and CNAs), epigenetic (DNA methylation and hydroxymethylation), and fragmentomic features from a single run.47,48 Hence, Nanopore sequencing provides same-day delivery of (epi)genomic and fragmentomic signatures from cfDNA.

PCR

Two main PCR-based methods, real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) and digital PCR (dPCR), are used for ctDNA analysis.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Commonly used for nucleic acid quantification, qPCR involves annealing fluorophore-coupled probes complementary to the region of interest. During PCR, the separation of the fluorophore from the quencher increases fluorescence, which can be measured in real-time. Various probe types can be used (Beacon probes, TaqMan probes, Scorpion probes, and HyBeacon probes). qPCR can be used to detect mutations or methylation in tumors and plasma. However, each assay can only detect a few mutations, as each probe is specific to a single alteration.

Digital PCR

Digital PCR is a precise method for the detection and absolute quantification of ctDNA at low allele fractions. It involves partitioning a sample into numerous individual reactions, using either microfabricated chambers on a microfluidic panel or water-in-oil droplet generation. This approach enhances sensitivity and accuracy compared to traditional qPCR. Each partition undergoes PCR amplification, and the target is detected via its fluorescent signal. Unlike qPCR, dPCR isolates individual DNA molecules, increasing detection sensitivity and target amplification. Based on this principle, several technologies have been developed with various partitioning and detection methods.

Droplet digital PCR (ddPCR)

Droplet digital PCR compartmentalizes PCR reactions into aqueous droplets dispersed in oil.49 It allows for a theoretically unlimited number of compartments.37 Each droplet is subjected to individual end-point PCR, permitting its analysis via oil sphere microfluidics for absolute quantification using fluorescent dual-labeled probes. An impressive sensitivity below 0.001% can be achieved, even with minimal DNA input.37,50 Furthermore, no specific bioinformatic interpretation is required. While ddPCR stands out for its cost-effectiveness, it is limited by its multiplexing capabilities, detecting only 5 to 10 target sequences per run.11,37 Despite these limitations, ddPCR is particularly valuable in analyzing cancer hotspot (epi)mutations and also plays a considerable role in detecting rare mutations, CNVs, and gene rearrangements in various clinical samples.51,52

Oncogenic mutations, though well-characterized, can occur in various genes and loci, making a single assay insufficient for all patients and diseases. In contrast, DNA methylation tends to occur at defined genomic loci, and methylation profiles are more universally applicable across patients. After bisulfite conversion and PCR amplification, TaqMan probes enable simultaneous detection and quantification of the methylated target gene.53–55

BEAMing (beads, emulsion, amplification, magnetics)

Introduced in 2003, BEAMing involves beads, emulsion, amplification, and magnetics.56 It combines primer-bound beads with DNA templates, distributing the mix in oil detergent to create aqueous compartments. Each compartment contains one strand of the PCR, ensuring that one strand of the PCR product is bound to the beads. After amplification, the beads in each compartment are coated with thousands of copies of the initial single DNA product. Finally, fluorescent hybridization and flow cytometry are applied to distinguish and count different templates. With BEAMing, one mutant DNA molecule can be detected within 10 000 wild-type molecules (0.01% sensitivity). Although highly sensitive and specific, its routine clinical use involves a complex experimental procedure.11

These techniques collectively form a comprehensive toolkit for ctDNA detection, each with its unique advantages and challenges, catering to diverse research and clinical needs. An overview of their clinical applications is provided in Figure 1 and Tables 1 and 2. This variety of options reassures researchers and clinicians that they can choose the method that best suits their specific needs, whether it is sensitivity, specificity, cost, or the nature of the study or clinical application.

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) detection assays utilized in all phases of cancer management. Detection assays involve the investigation of epigenetic and genomic alterations, including DNA methylation patterns, chromosomal rearrangements, single nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions/deletions (indels), and gene amplifications. These assays are used across the entire course of a cancer patient’s journey. (1) Cancer diagnosis: the detection of ctDNA confirms the presence of malignancy. (2) Molecular profiling: molecular targets are identified before treatment initiation to guide the selection of appropriate therapies. (3) Detecting recurrence: a decline in ctDNA level suggests effective treatment and reduced tumor burden. The continued presence of ctDNA after curative-intent treatment, accompanied by the absence of visible tumor signs, indicates the presence of MRD. Monitoring MRD over time is helpful for the early detection of cancer recurrence. (4) Detecting resistance: a targeted assay can identify acquired resistance alterations, enabling a switch to an alternative therapy. Fragmentomics represents a promising field in cancer diagnostics, where altered fragmentomic signals, such as changes in ctDNA fragment lengths, are assessed. Discussion and prospects

The selection of the appropriate method in molecular diagnostics is paramount for each clinical scenario. Although PCR is well-known for its sensitivity, simplicity, and cost-effectiveness, its gene scope is restricted. In contrast, NGS approaches provide extensive and informative cancer genotyping. For example, the Guardant360 CDx assay has outperformed traditional tumor tissue testing by identifying actionable mutations faster and improving clinical trial enrollment.75,76 Nevertheless, the clinical implication of multigene panels versus single-gene assays remains unclear.

There is good alignment between multigene ctDNA assays and tumor tissue assays, but more inter-assay concordance data are needed.45–47,75,77-80 The potential for ctDNA to complement tumor tissue-based testing is a key discussion. Limited biopsy material and inaccessible tumor sites can hinder comprehensive molecular profiling. Given cancer’s intrinsic heterogeneity, tissue biopsies may not fully represent the complete genetic landscape. A less invasive, real-time profiling option is provided by ctDNA. However, a low tumor burden may compromise its sensitivity, and somatic mutations in cfDNA may result in false-positive results. A more holistic diagnostic strategy could be achieved by combining tissue-based testing and ctDNA analysis, as liquid biopsies are not intended to replace tissue-based testing completely.81 Nevertheless, the necessity of both for biomarker assessments remains to be explored. The therapeutic relevance of ctDNA-detected mutations absent in tumor tissue remains uncertain.

Tissue-based tests, including Oncotype Dx and FoundationOne CDx,82,83 should serve as a model for integrating ctDNA assays into clinical practice. These tests, with their proven track record, have demonstrated the reliability and effectiveness of multigene assays. Their complexity and resource requirements have led to centralized testing, which has shifted diagnosis from hospital pathology departments to specialized laboratories. The latter is equipped with NGS devices and skilled personnel, reducing costs through high scale. Large multigene ctDNA assays will likely remain centralized, while single-gene or small multigene ctDNA assays may continue to be tested decentralized.

DNA hypermethylation in gene regulatory regions can serve as a potential early cancer marker, thereby expanding the diagnostic landscape.84 Large-scale prospective studies are exploring the potential of methylation-based assays as screening tools for healthy populations.84 These assays have the potential to identify over 50 different cancer types, making them a powerful tool for early cancer detection.67,85,86 The introduction of fragmentomics, a novel approach to cancer diagnostics, further expands the diagnostic landscape.87 Several studies have examined DNA fragment size analysis for ctDNA detection, often represented by a DNA Integrity Index (DII).88 The agnostic nature of fragmentomics focuses on evaluating broad patterns of cfDNA fragmentation associated with tumorigenesis rather than specific mutations. The detection of different fragment lengths involves PCR-based technologies, sequencing technologies, or a combination of both. When using PCR-based approaches, a wide variety of primer sets can be used to amplify sequences of various lengths, thereby differentiating the ctDNA.89 qPCR and ddPCR often target pre-selected genome loci, limiting the extent of information obtained. To address this limitation, primer sets that target repeated sequences can be used.90 Furthermore, selecting short DNA fragments before subsequent targeted sequencing can increase the sensitivity for analyzing low-concentrated plasma samples.91 To gain a more comprehensive view, NGS-based methods, including low-coverage WGS, are employed.87 These methods can be combined with machine learning systems, boosting their sensitivity and specificity.87 Delfi Diagnostics is currently pursuing the commercialization of DELFI (DNA evaluation of fragments for early interception). This technology uses low-­coverage, whole-genome sequencing and machine learning to detect stage I/II lung cancers.92 Ongoing clinical validation studies in the United States (NCT05306288) and Europe (Netherlands Trial Register NL9710) will further evaluate the fragmentome-based classifier.93 SMRT and Nanopore sequencing have recently been applied to fragmentomics, enabling rapid cfDNA sizing.44,45 Additionally, fragmentation-based nucleosome profiling offers an alternative method for interpreting various chromatin states in the genome. Nucleosome profiling enables tissue-of-origin deconvolution, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of cancer detection when combined with methylation analysis.47,48 While fragmentomics-based techniques are not yet part of standard clinical practice, they hold immense potential.

The most sensitive assays are expected to combine epigenomic and fragmentomic approaches using next-generation enzymatic methylation sequencing. The launch of two enzyme-based technologies has drastically shifted the field of using bisulfite for DNA methylation sequencing, namely TET-assisted pyridine borane sequencing (TAPS) and Enzymatic Methyl sequencing (EM-seq).94,95 Additionally, a novel, cost-effective NGS-based method combining methylation-sensitive restriction enzyme (MSRE) digestion with small molecule molecular inversion probes (smMIPs) has recently been created, known as IMPRESS.96 Initially focusing on tissue, the technique shows promise for potential use with liquid biopsies. Continued innovations in technology and multi-omics will address the current issues and propel ctDNA assays to become mainstream use.
