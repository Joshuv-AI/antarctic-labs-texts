Sustainable development of concrete through treated and untreated plastic waste aggregates
Authors: Ekkachai Yooprasertchai, Ahsan Khursheed, Muhammad Irshad Qureshi, Ali Ejaz, Qudeer Hussain, Amorntep Jirasakjamroonsri, Panumas Saingam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focused on sustainable and novel development of concrete through treated and untreated plastic waste aggregates. In this study, the surface of Elec-waste coarse aggregates was treated with sulfuric acid, marble dust coating, silica fume coating, and alkaline bleach. Elec-waste aggregates were used in concrete by replacing natural coarse aggregates in amounts of 10%, 20%, and 30%. The 10%, 20%, and 30% Elec-waste replacement ratios resulted in the compressive strength reduction of 8.97%, 27.99%, and 42.01%, respectively. The same percentage of Elec-waste aggregates reduced the splitting tensile strength by 21.77%, 35.89%, and 47.89%, respectively. However, the slump was observed to increase by 41.4%, 52.1%, and 68.8%, respectively. All the considered treatments enhanced the performance of Elec-waste aggregate concrete (E-WAC). Overall, the best improvement in the performance of E-WAC was imparted by silica fume coating, irrespective of the quantity of Elec-waste aggregates. The present study proposed an expression to estimate the reduction of the compressive strength of E-WAC. It was demonstrated that the equation by JCI-08 predicted splitting tensile strength of E-WAC close to experimental results.Keywords: Elec-waste aggregate, Surface treatment, Compressive strength, Splitting strength, SlumpSubject terms: Civil engineering, Structural materials

Introduction

Concrete is one of the most-used materials worldwide, attributed to the ever-growing construction realm. It is anticipated that the use of concrete will increase in the upcoming years. For instance, a rough estimate suggests that approximately 18 billion tonnes of concrete will be used annually by the year 20501. At present, the per capita share of concrete usage per year is estimated at three tonnes2. Aggregates comprise 70–80% of the total volume of concrete3. This suggests that the demand for raw aggregates is escalating to produce concrete. Hence, the risk of depletion of natural resources for raw aggregates is increasing4. Moreover, many countries rely on imports to satisfy the demand for natural aggregates (NAs)5. Therefore, the urgent need is to identify appropriate substitutes for NAs to alleviate their use without substantially impacting the characteristics of concrete. Numerous endeavors were undertaken to evaluate the viability of substituting NAs in concrete with brick waste6,7, plastic8, glass9,10, and electronic waste11,12.

The last three decades have seen a rapid evolution in the use of electronic equipment, thanks to the innovations that have facilitated the human lifestyle with great impact. However, there exists an increasing trend of improvements in technology with each passing year. As a result, modern electronic equipment may become obsolete in no time. Intuitively, the quantity of electronic waste being generated each year is evolving. For instance, the total quantity of electronic waste (Elec-waste) produced globally in 2019 was 53.6 million tonnes13. This amount is expected to rise to 74.7 million tonnes by the year 203014. At this point, a possible question arises: whether the Elec-waste has the potential to be utilized as a substitute for the NAs in concrete? An affirmative answer to this question can have two advantages: (1) the substantial demand for NAs and the corresponding danger of the depletion of their resources can be relaxed, and (2) the issues related to the processing and disposal of Elec-waste can be tackled.

The production of Elec-waste aggregates involves its heating in a kiln with the maximum temperature restricted below their melting point, followed by its crushing to the required size15. Many researchers have worked on the feasibility of incorporating Elec-waste into concrete as a partial alternative to NAs. Alagusankareswari et al.16 explored the behavior of concrete constructed with Elec-waste aggregates as a partial replacement for NAs. It was concluded that the incorporation of Elec-waste decreased the self-weight of the concrete. The compressive and splitting tensile strengths of the Elec-waste concrete were found to be lower than the compressive strength of the corresponding control concrete. Needhidasan et al.17 varied the replacement ratio of coarse aggregates by Elec-waste aggregates from 0 to 20%. A notable drop in the self-weight of Elec-waste concrete was observed, and the authors categorized the resulting concrete as lightweight concrete. The slump value increased as the replacement ratio increased. The authors found a decrease in the compressive and flexural strengths as the replacement ratio increased, whereas the splitting tensile strength increased. Balasubramanian et al.12 simultaneously used glass powder and Elec-waste as partial replacements for cement and coarse aggregate, respectively. The mix with Elec-waste alone exhibited increased water absorption and decreased compressive strength. However, concrete mix with both Elec-waste and glass powder exhibited better slump and compressive strength. Evram et al.18 explored the performance of concrete with coarse aggregates partly substituted by Elec-waste and partial substitution of cement by marble dust. The modulus of elasticity, unit weight, and compressive strength of the resulting concrete were found to be lower than the corresponding values of control concrete. Rathore and Rawat19 replaced 5%-30% coarse aggregates with Elec-waste aggregates. A 15% substitution of coarse aggregates resulted in a decrease of compressive and flexural strengths by 20% and 17.5%, respectively. In their study, Hilal et al.20 investigated the properties of cement mortar containing Polyethylene Waste (PW) treated with reactive materials and compared them to untreated PW and normal cement mortar. They used various reactive materials (cement, fly ash, and silica fume) to treat the PW and replaced 10% of the sand in the mortar by volume with the treated PW. The properties tested included density, compressive strength, modulus of rupture, and absorption at different ages. The findings revealed that treating PW with cement significantly improved the characteristics of the PW cement mortar. Furthermore, all treatments enhanced the properties of the cement mortar compared to untreated PW. Mohammed et al.21 studied self-compacting concrete (SCC) made with cement kiln dust (CKD) and reinforced with polypropylene (PP) and waste plastic fibers (WPL). Using CKD in varying percentages (0, 5, 10, 15%) as cement replacements, with and without fibers, he evaluated fresh properties (L-box height ratio, slump flow, segregation resistance) and hardened properties (water absorption, thermal conductivity, flexural and compressive strengths). The workability decreased with fiber addition but remained within standards. The combination of 15% CKD with 0.5% WPL and PP fibers improved flexural and compressive strengths by 46.1% and 35%, respectively, while also enhancing thermal conductivity and maintaining acceptable water absorption levels. Several other studies have also highlighted the substandard characteristics of concrete constructed with Elec-waste aggregates22–24.

The preceding discussion highlighted that the properties of Elec-waste concrete are substandard in comparison to the properties of natural concrete. This can be ascribed to the smooth surface of Elec-waste plastic aggregates and their resulting weak bond with the cementitious mixture23,25. As a result, it is intuitive to assume that Elec-waste aggregates with rough texture may exhibit a better bond with the cementitious mixture, resulting in better performance of the concrete. Naik et al.26 utilized post-consumer waste plastic as a soft filler in concrete. The waste plastic was pretreated either with water, bleach, or alkaline bleach. The findings suggested that the best enhancement in concrete properties was observed from the treatment with alkaline bleach. Mohan et al.27 created a mix of potassium dichromate and concentrated sulfuric acid. The mixture was heated, and plastic aggregates were introduced into it. Substantial enhancements in the mechanical properties of concrete were noted. Shaikh et al.28 enhanced the bond between recycled aggregates and concrete mix by introducing a nano-silica solution. Recycled aggregates were soaked in a nano-silica solution for 24 h. A significant increase in concrete compressive strength was observed when recycled aggregates were treated with the nano-silica solution beforehand. Abu Bakr and Singh29 investigated the effect of the biomineralization technique on the properties of recycled aggregate concrete with hydrated lime and brick powder. The blended hydrated lime and brick powder enhanced the strength and durability of recycled aggregate concrete. Abu Bakr et al.30 reported that the strength and durability could be significantly improved by calcite precipitation produced by bacteria. Rohini and Padmapriya31 replaced 0% to 20% of coarse aggregates with Elec-waste aggregates. The bacteria subtilis were added as 1% and 2% by weight of cement to treat the impact of Elec-waste aggregates in concrete. Compared to natural aggregate concrete, the bacterial treated concrete depicted greater compressive strength. Ahmed et al.32 replaced 5–50% of coarse aggregates with Elec-waste and reported a decrease of up to 52%, 47.5%, and 39.4% in the compressive strength, splitting tensile strength, and flexural strength, respectively.

The ongoing discussion suggests that several practices have been considered by researchers to increase the performance of concrete with recycled agrgegates. Till now, the performance of different pretreatment techniques has not been assessed for the case of Elec-waste plastic coarse aggregates. As per reports, the e-waste problem in Thailand has become an increasing concern. In 2013, Thailand generated 0.56 million tons of hazardous waste from households, and approximately 65% was e-waste33. In this regard, the objectives of this work involved the assessment of various techniques for the pretreatment of Elec-waste aggregates such as alkaline bleach, sulphuric acid, marble powder coating, and silica fume coating. The assessment of the effect of Elec-waste aggregates in different ratios as a replacement for coarse aggregates was desired.

Experimental program

Production of plastic aggregates

The present study utilized Elec-waste related to the group of Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) plastic. The composition and properties of ABS plastic have already been reported elsewhere34. The ABS plastic was obtained locally comprising the remains of electronic appliances, computers, TVs, etc. The raw ABS plastic was processed through various steps to get the desired plastic aggregates. Initially, the raw plastic was cleaned thoroughly with tap water. A shredder was then used to reduce the size of the raw plastic. At this stage, the shredded plastic was screened for any electric wires. Then, the screened plastic was heated in a kiln at a high temperature of 200 °C to melt all the plastic. After obtaining the plastic in the molten state, it was cooled in a water tank. The resulting cooled product resembled plastic rock-type material, similar to the large rocks obtained from natural quarries. The plastic rocks were then passed through a crusher to obtain plastic aggregates of the required size. The complete manufacturing process to obtain plastic aggregates is shown in Fig. 1. Test matrix

The present study investigates the role of various surface treatment techniques for Elec-waste aggregates to improve the properties of the resulting concrete. In this regard, sixteen types of concrete mix were prepared, as shown in Fig. 4. One mixture was prepared without Elec-waste aggregates and served as the reference mix. The remaining fifteen mixes were categorized into three groups depending on the replacement ratio of coarse aggregates, i.e., 10%, 20%, and 30%. Each group comprised five concrete mix types. In the first mix type, no pretreatment of Elec-waste aggregates was performed. The remaining four mixes comprised Elec-waste aggregate pretreated with either of the four considered techniques. Concrete mix proportions

The water-to-cement ratio was kept at 0.50 for all mix types, which were designed for the target strength of 20 MPa. The cement, sand, and coarse aggregates were used in the proportion of 1:1.15:3. The Elec-waste concrete was made using a mechanical tilting drum type concrete mixer with a 35 rev/min revolution rate. Two distinct steps were taken to complete the mechanical mixing of the concrete. First, half of the total water was mixed with both fine, coarse natural, and coarse Elec-waste aggregates for 4 min. Second, the remaining water and binder were added and mixed for the next 4 min. The mix proportions of concrete utilized in this work are presented in Table 3.

Test methods

The characteristics of concrete with Elec-waste aggregates with or without pretreatment were computed to assess the performance of various types of pretreatments. Table 4 provides the summary of test types, corresponding standards, and the number of test specimens for each test type. The workability of concrete was estimated by conducting slump cone tests as per the recommendations of ASTM C143/C143M-2036 and test results are reported as the mean of three slump cone tests. The compressive strength of concrete was evaluated by following BS EN 12390-337 recommendations. Standard cubes of size 100 mm × 100 mm × 100 mm were prepared for this purpose, and the average of three cube tests was reported as the representative compressive strength. The splitting tensile strength was estimated by following the protocols of ASTM C496/C496M-1738. Cylinders of size 150 mm × 300 mm were tested for this purpose, whereas the results reported in this work are also the mean of three tests.

Treatment of Elec-waste aggregates

The surface of the Elec-waste plastic aggregates was initially modified using a variety of surface treatment procedures, including physical and chemical treatment, as previously described, before including in concrete. In physical treatment, silica fume and marble powder coating were adopted, following the recommendations by Tam and Tam39 and Uysal et al.40. This study made use of silica fume made by Cormix International Ltd. and sold under the name Conmix SF1. Additionally, a local marble processing factory provided the marble powder utilized. Similarly, for the chemical surface modification technique, two oxidizing agents, namely, sulfuric acid and alkaline bleach, were utilized, as recommended by Naik et al.26 and Kaur and Pavia41. Both these chemicals were purchased from the local market and diluted as per requirement.

Chemical treatment involved soaking the manufactured Elec-waste aggregates for 24 h in diluted solutions of oxidizing agents such sulfuric acid and alkaline bleach (NaOH + Bleach), i.e., 5% HOCL and 4% NaOH. The acid solution was diluted in neutral water in a ratio of 10:1. Afterward, the aggregates were taken out of the solution and air-dried to ensure that there was no additional chemical left on the surface. Sulfuric acid (2.5% by weight) and calcium hypochlorite solution (5% by weight) were the oxidizing agents used. As a control, water-treated Elec-waste aggregates were used. The treated Elec-waste aggregates are shown in Fig. 5.

Chemically treated Elec-waste aggregates.

In physical treatment, a cement slurry was made by combining cement and marble powder in a 70:30 ratio and cement and silica fume in the same ratio with a water-to-cement ratio of 0.5, as recommended by Alqarni et al.42. Aggregates were then dipped into the mixture to get a surface coating. Aggregates and slurry were properly mixed to ensure that every particle had the required coating, and any extra slurry was then drained. The coated aggregates were spread out on a polythene sheet and allowed to dry in the sun for seven days. The aggregates were removed after curing to continue the process of designing the concrete mix. The process of physical treatment is displayed in Fig. 6.

Experimental results

Slump

Unlike compressive and splitting tensile strength, the inclusion of Elec-waste aggregates as a partial substitution for coarse aggregates resulted in an increase in the concrete slump. This is graphically demonstrated in Fig. 7, where the concrete slump values are plotted for various percentages of Elec-waste aggregates. In addition, the slump for concrete with Elec-waste aggregates is also shown, which was estimated as 34 mm. The incorporation of 10%, 20%, and 30% of Elec-waste aggregates increased the concrete slump to 58 mm, 71 mm, and 109 mm, respectively. This corresponds to a percentage increase in the slump of 41.4%, 52.1%, and 68.8%, respectively. It can be inferred that this increased slump resulted from the smooth surface of Elec-waste aggregates that reduced the overall friction between coarse aggregates and cementitious mix. Another reason behind the elevated slump of Elec-waste concrete is the lower water absorption capacity of Elec-waste aggregates than that of the natural coarse aggregates23.

Comparison of concrete slump for different percentages of Elec-waste coarse aggregates.

The various techniques adopted for the surface treatment of Elec-waste aggregates resulted in a reduction of the slump as compared to the slump of untreated Elec-waste aggregate concrete (E-WAC). This is displayed in Fig. 8, where the effect of surface treatment techniques for each replacement ratio of Elec-waste aggregates is shown. It is observed that the best improvement in the slump was observed for the case of silica fume coating, followed by marble dust coating, sulfuric acid treatment, and alkaline bleach treatment, respectively. Nonetheless, none of the considered treatments were able to neutralize the impact of Elec-waste aggregates completely.

Increase in slump for various treatment techniques applied to Elec-waste aggregates.

Compressive strength

The comparison of the compressive strength of concrete for various percentages of Elec-waste aggregates is displayed in Fig. 9a. It is observed that the compressive strength reduced as the percentage of Elec-waste aggregates increased. This is statistically shown in Fig. 9b, where a linear trend is noted in reduced compressive strength with increasing percentages of Elec-waste aggregates. The addition of 10%, 20%, and 30% Elec-waste coarse aggregates as partial replacements for natural coarse aggregates resulted in a compressive strength reduction of 8.97%, 27.99%, and 42.01%, respectively. Similar observations were also reported by Belmokaddem et al.43.

a Comparison of compressive strength for various percentages of Elec-waste coarse aggregates and b reduction in compressive strength for different percentages of Elec-waste aggregates.

Pretreatment of Elec-waste aggregates resulted in improvement of compressive strength as compared to the compressive strength of untreated E-WAC. This is graphically shown in Fig. 10, where a comparison of the reduction in compressive strength for untreated and treated E-WAC is displayed for different percentages of E-WAC. For the case of a 10% replacement ratio, the maximum improvement was imparted by silica fume coating (0.48% reduction), followed by the pretreatment using sulfuric acid (1.32% reduction). Silica fume coating and sulfuric acid treatment resulted in a compressive strength reduction of 19.00% and 20.05%, respectively, as compared to the 27.97% reduction for untreated Elec-waste aggregates in the case of a 20% replacement ratio. A similar tendency was also noted in the case of a 30% replacement ratio. The improvement in compressive strength reduction by alkaline bleach treatment and marble dust coating was marginal. In summary, the minimum reduction in compressive strength was observed for silica fume treatment, irrespective of the percentage of Elec-waste aggregates. The difference between the performance of silica fume and sulfuric acid treatment was negligible.

Splitting tensile strength

The results of splitting tensile strength for untreated E-WAC are displayed in Fig. 11a. Analogous to the trend noted in the case of compressive strength, a decreasing trend in the splitting tensile strength was also observed as the percentage of Elec-waste aggregates increased. The splitting tensile strength decreased to 1.94 MPa, 1.50 MPa, and 1.29 MPa from 2.48 MPa for 10%, 20%, and 30% Elec-waste aggregates, respectively. This corresponds to the percentage reduction of 21.77%, 35.89%, and 47.98% for 10%, 20%, and 30% Elec-waste aggregates, respectively. Figure 11b illustrates a robust linear decrease in splitting tensile strength with the proportion of E-waste aggregates, indicating a clear trend. Similar decreases in the splitting tensile strength of concrete when using Elec-waste aggregates have also been documented16,23,44. The enhanced properties show that the microstructural behavior was improved by the treatment of Elec-waste aggregates. For instance, Abu Bakr et al.45 realized that the weak interfacial transition zone in recycled aggregates adversely affects the performance of the resulting concrete. Therefore, they incorporated bacterial consortium that induced calcium carbonates at the crack interface, thereby enhancing the performance of the concrete. In another study, Abu Bakr et al.46 also reported similar improvements by using microbially induced calcium carbonate precipitation.

a Comparison of splitting tensile strength for various ratios of Elec-waste coarse aggregates and b decrease in splitting tensile strength for different percentages of Elec-waste aggregates.

The improvement in splitting tensile strength due to various treatments was also assessed. The effect of various treatments on Elec-waste aggregates on splitting tensile strength is displayed in Fig. 12. It was observed that the best improvement in the reduction of splitting tensile strength was imparted by silica fume treatment. A similar observation was also found in the case of compressive strength results. For instance, the splitting strength reduction in the case of a 10% replacement ratio for silica fume treatment was 7.66%. For the same group, the splitting strength reduction in the case of alkaline bleach, sulfuric acid, and marble dust coating was 16.13%, 12.90%, and 16.53%, respectively. A similar tendency was also noted in the case of 20% and 30% replacement ratios of Elec-waste aggregates. However, the difference between the improvements in strength reduction was not as significant as that in the case of a 10% replacement ratio.

Correlation between compressive and splitting strength

The compressive and splitting tensile strength of concrete can be related empirically. There exists a close relationship between the compressive and tensile strength of concrete. Several design codes present different equations for calculating splitting tensile strength when the compressive strength of concrete is known. A summary of a few expressions for the calculation of splitting tensile strength is presented in Table 5. The ratios of experimental to predicted values of each model are also presented in Table 5. It is observed that the models by ACI 318-1947, Eurocode 2-0448, and JSCE-0749 overestimated the splitting tensile strength of E-WAC and the rate of overestimation increased with the ratio of Elec-waste aggregates in concrete. However, the expression by JCI-0850 resulted in close agreement with experimental splitting tensile strengths of E-WAC. This is represented by their close-to-one values of the ratios of experimental to predicted ratios, as shown in Table 5. Therefore, new expressions for calculating the tensile strength of E-WAC are not proposed. It is recommended to use the model by JCI-0850 to estimate the splitting tensile strength of E-WAC.

Conclusions

The performance of E-WAC in comparison to that of natural aggregate concrete is known to be inferior. The issue has been related to the smooth texture of Elec-waste aggregates, resulting in a substandard bond performance with the cementitious mix. This study focused on improving the bond between Elec-waste aggregates and cementitious mix by adopting four surface treatment techniques. The surface of Elec-waste coarse aggregates was treated with sulfuric acid, marble dust coating, silica fume coating, and alkaline bleach. The performance of the resulting concrete was compared to that of the concrete without Elec-waste aggregates. The following important conclusions can be drawn from experimental results.

The compressive strength of E-WAC exhibited a negative linear trend as the percentage of Elec-waste aggregates increased. The addition of 10%, 20%, and 30% Elec-waste coarse aggregates as partial replacements for natural coarse aggregates resulted in a compressive strength reduction of 8.97%, 27.99%, and 42.01%, respectively. The improvement in compressive strength reduction by alkaline bleach and marble dust treatments was marginal. Overall, the best improvement in the compressive strength was imparted by silica fume treatment.

The compressive strength of E-WAC exhibited a negative linear trend as the percentage of Elec-waste aggregates increased. The addition of 10%, 20%, and 30% Elec-waste coarse aggregates as partial replacements for natural coarse aggregates resulted in a compressive strength reduction of 8.97%, 27.99%, and 42.01%, respectively. The improvement in compressive strength reduction by alkaline bleach and marble dust treatments was marginal. Overall, the best improvement in the compressive strength was imparted by silica fume treatment.

Splitting tensile strength, like compressive strength, also exhibited a negative trend with the percentage of Elec-waste aggregates. The splitting tensile strength reduced to 1.94 MPa, 1.50 MPa, and 1.29 MPa from 2.48 MPa for 10%, 20%, and 30% Elec-waste aggregates, respectively. The performance of silica fume treatment also resulted in the best performance among all treatments to reduce the impact of Elec-waste aggregates on splitting tensile strength.

The splitting tensile strength reduced to 1.94 MPa, 1.50 MPa, and 1.29 MPa from 2.48 MPa for 10%, 20%, and 30% Elec-waste aggregates, respectively. The performance of silica fume treatment also resulted in the best performance among all treatments to reduce the impact of Elec-waste aggregates on splitting tensile strength.

A significant increase in the slump of concrete was observed after the inclusion of Elec-waste aggregates. The slump increased as the percentage of Elec-waste aggregates increased. The inclusion of 10%, 20%, and 30% of Elec-waste aggregates increased the concrete slump to 58 mm, 71 mm, and 109 mm from 34 mm, respectively. The effect of silica fume treatment on E-WAC slump was also distinct from other treatments adopted.
