Emicizumab in the treatment of acquired hemophilia A: A Two-Center experience
Authors: Malcolm Su, Sean Yates, Melissa Sanchez, Yu-Min Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emicizumab is a FVIII mimetic antibody used in the treatment of inherited hemophilia A that could potentially be used in acquired hemophilia A (AHA) to achieve hemostasis and shorten hospital stay while reducing bypass therapies. We report on 12 cases of AHA patients treated with emicizumab in an academic center between January 2020 and June 2024. There were 5 male and 7 female patients, with median (interquartile range [IQR]) age and maximum inhibitor titer of 73 (60–76) years and 138 (36.75 -164.25) BU/mL, respectively. Ten patients were started on emicizumab while admitted inpatient, while 2 were started outpatient. The initial emicizumab dose was 3 mg/kg for 8 patients and 1.5 mg/kg for 4 patients; all patients then continued therapy with emicizumab 1.5 mg/kg every other week starting on day 8 and received immunosuppression with steroids and rituximab. Patients who began emicizumab therapy inpatient required a median (IQR) of 8 (6–10) days of bypass therapy and stopped bypass therapy 2.5 (1-5.25) days after emicizumab initiation. Furthermore, these patients were hospitalized for a median (IQR) of 10 (6.5-18.75) days and discharged 3 (2–6) days after starting emicizumab. One patient developed deep vein thrombosis and acute pulmonary thromboembolism while on emicizumab and was subsequently treated with anticoagulation. In conclusion, emicizumab appears to be an effective treatment in attaining hemostasis and reducing need of bypass therapy in patients with AHA. Emicizumab use in AHA may warrant monitoring for thromboembolic complications, and further study is needed to determine whether immunosuppression can be avoided.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-06905-5.

Introduction

Acquired hemophilia A (AHA) is a bleeding disorder caused by autoantibodies against coagulation factor VIII (FVIII) and often presents as an isolated prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) [1]. The disease is often associated with concomitant autoimmune disorders, hematologic or solid malignancies, and infections, with up to 50% of cases occurring without any comorbid conditions [1, 2]. Though AHA is an uncommon disorder with an incidence of about 1 per million per year, there is a high mortality rate up to 33% within the first few months of diagnosis [3]. This is because AHA can present with life-threatening bleeding, often requiring prompt hemostatic management with bypassing agents such as recombinant coagulation Factor VIIa (NovoSeven) and FVIII inhibitor bypassing activity (FEIBA). These therapies are utilized in patients with high titers of inhibitors to achieve hemostasis [4].

Another cornerstone in the treatment of AHA is immunosuppression to stop antibody production in up to 90% of patients [5]. These treatment regiments often include a combination of corticosteroids and cyclophosphamide. However, it may take several months to achieve disease remission with immunosuppression, predisposing patients to fatal infection and bleeding during this time [6]. These adverse effects of immunosuppressive treatments contribute to the overall high mortality among AHA patients. In addition, hemostatic treatments used to achieve immediate hemostasis are also limited by side effects and costs. Bypassing agents have high efficacy in achieving initial hemostatic control with a small thrombotic risk, but are limited by their cost and intravenous route of administration [7]. Recombinant porcine FVIII has also been shown to be safe in management of bleeding episodes among patients with AHA [8]. However, these treatments require close monitoring inpatient and are not utilized as long-term bleeding prophylaxis in patients with AHA.

As a result, the development of cost-effective and accessible treatment options with minimal adverse effects may significantly improve the quality of care provided for AHA patients [9]. One emerging agent as a potential treatment for AHA is emicizumab. Emicizumab is a FVIII mimetic bispecific antibody used in the treatment of congenital hemophilia A that could be used in AHA to achieve hemostasis, shorten hospital stays, and reduce bypassing therapy utilization. Additionally, it can be utilized to facilitate transition to outpatient bleeding prophylaxis in AHA management. Emicizumab is currently indicated for prophylaxis to reduce the frequency of bleeding episodes in hemophilia A patients with or without FVIII inhibitors [10]. In 2022, emicizumab was also approved in Japan for prevention of bleeding in patients with AHA, and has been shown to lead to shorter hospital stays [11]. In the United States, emicizumab is only approved in the treatment of congenital hemophilia A; however, it is used off-label in the United States and European Union for AHA. Additionally, emicizumab has recently been studied for its possible role in limiting the need for immunosuppression when treating AHA [12]. Studies have shown that emicizumab may be effective in significantly reducing bleeding time, but may require immunosuppression to control inhibitor production [13].

In summary, AHA can result in severe bleeding and is a potentially life-threatening condition when hemostasis is not achieved expeditiously. As treatments such as immunosuppressive therapy can cause increased risk of side effects and infection, there has been rising interest in utilizing emicizumab to attain hemostasis in patients with AHA. Although we have accumulated a great deal of knowledge and experience in the use of emicizumab in treating congenital hemophilia A, there are few studies detailing real-world experiences of emicizumab in treatment of AHA. Therefore, we aimed to characterize the outcomes of AHA patients treated with emicizumab, focusing on the impact of emicizumab on the amount of bypassing therapy received, dosing patterns, need for immunosuppression, and potential complications of therapy at two academic hospital centers.

Methods

We analyzed patients with AHA who were treated with emicizumab between January 2020 and June 2024 across two academic hospital systems, including University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center and Parkland Health and Hospital System, the safety-net system for Dallas County. Electronic medical records of patients were reviewed, and data on clinical presentation, demographics, and hospitalizations were gathered. Specifically, information regarding bypassing therapies utilized during inpatient stays, emicizumab dosage regimens, and complications of therapy were noted. Since there is no standardized dose for emicizumab in the treatment of AHA, emicizumab doses (1.5 mg/kg versus 3.0 mg/kg) were chosen at the discretion of the treating hematologist after consultation with local and national experts, including the hospital system’s thrombosis and hemostasis team consisting of classical hematologists and laboratory medicine specialists in coagulation, as well as review of the literature, taking into account individual patient characteristics. Patients who are refractory to prior interventions or have severe bleeding may receive emicizumab 3.0 mg/kg weekly initially, while patients who have bleeding that is controlled with bypassing agents, have concerns for high thrombotic risk, or have other significant comorbidities may receive emicizumab 1.5 mg/kg. To assess clinical improvement of bleeding over time, we retrospectively evaluated patient progress notes and laboratory findings to assess changes in bleeding along all active sites of bleeding. This included assessing need for blood product transfusion; hemoglobin trends; and physical exam findings such as changes in ecchymosis spread, size, and tension of area. Patients were noted to have clinical improvement once there was improvement in exam, stable hemoglobin, and no longer requiring blood transfusions. We present the median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and counts with frequency of occurrence for categorical variables. The study was conducted in accordance with principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. This research was approved by the University of Texas Southwestern Institutional Review Board (STU-2023-0637) and individual informed consent was waived.

Laboratory testing

To assess FVIII activity, we reviewed chromogenic FVIII activity assays, which measure factor Xa generation and is limited to a minimum reported value of 10%, as well as FVIII clot-based assays, which measure PTT-based factor VIII activity and is limited to a minimum reported value of 1%. Detection of FVIII inhibitors was achieved utilizing a chromogenic factor VIII inhibitor assay. Baseline FVIII inhibitor and FVIII activity assays were obtained prior to initiation of emicizumab and immunosuppressive treatment.

Results

Cohort characteristics

Of the 12 identified cases of patients with AHA treated with emicizumab, 11 were newly diagnosed AHA patients initiated on emicizumab at time of hospital admission: 10 patients were initiated while inpatient and 1 as outpatient. Of these 11 patients, 6 (54.5%) patients presented with hematomas, 2 (18.2%) with bruising in extremities, 1 (8.33%) with hemarthrosis, 1 (8.33%) with major bleeding after tonsillectomy, and 1 (8.33%) with neck swelling. The remaining case is a patient with known AHA who was initiated on emicizumab as outpatient without recent hospitalization for acute bleeding. As displayed in the sociodemographic information found in Table 1, there were 5 male and 7 female patients, with median (IQR) age of 73 (60–76) years. Patients had a maximum inhibitor titer of 138 (36.75-164.25) BU/mL and an initial FVIII activity level of < 10% and < 1% on chromogenic and blood clot-based assays, respectively.(Table 1)

Emicizumab dosing patterns and response monitoring

The initial emicizumab dose was 3 mg/kg for 8 patients and 1.5 mg/kg for 4 patients. All patients then continued therapy with emicizumab 1.5 mg/kg for maintenance therapy; 3 patients received emicizumab weekly and 8 received it every other week starting on day 8. The initial and maintenance dosing patterns are summarized in Table 2. Among 6 patients who have completed emicizumab therapy and are currently no longer receiving the medication, patients received a median (IQR) of 4 (2-6.5) emicizumab injections. Patients received a median (IQR) of 4 (3–8) injections of emicizumab and received a total of 66.5 (10.75–102.5) days of emicizumab therapy. One patient passed away after receiving one dose of emicizumab.

Patients who began emicizumab therapy inpatient required a median (IQR) of 8 (6–10) days of bypassing therapy and stopped bypassing therapy 2.5 (1-5.25) days after emicizumab initiation.

In this cohort, patients received a median (IQR) of 4 (2.25–12.5) doses of FEIBA and 28.5 (14.75–31.5) doses of NovoSeven throughout their treatment course. Immunosuppression

Ten patients received initial immunosuppression with prednisone 1 mg/kg/day followed by rituximab. One patient was unable to receive immunosuppression due to ongoing soft tissue infection at time of presentation, and another was unable to tolerate steroid therapy due to history of steroid-induced psychosis. In our cohort, patients received the first dose of emicizumab after a median (IQR) 4 (2.5–26) days after initiation of immunosuppression.

Adverse complications and outpatient utilization of Emicizumab

We note that three patients died due to causes unrelated to thromboembolism or bleeding complications: two patients died from septic shock and one patient died from cardiac arrest. Supplemental Fig. 2 displays the thrombotic risk factors within our study cohort. In total, one patient developed acute pulmonary embolism while on emicizumab whose case is described in detail in Supplemental Fig. 3 (Patient A). Additionally, emicizumab can be initiated safely outpatient to treat rising FVIII inhibitor titers and serve as bleeding prophylaxis. We describe a case highlighting outpatient management of emicizumab therapy in Supplemental Fig. 3 (Patient B).

Discussion

As bypassing agents often require inpatient stay and close monitoring, emicizumab is a promising alternate agent to reduce the need for bypassing therapies in treating of AHA. In our case series, emicizumab reduced the need for bypassing agents such as FEIBA when hemostasis was achieved during the acute management of bleeding. Our cohort of patients received a median of 8 days of bypassing therapy and were able to stop additional bypassing therapy in 2.5 days after emicizumab initiation. While patients continued to require additional doses of NovoSeven after starting emicizumab, we note that emicizumab can help attain hemostasis with signs of clinical improvement of bleeding after a median of 2 days. Emicizumab holds promising prospects as an additional agent in the treatment for patients with AHA long-term.

There is currently no standard loading dosing for the treatment of AHA with emicizumab. Emicizumab functions by bringing factor IXa and factor X closer together to promote factor Xa generation [14]. This circumvents the need for FVIIIa cofactor function. With adequate amount of factor IXa and emicizumab, the IXa/emicizumab complex can be generated to help achieve hemostasis [15]. The dosing of emicizumab needed to achieve an optimal response during the initial and maintenance phase of AHA treatment is an area of active study. Most AHA patients present with acute bleeding complications; achieving therapeutic levels quickly is highly desired. In our cohort, approximately two-thirds of our cohort received an initial emicizumab loading dose of 3 mg/kg, with the other third receiving a 1.5 mg/kg dose. Among both groups of hospitalized patients who received the 3 mg/kg and 1.5 mg/kg dose of emicizumab, the median (IQR) number of days to achieve clinical improvement was 2 (1–3) days. This suggests that both dosages may be comparable in achieving hemostasis.

Other institutions utilize loading doses of 3 mg/kg, with some starting with doses up to 6 mg/kg which is the dose utilized in prior prospective trial studying emicizumab in AHA [16]. A maintenance dose of 1.5 mg/kg per week is likely to maintain a steady state concentration of emicizumab [17]. When deciding the initial dose, patients with multiple co-morbidities may favor starting with the 1.5 mg/kg dose – for instance, one patient in our cohort developed atrial fibrillation while hospitalized, placing him at great thrombotic risk; in this case, a 1.5 mg/kg dose was chosen for him. In future studies, it will be crucial to examine whether there is a difference in 1.5 mg/kg and 3 mg/kg emicizumab loading doses in achieving hemostasis in larger cohorts.

Immunosuppressive treatment (IST) with steroids and cyclophosphamide is often utilized to eradicate the inhibitor and lessen bleeding in patients with AHA. Emicizumab may reduce the need for immunosuppression by effectively achieving hemostasis. Treatment with steroids-based regimens have been shown to reach complete remission in about 5 weeks, while rituximab-based treatments may take twice as long [18]. However, these IST regimens can predispose patients to complications including severe and often fatal infections. Amongst patients with congenital HA, efforts to develop reduced-intensive, risk-stratified immunosuppression among HA patients with inhibitors have also been studied; however, there have not been studies looking into the AHA patient population or been shown to significantly lower infection or mortality risk [19]. Furthermore, treatment with cyclophosphamide can lead to bone marrow suppression and subsequent cytopenias. For example, two patients in our cohort were treated with cyclophosphamide and both developed thrombocytopenia, leading to discontinuation of the medication. As a result of these prior findings, emicizumab has been studied as an alternative to aggressive IST and has been shown to reduce bleeding time, lower frequency of fatal infections, and improve overall survival [20].

Though IST can prevent recurrent bleeding and promote remission in AHA, its known side effects such as increased infection risk and cytopenias may limit its utility. In patients like Patient B, emicizumab may also be reasonable to start for hemostatic control among those who are unable to tolerate immunosuppressive therapy otherwise. In our cohort, all 11 hospitalized patients all received some form of immunosuppression during their initial inpatient stay. This limitation does not allow us to determine if emicizumab alone can significantly diminish bleeding risk. As a result, dedicated projects such as the Emicizumab in Patients With Acquired Hemophilia A trial, which analyzes outcomes of patients treated with emicizumab and initial IST, and related studies will be important to holistically assess the role of emicizumab as an adjunct to IST or steroid-sparing agent [12, 13]. The CTH-AHA-EMI trial, which examines the use of emicizumab without upfront IST, has shown reduced bleeding rate in patients with AHA treated with emicizumab without IST for in the 12 weeks after initial AHA diagnosis, for instance [13]. Taken together, further research and prospective studies as such are important to evaluate the role of emicizumab in reducing the need for immunosuppression in AHA treatment.

Nevertheless, emicizumab use in AHA may warrant monitoring for thromboembolic complications. Our cohort included patients with existing risk factors for thromboembolism development, including advanced age, active cigarette smoking, obesity, and active malignancy, as shown in Supplemental Fig. 2. One patient in our cohort had history of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. This patient did not experience any new thrombotic events after emicizumab initiation. Furthermore, in the case of Patient A, we illustrate a possible increased risk of thrombosis with initiating emicizumab, which has an elimination half-life of 28 days [21]. Specifically, Patient A received an initial 3 mg/kg of emicizumab the first week followed by a maintenance dose of 1.5 mg/kg of emicizumab weekly and developed a deep vein thrombosis and a pulmonary embolism on this regimen. Prior real-world studies have also found a higher reporting rate of thrombotic adverse events with emicizumab treatment when compared to other FVIII products [22]. In terms of emicizumab dosages associated with thrombotic events, other case reports noted thrombotic events in patients who were dosed up to cumulative 9 mg/kg weekly dose of emicizumab initially followed by a maintenance dose of 1.5 mg/kg weekly [22]. During analysis of the AGEHA study, one patient was also noted to have an asymptomatic deep vein thrombosis while receiving emicizumab, with a standard dosing schedule of 6 mg/kg on the first day, 3 mg/kg on the second day, followed by 1.5 mg/kg once weekly from the eighth day onward [17]. Patient A’s continuation of emicizumab despite rapidly rising factor VIII activity may have predisposed her risk to thrombotic events as well. It may be reasonable to have close monitoring of factor VIII activity when the factor VIII inhibitor titer decreases significantly. This way, emicizumab can be discontinued before factor VIII activity returns to normal levels. It remains unclear how elevated loading doses or weekly maintenance doses may impact the risk of thrombotic events.

As in the case of Patient A, a rapid rise or sudden increase in factor VIII activity may be a sign to consider withholding emicizumab. There have been studies suggesting that FVIII activity levels approaching 50% or higher may reduce risk of bleeding in AHA. We suggest, in patients with FVIII of 30 to 50%, providers should consider holding emicizumab to avoid increased risk of thromboembolism formation especially in patients with other risk factors such as impaired mobility or active malignancy. For those with FVIII of > 50%, emicizumab should be held if the patient is clinically stable without any signs of bleeding. Based on our clinical experience, we recommend close monitoring of the factor VIII activity by chromogenic method when the inhibitor titer reduces to 1 BU or less to decide whether to hold the emicizumab in a timely fashion.

There are several limitations to our study. This is a retrospective study with small sample sizes that limits the generalizability of our findings and ability to detect differences during analysis. We were unable to perform comparative statistical analyses due to the sample size. One major limitation is the use of bypassing agents and/or IST with emicizumab in the treatment of AHA for each of the patients reported, limiting our ability to tease out the individual impact of emicizumab. Additionally, our study included patients treated in variable settings (both inpatient and outpatient) with different emicizumab dosing patterns. As a result, we are limited in our ability to draw any definitive conclusions regarding emicizumab itself as a treatment regimen. Additionally, there may be a degree of selection bias as emicizumab is an expensive drug used off-label in AHA treatment, which may prevent patients who are uninsured from receiving the medication. However, uninsured patients who are eligible for the safety-net hospital financial assistance program are able to receive coverage for healthcare, including treatment with emicizumab. Certainly, future prospective studies aimed to further study the use of emicizumab on broader patient populations is crucial.

In conclusion, patients with AHA face the risk of fatal bleeding that often necessitates initiation of bypassing agents and intense immunosuppressing therapy which can both incur great financial costs and increases risk of fatal infections. Emicizumab is an alternative to these approaches given its ability to limit the amount of bypassing therapy needed. We highlight the ability of emicizumab to quickly achieve clinical improvement in bleeding, initiation both in the inpatient and outpatient settings, and comparable efficacy to current standard of treatment. However, we also note the importance of continued monitoring for thromboembolic complications when on therapy. Further studies should be conducted to best understand the optimal dose schedule for treatment of AHA.
