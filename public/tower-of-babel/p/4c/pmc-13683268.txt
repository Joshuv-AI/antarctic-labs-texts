Impact of neck nodal yield on immune checkpoint inhibitor outcome after recurrence in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Dong Hyun Kim, Jiwon Koh, Yoon Kyung Jeon, Kyeong Cheon Jung, Jungirl Seok, Eun-Jae Chung, Seong-Keun Kwon, Soon-Hyun Ahn, Bhumsuk Keam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNeck dissection and adequate lymph node yield (LNY) are important in the treatment of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Cervical lymph nodes represent the primary sites for T-cell priming; however, the impact of LNY on the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) remains unclear.MethodsA total of 120 patients with HNSCC who relapsed after surgical resection and subsequently received ICIs were included. The association of LNY, neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression with ICI outcomes were evaluated.ResultsAmong 120 patients, 31 underwent unilateral, 82 bilateral, and 7 no neck dissection. The median LNY was 26 (mean, 29.9; range, 0–145). PD-L1 expression was evaluated in 80 patients, 69 of whom had combined positive score ≥ 1. The low-LNY group had a higher overall response rate to ICI than the high-LNY group (42.9% vs. 16.9%, P = 0.003). Patients with high LNY had inferior PFS (median, 1.3 vs. 5.9 months; P < 0.001) and OS (median, 9.7 vs. 21.1 months; P = 0.007) compared with those with low LNY. In multivariable analysis, high LNY and high NLR were independent predictors of poor survival, whereas PD-L1 expression was not. Among patients with PD-L1–positive tumors, those with high NLR had inferior survivals compared with those with low NLR.ConclusionsExtensive lymphatic ablation, as reflected by high LNY, may be associated with diminished ICI efficacy in relapsed HNSCC. These findings suggest that LNY warrants further investigation as a potential biomarker for ICI outcomes in patients with post-surgical recurrent HNSCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00262-026-04504-x.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) arises from the mucosal epithelium of the oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx and represents a major global health burden [1]. Curative potential can be achieved with complete surgical resection with neck dissection; however, many patients subsequently experience locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis [2, 3]. The prognosis of patients with recurrent or metastatic (R/M) HNSCC remains poor, although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as a promising therapeutic option [4]. Low response rates and the lack of robust predictive biomarkers remain unmet clinical needs.

Cervical lymph node ablation, via neck dissection or irradiation, is critical in HNSCC given the high risk of occult nodal metastases and its role in locoregional disease control [5]. A lower lymph node yield (LNY) after neck dissection has been associated with inferior survival, even in pathologic N0 disease [6]. However, cervical lymph nodes are central to HNSCC biology, serving as the primary site for tumor antigen drainage and T-cell priming [7]. Additionally, a recent study demonstrated that ablation of tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) repolarizes the tumor microenvironment and abolishes the ICI response [8]. Given that ICIs enhance antitumor immunity by relieving T-cell inhibition, prior extensive neck dissection with a high LNY may impair host immune competence and attenuate the response to ICI therapy [9]. Nevertheless, the predictive role of LNY for ICI efficacy in R/M HNSCC remains unclear.

This study aimed to assess the impact of LNY on ICI treatment outcomes after recurrence in patients with R/M HNSCC. We hypothesized that extensive neck dissection or a high LNY may adversely affect the efficacy of ICI therapy in the post-recurrence setting. In addition, the predictive value of pre-ICI neutrophil–lymphocyte ratio (NLR), a blood biomarker reflecting lymphocyte counts, along with LNY, was investigated.

Methods

Participants and study design

Patients with histologically confirmed, R/M HNSCC involving the oral cavity, oropharynx, hypopharynx, or larynx who received ICIs at Seoul National University Hospital between August 2014 and December 2025 were included. Patients who initially underwent curative-intent surgical resection with or without neck dissection for primary HNSCC and subsequently developed locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis followed by ICI treatment were eligible for the study. Eligible patients were aged ≥ 18 years and had undergone response evaluation. Patients with nasopharyngeal or nasal cavity cancer, those without prior curative-intent surgery, or those with missing key data including pathology reports were excluded. Three pathologists (JK, YKJ, and KCJ) independently reviewed all histopathological slides to confirm the diagnosis.

A total of 175 patients were initially identified. Among these, 13 patients had nasal cavity cancer, 30 had not undergone surgical resection, 9 lacked pathology reports, and 3 were lost to follow-up without response evaluation after ICI treatment. Consequently, the final study cohort comprised 120 patients (Figure S1). Patients were stratified into high and low LNY groups, and treatment outcomes were compared between the groups.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB no. H-2602-040-1716). The informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Data collection and outcomes

Clinical and pathological variables, including age, tumor location, ICI types, and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, were collected from electronic medical records. Surgical and pathology reports were reviewed to quantify laterality and nodal yield. PD-L1 expression was assessed using the standardized 22C3 pharmDx assay on the Dako Link 48 platform (Dako, Carpinteria, CA, USA), and characterized by the combined positive score (CPS). CPS was defined as the number of PD-L1-positive cells (tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages) divided by the total number of tumor cells multiplied by 100 [10]. The CPS scoring was performed by three expert pathologists (JK, JYK, and JKC) who were blinded to patients’ responses to ICIs. Human papillomavirus testing was conducted in patients with oropharyngeal cancer using p16 immunohistochemistry. Peripheral blood data obtained on cycle 1, day 1 of ICI therapy were collected to calculate the NLR. The NLR was derived as the ratio of the absolute neutrophil count to the absolute lymphocyte count in peripheral blood. The cutoff value for NLR was set at 5.1, based on a previous study [11]. Tumor response was evaluated using RECIST version 1.1. Follow-up radiologic assessments were generally performed every 2–3 months after initiation of ICI therapy, according to routine clinical practice. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from ICI administration to disease progression or death of any cause. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from ICI administration to death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

Differences between the groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables, as appropriate. The Clopper-Pearson method was used for calculating the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the response rate. To define the cutoff values for LNY, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted. The results are presented as the area under the curve (AUC), and the Youden Index method was used to define the optimal cutoff level. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the log-rank test was used to assess the differences between groups. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed for overall response, with results reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Survival outcomes were evaluated using the Cox proportional hazards model, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs were calculated. The statistical software “R” version 4.4.1 (www.r-project.org) was used for all statistical analyses. P-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and disease characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 120 patients are presented in Table 1. The median age was 62 years (range, 23–87), and 91 patients (75.8%) were male. Oral cavity was the most common site (64.2%), followed by oropharynx (16.7%). Among 20 patients with oropharyngeal cancer, 16 (80.0%) were p16-positive. PD-L1 expression was assessed in 80 patients; among them, 69 had a CPS ≥ 1, and 50 had a CPS ≥ 20. The majority of patients (92.5%) received either PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors as ICI therapy, while the remaining 7.5% were treated with a combination of PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors. Lymph node dissection and ICI treatment outcomes

Among 120 patients, 7 did not undergo neck dissection, 82 underwent unilateral neck dissection, and 31 underwent bilateral neck dissection. Overall, the median LNY was 26 (mean, 29.9; range 0–145). Patients who underwent bilateral neck dissection had significantly higher LNY compared with those who received unilateral or no neck dissection (median, 47 vs. 21; P < 0.001; Table S1). In total, 86 patients received adjuvant therapy, including chemotherapy (n = 7), postoperative radiotherapy (n = 37), or concurrent chemoradiotherapy (n = 42). The high-LNY group more frequently received adjuvant treatment than the low-LNY group (81.7% vs. 57.1%, P = 0.006).

All patients relapsed at a median of 6.1 months (range, 0.7–141.5), and subsequently received ICI treatment at a median of 2.9 months (range, 0.1–62.1) after relapse. The best response to ICI was complete response in 6 patients (5.0%), partial response in 27 patients (22.5%), stable disease in 22 patients (18.3%), and progressive disease in 65 patients (54.2%). The overall response rate was 27.5% (95% CI, 19.7–36.4), and the disease control rate was 45.8% (95% CI, 36.7–55.2). No significant differences in CPS were observed according to best response or objective response (Figure S2).

Impact of lymph node yield on ICI response

The optimal cutoff level for LNY to predict overall response was determined using ROC curve analysis (Figure S3). The LNY cutoff value was 22, and the AUC of the ROC curve was 0.616. Using a threshold of LNY 22, 71 patients were classified into the high-LNY group and 49 into the low-LNY group. No significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between groups, except for LD laterality and line of ICI treatment (Table S2).

The low-LNY group showed a significantly higher overall response rate compared with the high-LNY group (42.9% vs. 16.9%, P = 0.003; Fig. 1). Complete response was observed in 4 (8.2%) and 2 (2.8%) patients in the low-LNY and high-LNY groups, respectively. The low-LNY group exhibited a significantly higher disease control rate compared with the high-LNY group (69.4% vs. 29.6%, P < 0.001). Among responders, there were no differences in time to response (median, 2.1 vs. 2.1 months, P = 0.881) or duration of response (median, 16.5 vs. 28.1 months, P = 0.860; Figure S4) between the low- and high-LNY groups.Fig. 1Response to immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment by lymph node yield

Impact of lymph node yield on survival outcomes

With a median follow-up duration of 26.6 months (95% CI, 16.6–51.4), the median PFS and OS were 1.9 months (95% CI, 1.6–3.6 months) and 11.9 months (95% CI, 9.7–19.6 months), respectively. Patients in the low-LNY group had longer PFS compared with the high-LNY group (median, 5.9 vs. 1.3 months; P < 0.001; Fig. 2A). Similarly, OS was significantly longer in the low-LNY group than in the high-LNY group (median, 21.1 vs. 9.7 months; P = 0.007; Fig. 2B). Additional stratification of LNY-defined groups according to NLR or PD-L1 expression

As an exploratory analysis, survival outcomes were further evaluated after stratification of the LNY-defined groups by either NLR or PD-L1 expression (Fig. 3). For the combined analysis of LNY and NLR, patients were categorized into three groups. The class 1 subgroup (low LNY and low NLR; n = 32) demonstrated the highest PFS, whereas the class 3 subgroup (high LNY and high NLR; n = 29) exhibited the poorest PFS (median, 7.5 vs. 1.7 vs. 1.2 months; Fig. 3A). Next, an exploratory combined analysis of LNY and PD-L1 expression was performed in the 80 patients with available PD-L1 CPS data. Given that most patients had PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1, patients were stratified into three groups based on PD-L1 status and LNY: PD-L1–negative (n = 11), PD-L1–positive with low LNY (n = 30), and PD-L1–positive with high LNY (n = 39). PFS was most favorable in patients with PD-L1–positive tumors and low NLR, followed by PD-L1–negative patients, and was poorest in those with PD-L1–positive tumors and high NLR (median, 8.4 vs. 3.5 vs. 1.2 months; Fig. 3C). Patients with PD-L1–positive tumors and high NLR had significantly worse PFS compared with those with low NLR (P < 0.001) and PD-L1–negative patients (P = 0.038). Median OS was not reached in patients with PD-L1–positive tumors and low NLR and was longer than in those with PD-L1–positive tumors and high NLR (10.1 months, P = 0.067) and PD-L1–negative patients (11.4 months, P = 0.043) (Fig. 3D). There was no significant difference in OS between patients with PD-L1–positive tumors and high NLR and PD-L1–negative patients (P = 0.980). Similar results were observed when a CPS cutoff of 20 was applied (Figure S8).

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact of prior LNY on the efficacy of ICI after recurrence in patients with R/M HNSCC. Bilateral neck dissection was not associated with ICI outcomes; however, a high LNY was associated with lower response rates of ICI and inferior survival outcomes. Multivariate Cox analysis confirmed that high LNY and NLR were independently associated with worse PFS and OS. In addition, patients with PD-L1 CPS could be further stratified by LNY. Among these patients, those with high LNY exhibited worse PFS and OS compared with those with low LNY, and also showed inferior PFS compared with patients with PD-L1–negative tumors.

Emerging clinical data on the impact of neck lymphatic ablation—including neck dissection and radiotherapy—on ICI efficacy in R/M HNSCC remain heterogeneous. Recently, Iwasa et al. reported findings consistent with ours; prior neck dissection was associated with inferior survival in patients treated with nivolumab, suggesting that disruption of TDLNs may impair therapeutic response to ICIs [12] . Similarly, prior high-dose radiotherapy has been identified as predictors of early progression following ICI therapy [13]. Conversely, other large retrospective studies have reported neutral effects of neck dissection on outcomes, likely reflecting confounding by disease burden, and nodal stage [14, 15]. In this context, our findings provide an important refinement by introducing LNY as a quantitative surrogate of lymphatic ablation. Unlike a binary classification of neck dissection, LNY may serve as a surrogate marker associated with the degree of prior lymphatic intervention and disruption of regional immune architecture. The inverse association between LNY and ICI efficacy is consistent with the hypothesis that more extensive lymphatic clearance may be associated with impaired antitumor immunity, although causality cannot be established in this retrospective study. In addition, these findings should be interpreted with caution. As our cohort included only patients who recurred after curative-intent resection, they do not imply that LNY should be reduced at surgery. Ablation of regional lymph nodes remains a cornerstone of standard care for resectable HNSCC [16], and higher LNY is associated with improved survival, likely through better locoregional control [17]. Accordingly, current guidelines recommend adequate, and when appropriate, bilateral neck dissection [18]. Rather, our findings may be relevant in the recurrent setting, where LNY could serve as a potential biomarker to inform prognosis and treatment decisions in patients receiving ICIs.

While maintenance immunotherapy following definitive local therapy has demonstrated survival benefit in other malignancies [19], the negative results of phase III IMvoke010 trial highlight a potential tumor-specific vulnerability to disruption of tumor-draining lymph nodes in HNSCC [20]. In addition, several phase III trials evaluating the concurrent addition of ICIs to definitive chemoradiotherapy in locally advanced HNSCC have yielded negative results. The KEYNOTE-412 trial failed to demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in event-free survival with pembrolizumab added to chemoradiotherapy [21]. Similarly, the JAVELIN Head and Neck 100 and NRG-HN004 trials evaluating avelumab in combination with chemoradiotherapy did not meet its primary endpoint [22]. NRG-HN004 trial also failed to show PFS improvement when durvalumab was delivered concurrently with radiotherapy [23]. These failures, together with preclinical data, have prompted reconsideration of treatment sequencing toward lymphatic-preserving strategies, in which ICIs are administered prior to cervical lymph node ablation. Saddawi-Konefka et al. demonstrated in murine models that neoadjuvant ICI induces durable antitumor immunity, as evidenced by sustained rejection upon tumor rechallenge even after subsequent lymphatic ablation, suggesting that effective immune priming within cervical lymph nodes can generate long-lasting systemic immunity [8]. Early-phase studies have shown encouraging pathologic responses with neoadjuvant ICI therapy in resectable HNSCC [24], and recently, perioperative approaches incorporating both neoadjuvant and adjuvant immunotherapy have demonstrated event-free survival benefit in phase III KEYNOTE-689 study [25]. Collectively, these findings raise the possibility that initiating ICI in the presence of intact TDLN architecture may enhance systemic antitumor immunity. Our findings are also consistent with the hypothesis that higher LNY may be associated with reduced immune priming and less favorable ICI outcomes.

While cervical lymph nodes serve as key sites for T-cell priming, PD-L1 represents an immunologic target, whereas the NLR serves as a surrogate marker of systemic immune status [26]. Although PD-L1 expression is an established biomarker for ICI response in HNSCC [27, 28], there remains room for further improvement in biomarker-driven patient selection [29, 30]. This may be related to the high proportion of patients with CPS ≥ 1 in our R/M HNSCC cohort, and suggests the need for additional biomarker-driven stratification among PD-L1–positive patients. In our study, patients with PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 and high LNY showed shorter PFS compared with PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1 and low LNY, and even compared with patients with PD-L1 CPS < 1. These results suggest that LNY may provide complementary information beyond established biomarkers and may help identify subgroups of patients who are more likely to benefit from ICI, particularly within PD-L1–positive populations where predictive discrimination is otherwise limited. In addition, patients with low LNY and low NLR had the best outcomes, whereas those with high LNY and high NLR had the worst survival, suggesting an interaction between locoregional immunity and systemic inflammation. Therefore, integrating LNY with systemic and tumor-specific immune markers may offer a more comprehensive framework for risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making in R/M HNSCC.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, it is subject to potential selection bias, and the timing of ICI initiation after recurrence was heterogeneous. In addition, detailed data on baseline stage and pathological risk factors at the time of initial surgery were limited. Although our analysis focused on ICI outcomes in the recurrent/metastatic setting, patients with high LNY may have had more advanced disease or adverse pathological features at baseline, which could have influenced subsequent treatment outcomes. Therefore, validation in external cohorts is warranted. Second, curative-intent surgical procedures were not standardized, and detailed information on LNY by neck level was unavailable, precluding further subgroup analyses. Third, detailed data on adjuvant therapy or prior radiotherapy were unavailable. Although prior neck radiotherapy was not associated with ICI efficacy in multivariable analysis, radiation dose was not accounted for.

Despite these limitations, our study is the first to evaluate the predictive value of LNY for ICI efficacy in a relatively large cohort of patients with R/M HNSCC. We comprehensively assessed three ICI-related factors—LNY, NLR, and PD-L1 expression—and analyzed their associations with treatment outcomes. Furthermore, exploratory analyses suggested that the prognostic impact of LNY could be further stratified according to NLR or PD-L1 expression.

In conclusion, this study suggests that high LNY, rather than bilateral neck dissection alone, is associated with reduced ICI efficacy after relapse in HNSCC. LNY is a surgery-related variable independent of PD-L1 CPS and NLR, and may provide additional value in informing ICI treatment decisions. For example, in cases of postoperative recurrence with a high LNY, ICI monotherapy often shows a poor response; therefore, pembrolizumab + chemotherapy or EXTREME regimen may be considered. Considering either PD-L1 expression or NLR in conjunction with LNY may improve the assessment of ICI outcomes. Our findings support the role of ICIs in the neoadjuvant setting rather than the adjuvant setting. Further studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs are warranted to validate the predictive role of LNY for ICIs in R/M HNSCC.
