The positive impact of a multidisciplinary preoperative diabetes clinic on improving glycaemic management in adults undergoing elective noncardiac surgery
Authors: Abby Yu, Karen Whitfield, Andrew Hale, Emma Ballard, Michael d'Emden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo investigate the impact of a multidisciplinary preoperative diabetes clinic on glycaemic and clinical outcomes in adults undergoing elective noncardiac surgery.MethodsSeventy‐nine adults scheduled for elective noncardiac surgery with haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels ≥64 mmol/mol (8.0%) were prospectively recruited. Participants received regular preoperative consultations with a pharmacist, diabetes nurse and diabetologist. Changes in HbA1c and serum fructosamine levels between baseline and the day of surgery were compared using a paired t‐test and linear regression.ResultsMean HbA1c at baseline was 83 mmol/mol (9.7%). There was a significant reduction in HbA1c levels measured on the day of surgery, with mean HbA1c being 63 mmol/mol (7.9%), equating to a mean reduction of −21 mmol/mol (95% CI −24, −16, p < 0.001). Linear regression analysis identified a significant negative relationship between baseline HbA1c and change in HbA1c, whereby each 1% increase in baseline HbA1c predicted a −0.759% decrease in day of surgery HbA1c. Median baseline fructosamine level was 361 μmol/L, and the median reduction on the day of surgery was −53 μmol/L (IQR −87 to −26 μmol/L, p < 0.001). Fructosamine levels measured 4 weeks postsurgery showed no significant change compared with the day of surgery. Median length of stay was 2 days (IQR 1–5), and there was a low incidence of in‐hospital infections and readmissions in the cohort.ConclusionsThe multidisciplinary clinic was effective in improving participants' glycaemic control before elective noncardiac surgery. Individuals with the highest baseline HbA1c levels had the greatest preoperative improvement. This study highlights the meaningful impact on glycaemic management that can be achieved in the preoperative period. INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of diabetes is increasing globally, affecting 1 in 10 adults. 1 Diabetes leads to an increased need for surgery due to complications like retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy and cardiovascular disease. 2 , 3 Perioperative hyperglycaemia is strongly linked to postoperative complications such as surgical site infections (SSI), acute kidney injury, myocardial infarction, respiratory infections and increased hospital length of stay. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Incremental postoperative blood glucose levels (BGLs) as well as levels above 11.1 mmol/L have been shown to increase SSI two to threefold, 4 , 5 , 8 with hyperglycaemia within the first 48 h after surgery posing the greatest risk for infection. 4 , 6 , 8

Elevated preoperative haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and preoperative BGLs above 10 mmol/L correlate with elevated postoperative glucose levels. 9 , 10 , 11 Studies have also found that each 1% increase in preoperative HbA1c is independently associated with an increase in major complications, intensive care admissions, acute kidney injury, surgical site infections and hospital length of stay. 12 , 13 International guidelines now recommend a preoperative HbA1c target of less than 64 to 69 mmol/mol (8.0%–8.5%) to mitigate the risk of postoperative complications. 14 , 15

Recent studies have also highlighted serum fructosamine as a useful marker for perioperative glycaemic management, by providing a more sensitive indicator of acute changes in BGLs compared with HbA1c. 16 , 17 Studies on people undergoing orthopaedic surgery have found that higher serum fructosamine levels are associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications. 16 , 17

To evaluate the potential role of improved preoperative glycaemic management on postoperative outcomes, a multidisciplinary preoperative diabetes clinic (MPDC) was established at the Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital (RBWH) in 2021. This clinic, the first of its kind in Australia, 18 offers a dedicated preoperative service for people with suboptimally managed diabetes who are scheduled to undergo elective surgery. The clinic, staffed by a pharmacist, diabetes nurse and diabetologist, runs twice a week, providing timely access to specialist diabetes care. It collaborates with surgeons, anaesthetists, perioperative physicians and external healthcare providers to ensure coordinated management.

The primary aim of this study was to investigate the impact of the multidisciplinary preoperative diabetes clinic on perioperative glycaemic outcomes in adults undergoing elective noncardiac surgery. The major secondary aim was to examine surgical outcomes in these patients.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This was a prospective interventional study conducted at the MPDC at RBWH. The RBWH is the largest hospital in Australia, performing over 27,500 surgeries per year. 19 The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Royal Brisbane & Women's Hospital Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC/2021/QRBW/74056).

Eligibility criteria

Adults with known diabetes and planned elective surgery, who were referred to the MPDC, were invited to participate. Participants were eligible for recruitment if they were aged ≥18 years, had a HbA1c level ≥64 mmol/mol (8.0%) within 3 months of referral and were scheduled for elective noncardiac surgery at the RBWH. Participants were excluded if they were pregnant or having bariatric surgery, as these cohorts were managed by other multidisciplinary services. Participants were also excluded if they were unable to provide written informed consent. If participants had more than one surgical procedure during the study period, only the first was examined.

Study procedures

Participants were recruited during their initial clinic consultation. Study investigators provided verbal and written information, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

During the initial consultation, a comprehensive medical history was taken, focusing on diabetes history and related complications, self monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) frequency, hypoglycaemia awareness and management, and access to specialist care. Medication regimens and self management skills were assessed, and relevant education was provided. Participants were encouraged to self monitor BGLs, and up to two Freestyle Libre 2 continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) sensors (Abbott Diabetes Care Inc., Alameda, CA, USA) were provided to those with compatible smartphones to assist with titration of pharmacotherapy. Participants typically received one sensor when they commenced with the clinic to assess glycaemic patterns and another closer to their scheduled surgery to optimise therapy and stabilise their glucose levels.

Figure S1 summarises the clinic process. Participants had regular consultations with the pharmacist and diabetes nurse to monitor their progress, adjust medication doses and discuss treatment concerns. The diabetes nurse had undergone local credentialing to enable them to independently provide advice on insulin dose adjustments. The team met weekly to discuss participants' treatment response and make necessary changes. Participants were reviewed by the diabetologist if there was a perceived need for additional pharmacotherapy. Consultation frequency was adapted to individual patient needs and surgery timelines, as surgeries were not postponed to facilitate glycaemic management. Once surgery dates were confirmed, participants were reviewed 1 week prior to surgery and provided with individualised instructions on adjustment of their diabetes medications. The team also communicated with surgical and anaesthetic teams regarding recommendations for perioperative management. Postoperative diabetes management remained the responsibility of the treating surgical teams, with inpatient diabetes services consulted at the surgical unit's discretion.

To assess changes in glycaemic status, HbA1c and serum fructosamine levels were measured at baseline and on the morning of surgery. Fructosamine levels were also measured 4 weeks after surgery to evaluate postoperative glycaemic management. In circumstances where participants' day of surgery HbA1c was not measured or there was less than 1 month from enrolment to surgery, CGM data, if available, were used to derive an equivalent day of surgery HbA1c. This only occurred if the CGM sensor was active for at least 60% of the time, and continuous data were available for the 2 weeks leading up to surgery.

Participant demographics, medical histories, diabetes therapies, surgical procedures, lengths of stay and any postoperative infections were documented from electronic medical records. Capillary blood glucose levels for the day of surgery and postoperative days one to three were noted from inpatient observation charts. It was routine practice for BGLs to be recorded on hospital admission and at least four times per day (pre‐meal and pre‐bed) for individuals with known diabetes. Intraoperative blood glucose levels were assessed at hourly intervals, as well as throughout the administration of insulin‐glucose infusions. All insulin prescriptions and corresponding administration records were retrospectively reviewed to identify any unexplained omissions or discrepancies from participants' prescribed regimens. Participants were followed up 30 days postsurgery to confirm if any SSI or unplanned readmissions occurred postdischarge. Consent was obtained to contact primary care physicians for details as necessary. Participant data were stratified by elective surgery urgency categories for comparison between groups. Outcomes

The primary outcome was a change in preoperative glucose management, defined as mean change in HbA1c and serum fructosamine level measured on the morning of surgery compared with baseline. Secondary outcomes included (1) perioperative glycaemia, defined as mean admission BGL, and mean daily postoperative BGL for the day of surgery and postoperative days one to three, (2) overall postoperative glycaemia, defined as serum fructosamine level measured 4 weeks after surgery, and (3) hospital‐acquired infections, including in‐hospital urinary tract infections (UTI), pneumonia, bloodstream infections and surgical site infections (SSI) or unplanned hospital readmissions within 30 days of surgery.

Statistical analyses

An internal pilot study with 10 participants from each elective surgery urgency category (n = 30) was conducted to determine sample size. The sample size calculation is detailed in Figure S2. Planned recruitment included 14 patients per surgery category, in addition to the 10 per category from the pilot.

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS® version 29 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). The comparison of interest was between the three elective surgery categories. Continuous variables were summarised as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables were reported as frequency and percentage. A Pearson Chi‐squared test was used to examine categorical variables. A Student t‐test was used to examine continuous variables or a Mann–Whitney U test for non‐normally distributed data. A paired t‐test was used to compare changes between baseline and day of surgery, baseline and 4 weeks postsurgery, and day of surgery and 4 weeks postsurgery, with the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test used for non‐normally distributed differences.

Linear regression was used to analyse changes in HbA1c/fructosamine from baseline to the day of surgery, considering baseline levels, elective surgery category, and days from first appointment to surgery and their interaction as main effects. Backwards stepwise regression was used to identify the most parsimonious model, with the final model only containing the baseline level for both outcomes. Residual and leverage plots were examined. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Repeat measures for BGLs from day of surgery to 3 days postsurgery were analysed as daily averages. Data were pooled across surgery categories to explore inpatient management, with sensitivity analysis removing data points with known insulin‐related errors.

Eleven participants (14%) were diagnosed with a suspected SSI within 30 days of surgery (Table S6). All infections were diagnosed postdischarge, and most were superficial infections managed with oral antibiotics prescribed by the participants' primary care physician (n = 9) or surgical outpatient clinic (n = 1). One participant with an SSI required hospital readmission for intravenous antibiotics, whilst another was diagnosed with a suspected UTI as an inpatient. There were no instances of postoperative pneumonia or bloodstream infections. Fourteen participants had unplanned hospital presentations within 30 days of surgery. Seven (8.9%) were treated and discharged from the emergency department, while another seven (8.9%) were readmitted. Only two readmissions (2.5%) were infection‐related. Reasons for emergency department presentations and readmissions are listed in Tables S7 and S8. Median hospital length of stay was 2 days (IQR 1–5) for the entire cohort, with a median of 3 days (IQR 2–5) for category 1 participants and a median of 4 days (IQR 1–7) for category 3 participants (Table S9).

DISCUSSION

This prospective real‐world study demonstrated the positive impact of a dedicated preoperative diabetes service on improving patients' perioperative glycaemic management. The significant reductions in preoperative HbA1c and serum fructosamine achieved are anticipated to translate into improved postoperative BGLs and surgical outcomes. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 The most significant finding was the −0.759% decrease in day of surgery HbA1c that was observed with each 1% increase in baseline HbA1c. A similar relationship was observed with serum fructosamine. Therefore, individuals with the highest HbA1c levels gained the most benefit from preoperative optimisation.

Previous studies in the United States 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 and Canada 27 had found that preoperative diabetes optimisation improved fasting glucose and ‘estimated’ HbA1c levels on the day of surgery. However, most were retrospective studies 23 , 24 , 25 , 27 that compared outcomes between intervention and historical controls 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 or converted day of surgery serum fructosamine into an ‘estimated’ HbA1c level. 22 , 24 Although serum fructosamine is useful for monitoring glycaemic trends, conversion to estimated HbA1c has not been widely validated and requires caution in interpretation, due to increased variability with fructosamine. 28 By contrast, our study was able to prospectively demonstrate improvement in both HbA1c and serum fructosamine following preoperative intervention. A key difference is that we had a multidisciplinary model with dedicated staffing, whereas other studies operated a ‘fast‐track’ referral to existing diabetes services 23 , 25 with a diabetologist only. 23 , 24 The dedicated staffing in our study allowed for more timely and frequent reviews, while the multidisciplinary approach improved clinic efficiency by allowing the diabetologist to focus on complex cases, while the diabetes nurse and pharmacist provided reviews and medication titration. Our study was also the first to use CGM technology to enhance glycaemic assessment and treatment titration. This possibly explains why time spent in the clinic (from enrolment to the day of surgery) was not a significant predictor of HbA1c change. Participants with shorter time frames to surgery were also reviewed more frequently, contributing to this finding.

Our study had comparable 30‐day readmission and postoperative infection rates to other similar studies. Our readmission rate of 8.9% was lower than the 13% 25 and 33% 23 quoted for other optimised cohorts. Although we had a higher incidence of postoperative infections (15%), this included SSI diagnosed in the community, which is not captured in routine hospital surveillance or reported in most studies. When in‐hospital infections were analysed separately, our incidence of SSI and UTI was 1.3% and 1.3%, respectively, which is comparable to that reported by the STOIC‐D Surgery trial. 29 This trial, which compared early postoperative specialist‐led diabetes consultation with standard care, reported an SSI incidence of 1.2% and UTI incidence of 1.0% in the intervention arm.

Notably, the mean HbA1c of the STOIC‐D intervention arm was 58 mmol/mol (7.5%), which was lower than the baseline HbA1c of 83 mmol/mol (9.7%) in our study. Previous randomised controlled trials have shown that HbA1c on admission predicted response to basal‐bolus insulin during hospitalisation, with higher levels linked to lower odds of achieving optimal glycaemia despite insulin adjustments. 10 As basal‐bolus insulin was the primary treatment for postoperative hyperglycaemia in the STOIC‐D trial, 29 it is possible that postoperative management alone may not achieve optimal glycaemia in people with elevated preoperative HbA1c levels. This suggests that both preoperative optimisation and early postoperative intervention are important to improve glucose management and surgical outcomes. Providing only postoperative intervention is also less likely to affect long‐term glucose management, particularly as no diabetes education was undertaken in the STOIC‐D trial. 29 Conversely, we observed negligible change in fructosamine levels between the day of surgery and 4 weeks postsurgery, indicating that preoperative improvements to glycaemic management were sustained. This was likely due to the extensive diabetes education provided to participants preoperatively.

Our study has several implications for perioperative diabetes management. Firstly, it highlights the preoperative period as an ideal time for improving glucose management, as surgery motivates patients to address lifestyle issues affecting their diabetes. With multidisciplinary team support, over 90% of our participants reduced their preoperative HbA1c levels and improvements in glucose management were sustained postoperatively. However, poor inpatient management, especially errors with prescribing and administration of insulin, affected postoperative glucose levels during their inpatient stay. This emphasises the need for integrated preoperative optimisation and proactive postoperative management by an inpatient diabetes team.

This study has several limitations. Conducted in a large academic hospital with multidisciplinary teams, the study's generalisability to smaller institutions is unknown. Potential biases include selection bias from more motivated individuals being more likely to consent to study participation, nonrandomised design and lack of a comparator arm. However, as it was unethical to deny people with poorly managed diabetes access to specialist diabetes care, it was felt that participants' baseline data would form an adequate control for comparison. Consideration was given to using historical data to form a control group for comparison; however, there were significant difficulties in creating a representative matched control group, given the lack of historical preoperative HbA1c measurements available. There were also a few participants who met the eligibility criteria for recruitment but had a delay in getting their baseline HbA1c and fructosamine levels measured. During this delay, some participants demonstrated a reduction in HbA1c to below 64 mmol/mol (8.0%). These individuals were retained in the study due to the presence of clinically significant postprandial hyperglycaemia, which is not fully reflected in the HbA1c values. Their inclusion in the study was considered appropriate given the ongoing need for preoperative optimisation. Although postoperative infections and readmissions were captured, the study was not powered for these secondary outcomes. Delays to elective surgeries during the COVID‐19 pandemic increased long‐wait participant dropouts due to disease progression, contributing to a smaller than expected sample size. The global shortage of glucagon peptide‐1 receptor agonists also impacted on Type 2 diabetes management, possibly attenuating the HbA1c reduction postintervention. There is also a possibility that observed changes to HbA1c were due to other factors affecting the accuracy of HbA1c levels and unrelated to improvements in glycaemic control. These include iron deficiency and Vitamin B12 deficiency, which may falsely elevate baseline HbA1c levels, and blood loss, low haemoglobin levels and chronic kidney disease, which may falsely lower day of surgery HbA1c levels. We attempted to address these confounders in the study by measuring both HbA1c and fructosamine levels, as well as using continuous glucose monitoring. Possible confounders affecting the incidence of postoperative infections were inpatient insulin errors and the subjective nature of SSI diagnoses. Most suspected postdischarge infections were not verified with diagnostic imaging or wound cultures, and antibiotics were sometimes prescribed based on redness or tenderness alone. Notably, participants' primary care physicians were unaware of the study; therefore, avoiding bias. Inpatient insulin errors may also have adversely affected participants' postoperative glucose levels, leading to hyperglycaemia and increased likelihood of SSI beyond what would have been expected in the absence of such errors. Although the results of our sensitivity analysis were nonsignificant, the detrimental impact of inpatient insulin errors on glycaemic outcomes is well‐described in literature and warrants significant attention. Lastly, although CGM was used in the clinic, it was not included in the formal analysis as not all participants could access it due to device compatibility issues or inability to manage the technology. It was also recognised that while there were limitations in using CGM data to derive an equivalent day of surgery HbA1c for some participants, research supports a strong correlation between CGM‐derived time in range and laboratory measured HbA1c. 30

In conclusion, the multidisciplinary preoperative diabetes clinic was effective in improving glycaemic management prior to elective noncardiac surgery. Improvements were sustained in the postoperative period, indicating a benefit to participants' overall diabetes self management. Participants with the highest baseline HbA1c levels had the greatest improvement, thereby identifying a key target population. An economic analysis is underway to assess the cost‐effectiveness of this model of care. Future research should explore the impact of preoperative optimisation and early postoperative management by an inpatient diabetes team, longer‐term impacts on patients' glycaemic management and the use of continuous glucose monitoring in the perioperative setting.
