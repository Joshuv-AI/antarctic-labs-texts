The interplay of night eating syndrome with resting metabolic rate, anthropometric measures, and sleep quality in normal-weight female university students: a case–control study
Authors: Serkan Aslan, Saniye Sozlu, Osman Bozkurt, Ayşe Çamli, Merve Arslan-Tanis, Betul Kocaadam-Bozkurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsNight eating syndrome (NES) is characterized by food consumption during bedtime or late night. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between NES and resting metabolic rate (RMR), anthropometric measurements, and sleep quality.MethodsThe study employed a case–control design to compare individuals with NES and healthy controls in terms of RMR, anthropometric parameters, and sleep quality. The study was conducted with 64 female university students aged ≥ 18 years with BMI in the normal range (18.5–24.9 kg/m²), including an NES group (n = 32) and a control group (n = 32). NES was assessed using the Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ). RMR was measured using the COSMED Fitmate PRO. Sleep disturbance was assessed using the Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS).ResultsNo significant difference was found between the groups in terms of RMR (p > 0.05). The relation of the NEQ score on body fat mass was not significant, but a significant negative correlation was found between the NEQ score and BMI (p < 0.05). JSS scores were higher in individuals with NES and were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). A significant difference was found between the groups in terms of the habit of consuming three main meals (p < 0.05).ConclusionAlthough sleep disturbances were more common in individuals with NES in our study, no significant difference in metabolic rate was found between the two groups. This is the first study to examine metabolic rate in healthy young adults with NES using indirect calorimetry, and further research is needed in this area.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01580-3.

Introduction

Night eating syndrome (NES) was first discovered by Stunkard and colleagues in patients with living obesity. NES is characterized by recurrent nighttime eating behavior after dinner [1, 2]. There are many studies on the prevalence of NES. The results vary from country to country, and there are uncertainties. In the United States, the prevalence of NES was 4.2% in young adults, 0.6% in Korea, 1.1% in Germany, and 9.5% in university students in Turkey [3–6]. A large-scale meta-analysis on the prevalence of NES found the prevalence to be 7.2% in women (7.2%) and 4.9% in men [7]. The etiology of NES is multidimensional and interacting. Factors such as circadian rhythm, personality traits, hormonal changes, and psychological problems are the underlying causes. The comorbidity with psychiatric illnesses and medical conditions creates difficulties in the diagnosis and treatment of NES [8].

Because NES is an eating behavior that occurs during sleep, there appears to be a direct relationship between sleep quality and NES [9]. Metabolic rate decreases during sleep. Furthermore, the deep sleep phase, which occurs later in sleep, has been shown to cause slight fluctuations in energy metabolism [10]. Studies have also shown a relationship between insufficient sleep duration and obesity [11, 12]. Restricted sleep can affect energy metabolism and increase the amount and frequency of eating. Furthermore, sleep duration and eating patterns can affect hormones such as leptin and ghrelin [13].

Resting metabolic rate (RMR) accounts for 60–70% of adults’ daily energy expenditure [14]. Many factors influence RMR. Various parameters, including race, age, body weight, height, gender, fat-free mass (FFM), menstruation, chronic diseases, and even sun exposure, can influence RMR [15, 16]. One study found a positive association between a higher night eating syndrome score and obesity risk in participants [17]. This was attributed to increased consumption of sugary drinks and fast food. However, the effect of night eating syndrome on metabolic rate was not evaluated. A study by Romon et al. investigated the thermic effect of meals consumed at different times of the day. The lowest thermic response was observed in the night meal when comparing morning, afternoon, or night meals with the same macronutrients and calories [18].

Although there are many studies in the literature on RMR and sleep disturbance, there is no study on the effect of NES on RMR [19–21]. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between NES and RMR, anthropometric measurements, and sleep quality in young adults.

Methods

This study was conducted with students from the Faculty of Health Sciences at Erzurum Technical University. We conducted the research in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and ethical approval from the Erzurum Technical University Ethics Committee, meeting number 06/13. The study sample consisted of volunteer adult females over the age of 18. Participants who had diabetes, hypertension, thyroid, cardiovascular, or lung disease, who were actively menstruating at the time of assessment, having the flu, smoking, alcohol or substance abuse, whose body mass index was not in the normal class (below 18.5 and above 24.9), and who had a high physical activity level were excluded from the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before participation in the study. The protocol of this study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Erzurum Technical University (meeting number 06/13).

Sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.4 software. The study used single regression analysis to test the effect of the Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ) score on body composition, sleep disturbances, physical activity level, and resting metabolic rate. Cohen’s f² standardized effect size was used in the regression analyses. According to Cohen (1988), f² = 0.02 is interpreted as a small effect size, f² = 0.15 as a medium effect size, and f² = 0.35 as a large effect size [22]. In this study, a large effect size (f² = 0.35), a 5% probability of error (α = 0.05), and a 99.7% power (1–β) were used to obtain significant results. Based on the calculations made according to the specified parameters, the minimum sample size was 64. After applying the exclusion criteria, this sample size was achieved, ensuring high statistical power for the study.

The study employed a case–control design to compare individuals with night eating syndrome and healthy controls in terms of resting metabolic rate, anthropometric parameters, and sleep quality. The study consisted of two phases. First, the NEQ was administered to 268 participants, and 41 individuals with night eating syndrome were identified. In the second phase, these 41 individuals were re-invited to the anthropometry laboratory of Erzurum Technical University’s Faculty of Health Sciences for measurements; after excluding those who did not meet the BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) and physical activity level (non-high-active) requirements or did not attend the anthropometry session, 32 participants remained in the night eating syndrome group. For the control group, 48 participants were randomly selected from the remaining 227 individuals without night eating syndrome and invited to the laboratory. After applying the same exclusion criteria, 32 participants constituted the control group (Fig. 1).

Study design diagram

Body weight, body fat mass, visceral fat and skeletal muscle mass were measured with a InBody 770 (Body Composition Analyzer – InBody770, InBody Co., Ltd., Chungcheongnam-do, Korea) device based on the principle of bioelectrical impedance. Height was measured with a Tanita stadiometer, with the subject in an upright position, in the Frankford plane of the head, to a precision of 0.1 cm.

Indirect calorimetry is used as the reference method for determining RMR by measuring O2 consumption and CO2 production [23]. RMR measurements were performed with a COSMED Fitmate PRO device (Cosmed, Rome, Italy), an indirect calorimeter (a metabolic analyzer designed for measuring oxygen consumption and energy expenditure during rest and exercise). RMR measurements were made in a thermoneutral environment (22–24 °C) between 08:00 and 10:00 in the morning after at least 8 h of overnight fasting. Upon arrival at the laboratory, all participants were asked to rest for 15 min in a stationary position. The total RMR measurement time is 15 min. For the first 5 min, the device detects the individual’s breathing pattern; this is the adaptation period for normalization of breathing, and measurements taken during this time are not recorded. After this period is completed, in the remaining 10 min, all respiratory processes are recorded, respiratory parameters are measured, and at the end of the measurement, resting metabolic rate (kcal/day) values are measured. Participants were asked to remain supine and motionless throughout the measurement period. REE measurements were taken at the same time in the morning. For each measurement, the device was automatically calibrated (30 s). Before the measurement, participants were asked to avoid eating a meal at night and engaging in strenuous physical activity (e.g. sports or training).

The Turkish version of the Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS), a 6-point likert-type scale consisting of four items (difficulty falling asleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, waking several times a night, and waking up tired), was used to assess sleep disturbances. The total score ranges from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating greater sleep disturbances [24, 25].

To assess night eating syndrome, participants completed the Turkish version of the Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ), originally developed by Allison et al. and later validated and tested for reliability by Atasoy et al. [26, 27]. The NEQ, which assesses behavioral and psychological factors of the NES, consists of 14 questions, and responses are rated on a Likert scale from 0 to 4. All items except item 13 (three questions are reverse scored) were summed to create a total score ranging from 0 to 52. A score above 25 is a cut off for diagnosing NES [26].

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was developed to monitor physical activity and inactivity levels [28]. It consists of seven items. It records the estimated duration and frequency of activities performed in the past week. It provides information on time spent sitting, walking, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity activities. Activities must be performed for at least 10 min at a time. A total physical activity score was calculated by multiplying the durations by the known Metabolic Task Equivalent (MET) values ​​per activity and summing the results of all items. Based on the scoring, three classifications were made: inactive, minimally active, and very active. The validity and reliability study of the physical activity questionnaire in Turkish was conducted by Sağlam et al. [29].

This study’s data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0 and AMOS 24.0 software. In descriptive statistics, mean ± standard deviation values are given for continuous variables, and frequency and percentage distributions are given for categorical variables. The conformity of the data to a normal distribution was assessed according to skewness and kurtosis values. In the analyses, normally distributed data are indicated with the letter “t”, and non-normally distributed data are indicated with the letter “z”. The Chi-Square test (χ²) was used to compare descriptive variables between groups. The Independent Samples t-test was used to compare two normally distributed groups, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used to measure data that did not show a normal distribution. Differences between categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-Square test (χ²). Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses were performed to determine the relationships between variables. The correlation coefficient (r) reflects the strength and direction of the association, while the p-value indicates statistical significance. Correlation strengths were classified as weak (|r|=0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49), and strong (≥ 0.50), consistent with established guidelines [30]. Path analysis was performed to evaluate the effects of the Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ) score on body composition, sleep, physical activity, and RMR. Standardized beta (β) coefficients, p values, and explained variance (R²) were reported as a result of the path analysis. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered.

No statistically significant difference was found between the groups regarding age, body weight (p > 0.05). However, body fat mass (16.95 ± 5.62 kg) was found to be lower in individuals with NES compared to individuals without NES (19.9 ± 4.28 kg), and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05). Similarly, body fat percentage was 29.09 ± 6.14% in those with NES and 33.83 ± 5.77% in those without NES, which was highly significant (p < 0.05). Visceral fat level was lower in the NES group (77.93 ± 31.54) and higher in the non-NES group (94.79 ± 32.33) (p < 0.05). A significant difference was also found in BMI; the mean BMI in those with NES was 21.64 ± 2.28, while 22.88 ± 1.72 in those without NES (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

When evaluated in terms of sleep disturbance, JSS scores were higher in individuals with NES (8.63 ± 5.08) and were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). No significant difference was found between the groups in terms of RMR (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The incidence of NES was 15%, with 41 of the 268 participants included in the study.

The correlation results between body composition, physical activity, sleep disturbance status, night eating behavior, and resting metabolic rate are shown in Fig. 2. Resting metabolic rate (RMR) was moderately positively correlated (p < 0.05) with skeletal muscle mass (r = 0.360), body fat mass (r = 0.295), visceral fat (r = 0.300), and BMI (r = 0.338), but had no significant correlation with NEQ score, JSS score, or physical activity (p > 0.05).

The effect of the NEQ score on different variables was evaluated using path analysis. The analysis found a significant negative correlation between the NEQ score and BMI (β = -0.24; p < 0.05). The JSS score was significantly and positively affected by the NEQ score (β = 0.52; p < 0.05). In contrast, the relation of the NEQ score on body fat mass (β = -0.19), skeletal muscle mass (β = 0.06), physical activity level (β = -0.23), and RMR (β -0.13) were not found to be statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we carefully evaluated the association between NES and RMR, anthropometric measurements, and sleep disturbances using a case-control design. Our research hypothesis was that night eating syndrome would reduce metabolic rate and induce a tendency toward obesity in anthropometric measurements. However, our data indicated that NES was not related to RMR. However, the relation of NEQ scores on body fat mass were not significant, but a significant negative correlation was found between NEQ scores and BMI. Finally, individuals with NES had significantly lower sleep quality than the control group.

The prevalence of NES in the general population is between 0.5% and 1.5%, considerably lower than the average rate found in young adults [31, 32]. Studies conducted in various countries have found the prevalence of NES in young adults to be 0.6% to 9.5% [3–6]. However, there are also studies that have found a higher prevalence. For example, a study conducted with students in Malaysia found the prevalence of night eating syndrome to be 15% [33]. A study conducted with students at a Faculty of Health Sciences in Turkey found night eating syndrome in 8.3% of participants [34]. Similarly, in a large-scale cross-sectional study conducted with 2,512 students in Turkey, the prevalence of NES was found to be 20.9% [35]. In this study, the prevalence of night eating syndrome was found to be 15%.

Resting metabolic rate has a complex structure influenced by many factors and is not yet fully understood [15]. Meal timing is also a factor that can potentially affect metabolic rate. Research on this topic overwhelmingly suggests that late-night meals may slow metabolic rate, but there are also studies reporting no effect on RMR. The number of studies is limited due to methodological challenges, and the thermogenic effects of foods can vary over time [36–38]. However, it is known that there is a difference in thermogenic value between meals consumed in the morning, midday, or at night, with the lowest value being at the night meal [18]. Consuming a late-night meal also leads to changes in the levels of leptin and ghrelin, the hunger and satiety hormones. Various hormones involved in energy metabolism exhibit varying amounts and frequencies of activity depending on the time of day [39, 40]. In one study, 20 healthy volunteers were examined as a late-night meal intervention and a control group. Increased plasma cortisol, nocturnal glucose intolerance, and decreased fatty acid oxidation and mobilization were found in the late meal intervention group [36]. A study by Vujovic et al. showed that eating late decreased mean 24-hour body temperature, energy expenditure, and serum leptin levels, and increased the drive for energy intake [37]. In a study by Sato et al., energy expenditure in the respiratory chamber was assessed in ten young adults according to meal times. Participants were divided into two groups: a normal (7:00 PM) and a late (10:30 PM) dinner. While the increase in energy expenditure shifted to later hours in the late-eating group, 24-hour energy expenditure was similar in both groups [38]. In our study, no significant difference was found in RMR values ​​between the groups, and the relation of NEQ score on RMR were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Tables 1 and 2). Resting metabolic rate is strongly influenced by fat-free mass and skeletal muscle mass, which are primary determinants of basal energy expenditure. In the present study, RMR values were reported as absolute measures (kcal/day) and were not normalized for lean body mass. Therefore, the absence of significant group differences in RMR may partly reflect similarities in fat-free mass between the groups rather than a true absence of metabolic variation associated with NES. Future studies should consider expressing RMR relative to lean mass to better elucidate potential metabolic differences independent of body composition.

Factors that contribute to a person’s energy expenditure, such as sleep metabolic rate, RMR, physical activity, and the thermal effects of food, can be affected by sleep to varying degrees [13]. While sleep has been shown to reduce metabolic rate, studies also show that restricting sleep duration (5 h) does not affect RMR [10, 41, 42]. In this study, sleep quality was found to be poor in those with night eating syndrome (p < 0.05) (Table 1). However, no relationship was found between sleep quality and RMR (Table 2). This result stems from the lack of a difference in RMR between those with and without night eating syndrome.

Our results show no significant association between NES and RMR in young adult women. This finding is consistent with a previous study in obese individuals that found night eaters had the same RMR as individuals without NES [43]. Although the participants in our study had a BMI within the normal range, the significant difference in BMI between the groups may have contributed to a difference in metabolic rate. The likely mechanism for the lack of a relationship between night eating syndrome (NES) and resting metabolic rate (RMR) is that RMR is primarily determined by body composition. In particular, lean mass (muscle tissue) is the most important determinant of RMR. Furthermore, although NES induces a shift in meal timing, this behavioral circadian shift influences when energy is expended rather than directly altering resting metabolism. This would explain why night eaters showed no significant difference in RMR relative to controls despite their shifted intake timing.

Studies have shown that university students, due to their lifestyles, engage in many unhealthy behaviors, such as consuming irregular meals or skipping meals, eating late, and snacking between meals [44–48]. Furthermore, eating late at night can lead to skipping breakfast the next day [49, 50]. Individuals with NES tend to consume high amounts of energy from foods consumed after dinner, especially afterward. However, the habit of skipping breakfast has been reported to be more common [50, 51]. A study examining the relationship between breakfast skipping and night eating syndrome among university students in Bangladesh reported a high rate of breakfast skipping among NES students, with a correlation between these two [52]. Another study among university medical students found a high rate of breakfast skipping, as high as 75% [53]. Similar to the literature, this study found that the consumption of regular meals was significantly higher in the control group compared to the NES group (Table 1).

Poor sleep quality can lead to skipping meals, especially breakfast [44]. A study examining the relationship between appetite, eating behavior, and sleep quality in 13 young adults in the United States reported a significant association between good sleep quality and breakfast [54]. Another study among university students from 28 countries found that students with sleep problems were more likely to skip breakfast [46]. However, contrary to the literature, this study found no significant differences in sleep quality and NEQ scores based on meal skipping (p > 0.05) (Supplementary file). These results suggest that NES has an effect on meal skipping habits, but meal skipping habits do not affect night eating syndrome.

Many studies in the literature agree that sleep quality is associated with night eating behavior [33, 38, 44, 55, 56]. A study aimed at determining the risks of NES in university students found an association between poor sleep quality and NES [33]. Similarly, a study involving 112 female students found a significant association between NES and sleep disturbance [56]. A randomized controlled trial found that eating late in the evening prolongs the time it takes to fall asleep [38]. In a study evaluating eating habits in university students, late-night snacking increased the risk of poor sleep quality and increased short sleep duration [44]. One study showed that participants with insufficient sleep duration for five consecutive days increased their calorie intake after dinner. It has been emphasized that there may be a bidirectional relationship between late-night snacking and sleep duration [55]. A study in individuals living with obesity reported that most individuals with a positive NES screen also had a positive insomnia screen [57]. However, a significant relationship was found between night eating syndrome and sleep disturbance in a study involving 112 female students, similar to the participants in our study [56]. In the present study, consistent with previous findings, JSS scores were significantly higher in individuals with NES, and a positive correlation was observed between NEQ and sleep disturbance scores (r = 0.520; p < 0.05) (Table 1; Fig. 2). Moreover, NEQ scores were independently associated with sleep disturbance scores in regression analyses (β = 0.52; p < 0.05) (Table 2; Fig. 3). However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, it is not possible to determine the direction of this association. While night eating behaviors may contribute to circadian misalignment and sleep fragmentation, it is equally plausible that sleep disturbances—particularly insomnia symptoms and nocturnal awakenings—may increase opportunities for nighttime food intake and dysregulate appetite-related hormones, thereby facilitating night eating behaviors. Therefore, the relationship between NES and sleep disturbance may be potentially bidirectional and possibly self-reinforcing rather than strictly unidirectional. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying this association.

Theoretically, foods consumed late at night may not be adequately utilized for glycogen synthesis in the muscles and liver, thus leading to fat accumulation [58, 59]. Numerous studies have examined the relationship between nighttime eating, body fat, and fat oxidation [38, 56, 60–62]. A study by Hibi et al. showed that nighttime eating leads to a decrease in fat oxidation and an increase in total cholesterol [60]. A study among female students in Malaysia found no significant relationship between body fat and muscle percentage and NES [56]. Although eating late at night generally has negative consequences for metabolic health, some studies suggest that consuming small amounts of snacks at night has healthy metabolic effects [61]. Studies also show that consuming cereals at night may be effective in weight management in adults [62]. In a similar study, fat oxidation was found to be up to 80% higher in the group consuming a late dinner compared to the control group and up to 24% lower during sleep. Furthermore, fat oxidation was higher in the 24-hour measurement in the group consuming a late dinner [38]. In this study, body fat mass, body fat percentage, and visceral fat levels were lower in individuals with NES compared to the control group (Table 1). A low-level, significantly negative correlation was found between body fat percentage and NEQ score (Fig. 2). As seen in some studies in the literature, the effects of meals consumed at night on metabolism may also vary depending on their energy density. Because the type and amount of food consumed at night were not evaluated in our study, it would be insufficient to examine the relationship between NES and body fat from this perspective. However, the relation of NEQ scores on body fat mass were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 2; Fig. 3). This may be due to the higher BMI in the NES group compared to the control group.

Large-scale cross-sectional studies examining the relationship between adiposity and obesity and sleep quality have shown that participants’ sleep quality and duration are associated with an increased risk of obesity and body fat percentage [63–66]. However, contrary to the literature, our study found no significant association between sleep disturbances and body composition parameters (Fig. 2). This may be due to the significant comorbidity of night eating syndrome in individuals with sleep disturbances in our study (Table 1).

BMI is an important health indicator, and its association with individuals with NES is noteworthy. Studies on individuals with NES have found a higher BMI associated with NES [67, 68]. Baron et al.‘s study on bedtime meal consumption reported that caloric intake after 8 PM was positively associated with BMI [21]. A study conducted in Japan found that eating dinner immediately before bed and snacking after dinner increased the risk of developing obesity in women [69]. Similarly, a study of 39 adults reported that individuals whose evening energy intake exceeded 33% of daily energy intake had twice the risk of becoming obese compared to those whose energy intake was below 33% [70]. An online-based study found that NES was associated with increased BMI and poor eating habits. However, the study’s BMI data were based on self-reported data [17].

Despite the consensus that skipping breakfast and eating late at night may increase the risk of an increase in BMI [71, 72]. Many studies have failed to detect a relationship between these parameters. Studies conducted on young adults, both high school and university students, also found no significant association between BMI and night eating syndrome [73–77]. A study of 400 medical students found no association between NES and BMI [76]. Another study of 333 university students found no association between NES and BMI classification or scores [77]. In contrast, in the present study, a significant difference in BMI was observed between the groups, and a significant negative correlation (p < 0.05) was identified between NEQ scores and BMI (Tables 1 and 2). These findings support the notion that behavioral irregularities, such as altered meal timing, may not immediately lead to adverse anthropometric outcomes in young adults. Emerging literature suggests that lifestyle behaviors may cluster in paradoxical patterns, whereby potentially unfavorable behavioral profiles coexist with preserved or relatively favorable body composition characteristics. Indeed, a recent study conducted in a female university population reported that health-related behaviors may exhibit complex and non-linear patterns, and that potentially unfavorable behaviors do not necessarily result in increased adiposity [78]. In this context, temporal shifts in energy distribution observed in NES may coexist with preserved metabolic regulation or short-term metabolic adaptation/flexibility rather than overt metabolic dysfunction. Nevertheless, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether this seemingly protective pattern persists over time or represents an early transitional stage preceding metabolic alteration.

The strengths of this study include its multifaceted design, which compares metabolic rates, anthropometric measurements, and sleep disturbances between individuals with and without NES. Another strength is that the sample was drawn from young adults, a group most frequently studied for night eating syndrome. However, our study has some limitations. A validated and reliable scale was used to validate the NES, but clinical interviews did not establish a diagnosis. Data were self-reported, and no dietary records were collected regarding the type and amount of food consumed at night. Participants may have consumed healthy snacks at night, which limits the interpretation of the anthropometric data obtained from the study. However, although the participants in our study had a BMI within the normal range, the significant difference in BMI between the groups may have contributed to a difference in metabolic rate. Given that the study included only young adult women with a BMI of 18.5–24.9 kg/m² and low-to-moderate physical activity levels, the results may not be generalizable to overweight or clinical populations, in which Night Eating Syndrome has been more commonly studied and may present with different metabolic and anthropometric profiles. However, the a priori power analysis supported the adequacy of the sample size, the relatively modest sample and use of path analysis may limit parameter stability and the precision of estimates. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory, warranting replication in larger and more diverse samples. Considering these factors in future studies would lead to less speculative results.

In this case–control study including only individuals with a BMI of 18.5–24.9 kg/m², NES was not associated with a reduced metabolic rate. Moreover, our findings suggest that NES may be associated with altered body composition, notably lower body fat mass and visceral fat, as well as increased sleep disturbances. Knowing the effect of NES on metabolic rate is crucial for implementing medical nutrition therapy. Longitudinal and large-scale studies are warranted to better elucidate the causal relationships and provide evidence-based clinical practice recommendations.
