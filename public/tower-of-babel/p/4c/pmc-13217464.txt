Hypertension in steelworkers: a global systematic review and meta-analysis of prevalence and associated factors
Authors: Xiaojie Xu, Yunzhu Wang, Jiaren Wu, Rujie Wang, Xingxin Zhan, Rong Liu, Haitao Zhang, Wenjing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis global meta-analysis aims to assess the prevalence of hypertension among steelworkers and its associated factors.ObjectiveTo systematically assess hypertension prevalence and its influencing factors among steelworkers.MethodsWe systematically searched nine databases (CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, SinoMed, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus and Ovid Medline) from inception to 13 April 2026. Following PRISMA guidelines and Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) standards, we used Stata software with appropriate effect models. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses and Egger's test were performed to evaluate heterogeneity and publication bias.ResultsThis meta-analysis included 23 studies with 79,262 steelworkers. The pooled hypertension prevalence was 31%, significantly higher than the general population rate (26%). Prevalence in all-male cohorts (34.1%) exceeded that in mixed-sex cohorts, suggesting a healthy worker effect. Key risk factors were high temperatures (OR = 1.97), noise exposure (OR = 1.56), alcohol consumption (OR = 2.03), and obesity (OR = 3.25).ConclusionHypertension prevalence among steelworkers is substantially elevated, primarily associated with occupational exposures (e.g., high temperatures and noise) and behavioural factors (e.g., obesity and alcohol consumption). Integrating occupational hypertension surveillance into the existing prevention and control system is recommended, establishing a comprehensive model based on engineering interventions, health management, and behavioural promotion.Keywords: Hypertension, Prevalence, Associated factors, Meta-analysis, Steelworker

Introduction

Hypertension is a major global public health challenge. It is also the leading risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol., 2014; Lancet (London, England), 2017). Occupational groups face a substantially elevated risk of hypertension due to exposure to specific workplace hazards (Kivimäki et al., 2002). Steelworkers face prolonged exposure to multiple risk factors. These include high temperatures, strenuous physical labour, shift work, dust, and occupational stress. These factors are associated with impaired blood pressure regulation and a higher hypertension risk (Bolm-Audorff et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2018; Guo et al., 2023; Melese et al., 2023).

Although existing research has examined the prevalence of hypertension among steelworkers, the available evidence shows substantial heterogeneity. Most studies, however, only report prevalence rates. They do not systematically analyse the sources of this heterogeneity (Choi et al., 2006; Gupta et al., 2013; Vo et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2006). Previous reviews have also focused mainly on other occupational groups (e.g., healthcare workers and drivers). Comprehensive assessments specific to steelworkers remain limited (Milutinović et al., 2012; Zamparoni Victorino et al., 2023). A key limitation is that existing studies often examine single risk factors in isolation. They fail to conduct multidimensional analyses integrating occupational exposures, individual characteristics, behaviours, and psychosocial factors. Moreover, systematic assessments of their relative contributions are lacking (Gamboa Madeira et al., 2021).

This study therefore aims to address these limitations. It moves beyond the mere aggregation of prevalence rates. It involves a systematic assessment and quantitative synthesis of hypertension prevalence among steelworkers and its multi-level determinants. This aims to elucidate the independent and combined associations of both occupational and non-occupational factors on hypertension risk. This will provide an evidence-based foundation for developing precise and effective workplace health intervention strategies.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Milutinović et al., 2012). The study protocol was registered a priori with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251104231). A systematic literature search was conducted across nine databases (CNKI, Wan fang Data, VIP, SinoMed, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, and Ovid Medline) on 13 April 2026, using comprehensive terms for hypertension and related occupations, adapted for both Chinese and English databases. The search covered the period from each database's inception to the search date, with full strategies detailed in Supplementary Table 1. To minimise omission, we supplemented the database searches by manually checking the reference lists of included studies and performing forward and backward citation tracking using the CitationChaser tool.

Study selection criteria

Eligible studies were cross-sectional investigations of frontline workers (aged ≥18 years) in the steel or primary metal manufacturing industries. Studies were required to report the prevalence of hypertension explicitly or to provide data (e.g., odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals) permitting its calculation. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm-Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90, and/or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication within the preceding mm-Hgweeks (Chaturvedi, 2004). Exclusion criteria were: non-cross-sectional design; irrelevant population; unavailability of a full-text article; insufficient data for analysis; unclear methodological descriptions; ambiguous or non-standard diagnostic criteria for hypertension; or publication type being a review, commentary, conference abstract, or case report; sample size <50. For duplicate publications, only the study with the largest sample size was retained. However, if multiple publications reported different outcomes from the same underlying study population, all were included to facilitate comprehensive data extraction.

Research selection

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (XXJ, WYZ) using a standardised form and then cross-checked. Extracted information included study characteristics (first author, publication year, country, design, sample size), participant demographics (age, sex distribution), hypertension diagnostic criteria, measurement device, number of cases, prevalence rates, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associated factors. Sociodemographic data, such as alcohol consumption, smoking status and educational attainment, were also collected. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (WJR). All extracted data were compiled in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for subsequent pooled analysis.

Statistical analysis

Measures and statistical analysis for quantitative studies

The statistical analysis plan was finalised prior to data extraction. The pooled prevalence of hypertension and the pooled ORs for associated factors, along with their 95% CIs, were calculated. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q statistic (significance threshold P < 0.10) and quantified with the I2 statistic. A fixed-effect model was employed when no significant heterogeneity was detected (I2 < 50% and P-value for Cochran's Q > 0.05); otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and meta-regression were performed to explore sources of heterogeneity and to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates. All meta-analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp).

Assessment of study quality and publication bias

The methodological quality of the included observational studies was independently evaluated by two reviewers (XXJ, WYZ) using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) checklist The checklist consists of 11 items, each rated as “yes”, “no”, or “unclear”. A score of 1 was assigned for a “yes” response, while “no” and “unclear” responses received 0 points. Based on the total score, studies were categorised as follows: ≤3 points indicated low quality, 4–7 points moderate quality, and 8–11 points high quality. Studies classified as low quality were excluded from the synthesis. Given that these assessments informed the grading of evidence, the composition of the included moderate- and high-quality studies is reported to reflect the overall robustness of the evidence base (Chaturvedi, 2004). Inter-rater reliability for the quality assessments was calculated using Cohen's kappa coefficient. Any discrepancies in quality assessment were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (WJR). Publication bias was assessed by examining funnel plot asymmetry and applying Egger's linear regression test. Visual asymmetry, when accompanied by a statistically significant result in Egger's test (P < 0.05), was considered indicative of potential bias. To ensure the robustness of the test, Egger's regression was only performed when ten or more studies were included in the analysis (Begg and Mazumdar, 1994; Sautter et al., 2024).

Ethical approval

This review adhered to PRISMA guidelines to ensure methodological rigor. As it was based solely on previously published data, ethical approval and participant consent were not required.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, selection, data analysis, interpretation, or implementation of this study. They will also not be informed of the study outcomes.”

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The initial search yielded 532 records. After removing 289 duplicates, 151 records were excluded based on title and abstract screening. Following full-text assessment of the remaining 92 articles, a further 69 were excluded, resulting in 23 studies that met the inclusion criteria for the systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1). These studies, reporting hypertension prevalence among steelworkers, were conducted in China, Germany, Japan, Iran, and an East African region. The cumulative sample size across the 23 studies was 79,262 participants, among whom 25,535 were identified with hypertension, yielding a crude overall prevalence of 31%.Table 1 summarises the key characteristics and methodological quality of the included studies, detailing the publication year, country, sample size, and Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) scores. According to the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) criteria, one study was rated as high quality and 22 as moderate quality. This quality assessment supports the conclusion that the overall pooled prevalence of hypertension among steelworkers is high, with the evidence being predominantly of moderate quality. The study populations were predominantly male. The publication years ranged from 2008 to 2023, with the majority of studies conducted in China. Meta-analysis of hypertension prevalence among steel workers

The pooled prevalence of hypertension among steelworkers was 31% (95% CI: 0.25, 0.36),with substantial heterogeneity between studies (I2 = 99.68%, P < 0.01). Subgroup analyses by region identified significant variation: the highest prevalence was observed in Germany (79%; 95% CI: 0.77, 0.81), followed by China (30%; 95% CI: 0.26, 0.34) and East Africa (28%; 95% CI: 0.23, 0.33). The prevalence in Japan was considerably lower at 15% (95% CI: 0.10, 0.19). The pooled prevalence of hypertension among Chinese steelworkers was 30.0% (95% CI: 0.26, 0.34), with heterogeneity remaining high (I2 = 99.18%, P < 0.01); a random-effects model was employed. This result is highly consistent with the overall prevalence (31%) reported in the included studies, indicating that the overall estimate is robust (Fig. 2 and Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis of factors associated with hypertension

The meta-analysis synthesising data from the included studies identified multiple factors significantly associated with hypertension among steelworkers (all P < 0.05). Using a random-effects model, the strongest association was observed for elevated body mass index (BMI) (OR = 3.25, 95% CI: 2.84, 3.73), followed by smoking (OR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.36, 3.38), alcohol consumption (OR = 2.03, 95% CI: 1.48, 2.80), high-temperature exposure (OR = 1.97, 95% CI: 1.53, 2.55), and noise exposure (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.31, 1.86). Physical exercise (OR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.17, 2.22) and shift work (OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.17) were also associated with increased risk. Regarding demographic factors, older age (OR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.05, 1.13) and a family history of hypertension (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.30, 1.68) were significant risk factors, whereas higher educational attainment showed a protective effect (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.48, 0.97). Subgroup analysis

To explore sources of the substantial heterogeneity (I2 > 99%) observed in the main analysis of hypertension prevalence among steelworkers, a multidimensional subgroup analysis was performed; the results are summarised in Table 3. Analysis by country showed a pooled prevalence of 30.0% (95% CI: 0.28, 0.33) in studies from China (19 studies), compared with 34.0% (95% CI: 0.53, 0.73) in studies from other countries (4 studies). The wide confidence interval in the latter group suggests considerable between-study variation or limited representativeness. Stratification by publication year indicated a lower pooled prevalence in studies published before 2015 (27.2%; 95% CI: 0.21, 0.34) than in those published in 2015 or later (32.2%; 95% CI: 26.6, 37.8). When stratified by sex, the prevalence was lower in all-male cohorts (27.3%; 95% CI: 0.24, 0.31) than in mixed-sex cohorts (33.1%; 95% CI: 0.26, 0.49).Subgroup analyses based on primary exposure factors yielded the following prevalence estimates: shift work, 33.3% (95% CI: 0.33, 0.34); smoking and alcohol use, 32.8% (95% CI: 0.30, 0.36); high-temperature exposure, 29.1% (95% CI: 0.25, 0.33); and noise exposure, 22.1% (95% CI: 0.10, 0.34). The wide confidence interval for noise exposure indicates relatively low precision for this subgroup. All subgroup analyses displayed high heterogeneity (P < 0.01), warranting the use of random-effects model.

Heterogeneity and publication bias

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2 > 99%), potentially attributable to geographical variations, differences in study design, and varying exposure factors. Funnel plot and Galbraith plot analyses identified several outliers, suggesting possible publication bias or small-study effects. Although Egger's regression test was not performed due to data constraints, visual inspection of the funnel plot indicated a moderately symmetrical distribution.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of the pooled prevalence estimate, a sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each included study. The results show that after the removal of any single study, the pooled prevalence of hypertension among steelworkers remained stable, ranging from 29% to 33%, which is highly consistent with the primary estimate of 31% (95% CI: 0.25, 0.36) obtained from the full analysis. This confirms the reliability of the main finding (Fig. 3).Notably, excluding the study by Barbara et al. from Germany, which reported an abnormally high prevalence, yielded a pooled prevalence of 28% (95% CI: 0.25,0.31), and the overall conclusion did not change in direction, confirming that outliers did not affect the stability of the findings. Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot combined with Egger's test, with blue dots representing Chinese studies and red dots representing foreign studies. The funnel plot showed that data points were symmetrically distributed on both sides of the pooled effect size axis, with most falling within the 95% confidence interval; Chinese studies were more numerous and concentrated in the central region, whereas foreign studies were fewer and located near the edges, though without compromising overall symmetry. Egger's test indicated no significant publication bias (P > 0.05), suggesting that the results of this study are minimally influenced by publication bias. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesised data from 23 cross-sectional studies (comprising 79,262 participants) to evaluate the global prevalence of hypertension and its associated factors among steelworkers. The pooled prevalence was 31% (95% CI: 0.25, 0.36), which is notably higher than the approximate prevalence of 26% reported in the general adult population (Lancet (London, England), 2017). This finding indicates that steel production work is associated with a higher prevalence of hypertension. Given the cross-sectional nature of the included studies, these associations should be interpreted as correlates rather than causal determinants.

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore sources of the substantial heterogeneity observed in the overall estimates. Analyses by region indicated differences between Chinese and non-Chinese steelworkers. The wider confidence intervals in the non-Chinese group may reflect influences such as variations in regional economic development, occupational health standards, and genetic background. Particular attention is warranted for a German study reporting an exceptionally high prevalence (79%) (Schumann et al., 2011). While the original authors attributed this finding to high automation levels, an ageing workforce, and a high prevalence of metabolic syndrome, the outlier may also reflect methodological particularities, including potential selection bias (e.g., health-examination data from specific worker subgroups), differing diagnostic criteria for hypertension, or distinct local occupational health contexts. Sensitivity analysis showed that excluding this study yielded a pooled prevalence of 28% (95% CI: 0.25, 0.31), underscoring its considerable influence on the overall estimate. Its inclusion highlights the substantial heterogeneity across study settings, though it may also represent an authentic extreme within the occupational-risk spectrum, possibly resulting from the interaction of advanced age with prolonged exposure in a highly industrialised environment. Temporal analysis indicated a higher pooled prevalence in studies published after 2015 (32.2%) than in those published in or before 2015 (27.2%), a trend that may be associated with increased work stress, accelerated production rhythms, and global trends in obesity (Kivimäki et al., 2015). Furthermore, prevalence estimates were higher in large-scale studies (sample size ≥4000; 33.1%) than in smaller studies (29.2%), which aligns with the principle that larger samples generally provide more precise epidemiological estimates (Kowall et al., 2024).

The prevalence of hypertension in the all male cohort (34.1%) was higher than that in the mixed sex cohort (27.3%). This difference is consistent with the “healthy worker effect” (HWE) when occupational stratification and survivor bias are considered (Hwang et al., 2021). In the steel industry, high exposure production roles (e.g., in blast furnaces and hot rolling mills) are predominantly occupied by males. The healthy worker effect operates through a dynamic selection process: healthier individuals are more likely to be recruited into and retained in these physically demanding positions, whereas those who develop significant health conditions such as hypertension are often transferred to low exposure administrative or auxiliary roles (which are typically included in mixed sex cohorts) or leave the workforce entirely. As a result, the all male, high exposure cohort represents a “healthy survivor” population. Nevertheless, the persistently elevated prevalence observed even in this selected group suggests the substantial occupational risk burden; the true risk for individuals entering such roles may be higher than our estimates suggest. In contrast, mixed sex cohorts generally include a wider range of health profiles, incorporating both workers who began in less physically strenuous positions and those who may have transferred from high exposure roles. Thus, the healthy worker effect in this setting does not lead to an underestimation of prevalence in high risk groups, but rather indicates that our estimates are likely conservative relative to the full risk faced by new production line employees.

Collectively, the findings suggest that hypertension in steelworkers is associated with the cumulative and interactive effects of multiple risk factors, rather than isolated causes. Individual characteristics, occupational exposures, and lifestyle factors appear to be interrelated in their association with hypertension risk, likely through complex and interrelated pathological mechanisms.

Increasing age (OR = 1.09) is a well-established factor associated with increased risk for hypertension, largely attributed to age-related arterial stiffness and reduced vascular compliance (Hwang et al., 2021). Among steelworkers, older age also corresponds to a longer duration of cumulative exposure to occupational hazards. The interaction between the physiological processes of ageing and these chronic exposures may be associated with a higher hypertension risk.

Conversely, higher educational attainment (OR = 0.68) exhibited a protective association. This effect may be mediated through greater health literacy, improved access to health information, and the adoption of more proactive health behaviours (Hahn and Truman, 2015). Furthermore, education may enhance an individual's sense of agency (Frieden, 2010)—that is, their capacity to make and enact autonomous, health-promoting choices.

Obesity (OR = 3.25) is a major factor associated with increased risk, with central obesity linked to chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and endothelial dysfunction (Whelton et al., 2018). Among steelworkers, fatigue from high-intensity labour may encourage consumption of high-energy-density foods (Yamazaki et al., 2005); without dietary guidance, this may promotes central obesity and be related to hypertension risk.

Alcohol (OR = 2.03) and smoking (OR = 2.14) are prevalent factors associated with hypertension. Alcohol is linked to vasomotor function and promotes oxidative stress (Puddey et al., 2019), while smoking is related to vasoconstriction and inflammation (National Center for Chronic Disease P, Health Promotion Office on S, Health, 2014). These behaviours, often used to cope with occupational stress, frequently co-occur (Clark et al., 2022), creating a syndemic of risk. Despite offering short-term relief, their synergy substantially is associated with cardiometabolic burden.

Occupational exposure is a primary factor associated with hypertension in this population. High-temperature exposure during steelmaking processes (OR = 1.97) subjects workers to intense heat. The resultant thermal stress elevates core temperature, inducing cutaneous vasodilation and increasing cardiac workload, while long-term exposure is linked to vascular endothelium and accelerated atherosclerosis (Wen et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2022). Noise exposure (OR = 1.56) is associated with the sympathetic nervous system, which may cause acute cardiovascular surges; prolonged exposure may additionally be related to epigenetic-mediated cardiovascular damage (Münzel et al., 2018). Shift work, particularly night shifts (OR = 1.11), is associated with circadian rhythms, suppression of nocturnal blood pressure dipping, and, in combination with sleep deprivation, may be linked to hypertension (Wang et al., 2011; Kecklund and Axelsson, 2016; Morris et al., 2016).

These factors rarely occur in isolation, often exerting synergistic effects. Middle-aged and older male workers with concurrent exposure to heat, noise, and night shifts, especially those who also smoke, consume alcohol, or have obesity experience a steep rise in risk. This cumulative risk model underscores that combined effects far exceed those of any single factor (Evans and Kim, 2010), complicating interventions and necessitating comprehensive, multi-level strategies.

This study has several notable strengths, including its adherence to PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive multi-database search strategy, and a rigorous quantitative synthesis. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all included studies were cross-sectional in design, which precludes causal inference regarding the observed associations. Second, occupational exposures such as heat and noise were assessed mainly through job titles or self-report, without objective individual-level measurements (e.g., dosimetry), potentially leading to exposure misclassification. Third, it should be noted that existing studies generally lack quantitative data on key occupational exposure factors, such as workload and degree of automation. As a result, we were unable to include these factors in our heterogeneity analysis or meta-regression, making it difficult to assess their independent and interactive effects on the risk of hypertension. Future studies are therefore recommended to systematically collect and report data on job automation levels, labor intensity, working hours, and the use of health services. Such efforts would enable more precise identification of sources of heterogeneity, enhance the quality of evidence synthesis, and provide a more robust basis for the development of targeted occupational health interventions. Fourth, despite subgroup analyses, substantial residual heterogeneity (I2 > 99%) remained, likely due to unmeasured factors such as differences in diagnostic protocols, variations in measurement devices, and unadjusted confounders (e.g., detailed medication history and dietary patterns). Finally, although funnel plots appeared broadly symmetrical, the possibility of publication bias cannot be ruled out entirely.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a significant hypertension burden among steelworkers, with a global pooled prevalence of 31% that exceeds rates reported in the general adult population. Key modifiable factors associated with hypertension include high temperatures and noise, while behavioural factors such as alcohol consumption and obesity also show strong associations. Based on the available evidence, the following interventions are recommended as priorities. Engineering controls should be strengthened by establishing clear exposure limits for high-temperature and high-noise roles (e.g., noise not exceeding 85 dB) and ensuring the provision of adequate cooling and hearing protection. Hypertension should be integrated into routine occupational health surveillance, with blood pressure screening every three months for workers in high-temperature positions and at least annually for others. Workplace health programmes should emphasise alcohol control and weight management, supported by regular health guidance. In addition, a collaborative hypertension monitoring network between enterprises and regional health authorities should be developed to facilitate data sharing and coordinated action. Governments, enterprises, and health departments must work together to implement these evidence-informed measures. Finally, long-term cohort and intervention studies remain necessary to generate higher-quality evidence for ongoing policy refinement.
