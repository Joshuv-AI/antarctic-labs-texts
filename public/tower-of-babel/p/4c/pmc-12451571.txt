Acute effects of 50 mg additive oral sildenafil on invasive exercise haemodynamics in pulmonary vascular disease
Authors: Simon R. Schneider, Mona Lichtblau, Julian Müller, Meret Bauer, Laura Mayer, Esther I. Schwarz, Michael Furian, Stéphanie Saxer, Silvia Ulrich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsPatients with precapillary pulmonary hypertension (PH) due to pulmonary vascular disease (PVD) have reduced exercise capacity, limiting daily activity and quality of life. Phospodiesterase-5 inhibitors, including sildenafil, are used to treat PVD. This study examined effects of sildenafil on pulmonary haemodynamics during exercise in PVD.MethodPVD patients diagnosed with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) or chronic thromboembolic PH (CTEPH) underwent right heart catheterisation assessed at rest and during mild exercise. Main outcomes were mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP), cardiac output (CO) and the mPAP/CO slope before and ∼60 min after additive sildenafil (50 mg oral).Results22 PVD patients (14 PAH, 8 CTEPH, 9 women and 15 pretreated (9 combination therapy)) with a mean±sd age of 54±14 years, mPAP 39±10 mmHg, CO 4.7±1.5 L·min−1 and pulmonary vascular resistance 6.9±3.8 WU were included. After sildenafil, mPAP and CO at rest were unchanged, but at end-exercise, mPAP and the mPAP/CO slope significantly decreased (mPAP 61±16 versus 54±16 mmHg; mean difference −7, 95% confidence interval (CI) −11 to −4; p<0.001 and mPAP/CO slope 9.8±4.9 versus 6.6±3.3; −3, 95% CI −4.9 to −1.2; p=0.002). The mean systemic arterial pressure was significantly lower after additive sildenafil at rest (−9 mmHg, −13 to −5; p<0.001) and at end-exercise (−9 mmHg, −13 to −5; p<0.001). One patient experienced dizziness.ConclusionsPatients with PVD, showed favourable acute pulmonary haemodynamic changes during exercise after additive sildenafil, indicated by a reduced mPAP and mPAP/CO slope. Sildenafil was generally well tolerated despite lower blood pressure. Further investigation is needed to determine if sildenafil as a “pill-in-the-pocket” strategy improves exercise capacity in PVD patients.

Introduction

Patients with chronic precapillary pulmonary hypertension (PH) due to the pulmonary vascular diseases (PVDs) pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and chronic thromboembolic PH (CTEPH) suffer from progressive exertional dyspnoea, which limits exercise capacity and impairs quality of life (QoL) [1–4].

Due to comprehensive drug combinations and supportive therapies, QoL and prognosis of PVD has improved [4–6]. Exercise pulmonary haemodynamics provide prognostic information in patients with PVD [7, 8] and in patients with exertional dyspnoea, a mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP)/cardiac output (CO) slope during exercise >3 WU is diagnostic of exercise PH and indicates worse survival and QoL [7–9].

Exercise intolerance in PVD is mainly due to the increased afterload to the right heart and reduced pulmonary blood flow reflected by the reduced CO, but also exercise-induced arterial oxygen desaturation and peripheral abnormalities such as impaired skeletal muscle morphology with limited oxygen transport may play a role [10–12].

Improving patient's exercise capacity is a major goal in the treatment of PVD, to alleviate daily activities, improve QoL and facilitate adequate training intensity. Randomised controlled trials have shown that exercise capacity in PVD assessed as incremental ramp or constant work-rate cycling tests can be significantly enhanced with high-dose supplemental oxygen therapy [13, 14]. However, high-dose supplemental oxygen therapy may not be logistically feasible to support exercise capacity in everyday life.

Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (PDE5-i) act as a synthetic alternative to the endogenous cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) and are widely used as pulmonary vasodilator drugs in the treatment of PVD [15, 16]. Sildenafil was the first PDE5-i approved for the treatment of PVD and can be used for vasodilator testing during right heart catheterisation (RHC) due to its rapid onset and short duration of action [16, 17]. Recently, it has been shown that PVD patients may benefit from higher sildenafil doses up to 80 mg three times daily [18].

The question arises whether a single additive dose of sildenafil as part of a “pill-in-the-pocket” concept has the right haemodynamic stress-reducing and exercise-enhancing effects. Those effects might allow patients with PVD to perform planned higher-intensity daily activities with less dyspnoea or to exercise at higher intensity levels with attenuated risk of right ventricular overload [10, 19]. The aim of the present trial was therefore to investigate in a well-controlled setting during RHC the effect of a single additive dose of oral sildenafil on invasive exercise haemodynamics and safety in patients with PVD.

Material and methods

Timeframe, trial design and intervention

The study was performed at the University Hospital Zurich, between March 2021 and March 2023 during clinically indicated RHC.

This predefined interventional study is part of a sequence-randomised crossover trial, in which each patient underwent four identical sequences of assessments. Each identical sequence consisted of inclining the coach with the cycle ergometer to semi-supine (45°), 10 min of quiet rest, thereafter taking all resting measures (haemodynamics, metabolic measurements, arterial and mixed venous blood gases), followed by the two 3-min steps of mild exercise at an intensity of 10 to 20 W followed by 20 to 40 W, depending on the patients’ fitness, where at the end all measurements were repeated. In the first two sequences, patients were randomised to breathe either ambient air or normobaric hypoxia, these data are not included in the present predefined analysis and data from the effect from normobaric hypoxia versus normoxia are published elsewhere. Thereafter, patients received a single oral dose of sildenafil 50 mg, and after at least 45 min of waiting time, sequences were repeated as described above. In this predefined analysis, we evaluate only the effect of a single additive oral dose of sildenafil 50 mg on the sequences where patients were breathing normoxic ambient air (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04706546). The study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethical review board (BASEC no. 2020-2163).

Patients

Adults of all sexes diagnosed with PVD defined as PAH or CTEPH [4], either prevalent or incident were included upon written informed consent [4]. Exclusion criteria were PH other than PAH/CTEPH, a resting arterial oxygen tension (PaO2) <8 kPa, nitrate use and any clinical significant concomitant end-stage diseases (e.g. renal failure, hepatic dysfunction or cancer). All patients took their regular medications before the study with the exception of oral anticoagulants, which were temporarily interrupted.

Exercise right heart catheterisation, metabolic measurements and blood gases

A standard procedure was applied to place a Swan-Ganz catheter (Swan-Ganz CCOmbo V, Edwards Lifesciences, CA, USA) via jugular vein access. Transducers were placed at the midthoracic level [20]. An arterial catheter was placed into a radial artery. Systemic arterial blood pressure (BP), pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP), heart rate (HR) and oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry (SpO2) at the fingertip and catheter tip were continuously monitored and recorded (LabChart 8, ADInstruments, USA). Pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) was assessed at rest and at the end of each exercise step. Arterial and mixed venous blood gases were drawn from the tips of the pulmonary and radial artery catheters during each sequence after 15 min of quiet rest in semi-supine position (45°) and at end-exercise and immediately analysed (ABL 90 Flex-blood Radiometer, Switzerland).

Mixed venous blood oxygenation saturation (SmvO2) was initially calibrated with mixed venous blood gas analysis (Vigilance II, Edwards Lifesciences, USA).

During each intervention sequence from rest to end-exercise, patients were connected to a breath analyser (Ergostick, Geratherm Medical, Germany) and a non-rebreathing two-way valve (T-shape; Hans Rudolph, USA), connected to a gas-mixing device (Altitrainer, Switzerland) to provide either ambient air or normobaric hypoxia in a blinded fashion. The analysis presented here, focuses only on normoxic conditions. Oxygen uptake (V′O2), carbon dioxide output, respiratory rate, tidal volume and minute ventilation were continuously assessed breath by breath and averaged.

Outcomes

The main outcome of this predefined study was the slope of pressure–flow relationship (mPAP/CO) during exercise while breathing ambient air before and ∼60 min after the additive oral single dose of sildenafil 50 mg.

Additional outcomes were differences in pulmonary and systemic haemodynamics, blood oxygenation and metabolic measurements at rest and end-exercise before and after the additive sildenafil pill and respective changes during exercise as well as adverse events.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated for the difference in mPAP/CO slope between the different sequences assuming a clinically important difference of –0.7±1 WU. With these assumptions, for a targeted power of 80% with a two-sided α level of 0.05, a sample size of 17 patients was calculated. In view of potential dropouts or technical challenges, we aimed to include 22–24 patients.

Data extraction and statistics

Continuous metabolic measurements and LabChart recordings were synchronised and measures averaged over 30 s. To measure CO, the direct Fick method was applied at rest and end-exercise [21, 22]. Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) was calculated as (mPAP–PAWP/CO). The mPAP/CO slope was calculated by mPAPend-ex – mPAPrest/COend-ex – COrest.

Differences between values before and after sildenafil were calculated using mixed linear regression models correcting for the intervention sequence, a time variable to test for carry-over effects and the dichotomous variable “PVD-targeted therapy” as a fixed effect and subjects as a random effect.

Results are reported as mean±sd, number (%) or in mean differences with 95% confidence intervals and analysis was performed per protocol. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed with statistical software R studio (R studio, v.4.3.2, PBC Boston, USA).

Results

Patients

We screened 31 patients, of whom six did not meet the eligibility criteria, one did not provide consent and two were excluded post-randomisation due to technical problems or dizziness with low BP. Finally, 22 patients were included for this per-protocol analysis on the effect of additive sildenafil while breathing ambient air (figure 1). Baseline characteristics are shown in table 1. 14 patients (64%) were diagnosed with PAH and eight (36%) with CTEPH. 9 (41%) were female and 16 prevalent and pretreated (table 1). The majority was in World Health Organization functional class 2 (73%) and the mean±sd age was 54±14 years (table 1). Six were treatment-naive, three incident with PAH and three with CTEPH. 16 patients were pretreated with endothelin receptor antagonist (9), tadalafil (9), riociguat (3) or prostanoids (6) as shown in table 1.

Primary outcome

The mPAP/CO slope during exercise was significantly reduced with additive sildenafil 50 mg from 9.8±4.9 to 6.6±3.3 WU with a mean difference and 95% CI of −3.0 (−4.9 to −1.2) WU; p=0.002 (figure 2), which was not influenced by randomisation or carry-over effects (data not shown).

Adverse events of additive sildenafil

One patient with an mPAP of 39 mmHg and PVR of 4.7 WU pretreated with macitentan 1×10 mg and tadalafil 2×20 mg was excluded from this analysis, as she reported dizziness and did thus not have assessments after additive sildenafil 50 mg, as she already initially revealed a very low BP of 90/43 mmHg. No other adverse events were reported and all other participants felt well throughout the study procedures.

Discussion

In this analysis, we have shown for the first time that a single oral additive dose of 50 mg sildenafil reduces the mPAP/CO slope during invasively measured exercise haemodynamics in patients with PVD, PAH or CTEPH. The increase of lactate during exercise was smaller after additive sildenafil. Of interest, at end-exercise after additive sildenafil, the mPAP but also the systemic PB were decreased along with an unchanged PAWP, the decrease in mPAP is due to the pulmonary vasodilator effect of sildenafil. Also, the end-exercise CO revealed a strong tendency to increase and PVR to decrease, but this did not reach significancy, most probably related to the relatively low sample size. Of interest, the reduced mPAP/CO slope did not result in a lower V′O2, which would have been a sign of improved exercise efficacy at the given iso-load.

The significant decrease in the mPAP/CO slope during exercise in response to a single additive dose of sildenafil 50 mg is especially remarkable as 15 of 22 patients were prevalent and pretreated with pulmonary vasodilator therapy, 9 of 22 even with combination therapy and that the decrease in mPAP/CO slope after additive sildenafil was independent of pretreatment. This may signify that the vasodilator drug therapy was insufficient even in pretreated patients. Of interest, the mean zero-flow extrapolated pressure intercepts of the mPAP/CO slopes was positive in our patients, reflecting the increased PVR in PVD, and was not significantly modified by vasodilator therapy, in line with previous findings after longer-term prostaglandin infusion [23, 24] and the pressure–flow slop intercept as shown in figure 2 was higher compared with the pulmonary artery wedge pressure. Whether an additive vasodilator therapy would enhance exercise capacity and reduce haemodynamic stress in PVD remains to be determined. It is well known that patients diagnosed with PVD have a greater increase in the mPAP/CO slope during exercise compared with unaffected controls [25–28]. In addition, several studies have shown that the slope of the pressure–flow relationship during exercise is of prognostic relevance in patients assessed for dyspnoea, but also in patients with established PVD, scleroderma patients at risk of PVD and COPD [7–9, 29, 30]. Real-life long-term data from the Swiss PH registry found that a less steep mPAP/CO slope was predictive for longer transplant-free survival and was also inversely correlated with patient-relevant outcomes such as QoL, functional class and exercise capacity [7, 9]. However, whether a reduced mPAP/CO slope by intensified vasodilator therapy would improve exercise capacity in PVD is not known and cannot be answered by the present results. If this would be the case, it may be worth investigating when higher doses of vasodilator therapy would be of benefit for patients and whether doses should be increased all day long or during certain active daytimes. PDE-5i such as sildenafil have been used in the long-term treatment of patients with PVD for more than two decades due to their pulmonary vasodilator properties and their effectiveness in mono- or combination therapy with other PAH-targeted drugs [16, 31, 32]. According to results of the SUPER-1 randomised controlled trial of sildenafil in PAH, a dose of three times daily 20 mg is recommended, albeit some studies advocated that higher doses might be more effective [16, 33]. A recent randomised controlled trial has shown that higher doses up to 80 mg three times daily are as safe as lower doses and may even be more effective in increasing exercise performance [18]. Among the different PDE-5i, sildenafil is characterised by a rapid onset of action after oral application. The haemodynamic effects are most pronounced after 40–90 min, with the effect dissipating thereafter [17]. If the herein shown reduced mPAP/CO slope after additive sildenafil is shown to be associated with improved exercise performance in PVD in future studies, these pharmacokinetic characteristics would make oral sildenafil an interesting intervention to be studied as a “pill-in-the-pocket” drug before planned higher-intensity daily activity or exercise training. In the present study, the reduced mPAP/CO slope after the single additive oral dose of sildenafil in patients with PVD was found after mild-to-moderate exercise during RHC. In line with the results from our trial, Claessen et al. [28] compared 14 CTEPH patients with seven healthy controls during exercise with and without sildenafil and described that sildenafil reduced the pressure–flow slope assessed by echocardiography, which was not the case in healthy controls, potentially explained by the lower mPAP/CO slope with lower potential to decrease. However, compared with our cohort, which comprised only seven (32%) participants who were not on a PH-targeted therapy, all CTEPH patients assessed by Claessen et al. were not on PVD-targeted drug therapies. The decrease in mPAP/CO slope after additive sildenafil was robust, since we could not detect an effect of pretreatment on mPAP/CO -slope in a mixed-effects regression model. However, our study was designed to investigate haemodynamic changes of the mPAP/CO slope and not maximal exercise capacity. In our study design, patients had to exercise repeatedly, which was only possible while choosing two steps of mild-to-moderate exercise. Thus, we cannot answer whether maximal exercise capacity or training effects by exercising longer on a predefined level would have been increased by additive sildenafil in PVD. However, the intensity we have chosen may very well reflect the activities of daily living of those patients.

Of interest, during the 10-min resting phase before exercise, we did not observe changes in pulmonary haemodynamics, potentially as the majority of these patients were already on pulmonary vasodilator therapy, so that no further change at rest was noted. However, we observed a decline in mean BP of −9 (−13 to −5) mmHg, which was, however, not clinically relevant in the majority of patients, but one of 23 who was pretreated with a macitentan/tadalafil combination, felt dizzy and investigators decided that she should not undergo testing after additive sildenafil and she was excluded from this analysis [34]. The potential of PDE-5i to reduce BP is well known and has been described in men with erectile dysfunction who were tested at rest and while exercising after sildenafil (100 mg oral) [35]. The BP reduction detected in our study after additive sildenafil persisted at end-exercise; however, the increase in BP with exercise was similar before and after sildenafil. In contrast to resting values, at end-exercise after additive sildenafil the mPAP was reduced along with a strong tendency towards lower CO and PVR. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo- controlled trial in 34 patients with heart failure who developed PH, sildenafil reduced PVR and increased CO during exercise compared with placebo [36]. However, in that study sildenafil was given for 12 weeks and not as a single additive dose like in our trial, which revealed relevant haemodynamic changes already after ∼60 min. The tendency towards reduced PVR is likely explained by the reduced afterload, reflected by the lower PAP and the tendency towards increased CO [16]. In men with erectile dysfunction exercising with sildenafil, a decreased O2 pulse, which may serve as a surrogate of stroke volume, was found [35]. This was in line with a study in patients with stable angina, which revealed a mildly reduced stroke volume after intravenous administration of sildenafil 40 mg along with an increase in HR [35, 37]. In our study, as in other studies [28, 35, 36], an increased HR was found after sildenafil at rest, whereas end-exercise HR remained unaltered and the increase in HR during exercise was similar before and after additive sildenafil, however, these were iso-load and not peak-exercise measurements. The PAWP was similar before and after additive sildenafil at rest and end-exercise, so the reduced mPAP was not attributed to a reduced filling pressure.

Of interest, the single additive sildenafil dose did not alter blood oxygenation. The only parameter that was higher after additive sildenafil at rest was arterial lactate. A probable explanation for this finding may be that when patients exercised after sildenafil, they already exercised at least twice before, albeit at a submaximal load, but they might not have fully recovered. However, the lactate increase during exercise was significantly lower after sildenafil, which may be considered as a positive sign that along with the lower mPAP/CO slope the cardiopulmonary and muscular system was less stressed after additive sildenafil. However, the fact that V′O2 was unchanged at given iso-loads of relatively mild exercise in our study may argue against an improved exercise efficacy related to the improved mPAP/CO slope, but this remains to be determined. Numerous studies showed that sildenafil given for prolonged time improves exercise capacity in subjects with cardiopulmonary disease and PH as well as in healthy subjects [36, 38, 39]. Hence, a blunted slope of lactate during exercise can be interpreted as a less strenuous exercise due to sildenafil.

Limitations

The crossover study design is advantageous in rare diseases such as PVD and complex study designs, as each patient serves as its own perfect control, and therefore smaller sample sizes are required. On the other hand, repeated assessments including exercise may influence outcomes. We accounted for this problem by choosing only mild-to-moderate exercise levels and adding sufficient wash-out periods, especially since the presented data are part of a four-sequence crossover trial. Randomisation was not performed after additive sildenafil due to the inevitable carry-over effects. Thus, increasing physical fatigue during the assessments after additive sildenafil cannot be ruled out. However, we found no randomisation sequence effects in mixed linear regression models, which argues against relevant randomisation-order effects. Furthermore, whether patients were pretreated with PVD-targeted therapy or not or classified with PAH or CTEPH, did not influence the main outcome, although the population was not homogenous. Patients were not blinded for additive sildenafil. Our trial aimed to look at the haemodynamic effects during mild-to-moderate exercise, so we cannot report on the exercise-enhancing potential of sildenafil or longer-term patient-reported outcomes [40].

Conclusion

In this first study on the effects of an additive oral dose of 50 mg sildenafil on invasive pulmonary haemodynamics during exercise in patients with PVD, we found that sildenafil reduces the pressure–flow slope during mild-to-moderate iso-load exercise. The single additive dose of sildenafil was well tolerated, but one patient felt dizzy and did not exercise any further. However, a mild reduction in BP was found at rest and end-exercise, but the BP increase during exercise was similar. Whether the improved mPAP/CO slope after increased vasodilator therapy, as shown here, would result in improved exercise performance and if yes, whether dose increase would be most beneficial for patients when given for the entire day, including nights, or only during active times remains to be determined by future studies.
