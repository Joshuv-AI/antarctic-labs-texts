Spleen and liver shear wave elastography for assessment of portal hypertension in children with biliary atresia
Authors: Chao Geng, Jiaoyan Tan, Xueqiang Yan, Shiying Huang, Manli Fu, Li Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren with biliary atresia (BA) who survive with their native liver after Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE) remain at high risk of progressive portal hypertension (PHT). Reliable, non-invasive tools for longitudinal risk stratification are therefore essential in pediatric follow-up. To evaluate the diagnostic performance of liver stiffness value (LSV) and spleen stiffness value (SSV), individually and in combination, for predicting a surrogate endpoint of portal hypertension (PHT) in children with BA after KPE, and to characterize their temporal evolution following surgery. In this prospective cross-sectional study, 40 post-KPE BA children and 45 age-matched healthy controls underwent conventional ultrasound, Doppler evaluation, and shear wave elastography (SWE) of the liver and spleen. Surrogate PHT was defined by splenomegaly and thrombocytopenia. Group comparisons, Spearman correlations, generalized linear models, logistic regression, and receiver operating characteristic analysis were performed. Forty BA patients (20 female; median age 72 months [IQR 48–84]) and 45 controls (24 female; median age 84 months [IQR 48–120]) were analyzed. Both LSV and SSV were higher in the BA group (p < 0.001). SSV correlated strongly with spleen size, splenic vein diameter, and platelet count (all p < 0.01). In multivariate analysis, SSV was independently predicted by splenic vein velocity, while LSV was influenced by total bilirubin (p < 0.05). For predicting surrogate PHT, SSV (AUC, 0.78; 95% CI 0.63, 0.93) outperformed LSV (AUC, 0.76; 95% CI 0.61, 0.90). The combined model achieved an AUC of 0.80 (95% CI 0.67, 0.94). SSV is a robust, non-invasive biomarker for PHT in children with BA, reflecting portal hemodynamic adaptation and being less affected by cholestatic confounding than LSV. Introduction

Biliary atresia (BA) is a progressive cholangiopathy that remains the leading cause of pediatric end-stage liver disease and liver transplantation worldwide1. The Kasai portoenterostomy (KPE) is the established initial surgery aimed at restoring bile drainage, delaying disease progression, and prolonging native liver survival. Despite this procedure, a significant number of children experience progressive liver fibrosis2. Therefore, the non-invasive monitoring for disease progression is a central challenge in the longitudinal management of native liver survivors.

The pathophysiology of BA involves periportal fibrosis accompanied by vascular remodeling, which progressively leads to portal hypertension and its associated complications, including variceal hemorrhage, ascites, and hepatic encephalopathy3. Traditionally, the hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) is the gold standard for assessing PHT; however, it is invasive, requires anesthesia, and is technically challenging in children, making it impractical for routine longitudinal surveillance4. These constraints have created a critical need for reliable, non-invasive tools.

While various clinical prediction models such as the alanine transferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI), the Varices Prediction Rule (VPR), and the King’s Variceal Prediction Score (K-VaPS)5,6 and other imaging modalities like magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) have been explored for measuring liver and spleen stiffness7,8. Ultrasound-based shear wave elastography (SWE) has emerged as a valuable non-invasive technology due to its ease of use, reliability, and time-saving performance. The spleen, in contrast to the liver, offers a more direct window into the hemodynamic state of the portal system. Chronic portal venous congestion induces not only splenic enlargement but also parenchymal remodeling and fibrosis, leading to increased spleen stiffness values (SSV). Baveno VII consensus recognizes SSV as a powerful predictor of clinically significant PHT in adults3, a finding supported by systematic reviews9,10. However, these thresholds are unvalidated in pediatric cholangiopathies. Although a few studies11,12 have explored the combined use of liver and spleen elastography with conventional ultrasonographic markers, the influence of perioperative variables—such as age at KPE and duration since surgery—on hepatic and splenic stiffness has not been systematically characterized. Moreover, it remains unclear how these stiffness metrics evolve differently over the long-term postoperative course, reflecting the longitudinal progression of PHT.

We therefore aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of LSV and SSV, individually and in combination, for predicting surrogate PHT in children with BA after KPE. We hypothesized that incorporating spleen stiffness into conventional liver elastography assessments would enhance non-invasive risk stratification in this population.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

This prospective, cross-sectional study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Wuhan Children’s Hospital (2024R083-E01). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, revised in 2013. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or legal guardian.

Study design and participants

40 children diagnosed with BA who had undergone KPE were enrolled in the BA group, while 45 healthy children matched by gender and age were enrolled in the healthy group. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of liver transplantation, (2) acute infection, cholangitis, or decompensated liver failure at the time of examination, (3) incomplete clinical, imaging, or laboratory data, or (4) poor elastography quality, defined as an interquartile range-to-median ratio > 30% or an acquisition failure rate > 20%.

To further explore factors influencing hepatic and splenic stiffness after (KPE), patients in the BA group were stratified into three predefined subgroups for secondary analyses. (1) Age at KPE: participants were classified into three groups based on the timing of surgical intervention (< 60 days, 60–90 days, and > 90 days) to assess the effect of operative timing on postoperative hepatic and splenic parameters. (2) Postoperative follow-up duration: participants were categorized based on the time interval between KPE surgery and the elastography examination (< 5 years, 5–10 years, and > 10 years), reflecting progressive stages of long-term disease evolution after KPE. (3) Portal hypertension status: within the BA group, participants were further divided into a surrogate PHT group and a non-PHT group, defined by the combined presence of splenomegaly (> 2 cm below the left costal margin) and thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 150 × 10⁹/L).

Ultrasound and elastography examinations

All examinations were performed using an Aixplorer ultrasound system (SuperSonic Imagine, Aix-en-Provence, France) with an XC6-1 convex probe. A conventional ultrasound was first conducted to assess hepatic and splenic morphology, including liver size, echotexture, and vascular anatomy, as well as splenic dimensions and parenchymal features. Doppler imaging was subsequently performed to measure hemodynamic parameters (vessel diameter, peak flow velocities) of the portal vein, splenic vein, and splenic artery.

Within the same session, two experienced radiologists (J.Y.T, M.L.F, all > 5 years of elastography) blinded to clinical and laboratory data, performed SWE of the liver and spleen. Measurements were obtained from the right hepatic lobe and splenic parenchyma via an intercostal approach, with the patient in a supine or lateral position during quiet breathing or a breath-hold. At least five valid acquisitions were obtained from homogeneous parenchymal regions 1–2 cm below the capsule, avoiding large vessels. The median value was used for analysis, and data quality was ensured by an interquartile range-to-median (IQR/M) ratio of ≤ 30%. This standardized protocol is based on our prior work, which established its high intra- and inter-observer reproducibility of supersonic-SWE in post-KPE biliary atresia patients (ICC = 0.90 and 0.89, respectively)13.

Definition of surrogate portal hypertension endpoint

Portal hypertension (PHT) was not defined by direct portal pressure measurement in this study, as invasive hemodynamic assessment is not routinely performed in pediatric patients. Instead, PHT was interpreted in the context of clinically evident portal hypertension (CEPH), based on established clinical manifestations of portal hypertension in children, including splenomegaly, hypersplenism-related thrombocytopenia, portosystemic collaterals, ascites, or gastroesophageal varices14,15. In the present study, surrogate PHT was defined as the concurrent presence of platelet count < 150 × 10⁹/L together with palpable splenomegaly (> 2 cm below the costal margin).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 25.0, IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; https://www.graphpad.com). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparisons between the BA group and the healthy group were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Comparisons across the three stratified subgroups (age at KPE and follow-up duration) were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test with Dunn’s post-hoc correction. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to assess relationships between elastography values and other clinic-sonographic parameters. To identify independent predictors of liver and spleen stiffness, multivariate generalized linear models (GLM) were constructed. Variables with a p value < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into the models. To evaluate the diagnostic performance for surrogate PHT, binary logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) for LSV, SSV, and a combined model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic accuracy and determine the area under the curve (AUC). The DeLong test was used to compare the AUCs of the different models. For all models, assumptions were verified and collinearity diagnostics were performed to ensure robustness.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 40 children who underwent KPE and 45 healthy controls were included in the study, the detailed study inclusion and exclusion process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Compared to healthy controls, the BA group exhibited distinct biochemical and ultrasonographic profiles (Table 1). Biochemically, children with BA had significantly elevated liver enzymes but lower platelet counts and albumin levels. Ultrasonographically, the BA group demonstrated a significantly larger left hepatic lobe anteroposterior diameter, increased portal vein flow velocity, splenomegaly (increased spleen length and thickness), dilated splenic vein and artery diameters, and a higher splenic artery resistive index (RI). Crucially, both LSV and SSV were significantly higher in the BA group compared to controls.

Correlation analysis of age-related variations in splenic parameters among healthy controls

To evaluate the potential influence of physiological development on our findings, Spearman correlation analysis was performed within the healthy control group (Table 2). The analysis revealed that splenic morphological parameters (length and thickness) and the majority of vascular parameters (including diameters and flow velocities of both the splenic vein and artery) exhibited significant positive correlations with age. Conversely, SSV and splenic artery RI remained physiologically stable and demonstrated no significant correlation with age. These results suggest that while the pediatric spleen and its associated vessels undergo natural expansion during growth, intrinsic parenchymal stiffness remains independent of age within the studied range.

Comparison of biochemical and ultrasound parameters

Compared with healthy controls, BA group exhibited significantly altered clinical and sonographic parameters (Table 1; Fig. 2). The BA group showed lower PLT and albumin levels. In contrast, ALT and AST were significantly elevated in the BA group (all p < 0.001). Ultrasound measurements showed greater left lobe thickness, liver and spleen stiffness, spleen longitudinal and thickness diameters, splenic vein and artery diameters, portal vein velocity and splenic artery resistive index in the BA group (all p < 0.001).

Impact of age at follow-up on hepatic and splenic parameters after KPE

When stratified by age at follow-up (< 5 years, 5–10 years, and > 10 years), significant differences were observed in multiple clinical and sonographic parameters (Table 6). LSV showed an upward trend with increasing postoperative duration. This progression in stiffness was paralleled by significant biochemical changes: TBiL and DBiL levels were significantly elevated in the > 10 years group, while PLT and albumin levels declined significantly with longer follow-up. Morphologically, the spleen exhibited progressive enlargement, with significant increases in spleen length and thickness. Signs of portal hypertension became more pronounced over time, evidenced by significant dilation of the splenic vein and portal vein, as well as increased splenic artery diameter and altered hemodynamic parameters (elevated PSV and EDV). No significant differences were observed in SSV splenic vein velocity, portal vein velocity, or liver enzymes (ALT, AST).

Diagnostic performance of elastography for surrogate PHT

To evaluate the utility of elastography for predicting PHT, three logistic regression models were constructed (Table 7).

Subgroup analysis based on surrogate portal hypertension

Further analysis was conducted within the BA group to compare patients meeting the surrogate PHT criteria (PHT group, n = 13) against those who did not (non-PHT group, n = 27). As detailed in Table 8, the PHT group exhibited significantly higher median SSV and LSV compared to the non-PHT group (all p < 0.001). Consistent with established indicators of portal hypertension, the PHT group also showed significantly larger spleen length and thickness, increased portal and splenic vein diameters (all p < 0.001), and greater splenic artery diameter (p < 0.05). Biochemically, the PHT group had significantly lower platelet counts (p < 0.001) and albumin levels (p < 0.01), coupled with elevated direct and total bilirubin (all p < 0.01). No significant differences were found for hepatic left lobe diameter, portal or splenic vein velocities, splenic artery RI and EDV, or liver enzymes (ALT and AST).

Discussion

This study evaluated the utility of combined liver and spleen elastography for non-invasive risk stratification in children with BA following KPE. Consistent with the established pathophysiology of progressive periportal fibrosis and portal hypertensive remodelling in BA, both LSV and SSV were significantly elevated in the patient group compared with healthy controls, and both stiffness metrics showed meaningful associations with morphological and hemodynamic indicators of disease burden. Our principal finding is that SSV serves as a superior and independent non-invasive biomarker for predicting surrogate PHT compared with LSV. While the incremental statistical benefit of adding LSV to SSV was modest, the combined model offers a complementary physiological assessment that may enhance clinical confidence in uncertain cases.

A critical consideration in interpreting liver elastography within the context of BA is that LSV elevation does not solely reflect hepatic fibrosis. The hepatic lesion in BA is histologically compound: portal tracts show edematous expansion with bile ductular proliferation, inspissated bile plugs, and a neutrophilic infiltrate16,17, each of which elevates parenchymal stiffness through a distinct mechanism — periductal fibroplasia stiffens the portal compartment mechanically; biliary obstruction raises intrahepatic ductal pressure; and portal inflammation increases stiffness through oedema and cellular swelling, independent of fibrosis stage. Raizner et al.18 showed that hepatic inflammation elevates liver stiffness to advanced-fibrosis-range values in children with minimal histological fibrosis, and Stefanescu et al.19 reported that cholestasis and inflammation measurably confound 2D-SWE in children with BA and other chronic liver diseases. Since cholestasis and portal inflammation fluctuate episodically with recurrent cholangitis, a rising LSV in the post-KPE period may signal acute inflammatory or cholestatic deterioration rather than true structural progression, and must be interpreted accordingly. Our cohort data supports this dynamic, we observed a significant univariate correlation between TBiL and LSV (r = 0.40, p = 0.01, Table 3). In contrast, SSV, was not independently associated with bilirubin in any of our models, therefore provides a more direct and reliable indicator of the portal hemodynamic burden.

The superiority of SSV is further supported by our subgroup analysis. Among the elastography parameters, SSV showed greater relative separation between the PHT and non-PHT groups (z = − 2.58; median 32.75 vs. 27.05 kPa) than LSV (z = − 2.84; 12.66 vs. 11.13 kPa), consistent with the spleen’s role as the primary pressure reservoir of the portal system — chronic venous congestion and parenchymal remodelling amplify SSV elevation proportionally more than fibrosis-driven LSV changes20. This suggests that SSV integrates information on both hepatic structural changes and splenic venous stasis. These findings align with previous studies using endoscopy as the reference standard, which consistently identify SSV as the single best predictor of esophageal varices in pediatric cholestatic liver disease. A study by Sintusek et al.11 involving 51 BA children used endoscopy as the reference and found that SSV (AUC = 0.870) was the single best predictor for the presence of esophageal varices, significantly outperforming LSV (AUC = 0.734). They also noted that combining SSV and LSV increased diagnostic specificity to 93.3%, which parallels the trend we observed in our combined model. Similarly, a study involving 34 children with BA concluded that SSV was the single most accurate non-invasive predictor of high-risk esophagogastric varices, a performance superior to that of LSV and other commonly used clinical prediction scores21. A pilot study12 in BA patients also found that a combined model incorporating both liver and spleen stiffness measurements achieved a very high AUC of 0.92 for diagnosing high-risk esophagogastric varices.

Among all measured parameters, platelet count was the single strongest overall discriminator of PHT status (z = − 4.79, p < 0.001), reflecting hypersplenism-driven platelet sequestration validated across paediatric PHT cohorts22. Among Doppler vascular parameters, splenic vein diameter was the strongest discriminator (z = − 4.69, p < 0.001), directly reflecting portal venous back-pressure and splenic outflow obstruction, which is supported by its strong correlation with both SSV and LSV (Table 3). Portal vein diameterand spleen dimensions provided complementary morphological evidence of portal congestion and splenic volumetric adaptation. Albumin and bilirubin reflected hepatic synthetic dysfunction and cholestatic progression, while ALT and AST did not differ significantly between groups, confirming that transaminase elevation is an insensitive PHT marker in late-stage cholangiopathy23. Collectively, the most discriminatory parameters span three distinct domains — biochemical (platelet count, albumin), haemodynamic (splenic vein diameter, portal vein diameter), and parenchymal stiffness (SSV) — underscoring the value of a multi-parametric approach to PHT risk stratification.

A critical methodological consideration for this study was the definition of the PHT endpoint. The reference standard HVPG is invasive, technically challenging in children, and may underestimate portal pressure in BA due to the frequent presence of veno-venous communications14. Therefore, expert panels endorse the use of clinical signs to identify children at risk. Guidelines adapted from the Baveno consensus explicitly recommends splenomegaly and thrombocytopenia as triage markers for surveillance endoscopy14. The mechanistic rationale is grounded in portal haemodynamics: splenomegaly reflects chronic portal congestion and splenic engorgement, while thrombocytopenia reflects hypersplenism secondary to platelet sequestration. Both of which are unambiguous downstream consequences of sustained portal pressure elevation. A cochrane review further validated PLT for diagnosing varices in children24, confirming the construct validity of our composite endpoint.

Healthy age-matched controls underwent the full ultrasound protocol to establish centre-specific reference values under identical acquisition conditions, enabling valid between-group comparison of Doppler and morphological parameters. To directly address age-related confounding of splenic parameters, Spearman correlation analysis was performed within the control group (Table 2). This confirmed significant positive associations between age and splenic length (r = 0.61), splenic thickness (r = 0.72), and vascular diameters (all p < 0.001), consistent with physiological growth-related expansion of the spleen and its vasculature25. In contrast, SSV showed no significant correlation with age (r = − 0.16, p = 0.27), confirming its age-independence across the peadiatric range studied26. This stability is clinically important: elevated SSV in BA patients cannot be attributed to normal physiological maturation, and age-related variation in morphological parameters should be acknowledged when interpreting raw between-group comparisons.

A unique finding of our study is the differential temporal evolution of liver and spleen stiffness. While LSV exhibited a linear, progressive increase with longer follow-up duration, SSV showed an upward trend that did not reach statistical significance. This divergence likely reflects the physiological distinction between cumulative structural damage and dynamic hemodynamic adaptation. LSV tracks the inexorable accumulation of hepatic fibrosis27. In contrast, the spleen possesses a reservoir function. As evidenced by the significant splenomegaly in our long-term follow-up group (Table 5), the spleen enlarges to accommodate increased portal inflow. This volumetric expansion may initially buffer the intrasplenic pressure, keeping SSV relatively stable even as liver fibrosis progresses.

It is crucial to interpret our results within the context of our specific study cohort: a population of native liver survivors with favorable prognoses who underwent a relatively late KPE (median age at surgery ≈ 67 days; median follow-up ≈ 4.6 years). Our study did not reveal a significant association between the age at KPE and postoperative liver or spleen stiffness. While earlier surgery is generally associated with better long-term outcomes28–31, the survival of this cohort into late childhood without transplantation suggests a group with particularly favorable underlying disease biology. Recent investigations also highlight that even children undergoing late KPE may achieve long-term native liver survival32. Factors such as the anatomical type of BA, the frequency and severity of cholangitis, and the degree of postoperative bile drainage likely exert a more dominant influence on long-term outcomes than surgical timing alone33–36.

The clinical implications of our findings are substantial. For children living with a native liver after KPE, surveillance for PHT and esophageal varices is essential37,38. Our results suggest that integrating SSV measurement into routine ultrasound follow-up could create a more accurate, multidimensional risk assessment. This combined approach could help clinicians better identify patients at high risk for clinically significant PHT and optimize the timing of endoscopic screening.

This study acknowledges several key limitations. First, the prospective, single-center design inherently limits the generalizability of the findings and may introduce selection bias. Second, the surrogate definition of PHT was used as the endpoint, rather than the gold standard of endoscopic presence of high-risk varices or the HVPG. While this composite outcome reflects clinically pragmatic indicators, it does not fully capture the clinical spectrum of PHT severity. Third, the small sample size in the KPE age subgroups limits the statistical power to draw definitive conclusions regarding the long-term impact of surgical timing on elastography parameters. Furthermore, while the combined LSV and SSV model yielded a numerically higher AUC for predicting surrogate PHT, the improvement was not statistically significant compared to the LSV-only model. This is likely due to the small sample size of our cohort, which may have provided insufficient statistical power to detect a significant difference. Future multi-center, prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings against the reference standard of endoscopic variceal status and to establish pediatric-specific SSV cut-off values for identifying high-risk patients, potentially refining the management for this specialized cohort.

Conclusion

In summary, SSV demonstrated superior diagnostic performance for surrogate PHT compared with LSV, while the combination of both parameters further improved risk stratification in children with BA after KPE. The distinct temporal behaviors of liver and spleen stiffness underscore their complementary reflection of structural hepatic remodeling and portal hemodynamics. Integrating spleen stiffness measurement into routine ultrasound follow-up may provide a more sensitive and longitudinal assessment of risk, helping to identify patients with silently progressing PHT who might benefit from earlier endoscopic screening.
