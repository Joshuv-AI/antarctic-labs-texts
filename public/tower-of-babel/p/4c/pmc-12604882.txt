INTRODUCTION

While the majority of bone tumours are benign, malignant forms account for approximately 10% of cases[1]. Key considerations in the evaluation of bone tumours include patient age, tumour location, and radiological features.

Radiologically, bone tumours are classified as osteolytic, osteoblastic, or mixed, depending on the predominant pattern of bone change. Osteolytic lesions are frequently seen in clinical practice and are most often caused by metastases, multiple myeloma, or primary bone tumours such as giant cell tumour (GCT). Granulomatous diseases, including Langerhans cell histiocytosis and sarcoidosis, as well as brown tumours (BTs) associated with hyperparathyroidism, may share similar radiological findings.

Bone lesions associated with hypercalcemia are most commonly observed in osteolytic metastases, multiple myeloma, granulomatous diseases, and BTs, where increased osteoclastic activity or enhanced vitamin D metabolism leads to hypercalcemia with variable phosphate levels. Therefore, measurement of both serum calcium and parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels is essential for differentiating among these bone lesions.

Here, we report a case of a solitary osteolytic lesion of the talus presenting with bone pain, initially diagnosed as a GCT. During follow-up, a parathyroid adenoma was incidentally discovered, leading to endocrinological evaluation. This report discusses the diagnostic pathway and highlights key considerations in the differential

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 59-year-old woman with no known comorbidities presented to a primary care clinic with a 3-month history of right ankle pain. Lateral radiography showed an expansile lytic lesion in the talus with cortical thinning, disruption, and peripheral sclerosis (Fig. 1). The patient was referred to a tertiary orthopaedic department for further evaluation. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the right ankle showed a cortical defect with dorsal extension and peripheral sclerotic and calcific changes (Fig. 1).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a predominantly hypointense lesion on T1-weighted images, with cystic components, fluid–fluid levels, and posterior contrast enhancement (Fig. 2).

Based on the overall radiological appearance, a GCT of bone with an associated aneurysmal bone cyst component was considered. A talar biopsy was initially consistent with GCT and aneurysmal bone cyst, and surgical excision was performed by the orthopaedics team. Histopathology showed fibroblastic stroma with hemosiderin pigment and multinucleated giant cells, findings consistent with GCT.

The patient was referred to oncology, where a postoperative CT scan incidentally revealed a parathyroid adenoma and thyroid nodule without evidence of metastatic disease. The patient was subsequently referred to endocrinology for further management.

Laboratory evaluation demonstrated calcium 13.7 mg/dl (N: 8.5–10.5), phosphorus 2.8 mg/dl (N: 2.5–4.5), parathyroid hormone (PTH) 869 ng/l (N: 18.5–88), vitamin D 8 μg/l (N: 30–70), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) 250 U/l (N: 30–120), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 13 mm/h (N:0–30), haemoglobin 13.8 g/dl (N:11–15), albumin 45 g/l (N: 35–52), and globulin 25 g/l (N: 20–35). These laboratory findings were primarily consistent with primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) and were not suggestive of malignant aetiology. The patient was then admitted to our endocrinology clinic for further evaluation. Intravenous hydration and oral vitamin D replacement were initiated. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) revealed a T-score of 3.9 at the lumbar spine (L1–L4) and 1.5 at the femoral neck. Following a 5 mg intravenous zoledronic acid infusion, the serum calcium decreased to 10.5 mg/dl. Neck ultrasound revealed a 35 × 13 mm hypervascular lesion posterior to the right thyroid lobe and a 4 mm irregularly marginated nodule within the right thyroid lobe. Parathyroid scintigraphy confirmed intense technetium 99m sestamibi (Tc-99m MIBI) uptake in the lesion, further supporting the diagnosis of parathyroid adenoma (Fig. 3).

Given the biochemical and imaging findings consistent with PHPT associated with parathyroid adenoma, along with the uncommon location of the lesion for a GCT, the patient was re-evaluated, and the histopathological specimens were re-examined. In addition to the presence of fibroblastic stroma, hemosiderin pigment, and multinucleated giant cells, immunohistochemical staining for H3F3A was negative, supporting a revised diagnosis of a BT associated with PHPT (Fig. 4).

Review of a cranial CT scan performed 3 years earlier revealed a “salt-and-pepper” skull, consistent with hyperparathyroidism. Current skull radiography showed similar findings (Fig 5). No other skeletal lesions were detected.

The patient underwent right inferior parathyroidectomy and right lobectomy. Histopathology confirmed a 3.5 cm hypercellular parathyroid adenoma and papillary thyroid microcarcinoma. Postoperatively, calcium and phosphorus levels normalized, and vitamin D therapy was continued. At 1 year, the patient remained asymptomatic with normal calcium, phosphorus, and PTH levels. Bone scintigraphy showed uptake only at the right ankle, corresponding to the site of BT excision, with no additional lesions. The patient received 5 mg of zoledronic acid and has remained stable on routine follow-up.

DISCUSSION

BTs are benign osteolytic lesions caused by hyperparathyroidism. Radiologically, BTs and GCTs can appear similar, presenting as lytic, expansile lesions with cortical thinning and peripheral sclerosis. Due to their similarity on imaging, BTs are frequently misdiagnosed as GCTs[2,3].

BTs usually affect females in their third to fifth decades, while GCTs present between 20 and 50 years of age, with peak incidence in the third decade. BTs most commonly occur in the mandible, ribs, pelvis, and clavicle, whereas GCTs typically arise in the distal femur and proximal tibia. Our patient’s presentation was unusual both in terms of age and talar involvement. The talus is rarely affected by either entity; in a 25-year case series, the incidence of talar GCT was reported to be only 0.4%[4]. Although talar BTs have been reported in the literature, they are exceedingly rare[5], and this uncommon localization further complicated the diagnostic process.

Another distinctive feature of our case was the solitary nature of the lesion. BTs usually show multifocal skeletal involvement in 2–5% of PHPT cases[6]. In contrast, our patient had a single talar lesion, with only diffuse skeletal changes such as the “salt-and-pepper” appearance of the skull. Because these lesions may not present typically in clinical practice, additional biochemical evaluation is important in the diagnostic process.

When a bone lesion is encountered, serum calcium measurement is a critical first step. Hypercalcemia narrows the differential diagnosis to metastatic disease, multiple myeloma or plasmacytoma, granulomatous disorders (such as sarcoidosis and Langerhans cell histiocytosis), and BTs associated with hyperparathyroidism. In GCT of bone, however, hypercalcemia is not expected. PTH measurement further refines the distinction: elevated PTH in the setting of hypercalcemia with low-to-normal phosphorus supports hyperparathyroidism-related BTs, whereas suppressed PTH with hyperphosphatemia suggests granulomatous disease or malignancy. Similar cases have been reported in which hypercalcemia with osteolytic lesions initially mimicked myeloma or metastasis, but elevated PTH confirmed BTs[7,8].

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry should be performed only after assessing calcium and PTH levels. At the pathological stage, it is crucial to inform the pathologist about serum calcium and PTH levels, as lesions occurring with or without hypercalcemia often have distinct aetiologies. Additionally, BTs and GCTs may share similar histological features, both containing fibrous stroma, hemosiderin deposits, and multinucleated giant cells. When morphology is equivocal, immunohistochemistry is particularly helpful. In our case, the absence of H3F3A staining in giant cells supported BT, since GCTs typically demonstrate H3F3A positivity[9]. Even in cases of PHPT, GCT of bone may rarely occur[10], and in such cases, H3F3A immunostaining can play an important role in establishing the diagnosis.

In our patient, the lesion was initially considered a GCT; however, subsequent biochemical assessment indicating PHPT, along with histopathological correlation and H3F3A negativity on immunohistochemistry, supported the diagnosis of a BT.

CONCLUSION

This case underscores the importance of integrating radiological, biochemical, and pathological data in the evaluation of bone lesions. Measurement of serum calcium and PTH remains an essential first step, as hypercalcemia should prompt consideration of BT along with malignant and granulomatous causes.
