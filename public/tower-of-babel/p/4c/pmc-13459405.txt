Cephalomedullary nailing of trochanteric fractures in geriatric patients restores natural hip geometry - a retrospective radiological analysis
Authors: Philippe Siegenthaler, Manuel Waltenspül, Cléa Nüesch, Marie-Garance Meuwly, Michael Dietrich, Method Kabelitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrochanteric fractures are common in geriatric patients, and cephalomedullary nailing is widely used. However, its ability to restore and maintain native hip geometry over time remains unclear.MethodsIn this retrospective single-center study, patients aged ≥ 75 years treated with cephalomedullary nailing between June 2022 and August 2024 were included. Centrum-collum-diaphyseal (CCD) angle, femoral offset, and leg length discrepancy (LLD) were assessed on the operated and contralateral sides postoperatively and at final follow-up (minimum 6 weeks). Changes over time and correlations with the cortical thickness index (CTI) and AO/OTA classification were analyzed. Implant-related complications were systematically recorded in the overall cohort and analyzed descriptively.ResultsSixty-six patients (mean age 86.2 ± 6.4 years, 76% female) were included. Postoperatively, CCD angle and femoral offset were restored close to the contralateral side without significant differences (p > 0.05). At final follow-up (mean 142 days), both parameters decreased significantly on the operated side (p ≤ 0.005) but remained comparable to the contralateral side (p > 0.05).LLD increased significantly over time (− 3.0 ± 3.3 mm to − 4.2 ± 4.0 mm, p < 0.001). No correlation was found between CTI, fracture classification, and geometric changes. In the overall study cohort, implant-related complications occurred in 13 of 150 patients (8.7%), with no significant independent predictors identified in exploratory multivariable regression analysis.ConclusionCephalomedullary nailing was associated with near-anatomical restoration of the proximal femoral geometry in geriatric trochanteric fractures in the short term, despite the nail's dynamic compression mechanism. However, reductions in CCD angle, femoral offset, and leg length occur during the healing phase, underscoring the importance of achieving an optimal initial fracture reduction. These findings are exploratory, and prospective studies are needed to clarify their clinical relevance.Level of evidenceLevel III, Therapeutic studyKeywords: Trochanteric fracture, Geriatric, Frailty, Cephalomedullary nail, Osteoporosis

Introduction

Cervical and trochanteric hip fractures are among the most common fractures in the elderly population and are considered severe osteoporotic fractures due to their high associated morbidity. These fractures potentially have an impact in geriatric patients, resulting in increased frailty and diminished quality of life [1–5]. Consequently, the probability of subsequent low-energy falls and additional fractures is elevated [4–6]. In recent decades, the average age of the population has increased at a consistent rate. Recent literature indicates that in certain geographic regions, the proportion of the population aged over 65 years exceeds 19% [7]. The rising prevalence of trochanteric fractures can be attributed to the ongoing demographic shifts in the population [4]. A recent study projects that by 2050, the incidence of hip fractures in the elderly population will approximately double [8].

For femoral neck fractures treated with total- and hemi arthroplasties, it has been established that the femoral offset exerts a significant influence on the subsequent gait pattern. Higher values have been observed to result in a more stable gait [9]. In the context of trochanteric fractures treated with cephalomedullary nailing, an inverse correlation has been demonstrated, indicating that a decreased offset is associated with enhanced functional outcomes [10].

Several authors have investigated postoperative radiographic changes following cephalomedullary fixation of trochanteric fractures, particularly regarding fracture-related shortening and leg length discrepancy (LLD), as well as their potential influence on functional outcome [11–13]. In addition, previous studies have evaluated postoperative femoral neck shaft angle restoration and maintenance of proximal femoral alignment following cephalomedullary fixation [14–16]. A recent prospective study by Wittauer et al. [17] further demonstrated that no substantial changes in the centrum-collum-diaphyseal (CCD) angle were observed during short-term follow-up after cephalomedullary fixation. Recent literature has additionally emphasized the importance of fracture reduction quality, medial cortical support, and overall construct stability for postoperative mechanical outcomes following cephalomedullary fixation [18, 19].

Nevertheless, despite increasing literature on postoperative radiographic parameters and mechanical stability, studies directly comparing postoperative hip geometry with the unaffected contralateral side remain limited. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether postoperative hip geometry correlates with the level of local bone mineral density, as indirectly measured using the femoral cortical thickness index (CTI).

The primary objective of this retrospective case series and radiographic analysis of geriatric patients was to examine the radiographic restoration of the natural hip geometry (CCD angle, femoral offset, LLD) following cephalomedullary nailing in comparison to the unaffected contralateral side. Furthermore, a correlation analysis was conducted between the CTI, the fracture classification according to the Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Osteosynthesefragen/Orthopaedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA), and the effects on the aforementioned radiologic measurements. In addition, implant-related complications were descriptively recorded as a secondary outcome to provide additional clinical context.

Materials and methods

We retrospectively analyzed all patients aged 75 years or older with an informed consent who sustained a trochanteric fracture (AO/OTA: 31A1.2, 31A1.3, 31A2.2, 31A2.3, 31A3.1, 31A3.2, 31A3.3) and underwent surgical fixation with a cephalomedullary femoral nail (TFNA, DePuy Synthes, Zuchwil, Switzerland) at our institution between June 2022 and August 2024, following the implementation of our institutional database. A minimum follow-up of 6 weeks was required for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included previous surgical intervention on the contralateral hip, missing postoperative or follow-up radiographs, pathological fractures. Implant-related complications, including cases requiring revision surgery, were systematically recorded and analyzed separately. However, anatomical restoration parameters such as CCD angle, femoral offset, and LLD were not assessed in revision cases, as implant failure mechanisms and secondary reconstructive procedures precluded reliable radiographic measurements. This study was approved by the local ethical committee (BASEC 2024–00458).

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed by five board-certified orthopedic trauma surgeons with substantial experience in the surgical treatment of proximal femoral fractures.

Patients were treated in the supine position on a traction table under either general or spinal anesthesia. Fracture reduction was achieved using closed, semi-open, or open techniques by surgical preference and fracture complexity. Accurate fracture reduction and correct placement was confirmed under fluoroscopic control.

Fixation was performed using a cephalomedullary femoral nail (TFNA, DePuy Synthes, Zuchwil, Switzerland) with a CCD angle of 125°, according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Both short (200 mm) and long nails were used. Nail diameter was determined intraoperatively based on fluoroscopic assessment of the intramedullary canal. All nails were distally locked. Short nails were distally locked in a static configuration. Long nails were distally locked using one screw in a static hole and an additional screw placed in the dynamic (elongated) slot, allowing for potential secondary dynamization. A comprehensive documentation of intraoperative parameters was conducted, encompassing the reduction technique, additional cerclage osteosynthesis, and drainage application.

Patient’s demographics

Demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, body mass index (BMI), relevant comorbidities, and mechanism of injury, were recorded.

Radiographic measurements

Radiographic measurements were performed using a standardized Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) (DeepUnity, Dedalus Global, Italy), and measurements were carried out with specialized planning software (mediCAD clinical software Version 6.5; Hectec, Altfraunhofen, Germany). Measurements of the CCD angle, femoral offset, and leg length were conducted in accordance with established techniques as delineated in prior studies [20–23]. The tip-apex distance (TAD) was calculated using anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiographs and axial hip views, following the methods of Baumgaertner et al. [24, 25]. The bilateral CTI was measured in the preoperative radiographs according to Sah et al. [25].

Measurements, as illustrated in Fig. 1, were obtained from both the unaffected femur and the operated side at two predefined time points: immediately postoperatively following initial mobilization and at the latest available follow-up. A minimum follow-up of 6 weeks was required for inclusion; however, the exact timing of the final follow-up varied depending on clinical scheduling.

Example of measurements of the centrum-collum-diaphyseal (CCD) angle (red line), femoral offset (yellow line), and leg length discrepancy (LLD) (blue line) on a standardized antero-posterior pelvis x-ray

Comparative analyses were conducted to evaluate differences between the operated and unaffected sides, as well as changes on the operated side over time. Specifically, CCD angles were compared between the contralateral unaffected side and the operated side at both initial postoperative and follow-up assessments. Additionally, changes in the CCD angle of the operated side over time were analyzed, defined as the interval between the immediate postoperative radiograph and the latest follow-up radiograph. Femoral offset and LLD were assessed using the same comparative approach.

All measurements were performed independently by two observers, a board-certified orthopedic surgeon (S.P.) and an orthopedic surgery resident (M.M.). Preoperative fracture classification was conducted according to the AO/OTA classification system [26].

Complications

Implant-related complications were systematically recorded. Postoperative implant positioning parameters including TAD, blade position, subchondral blade distance and reduction quality were evaluated in these cases.

However, anatomical restoration parameters such as CCD angle, femoral offset, and LLD were not assessed in revision cases because implant failure and secondary reconstructive procedures precluded reliable radiographic measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) using pseudonymized data. Descriptive statistics, including mean, range, and standard deviation, were reported. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages. T-test was used for normally distributed values. The Wilcoxon test was used for values that were not normally distributed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Interobserver reliability was assessed by using the ICC, calculated based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement for single measurements. Furthermore, correlation analyses were conducted using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. In addition, a multivariable regression analysis was performed, and results were reported as regression coefficients (β) and coefficient of determination (R2). A post hoc power analysis showed that with a sample size of 66 patients, the study had a power of 0.88 for CCD and 0.81 for offset discrepancy between the immediate postoperative and the follow-up x-ray at a level of significance of 0.05. Power calculation was performed with G-power (Version 3.1; Heinrich Heine University).

Results

One-hundred fifty patients with informed consent, aged 75 years or older underwent surgical fixation of trochanteric femoral fractures using a cephalomedullary femoral nail at our institution. After the implementation of the predefined exclusion criteria, 84 patients were excluded from radiologic analysis. Radiographic assessment could not be performed in four patients who either remained non-ambulatory or passed away before initial postoperative imaging. Furthermore, 41 patients were not available for follow-up examinations due to reasons such as relocation, immobility, or death. The final study cohort included 66 patients (Fig. 2).

Patient’s demographics

Of the 66 patients, 50 (76%) were female. Age and BMI showed an approximately normal distribution with slight skewness and are therefore presented as mean ± standard deviation. Mean age was 86.2 ± 6.4 years (range 75–101), and mean BMI was 23.2 ± 4.2 kg/m2. The ASA score, as an ordinal variable, is reported as median (IQR), with a median of 3 (IQR 3–3). All fractures resulted from low-energy trauma, specifically falls from standing height.

A total of 58 patients (87.9%) demonstrated evidence of cognitive impairment. The most common comorbidities were arterial hypertension (50.0%), renal insufficiency (22.7%), hypothyroidism (19.7%), atrial fibrillation (16.7%), type II diabetes mellitus (16.7%), and coronary artery disease (13.6%). Overall, 58 patients (87.9%) presented with more than one relevant comorbidity.

Surgical information

The closed reduction technique was employed in the majority of cases (61 patients (92%)). A mini-open reduction was necessary in five cases (8%), while no open reductions were required. A long TFNA nail (CCD angle 125°) was used in four cases (6%), all of which were AO/OTA 31A3 fractures [26]. In the remaining cases (94%), a short TFNA nail (200 mm, CCD angle 125°) was applied. In three cases (4.5%), additional cerclage osteosynthesis was performed. Postoperative drainage was applied in three patients (4.5%). No secondary dynamization was performed in this cohort. Radiographic measurements

The mean radiographic follow-up was 142 ± 140.9 (42–706) days. The mean TAD was 19.3 ± 7.1 mm. The preoperative femoral CTI were 0.51 ± 0.1 on the unaffected and 0.43 ± 0.1 on the affected side.

Centrum-collum-diaphyseal angle

The mean CCD angle of the unaffected side was 124.9 ± 5.5°, compared with 125.1 ± 6.3° on the operated side immediately postoperatively, with no statistically significant difference (95% CI: −0.94—1.45; p = 0.67). At final follow-up, the mean CCD angle of the operated side had decreased to 123.7 ± 6.7°. This presents a statistically significant decrease of the CCD at final follow-up compared with the immediate postoperative measurement on the operated side (95% CI: 0.53–2.28; p = 0.002). However, no statistically significant change occurred between the unaffected side and the final follow-up (95% CI: −0.26 – 2.55; p = 0.109). Interobserver reliability was excellent with all ICC > 0.9.

Femoral offset

The mean femoral offset of the unaffected side was 41.2 ± 5.6 mm compared with 41.2 ± 6.5 mm on the operated side immediately postoperatively, showing no statistically significant difference (95% CI: −1.20 – 1.26; p = 0.96).

Over time, a statistically significant reduction in femoral offset was observed on the operated side, decreasing to 40.2 ± 6.5 mm at final follow-up (95% CI: 0.31–1.73; p = 0.005).

Despite this reduction, the femoral offset at final follow-up remained comparable to the unaffected side, with no statistically significant difference (95% CI: −0.36 – 2.35; p = 0.148). Once more, the interobserver reliability was found to be excellent, with all ICC values exceeding 0.9. Leg length discrepancy

LLD assessment was not possible in 7 of 66 patients due to missing radiographic reference points. The mean LLD between the operated and unaffected sides was −3.0 ± 3.3 mm immediately postoperative and increased to −4.2 ± 4.0 mm at final follow-up. Interobserver reliability was moderate, with ICC values of 0.63 and 0.67 (p < 0.001). Paired comparison using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed a statistically significant increase in LLD over time (95% CI: −1.5 – −0.50; p < 0.001). Complications

Patients requiring revision surgery due to implant-related complications were excluded from the radiographic restoration analysis of CCD angle, femoral offset, and LLD, as reliable assessment was not feasible following implant failure and revision surgery.

Implant-related postoperative complications occurred in 13 of 150 patients (8.7%). Revision surgery was performed in 12 patients, while one patient declined operative treatment. Complications included femoral neck blade cut-in (n = 3), cut-out (n = 3), lateral blade protrusion (n = 2), progressive symptomatic osteoarthritis requiring conversion to total hip arthroplasty (n = 2), and peri-implant femoral fractures (n = 2).

A multivariable regression analysis was performed to explore potential predictors of postoperative implant-related complications. Evaluated variables included demographic factors (sex, age, BMI, ASA classification), fracture reduction quality, and implant positioning parameters such as TAD, blade position, and subchondral blade distance. No statistically significant associations with complications were identified in the exploratory regression analysis; however, the limited number of complication events likely reduced statistical power.

Additional findings

A correlation analysis was performed to assess potential associations between bone quality, fracture classification, and radiographic outcomes. Specifically, the femoral CTI was correlated with changes in femoral offset and CCD angle, while AO/OTA fracture classification was analyzed in relation to postoperative CCD angle and offset loss. No statistically significant correlation was found between CTI and changes in CCD angle (r = 0.050, p = 0.690) or femoral offset (r = 0.061, p = 0.629). Likewise, the AO/OTA fracture classification did not correlate significantly with postoperative CCD angle (r = −0.026, p = 0.836) or offset loss (r = 0.196 p = 0.115).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether cephalomedullary nailing restores hip geometry in geriatric trochanteric fractures compared to the unaffected, contralateral side. The principal finding of this study is that initial restoration of the CCD angle and femoral offset relative to the contralateral hip was achieved, and this restoration was maintained at final follow-up without a statistically significant difference compared to the unaffected side.

However, both parameters showed a significant decline over time on the operated side. For the CCD angle and the femoral offset, this decrease did not result in a significant difference compared to the unaffected side at either time point. In contrast, LLD could not be fully corrected at any time point and progressively increased over the course of follow-up.

Centrum-collum-diaphyseal angle

The native CCD angle appeared to be restored immediately postoperatively, with no statistically significant difference compared to the contralateral, unaffected side. Over time, a significant decrease in the CCD angle was observed on the operated side. However, this did not result in a statistically significant difference compared to the unaffected side at final follow-up.

This finding may be explained by an initially slight valgus reduction, followed by a mild varus shift over time relative to the contralateral side. Accordingly, the progressive decrease in the CCD angle observed in our cohort on the operated side is consistent with the concept of secondary varus collapse over time, particularly in patients with compromised bone quality. In this context, Chang et al. [18] emphasized the importance of reduction quality and positive medial cortical support (PMCS) for maintaining postoperative mechanical stability following fixation of extracapsular hip fractures. The authors observed that insufficient medial support may predispose to secondary varus displacement and progressive loss of reduction over time.

Pajarinen et al. [23] investigated unstable trochanteric femoral fractures and demonstrated that medialization of the femoral shaft is frequently associated with a reduction in the CCD angle, thereby predisposing to varus deformity. Similarly, Jiamton et al. [27] analyzed radiographic parameters after cephalomedullary nailing with the PFNA and identified the nail–shaft axis as a key prognostic factor for postoperative stability. They reported that even adequately reduced fractures tended to shift into slight varus, particularly during the early postoperative period, and therefore recommended achieving a modest valgus reduction. In line with this, Marmor et al. [28] demonstrated in a biomechanical analysis that reducing varus forces acting on the fixation construct decreases the mechanical burden on the implant, and further suggested that slight valgus alignment may enhance construct stability by increasing compressive forces at the fracture site. Consistent with these findings, Andruszkow et al. [29] examined risk factors for screw cut-out in trochanteric fractures and emphasized the importance of achieving optimal reduction, noting that a valgus alignment of 5–10° was associated with a tendency toward reduced cut-out rates.

Our findings are consistent with these reports, as we observed an initial restoration or even slightly overcorrection of the CCD angle followed by a progressive decrease during follow-up, consistent with the concept of secondary varus collapse, particularly in patients with compromised bone quality. Our findings may therefore support the recommendation to aim for a slight valgus overcorrection to counteract this tendency.

In contrast to these findings, a recent prospective study by Wittauer et al. [17] reported no substantial changes in the CCD angle following cephalomedullary fixation over a short-term follow-up period within the early postoperative phase. This discrepancy may be explained by differences in follow-up duration, as early assessments may not fully capture progressive varus changes over time.

Taken together, these findings suggest that even initially well-reduced fractures may undergo subtle progressive varus changes over time, although the clinical relevance of these alterations remains uncertain.

Femoral offset

In our cohort, femoral offset could be restored nearly anatomically after cephalomedullary nailing, yet a gradual loss was observed during follow-up, reaching statistical significance compared to immediate postoperative values. This finding corresponds with earlier studies highlighting offset reduction as a frequent phenomenon following intramedullary fixation of trochanteric fractures [23, 30, 31]. Unlike in hip arthroplasty—where insufficient offset restoration is clearly associated with impaired function, instability, increased joint reaction forces, and reduced implant longevity [9, 32–35]—the clinical significance in fracture fixation remains less well established.

Interestingly, in contrast to observations in hip arthroplasty, Paul et al. [22] reported that patients with a moderate decrease in femoral offset after cephalomedullary nailing did not exhibit inferior outcomes. Despite radiographic signs of telescoping and offset reduction – particularly in unstable fractures – these patients achieved comparable single-leg stance times and Harris Hip Scores at one-year follow-up. Similarly, Buecking et al. [10] reported a trend toward improved hip function with lower post-traumatic femoral offset, while offset overcorrection was linked to worse hip-specific and general outcome scores. These findings suggest that controlled impaction and fracture compression may compensate for offset loss and may even contribute to functional recovery.

In contrast, Gordon et al. [30] reported that excessive offset reduction was associated with inferior functional outcomes and a higher prevalence of lateral hip pain due to implant compression and lateral hip screw prominence. Likewise, Sivankumar et al. [31] analyzed 61 patients treated with cephalomedullary nails and assessed changes in gait biomechanics. They observed a significant reduction in femoral offset, with a moderate negative correlation between offset loss and gait biomechanics.

In the current study, the observed decline in offset may reflect a combination of controlled impaction at the fracture site and subtle implant migration in osteoporotic bone, without clinical implant failure. In our cohort, the magnitude of femoral offset reduction was limited, and no statistically significant difference compared to the unaffected contralateral side was observed at final follow-up, suggesting that this radiographic change is unlikely to be clinically relevant.

However, the clinical impact of this finding was not systematically evaluated in the present study, and thus our results should be interpreted in the context of previous reports suggesting that the functional relevance of offset loss after cephalomedullary fixation remains uncertain.

Leg length discrepancy

Complete restoration of leg length was not achieved at any time point, and LLD further increased during follow-up. This highlights a key limitation of cephalomedullary nailing in unstable trochanteric fractures: while angular and rotational alignment can be restored intraoperatively, restoring, and maintaining axial length over time remains challenging. Consistent with our findings, Zhang et al. [36] reported that although cephalomedullary devices provide reliable control of rotation and alignment, they do not consistently prevent axial shortening, particularly in complex fracture patterns and osteoporotic bone. Moreover, Platzer et al. [13] demonstrated that LLD after cephalomedullary nailing may occur even in younger patients, underscoring that this limitation is not confined to elderly or osteoporotic populations. The current study is in line with this, underscoring that standard implants may be insufficient to preserve leg length throughout fracture consolidation. The clinical relevance of LLD has been well established in arthroplasty, where even minor differences can impair gait and patient satisfaction [37]. Although the surgical context differs, the biomechanical principle is comparable. In accordance with this, Pearce et al. [38] demonstrated that patients over 65 years of age showed reduced gait speed and inferior clinical outcomes following cephalomedullary nailing when LLD was present, irrespective of its severity. On the other hand, Platzer et al. [13] found no differences in Harris Hip Score outcomes between patients with femoral shortening and those without LLD after femoral nailing, suggesting that moderate discrepancies may not necessarily translate into measurable functional impairment. In our cohort, the mean LLD at final follow-up was –4.2 mm, which is comparatively small relative to previous reports [13, 38]. As no dedicated clinical assessments were performed, the true functional impact of this finding remains uncertain. However, considering that Röder et al. [39] demonstrated in the setting of total hip arthroplasty that leg length inequality becomes clinically relevant only at discrepancies of approximately 10 mm or greater, we assume that the LLD observed in our study is unlikely to be of clinical significance. Techniques such as intentional valgus reduction, use of cement augmentation for blade fixation in osteoporotic bone, calcar-replacing implants, or use of nails with integrated mechanisms to resist collapse may mitigate varus deformity, offset loss and axial shortening [36].

In this context, it is noteworthy that the observed decrease in CCD angle, femoral offset, and LLD was smaller than anticipated; however, the true clinical relevance of these findings remains uncertain.

Additional findings

Implant-related complications were systematically recorded as a secondary descriptive outcome to provide clinical context regarding implant performance following cephalomedullary nailing. Although no statistically significant predictors of complications were identified in the exploratory regression analysis, the relatively low number of complication events may have limited statistical power. Patients requiring revision surgery were excluded from the primary radiographic restoration analysis, as reliable assessment of CCD angle, femoral offset, and LLD was not feasible following implant failure and revision surgery.

While TAD has traditionally been considered a major predictor of fixation failure [24], more recent literature increasingly emphasizes the multifactorial nature of implant-related complications following cephalomedullary fixation. In particular, fracture reduction quality, varus alignment, medial cortical support, and overall construct stability have emerged as important prognostic factors beyond isolated implant positioning parameters alone. Chang et al. [18] highlighted the importance of reduction quality and positive medial cortical support in maintaining postoperative mechanical stability and reducing fixation failure in extracapsular hip fractures. Similarly, Hernández-Pascual et al. [19] emphasized the complex and multifactorial nature of implant failure following fixation of extracapsular hip fractures. These concepts are in line with the present findings, in which no independent predictors of complications could be identified in the exploratory regression analysis.

Previous studies have demonstrated a significant correlation between CTI and bone mass density (BMD) [25, 40, 41]. To date, however, the potential association between CTI and postoperative hip geometry has not been investigated. A positive correlation could be postulated between patients with lower BMD, as indicated by a smaller CTI and a decreased postoperative femoral offset. However, in our series, CTI values showed no correlation with postoperative changes in CCD angle or femoral offset. Similarly, AO/OTA fracture classification did not predict offset loss. There is a clear need for prospective studies that analyze postoperative geometric changes and bone density parameters.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design, relatively small final cohort size, and substantial attrition during follow-up may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. In this frail geriatric population, patients who died, became immobile, relocated, or experienced severe medical decline were less likely to complete radiographic follow-up. Although a post hoc power analysis demonstrated adequate power for the primary paired comparisons of radiographic parameters over time, the study was likely underpowered for secondary analyses, including correlation and exploratory multivariable regression analyses.

Secondly, radiographic measurements were derived from standard anteroposterior pelvic radiographs without rotation-corrected imaging. Given the known influence of femoral rotation on projected CCD angle and femoral offset measurements, some degree of measurement bias cannot be excluded despite standardized acquisition and bilateral comparison techniques. Furthermore, assessment of LLD was not feasible in all patients, particularly in more complex fracture patterns.

Thirdly, the timing of postoperative and follow-up radiographs was not strictly standardized. Since postoperative fracture settling, collapse, and reduction loss after cephalomedullary fixation may evolve dynamically over time, variability in imaging intervals may have introduced temporal confounding and influenced the magnitude and detectability of geometric changes.

Fourthly, although implant-related complications were systematically recorded, the relatively small number of complication events limited statistical power, particularly for exploratory regression analysis, increasing the risk of type II error.

Exclusion of revision cases from the primary radiographic restoration analysis may have led to underrepresentation of severe mechanical failure patterns. In addition, anatomical restoration parameters such as CCD angle, femoral offset, and LLD could not be reliably assessed in revision cases following implant failure and revision surgery.

Fifthly, osteoporosis was not directly quantified using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA); instead, the CTI was used as an indirect surrogate for bone quality. Furthermore, although relevant comorbidities and cognitive impairment were documented descriptively, these variables were not incorporated into the primary statistical analysis due to the limited cohort size, heterogeneous comorbidity patterns, and restricted number of complication events.Consequently, residual confounding related to bone quality, frailty, and overall health status cannot be excluded.

Finally, no dedicated clinical outcome measures such as patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), gait analysis, or pain scores were assessed. Therefore, the functional relevance of the observed radiographic changes remains uncertain.

Given the retrospective design, heterogeneous follow-up, and limited number of complication events, the present findings should primarily be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

Cephalomedullary nailing was associated with near-anatomical restoration of the proximal femoral geometry in geriatric trochanteric fractures in the short term, despite the nail's dynamic compression mechanism. However, reductions in CCD angle, femoral offset, and leg length occur during the healing phase, underscoring the importance of achieving an optimal initial fracture reduction. These findings are exploratory, and prospective studies are needed to clarify their clinical relevance.
