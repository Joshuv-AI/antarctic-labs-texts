Gut microbiota and kidney aging: insights into current research
Authors: Yalda Rahbar Saadat, Abolfazl Barzegari, Zahra Saadatian, Soheila Montazersaheb, Sepideh Zununi Vahed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aging induces structural and functional alterations in the kidneys, including changes in renal morphology and progressive decline in renal function. During aging, the gut microbiota undergoes profound shifts in composition and activity, transitioning from predominantly commensal to more pathogenic communities. Renal dysfunction further exacerbates this process by reducing toxin clearance and promoting the accumulation of uremic metabolites, which disrupt gut microbial balance. In turn, gut dysbiosis impairs kidney function, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of microbial imbalance and renal damage. Hence, breaking this vicious cycle of dysbiosis and kidney damage is important. This review sheds light on the bidirectional relationship between gut microbiota and kidney aging. It also highlights the potential of microbiota-targeted interventions to restore microbial balance and delay the onset of age-related issues.

Introduction

The global population of elderly individuals is increasing rapidly. By 2050, the number of people aged 60 years and older is projected to double (2.1 billion) [1], which presents significant public health challenges. Aging is an inevitable and multifaceted phenomenon that progresses over time, leading to the gradual decline of physiological capabilities and an increased vulnerability to age-related disorders and mortality [2].

As a part of the human body, the kidney is inevitably exposed to age-related alterations [3]. Kidney aging is generally defined by a reduction in kidney mass and functional glomeruli associated with glomerular hypertrophy, fibrosis, fibro-intimal hyperplasia, and atherosclerosis [4]. Moreover, as life expectancies continue to rise, the prevalence of age-associated ailments such as exposure to nephrotoxic pharmaceuticals, diabetes, and hypertension is increasing [5]. These elements play a significant role in kidney dysfunction, further facilitating the aging of the kidneys and intensifying the overall aging process. As a result, identifying strategies to mitigate the aging of kidney tissue has emerged as an urgent priority within clinical settings.

Among the influential factors in the aging process, it is increasingly evident that the accumulation of senescent cells and alterations in the gut microbiota have critical roles [6–8]. The complex interplay between cellular senescence and gut microbiome alterations constitutes a central axis in the aging process, significantly contributing to systemic inflammation, physiological decline, and increased susceptibility to age-related diseases. The gut microbiota is essential for immune function, nutrient absorption, digestion, and pathophysiological processes. However, aging influences alterations in the composition and diversity of gut microbiota [9, 10]. This age-related imbalance in the gut microbial community, characterized by reduced microbial diversity, loss of beneficial bacteria such as butyrate producers, and an increase in pathogenic species, results in gut dysbiosis. Dysbiosis is associated with impaired intestinal barrier integrity, weakened immune function, and elevated systemic inflammation, creating a vicious cycle that accelerates cellular senescence, tissue aging, and age-related kidney diseases [11]. This association emphasizes the significance of the gut microbiome in aging and highlights the need for further research to promote healthy aging.

In patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD), the gut microbiota is often significantly altered [12, 13], leading to increased production of uremic toxins, increased gut permeability, and systemic inflammation. These microbiota-derived toxins promote oxidative stress and trigger cellular senescence in renal cells, creating a feedback loop that links gut health to renal aging and progression of kidney disease [14]. This issue is further compounded by the natural decline in renal function with aging, as reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR) impairs the clearance of uremic toxins from the bloodstream [14].

The interactions among AKI, CKD, cellular senescence, aging, and gut microbiota underscore the potential of integrated therapeutic approaches that target microbial balance and senescence pathways to slow kidney aging and improve renal health outcomes in the elderly. By manipulating the gut microbiome through dietary modifications, probiotics, or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), it may be possible to prevent or delay the onset of age-related health issues and maintain a robust and diverse gut microbial community throughout the lifespan [15–18].

This review explores the association between the gut microbiome and kidney aging, highlighting the potential of microbiome-directed interventions to promote healthy aging and reduce the global burden of age-related diseases.

Bidirectional relationship between kidney cell senescence and gut microbiome

Emerging evidence suggests a strong interplay between cellular senescence and the gut microbiome in the context of aging and age-related diseases (reviewed in Ref [19]). Aging kidneys show increased senescent cell accumulation and functional decline driven by systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, partly mediated by gut-derived metabolites. Moreover, there is a well-recognized reciprocal relationship between AKI and CKD, in which each condition predisposes to the other through mechanisms involving cellular senescence and inflammation. AKI induces senescence in renal cells, impairing repair and promoting fibrosis, whereas CKD sustains a senescent and pro-inflammatory milieu that impairs renal function and accelerates kidney aging [20, 21]. The gut microbiome modulates this process by influencing systemic inflammation and immune responses, further linking gut dysbiosis and senescence with kidney aging.

Cellular senescence is marked by the irreversible cessation of cell division triggered by diverse stressors, which leads to morphological and functional tissue changes. Cellular senescence represents a crucial biological mechanism frequently instigated by telomere shortening, oxidative stress, and DNA damage [22]. Senescent cells exhibit heterogeneity and secrete a variety of inflammatory factors known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which propagate aging by promoting inflammation and fibrosis, disrupting tissue microenvironments, and impairing immune function. Moreover, SASP can directly impact the gut environment, altering nutrient availability and creating conditions favoring certain microbial species over others. Cellular senescence causes a decline in immune system function, resulting in immune dysfunction. Immune dysfunction caused by senescent cells can also lead to changes in gut microbial diversity, function, and composition, which may further perpetuate a cycle of dysbiosis and increased senescence [19]. More importantly, the accumulation of senescent cells in lymphoid tissues with age contributes to chronic inflammation and deregulation of innate and adaptive immune responses [23].

The gut dysbiosis and microbial metabolites can influence the accumulation of senescent cells and the immune responses [19]. Certain gut bacteria are associated with longevity and healthy aging, while others are linked to accelerated senescence and age-related diseases. Gut microbiota-derived metabolites significantly influence cellular senescence through various mechanisms, including the production of beneficial short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and the modulation of senescence-inducing compounds like phenylacetic acid (PAA). SCFAs, such as butyrate, are produced through the fermentation of dietary fibers by gut bacteria. These metabolites have been shown to decelerate cellular senescence and promote anti-inflammatory responses. They can enhance the health of intestinal epithelial cells and support immune function, potentially counteracting age-related cellular decline [24]. Research indicates that increased levels of PAA can induce senescence in endothelial cells, suggesting a complex relationship where gut-derived metabolites can both promote and inhibit senescence depending on their nature and concentration [25].

Gut dysbiosis can also induce cellular senescence in ileal germinal center (GC) B cells. This leads to decreased IgA production and diversity, further exacerbating the imbalance in the gut microbiota. Increased oxidative stress, a hallmark of cellular senescence, can also be influenced by microbiota metabolites. Certain metabolites may diminish cellular senescence by reducing the stress response [24]. Age-related leaky gut allows the infiltration of Gram-negative bacteria into ileal tissue, likely inducing GC B cell senescence [19].

In summary, the interaction between gut microbiota and cellular senescence is bidirectional. While gut-derived metabolites can influence the senescence of various cell types, the accumulation of senescent cells can alter the gut microbiota composition [19].

Kidney aging

As a part of the human body, the kidney is inevitably exposed to age-related alterations [3]. During kidney aging and in kidney diseases, the number of senescent cells increases, leading to inflammation and fibrosis, which ultimately reduces kidney function. Kidney fibrosis, particularly in the renal cortex, is associated with the loss of nephrons and a gradual decline in GFR. The aging process is also correlated with vascular rarefaction, characterized by a decrease in the density of blood vessels that supply the kidneys. Furthermore, kidney aging leads to diminished mitochondrial energy production and elevated intrarenal cellular apoptosis, resulting in a decreased number of functional nephrons. A reduced nephron count subsequently impairs sodium homeostasis and urine concentration [26, 27]. In addition to structural and metabolic alterations, aging deteriorates ischemic tolerance and consequently increases the susceptibility of the kidney tissue to damage [28].

The gut-kidney axis

The gut-kidney axis has a crucial role in maintaining human health by linking gut microbiota composition to kidney function and overall metabolic processes. The gut microbiota plays an essential role in kidney function by synthesizing uremic toxins. Dysbiosis is commonly observed in patients with kidney insufficiency, resulting in the elevated production of harmful metabolites that exacerbate kidney pathology. Kidney diseases can change the composition of gut microbiota, while dysbiosis can further impair kidney function, creating a vicious cycle of dysbiosis and kidney damage [29, 30].

The gut-kidney axis is implicated in transplantation [31], response to drugs [32], and various kidney diseases, including AKI, CKD, glomerulonephritis, and other renal disorders (previously reviewed by the authors [32–35]). Recent evidence suggests that the gut-kidney axis plays a significant role in the aging process, influencing kidney health through mechanisms involving inflammation, microbial diversity, and metabolic interactions [36, 37]. Comprehending and manipulating this axis offers a potentially fruitful pathway for enhancing health outcomes among the elderly population.

Alterations in gut microbiota composition

The differences in gut microbiota composition from birth to death are widely accepted. In line with this, Conley et al. performed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing to identify the fecal microbiota composition in young and aged mice. Their results revealed that the genera Anaerotruncus, Sarcina, Clostridium, Eggerthella, and Mucispirillum were abundant in the aged mice when compared to their younger counterparts [38]. Consumption of foods containing high amounts of soluble fibers improved different organ functions, such as the kidneys in old dogs, by increasing proportions of Acidobacteria, Cyanobacteria, Ruminococcaceae, Christensenellaceae, Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium, Phascolarcobacteria, Oscillospira, and Adlercreutzia, as well as reducing the number of Fusobacterium, Enterobacteriaceae, Salmonella, and Megamonas [39]. In a recent study, it has been shown that gut-kidney crosstalk plays a critical role in the transition from AKI to CKD in elderly individuals. In aged mice, elevated abundance of Prevotellaceae and Akkermansia and decreased number of Muribaculaceae and Bacteroidetes were observed [40].

Microbial diversity and composition of the human gut also alter during aging [38]. Reduced microbial diversity and an increase in gut permeability result in systemic chronic inflammation in elderly individuals [40] with an unfavorable impact on them [14]. Recently, Chen and coworkers employed 16 S rDNA high-throughput sequencing technology to determine possible differences of gut microbiota in middle-aged and elderly individuals with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) with diabetic kidney disease (DKD) or non-diabetic kidney disease (NDKD). Analysis of patients’ fecal samples revealed that Ruminococcaceae NK4A214, Ruminococcaceae, Anaerotruncus, Ruminococcaceae UCG-004, Bilophila, Oscillibacter, and UBA1819 were abundantly present in DKD patients in comparison to the NDKD group [41]. Moreover, higher abundance of detrimental bacteria (e.g. Citrobacter, Coprobacillus, etc.) and a decrease in the number of butyrate and saccharolytic-producing bacteria including Roseburia, F. prausnitzii, and Prevotella were demonstrated in elderly CKD patients [42]. Margiotta et al. investigated the gut microbiota alternation in sarcopenic and not-sarcopenic elderly CKD patients. In sarcopenic patients, higher abundance of the Verrucomicrobiaceae and Micrococcaceae families and Akkermansia, Veillonella, Rothia, Coprobacillus, as well as Megasphaera genera and decreased levels of the Veillonellaceae and Gemellaceae families and Gemella and Acidaminococcus genera were detected [43]. A cohort study from a longevity region in China [Yunnan aging (n = 80) and Japan aging (n = 330) cohorts] identified specific bacteria, such as Desulfovibrio piger, Odoribacter splanchnicus, and Escherichia coli that were enriched in the oldest individuals and associated with impaired renal function. Furthermore, Ruminococcus spp. and Romboutsia ilealis were more abundant in the youngest individuals while Lactococcus garvieae, Desulfovibrio piger, Bilophila wadsworthia, Odoribacter splanchnicus, and Enterobacter cloacae were enriched in the oldest individuals. The microbial relative abundance and composition evaluation indicated that Odoribacter splanchnicus, Escherichia coli, Anaerostipes hadrus, and Clostridium symbiosum were closely related to aging [14].

Production of uremic toxins

In CKD patients, uremic microvasculature displays a senescent phenotype that contributes to early vascular aging, evident by vascular calcification and impairments in antioxidative defenses [44].

As kidney function declines with age, the detoxification capacity is compromised, leading to higher levels of harmful metabolites derived from gut bacteria such as PAG, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), indole-3 acetic acid, advanced glycation end products (AGEs), indoxyl sulfate (IS), and p-cresyl sulfate (pCS) [45]. These metabolites enter circulation and reach the kidney, setting the stage for renal aging effects. These toxins contribute to oxidative stress and inflammation in the kidneys, which can further impair renal function [46] and accelerate aging [47, 48].

The proximal tubule acts as a critical transport and exposure gateway in aforementioned process. Proximal tubular cells (PTCs) express organic anion transporters (OAT1 and OAT3) that facilitate the uptake of protein-bound uremic toxins (IS and pCS), and accelerate PTC senescence [49]. This high intracellular toxin exposure expose tubule cells to damaging effects. Upon entering these cells, IS promotes oxidative stress with impairment of the antioxidative system, DNA damage/p53/p21, DNA damage response, cell cycle arrest, apoptosis inhibition, mitochondrial dysfunction, and the production of SASP factors, stimulating senescence. It also activates NF-κB signaling (ROS-NF-κB-p53 pathway) [48] and downregulates the anti-aging protein Klotho, culminating in a senescent and pro-fibrotic tubular phenotype characterized by the SASP. SASP factors promote extracellular matrix deposition, leading to kidney fibrosis. This uremic toxin also induces cell senescence in the aorta of hypertensive rats and vascular smooth muscle cells [52, 53]. IS may also induce senescence in parenchymal cells and consequently participate in the progression of inflammaging [50]. Renal senescence and fibrosis are also accelerated with accumulated uremic toxins through the activation of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and degradation of PGC1α and suppression of mitochondrial biogenesis [51]. These mechanisms have been supported by multiple studies observing the induction of senescence markers and Klotho reduction in both cell culture and animal models. Notably, genetic or pharmacologic interventions targeting OATs can influence how these gut-derived toxins are handled systemically and by the kidneys.

Trimethylamine (TMA) is generated by gut microbiota from choline-, lecithin-, and L-carnitine protein–rich diet and is reabsorbed into blood circulation via the portal vein. Then, TMA goes to the liver and other organs, including the aorta, where it can be oxidized to generate TMAO. TMAO acts as a potent pro-fibrotic and pro-inflammatory mediator in the kidney and induce cell senescence by aggravating mitochondrial damage, increasing oxidative stress, promoting the production of SASP and fibrogenic gene programs, impairing autophagy, and inhibiting the mTOR signaling pathway, all aligned with accelerated biological aging [54]. The process is mediated by the stabilization of the transcription factor GATA4 [55]. Experimental evidence from human renal fibroblasts and animal models shows that TMAO promotes fibroblast activation, collagen deposition, and inflammatory signaling through several pathways, including PERK/Akt/mTOR, the NLRP3 inflammasome and caspase-1 activation, as well as endoplasmic reticulum stress [56]. TMAO also reprograms proximal tubule metabolism toward lactate; lactate then skews macrophage M2 polarization, reinforcing inflammation/fibrosis—an amplifying loop relevant to inflammaging [57]. TMA/TMAO can activate NLRP3, linking diet–microbe signals to age-like sterile inflammation in the kidney [57–59]. During the aging process, increased levels of circulating TMAO by increasing oxidative stress and reducing Sirtuin1 can accelerate endothelial cell senescence and vascular aging [60]. These pathways collectively contribute to maladaptive repair processes, fibrosis development, and the aging phenotype of the kidney.

Enterobacteriaceae have tyrosine phenol-lyase, by which the bacteria can degrade tyrosine into pyruvate, ammonia, and phenol. In the colonocytes, phenol is found in sulfated form (phenol sulfate), which circulates in the blood and is ultimately excreted into the urine. However, it can accumulate in the body during renal function decline and aging [36]. Additionally, gut bacteria can convert phenylalanine into phenylacetylglutamine (PAG). Augmented level of PAG is linked to renal function decline and aging [36]. The impairment of the intestinal barrier occurs in elderly individuals, leading to the translocation of gut bacteria and their metabolites/toxins into host tissues. As a consequence, these toxins provoke the production of pro-inflammatory mediators and induction of systemic and kidney inflammation as well as kidney damage, fibrosis, and aging [47].

The gut microbiota exerts an indirect influence on the aging process through its impact on renal function, mediated by the aforementioned uremic toxins. Sun et al. (2023) found that E. coli, by producing 4-aminohippuric acid, 2-oxindole, and PAG, affects aging. The effect of D. piger and O. splanchnicus on aging is also facilitated by p-cresol. Moreover, D. piger affects aging by PAG [14].

While gut-derived uremic metabolites are elevated during aging, fecal levels of SCFAs are diminished in elderly individuals [36]. SCFAs exert positive effects on renal and gut barrier function through reduction of Enterobacteriaceae growth and circulating levels of gut-derived uremic metabolites and urea [36]. The attenuation of SCFA-producing gut bacteria causes the activation of NF-ĸB signaling, triggers the production of proinflammatory mediators, and dysregulates Tregs activity. For example, butyrate improves mitochondrial activity, stimulates intestinal gluconeogenesis, as well as controls epigenetic processes through histone deacetylase prohibition; therefore, it seems to be a beneficial anti-aging metabolite [47].

Inflammation and inflammaging

Inflammation is a key player in the gut-kidney axis during aging, contributing to the development and progression of CKD. The aging process is characterized by a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, often referred to as inflammaging. This term was introduced by Prof. Franceschi and is defined as age-dependent dysregulation of the immune system [38]. During the aging process, some unfavorable events, including a decline in antigen-presenting activity of dendritic cells, dysregulated endocytosis, and decreased chemokine production, occur. The latter causes reduced cytotoxicity of the natural killer cells and consequently diminishes their killing capacity. Hence, there is overproduction of interleukin 4 (IL-4) and IL-10, as well as reduced production of interferon-gamma (INF-γ) in elderly individuals. Besides, the functionality of other immune cells, like naïve T cells and macrophages, decreased during aging [61]. However, the number of memory T cell pools increased and consequently led to a reduced T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoire. The gut microbiota contributes to this inflammatory state by producing pro-inflammatory cytokines and other mediators. This inflammation can adversely affect kidney health, leading to conditions such as CKD and further renal deterioration. It is considered one of the important risk factors for a decline in renal function [46]. The accumulation of senescent cells in the tissues and SASP production contribute to the loss of homeostasis, which in turn leads to the progression of organ dysfunction [23]. Accumulation of lymphocytes, macrophages, profibrotic factors [e.g. IL-4, IL-13, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), collagen I and IV], and inflammatory mediators [e.g. tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), IL-1, and IL-6] results in interstitial fibrosis development and renal function decline. Moreover, decreased proliferative potential of renal cells like proximal tubule cells, senescence, increased expression of cell cycle inhibitors (e.g., p16 and p21), apoptosis, as well as downregulation of growth factors (e.g., VEGF, IGF-1, and EGF) lead to the loss of repair capability of the kidneys [62]. It has been shown that the serum levels of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) are elevated in aged mice [38]. Toll-like receptors (TLRs), as part of the innate immune system, are involved in human renal inflammaging. Therefore, blockade of TLRs is deemed to be an effective way to slow ESRD progression [61]. It has been shown that during kidney aging and renal chronic inflammation, the expression levels of TLR1-5 and − 11 were significantly augmented in elderly rats, which led to NF-κB signaling activation [63].

As mentioned earlier, in CKD patients, the overload of uremic toxins affects gut microbiota, which ultimately leads to deterioration of the uremic state, loss of renal excretion function, and inhibition of their clearance [64]. Furthermore, elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), IL-6, and TNF-α are usually detected in CKD patients due to uremic toxins’ retention and gut dysbiosis [64].

Regulation of the immune system by gut microbiota depends on two main catabolic pathways: (1) the saccharolytic pathway, and (2) the proteolytic pathway. The high levels of Clostridium, Bacteroides, and Enterobacteria result in protein fermentation and elevated levels of urea, ammonia, indoles, phenols, as well as uremic toxins, consequently developing a proinflammatory status [65]. In contrast, abundance of species like Bifidobacteria and Lactobacilli triggers saccharolytic pathway and production of SCFAs (e.g. propionate, butyrate, and acetate), which exhibit immunomodulatory activity through TGF-β production and decreasing proinflammatory cytokines [66]. Additionally, TGF-β provokes proliferation of T-reg cells [67].

Ultimately, the combined effects of these uremic toxins and metabolite changes converge on key hallmarks of cellular senescence within the kidney. They induce ROS production, DNA damage, inflammasome activation, impaired autophagy, epigenetic alterations, and loss of Klotho, leading to tubular and endothelial cell senescence. These senescent cells influence the renal microenvironment by recruiting and altering macrophages and fibroblasts, driving interstitial fibrosis, and thus accelerating renal aging at both the cellular and tissue levels.

The potential therapeutic applications

Aging is deemed to be a natural process by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Therefore, it will be difficult to find FDA-approved therapeutics for aging itself. Consequently, scientists are attempting to identify therapeutic agents for the treatment of age-related diseases. Increasing evidence has demonstrated that restoration of gut bacteria homeostasis can be considered as an effective treatment strategy for kidney disease during the aging process [47].

The impact of diet

Renal function decline seems inescapable during aging, which may lead to an increasing mortality rate; therefore, investigating modifiable lifestyle factors such as diet requires additional attention. Diet, as one of the lifestyle factors, plays a significant role in renal failure prevention. Excessive consumption of potassium, sodium, protein, as well as alcohol contributes to renal failure development [68, 69]. Excessive protein intake, especially from animal sources that contain high levels of saturated fatty acids, exerts a detrimental impact on renal function [70]. However, the Mediterranean type of diet rich in fruits, olive oil, cereals, vegetables, and legumes with moderate (and/or low) animal protein and alcohol beverages, has been linked to better kidney function in the elderly because of its anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties. Dietary intake of fish and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) could reduce the risk for CKD in elderly individuals owing to their anti-inflammatory properties [71].

Hall et al. conducted an in vivo study to measure the impact of diet on renal function in client-owned geriatric dogs. Their results revealed that dogs that consumed tested food (including wet meat chicken, egg, vegetables, fruits, L-carnitine, antioxidants (e.g. vitamins E and C, lipoic acid), and fish oil) instead of their owner’s choice, showed enhanced renal function through decreasing serum symmetric dimethylarginine (SDMA) marker [72]. Moreover, a diet with anti-inflammatory components (including α-lipoic acid, carnitine, carrots, tomato pomace, and spinach) was tested in older dogs for six months. In the experimental group, the levels of serum SDMA, catechol sulfate (microbial toxins), and pCS were markedly decreased when compared to the control group [73].

One of the effective strategies that can extend life span and impede detrimental age-related renal decline is dietary or caloric restriction (DR/CR) [28, 74]. CR delays age-related physiological decline and extends lifespan by regulating vital physiological and biochemical processes such as proliferation, inflammation, autophagy, mitochondrial activity, and endogenous antioxidant systems [75, 76]. These effects lessen renal fibrosis, glomerulosclerosis, and tubular atrophy commonly seen in kidney aging. Lifelong CR or even adult-onset CR improves age-associated renal structural and functional decline, although its protective potential may decrease with advanced age, emphasizing the need for early intervention. DR beginning at an early age can protect against age-related renal histological anomalies, including tubular dilatation, thickening of the basement membrane, and glomerular lesions [28]. CR alleviated oxidative stress intensity in the kidneys of C57BL6/N mice [75]. A proof-of-concept study was performed by Ma and colleagues by building single-nucleus and single-cell transcriptomic atlases on different tissues (including kidneys) of rats during aging and CR. They revealed that CR decreased aging-related alterations in gene expression, cell type composition, and core transcriptional regulatory networks. Besides, CR prevented the aging-disturbed immune ecosystem [77]. However, in a renal rat model of I/R, it has been shown that DR for 4–8 weeks did not reduce AKI and was not as effective as in young counterparts; however, it could restore mitochondrial membrane potential and Bcl-XL levels, also could ameliorate oxidative stress. Thus, it can be concluded that in old animals, DR duration should be extended in order to obtain nephroprotective effects [78].

Applying CR or caloric restriction mimetics (CRMs) (e.g. metformin and resveratrol) enhanced age-related epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which causes age-related kidney fibrosis, in aging kidneys through activation of AMPK/mTOR signaling. The AMPK/mTOR signaling activation led to the preservation of human primary PTCs from high glucose-induced EMT and cellular senescence. Hence, short-term CR or CRM can be considered as an important renal anti-aging treatment in elderly individuals [79].

Fasting-mimicking diets (FMD) offer a more feasible CR alternative by providing cyclical low-calorie challenges while allowing some nutrient intake [80]. FMD has been shown to protect against AKI and reduce progression to CKD by diminishing inflammation, promoting renal tubular cell repair, and reducing profibrotic signaling [74]. In CKD patients, FMD (for 3 months; cycles of 5 days each month) presented renoprotective effects, including improved endothelial function and a decrease in proteinuria compared with the control group [81].

Chung et al. reported that the fatty acid oxidation pathway and expression of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor α (PPARα) were markedly reduced in the aging rat model and aggravated renal fibrosis. Antiaging CR could restore age-related PPARα and the fatty acid oxidation pathway; therefore, they proposed that PPARα is a new target in age-associated CKD prevention [82]. Furthermore, evidence indicates that long-term high-fat diet (HFD) and resulting obesity induce kidney inflammation in the aging Long-Evans rats, characterized by glomerulosclerosis, tubular damage, and increased number of macrophages as well as inflammatory mediators (e.g. eotaxin and MCP-1) [83]. The so-called “anti-aging” food could diminish serum levels of phenolic uremic toxins (e.g. 4-ethylphenyl sulfate and amicrobial brain toxin), SDMA, and pyrraline [39].

Probiotics

As mentioned earlier, gut dysbiosis worsens CKD by increasing harmful metabolites and systemic inflammation. Probiotics modulate immune responses by balancing pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, helping to reduce chronic kidney inflammation. Moreover, they restore microbiota balance, enhance intestinal barrier integrity, and reduce the production of uremic toxins. SCFAs produced by beneficial bacteria suppress inflammatory and fibrotic gene expressions in kidney tissue, reducing proteinuria and fibrosis, reviewed in Ref [84]. The result of a meta-analysis on 21 randomized controlled trial studies also confirmed the effectiveness of probiotic and synbiotic supplementation in improving inflammation and kidney function in 869 patients with CKD [85]. Supplementation with Lactobacillus johnsonii can reverse CKD and protect against kidney fibrosis by hindering AhR signaling pathway [86].

The beneficial effects of probiotics were studied on the expression of aging biomarkers, antioxidant enzymes, and oxidation products in mice. In this regard, probiotic Dahi consists of buffalo milk and Lactobacillus acidophilus LaVK2 (La-Dahi), L. lactis ssp. lactis biovar diacetylactis NCDC60, Lactococcus lactis ssp. cremoris NCDC-86, or combined Bifidobacterium bifidum BbVK3 (LaBb-Dahi) and L. acidophilus were used. Augmented levels of catalase activity in heart, kidney, and liver tissues and red blood cells (RBCs), as well as glutathione peroxidase activity in hepatic tissue and RBCs in aging mice that received LaBb-Dahi or La-Dahi probiotics were observed. Additionally, protein carbonyls and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) were markedly decreased in these treated mice. Besides, probiotics could inhibit the expression levels of antiaging biomarkers including klotho, senescence marker protein-30 (SMP-30), and PPAR-α in liver and kidney tissues. Thus, it can be deduced that probiotic Dahi can be utilized as a potential therapeutic intervention to combat molecular signaling associated with aging and oxidative stress [87]. Probiotic supplementation with B. longum BORI and B. bifidum BGN4 improved gut inflammation by reducing inflammatory cytokine levels and macrophage, neutrophil, and Th17 cells’ infiltration in the aged mice. The probiotics could also alleviate M1-dominant kidney inflammation and enhance kidney function, suggesting that gut microbiome modulation is a promising therapeutic strategy to prevent AKI to CKD progression in the elderly [40]. Likewise, Sookpotarom et al. (2025) demonstrated that the administration of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei MSMC39-1 for four months could significantly reduce kidney inflammation and fibrosis in older mice. This treatment could protect kidney epithelial cells from high glucose-induced damage, suggesting a mechanism for the anti-aging and kidney-protective effects of this probiotic strain [88].

Prebiotics

A systematic review and meta-analysis including 13 randomized controlled trials with 671 CKD patients showed that prebiotic supplementation significantly improved inflammation and oxidative stress markers [45]. A study was performed to assess the impact of omega-3 fatty acids and soluble fiber on gut microbiota and their metabolites in senior dogs. Findings indicated that tested food intake augmented beneficial groups of bacteria, including Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium and Phascolarcobacteria, as well as Ruminococcaceae and Christensenellaceae families. Furthermore, the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae, Fusobacterium, Salmonella, and Megamonas were decreased. The levels of phenolic (e.g. the microbial 4-ethylphenyl sulfate), SDMA, and pyrraline uremic toxins were reduced substantially [89]. A test food contained brown rice, broccoli, tomato pomace, fish oil, ash, omega-3 fatty acids, and crude fat, protein, and fiber exerted important role in lowering levels of metabolites such as uremic toxins (e.g. 2-oxindole-3-acetate, 4-ethylphenyl sulfate, 1-methylguanidine, dimethylarginine (SDMA + ADMA), urea, 3hydroxyphenylacetate sulfate, 3-hydroxyhippurate, 6-hydroxyindole sulfate, 5-hydroxyindole sulfate, and 3-indoxyl sulfate), gamma-glutamyl amino acids, advanced glycation end product (AGE) pyrraline, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN) as well as elevating plasma levels of beneficial metabolites like tocopherols in senior cats in comparison to the control food. High levels of butyric acid were detected in fecal SCFA analysis within cats consumed the test food. Besides, Lactobacillaceae Bifidobacteriaceae, Veillonellaceae, and Coriobacteriaceae families were abundantly found in feces of cats consumed the test food [90].

Antioxidants

Depletion of antioxidants can lead to accumulation of free radicals, which in turn, causes various organs aging. In this line, the beneficiary impact of exogenous antioxidants was studied in aged rats. Dadih -fermented buffalo milk- has antioxidant activity that can provoke endogenous antioxidants’ formation. Dadih consumption diminished malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and renal interstitial fibrosis rank in rats [91].

Resveratrol, a phenolic compound, has various anti-aging properties in the kidney that are comprehensively reviewed by Uddin et al. [92]. Resveratrol represents its protective impact on kidney aging through the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) regulation and improving cellular senescence, oxidative stress, and inflammation. The main anti-aging mechanisms of resveratrol in the kidney include the NF-κB, AMPK, and SIRT1 pathways. Besides, resveratrol promotes gut microbiota by preventing the colonization of pathogens like E. coli and Salmonella, subsequently enhances inflammation and ameliorates kidney damage [92]. El-far and colleagues performed a study to assess the protective effects of quercetin (plant flavonol and a CRM) against D-galactose (D-gal)-induced aging-associated oxidative alterations in male Wistar rats. Their findings showed that quercetin restored the aging-associated inflammation and oxidative alterations and, thus, could postpone the aging process [93]. Pan et al. showed that administering alginate oligosaccharide (200 mg·kg⁻¹·d⁻¹) for four weeks effectively mitigates kidney aging induced by D-gal, with the protective effects primarily attributed to activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway [94]. Black rice anthocyanins (BRAs) also exert significant protective effects against renal aging induced by D-gal. Treatment with BRAs could modulate the Nrf2 signaling pathway to boost antioxidant defenses and inhibit the NF-κB signaling pathway to attenuate inflammatory responses. BRAs also attenuated age-related renal deterioration by enhancing the relative abundance of norank_f_Oscillospiraceae, Dubosiella, and norank_f_Muribaculaceae [95].

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

Increasing evidence has elucidated the role of gut dysbiosis in the onset of CKD, progression to ESRD, and kidney aging, and FMT presents a promising therapy [96–98]. FMT is a therapeutic approach by which sterile fecal filtrate composed of healthy donors’ gut components is transplanted into patients to modify the gut microbiota [37]. A very limited number of studies regarding utilizing FMT to manipulate gut microbiota to alleviate kidney aging have been published to date. In this line, FMT from elderly individuals into mice revealed alterations of the gut microbiota and serum metabolome. The elevated levels of Odoribacter and Desulfovibrio species, as well as phenylacetylglycine, p-cresol glucuronide, and 2-oxindole, were detected in mice [14]. FMT in aged mice significantly alleviated renal senescence markers and reduced interstitial fibrosis by modulating intestinal microbiota and suppressing NF-κB signaling pathways, suggesting that gut microbial modulation has a direct effect on the renal microenvironment and aging phenotypes [99].

Numerous studies elucidated the promising role of FMT in CKD patients [100, 101]. For instance, recently Arteaga-Muller et al. conducted a clinical trial in patients with diabetes and/or hypertension with CKD who received FMT. Their results showed that the FMT group restored kidney function parameters in comparison to the placebo group. Besides, the frequency of Actinobacteria and Firmicutes reduced while Roseburia spp., Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes augmented in the FMT group. Overall, FMT administration led to a decline in CKD progression [15]. Thus, using FMT seems to be effective in kidney aging and its complications; however, it should be used carefully in elderly or immunocompromised patients in order to overcome possible uncontrollable risks for infectious [37].

Other natural and metabolic modulators

Ganoderma lucidum (G. lucidum) is a traditional medicinal fungus with documented anti-aging, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidative properties. Liu et al. (2025) demonstrated that administration of G. lucidum sporoderm-broken spore powder (Gl-SBSP) significantly alleviated kidney aging phenotypes in both naturally aged mice and models of radiation-induced premature senescence. Notably, Gl-SBSP treatment remodeled the gut microbiota, selectively enriching beneficial bacteria within the Lachnospiraceae family. This bacterial enrichment was associated with increased production of the metabolite nicotinamide riboside (NR), which played a critical role in enhancing renal nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) levels. The study showed that NR improved klotho levels and mitochondrial function, decreased the number of senescent cells, and suppressed inflammatory and cellular senescence markers (p21), ultimately leading to restored kidney structure and function. Experimental in vitro and in vivo studies that validated NR’s role in regulating renal steroid metabolism pathways, which are altered during aging, could find a mechanistic link between gut microbiota-derived metabolites and kidney rejuvenation [16]. This mechanism presents a novel therapeutic approach for combating age-related decline in kidney function by stimulating NAD+ biosynthesis and regulating steroid metabolism through the gut-kidney axis.

Polygonatum sibiricum polysaccharides (PSP) are bioactive compounds extracted from the rhizomes of Polygonatum sibiricum, a traditional medicinal plant known for its wide range of health benefits. Huang (2025) explored the impact of PSP against D-gal-induced renal injury in a model of aging. They found that PSP treatment significantly alleviated kidney damage in aging mice by reducing tubular atrophy, epithelial swelling, and collagen deposition. PSP modulated gut microbiota composition, increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, which are known to play protective roles in the gut-kidney axis. Additionally, PSP altered metabolic profiles of the mice, influencing key pathways including amino acid and sphingolipid metabolism, which correlated strongly with changes in gut microbiota. The results suggest that PSP mediates renoprotection via modulation of the gut microbiota and metabolic pathways to restore kidney function and reduce age-related renal pathology [17].

Tryptophan is an essential amino acid that humans cannot synthesize and must obtain from dietary sources. Tryptophan serves as a precursor for important metabolic pathways, including the synthesis of neurotransmitters (serotonin and melatonin), and metabolites such as NAD+, a key coenzyme used for energy metabolism. In the gut, the microbiota plays a crucial role in metabolizing dietary tryptophan into diverse bioactive compounds via several pathways, including the kynurenine, serotonin, and indole pathways. Tryptophan metabolism plays a central role in linking gut microbiota with kidney aging through multiple related pathways. Aging is associated with dysregulation of tryptophan catabolism, particularly via the kynurenine pathway, which results in increased degradation of tryptophan and accumulation of metabolites such as kynurenine and quinolinic acid. These metabolites activate immune and inflammatory pathways, including the AHR, contributing to kidney cellular senescence, chronic inflammation, and fibrosis [102, 103].

Gut microbiota regulate tryptophan metabolism through the serotonin, kynurenine, and indole pathways. The gut microbiota and changes in the gut microbiota during aging influence this process by modulating the balance of tryptophan metabolites, which can impact renal inflammation and aging. Microbial dysbiosis shifts the balance of tryptophan metabolites, driving increased production of inflammatory kynurenine pathway metabolites and reduced beneficial indole derivatives, thereby exacerbating renal senescence and chronic inflammation. Furthermore, altered tryptophan metabolism affects NAD+ biosynthesis, oxidative stress regulation, and immune cell function in the kidney, making it a critical metabolic hub in aging and age-related kidney diseases. This metabolic interplay underscores the importance of gut microbiota composition in shaping renal health during aging, as microbial dysbiosis disrupts beneficial tryptophan-derived metabolites while increasing toxic species that accelerate kidney damage. Hence, therapeutic targeting of key enzymes in tryptophan metabolism, probiotics, antibiotics, and diet holds promise for mitigating kidney aging through the gut-kidney axis [14, 104, 105].

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this review. Much of the existing research is observational or based on preclinical models, with a relative scarcity of large-scale clinical trials providing direct evidence. Additionally, the complexity of the gut-kidney axis, along with significant interindividual variability in microbiota composition, complicates the ability to draw definitive conclusions. These limitations highlight the need for more rigorous, longitudinal studies to better elucidate underlying mechanisms and explore therapeutic opportunities.

Conclusion

Aging is widely regarded as an inevitable biological process; however, increasing evidence suggests that therapeutic interventions—particularly those targeting the gut microbiota—hold significant potential for mitigating its effects. The gut microbiota and its metabolites play critical roles in various conditions, such as aging, particularly kidney aging. Microbial profiling could provide a useful indication for the early detection of kidney issues and the selection of appropriate treatment. Microbiota-modulated therapies can efficiently recover the immune responses and improve health outcomes in aging individuals. However, the safety and long-term implications of these therapies in elderly individuals warrant careful evaluation. In the future, there is an urgent need to initiate comprehensive mechanistic studies and clinical trials in order to extend our understanding of the crosstalk between gut microbiota composition and renal aging.
