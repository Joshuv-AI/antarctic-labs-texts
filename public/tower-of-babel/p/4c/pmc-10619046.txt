Association of parental self-efficacy with loneliness, isolation and community commitment in mothers with infant children in Japan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Azusa Arimoto, Etsuko Tadaka
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesParental self-efficacy is defined as an individual’s appraisal of their competence in the parental role and is one of the factors targeted by parenting interventions. Factors related to parental self-efficacy among mothers, focusing mainly on individual and family characteristics, have been clarified; however, there are limited findings on community/societal factors related to parental self-efficacy among mothers. We aimed to clarify the association between parental self-efficacy and community/societal factors among mothers with infants and toddlers to provide suggestions for maternal and child health activities.DesignThis was a cross-sectional study, where an anonymous, self-administered questionnaire survey was conducted by mail from November 2019 to February 2020.SettingA health and welfare centre in a ward certified by the largest designated city in Japan.ParticipantsSix hundred forty-nine mothers raising children aged 0–3 years who attended mandatory infant health check-ups.Primary outcome measuresThe Parental Self-Efficacy Scale.ResultsThe number of valid responses was 490 (valid response rate: 75.6%). The mean age of the mothers was 33.2 (SD 4.9) years. The items found to significantly relate to parental self-efficacy (p<0.05), loneliness, social isolation (social network), community commitment, greeting neighbours, willingness to interact with neighbours, participation in parent–child circles and neighbourhood association events were included as independent variables. Multiple regression analysis showed that the factors significantly related to high parental self-efficacy were low loneliness score (β=−0.436, p<0.001), high social network score (β=0.153, p<0.001) and high community commitment score (β=0.110, p<0.01). The adjusted R2 was 0.488.ConclusionsThe results suggest the importance of community-building, enhancing qualitative and quantitative support by developing initiatives to reduce loneliness and improving the environment to supplement social networks, and activating community activities by promoting interaction with local residents.

Introduction

Self-efficacy, a fundamental psychological construct, is characterised by the conviction in one’s ability to plan and execute a sequence of actions that lead to an accomplishment.1 According to Bandura, self-efficacy pertains to an individual’s belief in their capacity to excel in specific situations or tasks, significantly influencing the essential behaviour required for generating specific outcomes.1 Within the realm of parenthood, parental self-efficacy (PSE) signifies an individual’s self-assessment of their ability to fulfil their parental role.2 3 PSE emerges as a pivotal predictor influencing the decision-making process surrounding parental behaviours, the determination to undertake tasks, and the allocation of effort and time required for task completion.4 Crucially, a robust correlation exists between PSE and parental competence, where heightened PSE is linked to more effective parenting practices,3 while diminished PSE indicates less proficient and potentially dysfunctional parenting approaches.5 Notably, previous investigations have delineated a correlation between perceived parenting challenges and self-efficacy.3 4 Empirical evidence demonstrates that mothers grappling with low PSE in nurturing infants frequently encounter parenting difficulties, whereas those imbued with high PSE exhibit adept parenting skills even despite factors such as suffering from depression, challenges stemming from a child’s temperament and an unsupportive social environment.6–8 Moreover, it is noteworthy that PSE exerts a substantial impact on both parenting burden9 and parental mental well-being.8–11 Consequently, augmenting PSE holds the potential not only to ameliorate parenting complexities but also to safeguard the psychological health of mothers.

The crucial aspect of maternal PSE lies in identifying intervenable variables. The significance of loneliness, isolation and community factors has been underscored by both a systematic review6 and a comprehensive analysis of qualitative studies.12

First, in relation to loneliness and social isolation, the experience of loneliness manifests as a subjective and discomforting emotion, arising from a dearth in either the quantity or quality of an individual’s social connections.13 14 Extant research underlines the salient role played by support systems, such as spouses or extended family, in bolstering maternal self-efficacy and alleviating parenting burdens.15–17 Intriguingly, low general self-efficacy among mothers of infants has been associated with diminished cooperation with spouses in caregiving.18 Further evidence for the influence of social support on PSE stems from a systematic review of factors impacting PSE.6

Second, regarding community factors, prior investigations have commenced the exploration of the nexus between PSE and community/societal dynamics.6 8 10 Existing evidence suggests a positive correlation between perceived social support and elevated PSE.8 10 Aligned with Bandura’s theory, which underscores the environmental role in shaping self-efficacy,1 prior studies have confirmed a link between cognitive social support from family, friends, and professionals and PSE.6 8 10 In addition, insights from a systematic review on PSE interventions19 underscore the potency of a universal population-based strategy. Thus, a comprehensive elucidation of community factors influencing PSE can fortify this population-level approach and advance PSE outcomes. Qualitative inquiries have similarly hinted at the transformative impact of connections with neighbours and engagement in community activities on parenting competence and mental well-being.20

In Japan, the national policy ‘Healthy Parents and Children 21’ sets a target for the percentage of mothers who are not confident in their ability to raise children and states the need to create an environment to bear and nurture children.21 Low self-confidence among Japanese mothers raising infants has been shown to be associated with low PSE.18 As a background for low PSE and low self-confidence, loneliness and isolation among mothers raising children is a serious social issue in Japan,22 23 and the declining birthrate and an increasing number of nuclear families and the COVID-19 pandemic are exacerbating this problem.24 Several limited empirical studies suggest that PSE and a lack of self-confidence among Japanese mothers are associated with loneliness, social isolation or community factors. A cross-sectional study targeting Japanese mothers with children aged 0–4 years has shown that the lack of confidence in raising their children correlated with their feelings of loneliness25; however, this study evaluated the mothers’ confidence and loneliness using items of unknown reliability and validity. Two studies targeting Japanese mothers with children aged 4 years and 7–8 years, respectively, have shown that PSE was associated with friends’ support.26 27 Parents who had not used community resources had higher loneliness scores than those who had23; furthermore, this study suggests that mothers who had not used community resources had lower PSE. No studies on PSE from Japan have been included in scoping reviews12 or systematic reviews regarding interventions for PSE.6 19 Empirical studies focusing on community factors are needed to clarify the factors associated with PSE among Japanese mothers with infants and toddlers aged 0–3 years.

Therefore, the objective of this study is to clarify the association between PSE and community/societal factors among mothers with infants and toddlers to suggest maternal and child health activities and interventions.

Our decision to examine the association between PSE and community/societal factors was based on Bandura’s self-efficacy theory1 3 which suggests that PSE among mothers can be related to various aspects of the environment,1 that is, community/societal factors. Based on this theory and previous literature,5 6 12 we hypothesise that PSE is associated with community/societal factors such as loneliness, social isolation, recognition of community and participation in community activities, after adjusting for basic characteristics and individual factors among a community sample of mothers with children aged 0–3 years.

Materials and methods

Design

This was a cross-sectional survey study, in which an anonymous, self-administered questionnaire was administered.

Participants and setting

A total of 649 mothers who attended mandatory infant health check-ups were recruited from a health and welfare centre in a ward certified by the largest designated city in Japan, Yokohama, from November 2019 to February 2020. Health check-ups, including growth and development examinations and health counselling, are mandatory at 4, 18 and 36 months of age under the Maternal and Child Health Act in Japan. The potential sample size was set at one-quarter of the number of children aged 0–3 years per year in this ward. A sample size analysis using G*Power V.3.1.9.6 demonstrated significant results with the current sample size.

Recruitment procedures

We sent questionnaires with announcement letters for a health check-up by mail to 649 mothers. All eligible mothers were asked to complete the questionnaires and return them by mail. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) mothers raising children aged 0–3 years from November 2019 to February 2020, (2) living in a ward of Yokohama City in Japan, and (3) having sufficient knowledge of Japanese to answer the questionnaire and willingness to complete it.

Survey items

Demographic data

Based on previous literature,5 6 demographic data included the mothers’ age, children’s age (0/1/2/3 years), number of children, family structure (parents and children living together/three generations living together/mother and child household/father and child household/others), daytime caregiver (mother/nursery school or kindergarten/father/other family members), employment status (full-time housewife/full-time employee/part-time worker/self-employed/contract or temporary worker/others), spouse’s employment status (full-time and permanent staff/self-employed/contract or temporary worker/part-time job/full-time homemaker/others) and years of residence (less than 1 year/1–less than 5 years/5–less than 10 years/10 or more years).

Parenting self-efficacy

PSE was measured using the PSE scale.9 The instrument developer defines PSE as ‘parents’ confidence to flexibly handle previously unencountered situations’ based on Bandura’s definition.1 We adopted the PSE scale for three reasons: (1) PSE is a prerequisite factor that promotes or inhibits parenting behaviour,1–4 6 (2) this scale considers parental confidence in performing not only those parenting behaviours that caregivers perform but also those they have not yet experienced,9 and (3) there is no Japanese version of the PSE scale for parents of 0–3 years old; therefore, the only suitable task-specific scale was used to assess PSE among parents of children aged 0–36 months in Japan. The task-specific scale must assess the perception of competence in performing discrete parenting tasks according to a child’s age group5; however, systematic reviews on self-report measures of PSE28 have reported that one measure was designed for fathers of toddlers and preschoolers (the Fathering Self-Efficacy Scale),29 and there was no measure designed specifically for parents of preschoolers (3–5 years) available.28 This instrument comprises 13 items scored on a 5-point scale as follows: 0 (not confident at all), 1 (slightly not confident), 2 (neither), 3 (slightly confident) and 4 (absolutely confident). The total score ranges from 13 to 65 points, with higher scores indicating higher PSE. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85 for the original scale9 and 0.840 in this study, indicating good reliability. No cut-off point for the PSE scale has been determined. The validity of this scale has also been established.9

Individual factors

Mothers with low PSE tend to experience difficulties in parenting,5 8 12 and a systematic review of factors related to PSE suggests that there is an association between household income and PSE.6 Thus, as individual factors, we asked about the issues related to parenting and daily life, with participants answering ‘yes’ or ‘no’ to the question, ‘Do you worry about the following 12 items?’ The items were as follows: ‘illness and development’, ‘food’, ‘sleep’, ‘excretion’, ‘how to play with and care for your children’, ‘discipline (praise and scolding)’, ‘how to interact with your children’s friends’, ‘the children’s relationships with their siblings’, ‘financial matters’, ‘family matters (eg, spouse, partner and parents)’, ‘balancing work and parenting’, and ‘the relationship with parents of your children’s friends’. The total number of ‘yes’ items was calculated.

Community/societal factors

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured using the shortened 10-item version of the University of California, Los Angeles Loneliness Scale (third edition).14 30 Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale. The total score ranges from 10 to 40 points, with a higher score indicating higher loneliness. Cronbach’s alpha of the Japanese version of the scale was 0.888,30 and it was 0.858 in this study. The validity of this scale has been established.30

Social network

Parents’ social networks were measured using the shortened version of the Lubben Social Network Scale.31 32 This instrument includes six items rated using a 5-point Likert scale and comprises two subscales: family (three items) and friends (three items). The total score ranges from 0 to 30 points, with higher scores indicating wider social networks and lower scores (≤12) indicating social isolation. The reliability and validity of the Japanese version have been established32; Cronbach’s alpha was 0.820 in the translation study32 and 0.780 in the present study. Cronbach’s alphas of the subscales were 0.779 (family) and 0.774 (friends) in this study; the family subscales ranged from 0.840 to 0.890 in the original study,31 and the translational study did not report on either of these subscales.32

Awareness of the community

Awareness of the community was measured using the Community Commitment Scale.33 This instrument includes eight items rated using a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (strongly disagree) to 3 (strongly agree) and comprises two subscales: socialising (four items) and belonging (four items). The total scores range from 0 to 24, with higher scores indicating higher community commitment. The reliability and validity of this scale have been established; the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.780 in the scale development study33 and 0.859 in the present study. Cronbach’s alphas of the subscales were 0.871 (socialising) and 0.757 (belonging) in this study.

Interaction with neighbours

Two dichotomous items were created to measure whether parents greeted their neighbours (yes/no) and were willing to interact with neighbours (yes/no).

Use of local resources

The use of community childcare resources was assessed using six dichotomous items (yes/no) that measured the use of or participation in local resources including ‘local childrearing support centres’, ‘childrearing salons’, ‘events and classes at local facilities’, ‘park play conducted by local volunteers’, ‘parent–child circles’ and ‘events held by neighbourhood associations’. The items were taken from a checklist developed by Arimoto and Murashima to examine community childcare resources.34

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated, and the relationship between PSE and categorical data was examined using an independent samples t-test and one-way analysis of variance. The correlation between PSE and categorical data was examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Following this, among the items that showed statistically significant relationships or correlations with PSE (p<0.05), the variables were selected while considering the multicollinearity between items assessed for correlation and variance inflation. We conducted multiple regression analysis in which the selected variables were entered as independent variables by the mandatory input method, and PSE was used as a dependent variable. Variables and demographic data found to be associated with individual characteristics were used as control variables. Of the 490 valid responses, data were missing for loneliness (n=1, 0.20%), social network (n=1, 0.20%), community commitment (n=7, 1.43%), number of problems regarding parenting and life (n=1, 0.20%), and age (n=10, 2.04%); therefore, these cases were excluded from the multiple regression models. SPSS V.26.0 (IBM Corp) was used for the analysis, and the significance level was set at 0.05.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, procedure, reporting or dissemination of this study.

Results

Demographic data

There were 511 respondents (response rate: 78.7%), of whom 490 provided valid responses (valid response rate: 75.6%). Twenty-one people were excluded based on the exclusion criterion of having one or more missing items on the PSE scale. The mean age of the participants was 33.2 (SD 4.9) years. Of these, 266 (54.3%) participants were employed, including part-time workers. Nuclear families were the most common family structure, as seen in 444 (90.6%) families. The ages of the participants’ children were mainly 0, 1 or 3 years (approximately 30% each). Of the participants, 216 (44.1%) had two children and 207 (42.2%) had only one child. For 344 (89%) participants, the duration of residence in the study area was greater than 1 year (table 1). The mean total score for the PSE scale was 48.6 (SD 7.7), while the mean score for the number of worries (personal characteristics) was 2.7 (SD 2.2; table 1).

Association between PSE and each variable

There were no significant associations or correlations in the demographic data. As an individual factor, the higher the number of problems regarding parenting and life, the lower the PSE (r=−0.366, p<0.001) (table 3). Community/societal factors were significantly related to greeting neighbours (p<0.05), willingness to interact with neighbours (p<0.001), use of parent–child circle (p<0.01) and participation in community association events (p<0.05) (table 2). In addition, the PSE score had a significant negative linear correlation with the loneliness (r=−0.648, p<0.001), social network (r=0.436, p<0.001) and community commitment (r=0.356, p<0.001) scores. These scores were normally distributed for the analysis of regression coefficients.

Multiple regression analysis with PSE as the dependent variable

Among the items found to be significantly related to the PSE score (p<0.05), loneliness, social network, community commitment, greeting neighbours, willingness to interact with neighbours, participation in parent–child circles and participation in neighbourhood association events were included as independent variables.

In addition, mothers’ age, number of children, years of residence and number of problems regarding parenting and life were included as control variables. Multiple regression analysis using the forced imputation method revealed that the factors significantly related to high PSE were low loneliness score (β=−0.436, p<0.001), high social network score (β=0.153, p<0.001) and high community commitment score (β=0.110, p<0.01) (table 4). The adjusted R2 was 0.488. These scores were normally distributed for the analysis of regression coefficients.

Discussion

This study expanded on existing research, clarifying that loneliness, social networks and community commitment are the community/societal factors significantly associated with PSE among mothers raising children aged 0–3 years in Japan after adjusting for demographic data and individual factors. This has not been clarified in previous studies.

The PSE scale total scores’ mean was higher in this study than in previous studies, even though participants’ mean age and employment status were not significantly different.9 In addition, demographic data such as mean age and employment status were almost the same as the national average in this study.35 36

Loneliness was significantly associated with PSE. Loneliness is an unpleasant and painful subjective state caused by a lack of social relationships.14 Previous studies have shown that the more emotional support an individual experiences, the higher their PSE.9 In addition, a greater degree of support from people who praised parents and recognised their efforts is related to fewer parenting difficulties,16 and mothers who are less aware of social support are more likely to experience parenting burden and loneliness.8 10 22

New findings in this study reveal that not only the perception of social support but also loneliness itself, caused by the quantitative and qualitative lack of social support, is related to PSE. To improve PSE, it is important for professionals to focus on loneliness in addition to the quantity and quality of social support because loneliness occurs when the quantity of social support is sufficient in absolute terms but insufficient in relative terms.

Prior research has not focused on social networks and social isolation, which indicate quantity and presence. In this study, we found that the number of social networks is significantly related to higher PSE. Social networks with family and friends are an objective measure of social ties centred on emotional and instrumental support.32 37 Previously, husbands who were involved in parenting38 and had support from non-relatives32 were found to have significantly more positive attitudes toward parenting. Furthermore, mothers with support from non-relatives were better able to cope with difficult parenting situations.22 For mothers with parenting anxieties, the support of individuals perceived to be helpful in parenting impacts their PSE.39 This suggests that mothers of infants who have a large social network of objective human resources are able to receive greater emotional and instrumental support, and this is associated with high PSE. Therefore, the emotional and instrumental support received from a social network is related to a high level of PSE.

A scoping review reported support from professionals, friends and family as PSE factors; however, there is limited research clarifying the association with mothers’ awareness of their community or neighbourhoods.12 Although qualitative studies have described the relationship between mothers with infants and their communities, the relationship between community commitment and PSE has not been clarified. We consider the relationship between community commitment and PSE to be a new finding of this study. Community commitment is an expression of community awareness, which consists of ‘socialising’ and ‘belonging’.33 Previous cross-sectional studies have shown that mothers who perceived that they received social support from the community reported lower difficulty in parenting,8 10 and those who participated in a local mothers’ group had significantly higher social capital and mental well-being scores than those who did not participate in the mothers’ group.40 Good relationships with neighbours and participation in community activities can help people form connections with their community. Thus, increasing community commitment, comprising a sense of socialisation and belongingness in the community, could enhance PSE.

To improve the PSE of mothers raising infants and toddlers, the importance of reducing loneliness, strengthening social networks and increasing community commitment is suggested by the results of this study. Therefore, in maternal and child health services that include health check-ups for infants and toddlers and home visits, opportunities can be provided for mothers to individually consult people they can trust. These health services could also provide mothers with qualitative support to reduce loneliness. Moreover, previous studies have clarified the functions of community activities, such as ‘mothers’ social capital’ and ‘connections with the community and local people’.16 41 Therefore, connecting mothers to peer-support groups is important for creating an environment where they can easily receive social support, expand their social network, increase their awareness of social support, reduce their loneliness and increase their community commitment. In addition, it is important to revitalise community activities by fostering volunteer groups and creating opportunities for local residents to interact with each other.

This study had several limitations. First, despite the relatively high response rate, the setting was a single region, thus limiting the generalisability of the results. Therefore, it would be useful to investigate the factors associated with PSE in more diverse populations and regions. Second, this study used a cross-sectional design, which cannot indicate causal relationships. Longitudinal studies are required to clarify the causal relationships among the investigated variables. Lastly, there may be other potential unobserved variables or confounding factors related to PSE among mothers; further exploratory research is necessary to explain these factors. However, a major strength of this study is that it expands the range of factors associated with PSE and reveals community/societal factors (ie, loneliness, social networks and community commitment) that have not received sufficient attention in previous literature. Therefore, these results shed light on the mechanisms of PSE among mothers with infants.

Conclusions

This study aimed to clarify the relationship between PSE and community/societal factors among mothers raising infants to provide suggestions for health activities. New findings in this study reveal that higher PSE is associated with community/societal factors, including lower levels of loneliness, stronger social networks and stronger community awareness. The results suggest the importance of community-building, enhancing qualitative and quantitative support by developing initiatives to reduce loneliness and improving the environment to supplement social networks, and activating community awareness by promoting interaction with local residents. Future longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether community factors enhance PSE and how community factors influence parenting behaviour via PSE.
