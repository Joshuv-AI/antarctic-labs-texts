Endocarditis Associated With Coronary Cameral Fistula Presenting With Intracranial Hemorrhage
Authors: Waqas Hanif, Martin Sayegh, Muhammad Umer Farooq, Lili Zhang, Melanie Bechen, Rishi Malhotra, Mario J. Garcia, Min Pu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Coronary cameral fistulas are rare anomalous connections between coronary circulation and cardiac chambers. Coronary cameral fistulas are often asymptomatic but may cause volume overload on impacted chambers, and also create high velocity turbulent diastolic flow which may predispose patients to endocarditis. This paper presents a unique case of infective endocarditis revealed by intracerebral hemorrhage with a mycotic aneurysm in a patient who was found to have a nonvalvular infective endocarditis located on the mitral anterolateral papillary muscle, at the ventricular site of entrance of a large coronary cameral fistula.

Coronary cameral fistulas (CCFs) are rare anomalous connections between the coronary circulation and cardiac chambers. Most CCFs are congenital, asymptomatic, and discovered incidentally on cardiac imaging. We describe a unique case of infective endocarditis (IE) located on mitral anterolateral papillary muscle, at the site of ventricular entrance of a large CCF.

History of Presentation

A 46-year-old woman presented with complaints of headaches of 2 weeks’ duration with associated nausea, vomiting, and photophobia. She was initially treated with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and acetaminophen and discharged home. She returned to the emergency department the next day due to worsening headaches. The patient also reported intermittent fever, chills, and malaise for 2 weeks and a dental cleaning 4 weeks prior to presentation. In the emergency department, computed tomography head and computed tomography angiography head and neck were performed which demonstrated a right occipital intraparenchymal hemorrhage and aneurysm in the distal right posterior cerebral artery (Figure 1). Given the distal location of the aneurysm, there was high suspicion for mycotic aneurysm. The patient was transferred to a tertiary care center for further management. She developed leukocytosis; therefore, blood cultures were sent and transthoracic echocardiography was ordered. Digital subtraction cerebral angiogram confirmed the distal right posterior cerebral artery aneurysm, which was successfully coil embolized (Video 1). Past Medical History

Her past medical history was significant for migraine but she was not on any maintenance medications. The patient reports her headaches usually get worse around the time of menstrual cycle but this headache was different.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis at this time was valvular IE pending further work-up.

Investigations

Multiple blood cultures were positive for Streptococcus mitis/S oralis group. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular size, function, a mobile echo density attached to anterolateral papillary muscle, and diastolic turbulent flow at the lateral wall draining into the left ventricle within the anterolateral papillary muscle suggesting CCF (Videos 2 and 3). Further evaluation of mobile echo density and diastolic flow was conducted using transesophageal echocardiography, which revealed diastolic turbulent flow at the lateral wall draining into a cavity within the anterolateral papillary muscle confirming the presence of CCF (arterioluminal type) and a mobile vegetation (16 × 7 mm) at the fistula’s termination along the anterolateral papillary muscle (Video 4, Video 5, Video 6). There was no vegetation on the valves. Coronary computed tomography angiography was performed for further characterization and assessment of CCF. Management (Medical/Interventions)

Cardiothoracic surgery, structural heart team, and infectious disease services were consulted for a long-term management plan. Her case was unusual and there was no clear evidence to guide decision-making. The patient did not show any signs or symptoms of volume overload, with normal right and left chamber sizes and function. Percutaneous closure was considered but given the large size and complexity of the fistula, and potential risk of papillary muscle ischemia/damage, it was decided to adopt a conservative approach.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was treated medically for endocarditis. She responded very well to treatment with ceftriaxone and there was no recurrent stroke. The patient was discharged home.

Discussion

CCF is a rare congenital anomalous communication between coronary arteries and cardiac chambers. This anomaly more frequently arises from the right coronary system (≈55%) but can originate from the left side (35%) or bilaterally (5%). They are classified based on the type of communication with the cardiac chamber, either arterioluminal (direct communication with the cardiac chamber) or arteriosinusoidal (communication via sinusoidal network).1 Among all these fistulas that arise from either system, only about 10% drain into left heart chambers.2

The clinical presentation is variable and depends on the hemodynamic impact of the anomaly but is most commonly asymptomatic and is found incidentally. Most fistulas are small and do not cause any clinical signs or symptoms. However, left untreated, hemodynamically significant fistulas result in symptoms in 19% of patients under 20 years of age and 63% of the patients over 20 years of age. Larger fistulas can cause coronary artery steal resulting in ischemia of a segment of the myocardium perfused by coronary artery distal to the fistula. Other complications include heart failure, atrial fibrillation, bacterial endocarditis, thrombosis and/or embolism, rupture, sudden cardiac death, and premature atherosclerosis.3,4

IE is a complication of the CCF and is attributed to turbulent blood flow from these shunts, and it has been addressed in detail in a cases series of 25 cases. The most common organisms in this article were Streptococci and Staphylococci species.5

Most CCF-associated IE cases are valvular; however, the present case is unique because IE involved the terminal part of the fistula in anterolateral papillary muscles and such a location has not been reported in the literature. The risk of IE in patients with CCF is unclear; therefore, routine antibiotics prophylaxis for IE has not been standardized. Nevertheless, to reduce the risk of IE, patients with CCF should be counseled on maintaining good cutaneous and dental hygiene including regular tooth brushing and biannual dental consultation. In patients with previous episode of IE, antibiotic prophylaxis for endocarditis is recommended for future procedures in accordance with established American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines.

Conclusions

CCF infection–associated nonvalvular endocarditis may present with noncardiac manifestations. Endocarditis prophylaxis in patients with history of CCFs infection should be ensured. In a patient with known CCF, good oral cutaneous and dental hygiene can reduce the risk of infective endocarditis.
