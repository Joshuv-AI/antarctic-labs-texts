Essential minerals in colostrum of preterm and full-term Ghanaian mothers and related maternal factors
Authors: Justice Wiston Amstrong Jonathan, Daniel Elorm Kwame Kabotso, David Kofi Essumang, John Kwesi Bentum, Jesse Azebiik Anak, Innocentia Ruby Gborgblorvor, Emmanuel Kormla Danyo, Hintermann Mbroh, John Tampuori, Richard Harry Asmah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMineral elements in colostrum play crucial roles in neonatal growth and development. This study assessed the concentrations of ten essential mineral elements in the colostrum of moderately preterm and full-term mothers in Ghana, and examined their associations with maternal characteristics. A total of 47 mothers provided single 12 mL colostrum samples collected between postpartum days 1 to 4 using standardized, aseptic manual expression procedures. The samples were digested and analyzed using ICP-OES. Statistical analyses included tests for normality, Spearman correlation, and group comparisons using Kruskal-Wallis and Mann-Whitney U tests. The concentrations ranged from 0.1 ± 0.0 mg/L (Se) to 602.6 ± 77.6 mg/L (K). Significant positive correlations were observed among several mineral pairs. Notably, potassium levels were significantly associated with maternal employment status (p = 0.047), and zinc levels were significantly related to maternal parity (p = 0.028). Although differences between preterm and full-term samples were not statistically significant, preterm colostrum showed trends of higher zinc and potassium concentrations. Compared to global data, the Ghanaian samples exhibited relatively elevated levels of phosphorus, sodium, and selenium. These findings provide foundational data on early breast milk composition in Ghana and highlight maternal and gestational factors that may influence neonatal mineral intake.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15815-x.Keywords: Essential mineral elements, Colostrum, Breast milk, Breastfeeding, LactationSubject terms: Analytical chemistry, Environmental chemistry, Biochemistry, Physiology

Introduction

Breast milk is universally recognized as the optimal source of nutrition for infants during the first six months of life, offering a complete balance of macronutrients, bioactive compounds, and essential micronutrients necessary for growth and immune development1–3. The early postpartum secretion known as colostrum, produced during the first two to five days after birth, is particularly rich in immunoglobulins, leukocytes, antimicrobial peptides, and bioavailable minerals. Colostrum plays a vital role in neonatal adaptation to extrauterine life, supporting both immune defense and organ system maturation4,5.

Among its components, essential mineral elements – such as calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), zinc (Zn), iron (Fe), copper (Cu), sodium (Na), potassium (K), and phosphorus (P) – are crucial for numerous physiological functions. These include bone mineralization, enzymatic activity, immune competence, neurodevelopment, and cellular homeostasis6,7. Deficiencies or imbalances in these minerals during infancy can impair immune responses, slow growth, and cause long-term neurodevelopmental delays8. Thus, assessing the concentrations of these minerals in colostrum is essential to determine whether maternal milk meets neonatal nutritional requirements, especially in populations at risk of micronutrient deficiencies.

The levels of minerals in colostrum are known to be influenced by maternal factors such as age, diet, parity, health status, and environmental exposures9,10. Although several studies have investigated the mineral composition of mature human milk, limited data are available on colostrum, particularly in mothers across different socioeconomic and geographic contexts11,12. Moreover, reported mineral values in colostrum vary considerably, often due to methodological differences in sample collection timing, analytical techniques, and participant characteristics.

To address this gap, the present study aimed to quantitatively analyze the concentrations of key essential minerals in the colostrum of mothers using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). Moreover, the study attempted to determine the possible maternal characteristics that influence the levels of essential elements in CM. By doing so, we contribute to a clearer understanding of the variability and adequacy of mineral content in early lactation and its potential implications for neonatal health and nutrition policy.

Materials and methods

This study was carried out at the Ho Teaching Hospital in Ghana. A cross-sectional design approach was employed. Ho Teaching Hospital is situated in the Ho Municipality in the Volta Region of Ghana. According to Ghana’s 2021 population and housing census, the population of the Ho Municipality is 180,42013. The economy of the Ho Municipality is predominantly subsistence agriculture. The main catchment population is Ho and its surroundings. However, referrals are received from all over the districts and beyond the region. Foreigners such as Togolese, Beninois and Nigerians also considerably patronize the services of HTH.

The protocol for the study (S4), was reviewed and approved by the University of Health and Allied Sciences Ethics Research Committee (UHAS–REC), protocol number [UHAS-REC A.9 [6]19–20]. Written informed consent (S1) was obtained from all participants before they participated in the study. The authors, therefore, collectively confirm that, all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations set out in the Helsinki Declaration.

The study included 47 healthy mothers. The inclusion criteria comprised healthy mothers aged 18–48 years who gave birth in the maternity ward of the Ho Teaching Hospital during the study period and provided informed consent to participate. Mothers with twin births were excluded.

The main sample investigated in the study was colostrum. A purposive sampling technique was used to select respondents. Each mother provided a single 12 mL colostrum sample collected between postpartum days 1 and 4. Samples were obtained via manual expression approximately two hours after a prior feeding, following aseptic techniques conducted by trained midwives. The nipples and areolas were cleaned with denatured alcohol prior to expression. Collected samples were stored in a − 20 °C freezer until analysis. The samples were collected between December 25, 2020, and June 30, 2021. In addition to the breast milk samples, each respondent was asked to complete a structured questionnaire (S2) on sociodemographic information, biodata, and diet patterns, among other factors.

Self-employment in this study refers to a lactating mother who is an independent contractor or a sole proprietor who is responsible for her own income, while a trader refers to someone whose business deals with merchandise, involving buying and selling to make income.

Materials and reagents

Reagent grade concentrated nitric acid (HNO3; AR, 70% w/v; 1.42 sp.gr.). from BDH), 1:1 hydrochloric acid, HCl (36% w/v, Merck), Reagent water (AFS-Water-Purification-Systems, Merck KGaA), Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP 7400 Duo ICP‒OES Spectrometer equipped with a Teledyne ASX-560 Autosampler, hot plate (Karizma KZ – HP 2000 Double Hot Plate − 2000 W), 100 ml Griffin beakers, watch glasses, 20.0 ml sterile plastic sampling containers, Ultra Spec multi-elements standards with Lot Numbers CSG02-08-21US, CSG02.1-08-21US (from De Bruyn Spectroscopic Solutions).

Sample preparation for the determination of mineral elements in colostrum

Sample preparation and analysis of human colostrum milk for essential mineral elements were performed in an accredited laboratory. The procedure was based on EPA Methods 3010 A and 200.7. A ten-milliliter (10.0 mL) aliquot of each colostrum sample was measured and placed in a 100.0 mL Griffin beaker, to which was then added 1.0 mL concentrated HNO3 (analytical grade from Sigma Aldrich) and set on a hot plate (Karizma KZ–HP 2000 Double Hot Plate – 2000 W; temperature 90–95 °C). The sample was carefully evaporated to approximately 5.0 mL, ensuring that it did not boil and that no part of the beaker was dry.

After cooling the beaker, 1.0 mL of concentrated HNO3 was added to the sample. The beaker was returned to the hot plate, and the temperature was increased to 94 °C to induce gentle reflux. The sample was heated continually on a hot plate with the addition of 1.0 mL of acid as needed until the digestion was complete (generally indicated when the digestate was light in colour or did not change in appearance with continued refluxing). After the beaker was cooled, 1.0 mL of 1:1 HCl was added. After that, the beaker was covered and refluxed for 15 min. The beaker walls and watch glass were rinsed with distilled water, filtered and filled with distilled water to the 50.0 mL mark before being transferred to the ICP‒OES (Thermo Fisher Scientific iCAP 7400 Duo) equipment for analysis. The laboratory reagent blank samples were treated in the same way. The instrument operating for the ICP – OES is presented in the Table S4 as supplementary material.

Quality assurance protocols for the determination of mineral elements

For reliability of the results, all relevant quality assurance measures were assessed. For each batch of processed analytical samples, blanks (calibration blank and method blank), replicate samples and calibration verification standards (CVS) were used to determine the efficiency and accuracy of the analytical procedure. The reagents were subjected to the same procedures as the samples, including contact with all the glassware and tools used. The laboratory reagent blanks were used to ascertain the presence of any analytes or interferences in the laboratory environment, reagents, or equipment. Seven-point calibration was employed. The calibration standards were analyzed initially and periodically. Known concentrations of the multi-elements standard solution containing Ca, Cu, Fe, Mg, P, Na, K, S, Se and Zn were run through the instrument to calibrate the ICP‒OES curve for linearity. Afterwards, the colostrum extracts were run to determine the concentration of each of the essential elements.

Regarding quality assurance procedures, the percentage recoveries of the essential elements were as follows: Ca (110.9–111.4%; Cu (101.9–105.1%); Fe (100.0 − 102.6%); Mg (104.1 − 106.7%); K (97.7–98.8%); P (109.2– 110.1%); Se (99.9–103.3%); S (103.2–112.5%); and Na (94.4–96.5%), which were all in line with the US EPA Method 200.7 and within the acceptable limits prescribed by the scientific community. The limits of detection (LODs) for each of the ten mineral elements were measured on 6 replicates of the lowest calibration standard and included Cu (0.05 mg/L), Fe (0.50 mg/L), Mg (1.00 mg/L), P (1.00 mg/L), Na (1.00 mg/L), K (1.00 mg/L), S (1.00 mg/L), Se (0.01 mg/L) and Zn (0.50 mg/L).

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (v26), Excel Tool Pak, and XLSTAT. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Spearman correlation was used for nonparametric correlations. Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test for variables with more than two categories and the Mann-Whitney U test for binary group comparisons.

Possible factors influencing the levels of essential mineral elements in the colostrum of mothers

The qualitative variables on CM data such as education, diet and drinking water source were subjected to nonparametric analysis via the Kruskal‒Wallis test at a 95% confidence interval, while infants’ sex comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The results revealed no significant relationships between the levels of essential elements in CM and qualitative variables such education, diet, infant sex or drinking water source.

Discussion

This is the first study in Ghana examining essential mineral element levels in maternal colostrum. Essential mineral elements help the human body build soft and hard tissues, regulate body fluids, produce red blood cells, and support proper muscle and nerve functions.

14,15. Very few studies have determined the levels of essential elements in human colostrum milk. Moreover, due to differences in sample sizes, methods of analysis and units of reporting, comparisons of results from one study to another have become quite difficult and challenging. Despite these limitations, the results of the present study for all the essential elements were comparable to those reported in other studies16–19.

Although no statistically significant differences were observed between preterm and full-term colostrum samples (Table 3), notable trends were evident. Zinc and potassium concentrations were higher in preterm samples—a pattern consistent with previous reports20–22 which suggest enhanced zinc content in preterm milk as a compensatory mechanism for immature immune systems. Conversely, calcium and phosphorus levels were slightly lower in preterm samples, aligning with previous findings7,23 who noted mineral deficits in preterm milk that could necessitate supplementation. Sodium levels were reduced and potassium modestly elevated in preterm colostrum, partially mirroring the early ionic shifts reported by24. Iron concentrations showed a slight elevation in preterm milk, consistent with observations by25 that preterm milk may contain more iron to address lower prenatal iron stores.

The correlation analysis of essential mineral elements in colostrum (Table 4) revealed several statistically significant relationships, highlighting potential interactions and regulatory mechanisms in maternal milk composition. Notably, magnesium (Mg) exhibited positive correlations with zinc (Zn), potassium (K), and maternal age, suggesting that magnesium levels may rise with age and align closely with other mineral profiles, possibly due to shared physiological transport systems26. Zinc was significantly correlated with potassium, calcium (Ca), iron (Fe), and maternal age, supporting its pivotal role in enzymatic activity and nutrient transport in the mammary gland10,27.

Potassium demonstrated strong associations with calcium and iron, indicating possible coordinated secretion or regulation of these minerals during colostrum production7. Sodium (Na) showed meaningful correlations with iron and sulphur (S), while sulphur was strongly linked with copper (Cu), suggesting their involvement in redox reactions and protein synthesis pathways28. Selenium (Se), in contrast, displayed weak or negative correlations with most variables, likely due to its tight homeostatic control and dependence on dietary intake29. Maternal characteristics such as age showed moderate associations with several minerals, while BMI and gestational age showed limited or no significant correlations30. These findings underscore that mineral elements in colostrum are co-regulated, particularly Mg, Zn, K, Ca, and Fe, and are influenced more by maternal age than by BMI or gestational duration. Overall, the data align with existing literature on the physiological regulation of breast milk composition and emphasize the need for further exploration into maternal dietary and metabolic factors that influence colostrum quality31.

Association of levels of mineral elements in colostrum with maternal characteristics

The study identified a significant relationship between potassium (K) levels in colostrum milk (CM) and mothers’ employment status, indicated by a p-value of 0.047. The findings suggest that the employment status of mothers may significantly influence the potassium concentration in their colostrum, which is critical for neonatal health and development.

This variation may reflect occupational differences in maternal diet, stress, and overall health, all of which can influence electrolyte composition in early milk. Potassium is vital for neonatal cellular metabolism and neuromuscular function32. It is a vital electrolyte involved in numerous physiological processes, including maintaining fluid balance, supporting cellular function, and facilitating nerve and muscle activity33–36. In the context of lactation, adequate potassium levels in colostrum are essential for infant growth, neurodevelopment, and metabolic regulation37,38. Although typically stable in human milk, colostral K concentrations may be affected by maternal physiology and mammary epithelial permeability during early lactation14,39. These findings suggest that maternal occupation, potentially linked with nutritional status and parity, may influence the secretion of essential minerals like K into colostrum.

Research has shown that maternal lifestyle factors, particularly employment status, can significantly impact dietary intake and nutritional adequacy during pregnancy and lactation40–45. Employed mothers may face unique challenges that influence their dietary patterns, including time constraints and stress, which could lead to variations in nutrient intake and subsequent colostrum composition46–48. For instance, working mothers may be more inclined to opt for convenience foods, which often lack essential nutrients, potentially resulting in lower potassium levels in colostrum49,50.

Furthermore, maternal employment may affect the frequency and quality of meals, as well as the ability to prioritize nutrient-dense foods, which are critical for both maternal and infant health47,51,52. This significant relationship highlights the need for tailored nutritional education and support for lactating mothers, essentially, those balancing employment with caregiving responsibilities. Ensuring adequate potassium levels in colostrum is vital for optimizing both maternal well-being and infant outcomes.

Although the study found a significant relationship between Zn levels and maternal parity, the direction and magnitude of this finding differ from most prior studies. This discrepancy highlights the need for further investigation into regional and individual nutritional differences.

Zinc is an essential trace element that plays a vital role in numerous biological functions, including immune response, growth, and development53,54. Some studies indicate that higher zinc levels in colostrum are associated with enhanced immunological outcomes in infants53,55–57. This reinforces the importance of maternal zinc status during pregnancy and lactation. Variability in zinc levels may be influenced by factors such as maternal diet, body stores, and physiological changes during successive pregnancies58–62. The works of56,63 provides insights into how dietary intake and maternal reserves can influence zinc availability in colostrum.

Some studies, such as64 did not find any significant relationship between the levels of trace elements and maternal age, maternal education, parity, infant sex or drinking water sources. Aumeistere65 also reported that maternal age, parity and infant sex did not significantly influence the levels of essential elements in breast milk.

Compared to values reported in other countries, most of the essential mineral concentrations in Ghanaian colostrum fall within globally accepted ranges, though certain elements stood out. For example, the mean phosphorus level (193.2 ± 46.8 mg/L) was higher than values reported in Spain (134.9 mg/L), Korea (120–160 mg/L), and Brazil (112 ± 28 mg/L)16,17,63. This could reflect maternal dietary patterns or environmental factors unique to the study region. Similarly, sodium (403.9 ± 143 mg/L) and potassium (602.6 ± 77.6 mg/L) levels were elevated compared to most countries except Nigeria and Detroit, where extremely high concentrations have been documented15,67. These elevations may be related to early postpartum changes in mammary epithelial permeability or maternal hydration status, consistent with previous observations14,39.

Zinc (3.8 ± 1.4 mg/L) levels were above those reported in Spain, Taiwan, and Korea, but lower than levels from Nigeria and Germany16,17,66,72. The statistically significant relationship between Zn concentration and maternal parity (p = 0.028) observed in this study aligns with findings from previous research suggesting that multiparity may enhance maternal mineral mobilization during lactation56,62. Higher zinc concentrations in colostrum have been linked to improved neonatal immune outcomes53,55,56emphasizing the nutritional importance of adequate maternal Zn levels.

The significant association between K concentration and maternal employment status (p = 0.047) also raises important considerations. Employed mothers may experience different dietary routines, stress levels, or hydration habits, all of which could influence the secretion of electrolytes into colostrum. Potassium is vital for cellular function, neuromuscular activity, and neonatal metabolic regulation32,33,38; hence, its variation across maternal groups warrants further investigation.

Other minerals such as calcium, magnesium, iron, and copper were within or close to internationally reported ranges16,17,67. Notably, selenium (0.11 ± 0.0 mg/L) levels in Ghanaian colostrum were relatively higher than those reported in most countries, possibly due to environmental selenium availability or dietary practices16,17,72.

These findings suggest that mineral composition in colostrum is shaped by both physiological regulation and contextual maternal factors, such as diet, parity, and socioeconomic conditions. The present study contributes critical data from a sub-Saharan African context, which remains underrepresented in global lactation research.

Limitations of the study

The main limitation of the study was the small sample size; therefore, the findings cannot be generalized. However, these findings are significant because they can serve as baseline data for future studies.

The smaller sample size was because most mothers were not willing to donate their breast milk for research purposes owing to their cultural beliefs. Even though sample collection lasted for six (6) months (December 25, 2020 – June 30, 2021), we struggled to obtain only 47 mothers to donate their colostrum milk.

Conclusions

This study provides the first dataset on essential mineral element concentrations in colostrum from Ghanaian mothers. Notable associations were observed between potassium and maternal employment status, and between zinc and parity. While a subgroup analysis of preterm versus full-term births showed no statistically significant differences, trends such as elevated zinc and potassium levels in preterm colostrum may suggest physiological adaptations. These findings offer insight into the potential influence of maternal characteristics on neonatal nutrition and emphasize the importance of context-specific maternal-infant health strategies in sub-Saharan Africa.
