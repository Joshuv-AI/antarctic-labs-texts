Novel variants in ARID1B, SPSB1, and RAET1-AS shape genetic susceptibility and protection in systemic lupus erythematosus and lupus nephritis
Authors: Hung-Yi Chen, Feng-Cheng Liu, Hsian-Cheng Chen, Tan-Mei Liu, Fu-Chiang Yeh, Yu-Tien Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by immune dysregulation affecting multiple organs, most notably lupus nephritis (LN). Genetic susceptibility significantly contributes to disease severity and prognosis. However, genomic studies specific to Taiwanese SLE patients, particularly those focusing on LN, remain limited. This study aimed to identify genetic variants associated with SLE and LN in a Taiwanese cohort.MethodsGenome-wide association studies (GWAS) were performed on 276 Taiwanese SLE patients compared with 3393 patients with other rheumatic diseases. Single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) genotyping was conducted using the Taiwan Precision Medicine Array, with analyses adjusted for age, sex, and population structure. Functional annotations and biomarkers of renal function were assessed for identified SNPs.ResultsCompared with patients with other rheumatic diseases, a novel protective variant, rs9480438 (ARID1B), was identified in the SLE group, suggesting an association with dysregulation of cell cycle checkpoints. In the LN group, a protective variant, rs1294028 (SPSB1), and a risk variant, rs17079029 (RAET1-G/RAET1-AS), were linked to altered serum biomarkers and potential pathogenesis influenced by TGFβ and NK cells.ConclusionsNovel genetic variants related to immune regulation and autoimmunity in Taiwanese SLE and LN patients were identified. These variants provide insight into disease mechanisms and may guide early detection and personalized therapeutic approaches.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus, Lupus nephritis, Genome-wide association studies

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a heterogeneous autoimmune disease, which is characterized by autoantibodies formation, complement activation, and immune complex deposition, resulting in multiple organ damage such as kidney, brain, skin, lung, or joints. SLE predominantly affects women of childbearing age (Women: men = 9:1) and has higher prevalence in African-Americans than Asian and Caucasians, with strong gender and ancestry bias [1]. In Taiwan, the overall prevalence of SLE per hundred thousand people is 8.11 and the incidence is 0.81–1 per 100,000 person-years [2,3]. Compared to the Western countries, SLE population in Asia has a more severe disease course and a higher complication rate [1,4]. Lupus nephritis (LN), affecting up to 40 % of adults and 80 % of children with SLE, is the most significant complication and a major cause of morbidity and mortality [5]. Although earlier diagnosis and optimal management have improved LN outcomes, the risk of progression to end-stage renal disease has not decreased over the past decade [5]. Thus, early identification of patients at risk for SLE and LN based on specific factors, such as genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers, is crucial [[6], [7], [8], [9]].

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified genetic association between numerous single nucleotide polymorphisms and different immune disease, such as rheumatoid arthritis [10,11], inflammatory bowel disease [[12], [13], [14]], ankylosing spondylitis [[15], [16], [17]], and SLE. Recent studies have confirmed disease susceptibility is highly associated with single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) variation [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. In a meta-analysis, Yin et al. identified 113 SLE susceptible loci containing 46 loci were novel in East Asians, while 2 novel SNP rs10823829 and rs35985016 were found with lead missense variants at CHD23 and LRRK1, respectively [23]. Meanwhile, the mRNA of LINC00470 and ADCYAP1 were significantly lower in LN patients, influenced by the closet SNP rs12606116 [24]. Most SNPs demonstrated suggestive associations with LN [25], highlighting additional susceptibility markers that may clarify disease pathogenesis. However, similar studies remain limited in Taiwan.

In this study, we aimed to identify SNPs associated with the disease to uncover potential genetic risk and protective factors that contribute to the pathogenesis and clinical progression of SLE and LN. To bridge the existing knowledge gap between diseases and newly identified SNPs, the biological significance of these SNPs within the broader context of autoimmune diseases was explored, providing insights that may guide future personalized care.

Methods and materials

Ethics

The study procedures adhered to the ethical guideline of the 2013 Helsinki Declaration-Ethical Principles for medical research. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taiwan Tri-Service General Hospital (TSGH IRB No. 1-108-05-174 and 2-106-05-001).

Study participants and genotyping

Participants were recruited from the Department of Rheumatology, Immunology, and Allergy, Tri-Service General Hospital, as part of the Taiwan Precision Medicine Initiative (TPMI), a genetic database project led by Academia Sinica in collaboration with 15 top medical centers in Taiwan. To obtain genomic DNA, 3 mL of peripheral blood was collected from participants in EDTA vacutainers. DNA purification from blood cells was performed using the QIAGEN QIA symphony SP system (Hilden, Germany). For genotyping, each participant's purified DNA was loaded onto the Taiwan Precision Medicine (TPM) Array chip [26]. Genotype signals generated from the TPM Array were detected using the Axiom GeneTitan platform (ThermoFisher Scientific, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Genotyping data were processed through SNP calling, followed by quality control (QC) procedures. SNP annotations were subsequently conducted using the Axiom Analysis Suite (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

All patients were identified using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 9th and 10th Revisions (ICD-9 and ICD-10). For the analysis of genetic variation between SLE and other rheumatic diseases, patients were classified into two groups: (1) patients with SLE and (2) patients with non-SLE rheumatic diseases. Patients with SLE fulfilled either the 1997 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) [27,28] or the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) classification criteria [29,30]. The Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI-2K) [31] was used to evaluate disease activity at enrollment, but no minimum SLEDAI score was required for inclusion. Non-SLE rheumatic diseases included (1) rheumatoid arthritis (2010 ACR-EULAR Classification Criteria [32]), (2) Sjögren's syndrome (2016 ACR-EULAR Classification Criteria [33]); (3) inflammatory myopathies (2017 ACR-EULAR Classification Criteria [34]); and (4) psoriatic arthritis (2006 Classification Criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis [35]) and (5) ankylosing spondylitis (Assessment of Spondyloarthritis International Society (ASAS) criteria [36]); and (6) other connective tissue diseases (Supplement Table 1). This classification was chosen to ensure a broad comparison base and to explore the unique features of SLE in contrast to other autoimmune disorders.

In secondary analysis, SLE patients were subdivided to lupus nephritis (LN) and non-LN group. LN patients were included if they had biopsy-confirmed Class III, IV, or V nephritis, a definite SLE diagnosis with a serum creatinine level greater than 2 mg/dL, or required maintenance hemodialysis. Among 77 LN patients, 42 (54.5 %) were biopsy-confirmed (Class III, n = 13; Class IV, n = 26; Class V, n = 3), while 35 (45.5 %) were clinically defined based on renal dysfunction (serum creatinine >2.0 mg/dL) or dialysis requirement (n = 14). Biopsy-confirmed and clinically defined LN patients were further compared with respect to demographics, renal function, complement levels, and serological markers (Supplement Table 2). Clinical characteristics and biomarkers—including age, gender, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), complement levels, and autoantibody levels—were collected concurrently for functional annotations.

Genome-wide association analysis

Association testing was performed using Fisher exact test (unadjusted model) and logistic regression models adjusting for various combination of age, sex and population structure to reach the optimal lambda value (≈1) in PLINK 1.9 (https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/plink/). Initially, we filtered genotyping data from the TPM array SNPs (totaling 743,227 SNPs) obtained from TPM array with a low typing call rate (<80 %) and low minor allele frequency (<0.05). After filtering out eligible genotyping data, we conduct a comprehensive quality control (QC) process to ensure its integrity. Initially, participants with missing genotype data exceeding 5 % were excluded, along with SNPs exhibiting high missing data rates over 95 %. Heterozygosity rates were then calculated, and individuals with rates deviating by more than three standard deviations from the mean were removed. To minimize relatedness, identity by descent (IBD) was assessed, and individuals with a relatedness coefficient greater than 0.1875 were excluded [37]. Further SNP filtering was performed by removing variants with a minor allele frequency below 5 % and those that failed Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium tests (p < 0.0001). Differential missing genotype rates between cases and controls were also addressed by excluding SNPs with significant differences (p < 0.001). Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on pruned SNPs to identify potential population stratification and outliers, ensuring the dataset was appropriately cleaned for downstream analysis. Genome-wide suggestive significance was defined at P < 1 × 10−4 level by Fisher exact test due to small sample size. Significant loci were visualized using Manhattan plots, quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots and locuszoom plots generated using R packages, “CMplot”, “qqman” and “locuszoomr”. The genomic inflation factor, λ (lambda), was calculated to assess potential systematic bias in the analysis. A value of λ close to 1 indicates the absence of such bias, suggesting that no further correction is necessary.

SNP annotation

TPMI SNPView (https://healthit.ibms.sinica.edu.tw/snpview/) and GWAS catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) were used to search mapped or nearby genes of genetic SNPs variants. Corresponding genes were reviewed in gprofiler, national center of biotechnology information (NCBI) dbSNP database, and forgedb database for the correlation between gene and protein translation and function.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) between chromosome 6 index variants and HLA DR haplotype tags (DR3: rs2187668; DR2: rs3135388; for East Asians, rs9271366 was additionally evaluated) was quantified as r2 and D’ using LDlink (EAS panel; GrCh38; Population: CHB + CHS) and replicated with PLINK on 1000 Genomes EAS reference data. Genome build consistency was ensured across resources.

Association between SNPs and kidney function

Kidney function biomarkers were obtained from patient laboratory reports at the Tri-Service General Hospital. The biochemical markers analyzed included blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), urine protein, urine creatinine (Urine Cr), urine protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR), antinuclear antibody (ANA), and anti-double stranded DNA (Anti-dsDNA). For each significant SNP identified in the genotyping analysis, violin plots were generated via R package “ggpubr” to visually assess the association between SNP genotypes and the aforementioned kidney function biomarkers. Only those SNPs demonstrating a statistically significant association with the biomarkers were included for display in the final plots. Student t-test and Wilcoxon-Mann Whitney test were performed to evaluate the significance of differences across genotypes for each biomarker. Genotypes for SNPs were encoded using a 0, 1, 2 scheme: 0 for homozygous for major allele, 1 for heterozygous, and 2 for homozygous for minor allele.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R version 4.2.2. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies or percentages, while continuous variables are expressed as means with standard errors. Differential analyses between case and control groups were conducted using the Student's t-test for continuous variables and the Pearson Chi-square test for categorical variables. In cases where sample sizes were small among specific subgroups, Fisher's exact test was employed to assess statistical differences.

Results

The demographic profiles differed notably between the SLE and non-SLE groups, as shown in Table 1. SLE patients were more female-predominant (89.5 % vs 65.6 % with p < 0.001) and younger in age (Mean: 47.2 vs 53.1 years with p < 0.001).

SLE-associated SNPs

In the first GWAS, genetic variants associated with SLE, compared to other rheumatic diseases, were identified. Considering relatively small SLE sample size, four models were employed to ensure variant identification: one unadjusted model and three adjusted models accounting for different combinations of age, sex, and population structure (Supplement Fig. 1). Four significant SNPs consistently identified across all four models were extracted for further analysis (Table 3 and Supplement Table 3). Two SNPs were associated with a decreased risk of SLE, while two were linked to an increased risk. Functional annotation of the identified SNPs revealed that rs4251697 and rs117026326 were specifically associated with SLE.

LN-associated SNPs in the Taiwanese SLE patients

A secondary genome-wide association study (GWAS) was performed on 202 systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients without lupus nephritis (non-LN) and 74 SLE patients with lupus nephritis (LN) (Table 2). Gender was not included as a covariate because there was no significant difference in gender distribution between these two groups. The association results of the Fisher's exact test model presented associated SNPs after applying three models for the analysis: an unadjusted model and two adjusted models that accounted for combinations of age and population structure. Notably, LN-associated SNPs demonstrated consistent significance across all models (Supplement Fig. 2).

Six SNPs were identified to be significantly associated with lupus nephritis (LN) in SLE patients (Table 4). In our analysis, four SNPs conferred an increased risk of LN and three of them were located chromosome 6: rs16881832 (mapped to RRAGD, OR = 2.23 [1.50–3.30]), rs17079029 (mapped to RAET1-AS and RAET1-G, OR = 2.45 [1.67–3.62]), and rs3012541 (near CD109, OR = 5.08 [2.42–10.68]). The other risk SNP, rs12272312 on chromosome 11 (mapped to SDK1-AS1), showed the strongest association (OR = 10.3 [9.57–10.50]).

Combining with serum biomarkers, patients carrying the homozygous minor allele of rs16881832/G exhibited significantly higher urine protein-creatinine ratios compared to those carrying the major allele of rs16881832/C. Similarly, trends towards elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (Cr) levels were observed in patients with minor allele of rs17079029/C compared to those with the major allele rs17079029/T (Fig. 1D–G). The minor T allele of rs12272312 was associated with significantly reduced serum complement levels (C3/C4) (Fig. 1H and I).

In contrast, two SNPs were associated with a decreased risk of LN. rs1294028, located on chromosome 1 and mapped to SPSB1, had a protective effect with an OR of 0.29 (95 % CI: 0.16–0.50). Similarly, rs10276303 (on chromosome 7, mapped to SDK1-AS1) was associated with an OR of 0.36 (0.36–0.37). Notably, patients carrying the minor A allele of rs1294028 displayed higher serum C3 levels compared to those with the G allele (Fig. 1J). These findings suggest that SNP variations are associated with the pathogenesis of LN. Such genetic variations may either increase susceptibility to the disease or, conversely, confer a protective effect that reduces risk. No overlapping SNPs were discovered between SLE-associated and LN-associated SNPs.

To further visualize the regional associations of LN-associated SNPs, we generated locuszoom plots with 200 kb flanking regions around the lead variants (Fig. 2). For rs1294028 on chromosome 1 (SPSB1), the association peak was confined to the locus itself, with no nearby variants in strong linkage disequilibrium (LD). Similarly, rs16881832 (RRAGD) and rs17079029 (RAET1E-AS1/RAET1G) on chromosome 6 demonstrated distinct association signals, each represented by a clear peak, while surrounding SNPs showed low LD, suggesting independent effects within these loci. In contrast, rs3012541 (CD109) displayed a sharper signal peak with fewer neighboring SNPs in moderate LD.

Linkage disequilibrium analysis with HLA-DR haplotypes

To address the possibility of confounding by classical HLA alleles, we further examined LD patterns between the chromosome 6 index SNPs and HLA-DR haplotype tags by LDlink (CHB + CHS panel; DR3: rs2187668; DR2: rs3135388; East Asian tag: rs9271366). Across all tested SNPs (rs16881832, rs17079029, rs9480438, rs3012541), r2 values were consistently <0.01, indicating no meaningful LD with either DR3 or DR2 haplotypes (Supplement Table 4). Together with the regional association plots, these findings suggest that the chromosome 6 signals are independent of classical HLA-DR risk alleles and represent distinct loci contributing to LN susceptibility.

Discussion

This genome-wide association study (GWAS) provides novel insights into the genetic underpinnings of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and lupus nephritis (LN) in the Taiwanese population. Our study identified four significant SNPs associated with SLE and six SNPs linked to LN, highlighting potential genetic risk factors and protective markers that contribute to disease susceptibility and clinical progression [4]. Notably, we discovered two novel protective variants, rs9480438 (ARID1B) and rs1294028 (SPSB1), and a novel risk variant rs17079029 (RAET1-G/RAET1-AS), which had not been previously reported in relation to SLE or LN. Functional annotation suggests that these variants may play critical roles in immune regulation, cell cycle control, and inflammatory pathways, furthering our understanding of SLE pathogenesis.

The association of SLE and the SNP rs4251697 within CDKN1B gene encoding the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor p27kip1 [38] is compatible with previous studies. The upregulation of p27kip1 is highly correlated with the induction of T lymphocyte anergy and apoptosis, both are important for immune tolerance [38,39]. When these processes are defective, both are thought to contribute to the development of SLE and LN. Yang et al. confirmed lower expression of CDKN1B in peripheral blood myeloid cells (PBMCs) of individuals with SLE [39].

The first protective SNP rs9480438 correlates with gene ARID1B [40]. ARID1A and ARID1B encode variant subunits in BRG1-associated factor (BAF) complexes, large nuclear proteins, BAF250a and BAF250b, respectively. These complexes regulate the transcription of certain genes by altering the chromatin structure around those genes [41]. ARID1b has been associated with cancers, acquired aplastic anemia, and intellectual disability [42,43]. Additionally, its family member ARID3a has been strongly linked to SLE autoimmunity by promoting the production of autoreactive B cells and ANA [40]. Our findings indicate that dysregulation of cell cycle checkpoints and associated regulatory proteins contributes to the altered immune responses and heightened autoantibody production characteristic of SLE. These aberrations in cell cycle control appear to influence key processes such as lymphocyte proliferation, apoptosis, and tolerance mechanisms, thereby exacerbating disease pathogenesis. Nonetheless, the precise molecular pathways linking cell cycle dysregulation to SLE manifestations have yet to be fully delineated, warranting further in-depth investigation.

The identification of rs117026326 as SLE risk-associated variants merits attention. In a meta-analysis of SLE susceptibility, Yin et al. revealed strong evidence of rs117026325 (OR = 1.490, p = 1.27E-45), consistent with our results [23]. Notably, the general transcription factor 2I (GTF2I) gene, which regulates nuclear NF-κB activation through p65 binding in non-immune cells, has been implicated in multiple autoimmune diseases, including systemic sclerosis and Sjögren's syndrome [44,45]. Furthermore, individuals carrying the rs117026326 variant exhibited significantly lower level of complement C3 and C4, suggesting a potential link between genetic susceptibility and complement deficiency. These findings underscore the critical role of intranuclear adaptor proteins complement dysregulation in the SLE pathogenesis.

While extensive GWAS have revealed genetic variations in SLE patients, our focus on LN development remains different. Two novel protective SNPs were identified. First, rs1294029 coding SPSB1 (a Spry domain-containing Socs box protein) as a novel regulator of the Transforming Growth Factor (TGF)-βand interferon (IFN)-α signaling pathway [46] was found with lower risk of LN. In monocytes, SPSB1 promotes TGFβ receptor II ubiquitination or binds directly to TβRII, both mechanism leading to suppressed TGF-β signaling [47]. The defective TGF-β signaling results in peripheral tolerance loss and hyperactive B cell. In addition, IFN-α signaling remains hyperactive because autophagic degradation of mitochondrial DNA is impaired [47]. The other protective SNP rs10276303 within SDK1-AS1 (side kick cell adhesion molecule 1-antisense RNA 1) influence expression of SDK1 by encoding long non-coding anti-sense RNA. To the best of our knowledge, SDK1 participates in the formation of neural circuits in the retina [48]. However, the specific function of SDK1 in this context has not yet been clarified. Further investigation into the interactions between these factors is needed.

One more interesting finding was the SNP rs17079029 in retinoic acid early transcript 1 Antisense RNA 1 (RAET1-AS) significantly associated with risk of LN development. RAET1E gene is a member of the RAET1/ULBP gene cluster, which translates NKG2D receptor ligand ULBP. NKG2D is a C-type lectin-like receptor present on NK, γδ, CD8+, and CD4+ T cells and plays a pivotal role in modulating immune responses. In previous studies, NKG2D expression on the surfaces of NK cells was reduced in SLE patients [49] and there was a significant negative correlation between the expression of this receptors and SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) scores [50]. Yang et al. found that NKG2D expression could be induced on normal CD4+ T cells by co-culturing with monocytes from SLE patients, and then NKG2D + CD4+ T cells produced Th1 cytokines depended on the cell–cell contact with CD14 cells [51]. The mechanism may involve NKG2D + CD4+ T cells killing Treg cells in an NKG2D-NKG2D-L-dependent manner. Therapeutic targeting of NKG2D interactions, such as through anti-NKG2D or anti-IFN-α receptor antibodies in animal models, has demonstrated restoration of regulatory T cells and significant improvement in symptoms [52]. Collectively, these results implied another aspect of the impact of T-reg cells and monocytes on disease pathogenesis.

There were some limitations in our study. First, the sample size in recruit stage and SNPs association study was not enough. Due to small effect sizes of 276 SLE participants and absence of a health control group from the same population, this study had limited power to demonstrate association between clinical manifestation and genome variation. Second, not all patients classified in the lupus nephritis group had biopsy confirmation, which may lead to potential misclassification. In these cases, renal biopsy was contraindicated owing to increased procedural risk associated with persistent thrombocytopenia or ongoing anticoagulant therapy. Third, the use of less stringent significance thresholds substantially increases the risk of false-positive findings and reduce the ability to detect true associations. Comparing SLE patients to individuals with other rheumatic diseases likely masked established risk loci due to shared genetic architecture. Furthermore, there were still limitations of patients recruited by serum test despite the gold standard of diagnosis of lupus nephritis is biopsy. Both factors reduce statistical power potentially. Finally, this study has limited application due to retrospective cross-sectional data and lack of replication of previous SLE and lupus nephritis loci from prior East Asian GWAS. Future longitudinal study was necessary to show how these genetic markers influence disease outcomes over time.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we detected two novel loci for SLE and one novel risk locus for LN susceptibilities in the GWAS analysis combining clinical characteristics. This study underscores the interplay between genetic predisposition, immune cells dysregulation, and intracellular signals involvement in SLE and LN. These findings provide a foundation for further functional and translational studies, ultimately advancing precision medicine in autoimmune diseases. Considering the complexity of pathogenesis, further research integrating genomic, immunological, and clinical data will be essential for advancing the understanding and optimizing immunosuppressive therapy of SLE and its complications.
