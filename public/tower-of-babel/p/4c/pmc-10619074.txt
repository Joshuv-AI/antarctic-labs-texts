Facilitators and barriers to using AI-enabled robots with older adults in long-term care from staff perspective: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Lillian Hung, Karen Lok Yi Wong, Joey Wong, Juyoung Park, Abdolhossein Mousavinejad, Hui Zhao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAssistive and service robots have been increasingly designed and deployed in long-term care (LTC) but little evidence guides their use. This scoping review synthesises existing studies on facilitators and barriers to using artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled robots with older adults in LTC settings.Methods and analysisWe will follow the Joanna Briggs Institute’s scoping review methodology for the study, to be conducted from November 2023 to April 2024. We will focus on literature exploring the use of AI-enabled robots with older adults in an LTC setting from healthcare providers’ perspectives. Three steps will be taken: (a) keywords and index terms will be identified from MEDLINE and CINAHL databases; (b) comprehensive searches will be conducted in MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, AgeLine, PsycINFO, ProQuest and Google, using keywords and index terms identified in step (a); and (c) examining reference lists of the included studies and selecting items in the reference lists which meet the inclusion criteria. Searches for grey literature will also be conducted via Google. The results will be presented in a charting table and a narrative summary will be presented in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval and participation consent are not required because the data are publicly available. The results will be presented via a journal article and conference presentations.Keywords: aged; aged, 80 and over; dementia

Introduction

According to a United Nations World Social Report in 2023, 1.6 billion people will be age 65 years or older in 2050, with demand for healthcare services expected to continue to rise due to age longevity and the growing prevalence of chronic conditions and disabilities.1 Providing high-quality care to long-term care (LTC) residents presents complex challenges associated with comorbidities and diverse cultural backgrounds.2 3 The rapid advancement of technology has brought significant changes to the quality of care for the older people, accelerated by the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled robots with advanced intelligence capabilities may offer personalised services for LTC) residents and may provide efficient support to staff. Robots have been considered as solutions to provide emotional support, social companionship and automated services such as monitoring and telehealth.4 5 As staff’s perceptions influence technology adoption, it is crucial to explore facilitators and barriers to technology implementation from their perspective.6–9 This understanding will refine current technological applications and inform future adoptions in healthcare. Robotics and AI are two emerging components in healthcare technology with the potential to improve care and quality of life for residents in care homes.

The literature on implementing AI-enabled robots in LTC from a staff perspective is emerging. For example, Getson and Nejat10 examined the staff’s acceptance of Pepper, an AI-enabled Socially Assistive Robot (SAR), which was used to do health screening of staff in an LTC facility in Canada during COVID-19. Lukkien et al11 conducted a scoping review on ethics and the design and implementation of AI, including AI-enabled robots, in LTC. Loveys et al12 also conducted a systemic review on the acceptability and effectiveness of AI, including AI-enabled robots, in LTC. Nevertheless, both reviews focused more on residents than staff. Felding et al13 carried out a scoping review to examine the facilitators and barriers of acceptance for social robots, including AI-enabled robots, in LTC, of different groups of people, including staff. However, acceptance is only one aspect of the implementation. Their review did not look into other aspects of implementation.

This scoping review will examine the existing literature on facilitators for and barriers to implementing AI-enabled robots with older adults in LTC settings from the perspective of healthcare providers. It will contribute to building evidence for future research and development, as well as implementation of AI-enabled robots in supporting quality of life of older people living in LTC.

Defining robots and AI

Robots are mechanical devices that can be of various physical forms and are designed to perform a wide range of tasks. For example, robots can be vacuum cleaners at home and carts to deliver meals to patients in hospitals. AI is known as ‘the science of making [a] machine or computer to act intelligently’ (Murphy,14 p.3). There have been increasing attention to and adoption of AI technologies in healthcare. For example, AI in the form of personal medical assistant helps to monitor and support patients’ needs.15 AI can suggest treatment methods based on the patients’ health data.15

AI-enabled robot in LTC

The AI-enabled robot, or intelligence robot, can be defined as ‘a physically situated intelligent agent in the ‘real world’, regardless of shape, that can sense and act on its operational environment’ (Murphy14, p.15). AI allows robots to (a) present the world symbolically in a way that can be easily understood by computers, (b) understand natural language and explore clear communication required for comfortable social interaction between humans and robots, (c) learn by self-iterative trials and apply that learning to a range of functions, (d) plan and solve problems, (e) generate an answer without complete information, (f) use search algorithms to generate solutions in navigation or search for optimal knowledge representation and (g) improve robotic actions with vision systems in the robots.14

AI-enabled robots have various applications in healthcare, aimed at assisting staff with daily tasks and addressing the unmet needs of residents in LTC. In care of older adults, assistive and SARs are two common types.16 For example, a humanoid robot called Pepper is designed to interact with and engage older adults17 and a social robot called PARO is used to provide companionship and support for the psychosocial needs of older adults.18 19 Telepresence robots are used to facilitate virtual family visits to facilitate residents’ social connections.20 21 These robots could potentially improve older adults’ quality of life and physical functions in LTC.22 Service robots assist with certain tasks, reduce the workload of healthcare workers, and allow them time to focus on direct care. For example, cleaning robots that help with household chores and robots may deliver food in healthcare settings.16 23

Methodology

We will use the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for conducting scoping reviews, which provides step-by-step guidelines for building a protocol, conducting literature searches, selecting literature, extracting and synthesising data, and reporting results. This methodology has been widely adopted across disciplines, particularly in healthcare.

An initial search was conducted on 31 March 2023, referring to various databases and the JBI Database of Systematic Reviews and Implementation Reports. No systematic reviews were found addressing facilitators for and barriers to using AI-enabled robots with older adults in an LTC setting from the perspective of healthcare providers.

Our scoping review is expected to have two significant contributions. First, it will provide a synthesis of the most recent evidence on facilitators and barriers of using AI-enabled robots with older adults in LTC settings from the perspective of healthcare providers. Second, given the large population of people living with dementia in LTC settings, patient partners with experience with living with a person with dementia and family partners with experience in caring for family members with dementia will be involved in various stages of the review. This collaborative approach will enhance the relevance and quality of the review.

Methods

The JBI methodology was chosen over other scoping review methodologies for two reasons. First, the JBI methodology is widely used and recognised internationally. Second, the guidelines of the JBI methodology are clear and straightforward. The scoping review is appropriate for our study questions because of the exploratory nature. Scoping reviews are often conducted when the research question is broad or when the topic is complex or emerging. The review method is designed to map the existing literature, identify gaps, and provide an overview of the available evidence, rather than to address a specific research question.24

Inclusion criteria

Participants

We will include older adults living in LTC settings. Older adults will be defined as people age 60 or older, which is the age commonly accepted internationally to be the age for a person to be considered as an older adult. For instance, the WHO25 considers people age 60 or older as older adults.

Concept

The scoping review will be designed to map facilitators for and barriers to using AI-enabled robots with older adults in LTC settings. The core concepts will be AI, older adult, LTC and healthcare provider.

Context

This scoping review will consider only using AI-enabled robots in an LTC context. In this study, LTC refers to care settings that provide 24-hour personal care support for people with complex needs who are unable to remain at home.26 We will exclude literature on community setting, such as home care, senior centres or adult day healthcare programmes.

Types of studies

Only literature in English published in the past 10 years (2013–2023) will be considered. All types of research designs will be considered, except meta-analysis. All sources will be considered, including qualitative and quantitative research articles, theses and dissertations.

Search strategy

Three steps are recommended by JBI scoping review methodology.24 First, a preliminary search will be conducted referring to two databases relevant to the topic of interest: MEDLINE and CINAHL. We will use keywords: AI, older adults, LTC and health care providers. Titles, abstracts and index terms of the identified studies will be reviewed. Second, all identified keywords and index terms will be used for searches. Third, the reference lists of chosen studies will be reviewed for additional literature. (See online supplemental file 1 for the full search strategy for MEDLINE). Our university medical librarian will be consulted for feedback on our search strategy. She will also be invited to collaborate with us in the scoping review. The authors of this protocol LH, JP and HZ are familiar with the literature of this scoping review, as they are experts on the topic. They will provide supervision on specific reference searches to the team.

Information sources

The following databases will be used: MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, AgeLine and PsycINFO. ProQuest will be used for theses and dissertations. Phase searches will be done for grey literature, using Google.

Study selection

We will store all mapped studies in the reference manager tool Mendeley. We will delete duplicates. The titles and abstracts will be screened separately by two research assistants. The inclusion criteria will be referred to during screening. The full text of the selected literature will be reviewed. The details of the citation of the chosen literature will be imported into the JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information.24

The full texts of the selected literature will be assessed separately by three researchers. The inclusion criteria will be referred to during the assessment. Studies not meeting the inclusion criteria will be excluded. The reasons for exclusion will be recorded.

The medical librarian will provide support to fine tune the search and selection processes. In case reviewers have divergent views on including a study not, they will discuss their views. If an agreement cannot be reached LH, JP and HZ and will decide whether to include the study or not.

The findings of the searches and the (PRISMA) flow diagram will be presented in the final scoping review article.

Data extraction

A charting table (online supplemental file 2) will be presented to document extracted data. The data to be extracted will be first author, year of publication, country, type of literature, design of study, setting, population and sample sizes, facilitators for and barriers to using AI-enabled robots with older adults in an LTC setting from the perspective of healthcare providers, strategies to overcome the barriers or to facilitate use of AI-enabled robots with older adults in an LTC setting from the perspective of healthcare providers, and results and implications.

A pilot test of the charting table will be conducted. Data will be extracted from three studies by two researchers independently and the researchers will compare their extractions.

The charting table may be changed during the data extraction process. Changes will be documented in the final scoping review article.

Patient and public involvement

One patient partner (ie, person with dementia) has been consulted about the study questions. One patient partner and one family partner (ie, a person with experience in caring for a person with dementia) will be involved in conducting the scoping review. The patient and family partners will be recruited from the Community Engagement Advisory Network, a community organisation that facilitates patient and public involvement in research (http://cean.vch.ca). They will review the full texts of chosen articles that they would like to review. Each partner is expected to review 2–5 articles.

We will have regular meetings via Zoom with patient and family partners. Patient and family partners will be involved in team analysis to synthesise the results. They will share the findings in their networks to support knowledge dissemination.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval and participation consent will not be required because the data are publicly available. We will present the findings via a journal article and conference presentations. Healthcare providers, policy-makers and the public will be able to access the findings.

Data synthesis and presentation

A charting table will be used to present the extracted data. We will read the findings repeatedly individually and as a team to answer research questions. The results will be sorted into categories and grouped into themes. A narrative summary of the findings and key themes will be given in the final report.
