Evidence-Based Behavioral Interventions for Diabetes Prevention in the United States: A Scoping Review Using 24-Hour Recalls and Objective Activity Measures
Authors: Sheida Zeraattalab-Motlagh, Claudia E Irazabal Lopez Henriquez, Maritza Cantu, Tracey Ledoux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe prevalence of type 2 diabetes continues to rise, with behavioral interventions recognized as effective preventive strategies among adults in the United States. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommends improving diet and activity behaviors (physical activity and sedentary behavior) as key targets for type 2 diabetes prevention. Thus, this scoping review aimed to identify and describe key features of behavioral interventions that successfully improved diet and/or activity behaviors (aligned with ADA), including their delivery modes, target populations, and behavior change techniques (BCTs) used. PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, and Excerpta Medica Database were searched until 7 February, 2026, for randomized controlled trials evaluating efficacious behavioral interventions targeting diet and/or activity. Efficacious interventions were defined as those that demonstrated statistically significant effects on at least diet or activity outcome, assessed with validated measures, such as 24-h dietary recalls and objective activity assessments, among adults. Data were extracted on study samples, target populations, delivery modes, and BCTs, which were classified using Michie’s taxonomy. Twenty-three trials encompassing 2583 adults were included. Most efficacious United States–based interventions were tested among females, non-Hispanic White adults with at least some college education, aged 31 to 50 y, in an individual-based format (71%). According to delivery mode, intervention types were grouped into face-to-face (8%), electronic interventions (29%), environmental (8%), and combined modes (54%). Common BCTs included goal setting (96%), instruction on how to perform a behavior (83%), social support (79%), and feedback on behavior (67%). Although digital and combined delivery approaches were common among efficacious interventions and several frequently used BCTs were identified, the limited representation of diverse populations highlights the need for future research to evaluate the applicability and effectiveness of these strategies across broader demographic groups.This trial was registered at Open Science Framework as https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/UDNV7.Keywords: behavior change techniques, diabetes prevention, USA, delivery mode, adult

Introduction

Statement of significance

By mapping behavior change techniques across delivery modes in interventions that demonstrated significant behavioral change, this review provides new insight into how successful lifestyle interventions are structured.

The prevalence of diabetes has increased 2-fold in the last 20 y, impacting 38.1 million adults in United States, with ∼36 million cases attributed to type 2 diabetes (T2D) [1]. T2D is a metabolic disorder characterized by unusually elevated blood glucose concentrations and is one of the main drivers of mortality and morbidity [2]. It can also impact various aspects of an individual’s life, leading to considerable societal, economic, and personal challenges [3]. T2D is preceded by prediabetes, a reversible high-risk condition that represents an intermediate stage of glucose dysregulation [4]. This stage presents a unique opportunity for the primary prevention of T2D.

More than 20 y ago, the United States diabetes prevention program (DPP) showed that intensive lifestyle changes reduced risk of developing T2D by 58% [5], which was remarkable given that pharmacological intervention reduced risk of developing T2D by only 31%. In general, lifestyle interventions are preferred given they are safer and more affordable than medications [6,7]. Based on these findings, among others, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) identified diet and activity behaviors, including physical activity (PA) and sedentary behavior (SED), as core strategies for T2D prevention [5,8].

The national DPP is an intensive lifestyle intervention that is recognized by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention as an evidence-based diabetes prevention intervention [1]. Although the original DPP and similar behavioral interventions in China, Finland, and India demonstrated that intensive lifestyle interventions are both effective and cost-effective [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]], it is underutilized for many reasons related to the healthcare system, insurance coverage, and access [14]. Since the original DPP was adopted as an evidence-based intervention for T2D prevention, there have been advances in behavior change science, including options for telehealth services and understanding of determinants of health behaviors and behavior change techniques (BCTs). It is possible that more efficacious strategies for reducing T2D risk have been discovered since the original DPP was studied. Moreover, uncertainty remains regarding optimal lifestyle intervention approaches for diverse United States community settings [15]. The purpose of this scoping review is to describe features of behavior change interventions that have successfully changed diet and activity behaviors. This scoping review aimed to identify 1) the behavior change components of United States–based efficacious behavioral interventions for T2D prevention that targeted diet and activity behaviors (aligned with ADA); and 2) describe how these efficacious interventions were delivered across different modalities, as well as the adult populations they targeted.

Methods

This review was carried out according to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) framework for scoping review [16] and adhered to the instructions of the PRISMA-Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [17] (Supplemental Table 1). The protocol of this study was registered with the Open Science Framework (doi: 10.17605/OSF.IO/UDNV7).

Search strategy

An author (SZ-M) and the corresponding author (TL) developed the search string. Search strategies were created by searching using keywords related to evidence-based behavioral interventions in conjunction with terms related to diet and PA outcomes measured by 24-h dietary recalls and objective PA measures along with database-controlled vocabulary, such as Medical Subject Headings for Medical Literature Analysis and Retrieval System Online (MEDLINE), when available. The initial search was conducted on 15 May, 2025, and updated on 7 February, 2026, in the following electronic databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, and Excerpta Medica Database (Supplemental Table 2). Following the initial search, reference lists of papers were reviewed for additional papers that may be eligible for inclusion. Only studies published in English and conducted in the United States were included. No restriction was applied to the publication date.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined according to the “population, concept, context” framework for scoping reviews. To be eligible, studies had to be randomized controlled trials (RCTs) conducted in the United States, published in English, and including participants aged ≥18 y who were apparently healthy, at risk of T2D, or had prediabetes but were not yet diagnosed with T2D (population). Interventions had to be conducted in community settings (context). The concept of interest was the characterization of behavioral interventions for T2D prevention, including their BCTs, delivery modes and formats, target populations, and intervention characteristics. To identify evidence-based behavioral interventions aligned with this concept, eligible studies were required to demonstrate statistically significant between-group improvements in ≥1 diet, PA, or SED outcome compared with a control group (P < 0.05). The review focused on RCTs to prioritize study designs with stronger internal validity and reduced risk of confounding, which is particularly important for diet and activity outcomes that are highly susceptible to self-selection and contextual bias; this approach is consistent with PRISMA-ScR guidance permitting design-based eligibility criteria when aligned with review objectives [17,18].

Dietary and activity outcomes were defined according to ADA recommendations (Supplemental Table 3). The outcomes listed below represent those that demonstrated statistically significant improvements in the included efficacious interventions. Activity outcomes included significant improvements in PA [moderate-to-vigorous PA, light PA (minutes per hour, day, or week); steps per day or week] and SED (hours per day, minutes per day or hour, or total across multiple 2-h blocks per day).

Diet outcomes included significant improvement in the healthy eating index (HEI) score, carbohydrate intake, solid fruit intake, and reductions in sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB), sweet baked goods, candies, saturated fat, and other sweet foods and desserts (points for diet quality, grams per day, or servings per day for dietary intakes).

For the comparison group, studies were eligible if they followed a no-treatment, waitlist control, and attention control (e.g., similar format and intensity to intervention but different content area). Eligible studies were further required to use at least one 24-h dietary recall for diet and objective assessments (e.g., accelerometry, wearable devices) for activity behaviors. We chose objective assessment because many studies relied on self-reported dietary intake and PA [19], which are subject to reporting bias and memory inaccuracies [20,21]. In contrast, 24-h dietary recall is considered better than other self-reported dietary methods because it is the least biased and can capture dietary intake accurately [22]. PA measurement approaches differ in their ability to capture activity accurately. Self-reported measures such as recalls and diaries are practical and low-cost but are limited by recall bias, misinterpretation of questions, and reduced sensitivity to light or sporadic activities, which can reduce reliability and validity. Objective measures directly assess dimensions of PA, including frequency, intensity, and duration, and allow continuous assessment of free-living activity. Although no single gold standard exists for PA measurement, objective methods have been shown to provide more precise and consistent estimates of PA compared with subjective measures [23]. For this reason, only studies using objective PA assessment were included in this review.

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they enrolled participants already diagnosed with T2D or those with other medical conditions such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, or mental illness. Trials conducted in hospital, laboratory, or rehabilitation settings were also excluded to enhance ecological validity and relevance to real-world prevention, as these settings often involve controlled or temporary environments that may limit the generalizability and sustainability of observed behavior change once participants return to their usual routines [24,25]. Moreover, studies targeting specialized populations such as athletes, military personnel, pregnant or lactating females, as well as those that relied solely on self-reported dietary intake or activity behaviors, nonrandomized designs, protocols, reviews, dissertations, or non–peer-reviewed reports were also excluded.

Data extraction

Three reviewers (SZ-M, MC, and CEILH) separately screened and extracted the data from each trial, addressing discrepancies with the fourth investigator (TL). Extracted data comprised 1) study characteristics: last name of the first author, year, city, state, recruitment place, race/ethnicity, educational level, socioeconomic status (SES), mean age, number of participants (percentage female), retention (percentage), adherence (percentage), health status, and baseline BMI (in kg/m2); 2) intervention and control particulars: behavioral intervention components and their BCT categorization, provider, delivery mode, format, study duration and dose, comparison type, and theoretical framework; 3) Outcome components: targeted outcomes, assessment tool, number of assessments, and 4) other components: main findings, incentives, adverse events, and study fundings.

Regarding coding the behavioral interventions, BCTs were identified from the included intervention articles using the BCT Taxonomy v1 (BCTTv1) [26,27], following adapted guidance from the official BCTTv1 online training website (www.bct-taxonomy.com). BCTs were coded only if they explicitly targeted 1 or more intervention behaviors or preparatory behaviors. Coders reviewed the entire intervention description before assigning codes. Two trained authors (SZ-M and CEILH) independently coded the interventions, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third author (TL). We also examined whether studies explicitly articulated theory-derived constructs, operationalized them using specific BCTs, and empirically measured construct-level outcomes.

Moreover, behavioral interventions were also categorized into 4 groups based on their delivery mode, including face-to-face interventions, environmental interventions, electronic (e)-interventions, and combined modes of intervention [28]. Face-to-face interventions involved in-person delivery, such as educational programs, workshops, or counseling sessions. e-interventions were delivered entirely through digital tools, such as websites, mobile applications, or wearable devices. Combined modes of interventions integrated digital components with interpersonal support, such as tailored coaching or peer education [28]. Environmental interventions were defined as interventions that modified the immediate physical or economic environment to support healthy behaviors (e.g., sit–stand desks or financial incentives for purchasing healthy foods). These interventions targeted proximal environmental conditions rather than broader policy- or system-level environmental changes.

Critical appraisal

Critical appraisal was assessed to support the study’s objective of mapping available evidence by evaluating the methodological rigor and potential risk of bias in each study, thereby clarifying the strengths and limitations within this research area. Two authors (SZ-M and MC) independently conducted the appraisals using the 13-item JBI critical appraisal checklist for RCTs [29]. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third author (TL) (Supplemental Table 4).

About 71% of the included trials were delivered in an individual-based format. The intervention duration of trials spanned from 1 [35,36,42,47] to 24 [34] mo, with 58% lasting ≤3 mo. Most interventions reported significant improvements in activity behaviors, including PA (67%) and SED (26%), whereas only a few trials showed effects on diet (7%). No trial demonstrated beneficial effects on both diet and activity behavioral outcomes using 24-h dietary recall and objective activity measures. Participants in 26% of trials received incentives [34,35,37,40,42,50], whereas in the remaining trials, they did not receive any incentives or did not report any data. About 56% of trials reported being based on a theoretical framework [30,31,33,34,37,39,41,43,45,47,51,52], with social cognitive theory comprising the most (36.6%). However, only 2 trials demonstrated full alignment between behavioral theory, theory-derived constructs, operationalization through BCTs, and construct-level measurement [45,47]. Five trials showed partial alignment, in which theory-informed intervention design constructs were not explicitly operationalized through BCTs or empirically tested [34,37,43,51,52], whereas the remaining 5 did not integrate behavioral theory [30,31,33,39,41].

Adherence was defined differently across trials, reflecting engagement with specific intervention components such as device wear, session attendance, digital platform use, or incentive participation. For Fitbit, adherence ranged from 65% to 84% in the intervention groups and 48% to 77% in the control groups [30,39]. For pedometers, adherence was lower (intervention: 53% compared with control: 40%) [32]. Accelerometer usage ranged from 53% to 68% in both groups [34,42,45], with 1 study reporting a higher adherence rate (intervention: 92% compared with control: 88%) [43]. Higher adherence was likely driven by trained peer-led delivery within residential settings, multilevel intervention design (individual goal setting and self-monitoring, peer-led group activities, and community walkability enhancements), and low-burden, achievable activity goals [43]. The embodied conversational agent, defined as “more or less autonomous and intelligent software entities with an embodiment used to communicate with the user” [53], was completed 36 times in 2 mo [32], and the mobile application diary had a 39% completion rate [38]. Web, pedal, and treadmill adherence rates were 71%, 38% [33], and 66% [48], respectively. Mean session attendance was 5.1 of 6 sessions [38], and treatment adherence was reported as 100% in 1 trial [52]. In 1 food benefit intervention, 94% of participants complied with study debit card procedures, as assessed through food purchase receipts and transaction records [40]. The remaining 11 studies did not report the adherence rate [31,[35], [36], [37],41,44,46,47,[49], [50], [51]]. Regarding retention, except for 1 study with a low retention rate (intervention: 45% compared with control: 34%) [47], the retention rate was generally high, with most studies reporting >70% in both intervention and control groups. All included studies reported various sources of research funding (Supplemental Table 9).

Behavioral change techniques

Across the 23 trials, 23 separate BCTs were found, appearing in 127 different instances. The BCTs that were used most often were “goal setting (behavior) (1.1)” (n = 23), “instruction on how to perform a behavior (4.1)” (n = 20), “social support (unspecified) (3.1)” (n = 19), and “feedback on behavior (2.2)” (n = 16). When grouped into the 16 BCT classifications, 12 categories were observed (Figure 2). A detailed description of the coded BCTs for every intervention is presented in Supplemental Tables 7 and 8.

Face-to-face interventions

Two trials were classified as face-to-face interventions [37,46] and were focused on activity behaviors through increasing PA, with 1 also targeting dietary changes. One program combined aerobic exercise sessions with dietary education and behavioral strategies such as goal setting and self-monitoring, with pedometers used as outcome measures of PA [37]. The other program consisted of supervised group exercise sessions at a Young Men's Christian Association (aerobic, strength, balance, and flexibility) supplemented with prescribed home-based activity, with accelerometers used to measure PA [46]. Both interventions were in-person, with 1 including additional home-based support [46], and lasted 3‒4 mo (Supplemental Tables 6 and 7).

Face-to-face intervention also indicated the lowest frequency of BCT application (mean: 5 ± 1), with “goal setting (1.1),” “feedback on behavior (2.2),” “social support (3.1),” and “instruction on how to perform a behavior (4.1)” comprising the most (Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 8).

e-interventions

Seven trials were classified as e-interventions [32,36,39,44,47,49,51], where technology-based methods were used to improve PA and reduce SED. Pedometers were used as outcome measures of PA and as intervention tools in combination with embodied conversational agents [32], where participants interacted with a virtual coach via tablets or kiosks to support walking goals. Pedometers were used as PA outcome measures and were also combined with tailored educational materials [51] and daily activity diaries for self-monitoring [49]. Fitbit devices were used as intervention tools in 3 trials: the Fitbit Zip (triaxial accelerometer) with a companion mobile application, MapTrek gamification software (developed by an interdisciplinary research team at the University of Iowa, Iowa City, IA, USA) layered onto Fitbit devices to create step challenges [39], and daily e-mail prompts were used for implementation intentions with Fitbit monitoring [47], with Fitbit devices used to assess PA and/or SED in all 3. One trial tested a smartphone social PA application that relied on the phone’s built-in accelerometer both as the intervention and to capture PA and SED [44]. Trials delivered interventions through individually delivered virtual platforms, supplemented in 2 trials with optional group-based virtual teams [44,47]. The programs lasted between 1 mo and 12 mo (Supplemental Tables 6 and 7).

The mean number of BCTs used in each intervention was 5 ± 2 in trials that used e-interventions. “Self-monitoring of behavior (2.3)” was presented in all 7 studies, indicating the importance of self-monitoring in digital platforms. Moreover, the most frequently identified BCTs were “goal setting (behavior) (1.1),” “feedback on behavior (2.2),” “social support (unspecified) (3.1),” and “instruction on how to perform a behavior (4.1)” (Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 8).

Environmental interventions

Two trials were classified as environmental interventions that improved diet, including the HEI score, servings/d intake of solid fruits, SSBs, sweet baked goods [40], and candies, as well as activity behaviors, including SED [35]. One workplace-based study provided sit–stand desks as the intervention tool to replace 50% of sitting time during workdays, with accelerometers used as outcome measures of SED [35]. The program was delivered in-person, individually, and supported with weekly reminder e-mails over 3 mo [35]. The second study was community-based and embedded in a food benefit program (SNAP) and tested 30% financial incentives for fruit and vegetable purchases alongside restrictions on SSB, candies, and sweet baked goods, and dietary outcomes were measured using 24-h dietary recalls [40]. The delivery and format were mixed (in-person orientation meeting and remote follow-up) and individual-based through monthly debit card reloads and weekly submission of food purchase receipts, supplemented with automated text/e-mail notifications [40] over 6 months of intervention (Supplemental Tables 6 and 7).

The mean number of BCTs used in each intervention was 5.5 ± 1.5 in trials that used environmental interventions. “Goal setting (behavior) (1.1),” “feedback on behavior (2.2),” “social support (unspecified) (3.1),” and “instruction on how to perform a behavior (4.1)” were presented in both studies (Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 8).

Combined modes of intervention

Thirteen trials were classified as combined interventions, where technology was integrated with counseling, home-based support, or environmental strategies to improve PA, SED, and diet [30,31,33,34,38,[41], [42], [43],45,48,[50], [51], [52]] (Supplemental Tables 6 and 7). Pedometer-based approaches included tailored print education with telephone counseling [51], culturally tailored Spanish-language programs delivered by mail, text, and phone support [45], and adaptations of the DPP using step goals [38]. Family-focused models featured culturally tailored manuals delivered through home visits with follow-up calls [52], multilevel retirement community interventions combining goal setting, group walks, and counseling [43], and a peer-led program integrating group walks, educational sessions, and pedometer use [34]. Technology-supported strategies included smartphone self-monitoring applications for 2 sequences of diet and PA change [50], Fitbit trackers with or without personalized coaching [30], and internet-based PA promotion with tailored reports and follow-up calls [41].

Workplace programs used treadmill desks with remote support [48], sit–stand desks with behavioral coaching and text prompts [31], and portable pedal machines with pedometer self-monitoring supported by online platforms [33]. A shelter-based intervention for homeless adults combined newsletters, fruit/vegetable snacks, and pedometers [42]. Delivery ranged across in-person [34], virtual [30,33,41,42,45,50,51], and hybrid [31,38,43,48,52] models, and formats ranged across individual-based [30,33,38,41,42,45,48,[50], [51], [52]], group-based [31,34], and mixed [43]. The programs lasted between 1 mo and 12 mo. All included trials assessed PA and SED outcomes using accelerometers [30,31,33,34,38,[41], [42], [43],45,48,50] or pedometers [51] and assessed diet using the Automated Self-Administered 24-h dietary recalls [52].

Combined models of interventions used the highest frequency of BCT application (6 ± 3) and also revealed the highest heterogeneity in content, involving 12 BCT categories throughout the trials. “Goal setting (behavior) (1.1)” was presented in all 13 studies, indicating the importance of goal setting in interventions that used multiple types of behavioral interventions. Moreover, the most frequently identified BCTs were “feedback on behavior (2.2),” “social support (unspecified) (3.1),” and “instruction on how to perform a behavior (4.1)” (Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 8).

Adverse events

Overall, 7 trials reported adverse events [31,32,34,38,43,44,46], whereas the remaining trials did not report data on them. Among the trials that reported adverse events, 4 reported no intervention-related adverse events [38,43,44,46]. In 1 trial, 289 adverse events were reported, of which 10 were mild-to-moderate in severity and considered likely or probably related to study participation (n = 8 in the control group and n = 2 in the intervention group) [32]. The study did not provide any additional information about the nature of these events, and the remaining adverse events were considered unrelated to study participation [32]. Additionally, the trial by Crist et al. [34] indicated a total of 387 adverse events in the intervention and 183 in the control groups, largely due to differences in data collection methods. Most events were minor (e.g., cramping, joint pain, respiratory issues), whereas ∼1-quarter of the recorded events were classified as serious (26% and 28% in the intervention and control groups, respectively) (e.g., hospitalizations, falls, injuries). Importantly, no unexpected events were deemed related to study participation. Moreover, another study [31] reported adverse events by 23.5% of intervention and 17.8% of control participants, mostly unrelated to the study. Serious events were rare (4 per group) and none were intervention-related. A small number were linked to study assessments (e.g., bruising, skin irritation), whereas 5 events were possibly related to the intervention, mainly musculoskeletal issues (Supplemental Table 9).

Discussion

The aim of this scoping review was to describe the components of efficacious behavioral interventions targeting diet and activity behaviors in adults and to identify the most common BCTs used in these interventions. In addition, this review examined how interventions were delivered across different modalities and target populations. This review identified 23 RCTs, all conducted in the United States, with ∼70% published in the last decade. Most efficacious intervention trials used an individual format and were tested among educated non-Hispanic White adults aged 31 to 50 y, with limited inclusion of young and older adults and underrepresented racial groups. Diverse behavioral intervention modes were utilized across the entered RCTs, which were grouped into 4 categories: face-to-face (n = 2), e-intervention (n = 7), environmental (n = 2), and combined modes of intervention (n = 13). The frequency and types of BCTs utilized differed by intervention category, with combined modes of intervention using the highest number and type of BCTs, whereas face-to-face used the fewest. Nevertheless, only 23 of 93 BCTs were utilized, with “goal setting,” “instruction on how to perform a behavior,” “social support,” and “feedback on behavior” being the most frequently applied.

Delivery modes

Face-to-face interventions provided structured opportunities for guided practice, interpersonal support, and motivational enhancement, particularly through group-based activities [54]. In our review, only 2 trials were classified as face-to-face interventions, and they used the fewest BCTs. These trials incorporated structured exercise sessions with/without dietary recommendations supported by behavioral strategies such as goal setting and feedback, techniques known to strengthen self-regulation and motivation [55]. A previous review of RCTs indicated that face-to-face interventions had moderate positive effects on self-reported PA, with ≤12 mo follow-up [54]. However, these effects were non-significant when including only high-quality trials [54]. This mentioned review further reported that including some group work in the intervention appeared to improve effectiveness; however, the effectiveness of purely group-based face-to-face interventions could not be determined because most studies used multiple delivery methods, making it difficult to identify which components were most influential. The authors also noted that the complexity of PA interventions has increased over time, suggesting that the interaction among multiple components may be critical in facilitating behavior change and warrants further investigation [54].

Although effective, this study shows that face-to-face interventions are less popular, possibly because they are resource-intensive and affected by participant transportation barriers. For example, the national DPP typically includes 16 1-h weekly sessions in the first 6 mo, followed by ≥6-mo sessions delivered by trained lifestyle coaches [56]. Implementation studies further highlighted substantial scalability barriers of these programs, including low income and transportation access, curriculum and scheduling misalignment, limited referrals and program visibility, insufficient staffing and coach burnout, poor group dynamics, and inadequate administrative or financial support, as well as maintaining engagement after supervised phases [57].

e-interventions, including smartphone applications, wearable devices (such as Fitbit and pedometers), web-based platforms, and automated e-mail or text messaging, have emerged as an increasingly common delivery mode of effective behavior change interventions. Digital health technologies allow for the efficient dissemination of health programs and interventions to individuals in rural and remote regions, those with limited mobility, and those without access to transportation [53]. They have been successfully utilized for managing and preventing a range of health conditions, including diabetes [58], obesity [59], sleep problems [60], and depression and anxiety [61]. These approaches utilized technology to deliver self-monitoring, goal setting, and feedback; BCTs linked to sustained improvements in dietary and activity behaviors [62]. Evidence from recent reviews underscores that digital behavioral interventions can effectively promote healthy lifestyle behaviors but remain limited by issues of engagement, equity, and implementation. Chatterjee et al. [63] (2021) demonstrated that digital tools enhance motivation and self-regulation, and motivation leads to improvements in diet, PA, and weight management through personalization and user-centered designs and integration of behavioral theories like self-determination and self-regulation. However, low adherence, digital literacy gaps, and inconsistent evaluation methods constrain long-term effectiveness [63]. Moreover, a review of 19 RCTs reported that digital interventions significantly increased PA among high- SES participants but not among low-SES groups, suggesting that such approaches may inadvertently reinforce health inequities if accessibility and contextual factors are not addressed [64]. Thus, these reviews highlighted that although digital tools hold strong potential for scalable and cost-effective health promotion, future interventions must prioritize inclusivity, digital literacy, and sustained engagement to ensure equitable impact across diverse populations.

Environmental interventions were less common but yielded meaningful improvements in dietary and SED outcomes by altering the surrounding context of behavior. Interventions that provided fruit- and vegetable purchase incentives or reduced access to SSBs significantly improved dietary quality [10], whereas workplace-based programs employing sit–stand desks reduced SED [35]. Moreover, Shaw et al. [65] (2019) supported the potential of environmental strategies to improve dietary behaviors by restructuring food environments, increasing the availability of healthy options, and incorporating labeling or promotional cues to influence choices. These interventions were shown to reduce total energy and fat intake while increasing fruit and vegetable intake, with participants reporting improved meal appeal and satisfaction.

Combined modes of intervention were the most common type of intervention and incorporated the highest diversity and frequency of BCTs. These multilevel approaches typically integrated self-monitoring digital tools [e.g., wearable activity monitors (such as pedometers), smartphone applications, or web] with counseling (either remote or in-person), group sessions (either remote or in-person), or environmental supports. This aligned with a previous review that showed interventions incorporating wearable activity monitors, such as pedometers, along with additional elements like regular consultations with a healthcare professional (either in-person or virtually), demonstrated the strongest links to improvements in PA [66]. Another review also indicated that blended interventions (including face-to-face sessions with eHealth components such as applications, short message service, e-mail, or web) were more effective in improving diet quality and reducing energy intake than stand-alone, face-to-face, or waitlist control programs [67]. The rationale behind combined interventions lies in their ability to provide continuous engagement and personalized feedback across multiple delivery modes [66,68]. Moreover, because most included RCTs targeted individuals with overweight or obesity, combined modes of interventions may also be effective for individuals requiring structured dietary support for weight management. Given the increasing prevalence of noncommunicable diseases such as obesity and diabetes, and the limited capacity of traditional face-to-face programs to meet patient needs [64], integrating digital and in-person strategies may improve participants’ adherence and reduce barriers to access, leading to improved targeted behaviors.

Target populations

Most successful interventions used an individual format and primarily targeted White adults aged 31 to 50 y having some college education, with limited inclusion of young and older adults and racially diverse groups. This reflects a gap in the current evidence base, suggesting either insufficient intervention efforts targeting non-White populations or a need for more efficacious strategies to improve diet and PA behaviors in these communities. The predominance of individually delivered interventions may also limit reach among populations facing structural barriers to engagement. This level of interaction and support is often infeasible for the general population due to the lack of access to nutrition and behavioral services, particularly in rural, remote, and disadvantaged areas [61]. To achieve health equity, health promotion research should more systematically apply implementation frameworks such as reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, maintenance (RE-AIM) in conjunction with behavior change theories and techniques. These frameworks can help ensure real-world feasibility and that the positive health effects of interventions are accessible to all individuals, not only those with the resources and opportunities to participate in research [[69], [70], [71]].

Behavior change techniques

Across the 23 included trials, 23 distinct BCTs were identified (127 instances). The most frequently applied (>50%) were goal setting and planning, feedback and monitoring, social support, and instruction on how to perform a behavior, core self-regulatory and motivational strategies known to enhance self-efficacy and maintenance [55,72]. As emphasized by Arigo et al. [73] (2022), interventions are most effective in behavioral change when these BCTs are activated through concrete delivery procedures rather than merely listed. For instance, interventions should specifically indicate how to set goals or usefully self-monitor. Face-to-face interventions [37,46] activated goal setting, instruction on how to perform a behavior, and social support through group education, guided skill-building, and instructor feedback, directly engaging participants in behavioral practice and problem-solving. e-interventions [32,36,39,44,47,49,51] activated self-monitoring, feedback, and goal setting using applications, pedometers, wearables, or online dashboards that provided real-time progress tracking, tailored reminders, or virtual feedback. For instance, King et al. [44] (2016) employed smartphone applications that delivered adaptive activity feedback and motivational prompts, concretely operationalizing feedback and monitoring.

The 2 environmental interventions [35,40] included instruction on how to perform a behavior, feedback, and reward through policy and environmental cues, such as tailored health messages, financial incentives, and structured reinforcement to encourage fruit and vegetable purchases and reduce SED. Combined modes of interventions, the largest group [30,31,33,34,38,[41], [42], [43],45,48,[50], [51], [52]], most often included multiple BCTs, combining digital self-monitoring and feedback with social support, instruction on how to perform the behavior, or environmental restructuring. For example, Spring et al. [50] (2018) integrated a smartphone application with connected coaching and small weekly incentives, thereby activating goal setting, feedback, and reward through continuous interaction between participants and coaches. Across modes, the most frequently used BCTs among interventions were self-regulatory (goal setting, feedback), social (support, instruction), and environmental (restructuring, reward). These findings suggest that these BCTs are commonly used because they are versatile and may be successfully incorporated into behavioral interventions regardless of delivery method, or perhaps these BCTs are fundamental to successful behavior change. More research is needed on the effectiveness of BCTs. It is unknown whether some are more potent than others.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review makes a distinct contribution to the literature on behavioral interventions for T2D prevention. Unlike prior reviews that synthesize interventions irrespective of outcome measurement or effectiveness, this review restricted inclusion to efficacious behavioral interventions that demonstrated statistically significant improvements in diet and/or activity outcomes, which are key diabetes prevention behaviors. In addition, by integrating intervention delivery modes, BCTs, and target population characteristics, this review offers implementation-relevant insights into how efficacious behavioral interventions are designed and which populations they currently reach within United States settings.

This scoping review also had several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. As a scoping review, this study aimed to describe intervention components rather than to evaluate intervention effectiveness. The definition we chose for identifying efficacious or evidence-based interventions is not a standardized set of criteria. A key limitation of this review is the variability in adherence to wearable devices across included studies, which may compromise the completeness and representativeness of objectively measured activity outcomes. Because activity estimates were based only on participants with sufficient device data, results may be subject to selection bias and may not reflect intervention effects among less adherent individuals. In addition, vigorous PA was infrequently reported across trials, and the 3 studies that assessed vigorous PA did not demonstrate statistically significant intervention effects, limiting conclusions regarding intervention impact on higher-intensity activity [33,38,46].

Additionally, although the BCTTv1 taxonomy provided a structured framework for coding behavioral components, the process remains partly subjective. Variability in the completeness and precision of intervention reporting often constrained accurate identification of applied BCTs. As demonstrated in prior studies [74,75], intervention descriptions in primary publications often lack sufficient detail to accurately identify all BCTs applied or to determine the intensity, fidelity, and activation of their delivery. Consequently, some techniques may have been underrepresented or misclassified. To enhance methodological transparency, future research should publish detailed intervention manuals or protocols as appendices or in open repositories and adopt standardized BCT terminology when describing behavioral components within interventions [76]. These practices would improve comparability across studies and facilitate replication.

Moreover, included trials showed inconsistent operationalization and empirical testing of behavioral theory. Although most studies referenced a behavioral framework, only a small subset explicitly translated theory-derived constructs into intervention content through specific BCTs and assessed these constructs alongside behavioral outcomes. Finally, the generalizability of these findings may be limited, as the included studies predominantly involved females, non-Hispanic White participants, and adults aged 31 y and older from North America with at least some college education, and the restriction to English-language, peer-reviewed articles conducted in United States populations may further constrain applicability to males, younger or older age groups, and other sociocultural or health system contexts.

Future directions

The findings of this review indicate that multicomponent interventions incorporating BCTs, such as goal setting, instruction, social support, and feedback, may be effective for improving diet and activity. For practice, these components provide useful guidance for community health providers seeking to implement evidence-informed DPPs. Rather than re-evaluating single BCTs in isolation, future research should examine how these techniques work together, determine the most effective combinations and levels of support, and assess whether their effects are sustained over time and across different delivery modes. Studies should also clearly specify how theoretical constructs are translated into BCTs and include construct-level measures to better understand intervention mechanisms. In addition, future research should use validated dietary assessments and objective activity measures to ensure comparability across studies, assess strategies to improve adherence to wearable devices in activity interventions, and identify best-practice guidance for BCT taxonomy use and reporting [77]. Within the RCTs included in this review, intervention effects were less consistently observed among non-Hispanic White populations. This highlights an important gap in the current evidence base and suggests a need to examine whether existing intervention approaches are equally effective across diverse racial/ethnic groups or whether culturally responsive strategies are required. Trials should prospectively report key implementation outcomes to support translation, reach, and adoption (per RE-AIM, i.e., the representativeness and proportion of individuals engaged and settings/providers initiating the program), implementation cost, and scalability, so researchers can predict whether effective interventions are deliverable at scale in routine practice [70].

Conclusions

This review revealed important insights into the wide range of strategies and populations of effective interventions targeting diet and activity behaviors conducted among adults in the United States. The recognized intervention groups, including face-to-face, e-intervention, environmental, and combined interventions, varied in the frequency and diversity of BCTs applied. Efficacious behavioral interventions that targeted diet and/or activity for T2D prevention most often included females, non-Hispanic White, middle-aged adults, with at least some college education, and commonly used digital or combined delivery approaches in an individual-based format. The most common BCTs were goal setting, instruction on how to perform a behavior, social support, and feedback on behavior. Future research is needed to determine the most efficacious BCTs in behavioral interventions for more diverse populations in terms of education, race, ethnicity, sex, and age.
