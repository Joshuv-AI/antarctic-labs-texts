Detection of Genetic Polymorphism in Growth Hormone Receptor Gene and Its Influence on Growth Traits in Kari and Balkhi Sheep
Authors: Hanan Al‐Khalaifah, Muniza Kiran, Sher Hayat Khan, Ihtesham Ulhaq, Muhammad Ibrahim, Sohail Ahmad, Haris Bilal, Zeng Shenming, Shabana Naz, Rifat Ullah Khan, Ibrahim A Alhidary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVariations in the growth hormone receptor (GHR) gene influence growth traits, which are important for economic selection in sheep breeding. This study aimed to investigate GHR gene sequence variations and their association with growth traits in two sheep breeds (Kari and Balkhi). A total of 150 animals per breed were included, and blood samples were collected. DNA was extracted using the NucleoSpin Blood Genomic DNA Purification Kit, and the gene was amplified through PCR with pre‐designed primers. PCR products were sequenced using next‐generation sequencing (NGS). Sequence analysis identified 620 variants, with 612 located in the intronic region. Five variants were found in the upstream region, and two synonymous variants were detected, along with one in the 3′ UTR region. Correlation analysis showed that four SNPs (33,665,691 C>T, 33,668,886 T>A, 33,665,855 C>T and 33,667,058 C>T) in high‐growth rate groups were positively correlated with weight gain, while four SNPs (33,662,328 G>A, 33,729,972 C>T, 33,753,532 A>G and 33,760,254 G>T) in low‐growth rate groups were negatively correlated. These SNPs could serve as markers for marker‐assisted selection to improve growth traits in local sheep breeds.Keywords: Balkhi sheep, growth hormone receptor, Kari sheep, NGS, SNP

Introduction

The growth traits of livestock, such as sheep, play a vital role in improving livestock production. Farmer or breeder income is greatly affected by the measurements and weights of their animals (Al‐Atiyat et al. 2012; 2014). These economic traits are affected by genetic polymorphism, environmental conditions and nutrition (Ajafar et al. 2022; Al‐Thuwaini and Al‐Hadi 2022; Dongjing et al. 2024). Among various growth‐associated genes, the growth hormone receptor (GHR) gene plays a pivotal role in mediating the physiological actions of growth hormone (GH), which include stimulating somatic growth, protein synthesis and lipid metabolism (Itana and Duguma 2021; Tavares et al. 2023; Granata et al. 2025). Upon binding to GH, the GHR activates the JAK‐STAT signalling pathway, promoting transcription of growth‐related genes (Dehkhoda et al. 2018). Therefore, variations in the GHR gene can significantly alter receptor function and consequently affect phenotypic traits related to growth (Hwa et al. 2021).

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the GHR gene have been reported to influence growth traits such as body weight, daily weight gain and carcass characteristics across various livestock species, including cattle, pigs, goats and sheep (Yan et al. 2023; Ding et al. 2022; Saleh et al. 2024). In sheep, where a fast growth rate is a desirable selection criterion for breeding, identifying genetic markers linked to superior growth traits is essential for implementing marker‐assisted selection (MAS) strategies (Z). Despite the recognized potential of GHR polymorphisms in influencing growth traits, there is limited data on sequence variation and its phenotypic associations in indigenous sheep breeds of Pakistan.

Indigenous sheep breeds play a vital role in the livelihood of smallholder farmers by contributing to meat, wool and income generation under low‐input production systems. Among them, Kari and Balkhi sheep are widely maintained in the Khyber Pakhtunkhwa region of Pakistan, where they are valued for their adaptability to local environmental conditions (Ullah et al. 2025). Despite their economic importance, these breeds exhibit noticeable differences in growth performance, yet the genetic mechanisms underlying such variation remain insufficiently understood (Ali et al. 2025).

The GHR gene is a key regulator of growth and metabolism, and polymorphisms within this gene have been associated with growth traits in several livestock species (Wang et al. 2025). Importantly, SNPs occurring in regulatory and non‐coding regions such as introns, upstream elements, and untranslated regions (UTRs) can alter gene expression or splicing patterns, thereby contributing to phenotypic variation (Li et al. 2015). However, the role of GHR gene variation in shaping the growth performance of indigenous Pakistani sheep breeds has not yet been investigated.

To address this knowledge gap, the present study investigated sequence variation within the GHR gene of Kari and Balkhi sheep using next‐generation sequencing (NGS). Furthermore, the association between identified genetic variants and monthly weight gain was evaluated, with the goal of identifying molecular markers that may be used in MAS programs aimed at improving growth performance in local sheep populations.

Materials and Methods

Animal Selection

The study was conducted at the Livestock Farm of the University of Agriculture, Peshawar, during 2021. Two sheep breeds, Kari and Balkhi, maintained at the Sheep Resource Center of the University, were selected for the experiment. A total of 300 animals, comprising 150 individuals from each breed, were included in the study. The animals were selected based on availability in the farm records, ensuring representation across sex and age groups to avoid selection bias.

Growth performance data were collected from farm records and supplemented by direct observations. The parameters recorded included initial birth weight, three‐month body weight and average monthly weight gain for each animal. Birth weights were measured within 24 h of lambing using a digital weighing scale, while subsequent body weights were recorded monthly using the same scale (calibrated before each measurement using standard test weights to ensure accuracy and minimize instrument drift).

Animals were maintained under the farm's standard management and feeding conditions. All animals grazed collectively on natural pasture dominated by berseem (Trifolium alexandrinum) and lucerne (Medicago sativa) and received supplementary concentrate feeding, composed of maize (40%), wheat bran (25%), cottonseed cake (20%), canola meal (10%) and a premix of vitamins and minerals (5%) on a dry matter basis. Feeding was not carried out individually; therefore, dietary figures represent averages per breed rather than per animal. Clean drinking water was available ad libitum. The environmental conditions during the trial were recorded; the average ambient temperature ranged between 18°C–34°C with relative humidity of 40%–65%, under a natural daylight cycle (approximately 12–13 h). This systematic data collection allowed evaluation of the comparative growth performance of Kari and Balkhi sheep breeds under similar environmental and management conditions.

DNA Extraction

DNA was extracted from blood samples with the help of the NucleoSpin Blood genomic DNA purification kit (MACHEREY‐NAGEL, catalogue No. 740951.250) using the manufacturer's protocol. After incubation at room temperature and centrifugation, DNA was collected in Eppendorf tubes and stored at −20°C. For purity and concentration check of DNA, 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and Nanodrop spectrophotometry were used. Each Nanodrop reading was taken in triplicate, and samples with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered acceptable for downstream analysis.

PCR Amplification

Primers for the GHR gene were designed to cover the whole gene (see Supporting Information) and blasted using NCBI BLAST, Primal Scheme and the UCSC Genome Browser for specificity confirmation. For the PCR reaction, high‐fidelity Phusion polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used under the manufacturer's recommended conditions. Gel electrophoresis was performed using a 1.5% agarose gel in 1× TBE buffer to visualize PCR products. Bands were visualized using a UV transilluminator (Table S1; Figures 1S and 2S). Positive controls (known DNA template) and negative controls (no template) were included in all reactions to validate amplification specificity.

Next Generation Sequencing

The PCR products were pooled and sent for sequencing. They were sequenced using NGS on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Illumina Inc., USA) with paired‐end reads (2 × 150 bp), following standard library preparation protocols. Data quality was checked using FastQC, and sequences with Phred quality scores below 30 were filtered prior to downstream analysis.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS software was used for the analysis of means and standard errors. One‐way ANOVA was also performed using SPSS software for the analysis of differences among selected sheep breeds and low and high growth rate groups within breeds. Data obtained from Next‐Generation‐Sequencing were analysed to assess sequence variation in selected sheep breeds and groups. Bioinformatics tools and software, Popgene, were used to calculate allelic frequencies. To assess genetic diversity and to construct a Dendrogram, MEGA X software was used. GHR gene polymorphism and variations in weight gain per month were correlated using Principal Component Analysis through SPSS software for the identification of mutations related to high or low growth rates in sheep breeds.

Results

Variation in Body Weight of Kari and Balkhi Sheep

The mean values for birth weight, three‐month body weight and average monthly weight gain were compared between the two sheep breeds, Kari and Balkhi. The Balkhi breed exhibited a higher mean birth weight (2.74 ± 0.072 kg) than the Kari breed (2.24 ± 0.10 kg). A significant difference was also observed in three‐month body weight, with Balkhi sheep (16.48 ± 0.25 kg) weighing significantly more than Kari sheep (12.05 ± 0.30 kg). Over a six‐month period, the average monthly weight gain was notably higher in the Balkhi breed (10.78 ± 0.11 kg) compared to the Kari breed (3.27 ± 0.09 kg). Overall, the Balkhi breed demonstrated superior growth performance in all measured parameters, including birth weight, three‐month weight and average monthly weight gain (Figure 1; Figures 3S and 4S).

Intronic Variants

A total of 612 variants were identified in the intronic region of the GHR gene across both sheep breeds. In the Balkhi breed, 508 variants were detected in the low growth rate group, while 480 were found in the high growth rate group. In the Kari breed, 471 variants were observed in the low growth rate group, compared to 492 in the high growth rate group. Notably, the majority of mutations in the Balkhi sheep were heterozygous, whereas in the Kari breed, most mutations were homozygous (Figure 2).

Upstream Variants

Out of the total 620 identified variants, five were located in the upstream region of the GHR gene. Among these, one variant was detected in the low growth rate Balkhi group, while two were present in the high growth rate Balkhi group. In the Kari breed, three upstream variants were observed in the low growth rate group, and one was found in the high growth rate group. Unique Variants

Several unique variants were identified across the four experimental groups, with a total of 34 unique variants observed in both sheep breeds. The Balkhi breed exhibited the highest number of unique variants, with 15 identified in the low growth rate group—14 of which were intronic and one a synonymous variant. In the high growth rate Balkhi group, 13 unique variants were recorded, including one in the upstream region and one in the 3′ UTR, while the remaining 11 were located in the intronic region. In the Kari breed, the high growth rate group showed four unique variants, all confined to the intronic region. The lowest number of unique variants was found in the low growth rate Kari group, with only two variants detected, both in the upstream region (Table 3).

Discussion

The comparative evaluation of Kari and Balkhi sheep revealed significant breed differences in growth performance, largely influenced by genetic potential and adaptation. Kari sheep exhibited superior body weight gain, reflecting a higher capacity for muscle accretion under semi‐arid conditions. This aligns with earlier reports that (indigenous breeds often show improved growth efficiency due to evolutionary adaptation to local feed resources Mathew and Mathew 2023). Recent studies have similarly emphasized that breed‐specific variations in body weight are strongly linked to metabolic efficiency and nutrient utilization (Chacko Kaitholil et al. 2024 ).

The role of the GHR gene further supports this interpretation. The GHR gene encodes a receptor essential for GH binding and subsequent activation of the insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1) pathway, which regulates protein synthesis, tissue accretion and overall growth (Al‐Samerria and Radovick 2021). Polymorphisms detected in this study may alter receptor binding affinity or downstream signalling, leading to differential growth performance between breeds. Recent findings confirm that GHR variants are significantly associated with body weight and carcass traits in sheep ( Wu et al. 2020 ) and goats ( Selionova et al. 2022 ), highlighting its potential as a candidate marker for MAS.

Mechanistically, Kari sheep may possess GHR allelic variants that enhance GH–IGF‐1 axis activity, promoting higher protein accretion and lean tissue growth. In contrast, Balkhi sheep, as a fat‐tailed breed, may partition nutrients preferentially toward adipose deposition rather than muscle, an adaptation for energy storage in resource‐fluctuating environments. This is consistent with evidence that fat‐tailed breeds show higher lipogenic gene expression and slower lean growth rates (Farhadi et al. 2021).

These genetic and physiological adaptations suggest that Kari sheep are better suited for rapid growth under semi‐arid grazing systems, while Balkhi sheep are adapted for survival under nutritional stress by storing energy reserves. This duality is consistent with recent research on breed‐environment interactions, which showed that adaptive alleles in local sheep populations optimize survival strategies depending on climatic and nutritional pressures (Nel et al. 2023).

A strength of this study is that uniform feeding and management were applied, minimizing environmental confounders and allowing clearer attribution of differences to genetic background. However, limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study relied solely on growth performance and GHR gene polymorphism analysis without functional validation of gene expression or protein activity. Second, the sample size was moderate, which may limit the detection of rare allelic variants. Finally, environmental adaptability was not directly measured, meaning that conclusions regarding resilience remain inferential. Addressing these limitations in future studies by incorporating transcriptomic, proteomic and larger population‐scale genomic analyses would strengthen the findings.

Overall, the results demonstrate that Kari sheep possess superior growth potential under semi‐arid conditions, partly explained by favourable GHR variants, whereas Balkhi sheep reflect fat‐tail adaptive strategies. Future breeding programs could integrate GHR polymorphisms into MAS to accelerate genetic gains for growth while maintaining resilience to local environments.

Conclusion

A total of 620 genetic variants were identified in the GHR gene across Kari and Balkhi sheep breeds, with the majority located in the intronic region. The low growth rate Balkhi group showed the highest number of variants (511), while the low growth rate Kari group had the fewest (475). Several SNPs were significantly associated with monthly weight gain, including four (33665691, 33668886, 33665855, 33667058) that were positively correlated and four (33662328, 33729972, 33753532, 33760254) that were negatively correlated. These findings highlight the potential of GHR gene variants as markers for genetic improvement of growth traits in local sheep breeds.
