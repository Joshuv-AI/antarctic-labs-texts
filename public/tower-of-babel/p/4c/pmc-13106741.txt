Cultivation system and plant health influence root-associated bacterial community structure and interaction networks in strawberry
Authors: Mehwish Roy, Dongsoo Han, Dongmin Lee, Byeonghyeok Kang, Kihyuck Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStrawberry is cultivated in both soil-based field and substrate-based soilless hydroponic systems, yet how cultivation context shapes root-associated bacterial communities and their interaction architecture remains unclear. We compared root-associated bacterial communities from field root-associated soil and hydroponic root-adhering substrate under asymptomatic and symptomatic conditions using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Cultivation system was the primary driver of community structure, clearly separating field and hydroponic samples. Field communities were enriched in Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, such as Bacillaceae and Nocardioidaceae, whereas hydroponic communities showed higher relative abundances of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Planctomycetes, and Verrucomicrobia, including Chitinophagaceae and Sphingomonadaceae. Differential abundance and Random Forest analyses revealed consistent enrichment of Bacillus-associated ASVs in field samples, whereas asymptomatic and symptomatic communities showed greater compositional differentiation in hydroponic than in field samples.. Co-occurrence network analysis further demonstrated that hydroponic communities contained more taxa and interactions but exhibited lower density and clustering compared to field communities, indicating reduced structural cohesion. Introduction

Strawberry (Fragaria × ananassa Duch.) is a globally significant fruit crop, cultivated on approximately 372,000 hectares and yielding over eight million tons annually1. Despite its economic importance, strawberry production is threatened by diverse plant pathogens, particularly soil-borne bacterial and fungal diseases, which contribute to substantial crop losses and reduced fruit quality2–4. Key soil-borne pathogens include Pantoea ananatis, the causal agent of bacterial leaf blight, Macrophomina phaseolina causing charcoal rot, and Meloidogyne hapla, responsible for root-knot nematode diseases4,5. Fungal pathogens such as Botrytis cinerea, Colletotrichum sp., and Verticillium dahliae further exacerbate production challenges, leading to significant economic losses for strawberry growers6,7.

Traditional management strategies for these diseases, including soil fumigation, resistant cultivars, and cultural practices like crop rotation and soil solarization, often yield inconsistent results8,9. Chemical fumigants may harm the environment, resistant cultivars can lose effectiveness over time, and intensive farming systems may not accommodate cultural practices effectively10. These limitations highlight the urgent need for innovative, sustainable disease management strategies that enhance plant health through natural microbiome interactions.

The rhizosphere, a dynamic zone surrounding plant roots, is a hotspot of microbial diversity with profound effects on plant growth, nutrition, and disease suppression11. In strawberries, beneficial bacteria in the rhizosphere, such as Pseudomonas, Bacillus, Sphingomonas, and members of the Chitinophagaceae and Dokdonella genera, contribute to plant health by producing antimicrobial compounds, degrading fungal cell walls, and promoting competitive exclusion of pathogens5–7. For example, Pseudomonas stutzeri and Bacillus subtilis produce bioactive lipopeptides and extracellular enzymes effective against fungal pathogens including Botrytis cinerea, Fusarium oxysporum, and Rhizoctonia solani6,7. Additionally, Sphingomonas and Chitinophagaceae bacteria enhance disease suppression through chitin degradation and microbial competition, contributing to natural biocontrol mechanisms5.

Modern agricultural practices increasingly integrate traditional soil-based cultivation with soilless hydroponic systems. Hydroponics offers advantages such as precise nutrient management, reduced water use, and minimized risks of soil-borne diseases by eliminating soil as a primary pathogen reservoir12. However, the absence of a soil matrix modifies the microbial environment surrounding plant roots, potentially impacting the recruitment of beneficial microorganisms for disease suppression13. Although field systems are generally expected to harbor greater microbial diversity due to their complex soil environment, recent findings suggest that hydroponic systems, particularly those using reused organic substrates, can also support high bacterial and fungal diversity14. This highlights the need to better understand how different cultivation systems shape root-associated microbial communities and their roles in disease suppression.

Although strawberry health is also influenced by fungal pathogens, mycorrhizal fungi, and nematodes, the present study focuses specifically on bacterial communities. In soil systems, bacteria represent one of the most dynamic and rapidly responding components of the rhizosphere microbiome and are frequently implicated in disease suppression, nutrient cycling, and microbial network stability15,16. Fungal and other microbial groups undoubtedly play important roles in plant health; however, their investigation requires distinct methodological approaches and is therefore proposed as a priority for future studies.

In the present study, we distinguish between root-associated soil communities (field-grown plants) and root-associated substrate communities (hydroponic plants), collectively referring to them as root-associated bacterial communities where appropriate. This study investigates bacterial community structure associated with strawberry roots under two cultivation systems: a soil-based field system and a substrate-based soilless hydroponic system. The study was conducted at a single representative site for each cultivation system; while this limits spatial replication, it enables a focused comparison of system-associated microbial dynamics. By comparing plants with and without visible decline symptoms in each system, we aimed to assess how cultivation system and plant health status correspond to shifts in microbial composition and interaction networks. Hereafter, asymptomatic and symptomatic plants from the two cultivation systems are referred to as field healthy (FH), field diseased (FD), hydroponic healthy (HH), and hydroponic diseased (HD), respectively. We hypothesize that (1) the cultivation system (soil-based vs. hydroponic) would be the primary factor shaping root-associated bacterial community structure, and (2) the relationship between bacterial community composition and plant health status would differ between the two systems. Understanding these cultivation-associated microbial patterns may inform microbiome-based strategies for enhancing disease resilience in strawberries grown under different systems.

Results

Experimental design

To assess the effects of cultivation system and plant health on root-associated bacterial communities, strawberry plants were sampled from two contrasting production systems: a soil-based field cultivation and a substrate-based soilless hydroponic cultivation. Based on visual symptom assessment, plants were categorized into four groups: field healthy (FH), field diseased (FD), hydroponic healthy (HH), and hydroponic diseased (HD), with ten biological replicates per group. A total of 40 root-associated samples (root-associated soil in the field system and root-adhering substrate in hydroponics) were subjected to 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Following quality filtering and denoising, 3,013,881 high-quality sequences were retained, resulting in the inference of 2,104 bacterial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) for downstream analyses (Supplementary Table S1).

Taxonomic composition of bacterial communities

At the phylum level, bacterial community composition differed clearly between cultivation systems (Fig. 1a). Field samples (FH, FD) were primarily dominated by Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, whereas hydroponic samples (HH, HD) exhibited higher relative representation of Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Planctomycetes, and Verrucomicrobia. These patterns were consistent under both healthy and diseased conditions, indicating that cultivation system was the primary determinant of broad taxonomic structure. Health-associated shifts were comparatively modest and were more apparent within the hydroponic system, where subtle changes in Actinobacteria and Verrucomicrobia were observed under disease conditions.

Relative abundance of bacterial communities at the phylum (a) family (b) and genus (c) levels in root-associated samples from soil-based field system (root-associated soil) and hydroponic system (root-adhering substrate) . Taxa with relative abundance greater than 0.01 are shown .

At the family level, system-specific signatures were again evident (Fig. 1b). Field communities were characterized by greater representation of Bacillaceae, Nocardioidaceae, and Xanthomonadaceae, consistent with the dominance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria. In contrast, hydroponic communities showed higher relative abundance of families within Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes, including Chitinophagaceae, Sphingomonadaceae, Xanthobacteraceae, and Caulobacteraceae. Disease-associated shifts were more pronounced within hydroponics, where certain families (e.g., Sphingomonadaceae) increased under diseased conditions, whereas field communities remained comparatively stable.

At the genus level, cultivation system–specific patterns were again evident (Fig. 1c). Field communities were characterized by greater representation of genera associated with Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, including Bacillus, Nocardioides, Flavisolibacter, and Gaiella, consistent with the broader phylum- and family-level trends observed in soil-grown plants. In contrast, hydroponic communities showed higher relative representation of genera affiliated with Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes, including Streptomyces, Terrimonas, Sphingomonas, and Ferruginibacter.

Bacterial diversity and community structure

Alpha diversity analyses revealed significant differences in within-sample bacterial diversity between field and hydroponic cultivation systems (Fig. 2). Shannon diversity was significantly higher in hydroponic samples than in field samples under both healthy and diseased conditions (HH vs. FH; HD vs. FD; p < 0.001), with no significant differences observed within each system (HH vs. HD; FH vs. FD) (Fig. 2a). Species richness showed a similar trend, with hydroponic samples exhibiting significantly higher richness than field samples under both health conditions (HH > FH, HD > FD, p < 0.001). Richness did not differ significantly between HH and HD, while FD showed slightly higher richness than FH (FD > FH, p < 0.05) (Fig. 2b). Faith’s phylogenetic diversity also varied across the groups. FD showed higher diversity than FH (p < 0.01), while HH exhibited significantly higher diversity compared to FH (p < 0.001), and HD had higher diversity than FD (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). No significant differences in Pielou’s evenness were observed between cultivation systems or plant health statuses (Fig. 2d).

Statistical analyses, including pairwise and two-way PERMANOVA, were conducted to assess differences in community composition across groups.

Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity revealed clear separation of root-associated bacterial communities according to cultivation system (Fig. 2e). Hydroponic samples (HH, HD) clustered distinctly from field samples (FH, FD). Although FH and FD showed some visual separation, pairwise PERMANOVA indicated a statistically significant but relatively small difference between these groups (R2 = 0.0918, p.adj = 0.024), suggesting that disease status had a limited influence on bacterial community composition under field conditions. In contrast, HH and HD differed more strongly (R2 = 0.1307, p.adj = 0.006), indicating a greater effect of plant health status in the hydroponic system. Two-way PERMANOVA showed that cultivation method explained the largest proportion of variance in bacterial community structure (R2 = 0.4454, F = 32.5526, p = 0.001). Plant health status contributed a smaller but statistically significant portion of the variation (R2 = 0.03114, F = 2.2753, p = 0.042), and the interaction between cultivation system and health was also statistically significant, although with a small effect size (R2 = 0.03071, F = 2.2439, p = 0.045). These results indicate that bacterial community structure was primarily shaped by cultivation method, with a modest contribution from plant health status. Between cultivation systems, pairwise comparisons revealed strong differences under both healthy (FH vs. HH, R2 = 0.4881, p.adj = 0.006) and diseased conditions (FD vs. HD, R2 = 0.4948, p.adj = 0.006) (Supplementary Table S2).

Identification of key bacterial ASVs using Random Forest and DESeq2

To identify bacterial taxa associated with cultivation system and plant health status, we performed integrated Random Forest (RF) and DESeq2 analyses. ASVs were considered robust indicators when they met both statistical significance criteria (adjusted p < 0.01; |log2 fold change|> 2) and exceeded comparison-specific Random Forest importance thresholds (Mean Decrease Gini values above the empirical importance cutoff defined for each model) (Fig. 3).

In the comparison between field healthy (FH) and hydroponic healthy (HH) samples, clear system-specific enrichment patterns were observed. Eight ASVs significantly enriched in FH belonged exclusively to Bacillaceae (genus Bacillus), indicating a strong dominance of Firmicutes in field-grown healthy plants. In contrast, hydroponic healthy (HH) samples were enriched in diverse members of Proteobacteria and Verrucomicrobia, including Devosia (Devosiaceae), uncultured representatives of Pedosphaeraceae, Parasegetibacter (Chitinophagaceae), members of Burkholderiaceae, and Oligoflexus (Oligoflexaceae) (Fig. 3a). In the diseased comparison between cultivation systems (FD vs. HD), a strong taxonomic contrast was observed. Field diseased (FD) samples were predominantly enriched in Firmicutes, particularly multiple ASVs assigned to Bacillus (Bacillaceae), along with Ammoniphilus (Paenibacillaceae), Lysinibacillus (Planococcaceae), and Bhargavaea. In addition, one ASV affiliated with Micromonosporaceae (Actinobacteria) was significantly enriched. This pattern indicates that, under field conditions, disease was associated with the persistence or amplification of a Firmicutes-dominated community structure rather than a major taxonomic shift. In contrast, hydroponic diseased (HD) samples were enriched in a more taxonomically diverse assemblage, including Desmonostoc (Cyanobacteria), uncultured members of Pedosphaeraceae (Verrucomicrobia), Pedobacter (Bacteroidetes), Paenarthrobacter (Actinobacteria), and members of Sphingomonadaceae (Alphaproteobacteria), suggesting a broader restructuring of the root-associated bacterial community under disease in hydroponic systems. (Fig. 3b).

Within the hydroponic system, plant health status was associated with shifts in bacterial community composition. Healthy hydroponic (HH) samples were significantly enriched in multiple ASVs affiliated with Ferruginibacter (Chitinophagaceae), Luteolibacter (Rubritaleaceae), and Mesorhizobium (Rhizobiaceae). In contrast, no ASVs were significantly enriched in hydroponic diseased (HD) samples under the applied significance thresholds (Fig. 3c). In contrast to the hydroponic system, the field-based system exhibited minimal compositional differentiation between healthy and diseased plants. Only a single ASV, affiliated with Bosea (Beijerinckiaceae), was significantly enriched in field diseased (FD) samples, while no ASVs were significantly enriched in field healthy (FH) plants under the applied statistical thresholds. This limited differential response suggests that the soil rhizosphere bacterial community in field conditions remains comparatively stable across health states, displaying reduced compositional turnover relative to hydroponic systems (Fig. 3d).

Co-occurrence network analysis and identification of Hub ASVs

ASV-level co-occurrence network analysis revealed clear structural differences among cultivation systems and plant health states (Supplementary Table S7; Fig. 4). Hydroponic networks contained a substantially greater number of nodes (HH = 1,411; HD = 1,363) compared to field networks (FH = 840; FD = 923), indicating higher ASV richness in the hydroponic root-associated microbiome. Edge counts were also markedly higher in hydroponic systems (HH = 10,308; HD = 10,336) than in field systems (FH = 6,714; FD = 6,015), reflecting a denser overall interaction landscape in soilless conditions. Despite higher node and edge counts in hydroponics, network cohesion metrics indicated stronger structural organization in field systems. Graph density and clustering coefficient were higher in field networks (density: FH = 0.019, FD = 0.014; clustering coefficient: FH = 0.684, FD = 0.588) than in hydroponic networks (density: HH = 0.010, HD = 0.011; clustering coefficient: HH = 0.541, HD = 0.559), suggesting more compact and locally clustered microbial interactions in soil-associated communities. Average degree was highest in FH (15.986) and HD (15.167), followed by HH (14.611) and lowest in FD (13.034). Notably, degree decreased under disease in the field (FH → FD) but increased slightly under disease in hydroponics (HH → HD). Network diameter was larger in field networks (FH = 20; FD = 19) than in hydroponic networks (HH = 16; HD = 17), indicating longer maximal interaction paths in soil systems. Average path length followed a similar pattern, being higher in field samples (FH = 6.624; FD = 6.863) than in hydroponic samples (HH = 6.076; HD = 5.816), suggesting relatively shorter interaction paths and more compact connectivity in hydroponics. Importantly, the number of modules was highest in FD (72), followed by FH (68), HD (59), and HH (57). This indicates that modular fragmentation was most pronounced in field diseased samples rather than hydroponic diseased samples. Therefore, disease in soil systems appears to increase network compartmentalization, whereas hydroponic systems maintain comparatively fewer modules but lower density. Together, these ASV-level results indicate that field-associated bacterial networks are more tightly clustered and structurally cohesive, while hydroponic networks contain more taxa and edges but display lower density and clustering, suggesting a more diffuse interaction structure.

Hub ASVs were defined as those within the top 5% of nodes ranked simultaneously by degree and betweenness centrality (Fig. 4a), indicating taxa that are both highly connected and serve as potential interaction bridges within the network. The FH network was structured around hub ASVs affiliated with Microbispora (Streptosporangiaceae), Lysinibacillus (Planococcaceae), Parasegetibacter (Chitinophagaceae), and members of the WD2101 soil group (Planctomycetes-related lineage). The FD network exhibited a broader taxonomic distribution of hub ASVs compared to FH. Central nodes were affiliated with Luteolibacter (Verrucomicrobia), multiple Bacillus ASVs (Firmicutes), Ammoniphilus, Pseudomonas, Devosia, and Bradyrhizobium. The recurrence of Bacillales- and Rhizobiales-associated taxa among hub ASVs indicates that disease in the field system did not eliminate dominant structural members but rather reorganized the network around specific Firmicutes and Alphaproteobacteria lineages. This pattern suggests selective reinforcement of particular taxa within the interaction network under field disease conditions. Hub ASVs in HH were affiliated with Luteolibacter (Verrucomicrobia), an unclassified Ktedonobacteria lineage (Chloroflexi), Mucilaginibacter (Bacteroidetes), and a Burkholderiaceae-associated ASV (Proteobacteria). These taxa represent phylogenetically diverse lineages that occupy central positions within the hydroponic healthy network. Although several ASVs displayed high connectivity, their overall network influence appeared more distributed, suggesting that microbial interactions in healthy hydroponic roots were organized without strong dominance of specific bridging taxa. The HD network exhibited the most taxonomically heterogeneous hub composition among the four groups. Central ASVs were affiliated with Pedosphaeraceae (Ellin516 lineage), Actinoallomurus, multiple Bacillus ASVs (including Bacillus nealsonii), Schlesneria, Gemmata, members of the Burkholderia–Caballeronia–Paraburkholderia complex, Steroidobacter, Micropepsales-affiliated taxa, and Allorhizobium–Neorhizobium–Pararhizobium–Rhizobium lineages. Compared to HH, hub ASVs in HD were more numerous and spanned a broader range of phyla, indicating substantial reorganization of central interaction nodes under hydroponic disease conditions (Fig. 4a).

The node degree distributions differed across cultivation systems and plant health states. The FH network showed no ASVs with degree > 50 (ASVs > 50: 0), indicating the absence of extremely highly connected nodes in field healthy plants. In contrast, FD, HH, and HD each contained multiple ASVs exceeding this threshold (ASVs > 50: 41, 38, and 35, respectively), reflecting the presence of a high-degree tail in these networks. Overall, the hydroponic networks (HH and HD) displayed pronounced right-skewed distributions, consistent with many low-degree nodes alongside a subset of highly connected ASVs, whereas the field diseased network (FD) showed a similarly extended high-degree tail compared with FH (Fig. 4b).

Network centrality metrics revealed significant differences in node-level connectivity across cultivation systems and plant health states (Fig. 4c). Degree centrality was significantly higher in FD compared to FH (p < 0.001). Moreover, HD exhibited significantly higher degree centrality than FD (p < 0.01), indicating further enhancement of node connectivity under hydroponic disease conditions. Betweenness centrality did not show statistically significant differences among the four groups. Closeness centrality was significantly higher in FD than in FH (p < 0.001). HH also displayed significantly higher closeness centrality compared to FH (p < 0.001). Furthermore, HD exhibited significantly higher closeness centrality than both FD (p < 0.001) and HH (p < 0.001), indicating progressive centralization of network structure under hydroponic disease conditions. Similarly, eigenvector centrality was significantly higher in FD compared to FH (p < 0.001). HH also showed significantly higher eigenvector centrality than FH (p < 0.001). In addition, HD exhibited significantly higher eigenvector centrality than FD (p < 0.001), suggesting increased dominance of highly influential taxa under hydroponic disease conditions (Fig. 4c).

Integrated enrichment–network analysis

To assess whether differentially enriched taxa also occupied structurally central positions in the networks, we compared ASVs identified by DESeq2 and Random Forest with hub ASVs defined as the top 5% of nodes ranked simultaneously by degree and betweenness centrality (Supplementary Table S8). Overall, overlap between enriched ASVs and hub taxa was limited. Most taxa identified as differentially abundant across cultivation systems or health states did not qualify as network hubs. This indicates that abundance shifts and structural centrality represent distinct ecological properties within the bacterial communities. In field samples, several Bacillus-related ASVs enriched under diseased conditions appeared as hubs in the FD network, whereas in hydroponic samples, enriched taxa such as Ferruginibacter and Mesorhizobium were not central nodes. These findings suggest that disease-associated compositional shifts do not necessarily translate into dominant structural roles within microbial interaction networks.

Discussion

This study provides a comparative assessment of strawberry root-associated bacterial communities under soil-based field and substrate-based soilless hydroponic cultivation systems, integrating compositional and network-level perspectives. Both cultivation system and plant health status influenced microbiome structure, diversity, and interaction topology, with cultivation system emerging as the dominant driver, consistent with studies demonstrating strong environmental control over root microbiome assembly17. Although soil environments are generally expected to support greater microbial diversity due to physicochemical heterogeneity18, hydroponic samples showed higher alpha diversity across multiple indices. This pattern contrasts with previous findings of lower diversity in hydroponics19, suggesting that microbial diversity outcomes may vary depending on crop species, environmental management, substrate, and plant–microbe interactions. The relatively lower diversity observed in field-grown strawberries may reflect the cumulative effects of long-term soil management practices such as continuous monoculture and tillage, which are known to alter soil structure, deplete organic matter and reduce microbial richness19–21. As this study was conducted at a single representative site per cultivation system, system-specific factors such as soil management history or substrate characteristics cannot be excluded. Therefore, extrapolation beyond the studied systems should be made cautiously.

ASV-level enrichment analyses revealed clear system-specific patterns under both healthy and diseased conditions. In the healthy cross-system comparison (FH vs HH), field healthy samples were consistently enriched in multiple Bacillus-associated ASVs, consistent with the well-documented rhizosphere competence and antagonistic potential of Bacillus spp.15,22. In contrast, hydroponic healthy communities were enriched in taxonomically diverse lineages, including members of Rhizobiales (Devosia), Betaproteobacteriales (Burkholderiaceae), Chitinophagaceae (Parasegetibacter), Verrucomicrobia (Pedosphaeraceae), and Oligoflexales (Oligoflexus), reflecting system-associated differences in community assembly. In the diseased cross-system comparison (FD vs HD), field diseased samples remained enriched in Bacillales-associated ASVs, indicating persistence of Firmicutes-dominated assemblages under soil conditions. In contrast, hydroponic diseased samples were enriched in taxonomically diverse lineages, including Desmonostoc (Cyanobacteria), Pedobacter (Bacteroidota), Paenarthrobacter (Actinobacteria), and Verrucomicrobia-affiliated ASVs, reflecting broader compositional restructuring under hydroponic disease conditions. Within the hydroponic system (HH vs HD), disease was primarily associated with depletion of specific taxa rather than enrichment of disease-associated ASVs. Healthy hydroponic plants were enriched in Ferruginibacter, Luteolibacter, and Mesorhizobium, whereas no ASVs met the significance thresholds for enrichment in hydroponic diseased samples. This pattern suggests that disease in hydroponics may involve destabilization or loss of particular bacterial lineages rather than replacement by dominant alternative taxa. In contrast, within the field system (FH vs FD), only a single ASV affiliated with Bosea was significantly enriched in diseased plants, suggesting minimal compositional differentiation between health states in the root-associated soil community.

Network analysis further distinguished the two systems. Despite harboring more nodes and edges, hydroponic networks exhibited lower density and clustering coefficients than field networks, indicating weaker local cohesion. Previous work has shown that agricultural management can alter microbial network complexity and keystone taxa composition23, and that highly connected taxa play important roles in microbiome structure and function24. The present results extend this understanding by demonstrating that cultivation system shapes interaction topology: soil-associated communities formed more tightly clustered interaction neighborhoods, whereas hydroponic communities, although richer in ASVs, displayed a more diffuse interaction structure. These findings indicate that taxonomic richness and structural cohesion represent distinct ecological properties and are not necessarily coupled.

Plant health status exerted system-dependent effects on network organization. In hydroponics, disease was associated with significant increases in degree, closeness, and eigenvector centrality, reflecting enhanced node-level connectivity and influence within the network. Betweenness centrality did not differ significantly among groups, suggesting that bridging roles were largely preserved. In contrast, disease in the field system was associated with increased modular compartmentalization, indicating greater partitioning of interactions without substantial compositional turnover. This pattern suggests that the soil-associated bacterial community maintained taxonomic stability while reorganizing its interaction structure under diseased conditions.

It is important to note that plant health classification was based on visual symptom assessment without pathogen confirmation. Because decline-like symptoms may arise from both biotic pathogens and abiotic stress factors, microbiome differences should be interpreted as stress-associated patterns rather than pathogen-specific responses. Although hydroponic systems are typically managed under controlled conditions, localized abiotic variation in irrigation, nutrient delivery, or root-zone conditions cannot be fully excluded.

Finally, integration of differential abundance and network hub analyses revealed limited overlap between enriched ASVs and hub taxa, underscoring that abundance shifts and structural centrality capture distinct ecological dimensions. Differential abundance reflects changes in representation across conditions, whereas hub detection identifies taxa occupying central interaction positions within the co-occurrence network. Together, these complementary approaches provide a more comprehensive understanding of how cultivation context and plant health shape microbiome composition and structural organization.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that cultivation system is the primary determinant of both the taxonomic composition and interaction architecture of strawberry root-associated bacterial communities, while plant health status exerts system-dependent effects. Soil-based systems maintained more cohesive microbial networks, whereas hydroponic systems exhibited greater structural reorganization under symptomatic conditions. These findings highlight that microbiome structural organization and health-associated responses are cultivation-dependent properties, underscoring the importance of system-specific microbiome management strategies in intensive strawberry production.

Materials and methods

Experimental setup

Strawberry seedlings (cv. Seolhyang) were obtained from a commercial nursery operating under controlled hygienic conditions. Although seedlings were not germ-free, root surfaces were gently rinsed with sterile distilled water prior to transplantation into the hydroponic system to minimize carryover of loosely attached nursery-associated microbes and to standardize initial root conditions.

Two cultivation systems were established: a soil-based field system and a substrate-based soilless hydroponic system. The field cultivation system was located at an experimental strawberry plot characterized by loamy soil (pH 6.3 ± 0.2). Seedlings were transplanted into raised beds (20 cm height, 1.2 m spacing) covered with polyethylene mulch and irrigated by drip using untreated groundwater. No fertilizers, pesticides, or microbial amendments were applied during the experimental period. The hydroponic system consisted of an inert cocopeat–perlite substrate housed in elevated plastic troughs. Plants were irrigated with a recirculating nutrient solution prepared using YaraTera CALCIMAG (Reservoir A) and YaraTera KRISTALON Yellow (Reservoir B). Electrical conductivity (EC) was maintained between 0.9 and 1.2 mS/cm, and pH was maintained at 6.4 throughout cultivation. No microbial inoculants or biocontrol treatments were applied. Each cultivation system initially comprised 40 seedlings. Based on plant appearance at the time of sampling, 10 plants per health category (asymptomatic and symptomatic) were selected for microbial analysis.

Root-associated sampling and DNA extraction

Sampling was conducted four weeks after transplantation at the early flowering stage. In this study, root-associated bacterial communities were operationally targeted from both cultivation systems, recognizing that the sampled compartment differs between soil-based and substrate-based soilless conditions. For field-grown plants, roots were gently exposed by careful excavation. Soil from the immediate vicinity of exposed roots was collected using sterile spatulas (approximately 5 g per plant), representing the root-associated soil community influenced by root activity. For hydroponic plants, roots were accessed within the cocopeat–perlite substrate matrix. Substrate material from the immediate root zone surrounding exposed roots was collected using sterile spatulas (approximately 5 g per plant), representing the root-associated substrate microbiome. Throughout the manuscript, the term “root-associated bacterial community” is used when referring collectively to field root-associated soil and hydroponic root-adhering substrate samples. Plants were classified as healthy or diseased based on visible aboveground characteristics at the time of sampling. Diseased plants exhibited one or more decline-associated symptoms, including wilting, persistent leaf drooping, chlorosis (leaf yellowing), and reduced vigor compared to neighboring plants. Symptom severity ranged from mild chlorosis and slight drooping to pronounced wilting and visible growth suppression. Healthy plants showed no visible signs of wilting, yellowing, or growth decline. No pathogen isolation, culturing, or molecular diagnostics were performed to determine the causal factor of symptoms. Accordingly, the term “diseased” in this study refers to symptom-defined plant stress status rather than a laboratory-confirmed pathogen etiology. Ten biological replicates were collected per group: field healthy (FH), field diseased (FD), hydroponic healthy (HH), and hydroponic diseased (HD), resulting in 40 total root-associated samples. All samples were transferred to sterile 50 mL tubes, transported on dry ice, and stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted using the FastDNA™ Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals™) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

PCR amplification and sequencing

Hypervariable regions V3 and V4 of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified using the universal primers 341F (5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′) and 805R (5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′)25, where the bold regions represent Illumina overhang adapter sequences and the remaining bases correspond to the gene-specific primers. Amplification was carried out using the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 55°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension step at 72°C for 5 min. The amplicons were subjected to clean-up by AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) for removing PCR primers and primer dimers. Second PCR was done with the Nextera XT Index Kit with dual indices and Illumina sequencing adapters (Illumina, USA). Amplification was performed under the same conditions as described above, but using eight amplification cycles. PCR products were separated on 1% agarose gels and visualized using a Gel-Doc imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Amplicons were purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform using a 2 × 300 bp paired-end read configuration at Macrogen, Seoul, South Korea.

Processing and filtering of sequences

The CASAVA-demultiplexed FASTQ sequences were quality-filtered and denoised using the DADA2 plugin implemented in QIIME2 (version 2024.10)26. Quality filtering parameters were determined based on inspection of per-base Phred quality profiles (Fig. S1). Forward reads were trimmed by 12 bp from the 5′ end (–p-trim-left-f 12), while reverse reads were not trimmed at the start (–p-trim-left-r 0). Reads were truncated at 250 bp (forward) and 230 bp (reverse) (–p-trunc-len-f 250; –p-trunc-len-r 230) at positions where quality scores declined below Q25. These truncation lengths provided sufficient overlap (~ 20 bp) for reliable merging of the V3–V4 region. Paired-end reads were denoised, merged, and screened for chimeras within DADA2. An initial total of 30,992 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) was inferred. This set included host-derived sequences (e.g., mitochondrial and chloroplast DNA) and unassigned ASVs. ASVs assigned to the orders Chloroplast and Rickettsiales, as well as those unclassified at the kingdom level, were removed, reducing the ASV count to 30,137 (Supplementary Table S1). Thus, 855 ASVs corresponding to non-bacterial or unassigned sequences were excluded. Taxonomic classification of non-chimeric ASVs was performed using a Naïve Bayes classifier implemented in q2-feature-classifier against the SILVA v138 database, trained specifically for the V3–V4 region27. To minimize potential sequencing artifacts and extremely low-abundance variants, ASVs with fewer than three total reads in at least 20% of all the samples were removed. Following abundance-based filtering, 2,104 high-confidence ASVs remained for downstream analyses (Supplementary Table S1). Samples were rarefied to 9,023 reads per sample based on rarefaction curves that reached a plateau at this depth (Fig. S2), ensuring standardized sampling effort across samples. The resulting feature table, taxonomy assignments, sample metadata, and rooted phylogenetic tree were exported for subsequent analyses in R (version 4.4.1).

Statistical analyses and visualization

For statistical analyses, the BIOM feature table, taxonomy assignments, metadata, and rooted phylogenetic tree were imported into R (version 4.4.1) and analyzed using the ‘phyloseq’ package (version v1.50.0)28. Alpha-diversity was assessed by rarefying read depths with the ‘rarefy_even_depth’ function, followed by calculating Observed ASVs, Shannon index, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, and Pielou’s evenness using estimate_richness. Normality and homogeneity of variances were checked using Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honest significant difference (HSD) tests was used to determine the statistical significance in multiple comparisons. The Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test was used to assess differences in relative abundance (RA) of microbial taxa among groups. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Beta-diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity calculated from a cumulative sum scaling (CSS)-normalized ASV table, with normalization performed via the ‘metagenomeSeq’ package (version 1.48.1). To assess differences in community composition, pairwise and two-way PERMANOVA were conducted using adonis2 (vegan, 999 permutations). Community structure was visualized using PCoA on Bray–Curtis distances, reducing dimensionality for cluster interpretation. The analysis of the microbial enrichment was performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.46.0)29 to identify the significantly enriched genera in pair-wise comparisons with an adjusted p-value (False Discovery Rate, FDR p < 0.05). Differences in the abundance were considered significant when FDR adjusted p-values were lower than 0.01. The randomForest function from the randomForest package (version 4.7.1.2) in R30 was used to perform Random Forest classification to identify the most important ASVs discriminating between sample groups (e.g., healthy vs. diseased plants in field and hydroponic systems). The model was trained using normalized ASV abundance tables as input features, with sample condition as the categorical response variable. Default parameters were applied (number of trees = 500), and feature importance was ranked based on the mean decrease in Gini (MDG). The top 20 discriminating ASVs identified by Random Forest, along with differentially abundant taxa detected by DESeq2, were visualized using ggplot2 package (version 3.5.2). This approach provided complementary evidence for microbial signatures important in distinguishing plant health status and cultivation conditions.

Network construction and analysis

Networks were constructed using the igraph package (version 2.1.2). Prior to network construction, ASV counts were transformed using the centered log-ratio (CLR) method to account for the compositional nature of amplicon sequencing data and to mitigate false correlations arising from unit-sum constraints31,32. CLR transformation maps compositional data into Euclidean space, permitting valid use of linear correlation measures such as Pearson’s r. Four separate networks were generated for field-healthy, field-diseased, hydroponic-healthy, and hydroponic-diseased samples. Pearson correlation coefficients were then calculated from the abundance tables, applying a filter threshold of 0.001. A correlation threshold of 0.85, determined through correlation optimization, was used for network construction. The resulting correlation matrix was converted into an adjacency matrix, where ASVs with correlation coefficients exceeding this threshold were considered connected. Networks were visualized in Gephi (version 0.8)33 using the Fruchterman–Reingold layout algorithm 34,35. Differences in network parameters (degree, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and eigenvector centrality) among sample groups were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by Dunn’s post hoc tests.
