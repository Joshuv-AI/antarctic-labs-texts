Impact of Age and Sex on Coronary Flow Reserve and Microvascular Function in Patients With Angina and Nonobstructive Coronary Arteries
Authors: Spyridon Kostantinis, Gabriel Garcia Castro, Nida Latif, Natasha Cigarroa, Steffne J Kunnirickal, Natalija Odanovic, Samit M Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAngina and nonobstructive coronary arteries is increasingly recognized, but the impact of age and sex on coronary microvascular function in this population has received limited study.MethodsIn 206 consecutive patients with angina and nonobstructive coronary arteries, invasive coronary function testing was performed including vasoreactivity testing with acetylcholine provocation, guidewire–based assessment of coronary flow reserve (CFR) with bolus thermodilution, and index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR). Multivariable regression models assessed associations of age and sex with coronary physiologic indices, and subgroup analyses were conducted to examine age-related trends in microvascular function separately in men and women.ResultsMean age was 57.1 ± 11.2 years (range, 27-81 years), and 149 (72.3%) were women. Increasing age was associated with progressive decrease in CFR (β = −0.05; P = .001) and increase in hyperemic mean transit time (Tmn; β = 0.009; P < .001) and IMR (β = 0.63; P < .001). Baseline Tmn was similar among the 3 age groups. Women had lower IMR (24.5 ± 13.5 vs 30.2 ± 18.1; P = .038), and shorter baseline and hyperemic Tmn, but similar CFR (3.43 ± 1.59 vs 3.73 ± 1.94; P = .315) compared with men. In sex-stratified analysis, age was associated with higher IMR in women (β = 0.51; P < .001), but not in men (β = 0.29; P = .154). CFR declined significantly with increasing age in both sexes, with a stronger association in women (β = −0.04; P < .001) than in men (β = −0.06; P = .011).ConclusionsIncreasing age was associated with a decrease in coronary microvascular function and increase in microcirculatory resistance, which was pronounced in women but not in men. There was no association of baseline Tmn with age, suggesting that age-related impairment in CFR was primarily driven by reduced hyperemic flow.Keywords: angina and nonobstructive coronary arteries, coronary function testing, coronary microvascular dysfunction, vasospastic angina

Introduction

The prevalence of ischemic heart disease increases with age, which has historically been attributed to a greater prevalence of obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD). However, aging is associated with structural and functional changes in the coronary microcirculation.1 Previous studies with positron emission tomography imaging suggested that aging is associated with an increase in resting myocardial blood flow rather than a decrement in vasodilatory capacity, but more recent studies with intracoronary Doppler and continuous thermodilution have demonstrated reduced hyperemic flow and increased microcirculatory resistance with greater age.2, 3, 4, 5 Recently, there has been increasing recognition of angina and nonobstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA), including vasomotor disorders such as coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) and vasospastic angina (VA), which are more prevalent in women than that in men.6 A previous study in a predominantly male population demonstrated a significant decrease in coronary flow reserve (CFR) associated with aging in patients in whom revascularization was deferred.7 Recently, Jansen et al8 evaluated microvascular function using continuous thermodilution in a cohort of patients with suspected ANOCA and reported an age-dependent physiological increase in minimal microvascular resistance and decrease in microvascular function. Although continuous thermodilution offers lower variability, bolus thermodilution is the most commonly used method for invasive assessment of coronary physiology in patients with ANOCA. The goal of our study was to examine the effects of age and sex on coronary microvascular function and physiologic diagnosis in patients with ANOCA who underwent a comprehensive physiologic assessment with bolus thermodilution.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective, single-center study included consecutive patients who were referred for invasive coronary function testing at Yale New Haven Hospital in New Haven, Connecticut, between 2017-2024 for assessment of suspected ANOCA. Participants were included if they were found to have nonobstructive coronary arteries defined as diameter stenosis of ≤50% angiographic stenosis, or with fractional flow reserve (FFR) of >0.80 or non-hyperemic pressure ratio of >0.89 for any stenosis ≥50% but ≤70%. All participants had undergone clinically indicated invasive coronary function testing (CFT) with bolus thermodilution using a standardized protocol and diagnosed with an ANOCA endotype, as defined by the Coronary Vasomotion Disorders International Study Group criteria, including CMD or VA.9,10 This study was approved by the Yale institutional review board (IRB protocol 2000028525).

Invasive coronary function testing protocol

The CFT protocol included guidewire–based testing with bolus coronary thermodilution using PressureWire X (Abbott Vascular) and provocative testing with intracoronary acetylcholine administration using a previously published protocol.11 Briefly, CFT included vasoreactivity testing with acetylcholine provocation, guidewire–based assessment of CFR, index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR), and FFR. Criteria for a diagnosis of CMD included an abnormal IMR of ≥25 and/or impaired CFR ≤2.5.12,13 Criteria for VA included ≥90% narrowing of the epicardial vessel during acetylcholine testing and either or both angina and transient electrocardiographic changes. Endothelial dysfunction was defined as angiographic diameter change of ≤0% and but not greater than −89% in response to acetylcholine infusion. The transradial approach was used in 171 cases (83.0%) without use of intra-arterial vasodilators, with the remainder performed via transfemoral access. The vessel selected for guide wire placement was the left anterior descending artery in 91.3% of patients, left circumflex in 5.3%, and right coronary artery in 3.4%.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and were compared between groups using Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were described as mean ± SD or median with IQR unless otherwise specified and were compared using the Student t test, analysis of variance, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, or Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Patients were divided into 3 groups to test for differences based on age quartiles (<50 years [n = 52, 25.2%], 50-65 years [n = 107, 52.0%], and >65 years [n = 47, 22.8%]). The impact of age on measures of microvascular function was evaluated using linear regression with age as a continuous variable. Correlations between coronary physiology parameters and age were adjusted for several potential cofounders, including sex, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, baseline creatinine, ejection fraction, and left ventricular end diastolic pressure (LVEDP). Multivariable regression models assessed associations of age and sex with coronary physiologic indices, and subgroup analyses were conducted to examine age-related trends in microvascular function separately in males and females. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP, version 17.0 (SAS Institute). A P value of <.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Age-related differences

A total of 206 patients were identified. Mean age was 57.1 ± 11.2 years (range, 27-81 years), and 149 (72.3%) were women. Older patients had higher rates of hypertension and previous diagnosis of heart failure (Table 1). The anatomic description of the coronary angiogram was normal coronary arteries in 69.2% for patients aged <50 years and decreased to 40.2% and 44.7% in the 50- to 65-years-old and >65-year-old age groups. Non-obstructive CAD was more common in the older age groups (30.8%, 59.8%, and 55.3%, respectively) (Table 2). There was no significant difference in LVEDP between the 3 age groups.

Older patients had lower CFR and higher IMR values than younger patients (Table 2). Increasing age was associated with progressive decrease in CFR (β = −0.05; P = .001) (Figure 1A, B) and increase in IMR (β = 0.63; P < .001) (Figure 1C), after adjusting for sex, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, baseline creatinine, ejection fraction, and LVEDP. Baseline hyperemic mean transit time (Tmn) were similar among the 3 age groups, but hyperemic Tmn were higher in older patients (0.27 ± 0.17 vs 0.34 ± 0.16 vs 0.47 ± 0.25 seconds; P = .001), suggesting that age-related impairment in CFR is primarily driven by reduced hyperemic flow.

Multivariable linear regression was performed to evaluate determinants for baseline Pd/Pa, baseline Tmn, hyperemic Tmn, CFR, and IMR (Tables 3 and 4; Figure 1). After adjusting for multiple clinical factors, increasing age was not associated with baseline Pd/Pa or baseline Tmn but was associated with higher hyperemic Tmn (β = 0.009; P < .001), lower CFR, and higher IMR.

Older patients had higher rates of CMD (11.5% vs 30.8% vs 48.9%, respectively, in the 3 age groups; P = .001), but lower rates of VA (34.6% vs 35.5% vs 17.0%; P = .047) and symptomatic myocardial bridging (21.2% vs 9.4% vs 4.3%; P = .019).

Sex-related differences

Female patients had lower body surface areas and lower baseline creatinine values but had otherwise similar baseline characteristics (Table 1). There were significant differences in medical therapy at baseline with female patients being less likely to be on statins (53.0% vs 68.4%; P = .046), β blockers (32.9% vs 56.1%; P = .002) or angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blocker (22.8% vs 36.8%; P = .042) at the time of testing compared with males (Supplemental Table S1). There was no significant difference in LVEDP between sexes.

When compared with men, women had lower IMR (24.5 ± 13.5 vs 30.2 ± 18.1; P = .038), but similar CFR (3.43 ± 1.59 vs 3.73 ± 1.94; P = .315). Women had lower baseline Tmn (0.99 ± 0.43 vs 1.27 ± 0.49 seconds; P = .001) and hyperemic Tmn (0.33 ± 0.19 vs 0.41 ± 0.22 seconds; P = .031), compared with men (Table 2; Figure 2). Overall, there were no significant differences in physiologic diagnosis between men and women (Table 2).

Combined effect of age and sex

In sex-stratified analysis, age was significantly associated with higher IMR in women (β = 0.51; P < .001), but not in men (β = 0.29; P = .154). CFR declined significantly with increasing age in both groups, with a stronger association in women (β = −0.04; P < .001) than in men (β = −0.06; P = .011). Baseline and hyperemic Tmn increased significantly with age in women (β = 0.007; P = .032 and β = 0.008; P < .001, respectively), whereas in men, the associations were not statistically significant (β = −0.006; P = .352 and β = 0.003; P = .305, respectively) (Figure 3; Supplemental Tables S2 and S3).

Figure 4 demonstrates a subanalysis of physiologic diagnosis according to age group (<50, 50-65, and >65 years) separately in women and men. Older female patients with ANOCA had higher rates of CMD (P < .001), with 55% of female patients older than 65 years and only 10% of female patients younger than 50 years old having CMD. Symptomatic myocardial bridging was more frequent in younger patients (19% vs 5% and 7%, respectively, in the 2 older age groups; P = .039) (Figure 4A). A trend toward more frequent diagnosis of VA was observed in patients younger than 65 years, with only 14% of female patients older than 65 years having VA (P = .072). Interestingly, there were no significant differences in physiologic diagnosis in the 3 age groups among men (Figure 4B).

Discussion

The main findings of our study are as follows: (1) using bolus thermodilution, aging was associated with a decrease in coronary microvascular function and increase in microcirculatory resistance, represented by decreased CFR, higher hyperemic Tmn, and increased IMR; (2) aging was not associated with baseline Tmn, suggesting that age-related impairment in CFR is primarily driven by reduced hyperemic flow; (3) women had lower IMR, shorter baseline and hyperemic Tmn, and similar CFR values compared with men; and (4) in sex-stratified analysis, age was significantly associated with higher IMR in women, but not in men, while CFR declined significantly in both sexes, with a stronger association in women than that in men (Central Illustration).

Approximately one-half of patients undergoing invasive coronary angiography for suspected ischemic heart disease are found to have normal coronary arteries or nonobstructive CAD (no stenosis >50%).6,14 The 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines on chronic coronary syndromes have readdressed the ANOCA issue and upgraded the recommendation for invasive coronary functional testing in persistently symptomatic patients with suspected ANOCA to Ib.13 Historically, patients with ANOCA tend to be younger, have fewer cardiac risk factors than those with obstructive CAD, and are more likely to be women.15 Our findings suggest that older patients may be underrepresented in previous studies of ANOCA populations and that there may be underlying physiologic differences, which increase the prevalence of CMD in this population. Our study specifically examined patients with ANOCA who underwent invasive coronary function testing. While our findings inform mechanisms underlying age-related and sex-related differences in CMD and VA, they may not generalize to asymptomatic individuals with nonobstructive coronary arteries.

Previous studies have assessed the effects of aging on CFR using15 O-water positron emission computed tomography or transthoracic Doppler echocardiography in patients with a normal coronary angiogram.3,5 Ramandika et al16 evaluated the impact of aging on coronary flow velocities and CFR in patients with no evidence of myocardial perfusion abnormality on single-photon emission computed tomography, and found that age was not a determinant of resting coronary flow velocity, but was a determinant for hyperemic coronary flow velocity as well as CFR.16 Faria et al7 documented a significant decrease in CFR associated with aging in patients in whom revascularization was deferred on the grounds of FFR values, but they did not report changes in flow with aging, as a cause of low CFR. In contrast to Faria et al,7 who studied patients with deferred revascularization based on FFR, our cohort excluded obstructive CAD and focused exclusively on patients with ANOCA, limiting confounding by epicardial disease. Jansen et al8 recently evaluated microvascular function using continuous thermodilution in a cohort of 314 patients with ANOCA, and found no differences between groups in terms of resting absolute flow or microvascular resistance, but noted a small decrease in hyperemic flow with increasing age and a corresponding small, but significant, increase in hyperemic microvascular resistance. The findings of our analysis are similar to those of Jansen et al8 and help inform the interpretation of physiologic assessment using bolus thermodilution, which is far more commonly used in real-world practice. Our results expand those of prior findings to demonstrate that bolus thermodilution hyperemic Tmn, which is inversely proportional to hyperemic flow, increases with age, supporting reduced vasodilatory capacity as a mechanism for age-related decrement in microcirculatory function. As a result, we observed reduced CFR and increased IMR with advancing age after adjusting for multiple clinical factors. There were no significant differences in baseline Tmn, suggesting that the dominant mechanism leading to CFR impairment with advancing age is a progressive decrease in hyperemic flow. Potential explanations could be the age-related loss of vasodilatory function in vascular smooth muscle, endothelial dysfunction, or external compression of the microvascular vessels from myocardial thickening/fibrosis.

Our sex-stratified analysis revealed that the decline in CFR and increase in IMR with advancing age were significant in women but not in men. Additionally, age-related increases in both baseline and hyperemic Tmn were only observed among women, suggesting impaired vasodilatory reserve. These findings support the hypothesis of a sex-specific vulnerability to microvascular aging in females with ANOCA, which may contribute to their disproportionately higher prevalence of CMD. The lack of statistically significant age-related trends among males may reflect true biological differences but may also be attributed to the smaller sample size of male patients in our cohort.

Kobayashi et al17 investigated differences in CFR and IMR in patients with angina in the absence of obstructive CAD and reported that IMR was similar between sexes, but CFR was lower in women, primarily due to a shorter resting Tmn in women, but the effect of age was not analyzed.17 They also reported that female sex was independently associated with both a lower CFR and shorter resting Tmn.17 In our study, women had significantly shorter baseline as well as hyperemic Tmn values, similar CFR values, and lower IMR compared to men.

The WISE (Women’s Ischemia Syndrome Evaluation) study reported that one-half of symptomatic women with nonobstructive CAD had CMD, which was associated with an adverse cardiovascular prognosis.18 Chung et al19 reported nonsignificantly higher rates of vessel-specific CMD in women (46.6%) than those in men (39.5%). In our study, there was no significant difference in the prevalence of CMD between men and women, which could be explained by technical differences between bolus thermodilution and hyperemic Doppler or differences in the categorization of postprocedural diagnoses between our study and the other studies.

Limitations

Although our work provides important insights into the experiences of patients with ANOCA, this study has several limitations. First, participants were selected as a convenience sample from a single clinical center; thus, selection bias may have influenced our findings. Second, as a retrospective study, we were limited by potential unmeasured confounding and missing data. Third, the reliance on operator-dependent techniques for thermodilution and IMR measurements introduces variability that can impact the consistency of the results. Fourth, we did not separately report Pd and Pa values, limiting our ability to assess the effect of blood pressure on our findings, although thermodilution transit times are not significantly affected by proximal or distal coronary pressure. Fifth, the small sample size of males in our study may have limited our ability to detect age-related trends among males, reducing the power of sex-specific subgroup analysis. Sixth, no standardized long-term follow-up is available in this cohort, so we cannot evaluate long-term clinical outcomes related to the physiologic diagnosis, but this is a planned analysis of the DISCOVER INOCA registry.11

Conclusion

In conclusion, increasing age was associated with a decrease in coronary microvascular function and increase in microcirculatory resistance, represented by decreased CFR and increased IMR. Aging was associated with higher hyperemic Tmn, but there was no association of baseline Tmn with age, suggesting that age-related impairment in CFR was primarily driven by reduced hyperemic flow. Our sex-stratified analysis revealed that the decline in CFR and increase in IMR with advancing age were significant in females, but not in males. As invasive assessment of microvascular function is increasingly used in clinical practice, thresholds for abnormal CFR and IMR should be interpreted in the context of patient age and sex.
