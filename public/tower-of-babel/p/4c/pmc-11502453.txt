Limiting access to assisted reproductive technologies for males of advanced age—Pros and cons from a Nordic perspective
Authors: Hans Ivar Hanevik, Christina Bergh, Hannele Laivuori, Anne Lærke Spangmose, Åsa Magnusson, Anja Pinborg, Terhi Piltonen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is not controversial to state that parental age is increasing in several countries. But how to deal with this increase might be. Some Nordic countries have set an upper age limit for females seeking assisted reproduction in their national legislation, but none have done so for males. There are also recommendations in place that restrict access to publicly funded assisted reproduction for both females and males of advanced age in some Nordic countries. As recent data now show somatic and psychiatric health risks related to advanced paternal age, we ask if the time has come for countries to set an upper age limit for males seeking assisted reproduction like there already is for females, and summarize some of the risks and rewards involved in treating couples with advanced age in fertility clinics.

INTRODUCTION

In the Nordic countries, the upward trend in parental age continues, highlighting the ongoing rise in maternal age at conception and increased paternal age. This happens despite public efforts such as campaigns and policies that aim to incite and entice citizens to start their reproductive careers earlier, thus promoting more children and less infertility, for both women and men. Simultaneously, laws and guidelines that govern assisted reproductive technology (ART) in the Nordic countries set sex‐specific upper age limits. Indeed, access to ART for females of advanced age is restricted by law in some Nordic countries as shown in Table 1 which is modified from a recent overview by The Nordic Committee on Bioethics. 1 Interestingly, and in contrast, there is currently no legal restriction on access to ART for males of advanced age in any of the Nordic countries. In the public sector, reimbursement of ART to both male and female patients of advanced age is, however, limited (see Table 1). These limitations in reimbursement are oftentimes based on priority debates in public healthcare that typically aim to guide public resources toward those infertile patients with the highest likelihood of achieving a healthy pregnancy by ART. While some data do suggest that increased age of the male partner is associated with decreased chances of conception by ART independently of female age, these results are not uniform. 2 Notwithstanding, most Nordic sperm banks have set an upper age limit on their sperm donors, commonly between 35 and 46 years. On the background of increasing parental age, there is mounting concern about the effects of advanced paternal age on the health of the offspring. 3 , 4 , 5 In fact, the recently published guidelines for male factor infertility from the American Society for Reproductive Medicine state that “Clinicians should advise couples with advanced paternal age (≥40) that there is an increased risk of adverse health outcomes for their offspring.” 6

Taking into consideration the sex‐specific differences in the laws governing ART, as well as limitations on public funding toward healthcare, it is our experience that the discussions on restricting access to ART in couples with advanced male age often become “heated debates” in ART clinics, both public and private. Furthermore, the absence of laws or common guidelines regarding advanced male age puts the final decision to treat or not on the shoulders of the ART physician. To facilitate discussion on the topic, this commentary attempts to summarize some of the risks and rewards involved in treating couples of advanced male age with ART while not adopting a position either in favor or against limiting access to ART to males of advanced age in the Nordic countries. In this regard, it is noteworthy that although the majority of the Nordic countries are represented in the commentary's group of authors, Iceland is not, meaning that possibly unique, Icelandic perspective is not considered.

LIVE BIRTH RATE IN COUPLES WITH ADVANCED MALE AGE

Several studies, reviewed by Jimbo and co‐workers, 2 reported impaired semen quality with increasing male age leading to decreased fecundity, and an increased risk of miscarriage in non‐assisted conceptions. 7 However, studies on ART success rates stratified by paternal age in autologous cycles show conflicting results. One retrospective cohort study found that advanced paternal age was associated with a decreased live birth rate after ART across all female age groups, 8 while another retrospective cohort study by Punjani and co‐workers 9 did not find such an association. A recent systematic review and meta‐analysis found higher odds of live birth in autologous cycles if the father was <40 years old (OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.25–3.51), although with a high grade of data heterogeneity. 10 Based on oocyte donation programs, Begon and co‐workers 11 recently published a systematic review reporting a linear decrease in live birth rate with increasing paternal age, without any specific cut‐off age. In addition, a Danish study reported delayed blastocyst development with increasing paternal age. 12 The results in this study were adjusted for maternal age, and the authors hypothesize that the delay may have been due to increased time for the embryo to repair sperm‐related DNA damage with advanced male age.

SOMATIC, NEURODEVELOPMENTAL, AND PSYCHIATRIC HEALTH IN OFFSPRING IN COUPLES WITH ADVANCED PATERNAL AGE

Several high‐quality publications were recently published that assessed and synthesized available data on the somatic health effects on children in couples with advanced paternal age 3 , 5 independently of whether ART is the method of conception or not. In general, the results are reassuring, but for some outcomes, the risk of morbidity was increased in offspring born to such couples. Pregnancies and perinatal periods involving fathers older than 44 years were associated with a 2% increased risk of complications such as preterm birth in one meta‐analysis. 3 A recent cohort study found an increased risk of 9% for preterm birth in couples with males >44 years of age compared to 25–34 of age. 13 As for stillbirth, the risk was increased by 19% in couples with advanced paternal age in the meta‐analysis by Oldereid and co‐workers. 3 Other data showed a linear increase in the number of paternally derived de novo mutations in the offspring born to fathers of advanced age, putatively derived from a paternal age effect. In general, these de novo mutations cause rare disorders with severe phenotypes, and the estimated incidence in the offspring is <0.5%. 14

Regarding psychiatric health, the risk increase associated with paternal age is more pronounced. A meta‐analysis from 2017 based on 27 studies found a 55% increased risk of autism in offspring with older fathers, and an increase of 10 years in paternal age was associated with a 21% higher risk of autism in offspring. 4 The calculated prevalence of autism based on the cohort studies included in the meta‐analysis ranged from 0.08 to 0.72% of children, with the highest prevalence in the newest studies. 4 In a more recent meta‐analysis, where all included studies were adjusted for maternal age, the risk of autism was increased by 25% in offspring from older fathers in general, and 43% for fathers above 45 years of age. 3 This is in line with another meta‐analysis which found that when comparing fathers aged ≥50 years to fathers ≤29 years at birth of the affected child, the increased risk of autism spectrum disorder in offspring was 146%. 15 The two most recent cohort studies from Sweden and Denmark included in Oldereid and co‐workers' paper 3 showed a prevalence of autism ranging from 0.64 to 1.14%.

Regarding schizophrenia, the overall lifetime prevalence of developing that disease is about 1%, and one meta‐analysis reported a 31% increase in risk for developing schizophrenia in children with fathers of advanced paternal age. 3 Furthermore, for fathers 45–50 years of age, the increase in risk was 40%, and for fathers >50 years, it was 104%. 3 This is in accordance with a previous meta‐analysis showing that the increase in relative risk of schizophrenia in the offspring of fathers >55 years was >100% when compared to a reference group of fathers of 25–35 years. 16 For psychiatric health, then, for the diseases mentioned here, the increase in risk associated with older fathers is large, but the diseases in question have a low prevalence.

CHILDLESSNESS AND HEALTH

In a Swedish cohort, it was found that advanced paternal and maternal age was associated with a decrease in mortality in the adult offspring. 17 Nevertheless, a recent review discussed some of the negative effects of growing up with an older father and addressed issues like handling parent's frailty and their death. 18 According to that review, early parental death in childhood or adolescence was associated with a 50% increased risk of a psychiatric disorder compared to individuals who do not experience early parental loss. Losing a father prematurely is not a rarity. In the United States, for a father who is 55 years old at delivery, there is a 24% risk that the father dies before the child is 18 years old. 18 In general, adults who are childless have a higher morbidity and mortality. One example is cardiovascular health where a Swedish study found that childlessness was an independent risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity in males over 45 years of age, 19 although residual confounding may affect this association.

ETHICAL AND SOCIAL ASPECTS OF LIMITING ART ACCESS TO MALES OF ADVANCED AGE

A recent scoping review by Couture and co‐workers 20 identified terminological, social, psychological, ethical, and regulatory issues, as well as issues pertaining to the field of public health, that concern advanced male age and ART. A social issue raised, which is that while older fathers tend to have a higher socio‐economic status, better parenting skills, and a better behavioral profile compared to younger fathers, children who were surveyed regarding the issue responded that they preferred having parents who had them before the age of 30, perhaps due to better stamina and less of a generational gap in younger fathers. 21 One of the ethical concerns discussed was whether limiting ART access to older males would amount to discrimination in the form of ageism, as some men thus would be denied treatment because of their age. This argument also applies to women where legal limits have nonetheless been set. Another form of discrimination promoted by age limits for ART was labeled “infertilism” by Tonkens and co‐workers. 22 Infertility occurs when individuals are discriminated against because of their medical condition or disability, in this case, low sperm count. Discrimination by infertilism is relevant to females too since a decreasing ovarian reserve by age today could be managed by oocyte donation.

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

Since its inception, ART has been considered controversial in the Nordic countries. Compared to most other medical treatments, the field of ART also continues to draw legal interest and meddling, for better or for worse. As ART practitioners, we should not be blind to the fact that the pregnancies and children that result from our work are under increased risk for short‐ and long‐term adverse health outcomes compared to those conceived without assistance. Although the jury is still out as to whether it is the ART treatment per se or the parents' underlying infertility that is the root cause, 23 it is clear that children born after assisted reproduction have an increased risk of both adverse perinatal outcomes (prevalence 1.7% vs 1.1% 24 ) and congenital malformations (prevalence 3.4% vs 2.9% 25 ) compared to those born after unassisted conception. In fact, the risk of adverse perinatal health outcomes associated with an older father, such as preterm birth, could be considered minor (pooled adjusted OR 1.1 for fathers above 45 in one meta‐analysis 3 ) compared to those risks that are attributed to the ART procedure per se (adjusted ORs for premature birth in singletons born after ART compared to singletons born from natural conception 1.4–2.0 in one study 23 ). Even so, to infertile patients access to ART treatment is crucial for having a child, and establishing upper age limits means there would be many infertile patients with no access to ART. Nevertheless, legal or non‐legal age limitations have already been set for women in all Nordic countries.

There are various pros in favor of setting an upper age limit for males in ART. Among them is to lower the risk of perinatal health problems and decrease the risk of neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders in the offspring. Furthermore, it would increase the chance that children grow up with healthy parents and that they can interact with grandparents. The limited public financial resources spent on ART also call for prioritization among infertile patients where age limits for both sexes need to be discussed. Simultaneously, some cons should be taken into consideration—ageism and infertilism for males and females are some. Furthermore, there are possible health benefits related to parenthood in general, and there are societal benefits of not arbitrarily limiting the male and female reproductive period. Finally, the value of maintaining individual freedom concerning major life events such as entering parenthood for both sexes is at play. This weighing of different arguments and values is complex and calls for a broad debate and engagement to unify decision making and facilitate clinical infertility care in the Nordics and worldwide.

IF WE WANT AN AGE LIMIT FOR MALES, HOW TO SET ONE?

The exact age above which ART is limited might be seen as arbitrarily set unless justified by data. One way of setting an age limit is by agreeing on a cut‐off value for health risk in offspring above which ART is denied. For example, the risk for neurodevelopmental and psychiatric morbidity in offspring appears to be particularly significant when paternal age reaches 50–55 years.
