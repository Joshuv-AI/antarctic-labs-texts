Review of Herpes Zoster Occurrence in Patients With Atopic Dermatitis Treated With JAK Inhibitors
Authors: Anna Mandecka, Karolina Perlak, Wiktoria Bajek, Wiktor Kruczek, Aleksandra Frątczak, Karina Polak, Bartosz Miziołek, Beata Bergler‐Czop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAtopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease that markedly impairs quality of life. Janus kinase inhibitors (upadacitinib, abrocitinib, and baricitinib) are effective therapies for moderate‐to‐severe AD but are associated with viral reactivation, particularly herpes zoster (HZ), due to immunosuppressive effects on interferon signaling and natural killer cell activity. The objective of this study was to systematically evaluate the risk of HZ among AD patients treated with JAK inhibitors (JAKis), identify key risk factors, and assess the clinical outcomes and management of HZ in this population. A systematic review of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library evaluated HZ risk in adolescents and adults with moderate‐to‐severe AD treated with JAK. Searches followed PRISMA guidelines using MeSH and EMTREE terms. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251070483). Eligible English‐language studies included randomized trials, cohort studies, pooled safety analyses, and case series with ≥ 5 participants; reviews, case reports, and pediatric, animal, or in vitro studies were excluded. Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers. The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach. Eleven studies were included. The risk of HZ incidence appears higher in older patients. In some cases, the risk correlates with higher doses of JAK inhibitors, and a trend toward increased HZ events has been observed in Asian populations. HZ is a relevant but generally manageable adverse event in AD patients on JAKis. Risk stratification by age, dose, and comorbidities is advised. Ongoing real‐world monitoring is crucial for optimizing outcomes.Keywords: adverse drug reaction, atopic, dermatitis, herpes zoster, Janus kinase inhibitors, treatment outcome

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease with a high prevalence estimated to affect 10%–20% of children and 7%–10% of adults worldwide [1]. The clinical course of AD is characterized by intense pruritus, xerosis, and recurrent eczematous lesions, significantly impairing patients' quality of life [2].

The pathogenesis of AD is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and immune dysregulation [3]. A key role is attributed to mutations in the filaggrin (FLG) gene, which compromise epidermal barrier function, leading to increased transepidermal water loss and enhanced penetration of allergens and microorganisms. Disruption of the skin barrier also promotes colonization by Staphylococcus aureus , which further stimulates the inflammatory response and exacerbates the disease course [4]. This barrier dysfunction initiates an immune cascade dominated by T‐helper 2 (Th2) lymphocytes and associated cytokines such as IL‐4, IL‐13, IL‐22, thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), and IL‐31 [5, 6, 7]. Subsequently, downstream signaling is mediated through intracellular JAK/STAT pathways, which play a critical role in regulating the expressions of genes involved in inflammatory and immune responses [5, 7]. Inhibition of these pathways using JAK inhibitors (JAKi), including upadacitinib, baricitinib, and abrocitinib, effectively reduces disease activity and improves quality of life in patients with moderate‐to‐severe AD [5, 8, 9].

These agents act systemically, alleviating itch and skin lesions, and have demonstrated favorable efficacy profiles in clinical trials and long‐term safety analyses [5, 9, 10]. However, JAK–STAT pathway inhibition also affects antiviral defense mechanisms, particularly by suppressing type I and II interferon signaling and impairing the activity of natural killer (NK) cells. This immunomodulation may promote the development of viral infections and the reactivation of latent viral infections [10, 11].

Among patients with AD treated with JAKis, the most frequently diagnosed viral infections are those caused by varicella zoster virus (VZV), leading to herpes zoster (HZ) and herpes simplex virus (HSV), ranging from mild manifestations to severe HSV infection known as eczema herpeticum (EH) [6, 9, 12, 13]. Sporadic reports have also described cases of COVID‐19 among cohorts of patients receiving JAK inhibitor therapy [10].

Summary safety analyses of JAK inhibitor use in atopic dermatitis report an incidence of herpes simplex of 6.1% (95% CI: 3.9–8.6), eczema herpeticum of 1.9% (95% CI: 0.0–5.2), herpes zoster of 2.0% (95% CI: 1.0–3.3), and respiratory infections without pathogen‐specific differentiation of 6.0% (95% CI: 3.3–9.2) [14].

HZ infection has been reported as one of the most recognized viral infections associated with JAKis [15]. The incidence of HZ in patients appears to be influenced by factors such as JAKi dosage, age, disease severity, and geographic region [13, 16, 17]. Most infections are mild to moderate in severity, localized to cutaneous and single dermatome, and responsive to antiviral therapy [6, 9]. However, some studies reported events classified as opportunistic, mostly presenting as multidermatomal cutaneous infections and extracutaneous events, such as serious disseminated VZV and serious HZ meningitis, in patient cohorts [8, 9, 10].

Other opportunistic infections noted in safety analyses of JAK inhibitors were most commonly classified as eczema herpeticum, cutaneous multidermatomal herpes zoster, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, esophageal candidiasis, and disseminated tuberculosis [5, 9, 10, 16, 18].

Given the increasing use of JAK inhibitors and biologics in dermatology, especially in AD, HZ remains an important adverse event during therapy. While several previous reviews have described HZ risk with these therapies, this review provides an update including data from 2024 to 2025, examining HZ risk in patients treated with upadacitinib, abrocitinib, and baricitinib. It highlights the need for comprehensive evaluation of HZ risk and identification of high‐risk subgroups to guide possible prophylaxis.

Materials and Methods

A systematic literature review was performed using PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library databases. The research question for this systematic review was formulated using a structured framework commonly applied in evidence‐based medicine (PICO). The review focused on adult and adolescent patients (≥ 12 years) with moderate‐to‐severe atopic dermatitis treated with Janus kinase inhibitors, including upadacitinib, abrocitinib, and baricitinib. Where applicable, outcomes were compared with placebo, alternative systemic therapies, or different dosing regimens of Janus kinase inhibitors. The primary outcome assessed was the occurrence of herpes zoster infection, including its incidence, severity, clinical characteristics, and impact on treatment continuation. This structured approach informed the search strategy, study selection, and data extraction process.

The search strategy included combinations of terms such as “Atopic Dermatitis,” “Herpes Zoster,” and “JAK Inhibitors” to identify relevant publications. To maximize comprehensiveness, additional terms were used: (“Janus kinase inhibitors” OR “JAK inhibitors”) AND (“eczema” OR “dermatitis”) AND (“adverse effects” OR “treatment outcomes”). The search covered the full range of database records up to March 2025 and was carried out using both MeSH and EMTREE subject headings in accordance with PRISMA standards. The protocol was registered in the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD420251070483).

Studies were considered eligible if they were randomized controlled trials, cohort studies (prospective or retrospective), pooled safety analyses, or case series involving five or more patients and published in English. Exclusion criteria included narrative reviews, case reports with fewer than five subjects, editorial pieces, abstracts lacking methodological clarity, and studies limited to pediatric, animal, or in vitro data. The screening process followed PRISMA methodology and was independently conducted by two reviewers who assessed titles, abstracts, and full texts. Any discrepancies were resolved by discussion and consensus. The certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach.

Ultimately, 11 studies that met all eligibility requirements were included in the final analysis. Key data such as study design, patient characteristics, treatment type, and herpes zoster occurrence were extracted and compiled into a structured summary table.

Results

A total of 11 clinical studies were included in this systematic review (Figure 1. [PRISMA]). These comprised 2 randomized controlled trials, 7 integrated analyses of pooled RCT data, and 2 retrospective cohort studies. The studies collectively examined the relationship between herpes zoster occurrence and the use of Janus kinase inhibitors specifically upadacitinib, abrocitinib, and baricitinib in patients with moderate‐to‐severe atopic dermatitis. Discussion

Due to epidermal barrier defects, reduced expression of antimicrobial peptides, and alterations in both innate and adaptive immunity associated with Th2‐skewed inflammation in AD, the risk of infections such as HZ is increased [8, 21]. Furthermore, it has been suggested that the risk of serious infections elevates with disease severity [8]. JAK inhibitors are prescribed for patients with moderate‐to‐severe AD requiring systemic or advanced therapies to manage symptoms, but these patients are already at increased risk of infections [17].

One of the biologic therapies approved as first‐line treatment for moderate‐to‐severe adult AD in the United States in March 2017 and in Europe in September 2017 is dupilumab. This medication is a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting the shared α‐subunit of the interleukin‐4 and interleukin‐13 receptor [22, 23]. In a recent review by Manzar et al., dupilumab was shown to be associated with the lowest risk of herpes zoster among the compared JAKis therapies, including upadacitinib, abrocitinib, and placebo [17]. This observation is supported by the findings of Blauvelt et al., who reported that herpes zoster occurred less frequently in the dupilumab group compared with the upadacitinib group (3 [0.9%] vs. 7 [2%]). On the other hand, upadacitinib demonstrated superiority over dupilumab, achieving higher levels of skin clearance and itch relief with a more rapid onset of action [5]. Other analyses, such as the study by Katoh Norito et al., evaluating the safety profile of upadacitinib, showed that the incidence of HZ at week 16 was 0/100 PY (15 mg plus topical corticosteroids) vs. 4.4/100 PY (30 mg plus topical corticosteroids) vs. 0/100 PY (placebo plus topical treatment). At week 112, the incidence was 7.4/100 PY (15 mg) and 12.5/100 PY (30 mg). The rate of HZ was slightly higher in the upadacitinib 30‐mg group than in the 15‐mg or placebo groups [16]. For the same JAK inhibitor, Burmester et al. reported lower herpes zoster incidence rates of 3.0/100 PY for 15 mg and 5.7/100 PY for 30 mg, noting that the lack of extended placebo control arms was a study limitation [18]. Similar HZ rates were observed in patients treated with upadacitinib in the study by Hagino et al., with 2.8% in the 15‐mg group and 8.5% in the 30‐mg group; however, the study lacked a placebo‐controlled group [6]. In a review by Manzar et al., it was noted that treatment with abrocitinib 200 mg was associated with a markedly increased incidence of HZ compared to placebo (OR = 3.34 [1.34–8.31]) [17]. Simpson et al., describing a study with abrocitinib, also observed that herpes zoster rates in the placebo‐controlled cohort appeared to be dose‐related: 1.90/100 PY for 100 mg and 5.16/100 PY for 200 mg [9]. Another integrated analysis from 2024 reported that the incidence rate of HZ was 4.7/100 PY for 200 mg and 2.1/100 PY for 100 mg, suggesting a dose‐dependent effect [10]. Comparing another JAK inhibitor, baricitinib, in the study by Bieber et al., the incidence rate of HZ for all baricitinib doses was 2.8/100 PY [8]. Similarly, in the analysis by Katoh et al., the incidence rate of HZ was 2.4/100 PY [13]. In the integrated analysis by Werfel et al., there was no evidence of a dose‐dependent increase in HZ incidence [19]. The lowest risk of HZ appears to be observed in cohorts of patients treated with baricitinib.

The lower risk of HZ among patients receiving dupilumab may potentially result from its targeted action, which ameliorates epidermal barrier dysfunction. In contrast, the tendency for a higher susceptibility to HZ with upadacitinib (JAK1 inhibitor) or abrocitinib (JAK1 inhibitor) is most likely due to their immunological activity in interferon I‐ and II‐dependent pathways. These interferons are, in turn, involved in innate antiviral defense mechanisms [11, 24]. Distinctions among JAK inhibitors may be drawn based on emerging and pooled data, which suggest that inhibition of JAK2 and JAK3 is associated with a higher risk of VZV infection, whereas greater selectivity for JAK1 may confer a lower risk of herpes zoster [25]. However, these assumptions are not fully consistent with the findings for baricitinib, a dual JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor, in our review. Based on its mechanism of action, baricitinib might be expected to confer a higher risk of HZ compared with the more selective JAK1 inhibitors; however, this was not observed. Notably, differences in HZ incidence were also evident across disease indications. In the analysis by Katoh et al. involving patients with AD, the incidence rate of HZ was 2.4/100 PY, whereas in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), the study by Harigai et al. reported a substantially higher incidence rate of 6.5/100 PY [13, 26].

Summary of Risk Factors Associated With Increased HZ Risk

As noted, the incidence of herpes zoster varies across disease entities, with a higher risk observed in RA [9]. This is likely related to older age, greater disease severity, and more frequent use of systemic corticosteroids or immunosuppressants compared to patients with ad [8, 13]. The hypothesis of age‐related predisposition and dose dependence is supported by studies by Cork et al. and Simpson et al., the latter indicating that severe disease at baseline was also a risk factor. The incidence of HZ was highest among patients aged ≥ 65 years receiving 200 mg of abrocitinib, while lower incidence rates were observed in younger patients and with lower doses with ad [9, 12]. Another study involving abrocitinib confirmed the previously identified risk factors and additionally identified new ones: a history of herpes zoster, an absolute lymphocyte count < 1 × 103/mm3 prior to the event, and residing in Asia [10]. Another analysis of upadacitinib safety showed that the rates of HZ were similar across diseases (RA and AD) and increased in a dose‐dependent manner. An upward trend in HZ rates with longer exposure to upadacitinib 30 mg was observed in ad [18]. Although some studies identify age as a risk factor, Bieber et al., for baricitinib in AD, did not observe a clear trend of increasing HZ incidence with age [8]. A similar conclusion was drawn in the integrated analysis of baricitinib, which found no specific risk factors for patients with AD developing HZ. However, in RA, advancing age and region (South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan) were identified as independent risk factors for HZ [19]. It is currently unknown why herpes infections associated with JAK inhibitors differ between ethnic groups; genetic polymorphisms may be involved. One possible factor is that varicella and zoster vaccines were approved later in Japan than in Europe, which might have led to higher incidence rates through lower herd immunity and limited availability of vaccination programs [25].

Vaccination against HZ prior to initiating treatment, although recommended, is still insufficiently adopted. In integrated analyses Werfel et al. reported that only 5.4% of patients with known vaccination status were actually vaccinated [19]. Lack of vaccinations may have contributed to HZ treatment‐emergent events and led to treatment discontinuation in three patients in the study by Pezzolo et al. [20]. Several studies emphasize the importance of vaccination before starting upadacitinib or abrocitinib therapy [9, 12, 18]. Furthermore, in cases of treatment‐emergent HZ episodes, oral antiviral therapy, such as acyclovir or valacyclovir, is recommended for uncomplicated zoster and should ideally be initiated within 72 h of symptom onset. For complicated zoster and in all immunocompromised patients, intravenous acyclovir (10–15 mg/kg every 8 h) is recommended as the initial therapy and can be administered for 10–14 days, even if more than 72 h have elapsed since symptom onset [27].

Limitations of the Studies

The limitations of integrated analyses of clinical trials and cohort studies include the restricted interpretability of incidence rates (IRs), the lack of long‐term control groups, and the absence of comparative data with other treatments. Additionally, there is limited precision in assessing dose–response relationships, and small sample sizes in older age groups result in wide confidence intervals, complicating the interpretation of results. Unequal group sizes and potential underreporting of adverse events further limit the reliability of findings. Differences in inclusion criteria, patient characteristics, and exposure of specific subpopulations reduce comparability with previous studies and the robustness of data for rare events. Finally, the observational nature of some data may limit its applicability to real‐world clinical practice.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the presented studies indicate an association between the use of JAKis for the treatment of moderate‐to‐severe AD and the risk of developing HZ. Across randomized controlled trials and real‐world cohort studies, the incidence of HZ among patients treated with JAKis, including upadacitinib, abrocitinib, and baricitinib, varied depending on dose, age, baseline severity of AD, and in some cases, geographic population (e.g., Asian cohorts). However, most reported HZ cases were mild to moderate in severity, responded well to standard antiviral therapy, and rarely necessitated treatment discontinuation. These findings highlight the importance of careful patient selection, dose optimization, and monitoring, particularly in older adults. Prophylactic herpes zoster vaccination prior to initiating JAK inhibitor therapy may further reduce risk. Despite the increased incidence of HZ, JAK inhibitors remain a valuable therapeutic option for AD, offering a favorable benefit–risk profile when used judiciously.
