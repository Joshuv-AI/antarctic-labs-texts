AI-assisted videolaryngoscopy improves intubation success: results from a randomized simulation trial
Authors: Ariadna Comaniciu, David Schurter, Ajsi Kanapari, Giulia Lorenzoni, Asimina Lazaridou, Michael Thomas Ganter, Dario Gregori, Christoph Karl Hofer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVideo laryngoscopy has become an integral part of today’s airway management. Despite its advantages, loss of depth perception and increased cognitive load remain a challenge and not all airway scenarios can be successfully managed and resolved. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of larynGuide, an AI-based software integrated with the C-MAC video laryngoscope, in improving tracheal intubation success rates. This prospective, randomized, 2 × 2 crossover, simulation-based study included 74 medical professionals, 35 experienced and 39 inexperienced participants. Each participant performed tracheal intubations using a C-MAC video laryngoscope with and without the larynGuide AI overlay in both easy and difficult airway scenarios. The primary outcome was the first attempt success rate. Secondary outcomes included overall success rate, time to intubation, procedural performance scores, and incidence of dental injury. Data were analyzed using mixed effects models with binomial response and non-parametric tests as appropriate. The overall first attempt success rate was 89% with larynGuide and 85% with standard C-MAC, the odds of successful intubation were 72% higher with larynGuide (OR = 1.72, 95%CI = 1.08, 2.73; p = 0.022). Among experienced participants, the benefit was more pronounced (OR = 3.37, 95%CI = 1.2, 8.9; p = 0.016). Time to intubation was longer with larynGuide (median = 29 s) compared to standard C-MAC (median = 23 s, p < 0.01). The incidence of dental damage was lower with larynGuide (22%) compared to C-MAC (27%), although not statistically significant (p = 0.095). The larynGuide AI-powered software improved tracheal intubation success rates compared to standard C-MAC videolaryngoscopy in this simulation-based study, highlighting the potential role of AI in enhancing airway management. Further clinical studies are needed to confirm these results and support broader integration into practice.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06657417.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10877-026-01431-1.

Introduction

Video laryngoscopy has gained tremendous popularity in recent years and has become an integral part of the standard airway management toolkit in specialties such as anaesthesia, intensive care and emergency medicine. Recent studies have provided substantial support for the use of video laryngoscopy over direct laryngoscopy, demonstrating a safer risk profile and greater effectiveness in adult tracheal intubation in different users, patient populations and clinical settings [1, 2].

Video laryngoscopy offers several key advantages, including higher first-attempt intubations [3], a key factor in reducing morbidity and mortality associated with tracheal intubation [4–6]. It also lowers failure rates, reduces intubation difficulty [7–10], minimizes the frequency of esophageal intubations [11], and provides better visualization of the glottis ( 2,7). In addition, video laryngoscopy has proven to be an invaluable tool in the management of both anticipated and unanticipated situations of a difficult airway [9, 12]. Consequently, video laryngoscopy has become an integral part of airway management protocols in healthcare institutions around the world.

Despite these benefits, even with advanced airway devices and guidelines, failure to manage the airway can still lead to life-threatening complications [13–15]. To reduce these risks, airway management guidelines are continually updated to reflect the latest advances in medical technology. A major problem with the use of video laryngoscopy is the two-dimensional screen, which results in a loss of depth perception. Moreover, the cognitive load is increased during its use due to the simultaneous manipulation of several tools [16]. Recent technological advances addressing these issues have led to promising medical innovations. Lately, studies have been exploring the application of artificial intelligence [17] and robotic technology in airway management to improve the safety and precision of intubation. However, these AI-assisted airway devices are still in the early prototype phase [18].

larynGuide is a newly developed AI-powered software for Macintosh blade video laryngoscopes. It guides the user through the correct placement of the laryngoscope blade and the endotracheal tube using audio-visual signals and warns of incorrect or potentially dangerous maneuvers. So far, this new AI-assisted intubation technology has never been evaluated, used by clinicians or compared with standard video laryngoscopy.

This study aims to evaluate the usability and performance of larynGuide AI-powered software when used with a C-MAC video laryngoscope.

Methods

This simulation-based intubation study using a manikin was designed as a prospective, randomized, two-group, two-stage crossover trial (Fig. 1) and was conducted following ethical approval. The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06657417) on Sept. 3rd 2024, and the findings are reported in accordance with STROBE and CONSORT-AI guidelines. There are no conflicts of interest to be disclosed.

Participants

Seventy-four volunteer medical professionals participated in the study, comprising 35 experienced and 39 inexperienced individuals. The experienced group consisted of consultant anaesthetists, anaesthesia residents, and certified nurse anaesthetists, each with a history of at least 200 endotracheal intubations. The inexperienced group included junior doctors (excluding anaesthetists), clinical-year medical students, nurses, and paramedics, all of whom had performed fewer than 200 tracheal intubations. Prior to the intervention, participants completed a questionnaire about their professional experience in airway management, which also included an informed consent form explaining the aims of the study and the methods of data collection. To ensure uniformity among participants, all individuals, regardless of earlier intubation experience, received standardised didactic instruction on airway anatomy, proper use of the C-MAC video laryngoscope, and handling of Laerdal airway manikins via a short instructional video. Participants were not allowed to test the software prior to the actual study begin.

Technology of larynGuide

A new Artificial Intelligence (AI) based software called larynGuide (Version 0.1, aiEndoscopic; Zurich, Switzerland) has been developed as an overlay for the C-MAC video laryngoscope software. This overlay provides maneuver instructions to the operator directly on the video laryngoscope screen. The larynGuide software backend implements a proprietary AI model along with AI interpretation software that performs real-time analysis of the incoming video stream. The software was trained for pattern recognition using numerous intubation recordings from different users, using both proper and improper technique, in easy and difficult scenarios with the same C-MAC and Laerdal manikin as in this trial. This real-time analysis enables the software to identify anatomical landmarks (e.g. uvula, epiglottis, vocal cords) and draw bounding boxes around them. It provides instructions to the user through graphical, written and auditory cues via the graphical user interface (see Fig. 2). The technical AI model performance anatomical structure detection was assessed using standard computer-vision metrics. The mean average precision (mAP@50) assesses correct detection and localization of anatomical structures while the F1 score reflects the balance between sensitivity and precision. The model achieved a mAP@50 of 0.77 and an F1 score of 0.75, indicating good overall accuracy. The source code underlying the larynGuide algorithm is proprietary and owned by aiEndoscopic and is therefore not publicly available.

Study procedures

Each participant attended two experimental sessions. In one control session (C session), they performed standard videolaryngoscopy using a C-MAC video laryngoscope MAC #3 (Karl Storz Endoscopy, Tuttlingen, Germany). In the other session, the same C-MAC video laryngoscope was used with the additional larynGuide overlay (L session, Fig. 1). To reduce carry-over and period bias, the two simulation sessions (Sequence 1 and Sequence 2) were spaced one week apart. The session order was randomized using sealed envelope technique determining group allocation, which was performed just before start of the procedure. All procedures were conducted under the supervision of an investigator experienced with both protocol and equipment. The investigator remained neutral towards the participants and offered no assistance or instructions beyond those provided in the instructional video. The supervisor ensured that the procedures and time limits were adhered to. Each session consisted of six intubations: three consecutive intubations in an easy airway scenario followed by three in a difficult airway scenario. The Laerdal Airway Management Trainer manikin (Laerdal, Stavanger, Norway) was used for both scenarios. In the normal airway scenario, the participants optimized the positioning of the intubation by placing the head in the sniffing position. For the difficult airway scenario, the manikin’s neck was immobilized with a stiff neck collar (adult size Ambu Perfit ACE Ambu, Ballerup Sogn, Danmark) to simulate reduced neck extension and restricted mouth opening. In addition, for the difficult airway scenario, the endotracheal tube (Shiley™ Hi-Lo Oral/Nasal Endotracheal Tube Cuffed, Murphy Eye, inner diameter of 7.0 mm; Medtronic, Medtronic Parkway, Minneapolis, MN) was preloaded with a rigid stylet (Shiley Intubation Stylet, 4.7 mm; Medtronic, Medtronic Parkway, Minneapolis, MN) by the supervisor. Before each intubation, the manikin, laryngoscope blade and endotracheal tube were lubricated by the supervisor.

Primary outcomes

An intubation was classified successful if it was performed within 60 s, if the tip of the ETT (Shiley™ Hi-Lo Oral/Nasal Endotracheal Tube Cuffed, Murphy Eye, 7.0 mm internal diameter) passed through the vocal cords, and if it took no more than three attempts to complete (an intubation attempt was defined as entry and exit of the laryngoscope from the oral cavity without tube placement through the vocal cords). If participants needed more than three attempts, the procedure was terminated by the supervisor. However, participants were instructed to stop the procedure only if it took longer than 120 s, so that the first attempt success rate and overall success rate could be measured without time constraints.

Secondary outcomes

Intubation time was measured from the moment the laryngoscope entered the oral cavity until the tip of the endotracheal tube (ETT) successfully passed through the vocal cords. Each intubation was recorded on video from two perspectives: an internal view through the VL camera and an external view showing the hand movements of the operator, without compromising their anonymity. The procedural performance was analysed by two independent investigators with thorough intubation knowledge. The process was single-blinded, meaning the evaluators were not aware of what software the subjects were using as they evaluated their procedures. The evaluators used the Global Rating Scale of Procedural Performance to score the instrument handling, flow and knowledge of procedure for each intubation on a grade scale from 1 to 5. In a second run of the video footage dental clicks representing dental damage were counted. At the end of each session, participants were asked to complete a usability questionnaire. This questionnaire was used to assess their subjective individual performance.

Power analysis

Sample size estimation was performed following a meta-analysis of previous studies which reported a pooled first-attempt intubation failures with video-assisted laryngoscopy, which suggests a pooled proportion of 26% failures with video-assisted procedures. Accordingly, the null hypothesis assumed a success rate of 74% for both the control and intervention groups. To detect a clinically meaningful difference of 16.5% in success rates between groups, a total sample size of 74 participants was determined to achieve 80% power at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05. Calculations were performed using the TrialSize R package 1.4 (R-Core Team, https://cran.r-project.org/ 2024).

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were presented in tabular format, reporting overall values as well as stratified by group. Continuous variables were summarized with medians followed by interquartile range (IQR: 25th and 75th percentiles), and categorical variables were summarized with absolute corresponding percentages (%). To evaluate differences in failure proportions while accounting for repeated measures within participants, generalized linear mixed-effects models with a binomial distribution were employed. Interaction terms were included where relevant to assess differential effects across subgroups. Model results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and corresponding p-values. Comparisons of continuous outcomes between settings were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (paired data) and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (unpaired data). Categorical comparisons used the McNemar’s test for paired data and chi-squared tests for independent samples. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with adjusted p-values reported as q-values. To evaluate learning effects over time, mixed-effects models with interaction terms were applied, using the cumulative number of intubations performed as a continuous predictor. For all analyses, significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed) All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2 (R-Core Team, https://cran.r-project.org/ 2024).

Ethics and Ethical Approval

Ethical approval

was obtained from the ethic committee Zurich (Kantonale Ethikkommission Zürich – KEK), BASEC-ID Req-2023-00896. All data were handled according to the Swiss Data Protection Act. The study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki declaration.

Results

All 74 participants completed the entire study (43.2% female and 56.8% male participants). All individuals in the experienced group were anesthesia professionals, who were older, had significantly more years of professional experience, and regularly performed intubations in their clinical practice. (Table 1).

The total first attempt success rate was 89% with the larynGuide video laryngoscope and 85% with the C-MAC (Table 2). The odds of a successful intubation using the larynGuide were 72% higher compared to C-MAC (OR = 1.72, 95%CI = 1.08–2.73, p = 0.022). Both inexperienced and experienced participants showed increased success rates with the larynGuide video laryngoscope, however this difference was mainly driven by performance in the easy scenario while it was less noticeable in the difficult scenario (Table 2; Fig. 3). The effect was more pronounced and reached statistical significance in the experienced group (OR = 3.37, 95%CI = 1.2 = 8.9, p = 0.016). Over time, intubation success rates improved, i.e. one additional intubation increased the odds of a successful intubation by 22% (OR = 1.22, 95%CI = 1.14–2.31, p < 0.001). Performance improved with both larynGuide and C-MAC, with a more pronounced gain seen in the C-MAC group; however, initial success rates remained higher with the larynGuide (Fig. 4).

Time to intubation was longer with use of the larynGuide video laryngoscope as compared to the C-MAC use (Table 3; Fig. 5). This effect was predominantly seen in the experienced group in both, the easy and difficult scenario. Time to intubation decreased with repeated attempts, with a more pronounced improvement observed in inexperienced group (Fig. 6). On average any additional intubation in the sequence was associated with a decrease of 1.96 s (p < 0.001).

The Global Rating Scale (GRS) did not reveal a statistically significant difference between the C-MAC (median: 4.0, IQR: 3.0–5.0) and the larynGuide (median: 4.0, IQR: 3.0–5.0; p = 0.35), both overall and within the inexperienced and experienced subgroups. Subjective individual performance ratings differed between groups, with the inexperienced participants reporting a median score of 3.5 (IQR: 3.0–5.0) and the experienced participants a higher median of 4.0 (IQR: 4.0–5.0), a statistically significant difference (p = 0.035). Incidence of dental damage was 27% for the C-MAC and 22% for the larynGuide device (p = 0.095), The rate was higher for the inexperienced group (C-MAC = 33%, larynGuide = 28%), than for the experienced group (C-MAC = 19%, larynGuide = 14%), although without statistical difference.

Discussion

This study aimed at evaluating the effectiveness of larynGuide, an AI-based software integrated with the C-MAC video laryngoscope, in improving tracheal intubation success rates. Main findings showed that larynGuide significantly improved intubation success rates. In addition, there was a notable trend towards reduced dental trauma, although the time required for intubation was slightly longer when using larynGuide.To our knowledge, this study was the first to evaluate the performance of larynGuide, an AI-based software integrated with the C-MAC video laryngoscope, in simulated intubation on a mannequin.

The first attempt success rate of a laryngoscopy device has a direct impact on the outcome of tracheal intubation, influencing both morbidity and mortality, making it a critical metric in the evaluation of video laryngoscopes [13–15]. Reported success rates range from 73% to 100% [19–25] and vary by device type, procedural setting, user experience and airway complexity. The success rate of the C-MAC video laryngoscope has improved significantly, from 73% when first tested by clinicians experienced in direct laryngoscopy [19] to up to 100% in more recent studies [24]. This trend reflects the completion of the learning curve as the device becomes integrated into routine practice [26, 27]. According to a meta-analysis by de Carvalho et al. [2], the pooled first attempt success rate for video laryngoscopy across different patient populations and levels of intubation difficulty is approximately 94%. In our study, the success rates were 85% with C-MAC and 89% with larynGuide overlay, slightly lower but within the expected range. Depending on airway difficulty, success rates reported in the literature range from 94% [20, 21] to 100% [24, 28] in normal airways and from 79% to 99% [3, 8, 29] in difficult airways. Experienced users consistently achieve near perfect success rates in both scenarios [21–24]. Our results also highlight the performance gap between experienced and less experienced operators, which is largely attributed to the importance of procedural expertise, motor memory and cumulative experience. While direct laryngoscopy skills provide a foundation, video laryngoscopy requires specialized techniques such as refined hand-eye coordination, image interpretation and troubleshooting [26, 27]. In our clinic, C-MAC has been a routine part of practice for years, allowing experienced clinicians to achieve almost 100% success rates. In contrast, our inexperienced participants ranged from novices to those with up to 200 intubations, probably spanning the learning curve of video laryngoscopy [26]. Definitions of ‘novice’ and ‘inexperienced’ vary between studies, making comparisons difficult, but recent reports show first attempt success rates of 90%-100% for normal airways [21, 28] and 75%-92% for difficult airways [25, 28], consistent with our results. Typically, higher success rates are expected in normal airways, but practice and research show nuances. Studies of video laryngoscopy often show considerable heterogeneity in design, definitions of success and procedural details. Interestingly, our study found a higher first attempt success rate in difficult airways than in easy airways, possibly due to a session learning effect as difficult cases were performed later. In addition, the lack of a significant difference between larynGuide and C-MAC may be due to the high success rates in the difficult airway scenario, where a larger sample size would be required to detect subtle differences.

One of the core principles of laryngoscopy practice is to secure the airway as quickly as possible and to minimize intubation time, particularly in patients with apnea intolerance or in emergency intubations. However, there are currently no established guidelines that specify an exact time limit for intubation, given the wide range of patient, environmental and equipment factors that need to be considered. As a result, reported intubation times for video laryngoscopy vary widely, ranging from 9.8 to 39 s in the literature [2, 30]. This wide range is further influenced by differences in the definition of intubation time between studies. For example, the starting point may be marked by either initial contact with the laryngoscope or the moment it enters the mouth, whereas the endpoint may be defined as visualization of the endotracheal tube passing through the vocal cords or the appearance of a capnography waveform. Intubation times for both normal and difficult airways typically range from 20 to 40 s when reviewing comparable studies using the C-MAC video laryngoscopy [19, 31]. Our results are consistent with these reported intervals. As expected, experienced subjects demonstrated faster, and more efficient performance compared to their less experienced counterparts. In particular, use of the larynGuide software resulted in longer intubation times. We attribute this, at least in part, to the increased cognitive load imposed by this new device during the familiarization period - a challenge similar to that faced by clinicians when video laryngoscopy was first introduced into practice. Over time, intubation times decreased, reflecting a learning curve as subjects became more familiar with both the video laryngoscopy and the manikins. This learning effect was particularly pronounced in the inexperienced group, many of whom were in the early stages of their learning curve (Fig. 6). On average, each additional intubation in the sequence was associated with a significant decrease of 1.96 s.

Our study demonstrated a clear procedural learning curve in both groups of subjects, as evidenced by improved success rates at the first attempt, shorter intubation times, and higher scores on objective measures of performance (Figs. 4 and 6). Achieving a successful intubation at the first attempt, and doing so as quickly as possible, is of critical importance in the clinical setting. Continued efforts to achieve a 100% first-pass success rate are essential, as failed intubation attempts can compromise the success of subsequent attempts, even if performed by a more experienced clinician or with a different device [32]. In addition, multiple intubation attempts are associated with an increased risk of adverse events such as dental trauma, cuff leak, laryngospasm, arrhythmias and hypotension [6, 33, 34]. Thus, even small improvements in initial success rates and intubation time may result in a clinically meaningful reduction in morbidity and mortality associated with tracheal intubation. With regard to adverse events, while there is considerable research on dental damage associated with direct laryngoscopy, data on dental trauma associated with video laryngoscopy are more limited. Existing studies suggest that video laryngoscopy is associated with less dental damage than direct laryngoscopy [27], probably due to the reduced force applied to the teeth [27, 35]. Reported incidences of video laryngoscopy related dental trauma range from 0% to 3% [23, 29], but these results are from clinical trials. In contrast, our study showed a significantly higher incidence of dental damage: 27% in the C-MAC group and 22% in the larynGuide group. A possible explanation for this increased rate is the more relaxed and possibly less cautious behavior of subjects when working with manikins, as they may subconsciously know that there is no real risk of harm, unlike when performing on live patients.

The ‘video revolution’ in laryngoscopy has highlighted the transformative potential of video technology in airway management. This study now suggests that the AI-based larynGuide software may further advance this revolution. It highlights the emerging role of AI and robotic innovations in improving procedural safety and outcomes. The real-time feedback and guidance provided by the larynGuide demonstrates how AI can enhance the traditional benefits of video laryngoscopy, resulting in higher success rates and potentially lower complication rates. larynGuide addresses some of the limitations of traditional video laryngoscopy, such as loss of depth perception and increased cognitive load. To optimize the AI interface and further reduce cognitive load, continued technological refinement is required. Incorporating user feedback into the development process is crucial to ensure that the system is intuitive and easy to use. In addition, future research should explore the integration of AI with other advanced technologies, such as augmented reality, to further improve the accuracy and safety of airway management. The integration of AI into medical devices also raises important ethical and legal considerations. The development of regulatory frameworks and guidelines for the use of AI in healthcare is essential to ensure patient safety and the ethical application of this technology.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the study was conducted in a simulated environment, which does not capture the complexity of human anatomy, and the variability found in real clinical settings. Moreover, the AI model was trained and evaluated exclusively on manikin video input; real airways introduce secretions, blood, and tissue variability, and performance may degrade under such domain shift. Retrospective analysis of clinical intubation videos however shows that the model performs well in humans (unpublished data outside of this study). Should the anatomical recognition fail due to domain shift or poor-quality input data, the model is expected to rather withhold output than to provide incorrect instructions. Although no systematic error analysis was conducted, the system was evaluated by experienced clinicians prior to the study, and all intubations were supervised by a trained investigator. No misinterpretations of visualized anatomy or erroneous guidance were observed in the simulated context of this study. Second, the generalizability of the results is limited by the relatively small sample size of the study. A larger sample size would be needed to confirm the results and draw more robust conclusions. Additionally, the study cohort consisted of volunteers, who may exhibit higher motivation and engagement than a representative clinical population, introducing potential volunteer bias. This may also limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, the study did not stratify or analyze potential performance variability across different healthcare provider roles, such as paramedics, nurses, and physicians, which could influence the applicability of the results to broader clinical settings.

Collectively, this simulation-based study demonstrates that the use of the larynGuide AI-assisted video laryngoscopy system significantly improves first-attempt tracheal intubation success rates compared to standard video laryngoscopy. These findings suggest that AI-guided technologies may enhance procedural safety and effectiveness in airway management. However, further validation in clinical environments is necessary to confirm these results and to assess the practical utility and scalability of AI-enhanced laryngoscopy in routine practice.
