Early dyadic interventions for critically ill neonates: a scoping review
Authors: Mariana C. Aokalani, Louisa Flynn, Anniette Maldonado, Katherine L. Wisner, Catherine Limperopoulos, Nickie N. Andescavage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly dyadic interventions improve parental mental health, infant development, and parent-infant relationships. We reviewed and synthesized existing research on early dyadic interventions with a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) inpatient component, with an emphasis on psychotherapeutic approaches. We mapped the available evidence and identified gaps in the literature. Followed PRISMA-ScR reporting guidelines.MethodsSearch engines used:PubMed, MEDLINE, MEDLINE Plus, EBSCO, Cochrane Central, Google Scholar, and Science.gov. Key search terms included “dyadic interventions in the NICU,” NICU dyadic health,” “attachment-focused interventions in NICU, ‘neonatal intensive care unit, “Early Relational interventions” and “relational interventions in the NICU.” English papers between 2014 and 2024. Feasibility, physical and/or mental health, and relational outcome measures were included. Nine of the 2331 papers reviewed met the inclusion criteria. Extracted author, year of publication, intervention type, study design, sample characteristics, location outcomes, mental health intervention, and the number of early dyadic interventions included.ResultsAll early dyadic interventions improved parents’ mental health, infant neurodevelopment, and parent-infant relationships. We found substantial heterogeneity across included studies in terms of study design, sample characteristics, operationalization of dyadic intervention, and outcome measures. However, most interventions were not psychotherapy; more detailed research is needed to understand the long-term impact of early psychotherapeutic dyadic interventions.ConclusionInfant and Early Childhood dyadic interventions have yet to be studied in the inpatient NICU setting. This highlights a critical need for research to study the feasibility and impact of infant mental health dyadic interventions for this vulnerable population in this setting.

Introduction

Admission to a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) can profoundly affect caregiver mental health, disrupt parent-infant relationships, and threaten infant development. These hospitalizations are associated with substantial psychosocial stress, with contributing factors such as early separation, disrupted parental roles, limited involvement in care, and physical distance from the infant, collectively increasing the likelihood for impaired dyadic interactions [1–3]. Among parents of infants requiring NICU admission, there is a 20–30% increase in the incidence of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and anxiety relative to the general perinatal population [4–6].

Disrupted parent-infant relationships in the NICU are often both a consequence and contributor to caregiver psychological distress, forming a negative feedback loop that jeopardizes long-term outcomes for both parent and infant [7]. NICU-hospitalized infants are at a sixfold increased risk for attachment disorders by age 36 months, which are associated with lasting academic, social, and emotional difficulties [8, 9]. These infants also face elevated risks for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), depression, anxiety, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), and personality disorders [10, 11]. Research suggests that early disruptions in the parent-infant attachment relationship may amplify vulnerabilities in self-regulation, attention, and social-emotional development—core domains implicated in the emergence of neurodevelopmental disorders such as ADHD, ASD, and ODD [12, 13]. Although these disorders are multifactorial in origin, early relational experiences may play a modulatory role in shaping developmental trajectories in at-risk populations [10, 11]. The elevated prevalence of psychiatric and developmental disorders from infancy through adolescence underscores the urgency of targeted, early interventions that support both parents and infants beginning in the NICU [14].

Early dyadic interactions between parent and newborn are foundational to the socio-emotional, cognitive, and regulatory development of the infant [14]. Early dyadic interactions can occur in inpatient and outpatient settings. NICU-related separation disrupts emotional connection and bonding [15], and attachment [16], while also limiting access to nurturing interactions that serve as buffers against toxic stress [17]. This disruption impairs NICU-related separation, disrupts emotional connection, bonding, maternal sensitivity, and infant co-regulation capacity, compounding stress within the dyad [3, 18]. In this review, dyadic interventions are defined as structured interventions that explicitly engage both caregiver and infant with explicit intent to support relational reciprocity, emotional availability, co-regulation, and early attachment processes. Although some dyadic interventions have shown promise in NICU settings, they are not consistently implemented as a standard of care [14] and evidence supporting their effectiveness remains methodologically heterogeneous. There is a pressing need to identify, standardize, and integrate effective dyadic interventions into routine NICU practice to support early emerging and critically important parent-infant relationships.

This scoping review aims to assess the presence and efficacy of early dyadic interventions in NICU settings that target parent-infant relationships. Certain well-established dyadic interventions, such as Family Nurture Intervention (FNI), infant-directed singing, and Kangaroo Care (KC), have consistently demonstrated benefits to caregivers and/or infants [19]. Given the extensive existing literature on these interventions, we synthesized these approaches through high-quality systematic reviews and meta-analyses for the purpose of this review, with opportunity to expand on lesser-known approaches.This article is written in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) reporting checklist. Our goals are to: (1) synthesize existing research; (2) map the current landscape of dyadic interventions; and (3) identify potential opportunities for innovation. Guided by the Patient/Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) framework [20], we posed the following research question:Among infants hospitalized in the NICU and their caregivers (P), what is the impact of early dyadic interventions (I), compared to standard NICU care (C), on parent-infant attachment, caregiver mental health, and infant developmental outcomes (O)?

Methodology

We used a five-step protocol adapted from the conventions of Arksey and O’Malley [21]. We aimed to: 1) identify research questions, 2) identify relevant studies, 3) select studies, 4) chart the data, and 5) collate/summarize and report the findings. We aimed to: 1) synthesize and share research findings, 2) examine the state of research on NICU dyadic interventions, and 3) highlight gaps in the literature [22].

Data collection process and data Items

For data extraction, we utilized a modified form based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist [23] (see Fig. 1). We extracted data that included the author (s), publication year, study aims, study design, location, sample, number of early dyadic interventions described, and key findings or outcomes. Selection process

In November 2024, three reviewers searched for relevant publications. Search were conducted across seven search engines: Medline, Medline Plus, EBSCO, Cochrane Central, PubMed, Google Scholar, and Science.gov. We used the following keywords and phrases a “dyadic interventions AND NICU or neonatal intensive care unit,” “attachment-focused interventions AND NICU or “neonatal intensive care unit”, “early relational intervention”, “Dyadic NICU”, and “relational interventions” AND “NICU or neonatal intensive care unit.” Stand-alone or multimodal dyadic interventions in NICUs from English peer-reviewed articles, published between 2014 and 2024, were included. Quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods papers were eligible. Articles retrieved via database searches were reviewed for duplication. Three reviewers independently screened database search results, titles, and abstracts to determine inclusion for full-text review and then assessed the eligibility of each inclusion. Non-dyadic interventions, which were primarily psychoeducational and did not target the parent-infant relationship, poorly defined interventions (for example, the dyadic component of the intervention was not described), with poorly defined or absent methodology, and non-English publications were excluded. Any disagreements or uncertainties regarding study selection were flagged and resolved through team discussion with senior researchers and consensus. Because this review included systematic reviews and meta-analyses of well-established early dyadic interventions, we limited the number of articles focused exclusively on the FNI, KC, and infant-directed singing that were returned to minimize redundancy and to maintain a focused synthesis on NICU dyadic interventions that explicitly target the qualitative, reciprocal, and psychotherapeutic dimensions of the caregiver–infant relationship.

Data charting process and data synthesis

We utilized Microsoft Excel as a data extraction tool to capture, analyze, and interpret the collected data, and assessed whether the selected full-text articles met the study’s inclusion criteria. Data was mapped into major themes. Then grouped according to interventions, psychotherapeutic components, aims, study design, sample size, outcomes, and study location, as location may represent unique aspects of healthcare systems that may influence generalizability.

Thematic synthesis of findings

The results of the nine papers were grouped into two major themes: 1) feasibility and 2) outcomes of dyadic early intervention. Each central theme was then organized into sub-themes. After analyzing the findings, we identified gaps in dyadic early intervention. We also separated the types of dyadic intervention into psychotherapeutic and non-psychotherapeutic, to emphasize the central target of the dyadic intervention. Non-psychotherapeutic dyadic interventions (e.g., KC) may have a secondary effect on caregivers’ mental health, but do not assess psychological interpersonal dynamics, nor use mental health interventions and generally consist of manualized, evidence-based interventions. In contrast, psychotherapeutic dyadic interventions target interpersonal dynamics resulting from psychological factors, addressing caregivers’ mental health as a primary outcome through mental health interventions (e.g., cognitive reframing) [29]. Not all articles included manualized, evidence-based interventions.

Addressing bias and limitations

To ensure close adherence to pre-established inclusion and exclusion criteria, three reviewers independently screened the search results. All screening steps were documented. Any disagreements or uncertainties regarding study selection were flagged and resolved through team discussion and consensus. To mitigate the potential for reviewer bias in interpreting study quality or synthesizing findings, PRISMA guidelines and structured Excel templates were used. Study data and interpretations were determined by consensus among reviewers. The literature search was conducted without language restrictions. Non-English articles identified through the search were excluded during the screening phase due to language constraints. As a result, non-English studies did not meet the final inclusion criteria, which may have limited the comprehensiveness of this review and the generalizability of the findings.

Results

We organized findings from the nine included studies into two overarching themes. The first theme, feasibility and acceptability of the interventions (subdivided into barriers, facilitators, family engagement, and staff feedback). The second theme, outcomes of dyadic early interventions (subdivided into outcomes for caregivers, infants, and parent-infant relationship).

Study selection

From 4877 initial articles returned across engines, 2671 were duplicated. Thus, 2331 papers were screened for title and abstract, resulting in the removal of 2304 papers. Of these, 1752 studies did not refer to early dyadic interventions, and 550 did not occur in the ICU. Of the remaining 27 documents that underwent full-text review, seven had non-existent or poorly described methods sections, four were outpatient interventions, and three did not describe early dyadic interventions. Four were excluded due to both setting (not in the ICU) and intervention (non-early dyadic interventions). In total, nine reports met the inclusion criteria (see PRISMA flow diagram in Fig. 1).

Characteristics of the included systematic reviews

From the nine papers reviewed, four were either a scoping review (n = 2), meta-analysis (n = 1), or systematic review (n = 1). The remaining reports included one case presentation, one randomized controlled trial, one exploratory mixed-methods study, one prospective single-group non-randomized study, and a qualitative feasibility trial. Most reports were conducted within the United States (6 out of 9), while one did not specify the location of the intervention. The sample sizes varied considerably; the review articles ranged from 11 to 24 individual studies reported within the review publication, and the original science reports included between 13 and 311 participants. Of the nine papers reviewed, four included mental health interventions, of which two were exclusively focused on mental health interventions, and five were non-psychotherapeutic dyadic interventions. From those five papers, a myriad of interventions were assessed (see Table 1: Study Characteristics), including two reports inclusive of FNI and three of KC.

Characteristics of the intervention

From the nine papers included in this scoping review, five papers discussed early dyadic interventions. These included different types of psychotherapeutic interventions (e.g., infant mental health, infant-parent psychotherapy, supporting play exploration, and early development intervention) (see Fig. 2). No papers reported data on parent-infant attachment, traumatic parental experience, or vulnerable infant syndrome.

Early dyadic intervention feasibility and acceptability

Five of the reviewed articles (see [4, 24] Lakatos et al. [3, 19, 26])] directly addressed the feasibility and acceptability of the interventions. Two articles examined the general feasibility and acceptability of the interventions; one paper investigated the facilitators and barriers to their implementation, another analyzed family engagement, and the fifth explored feedback from participants and medical staff.

Discussion

We conducted a scoping review to synthesize and map the current landscape of dyadic interventions. By doing so, we identified potential opportunities for innovation. Below, we highlight key findings that emerged from this review, existing gaps in early dyadic interventions, limitations, and future directions.

Key findings

Early dyadic interventions are feasible and positively impact parents, infants, and their interactions. Infants involved in music therapy, multimodal stimulation, and KC had improved cognitive, motor, and emotional development. Parents experienced significant reductions in anxiety, enhancing their parenting quality and relationships with their infants. Notable relational benefits, including improved bonding, caregiving, and co-regulation, were observed in interventions like FNI and KC. Participants also appreciated tailored programs that clarified infant behaviors (e.g., self-regulation, active sleep) and addressed cultural, familial, and logistical barriers (e.g., home visits, telehealth options). However, challenges such as financial costs, time constraints, healthcare provider workload, and parental discomfort or anxiety were also identified.

The effectiveness and fidelity of early dyadic interventions varied across studies. Most studies demonstrated benefits only on short-term outcomes, with limited evidence for sustained impact on long-term infant or caregiver wellbeing. Moreover, fidelity of implementation was rarely tracked, and there is a lack of standardized protocols across interventions, making it difficult to compare results and replicate findings.

While findings for infant outcomes (hospitalization length, neurodevelopmental functioning, behavioral indicators) were inconsistent across studies, caregiver well-being and attachment measures demonstrated greater consistency. To attribute an explanation for this variety, more information was needed about implementation variations across studies and participant characteristics. Further, the lack of a standardized protocol for assessing outcomes could explain these discrepancies. Strong staff relationships, communication, and a shared understanding of the program’s benefits facilitated implementation. However, staff highlighted the need for more communication between the medical and IMH teams regarding family clinical progress. IMH and ICU staff collaboration was especially valued for its role in normalizing parental experiences and fostering healthy attachment.

Gaps in early dyadic intervention literature

Although there were positive results regarding the feasibility of early dyadic intervention programs, this scoping review indicates that the sustainability and long-term impact of these interventions remain unclear. Further studies are needed to evaluate how these programs can be consistently implemented and maintained in diverse healthcare settings. While several interventions have shown positive effects on infant development, there remains a gap in understanding the specific, long-term effects of various interventions (e.g., music therapy, KC) on infant health outcomes, particularly regarding neurodevelopmental milestones over time.

Only a few studies explored early psychotherapeutic dyadic interventions. Early dyadic interventions are rarely integrated into the ICU care model, especially for families with fewer healthcare resources, such as those with Medicaid, transportation, or other logistical issues, which impact access to early dyadic interventions. There is limited information regarding both short-term and long-term outcomes for parents, infants, and their relationships. Data about the adaptations made to the ICU setting of evidence-based interventions is lacking. Only a few family-centered psychotherapeutic dyadic interventions were offered in ICU and did not address other caregivers aside from biological mothers. Limited information is available about early dyadic interventions in inpatient settings compared with different settings or a combination of settings, such as inpatient and home visits. Lastly, the lack of standardization for psychotherapeutic intervention protocols necessitates the identification of optimal implementation, feasibility, and acceptability guidelines.

There is limited assessment of the sustained impact of dyadic interventions beyond the immediate post-intervention period, which is critical for understanding whether observed benefits translate into meaningful, long-term improvements in parents, infants, and the parent–infant relationship. Although longitudinal research requires substantial investment to support follow-up, assessing outcomes at developmentally relevant timepoints (e.g., post-discharge, infancy, toddlerhood) is essential to determine whether intervention effects persist and to guide clinical uptake. Few studies have employed multi-level approaches to capture these effects. A comprehensive assessment could integrate self-report, physiological, behavioral observation, and neural measures across the three levels. For parental mental health and attachment representations, established self-report questionnaires or semi-structured interviews can measure symptoms of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress. For infant mental health and development, parent-report questionnaires and standardized neurodevelopmental assessments provide robust indicators of socio-emotional and cognitive outcomes. Dyadic interactions and relationship quality can be evaluated using gold-standard relational assessments. Together, these measures offer a multidimensional framework, guiding researchers and clinicians to understand whether intervention benefits are lasting, which in turn can inform intervention refinement and increase confidence in adopting these approaches in practice.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this scoping review is the first to systematically synthesize psychotherapeutic dyadic interventions in the NICU. We uniquely disentangle feasibility from acceptability findings across studies, allowing for a clearer interpretation of implementation-related outcomes. In doing so, we identify critical gaps—including the absence of trauma-informed, infant mental health–focused models—thereby outlining clear priorities for future research.

Although this scoping review provides a summary of the existing literature on early dyadic interventions, it is essential to acknowledge potential limitations. Despite the independent selection by three reviewers, residual selection bias may remain. Due to language fluency, the search was restricted to English-language studies and does not include studies published in other languages. Lastly, the small sample sizes of many studies limit the generalizability of their findings. Future research should involve larger, more diverse samples and adhere to methodological rigor by clearly detailing intervention protocols, participant characteristics, and outcome measures to strengthen the evidence base. Transparent reporting of intervention fidelity and outcomes will help ensure consistency across studies and enhance the credibility of the evidence supporting these interventions.

An additional limitation inherent to the existing literature is the difficulty of isolating the effects of specific dyadic intervention modalities from the relational processes they activate. Dyadic interventions such as music therapy, Kangaroo Care, infant massage, and Family Nurture Intervention vary widely in structure, dosage, and setting, are often implemented alongside other supportive practices, and likely share overlapping mechanistic pathways and therapeutic benefits. For example, many of these interventions engage common relational processes, such as enhanced caregiver sensitivity, co-regulation, and physiological attunement. This overlap, combined with variability in implementation, makes it difficult to draw mechanistic inferences regarding which intervention components are responsible for observed improvements in infant, caregiver, or parent–infant relational outcomes. Distinct dyadic interventions may target specific aspects of relational health within the parent–infant dyad. From this perspective, individual intervention modalities may activate partially distinct relational mechanisms, leading to either convergent or divergent outcomes across studies. These mechanisms may also exert cumulative effects, triggering cascades of relational change that enhance the quality of parent–child interactions over time. This suggests an additional mechanistic consideration: the goodness of fit between a given intervention and the characteristics of the dyad and its environment. Assessing fit—based on factors such as caregiver readiness, infant medical complexity, and social determinants of health—may help determine which interventions are most likely to yield meaningful improvements in child, caregiver, and relational outcomes.

Further studies are needed to clarify if health systems may benefit less from identifying a single “optimal” dyadic intervention and more from adopting any intervention that reliably promotes sustained parent–infant interaction within a supportive, trauma-informed framework. It remains unclear whether modality-specific effects exist beyond these shared relational processes or whether observed benefits primarily reflect common mechanisms of dyadic engagement and co-regulation. Future research should explicitly test whether modality-specific effects exist beyond these core relational processes and examine how system-level barriers and facilitators shape the effectiveness of dyadic care.

Conclusions and future directions

Though early dyadic interventions in ICU settings can significantly enhance parent-infant interactions and improve maternal mental health, mother-infant attachment, and infant regulatory capacities, integrating these interventions into routine care reduces parental anxiety, supports bonding, and promotes infant development. Research on dyadic interventions with psychotherapy in ICU setting remains limited. Incorporating trauma-informed approaches and expanding caregiver involvement, especially fathers and grandparents, further enhances support provided to families. Integrating these practices into ICU care models may improve long-term outcomes for both parents and infants, particularly in mitigating the risk of insecure attachment and perinatal psychiatric disorders.
