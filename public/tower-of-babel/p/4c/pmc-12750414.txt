A high‐recovery, high‐density targeted genotyping platform for cranberry
Authors: Shaun J Clare, Ping Zheng, Mandie Driskill, Joseph Kawash, Jeffrey Neyhart, Timothy R Millar, Dorrie Main, James Polashock, Patrick Edger, Nicholi Vorsa, Jennifer Johnson‐Cicalese, Andrew F Maule, Juan Zalapa, Gina M Sideli, Massimo Iorizzo, Nahla Bassil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe American cranberry (Vaccinium macrocarpon) is a commercially important berry species native to North America. Cranberry is a popular fruit crop with many known health benefits. Thus far, genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS) was the only high‐throughput genotyping technique available in cranberry. While GBS is cost efficient, it has the disadvantage of producing variant calls that cannot be easily transferred between populations and across studies. In this study, we report the development of the first targeted high‐throughput genotyping platform for cranberry. The Flex‐Seq genotyping technology offers repeatable, high‐recovery, and high‐density single nucleotide polymorphisms as well as concomitant haplotypes. The cranberry Flex‐Seq platform contains 17,502 loci, boasting a recovery rate of 99.8% in a diversity panel of 192 cranberry accessions. The level of incompatible marker inheritance in 22 analyzed parent‐offspring trios ranged from 1.1% to 13%, with an average of 5.5%. Nine of the 22 crosses had an excess of incompatible markers, indicating inconsistent parentage that could be investigated further. In quantitative trait loci (QTLs) analyses, Flex‐Seq identified a greater number of QTLs compared to previously used GBS as well as higher overall logarithm of odds scores for overlapping QTLs. In addition, construction of locus haplotypes resulted in a substantial improvement in resolving the relatedness of cranberry accessions. This platform will aid in utilizing the untapped potential of wild cranberry, mapping key traits with higher precision, and ensuring robust characterization of accessions. The developed genotyping platform will help in the rapid improvement of cranberry cultivars to benefit breeders, growers, and consumers.

INTRODUCTION

Vaccinium is a genus within the Ericaceae family, consisting of trailing plants, shrubs, and small trees, and contains over 450 species (Edger et al., 2022). These include economically important fruit crops such as blueberry (Vaccinium corymbosum, Vaccinium angustifolium, Vaccinium myrtilloides, and Vaccinium virgatum syn. ashei), cranberry (Vaccinium macrocarpon, Vaccinium microcarpum, and Vaccinium oxycoccos), and lingonberry (Vaccinium vitis‐idaea), but also culturally significant species such as bilberry (Vaccinium cespitosum, Vaccinium deliciosum, Vaccinium myrtillus, and Vaccinium uliginosum), huckleberry (V. deliciosum, Vaccinium membranaceum, Vaccinium parvifolium, and Vaccinium ovatum), and ohelo berry (Vaccinium reticulatum) (Edger et al., 2022; Prevéy et al., 2020). Research has shown cranberry to be beneficial for kidney health and gut microbiota (Heiss et al., 2022). In addition, cranberry was historically used by native North Americans to preserve food, dress wounds, and treat urinary tract infections (Guay, 2009; Hisano et al., 2012; J. Polashock et al., 2014), of which the latter treatment has become widely used. These health benefits have helped increase world demand of cranberry with an economic value surpassing $3.5 billion (Diaz‐Garcia et al., 2021).

Cranberry is one of the few commercially produced fruit species native to North America and is a relatively recent crop, with domestication occurring in the early 1800s (J. Polashock et al., 2014; Vorsa & Zalapa, 2019). Unlike commercially cultivated highbush blueberry (V. corymbosum), which is predominately tetraploid (2n = 4x = 48), the commercially cultivated American cranberry (V. macrocarpon) and its wild relative (V. microcarpum) are predominately diploid (2n = 2x = 24) (Diaz‐Garcia et al., 2021; Mengist et al., 2023). While Smith et al. (2015) suggests recognizing diploids as a distinct species, in this study we treated V. microcarpum as part of the V. oxycoccos s.l. complex according to the more conservative treatment (Vander Kloet, 2009). The first draft genome assembly of American cranberry was of the cultivar Ben Lear (J. Polashock et al., 2014), followed by chromosome level assemblies of Stevens (Diaz‐Garcia et al., 2021) and Ben Lear (Kawash et al., 2022). The cranberry genome size is estimated to be approximately 470 Mb (J. Polashock et al., 2014). More recently, the cranberry pangenome of 10 additional cranberry cultivars facilitated the identification of core and accessory genes (Yocca et al., 2023). Core genes are found in all or almost all accessions (i.e., hard and soft), whereas accessory genes are found in a lower proportion of accessions (Tettelin et al., 2005). Core genes are predominately attributed to vital “housekeeping” genes required for essential metabolic processes, whereas accessory genes are attributed to adaptive traits, such as disease resistance (Barragan & Weigel, 2021), environmental adaptation (Conrad et al., 2022), or secondary metabolite production (Adamek et al., 2018), which impact flavor and aroma (Schwab et al., 2008).

Previously, genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS) was employed for cranberry genotyping (Covarrubias‐Pazaran et al., 2016; Daverdin et al., 2017; Diaz‐Garcia, Covarrubias‐Pazaran, et al., 2018; Diaz‐Garcia, Schlautman, et al., 2018; Maule et al., 2024) and was recently used to showcase the untapped potential present within cranberries to accelerate breeding efforts (Diaz‐Garcia et al., 2020). However, GBS has the disadvantage of not producing easily transferable variants between populations and across studies as the technology relies on restriction sites (Schlautman et al., 2017). To address this challenge, targeted sequencing/genotyping technologies have been developed as an alternative to GBS and have become widely used (de Ronne et al., 2024). Recently, a low‐mid density (targeting 3000 loci) DArTag (Diversity Array Technologies) platform has been developed for cranberry (Chen et al., 2025). The platform is being tested for linkage map construction and genomic selection analysis, two types of analyses that do not necessarily require high marker density to break genetic linkage.

To complement these efforts, in this study we describe the development of a high‐density Flex‐Seq genotyping technology (RAPiD Genomics/LGC Group for cranberry) that is better suited for analyses that require higher marker density to delimit recombination points. The Flex‐Seq platform relies on the development of two flanking probes, which increases the specificity compared to single probe genotyping platforms such as DArTag (Jaccoud et al., 2001; Wenzl et al., 2004). The proprietary design process allows for the multiplexing of tens of thousands of targets. Recently, the Flex‐Seq genotyping technology was developed for tetraploid and hexaploid blueberry, offering repeatable, high recovery of high‐density loci (Clare et al., 2024). Due to the nature of the panel, microhaplotypes (two or more mutations within 300‐nucleotide window) surrounding the target variant can also be reconstructed for enhanced data capture, and the platform can be rapidly modified compared to array‐based or GBS technology to add or remove target loci. The cranberry Flex‐Seq platform contains 17,502 loci, boasting a recovery rate of 99.8%. Within recovered loci, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are uniformly distributed in core genes, accessory genes, and intergenic loci. In addition, construction of locus haplotypes resulted in a substantial improvement in resolving the relatedness of cranberry accessions. This platform has been led by a consortium of cranberry researchers and breeders under the auspices of the Vaccinium Coordinated Agricultural Project, funded by the United States Department of Agriculture in 2019 (Iorizzo et al., 2023), for the accelerated breeding of cranberry.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

A diverse set of 192 cranberry accessions was obtained from cranberry researchers and used in this study (Table S1). The set consists of 30 cultivars or breeding accessions, 102 landraces, 52 wild V. macrocarpon accessions, and eight V. oxycoccos and hybrids (three V. oxycoccos accessions and five interspecific hybrids [V. macrocarpon and V. oxycoccos]). Cultivars, or breeding accessions, are referred to as named cranberry varieties or those resulting from controlled crosses. Landraces are cranberry accessions that are locally adapted and grown by farmers. The four V. oxycoccos accessions and four hybrids were grouped together as V. oxycoccos for analysis.

Catalogue data collection and quality control

Generation of the catalogue data followed a similar procedure to the blueberry Flex‐Seq platform (Clare et al., 2024). In brief, paired‐end genomic sequences (PRJNA687008, Vaccinium macrocarpon, 11 datasets) were downloaded from NCBI Short Read Archive database as fastq files using fastq‐dump 2.10.9. Quantitative trait locus (QTL) files provided by collaborators (Table S2) consisted of approximately 150–250 bp of flanking sequence with a centrally positioned variant, fastq files, or a coordinate file containing the chromosome and position. Fastq files were evaluated for quality using fastqc 0.11.9 and multiqc 1.9 using default settings and removed using BBMap 38.878 (Andrews, 2010; Bushnell, 2014; Ewels et al., 2016). Curated sequencing files were aligned, indexed, and sorted against the cranberry consensus reference genome assembly (https://www.vaccinium.org/bio_data/7550323) with bwa 0.7.17 and samtools 1.14 sort (Li & Durbin, 2009; Li et al., 2009). Duplicate reads were removed using GATK 4.1.9.0 MarkDuplicate, and read groups were added using AddorReplaceReadGroups (McKenna et al., 2010). The depth and coverage across the genome was determined for each individual bam file using samtools 1.14 coverage (Li et al., 2009).

De novo and a priori SNP calling

De novo SNP calling was performed as previously described for the blueberry Flex‐Seq platform (Clare et al., 2024). In brief, all bam files with highest depth and uniform coverage were merged and sorted by genomic coordinates into a single bam file using samtools 1.14 merge and sort (Li et al., 2009). Variant calling was performed using Freebayes 1.3.2. The variant call format (VCF) file was sorted by coordinates and filtered with bcftools 1.11 sort and view (Li et al., 2009). The flanking sequences (200 bp each side) surrounding the filtered SNPs were extracted from the cranberry reference genome using a custom Python script and re‐aligned to the cranberry reference genome using bwa‐mem and samtools sort and BLAST+ (basic local alignment search tool) 2.12.0 to ensure specificity. Single mapping variants based on these alignments resulted in 2,411,908 putative SNPs (Table S3). A priori calling was performed identically to the blueberry Flex‐Seq platform (Clare et al., 2024). In brief, collaborator‐identified QTL target sequences were converted to fasta format and aligned to the cranberry reference genome with bwa‐mem (Li & Durbin, 2009). Each alignment file was utilized in a custom Python script that generated liftover coordinates for the target sequences. Each alignment file was used to parse out the chromosome and position for each SNP.

Final SNP catalogue

The final SNP catalogue compiled all de novo and a priori SNPs into tab‐separated file containing the variant name, the chromosome, the position(s), the origin of the data (de novo or a priori), and SNP class (hard, soft, etc.) using a custom Python script that removed shared SNPs. The SNPs to be targeted by the initial Flex‐Seq SNPs were selected from the final SNP catalogue (Figure 1). In the first step, the cranberry genome was divided into 2 kb intervals, regions containing genic SNPs were determined, and a 100 bp sliding window was applied to assign a maximum number of the genic SNPs within the sliding windows for the region, with SNPs located within the middle position chosen to represent that genomic region. In the second step, intergenic SNPs were chosen in a similar manner except the cranberry genome was divided into 40 kb intervals. In the third step, specific genomic regions were targeted to improve coverage. If two adjacent SNPs were separated by more than 50 kb, a single SNP representing the gap was incorporated. Finally, SNPs attributed to collaborator QTL were added. The 62K SNP catalogue was provided to RAPiD Genomics for the initial Flex‐Seq design. The selection process of the 62K SNP catalogue (62,551 SNPs) was implemented using custom Perl scripts.

Flex‐Seq platform design and genotyping

The cranberry Flex‐Seq design procedure was identical to the blueberry Flex‐Seq design procedure (Clare et al., 2024). In brief, the 62K SNP catalogue and class derived from the pangenome were used to guide selection of the target probes. SNPs located within genes or previously associated with a QTL were prioritized over intergenic SNPs. Flex‐Seq probes were designed by RAPiD Genomics using a proprietary in‐house pipeline that uses a 1 bp sliding window across the 300–500 bp flanking sequence for each target, generating a list of all possible probe sequences. The candidate probes are filtered for extreme GC content, homopolymer runs, ambiguous bases, and specificity. Probes were also assessed for uniform GC content, melt temperature, dimer potential, hairpin potential, and ability to be sequenced by 2x150 bp Illumina sequencing.

The final Flex‐Seq design procedure utilizes a two‐step approach. The first step consisted of a larger ∼46K panel was assessed on a set of 96 representative samples in 12 pools of eight samples each (Table S1). The second step selected loci from the larger panel based on optimal recovery across initial samples, minimal variation within a locus among samples, and optimal uniformity across loci. The final panel consisted of 17,502 loci (Flex‐Seq Panel Code: FS_12902) from the cranberry panel. Subsequently, all 192 samples were paired end sequenced using the 2x150 bp Illumina NovaSeq Platform using the FS_12902 panel. Data files included sample fastq files, sample haplotype files, a raw population VCF file, and a filtered population VCF file generated using FreeBayes (Garrison & Marth, 2012).

Platform assessment

Platform assessment was conducted in a similar manner to the blueberry Flex‐Seq genotyping platform (Clare et al., 2024). In brief, locus overlap, locus recovery, and comparison between SNP and haplotype phylogenetic trees are compared. In addition, trio analysis using SNP variants and haplotypes was performed, and GBS and Flex‐Seq‐based QTL analyses were also compared.

Marker overlap between platforms

Marker overlap was determined as described in the blueberry Flex‐Seq analysis (Clare et al., 2024). Currently, only a DArTag genotyping platform is available for cranberry. Genomic positions for the DArTag loci were obtained using blastn 2.15.0 and the cranberry genome assembly. The Flex‐Seq and DArTag platforms consist of 17,502 and 3059 loci, respectively. Only the top hit with query coverage and identity above 90% was utilized (Clare et al., 2024). The output files were converted to bed files and overlapping and unique loci determined using bedtools 2.30.0 intersect with ‐wa and ‐wb, or ‐v options, respectively. Overlap was plotted using ggplot2 3.5.1 and eulerr 7.0.2 within R 4.4.1.

Locus recovery

Raw sequencing fastq files were assessed using fastqc 0.12.1 (Andrews, 2010) and multiqc 1.21 (Ewels et al., 2016). Reads were trimmed using fastp 0.22.0 (Chen et al., 2018), with the front and tail trimmed 10 bp, low complexity filter on, cut right sliding window with default settings (4 bp, Q20), and paired end error correction. Raw (prefiltered) and curated (postfiltered) sequencing reads were assessed using fastqc and fastp reports, respectively. Paired‐end reads were aligned to the cranberry reference genome using a custom piping loop with bwa‐mem2 2.2.1 (Vasimuddin et al., 2019) and samtools 1.20 view, sort and index commands for sorted and indexed bam files (Li et al., 2009). Locus recovery was evaluated by locus and individuals using bam files and bedtools 2.31.1 multicov with the options to include duplicate reads and mapping quality set to 30.

Variant filtering and haplotype reconstruction

Prefiltering variant metrics were calculated on the unfiltered VCF file using bedtools intersect. The variants were filtered for biallelic SNPs, call quality above 30, mapping quality above 30, and sequencing depth equal to or greater than six. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay was calculated as squared correlations using plink 1.90 with –ld‐window 2, –ld‐window‐r2 0 and –vcf‐half‐call m options and polynomial regression within R 4.4.1.To calculate postfiltering variant metrics, first the filtered variants were subsampled into sets (breeding material, landraces, wild accessions, and V. oxycoccos) using bedtools view and refiltered using vcftools 0.16 to remove monomorphic loci from the respective set. Loci density was visualized using CMplot 4.3.1 in R 4.4.1. Haplotypes for all cranberry accessions were reconstructed using the VCF file containing all high confidence biallelic SNPs prior to subsampling as previously described in the variant filtering stage from all cranberry accessions, and bam files generated during the locus recovery stage. A bam list, a target loci bed file, and an indexed VCF file of variants and the indexed reference genome were used as inputs to mchap 0.9.2 assemble (Millar, 2023). The number of variants and haplotypes per locus was determined using bedtools intersect. Subsequently, haplotypes were reconstructed for each subsample set by providing a bam list with only the respective samples, whereas all other files remained the same. This was due to the inability to subsample using bcftools view using an mchap assemble VCF output file. Both SNP and haplotype data were also analyzed based on locus class: core, accessory, and intergenic. If loci contained more than one classification, for example, spanned the border of a core gene and intergenic, the locus was given the higher designation. Loci classed as unknown were grouped with intergenic loci as there were only 46 loci with this designation. Variants were visualized using lvplot 0.2.1 in R 4.4.1.

Population structure

A total of 12,005 polymorphic loci consisting of 54,960 high‐quality biallelic SNPs and 79,292 reconstructed haplotypes were used to compare the phylogenetic trees. SNP‐based and haplotype‐based phylogenetic trees were constructed using unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean hierarchical clustering using 100 bootstraps and the aboot command and nei.dist genetic distance within poppr 2.9.6 (Clare et al., 2024). Phylogenetic trees were plotted using the ggtree 3.12.0 extension of ggplot2 3.5.1 within R 4.4.1.

Trio analysis

To validate the utility of the SNP dataset to track the inheritance of polymorphisms across generations, we investigated the marker inheritance pattern using the check_trio function from the package polyBreedR 0.44 in R 4.4.1. This function computes the percentage of incompatible markers for each trio given the parent and offspring marker genotypes. Twenty parent‐offspring trios were analyzed, including 10 more recent advanced selections or released cultivars from the Rutgers University Cranberry Breeding Program, along with 10 historical crosses (Table S4; Chandler et al., 1947). Of the latter, two were duplicated where the same cultivar name of the offspring corresponded to more than one accession in the dataset.

Quantitative trait loci mapping

Sequence information and phenotypic information of the population used for QTL comparison were obtained from previous studies for GBS and Flex‐Seq genetic data, as well as epicuticular wax and fruit rot phenotypes (Erndwein et al., 2023; Kawash et al., 2024). Both datasets were identically processed for direct comparison. FastQC 0.12.0 was used to screen sequence data to ensure quality and no abundance of adapter contamination. Reads were aligned to the American cranberry genome ‘Ben Lear’ with default parameters of bwa‐mem 0.7.17 (Erndwein et al., 2023; Kawash et al., 2022). Samtools 1.10 view, sort, and index were used to generate bam files and mpileup was used with default parameters to call SNPs. Biallelic SNPs were filtered using a custom Python script/vcftools, with a minimum heterozygosity of 5% and missing data below 5% (Erndwein et al., 2023; Kawash et al., 2024). Markers were ordered according to their genomic positions, and R/qtl 1.66 est.map used to calculate genetic distance between markers using default settings (Erndwein et al., 2023; Kawash et al., 2024). QTLs were identified using R/qtl function scanone, with an F2 cross class, normal model, and epicuticular method (Erndwein et al., 2023; Kawash et al., 2024). A logarithm of odds (LOD) significance threshold was calculated at p < 0.05 using 1000 permutations and intervals defined using a 1.5 LOD decrease (Erndwein et al., 2023; Kawash et al., 2024).

RESULTS

SNP catalogue composition, data curation, and Flex‐Seq platform composition

In total, de novo variant calling identified 32,312,371 variants, with 30,652,204 distributed across pseudomolecule chromosomes. Of those, 2,411,908 SNPs mapped to single locations, and 160 a priori polymorphisms provided by collaborators were collated from the SNP catalogue. A total of 50,609 and 8062 SNPs were selected in the core and accessory genes, respectively. In the second step, a total of 1255 intergenic SNPs were selected. In the third step, specific genomic regions were targeted to improve coverage, resulting in 2404 intergenic SNPs and 61 genic SNPs. Finally, 160 SNPs provided by collaborators, based on previously identified QTL, were added. The 62K SNP catalogue contained a total of 62,391 de novo SNPs and 160 a priori SNPs that were provided to RAPiD Genomics for Flex‐Seq design. The ∼62K SNP catalogue was used to develop an initial Flex‐Seq design that consisted of 46,450 loci (Flex‐Seq Panel Code: FS_12901). The design was assessed on 96 samples and reduced to a final probe set targeting 17,502 loci (Flex‐Seq Panel Code: FS_12902) distributed uniformly across the cranberry genome (Figure 1). From 160 existing QTLs associated SNPs, 112 and 36 were included in the ∼46K and ∼17.5K Flex‐Seq panel, respectively.

Platform assessment

Marker overlap between platforms

Genomic positions for all 17,502 Flex‐Seq and 3,059 DArTag loci could be obtained using BLAST. In total, 15,709 loci are unique to the Flex‐Seq platform and 135 are unique to the DArTag platforms. In terms of platform overlap, a total of 2921 DArTag loci are covered by 1793 Flex‐Seq loci. The Flex‐Seq and DArTag genotyping platforms have a mean amplicon length of 219 bp (±109) and 81 bp, respectively.

Raw sequencing and locus recovery

Total raw reads for the Flex‐Seq exceeded 818 million reads across all 192 samples. Total reads that passed filtering exceeded 810 million reads, with a mean of 4.26 million reads per sample and ranged from 2160 to 7.46 million reads per sample. Filtered reads were predominately removed due to being under 20 bp. Total reads that mapped to the reference genome were 699.9 million, with a mean of 3.65 million reads per sample and ranged from 844 to 6.98 million reads per sample.

All 17,502 loci were recovered in at least one accession and one read. However, when considered as a population (192 individuals), 17,468 (99.8%) and 17,179 (98.2%) loci were recovered in at least 90% and 80% of the population with at least one read, respectively. Locus recovery ranged from 346 loci (Table 1) to 16,451 loci per individual, with a mean of 14,550 loci recovered per individual. Mean coverage of all loci per sample was 208x and ranged from 0.04x to 399x, whereas mean coverage of individual loci across all samples was 208x but ranged from 0.03x to 8535x. Samples that exhibited substantially lower recovery below the population mean of 14,550 loci were investigated further (Table 1). Ten samples exhibited low locus recovery throughout the platform with less than 2x coverage across all loci (Table 1). Samples with low locus recovery also had lower insert size of below 90 bp, with a mean insert size of approximately 121 bp for the remaining population.

Variant filtering and haplotype reconstruction

A total of 17,262 out of 17,502 loci (98.6%) were recovered and contained polymorphisms before filtering, whereas 240 loci contained no polymorphisms. This equates to 150,084 variants (including SNPs, multiallelic nucleotide polymorphisms, and structural variants) for a mean of 8.5 variants per locus (median 7). After filtering, 12,005 were considered polymorphic, whereas 5501 were monomorphic. There was a total of 54,960 biallelic SNPs for a mean of 3.1 variants per locus when considering all accessions (Table 2). This increased to a mean of 4.6 variants per locus, ranging from 1 to 46 variants per locus, when considering only polymorphic loci. The number of polymorphic loci decreased slightly to 11,669, 11,865, and 11,782 in breeding material, landraces, and wild accessions, respectively. The number of polymorphic loci in V. oxycoccos accessions decreased substantially to 9662 polymorphic loci. Subsequently, the number of variants and mean distance (physical) decreased proportionally to the reduction of polymorphic loci. The amount of missing data was 5.6%, 6.0%, 4.2%, 1.9%, and 44.7% for all samples, breeding material, landraces, wild accessions, and V. oxycoccos samples, respectively.

A total of 54,960 high confidence biallelic SNPs were utilized for haplotype reconstruction of the 17,502 Flex‐Seq loci. A slight decrease in polymorphic loci was achieved with a total of 12,001 and 5501 considered polymorphic and monomorphic loci, respectively. However, the opposite occurred in all subsampled sets, increasing between 124 and 266 loci in breeding material, landraces, and wild accessions. The most striking difference was the increase from 9662 loci with polymorphic variants to 11,613 loci with polymorphic haplotypes within V. oxycoccos. The increase in loci polymorphic for haplotypes is likely to be a result of lower confidence haplotype calls, relative to the confidence of SNP calls. Additionally, the total unique‐in‐state haplotypes increased for all sets in relation to the number of variants. For example, a total of 54,960 high‐quality biallelic SNPs were identified across all accessions and increased to 73,793 haplotypes, an increase of 33.4%. This increased the number of variants per locus from 4.6 to 6.1 haplotypes per locus. The number of unique‐in‐state haplotypes correlates with the sample size of each set. For example, the highest to lowest number of unique‐in‐state haplotypes were landraces, wild accessions, and breeding material, followed by V. oxycoccos sets, which consisted of 101, 51, 32, and eight respective samples.

The mean inter locus distance of the initial Flex‐Seq probe set design was 26.8 kb. Considering the majority (98.2%) of loci were recovered in 80% of the population, mean distance based on polymorphic loci was also calculated, which increased the distance to 39.2 kb (Figure 2). This marginally increased in breeding material, landraces, and wild accessions to 40.3, 39.6, and 39.9 kb, respectively. The V. oxycoccos mean distance increased more significantly to 48.7 kb. Lastly, the proportion of missing data in haplotypes is substantially reduced across all subsampled sets by at least 50% in comparison to biallelic SNPs. For example, missing values in breeding material decreased from 6.0% to 2.2% and landrace material decreased from 4.2% to 1.5% (Table 2). LD decay was estimated to be 31.6 kb when considering all high‐quality SNPs and all accessions at an R 2 of 0.2.

A total of 14,578, 2167, and 757 loci are considered core, accessory, and intergenic, respectively (Table 3). Regardless of cranberry class (breeding material, landrace, wild accessions, or V. oxycoccos), or locus class (core, accessory, and intergenic), a similar number of loci were recovered with polymorphic haplotypes, ranging from 63.9% to 70.5%. However, within the eight V. oxycoccos accessions, the number of loci with polymorphic variants decreased to 53.2%–55.2%. Recovery of locus class remained stable across each cranberry class; for example, breeding material recovered a mean of 68.2% loci with polymorphic haplotypes with 68.0%, 70.1%, and 66.8% in core, accessory, and intergenic loci, respectively. In general, the total number of haplotypes per locus exceeded the total number of biallelic SNPs per locus (Figure 3).

Population structure

To determine whether biallelic SNPs or haplotypes provide more informative data to recapitulate the evolutionary history of cranberry, phylogenetic trees were constructed as previously conducted in blueberry (Clare et al., 2024). Neither the SNP‐based nor the haplotype‐based phylogenetic trees form distinct clusters between breeding material, landraces, wild accessions, and V. oxycoccos accessions (Figure 4). However, key differences indicating that the haplotype‐based phylogenetic tree resolves the evolutionary history in greater detail is the fact that V. oxycoccos forms the most distantly related clade, with V. macrocarpon × V. oxycoccos hybrids more closely clustered. Most strikingly, branch length is vastly extended, allowing for the separation of additional clades. However, considering the recent domestication of cranberry and narrower gene pool compared to blueberry, distinct clades were not expected.

Trio analysis

The level of incompatible marker inheritance in the analyzed parent‐offspring trios ranged from 1.1% to 13%, with an average of 5.5%. Eleven of the trios exhibited an error rate at or below 3%, an acceptable threshold to validate a trio, while the remaining nine had 5% or more incompatible markers. There was no apparent pattern between the age of a trio (historical or modern) and the rate of incompatible markers; however, some trios with highly incompatible markers were found to share a common parent. Five of the nine crosses with an excess of incompatible markers shared the landrace parent McFarlin, indicating a potential misidentification of this accession when used in some crosses. To determine if the pedigrees of these five McFarlin trios could be resolved, we performed a search for all possible trios where McFarlin was replaced. None of these replacement trios exhibited incompatible marker rates below 3% (data not shown).

Quantitative trait locus mapping

LOD thresholds were calculated at 5.56 and 5.42 for fruit rot resistance using GBS and Flex‐Seq‐based QTL mapping, respectively. Using GBS information, a single QTL for fruit rot resistance was identified on chromosome 2 with an LOD score of 7.10 (Figure 5A). Comparatively, four QTLs were identified using the Flex‐Seq panel, one each on chromosomes 2 and 3 and two on chromosome 11 with LOD scores of 12.16, 5.59, 6.12, and 7.56, respectively (Figure 5B). The major QTL on chromosome 2 overlapped in physical position using both Flex‐Seq and GBS data. LOD thresholds were calculated at 5.37 and 5.29 for epicuticular wax using GBS and Flex‐Seq‐based QTL mapping, respectively. Using GBS, no significant QTLs were identified; however, a QTL that was below the significance threshold with an LOD score of 4.86 was identified on chromosome 12 (Figure 5C). Conversely, two QTLs were identified on chromosomes 1 and 2 using the Flex‐Seq panel with scores of 7.49 and 6.58, respectively (Figure 5D).

DISCUSSION

Cranberry has seen a rapid improvement in genomic resources with the development of fingerprinting sets (Fajardo et al., 2013; Polashock & Vorsa, 2002), publication of the first genetic map approximately a decade ago (Georgi et al., 2013), followed by a high‐density microsatellite genetic map (Schlautman et al., 2015), GBS application (Covarrubias‐Pazaran et al., 2016), draft genome assembly (J. Polashock et al., 2014), consensus map (Schlautman et al., 2017), pseudomolecule level assemblies (Diaz‐Garcia et al., 2021; Kawash et al., 2022), and a pangenome (Yocca et al., 2023). Currently, only a DArTag genotyping platform is available for cranberry. However, cranberry is still lacking a high‐density and easily repeatable genotyping platform. Therefore, the cranberry Flex‐Seq genotyping platform was initiated to facilitate genotyping data that is repeatable and transferable across future studies. The existing low‐density DArTag genotyping platform and the new high‐density Flex‐Seq genotyping platforms are designed from the same variants identified in the pangenome; thus, researchers can determine which platform would satisfy the requirements of each project and allocate resources accordingly with the ability to cross‐relate studies. The cranberry Flex‐Seq genotyping platform incorporates 17,502 amplicons that can capture significantly more variants due to the increased locus number and size over other platforms. The 219 bp (±109) long amplicons of the Flex‐Seq platform are significantly larger than the fixed length of 81 bp on the DArTag platform (https://breedinginsight.org/cranberry/). In addition, the DArTag pipeline only accepts amplicons containing a single target variant, and microhaplotypes are assembled based on serendipitous variants along the captured locus. Therefore, the increased length of the Flex‐Seq platform facilitates longer sequences from which more information can be leveraged. The cranberry Flex‐Seq and DArTag platforms exhibit considerable overlap in comparison to the overlap between the blueberry Flex‐Seq/DArTag platforms because the DArTag panel design was based on a subset of variants used to develop the Flex‐Seq platform. This was to ensure genotyping results that could be transferable between studies depending on data requirements and cost.

The Flex‐Seq platform has extremely high recovery with 98.6% of targeted loci recovered, which generated a total of 150,084 variants. For analysis, we only assessed high‐quality biallelic SNPs, resulting in 12,005 polymorphic loci and 54,960 SNPs. Despite this reduction in polymorphic loci from raw to filtered variant calls from stringent filtering, additional loci could be recovered in a variety of methods, such as including more complex variants, including highly diverse accessions, and increasing sequencing depth. Additional diverse accessions would provide additional evidence of polymorphic variants at the loci that otherwise would have been removed during stringent filtering. This would apply to subsample class, especially for V. oxycoccos and hybrids, which contained only eight accessions (four with low recovery), which therefore severely lacked diversity. The fact that over 99% of loci were recovered in 90% of the population and over 98% recovered in 80% of the population further showcases the platform's excellent recovery. Additionally, locus recovery metrics may have been higher if all DNA samples achieved similar recovery levels. This was especially pronounced in V. oxycoccos samples with 50% of samples exhibiting poor recovery (four out of eight). With a small sample size, it is not possible to determine if lower recovery was by chance, due to poor quality or low quantity DNA, or such extreme divergence that primer sites would no longer bind. In total, there were 10 samples that exhibited low recovery. This reduction in recovered loci in these samples did not appear to correlate with sample class with two breeding material samples, four landraces, and four V. oxycoccos samples exhibiting low locus recovery (Table 1) or gDNA concentration. For example, a sample with gDNA concentration of 0.7 was not identified to have low recovery. Since only eight samples constituted the V. oxycoccos set, this will have skewed recovery metrics and should be treated with caution, as the remaining samples exhibited recovery in line with other subsample sets.

The decrease of four polymorphic loci from SNPs to haplotypes (12,005–12,001) across all cranberries is likely due to the read support required by mchap for haplotype reconstruction that would identify putative SNPs more likely explained due to sequencing errors in the full population. However, the increase in polymorphic loci from SNPs to haplotypes in the subsample sets may be due to the lower sample sizes that inadvertently removed true variants during filtering or the addition of lower confidence haplotypes. In addition, the proportion of missing data present in haplotype to variant datasets decreased across all subsample sets. This is the opposite of what occurred in the blueberry Flex‐Seq platform, which was primarily assessing tetraploid accessions (Clare et al., 2024), and may therefore be the underlying cause. Despite not being explored due to the majority of accessions being diploid, this platform also offers the opportunity to reconstruct more informative dosage information of higher ploidy levels from the ability to phase longer amplicons. However, proportional sequencing depth will have to be considered at higher ploidy levels. Lastly, the Flex‐Seq platform would have the added benefit that microhaplotypes can be combined for more accurate dosage data in higher ploidy cranberries when required by the project. LD decay was estimated at 31.6 kb, larger than previous reports that estimated LD decay to be 17 kb at 0.2 R 2 in wild cranberry (Neyhart et al., 2022), but less than diploid blueberry at 73 kb. However, this could be due to different species, populations, and methodology in calculating LD decay. The mean distance between Flex‐Seq loci is 26.8 kb, with a mean distance of 39.2 kb across the whole population. Although the density of the Flex‐Seq platform will vary due to recovery and LD decay will vary across populations, the Flex‐Seq platform will provide robust genotyping for genome‐wide association mapping.

Phylogenetic analysis showcased the increased power of haplotypes over biallelic SNPs to extend branch lengths and cluster accessions that support their pedigree or their origin of collection. This is hypothesized to occur from capturing additional evolutionary history for the combined effects of linked polymorphisms. Distinct groups upon clustering were not expected as the members of the diversity panel were selected to represent cranberry diversity. Trio analysis was able to identify ‘McFarlin’ as a possible source of misidentification in five of the nine trios with elevated marker incompatibility rates. Elevated marker incompatibility rates among trios may be due to incorrect pedigrees, sample mix‐ups, or clonal impurity. As in many woody perennials, the purity and provenance of some named cranberry varieties may not be certain (Vorsa & Johnson‐Cicalese, 2012), in part due to the historical movement of varieties via vegetative cuttings from beds of unknown purity. Genetic heterogeneity in clonal cranberry cultivars and other clonal misidentification has been reported in McFarlin and other cultivars in several previous studies using genetic markers (Fajardo et al., 2013; Novy et al., 1996; Schlautman et al., 2018). Lastly, Flex‐Seq QTL analysis was able to identify four fruit rot resistance QTL and one epicuticular wax QTL, compared to one and zero QTL when utilizing GBS data.

Through phylogenetic analysis, trio analysis, and QTL analysis, the Flex‐Seq genotyping platform shows incredible power to resolve the complex ancestry of accessions, identify potential errors in pedigrees, and refine genomic intervals responsible for important cranberry characteristics. In QTL analysis, the Flex‐Seq platform identified a greater number of QTL compared to previously used GBS as well as higher overall LOD scores for overlapping QTL. This platform will aid in utilizing the untapped potential of wild cranberry (Kawash et al., 2022; Neyhart et al., 2022; Rodríguez‐Bonilla et al., 2019), mapping key traits such as fruit rot resistance (Daverdin et al., 2017; Kawash et al., 2024), malic acid content (Fong et al., 2021), or other quality traits with higher precision, and ensuring robust characterization of accessions to be preserved in breeder's and repository collections. Adoption of this targeted genotyping platform will help in the rapid development of elite cranberry varieties to benefit the breeders, growers, and consumers.

CONCLUSIONS

The Flex‐Seq platform offers a repeatable, high‐density genotyping platform with extremely high recovery within cranberry. Additionally, the platform offers the construction of microhaplotypes of loci that are significantly larger than other available platforms with the scope to incorporate dosage information if required. Construction of microhaplotypes allowed for a decrease in missing data while simultaneously increasing the number of variants that could be utilized for analysis. This increase in informative data allowed us to unravel the complex ancestry of cranberries in finer detail, identify misidentified pedigrees and refine QTL positions.
