A New Zealand Heat Pump Retrofit Programme: Warmer, Drier, Happier, Cheaper
Authors: Phoebe Taptiklis, Caroline Fyfe, Arthur Grimes, Shannon Minehan, Thomas Benison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe evaluate temperature, humidity, electricity use and subjective wellbeing impacts of installing heat pumps through New Zealand's Warmer Kiwi Homes programme. The government‐funded programme included provision of heat pumps in already insulated houses that lack efficient heating. Staggered installation enables difference‐in‐difference estimates of impacts of heat pump installation on living area temperatures and relative humidity, and on household electricity use. Surveys conducted before and after installation provide demographic and housing information plus measures of occupants’ wellbeing. Living areas of treated houses experience increases in temperature which are most pronounced around breakfast and evening times, and when outdoor temperatures are low. Houses also experience reductions in relative humidity. Winter electricity use falls in houses that have a heat pump fitted, with electricity savings peaking in the evening. Heat pump installation reduces perceptions of living in a cold house and improves aspects of residents’ mental wellbeing during winter.Keywords: electricity use, heat pump, humidity, indoor temperature, subjective wellbeing

Introduction

Programmes to encourage heat pump installation have gained pace in several countries including New Zealand, 1 the UK (HM Government 2023; Li et al. 2022), Norway (Sadeghi et al. 2022) and Germany. 2 Rather than generating heat, like most heaters, heat pumps use a refrigerant to extract latent heat from outdoor air, and move that heat into the indoor space (BRANZ 2024). 3 This method can produce around four times more heat per kw of electricity used than other electric heaters (Energy Efficiency Conservation Authority 2026), and has been shown to be more cost efficient than any other form of heating commonly available in New Zealand (Ellison et al. 2024). However, one reason that heat pump policies are still in their infancy in many countries is that there is contradictory evidence regarding their efficacy, in part because of difficulties in disentangling specific heat pump impacts from effects of other, often simultaneous, energy efficiency improvements.

We evaluate indoor temperature, relative humidity, electricity use, and subjective wellbeing impacts of a heat pump installation programme conducted through the New Zealand government's Warmer Kiwi Homes (WKH) programme, targeted at lower income households. 4 Specifically, we analyse impacts from installation of a heat pump in the living area of an already insulated house. The findings provide evidence of the impacts of a policy that promotes heat pump use in a developed country with a temperate climate.

The analysis uses household survey and monitoring data gathered from houses scheduled for heat pump installation over June 2021 to September 2022. Two cohorts of houses are included. The first cohort was scheduled to have heat pumps fitted in 2021 (the 2021 cohort) and the second was scheduled for heat pump installation in 2022 (the 2022 cohort). Analysis is conducted for two separate “seasons”: First winter (June–September 2021 for the 2021 cohort; June–September 2022 for the 2022 cohort), and Second winter (June–September 2022 for the 2021 cohort).

One‐fifth of New Zealand homes are described as cold and/or damp by occupants and a third had a winter daytime indoor temperature of less than 18°C (Stats NZ 2020; Pollard 2018; Taptiklis et al. 2022). Cold, damp housing contributes to poor health (Fisk et al. 2007; Shorter et al. 2018; Howden‐Chapman et al. 2021) and evidence shows that these negative effects can be mitigated through installation of retrofitted insulation (Howden‐Chapman et al. 2007; Grimes et al. 2012; Preval et al. 2017; Fyfe et al. 2020; O’Sullivan and Viggers 2021; Fyfe, Telfar‐Barnard et al. 2022). There is less thorough evidence regarding net benefits of installing heat pumps either in New Zealand or elsewhere, and the available evidence is conflicting (Lloyd et al. 2007; Howden‐Chapman et al. 2008; Preval et al. 2010; Marmot Review Team 2011; Grimes et al. 2012; Preval 2015; Grimes et al. 2016; Gibb et al. 2023). Several studies relating to broader retrofit programmes indicate that benefits may depend on contextual factors relating to household type, house characteristics, the environment, and the scheme itself (Stone et al. 2014; Barrington‐Leigh et al. 2019; Sovacool, et al. 2020; Rau et al. 2020; Carroll et al. 2020; Sharpe et al. 2020; Powells et al. 2022; Sawyer et al. 2022; Sustainability Victoria 2022). 5 While these studies indicate beneficial effects of housing retrofits across a diverse range of countries, there are few studies analysing benefits specifically attributable to fitting heat pumps.

We test heat pump impacts on occupants’ electricity use and on indoor environmental outcomes (temperature and relative humidity in the living area). We also examine effects of heat pump installation on the subjective wellbeing of residents. Section 2 outlines our data and methods while section 3 presents the results for effects of heat pump installation on indoor temperature, relative humidity, household electricity use, and occupant wellbeing. The discussion in section 4 includes analysis of whether the main temperature and electricity use findings vary according to particular household and house characteristics. Conclusions are presented in section 5.

Data and Methods

Data

Fyfe et al. (2022a, 2022b) provide details of the WKH scheme and of our sampling and data gathering strategy for houses included in the analysis. The sample covers New Zealand's three (pre‐2021) climate zones corresponding to the Building Code's H1 requirements : Auckland (climate zone 1), Waikato (climate zone 2), Wellington (climate zone 2) and Christchurch (climate zone 3). 6 Of the 127 houses in the 2021 cohort, 85 continued in the evaluation through 2022; they were supplemented by a new cohort of 37 Wellington houses beginning in 2022. Each house had a monitor 7 installed in the main living area to measure half‐hourly temperature and relative humidity before and after heat pump installation; outdoor temperature and relative humidity were also collated. 8 In addition, we collected smart meter half‐hourly electricity data from 121 households for which data were available.

These objective readings were supplemented by survey and inspection data. We administered a household questionnaire to the 2021 cohort in winter 2021 before heat pump installation to assess occupant wellbeing, energy use behaviours and self‐reported indicators of cold, dampness, and mould during the previous winter. The questionnaires were administered again to this cohort in spring (October/November) 2021 after most had received a heat pump. We refer to the two questionnaires henceforth as the Before and After surveys. The 2022 cohort completed the Before questionnaire in winter 2022 and the After questionnaire in spring 2022. For our subjective wellbeing analysis, we use the survey responses to resident perceptions of cold in their house and their responses to five questions (comprising the World Health Organization's WHO‐5 index), which refer to aspects of mental wellbeing over the 2 weeks prior to each survey. The questionnaire was supplemented, at the time of the Before survey, by a detailed assessment of indoor and outdoor house characteristics and condition. All 164 households (across the two cohorts) completed the Before survey while 153 completed the After survey.

For the 2021 cohort, logistics difficulties due to Covid‐19 lockdowns and the Suez Canal closure led to significant delays in heat pump installation. One effect of the variable delays in receiving a heat pump (and in some cases, not receiving a heat pump at all in the First winter period) was that date of heat pump installation was essentially randomised from the perspective of the household. At the time of the After survey, 19% of houses had yet to have the heat pump installed (or had had it installed for less than 1 week) while the maximum period with a heat pump was 20 weeks.

Fyfe et al. (2022b) detail demographic and household characteristics from the Before surveys. Almost a quarter of the sample (24.4%) comprised single person households and a further 29.9% were two person households (the remaining 45.6% had 3+ persons in the household). Most respondents were of working age with 30.5% aged at least 65 years (compared with an adult population proportion of 19.0%). 9 A majority of the sample (59.1%) was employed (compared with an adult population proportion of 68.6%). Approximately half of respondents reported having ‘more than enough’ (11.2%) or ‘enough’ (36.6%) income to meet their needs, but 44.7% stated they had ‘only just enough’ income and a further 7.4% had ‘not enough’. These proportions compared with adult population proportions of 21.1%, 47.0%, 23.3%, and 8.6% respectively. Overall, therefore, reflecting the eligibility criteria of the WKH scheme, sample participants on average experienced poorer income adequacy, were older, and were less likely to be in employment relative to the country's adult population. 10

The majority of participants lived in houses smaller than 100 m2, and most resided in detached single storey dwellings. Prior to heat pump installation, a third of houses were often or always draughty and over half the respondents said their house was always or often too cold in winter. Most study households (91%) heated their living room in winter (principally by electric heater) prior to the heat pump being installed, with almost a third always or often limiting their heating due to cost. Householder‐assessed dampness was always or often present in winter in a fifth of houses, while 62% reported that there was always or often condensation on living room windows during winter. Visible mould in the living area or bedroom was always or often present during winter in 15% of houses. Over half of the sample houses had at least one building envelope component in poor condition, while a fifth had three or more components in poor condition. 11

The WKH programme required the heat pump to be placed in the main living area. If the heat pumps were replacing another type of heater such as a fireplace, woodburner, or gas heater, a requirement was that these heaters were fully decommissioned. Most households who were present during installation received a brief training by the installer. Typically, this advice included turning the heat pump off when not at home and keeping the heat pump at a set temperature using the thermostat. Our survey of treated households showed that this training was often insufficient and poorly understood; as a result, many householders did not initially have a good understanding of best practice use. Consistent with our subsequent results, it is possible that practices improved with experience of using the heat pump.

Methods: Indoor Temperature, Relative Humidity and Electricity use

Analysis of heating outcomes over First winter utilises the staggered installation of heat pumps, so includes both the houses that received and did not receive a heat pump over the first winter. The dataset includes monitoring data prior to heat pump installation and post installation. 12 Over the 2 weeks prior to receiving a heat pump (in First winter), 47% of half‐hourly indoor temperature readings were below 18°C, while 27% were below 16°C; these proportions fell to 42% and 22% respectively for the 2 weeks after receiving a heat pump. In Second winter (when all houses had a heat pump), the proportions fell further to 37% and 18% respectively. While our difference‐in‐difference analysis is confined to First winter, these statistics indicate that any findings of warmer temperatures are likely to be maintained, or amplified, over Second winter. One reason that temperature effects may have been amplified over Second winter is that participants in treated houses may have modified their use of the heat pump as they learned which operational practices were best suited to their household situation.

We initially model the impact of heat pump installation in First winter on living area temperature. Our difference‐in‐difference specification controls for external temperature and includes fixed effects that control for: (i) each house in the study, to account for unchanging features of the house such as sun and house condition, and for unchanging features of the household such as number of occupants; 13 (ii) each half‐hour of the day, to account for different heating and living behaviours at particular times such as sleeping and meal times; and (iii) each separate day of the study for each climate zone (Day*CZone), to account for time‐varying national and regional factors, which may have varied by day that affected heat pump use. Inclusion of the day fixed effects also controls for the two (2021 and 2022) cohorts.

We leverage the fact that heat pumps were installed (with random timing from the household's perspective) on different days throughout the programme, with some properties not receiving a heat pump at all over First winter. The full equation for indoor temperature that we estimate is

Methods: Subjective Wellbeing

The Before and After surveys provide subjective wellbeing data both before and after the scheduled installation of a heat pump. The measures include perceptions of cold within the house and a measure of mental wellbeing. We use these data to examine the subjective impacts of heat pump installation for residents. We omit any household where we cannot be sure that the same individual answered the Before and After surveys leaving us with a sample of 130 respondents. Not all these respondents answered every survey question, so our sample size (including both Before and After responses) varies from 242 to 252 (depending on the variable).

We focus on two subjective measures. The first is Perceived cold (within the house) obtained from the question: ‘In the last 2 weeks, was your house/flat colder than you would like?’ 16 The potential responses were: ‘No; Yes – sometimes; Yes – often; Yes – always’ (numbered 1–4, respectively). 17 The mean response is 2.21 (i.e. a mean response that lies between Yes – sometimes, and Yes – often), with a standard deviation of 1.05.

Our second subjective variable is the WHO‐5 measure of mental wellbeing (World Health Organization 1998). WHO‐5 is derived from five questions, each of which is asked as: ‘Over the past 2 weeks, how often have you: (i) felt cheerful and in good spirits; (ii) felt calm and relaxed; (iii) felt active and vigorous; (iv) woken up feeling fresh and rested; and (v) felt that your daily life has been filled with things that interest you.’ (Henceforth, we shorten the descriptors to: Cheerful, Calm, Active, Rested, Interested). Each of the five questions has six potential responses: ‘Never, sometimes, less than half the time, more than half the time, most of the time, always’ (numbered 0–5 respectively). WHO‐5 adds the scores from the five subscales then multiplies the sum by 4 to give a mental wellbeing scale that ranges from 0 (low wellbeing) to 100 (high wellbeing). The WHO‐5 sample mean is 62 with a standard deviation of 16 (minimum = 16; maximum = 100). 18

We regress each of Perceived cold and WHO‐5 on two separate measures of heat pump installation. The first is a dummy variable, HP installed, which equals 1 if a heat pump had been installed by the survey date and equals 0 otherwise. All values are 0 for the Before survey while, of the 129 houses in the After survey, 82% had a value of 1 (i.e., 18% had yet to have a heat pump installed). The household fixed effects are important given that each respondent may have their own set‐point with respect to their personal perception of whether a house is cold or not (Rupp et al. 2023). Discussion

The temperature estimates reported in section 3 indicate that installation of a heat pump results in a warmer house during winter relative to the counterfactual of heating the same house with the household's prior heating devices. From Figure 1, internal temperature is estimated to reach 18°C – the WHO (2018) recommended indoor minimum temperature—once external temperature reaches 5°C, whereas without a heat pump that temperature is reached only once the external temperature reaches 12.5°C. A heat pump also reduces relative humidity.

Alongside these improvements in indoor temperature and humidity, electricity savings occur following heat pump installation. These savings reach a peak, both in terms of magnitude and statistical significance between 5 pm and 9 pm, despite substantial temperature gains also being experienced at these times. This result likely reflects that households were previously heating their living area in the evening using less efficient heating appliances prior to fitting the heat pump. Thus, during winter, installation of the heat pump is estimated to result in a warmer and drier house that uses less electricity than otherwise.

The estimates reported above include house fixed effects so control for unchanging household and house characteristics that affect baseline heating behaviours. It is possible that some characteristics may also affect the temperature and electricity use responses to installation of a heat pump (i.e., over and above any baseline impacts of those characteristics). To explore this possibility, we focus on two characteristics—one relating to the household and one to the house—which may influence the estimated effect of a new heat pump on objective outcomes (temperature and electricity use).

The first characteristic, which relates to the household, is the householder's perception of how well their income meets their needs. This Income‐meets‐needs variable is a (predetermined) indicator of their financial ability to heat their home. To test whether the estimated temperature and electricity responses vary according to Income‐meets‐needs, we form a binary variable, Income_binary (=1 if the household has ‘enough’ or ‘more than enough’ income, and = 0 if they have ‘not enough’ or ‘only just enough’). We interact Income_binary with the heat pump indicator (HP) and test whether the interaction coefficient is significantly different from zero for each of the temperature and electricity use specifications. 20 For temperature, the interaction coefficient has a p‐value = 0.429 while for electricity use, p = 0.304. In neither case, therefore, do we find that the estimated effects vary by the household's financial situation.

The second characteristic, which relates to the house, is the householder's perception of whether the house is draughty or not. We focus on this house characteristic as a draughty house is more likely to lose heat even with the installation of an improved heating device. We form a binary variable, Draughty_binary (=1 if the household reports their house is “sometimes”, “often” or “always” draughty and = 0 if it is reported to be “never” draughty). As before, we interact Draughty_binary with the heat pump indicator (HP) and test whether the interaction coefficient is significant for each of the temperature and electricity use specifications. For temperature, the interaction coefficient has p = 0.050 while for electricity use, p = 0.132. Hence, there is no strong evidence that draughtiness impacts electricity use but it likely impacts indoor temperature following installation of a heat pump. Specifically, a house that is never draughty is estimated to have an average 2.1°C lift in indoor temperature over winter when a heat pump is installed while a house that is draughty has a much reduced lift in temperature of 0.9°C (on average).

These preliminary tests imply that house characteristics may be more important than household characteristics in determining the impacts of a heat pump on indoor environmental outcomes following the fitting of a heat pump. These results, and especially the impact of draughtiness, suggest that further investigation of factors which moderate the impacts of heat pump installation on temperature and other outcomes is warranted.

Another objective aspect that warrants further attention is the impacts of a heat pump in seasons other than winter. In preliminary work, Fyfe et al. (2022b) find indications that heat pumps are used by some households as air conditioners over summer, especially in warmer parts of the country. If that is the case, then the annual electricity savings may be diminished relative to the degree of savings indicated by our winter estimates. 21 These preliminary findings relating to summer use were not able to leverage the same difference‐in‐difference setting as did the winter estimates due to all houses having a heat pump installed by summer. Consequently, we recommend a formal randomised controlled trial be undertaken of summer temperature and electricity use impacts of heat pump installation.

Estimated impacts of heat pump installation on subjective wellbeing are consistent with the positive outcomes seen in the objective data: Houses are warmer and drier after heat pump installation, and these objective outcomes translate into residents feeling warmer and experiencing an improvement in important aspects of their mental wellbeing.

Fyfe et al. (2022b) also reported findings of a cost benefit analysis of the heat pump component of the WKH programme. While the cost benefit analysis is not our focus here (since its findings are likely to be programme and country specific), we note that the study calculated a benefit cost ratio (BCR) for the WKH heat pump component of 2.2 when only objective outcomes were considered; the estimated BCR rose to 7.5 when subjective wellbeing outcomes were included. The importance of incorporating subjective as well as objective outcomes when evaluating the net benefits of a programme is highlighted by these figures.

Conclusions

The WKH programme, which included the retrofitting of heat pumps for lower socioeconomic households, resulted in their living areas becoming materially warmer and drier in winter, while simultaneously reducing their electricity use. These outcomes are consistent with the aims of the programme. They hold both for poorer and better off households, albeit within the context of our sample which, on average, comprised more financially disadvantaged households. House characteristics (specifically, draughtiness) are shown to affect the magnitude of the impact of a heat pump on internal temperature gains. Occupants record reductions in perceived cold in the house and have increased mental wellbeing especially through feelings of being active and being fresh and rested upon waking. These findings indicate that the WKH heat pump retrofit programme succeeded in achieving its health objectives (warmth, humidity, and wellbeing) while also reducing energy use in a sample of mainly lower income households living in dwellings with existing insulation but with inefficient prior heating sources. The findings can be used to inform policies in other temperate countries that are considering the promotion of heat pumps as household heating devices, especially amongst more financially disadvantaged recipients.
