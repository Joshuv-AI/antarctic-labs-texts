Body Region Dysmorphology Is Predictive of Genetic Diagnoses in Infants With Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: Benjamin M Helm, Leah Wetherill, Benjamin J Landis, Stephanie M Ware
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGenetic causes of congenital heart disease (CHD) are often underrecognized, though precision phenotyping may improve screening for genetic disorders. We demonstrate how body region dysmorphology predicts genetic diagnoses associated with CHD.MethodsWe used a test‐negative case–control study of CHD infants undergoing standardized genetics evaluations. We investigated correlations between body region dysmorphology (BRD) and CHD classes and developed novel models for predicting genetic diagnoses, including cytogenetic/monogenic disorders identified by genetic testing.ResultsIn 243/1008 patients with genetic diagnoses (24.1%), we found novel correlations between BRDs and CHD classes. Periorbital, maxilla/midface, ear, and mandible BRDs correlated with conotruncal CHD with genetic diagnoses (25.5%); left ventricular outflow tract CHD presented with few BRDs despite genetic diagnoses identified (18.5%). Septal and right ventricular outflow tract CHD had a wider spectrum of BRDs. Multivariable modeling identified multiple BRDs predicting genetic diagnoses, including of the face (OR = 2.57), forehead (OR = 2.55), neck (OR = 2.24), periorbital (OR = 1.57), and hands/feet (OR = 1.90) regions, after adjusting for extracardiac anomalies (OR = 3.65), CHD class (p = 0.02), and male sex (OR = 0.69). The BRD model had acceptable utility assessed by decision curve analysis. Face/forehead/neck BRDs predicted cytogenetic and monogenic diagnoses (ORs ranging 2.2–2.5), though BRDs of hands/feet associated with cytogenetic disorders (OR = 2.22). Males were less likely to have cytogenetic diagnoses compared to females (OR = 0.59), suggesting potential sex‐specific differences.ConclusionsThis is a novel investigation of body region dysmorphology patterns predictive of genetic diagnoses in CHD patients. Precision phenotyping can be an important part of care and allow clinicians to risk‐stratify young CHD patients suspected of having genetic disorders.Keywords: cardiovascular genetics, decision curve analysis, genetic testing, prediction, risk stratification

Introduction

Congenital heart disease/defects (CHD/CHDs) are the most common defects in humans, and genetic causes are identified in up to 20%–30% of patients (Pierpont et al. 2007, 2018; Zaidi and Brueckner 2017; Morton et al. 2022; Durbin, Helvaty, et al. 2023). However, genetic evaluations, including diagnostic genetic testing and geneticist examinations, remain underutilized within and across institutions caring for CHD patients (Durbin, Helvaty, et al. 2023). This has resulted in missed diagnostic opportunities impacting medical management and genetic counseling. Some centers have implemented minimum evaluation standards for CHD patients in pediatric intensive care settings, which have been shown to improve diagnosis and inform perioperative medical management with cost‐effectiveness (Shikany et al. 2020; Durbin, Fairman, et al. 2023; McAfee et al. 2021; Geddes et al. 2017, 2019). Previous research has highlighted the clinical utility of genetic testing for CHD using different modalities, including chromosome microarray (CMA) as a first‐line test and exome/genome sequencing (ES/GS) as potential first‐line tests in the future (Landis et al. 2023; Lalani et al. 2013; Alankarage et al. 2019; Griffin et al. 2023; Jin et al. 2017; Mone et al. 2021; Paige et al. 2018; Szot et al. 2018; Homsy et al. 2015; Hays et al. 2023; Guo et al. 2025). Adding to the complexity is the fact that most CHD is assumed to have oligogenic/polygenic, epigenetic, and multifactorial etiologies which will challenge clinical genetic testing strategies but motivate ongoing research (Morton et al. 2022; Theis and Olson 2022; Liu et al. 2017).

Lack of standardization in referrals for genetics evaluations for CHD may be influenced by under‐recognition of the utility of genetic diagnoses by clinical teams, in addition to a limited medical geneticist workforce (Durbin, Helvaty, et al. 2023; Jenkins et al. 2021; Nelson 2021). Failure to apply standardized genetic testing approaches across multiple institutions creates knowledge gaps regarding the prevalence of noncardiac phenotypic patterns associated with genetic diagnoses. Such patterns may be subtle and/or difficult to ascertain without clinician dysmorphology expertise. One of the strongest associations with the presence of genetic diagnoses in infants with CHD is the presence of extracardiac anomalies (ECA), that is, multiple congenital anomalies, indicative of syndromic genetic disorders (Pierpont et al. 2018; Shikany et al. 2020). This can bias clinical genetic testing strategies to be prioritized more often in patients with overtly syndromic presentations, despite the increasing recognition that genetic disorders occur in individuals with nonsyndromic, apparently isolated CHD (Shikany et al. 2020). Since geneticist evaluations are underused, there is an additional knowledge gap regarding the utility of dysmorphology evaluations for subtle minor anomalies; dysmorphic features may be predictive of genetic diagnoses, even in the absence of ECA or overtly recognizable syndromic clinical presentations.

While descriptive studies highlight characteristic dysmorphisms for well‐described genetic syndromes, few studies have specifically investigated aggregated dysmorphology patterns in CHD. Earlier studies confirmed increased risk of genetic disorders occurring in dysmorphic patients (Shikany et al. 2020; Ahrens‐Nicklas et al. 2016). We recently demonstrated that a dysmorphology score–an aggregate count of unique dysmorphisms per patient–is predictive of genetic disorders causing CHD (Helm et al. 2025). Prior to this, we determined that the presence of ≥ 1 dysmorphisms predicted a twofold increased risk of a genetic diagnosis; however, dysmorphology‐based screening for genetic disorders in CHD has some limitations (Helm et al. 2024). While the presence of ECA is the strongest predictor of genetic diagnoses to date, we have also shown that ECA‐centered screening for genetic disorders will also result in missed diagnoses. The limitations of ECA‐based or dysmorphology‐based screening are attributed to the prevalence of genetic diagnoses in apparently isolated CHD. Building upon the prior work investigating dysmorphology scores predicting the presence of genetic diagnoses in CHD, we sought to determine if there were dysmorphology patterns that might be informative for genetic disorder prediction.

The purpose of this study was to determine if there are novel dysmorphology patterns that would similarly inform prediction of genetic disorders—that is, moving from the “amount of dysmorphism” to the “types of dysmorphism” in patients. Therefore, we hypothesized that there are anatomic‐, or body‐region‐specific, dysmorphology patterns predictive of genetic diagnoses.

Primary and Secondary Aims

Our primary aims include the following: (1) Introduce the novel concept of body region dysmorphology/dysmorphism (BRD) as a phenotypic biomarker in CHD; (2) Describe BRD patterns in patients with genetic diagnoses and stratified by ECA status; and (3) Develop a prediction model incorporating ECA status, CHD class, and BRDs that may predict genetic diagnoses. An additional aim was to compare the performance of the body region dysmorphology model to the dysmorphology score model described above (Helm et al. 2025). A final aim was to investigate if BRD patterns might help predict types of genetic diagnoses, that is, chromosomal versus monogenic disorders. Results from this study may help improve clinical prediction of genetic disorders in CHD patients.

Methods

In following the Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) guidelines, the data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The Indiana University Institutional Review Board deemed this study exempt and did not require human subjects' informed consent (IRB #17818). Additional detailed methods are available in the Supporting Informations.

Overview and Study Design

This study originated from our institution's inpatient Cardiovascular Genetics program at Riley Hospital for Children/Indiana University Health (2014‐Present). The patient population consists primarily of neonates/infants (≤ 1 year) receiving standardized medical genetics evaluations. The Indiana University Institutional Review Board determined this study to be exempt (IRB #17818). The clinical consultative service includes the following: assessment by board‐certified medical geneticists and genetic counselors, coordination of genetic testing, and postconsultation follow‐up. We standardized genetic testing throughout the program, but it increased in comprehensiveness—prioritizing CMA initially, which progressed to exome sequencing (ES)‐based modalities and eventually genome sequencing (GS). We used a test‐negative case–control design, where case/control status is determined by diagnostic genetic testing results. Demographics and phenotypic data were collected at the time of consultation before results of genetic testing were available in most cases. This allowed retrospective investigation of associations between patient phenotypes and genetic testing diagnoses.

Subjects

The study included any patient admitted with CHD ≤ age 1 and referred to our inpatient cardiovascular genetics service. The period for this analysis was August 2014 through November 2023. We collected demographic data including age at evaluation (in days), age group (defined as neonate vs. infant, with the latter being > 30 days), and race/ethnicity (as reported in the electronic health record system). Early in the program, cases with suspected/confirmed trisomy 21 with CHD were not routinely referred for consultation (2014–2018); in late 2018, trisomy 21 referrals increased because of need for diagnostic confirmation and/or additional management. Exclusion criteria included patients with congenital heart disease (CHD) diagnosed after 1 year of age, those with known prenatal genetic diagnoses, and cases where dysmorphology assessment could not be completed due to clinical instability.

Case Classification

CHD was classified into mutually exclusive categories (Level 3) based on the framework developed by Botto and colleagues for the National Birth Defects Prevention Study (Botto et al. 2007). CHD classes included: anomalous pulmonary venous return (APVR); atrioventricular septal defects (AVSD); complex; conotruncal; heterotaxy/laterality spectrum defects; left ventricular outflow tract obstructions (LVOTO); right ventricular outflow tract obstructions (RVOTO); and septal defects. CHD was classified as “complex” for cases with multiple classes represented which could not be definitively categorized into one Level 3 class. Patients had evaluations by board‐certified medical geneticists, including documentation of ECA status, dysmorphology evaluation, and recommended genetic testing. ECA status was defined as presence of any noncardiac organ malformation(s) and/or major anomalies with medical significance (dichotomized as absent/present) as defined in the literature (Jones and Adam 2015; Adam and Hudgins 2003). ECA status also included clinically relevant functional anomalies, for example, seizures, dystonia, growth restriction, immunodeficiency, etc. (Jones and Adam 2015; Adam and Hudgins 2003). Dysmorphisms were defined as minor anomalies which may not have significant medical impact but could signify syndromic features. Dysmorphisms were defined and differentiated based on the Elements of Morphology (National Human Genome Research Institute: https://elementsofmorphology.nih.gov/). Dysmorphisms and ECA(s) were extracted from the medical record at the time of the consultation and by manual review of clinical genetics documentation and review of relevant neonatal, medical genetics, radiological, and cardiology documentation (BMH/SMW); CHD classification ambiguities were resolved by a pediatric cardiologist experienced with this classification scheme (BJL). ECA status and dysmorphology were distinguished based on guidelines for defining minor versus major anomalies and consistent with the Elements of Morphology (Jones and Adam 2015; Adam and Hudgins 2003). Phenotypic data did not include developmental disorders or other age‐dependent anomalies which cannot be ascertained at the time of evaluation for this mostly neonate/infant cohort.

We also defined each patient with a clinical description, including “apparently isolated/non‐syndromic CHD,” “apparently syndromic CHD (but not specified),” and “syndromic CHD.” The middle category was based on the presence of ECA/dysmorphisms in a patient who otherwise was unable to be clinically diagnosed by physical examination but who was suspected of having a possible genetic disorder/syndrome as noted by the geneticist. For apparently isolated/non‐syndromic descriptions, some patients could have ECA present (e.g., hypotonia), but geneticists had low/no suspicion for syndromic occurrence. Otherwise, the last category was reserved for patients who had a clinically diagnosed syndrome at the time of consultation and based on evaluations by the geneticists. For specifics of data collection, see the Supporting Informations.

Body Region Dysmorphology (BRD)

This study applied a novel method for documenting the body region for which discrete dysmorphism was occurring (body region dysmorphism(s), or BRDs). There are > 400 unique dysmorphisms detailed in the Elements of Morphology, and dysmorphisms were categorized by occurrence in one of the following 18 body regions as determined by the Elements of Morphology, including: cranium, scalp hair, face, forehead, maxilla/midface, mandible, chin, neck, periorbital, ear, nose, philtrum, lips, mouth, oral cavity, hands/feet, creases, and nails. To broaden this list based on geneticist evaluations, we added non‐Elements of Morphology minor anomalies in additional body regions, including nipple (spacing/symmetry), chest, sacrum, abdomen, genitalia, anus (placement/positioning), skin (texture/turgor/birth marks), and hair (texture/amount, nonscalp hair). Since each dysmorphic feature may be singly rare, and multiple rare dysmorphisms can co‐occur in a single body region, we collapsed dysmorphisms into a categorical variable reflecting the absence/presence of any dysmorphism(s) in a specific anatomic body region. We then classified patient dysmorphological patterns based on the occurrence of dysmorphism in any combination of these body regions to increase power of association testing. This resulted in each patient having numerous binary variables reflecting the absence vs. presence of dysmorphism(s) in these body regions.

Genetic Testing Practices and Diagnostic Outcomes

Our program has had three standardized minimum genetic testing strategies, including: (1) chromosome microarray (CMA) ± additional molecular genetic testing based on geneticist evaluations (or diagnostic prenatal genetic testing) (2014–2019); (2) CMA plus exome sequencing (ES) or ES‐based gene panels (2020–2022); and (3) genome sequencing (GS) ± additional genetic testing at the discretion of the genetics team (2022–Present). These practices have been described previously (Helm et al. 2025, 2021). Most of the cytogenetic tests—including CMA, karyotype, and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)—were performed by the Indiana University Cytogenetics Laboratory using standard methods. Molecular genetic testing, including gene panels, exome‐based gene panels, and exome sequencing (singleton, duo, or trio samples), were performed by commercial CAP/CLIA‐approved genetic testing laboratories in the United States using standard methodologies (GeneDx Inc., Gaithersburg, Maryland, USA; Prevention Genetics Inc., Marshfield, Wisconsin, USA). From 2019 to 2022, ES‐based methods used copy‐number variant (CNV) identification in next‐generation sequencing data, confirmed by CMA (Prevention Genetics Inc.). Genome sequencing was completed by Baylor Genetics Laboratory, Houston, TX. Beginning in 2019, all patients had CMA plus ES‐based testing at minimum, and the transition to GS occurred in 2022 (and continuing to the present).

Genetic testing results were classified as (1) negative/normal, (2) variant(s) of uncertain significance (VUSs), or (3) diagnostic (i.e., pathogenic or likely pathogenic results), following the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG)/Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) guidelines for variant interpretation. Diagnostic results were verified by laboratory geneticists and the consulting genetics teams. Strict interpretation was used for all VUS results, so no candidate CNVs or molecular VUSs were classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic results without additional supporting evidence or corroboration from previous research and/or additional data. Genetic testing results were classified as diagnostic (no vs. yes) for pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants that: (a) confirmed a genetic/syndromic diagnosis and/or (b) causally explained the CHD phenotype with confidence. Secondary and incidental findings were not classified as diagnostic and were not investigated in this study. For all diagnoses, a genetic diagnosis type was assigned to one of the following categories: cytogenetic, molecular genetic (monogenic), cytogenetic and molecular diagnoses (> 1 co‐occurring diagnoses), and clinical diagnosis (with uninformative genetic testing).

Statistical Analyses

We provide descriptive data for demographics, ECA status, genetic testing strategies, genetic diagnosis types, and dysmorphology findings (i.e., Body Region Dysmorphism(s), or BRDs). To assess differences in quantitative variables (e.g., age at consultation), we used Kruskal–Wallis test and provided the mean with standard deviation (SD) and the median with interquartile range (IQR). The main variables of interest were the BRDs, genetic diagnostic outcomes, and ECA status. Correlations between BRDs and CHD class, ECA status, and genetic diagnosis identified are described using tetrachoric correlation with results summarized descriptively in correlation heatmaps. We tested for differences in proportions using chi‐squared (X (Pierpont et al. 2018)), Fisher exact, or Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel X (Pierpont et al. 2018) tests and estimated effects‐measures with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals. We tested for associations between BRDs and genetic diagnosis, stratifying by ECA status. When assessing associations between BRDs and genetic diagnosis identified, we also restricted the patient sample to exclude cases of trisomy 21. These descriptive results informed later development of the multivariable model in this study and using purposive variable selection. Inference was based on review of two‐sided p‐values with type 1 error threshold of α < 0.05 for statistical significance and on review of odds ratio 95% confidence intervals. All analyses were performed in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Prediction Modeling

Multivariable binomial logistic regression was used to develop a prediction model incorporating BRD variables, ECA status, sex, CHD class, and adjusting for consulting geneticists (see Supporting Informations for details of model development, validation, and performance assessment adhering to guidelines) (Collins et al. 2015; Zamanipoor Najafabadi et al. 2020; Kerr et al. 2016; Van Calster et al. 2018). We also compared the BRD model to a prior “dysmorphology score” model we developed using decision curve analysis (DCA), as detailed in the Supporting Informations (Helm et al. 2025; Van Calster et al. 2018). Finally, we assessed how the model could predict cytogenetic versus molecular genetic diagnoses using multinomial logistic regression, as detailed in the supplemental methods (see Supporting Informations).

Results

Cohort Description and Associations With Genetic Diagnosis

Our cohort sample was n = 1008 patients, with all of them referred to the inpatient genetics team for evaluation and testing (Table 1). The patients were majority male (56.0%), with a median age of 3 days at evaluation, and were majority white/non‐Hispanic (Table 1). All CHD classes were represented, though LVOTO (25.8%) and conotruncal (24.5%) were most common. Less than half had ECA present (42.8%), and most patients were described clinically as having apparently isolated/nonsyndromic CHD (65.9%); some patients may have had ECA present, but consulting geneticists documented that the patient's CHD was likely nonsyndromic. There were 13 geneticists who completed evaluations, though one accounted for about 10% of all consultations; we showed subtle differences in geneticist practice and adjusted for this later in modeling. Most patients had genetic testing completed (97.8%), and n = 22 patients either had no genetic testing completed/documented or it was deferred/not recommended. GS and CMA had the highest diagnostic yields of 20.9% and 20.5%, respectively, though only 21% had GS available/offered at the time of this study; this was followed by a diagnostic yield of 15.1% for ES or ES‐based gene panels plus CMA. Overall, 24.1% (243/1008) had a genetic diagnosis identified—with most of these being cytogenetic diagnoses (see a list of diagnoses in Table S1).

We found that identifying a genetic diagnosis was predicted by CHD class (p < 0.0001), ECA‐positive status (p < 0.0001), apparently syndromic presentations (p < 0.0001), and genetic testing strategies (p = 0.0007 to p = 0.002). While males were more prevalent in the cohort, more females had genetic diagnoses identified (21.3% vs. 27.7%, respectively, p = 0.0199). Notably, there were no differences in proportions of patients with genetic diagnoses across age groups or race/ethnicity. We found a lower genetic diagnosis prevalence in patients with a history of maternal diabetes of 14.1% (pregestational or gestational). We observed incremental gains in genetic diagnosis identification over time; our previous work described this trend being explained by increasing detection of monogenic disorder with increased use of ES/GS (Helm et al. 2025).

Body Region Dysmorphology: Predicting Diagnostic Genetic Testing Results

We used the preceding results to inform variable prioritization in models using BRDs to predict/classify those with genetic diagnoses identified later by genetic testing. We excluded trisomy 21 and patients with missing data for relevant variables (resulting in a model sample size of n = 982, or 97.4% of the cohort). We also controlled for differences in consulting geneticists. The final model is summarized in Table 3. Several BRDs predicted genetic diagnoses, after adjusting for ECA status (OR = 3.65) and CHD class. Being male was associated with reduced risk of having a genetic diagnosis (OR = 0.69, p = 0.0368), and while CHD class was significant overall (p = 0.0194), the only significant difference was seen in the heterotaxy class which had reduced risk of genetic diagnosis compared to the reference conotruncal class (OR = 0.21, p = 0.0003). The strongest BRD associations were observed with the forehead (OR = 2.55 [1.39, 4.68]), followed by the neck (OR = 2.24 [1.21, 4.13]) and face (OR = 2.57 [1.34, 4.93]). BRDs with modest associations included the periorbital and hands/feet (Table 3). There were no statistically significant interactions between BRDs, CHD class, or ECA status (all p > 0.05). Modeling details can be reviewed in Figure S1 (Supporting Informations).

Body Region Dysmorphology: Predicting Types of Genetic Disorders

We then tested whether the significant BRDs from the previous model could predict or differentiate cytogenetic vs. molecular genetic diagnoses. We excluded the few cases with > 1 coinciding diagnosis type (n = 5) and trisomy 21. Table S2 summarizes descriptive associations between face, forehead, neck, periorbital, and hands/feet BRDs with genetic diagnosis types (supplemental materials); overall, there appeared to be more prevalent cytogenetic diagnoses across these five BRDs compared to molecular genetic diagnoses. Table S3 (Supporting Informations) summarizes the output of the multinomial model assessing cytogenetic vs. molecular genetic diagnoses. Overall, ECA‐positive status associated with increased risk of either cytogenetic or molecular genetic diagnoses. Males were less likely to have cytogenetic diagnoses compared to females (OR = 0.59, p = 0.0046), though molecular diagnoses were identified equally across sexes (p = 0.92). Presence of face, forehead, neck, and hands/feet BRDs indicated a 2.2‐ to 2.7‐fold risk of cytogenetic diagnoses. However, only face and neck BRDs were associated with molecular genetic diagnoses; association between face dysmorphism and molecular genetic diagnoses was marginally nonsignificant (OR = 2.50, p = 0.0523). While periorbital BRD was nonsignificant (p = 0.07), dysmorphisms in this region were seen more in molecular genetic disorders than cytogenetic diagnoses (OR = 1.92, p = 0.0351). CHD class was nonsignificant for both types of genetic diagnoses, though it was retained due to better model calibration. Figure S4 depicts the OR plots summarizing these patterns. Overall, there were subtle BRD pattern differences across cytogenetic versus molecular genetic disorders.

Discussion

Our standardized genetics evaluations for CHD patients allowed us to examine the predictive potential of the cardiac and noncardiac dysmorphology phenotypes for identifying genetic diagnoses. We demonstrated that genetic disorders are prevalent in CHD patients, with over 24% having genetic diagnoses identified. The test‐negative case–control design allowed us to investigate phenotypic predictors ascertainable by geneticists prior to genetic testing results. We applied a novel method for quantifying the associations between body region dysmorphisms and genetic diagnoses in CHD patients—including patterns representative of cytogenetic and molecular genetic diagnoses. This built upon our previous work of implementing a dysmorphology score method for genetic diagnostic prediction, showing that the amount of dysmorphism and where dysmorphisms occur can be quantified for prediction (Helm et al. 2025).

Our results provide evidence that dysmorphisms in certain body regions are predictive of diagnostic genetic testing results, even after adjusting for multiple congenital anomalies, that is, ECA status. For example, dysmorphism of the forehead, neck, and face was most predictive of genetic diagnoses, with each being associated with approximately twofold increased risk. Model predictions suggested that diagnostic yield of genetic testing would be approximately ≥ 20%–30% for ECA‐negative patients with ≥ 1 dysmorphism(s) in at least one of these regions (compared to ~14% for non‐dysmorphic status). These estimates ranged 38%–60% in similar patients but with ECA‐positive status. To our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify associations between body region dysmorphology and genetic diagnoses in CHD patients, building on previous investigation of dysmorphic classifications of CHD patients (Shikany et al. 2020; Ahrens‐Nicklas et al. 2016). In previous studies, investigators relied on retrospective classification of “dysmorphic” and “non‐dysmorphic,” but our study is advantaged by larger sample sizes and prospective detailing of dysmorphology phenotypes reflecting multiple geneticists' practices. We were able to report on the most relevant body region dysmorphisms for predicting genetic diagnoses, including in patients without recognized syndromes by geneticist examination. For example, 24% of patients had “apparently syndromic, not otherwise specified” clinical presentations, and identification and documentation of dysmorphism(s) in the forehead, face, neck, periorbital, or hands/ft regions would indicate increased risk of genetic disorders—with even higher risk for additive combinations of these. The “body region dysmorphology” framework may inform development of phenotype‐driven risk estimates for genetic disorders occurring in young CHD patients. Such tools could help risk‐stratify CHD patients at critical times of medical decision‐making and/or perioperative care early in life—prior to having genetic testing results available or with clinical diagnostic uncertainty by geneticists (McAfee et al. 2021; Landis et al. 2022). Similar approaches have been used to investigate dysmorphology patterns in individuals with autism spectrum disorder, and in future work we will explore how these phenotypic patterns in CHD patients might also predict longer‐term neurodevelopmental outcomes (Shapira et al. 2019; Tian et al. 2020). In circumstances where dysmorphisms do not identify a well‐characterized genetic syndrome, dysmorphology phenotypes might serve as proxies for genetic diagnoses and allow refined counseling for families either preceding genetic testing results or in settings with limited genetic testing availability/resources. CHD is associated with increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders, including patients with and without genetic diagnoses, and dysmorphological phenotypes may inform prognostication (Marino et al. 2012; Howell et al. 2019; Mebius et al. 2017; Rollins et al. 2017).

The strongest dysmorphism predictors of genetic diagnoses—including cytogenetic and molecular genetic disorders—occurred in patients with forehead and face dysmorphism. Another surprising result was that males were less likely to have a cytogenetic diagnosis compared to females, though this pattern was not seen for molecular genetic diagnoses. The observation that cytogenetic disorders were more enriched in females raises additional questions about differences in gestational survival of CHD/chromosomal abnormalities in females over males. It is unclear why forehead, face, and neck dysmorphisms had the strongest associations with genetic diagnoses. A reasonable explanation might be related to variation in genes involved with common developmental programs of the heart, brain/central nervous system (CNS), and craniofacial regions. For example, there are numerous genes encoding growth factors, transcription factors, and chromatin modifiers linked to delineated syndromes with craniofacial dysmorphisms as well as CHD—for example, PTPN11, TBX1, TBX5, KMT2D, and ZEB2 (Cao et al. 2017; Birkhoff et al. 2021; Shpargel et al. 2020; Funato et al. 2012; Liao et al. 2022; Gu et al. 2022; Nührenberg et al. 2014; Ford et al. 2025). Additionally, craniofacial development requires numerous members of the BMP/TGF‐β, Wnt, FGF, Notch, and Hedgehog pathways that are also critical for cardiac development (Liao et al. 2022; Gu et al. 2022; Nührenberg et al. 2014; Gou et al. 2015). It is also possible that copy‐number variants associated with craniofacial dysmorphisms and CHD may impact dosage‐sensitive genes, noncoding RNAs, or regulatory non‐coding regions involved with heart/craniofacial development. Our results may help contribute to future research investigating the overlap between maldevelopment of the craniofacial regions and CHD. The finding that dysmorphism of the hands/feet was predictive of cytogenetic diagnoses raises questions for future research. Forehead dysmorphisms may be reflective of underlying brain/CNS malformations and highlight potential genetic mechanisms of the association between CHD and neurodevelopmental disorders. Associations between CHD and nonacquired/noninjurious CNS anomalies have been investigated previously (Jones 1991; Glauser et al. 1990; Khalil et al. 2014; Kessler et al. 2020). This is potentially compelling since surgical factors may not play a major role in neurodevelopmental outcomes in CHD patients (Nattel et al. 2017). Additional research will explore the connection between genetic disorders in CHD patients with forehead/craniofacial dysmorphisms.

The results of this study are potentially useful for a wide range of clinicians involved in pediatric CHD care—cardiologists, medical geneticists, genetic counselors, and neonatologists. Our findings suggest that recognition of body region dysmorphism has utility even with inconclusive or nondiagnostic physical examination, considering that commonly recurring syndromes can elude geneticist recognition based on physical evaluation. However, applications of phenotype‐based risk models may be limited for CHD patients for a few reasons: (1) dysmorphisms may be challenging to recognize in young patients—even for experienced geneticists; (2) interventions in intensive care settings can impede detailed physical examination, for example, mouth, lips, and oral cavity dysmorphisms due to intubation; (3) we found that almost 8% of patients presented as apparently isolated/non‐syndromic CHD but had genetic diagnoses identified. Similarly, the predicted probability of genetic diagnosis in ECA‐negative and nondysmorphic patients was 14.8% based on our model. The decision curve analysis also informs policies for genetic testing in CHD patients. While clinicians may vary in their risk thresholds for considering genetic testing in CHD patients, this analysis suggests that if the chance of a genetic disorder occurring is ≤ 9%, then decisions to pursue genetic testing may not be significantly informed by dysmorphology. A Test‐All policy would be justifiable, independent of dysmorphology evaluation findings, and this is largely influenced by genetic disorders identified in apparently isolated and/or non‐ or minimally‐dysmorphic CHD patients.

Given the prevalence of genetic disorders in CHD patients and recognizing the limitation of dysmorphological evaluations in guiding diagnostic genetic testing, our results may support wider population genetic screening. We have previously shown the limitations of dysmorphology evaluation to screen CHD patients for genetic disorders, and recent work also supports policies favoring genetic testing for all pediatric CHD patients (Helm et al. 2024). More research is needed to determine the most efficient, cost‐effective genetic testing strategy, but this will likely be shown to be genome sequencing since it is the only singular strategy capable of detecting the wide spectrum of cytogenetic and molecular genetic causes. Finally, dysmorphology evaluation will continue to play an important role for CHD patients, though it may not sufficiently inform decisions to do genetic testing. Instead, it will have critical roles in diagnostic confirmation following genetic testing, adjudicating variants of uncertain significance, investigating novel genes and variants, and identifying rare or novel genetic disorders associated with CHD.

Limitations

This study has several notable limitations. First, misclassification bias is possible in any case–control study; however, our test‐negative case–control design may limit this and has many advantages (Vandenbroucke and Pearce 2019). Using the test‐negative case–control design, it is possible that assigning control status may be influenced by reporting practices of genetic testing laboratories (negative test results), though all clinical laboratories should follow the ACMG/AMP reporting guidelines. The estimate of “negative” test results and assignment of control status to patients may lead to an underestimate of genetic diagnoses in this study—for example, for currently unknown novel gene‐CHD associations; this may underestimate the ORs of study variables. Documentation of phenotypic predictors generally occurred before case status was determined by genetic testing—often weeks after initial geneticist consultation—so labs have clinical information to inform variant interpretation and reporting. To avoid influencing geneticist practices, we did not inform them about this study, and results should reflect real‐world dysmorphology practice. Our results likely capture an “average” effect of geneticists' practices and limit this bias. Due to the intensive care setting, it is possible that some relevant dysmorphisms were missed—for example, lips, mouth, oral cavity—because of intubation, surgery, etc. It is possible that this influenced our study results to inadvertently focus on those body regions that were most easily ascertained. Next, the “collapse” approach of body region dysmorphology in this study assumes that presence of dysmorphisms have the same direction of association—that is, positively associated with genetic disorders. While it is less plausible, some features may have an opposite associational direction and be considered normal variation in infants (e.g., depressed nasal bridge). While our cohort size was adequate, we had reduced power to detect interactions or to consider more granular cardiac classifications—especially given the overall rarity of discrete dysmorphisms. We also do not classify CHD based on physiological impact or cyanotic versus acyanotic status, though we will explore this in future studies. Our cohort is represented by infants and races/ethnicities common to central Indiana; future studies are needed for more diverse populations and inclusive of other age groups. Last, we did not perform external validation of our predictive models. External validation studies with larger sample sizes across different centers would be needed but remain unavailable.

Conclusions

Over 24% of infants with CHD had genetic diagnoses identified. We found that there are body region dysmorphisms that can help predict diagnostic genetic testing results. Dysmorphisms in the forehead, face, neck, periorbital, and hands/feet regions were associated with an approximately twofold increased risk of genetic diagnoses, even after adjusting for presence of multiple congenital anomalies. Forehead/face dysmorphisms were the strongest predictor of both cytogenetic and molecular genetic diagnoses, though cytogenetic diagnoses had a wider range of body region dysmorphisms. These results can inform the development of phenotype‐driven prediction models and allow early risk‐stratification of CHD patients (Stark et al. 2025).
