Ten-year trajectory of Life’s Essential 8 and aortic valve calcification
Authors: Zhijun Wu, Man Gui, Wei Huang, Maoxiang Zhao, Cheng Jin, Shuohua Chen, Shouling Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe American Heart Association developed Life’s Essential 8 (LE8) to improve the predictive ability of cardiovascular health (CVH).AimsWe sought to investigate whether a 10-year CVH trajectory is associated with the prevalence of aortic valve calcification (AVC), an early manifestation of calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD).MethodsThe current study included 8047 participants who participated in the surveys at baseline (2006) and during follow-up periods (2008, 2010, 2012, 2014 and 2016). Repeated measurements of LE8 components including body mass index, cigarette smoking, diet quality, physical activity, sleep health, lipids, blood glucose and blood pressure, were used to construct the CVH trajectories. Ten-year CVH trajectory patterns from 2006 to 2016 were constructed using latent mixture models. AVC was diagnosed by echocardiography from 2016 to 2023.ResultsDuring an eight-year follow-up period, 1005 AVC cases were identified. Compared to the moderate-decrease trajectory, the low-stable trajectory was associated with higher odds of AVC (odd ratio (OR): 1.46; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.17, 1.83). Participants in the groups of moderate-increasing trajectory (OR: 0.78; 95%CI: 0.62, 0.98), moderate-stable trajectory (OR: 0.62; 95%CI: 0.52, 0.74), and high-stable trajectory (OR: 0.30; 95%CI: 0.20, 0.43) had lower odds of AVC compared with those in the moderate-decreasing trajectory group. The association between the CVH trajectory and AVC prevalence was more pronounced in men, younger individuals and those without chronic kidney disease.ConclusionsMaintaining or improving CVH was associated with lower odds of AVC. Our findings suggest optimizing CVH may provide a primordial preventive benefit against subclinical CAVD.

Introduction

Calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) is a slow, progressive disease characterized by passive calcium deposition on the aortic valve or annulus, leading to impaired leaflet motion [1]. This process would result in severe valve stenosis and consequently substantial disability and mortality [2]. Aortic valve calcification (AVC) represents an early phase of CAVD, transitioning from sclerosis in the early stage to stenosis in the later stage [3]. The global incidence and mortality of CAVD increased 3.51-fold and 1.38-fold from 1990 to 2019, even after age standardization [4].

Currently, pharmacological strategies for preventing or slowing the progression of CAVD are limited and ineffective [5]. Lipid-lowering drugs (e.g., statins) [6], blood pressure (BP)-lowering drugs (e.g., angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors) [7] and bisphosphonates [8] have failed to halt the CAVD progression in randomized trials. Surgical valve replacement remains the only effective therapy for severe CAVD [9]. It is estimated that approximately 290,000 individuals aged >75 years qualify for transcatheter aortic valve replacement in Europe and North America, with an additional 27,000 new candidates emerging annually [10]. This growing demand imposes a high financial and physiological burden on aging populations worldwide [11].

Recent studies found that atherosclerosis-related features such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus and smoking are also linked to CAVD, supporting the hypothesis that modifying risk factors may be critical for primary prevention [12,13]. In 2010, the American Heart Association (AHA) introduced “Life’s Simple 7 (LS7)”, which included four health behaviors (smoking, diet, physical activity, body weight) and three metabolic factors (blood glucose, cholesterol, BP) to promote ideal cardiovascular health (CVH) in the general population [14]. In 2022, the AHA refined this framework by adding sleep health as the eighth component. The updated CVH assessment score was named as “Life’s Essential 8 (LE8)”, allowing for a more detailed assessment of CVH [15].

Our previous studies reported the CVH score based on LE8 metrics was significantly associated with the occurrence of stroke [16], myocardial infarction and cardiovascular death [17]. However, the association between CVH and CAVD remains unclear. In the current study, we used six repeated measurements of LE8 metrics collected between 2006 and 2016 to construct 10-year CVH trajectory patterns. We aimed to investigate whether population-level changes in CVH over time are associated with the prevalence of AVC, an early indicator of CAVD, in a Chinese cohort.

Methods

Study population

The current study is based on the Kailuan study, a large, ongoing community-based prospective cohort study conducted in the Kailuan community (Tangshan City, Hebei Province, China). This study is designed to investigate genetic and environmental risk factors and interventions for chronic diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases and cerebrovascular diseases, as well as other non-communicable diseases. Between June 2006 and October 2007, a total of 101,510 employees and retired workers (81,110 men and 20,400 women, aged 18–98 years) of the Kailuan Group Corporation were recruited into the cohort. Each participant underwent a standardized questionnaire, physical examination, and laboratory test at baseline and in subsequent biennial follow-up surveys conducted by trained nurses in 11 hospitals affiliated by the Kailun Group, as described in detail elsewhere [18,19].

A total of 8665 individuals participated in all follow-up visits from 2006 to 2016 and subsequently underwent echocardiographic assessment between 2016 and 2023. Participants were excluded if they had: (1) a history of rheumatic valvular disease (n = 3), bicuspid aortic valve malformation (n = 4), or prior aortic valve replacement (n = 2); (2) documented aortic valve calcification or aortic stenosis before 2016 (n = 501); or (3) missing information on LE8 components between 2006 and 2016 (n = 108). All aortic valve conditions were ascertained through review of medical records and echocardiographic findings according to established diagnostic criteria [5]. Ultimately, 8047 participants were included in the final trajectory analysis (Supplemental Fig. 1).

The study adhered to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki guidelines and was approved by the ethics committee of Kailuan General Hospital (No.2006–05). All participants provided written informed consent.

Assessment of LE8 metrics and other covariates

Information on demographic details, socioeconomic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, education, occupation and monthly salary), lifestyle behaviors (e.g., cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, dietary intake, sleep health and physical activity), medication use and chronic disease history (e.g., hypertension or diabetes mellitus) was collected using a standard questionnaire at baseline and updated every 2 years [17]. Since detailed dietary data was unavailable until 2014, we used a combination of salt intake, tea intake and fatty food consumption as a surrogate measure for diet quality [20].

Weight and height were measured according to standardized procedures by trained study staff. Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in meters. BP was measured twice after individuals rested for at least 5 min, and the average of two readings was recorded [21].

Fasting blood samples were collected via venipuncture into EDTA vacuum tubes and stored at -80 °C in the Central Laboratory of Kailuan General Hospital. Glucose concentrations were measured using the hexokinase/glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase method (coefficient of variation< 2.0 %, BioSino Bio-technology and Science Inc., Beijing, China) [22]. High density lipoprotein cholesterol and low density lipoprotein cholesterol were measured using a direct test method (Mind Bioengineering Co. Ltd, Shanghai, China) with upper limits of detection of 12.90 mmol/L and 3.88 mmol/L, total cholesterol was measured using an endpoint test method. Triglyceride was measured using an enzymatic colorimetric method (Mind Bioengineering Co. Ltd, Shanghai, China). Creatinine was measured using a sarcosine oxidase assay method (BioSino Bio-technology and Science Inc., Beijing, China). The intra- and inter-assay variable coefficients for each measurement were <10 % [23]. Non-high density lipoprotein cholesterol was calculated as total cholesterol minus high density lipoprotein cholesterol. High sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) concentrations were measured using a high-sensitivity particle-enhanced immunonephelometric assay (Cias Latex CRP-H, Kanto Chemical Co. Inc., Japan) [24]. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated based on the Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) Epidemiology Collaboration equation, incorporating creatinine, sex, and age [25,26].

Assessment of CVH

The overall CVH score was calculated as the average of all LE8 components, following the AHA Presidential Advisory guidelines [15] . The total CVH score ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores suggesting a better CVH status. The definition and scoring method of each LE8 metric have been described previously [20].

Ascertain of AVC

Quantitative echocardiography was performed to diagnose AVC following a standardized protocol in the Kailuan General Hospital and Linxi Hospital affiliated by the Kailuan community between 2016 and 2023. Aortic valve area, mean gradient across the valve, and peak flow velocity were assessed by trained echocardiographers using PHILIPS EPIQ Elite, IE33, or EPIQ 7C ultrasound system [27]. Valvular opacification is defined as focal, bright, dense echogenic area of the aortic valve leaflets that diminish posterior to the aortic wall echoes [28,29]. AVC severity was graded semi-quantitatively as absent (translucent, flexible leaflets without visible calcification), mild (valvular opacifications ≥1 mm on one or more cusps, without significant reduction in mobility), and severe (definite calcification with leaflet thickening and stiffness, accompanied by restricted motion) [30]. Because only a small number of participants were identified with calcific aortic stenosis, AVC was analyzed as a binary outcome (absent vs. mild-to-severe calcification).

Two cardiologists from the Kailuan endpoint committee reviewed and confirmed all AVC events annually before the present analysis. For validation, another two independent cardiologists, who were blinded to the study design, randomly re-evaluated these images data. The concordance between the original adjudication and the blinded re-evaluation was high (Cohen’s κ= 0.94, P < 0.001). Any discrepant cases were resolved through consensus discussion. These results support the reliability of AVC event adjudication in our study.

Between 2016 and 2023, 55.5 % of participants underwent one echocardiographic examination, 22.0 % underwent two examinations, 9.8 % underwent three examinations, and 1.09 % underwent ≥10 examinations. Participants with AVC were more likely to undergo repeated echocardiographic measurements compared with those without AVC (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

The CVH trajectories were modeled using latent mixture models, identifying subgroups with similar CVH trajectories through the PROC TRAJ procedure [17]. This method allowed for clustering individuals based on long-term LE8 metrics changes over time [31,32]. Model fit was estimated using the Bayesian information criterion with five- trajectory model providing the best fit. Logistic regression was used to calculate the odd ratios (ORs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) for prevalent AVC after 2016 across the CVH trajectories after adjustment for age, sex, monthly salary, alcohol consumption, eGFR, education, occupation and family history of myocardial infarction or stroke. The moderate-decreasing trajectory was set as the reference group. To determine whether the CVH-AVC association was explained by a single measurement of CVH, we further adjusted for the CVH scores measured at 2006 and 2016, separately. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex, age and eGFR level, even though interaction terms were not significant. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding individuals who developed atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases between 2006 and 2016 or those who used lipid-lowering drugs to reduce the confounding by comorbidity and medication. Given that salt, tea, and fatty food consumption may not adequately reflect diet quality, we additionally used the Eating Index–2015 (HEI-2015) and the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) score, derived from diet data collected in 2014, to construct the LE8 score [16].

All analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 8047 participants with a mean age of 49.3 ± 10.4 years (men:73.7 %) were included in the trajectory analysis. We identified 5 distinct trajectory patterns according to baseline and follow-up CVH scores: low-stable (n = 854); moderate-decreasing (n = 1957); moderate-increasing (n = 1174); moderate-stable (n = 3167) and high-stable (n = 895) (Fig. 1). Participants with the optimal trajectories (high-stable and moderate-stable trajectory) were more likely to be women, have higher education levels and monthly salary, and exhibit lower hs-CRP concentrations. Discussion

In the current study, which analyzed a 10-year trajectory, we identified five distinct CVH trajectory patterns and found that they were significantly associated with AVC prevalence. Participants with long-term poor CVH status (the low-stable trajectory) had 46 % increased odds of AVC, while those with long-term better CVH status (the high-stable trajectory and moderate-stable trajectory) and those who showed improvement in CVH (the moderate-increasing trajectory) had reductions of 71 %, 40 % and 23 %, respectively, in the odds of AVC, compared to those with the moderate-decreasing trajectory. These associations were independent of CVH scores measured at baseline or at the last visit.

Previous studies have reported a similar association between CVH and CAVD. In the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities Study and the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, a higher CVH score, based on 6 metrics (total cholesterol, BP, fasting glucose, physical activity, body mass index, smoking status) was significantly associated with a lower risk of AVC or aortic stenosis [33,34]. In the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition-Norfolk study, CVH with LS7 metrics significantly predicted the occurrence of CAVD [35]. A previous study reported an inverse association between mean CVH score with LE8 components and prevalent AVC. In that analysis, the CVH score was derived from the most recent health examination data before echocardiographic assessment, and the dynamic changes in CVH over time were not evaluated [36]. Individuals with similar mean CVH levels may follow different temporal patterns (e.g., improving versus declining), which may have distinct biological and disease-related implications. Our study extends those findings by capturing longitudinal CVH trajectories across repeated examinations over 10 years, thereby characterizing distinct exposure patterns that better reflect cumulative cardiovascular risk evolution in real-world clinical settings. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine 10-year dynamic changes in CVH using LE8 metrics and their association with the prevalence of AVC over an 8-year follow-up period in a large-scale Chinese cohort. Trajectory-based analyses demonstrate that sustained or improving CVH is associated with lower odds of AVC, providing incremental insight beyond static or average measures alone. Long-term adhere to healthy lifestyle behaviors and maintenance of better metabolic status can help prevent subclinical CAVD.

Prior statin-based pharmacotherapy trials failed to demonstrate that lipid lowering halts the progression of valvular calcification despite significant reduction in low density lipoprotein cholesterol [[37], [38], [39]].The discrepancy between clinical benefit observed in our study and the neutral findings of these trials may be explained by differences in population characteristics, disease stage and baseline CVD risk. The long-term favorable CVH trajectories observed in our community-based population may reflect the benefit of earlier modification of lifestyle and metabolic risk factors in preventing the initiation and progression of AVC. Controlling lifestyle and metabolic factors, as integrated within the LE8 framework, has been shown to be associated with lower risk and slower progression AVC [40]. Lifestyle-based CVH may confer primary preventive benefits against AVC through systematic and comprehensive mechanisms, including reduction of inflammation, improvement of endothelial function, attenuation of atherosclerotic burden, mitigation of cardiac stress and adverse remodeling, and suppression of prothrombotic and osteogenic signaling pathways [[41], [42], [43]]. These effects act upstream of osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells, thereby potentially preventing the initiation of irreversible mineral deposition within the valve leaflets [44,45].

In contrast, previous trials primarily enrolled patients with established aortic stenosis, representing a more advanced stage of calcific disease. Their findings suggest that late-stage, single-pathway pharmacologic intervention may be insufficient to reverse or halt the self-propagating osteogenic process characteristic of CAVD. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether pharmacologic monotherapy initiated at an earlier stage would provide cardiovascular protection, as asymptomatic community-based individuals are not routinely screened for subclinical AVC unless other indications (e.g., hyperlipidemia) prompt clinical evaluation. Large-scale randomized controlled trials incorporating population-based screening strategies are needed to determine whether early pharmacologic intervention can reduce the risk of incident or progressive AVC among individuals at mild to moderate cardiovascular risk.

Interestingly, we found the association between the CVH trajectory and AVC prevalence varied by sex, age and CKD status. This variation is likely due to the sex- and age- specific differences in lifestyle behaviors and the etiology of CAVD. The CVH-AVC association was more pronounced in men, younger participants, or those without CKD. These participants had poor lifestyle habits but better metabolic status compared to their counterparts in most CVH trajectory patterns. A high proportion of smokers (>90 %) in these participants, as indicated by a lower score of cigarette smoking, suggests that cigarette smoking may have greater influence on the development of AVC in these participates. Longitudinal studies have also reported the association between CAVD and smoking status. There was a dose-dependent association between cumulative smoking exposure and the presence and extent of degenerative aortic valve diseases including AVC [46]. A time-dependent decrease in CAVD risk was also observed in former smokers, with those who had quit for 10 years exhibiting a CAVD risk comparable to that of never-smokers [47]. Given the relatively small sample size of women, older adults, and CKD patients in the Kailuan study, the impacts of smoking on CAVD remain unclear in these populations. It is also worth noting that postmenopausal osteoporosis is an sex-specific risk factor for AVC [48]. Aging and CKD are well-established risk factors for CAVD [4,49]. Lifestyles behaviors and metabolic status may not fully explain the occurrent and procession of AVC in these participants. Further studies for specific populations are warranted to explore how overall CVH status influences AVC progression.

We also found that the scores of metabolic factors, particularly BP, gradually increased from poor to optimal CVH trajectories in all participants. In the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis study, metabolic syndrome-a cluster of conditions including central adiposity, hypertriglyceridemia, low how density lipoprotein cholesterol, elevated blood pressure, and/or impaired fasting glucose-was associated with a significant increase in incident AVC [50]. In a large cohort study of 5.4 million UK participants with a median follow-up of 9.2 years, each 20 mmHg increase in systolic BP was associated with a 41 % increased risk of CAVD [51]. Together with our study, these results suggest that maintaining better metabolic health, particularly good BP control, might be crucial in preventing the occurrence of CAVD.

Our study has several limitations. First, our cohort consisted predominantly of Chinese adults from the Kailuan community, which limited generalizability of our findings to other ethnic populations. Second, we did not assess dietary scores recommended for CVH evaluation until 2014 [15]. We thus used a combined score based on salt, fatty food, and tea intake as a surrogate measure of diet quality. Third, only 10 participants developed calcific aortic stenosis and met the inclusion criteria, which limited the statistical power to analyze stenosis-specific outcomes. Therefore, we used the presence of AVC as the primary outcome to well capture earlier-stage valvular pathology in this community-based population. Fourth, more than half of the participants (55.5 %) underwent only once echocardiographic examination between 2016 and 2023 and did not receive systematic echocardiographic assessments at earlier period (2006–2016). Therefore, we were unable to determine the precise onset or baseline severity of subclinical CAVD prior to imaging. Participants who developed AVC or aortic stenosis before 2016 but were firstly diagnosed during 2016–2023 may have been misclassified in our analysis. In addition, longitudinal echocardiographic follow-up was not available for all participants, limiting our ability to evaluate the natural history and temporal progression of AVC (e.g., changes in mean gradient, peak velocity, or valve area) over time. Fifth, echocardiography could not quantify AVC. Computed tomography provides a more precise measure of the density and volume of AVC [52]. Nevertheless, echocardiography remains a non-invasive, cost-effective and practical method for screening AVC in a community-based cohort. Sixth, lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)) was not included as a covariate because it is not routinely measured in the Kailuan study. As Lp(a) is a genetically determined and lifelong risk factor for aortic valve calcification, the lack of adjustment for Lp(a) may have introduced residual confounding. Seventh, Participants without echocardiographic data due to pre-assessment mortality were excluded in the current study, which would result in survivor bias. However, baseline characteristics were comparable between included and excluded participants and those who died before examination (Supplementary Table 2), and sensitivity analyses using inverse probability weighting produced results consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 3). These findings suggest that selection bias related to differential survival is unlikely to materially affect the observed association.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that long-term adherence to better CVH status or improvement in CVH, such as avoiding unhealthy lifestyle behaviors and maintaining a healthy metabolic profile is significantly associated with a low prevalence of AVC. Further longitudinal study with representative and prospective data are needed to assess the causal association between CVH status and the occurrence of AVC.
