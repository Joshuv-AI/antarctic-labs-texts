Single-Incision Distal Biceps Tendon Repair With Bicortical Tensionable Locking Button Fixation
Authors: Zeeshan A. Khan, Benjamin Kerzner, Garrett R. Jackson, Harkirat S. Jawanda, Johnathon R. McCormick, Christopher M. Brusalis, Jimmy J. Chan, Daniel J. Kaplan, Nikhil N. Verma
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Acute, traumatic distal biceps tendon injuries are common among the middle-aged athletic male population. Surgical repair of distal biceps tendon remains the most effective means to restore maximal strength of forearm supination and elbow flexion with relief of antecubital pain. To date, no consensus exists on the optimal fixation method during distal biceps tendon repair and multiple techniques are accepted, including 1- and 2-incision approaches and tendon fixation with suture anchors, transosseous sutures, interference screws, and cortical buttons. In this Technical Note, we demonstrate a distal biceps tendon repair technique using a single-incision approach with bicortical, tensionable suspensory button fixation.

Injuries to the distal biceps tendon are a common source of upper-extremity dysfunction among the middle-aged athletic population. The incidence of distal biceps ruptures is roughly 2.55 per 100,000 patient-years in the United States.1 A distal biceps tendon injury may occur during an acute traumatic incident from excessive, eccentric loading, or may occur secondary to chronic, progressive deterioration of the tendon from repetitive overuse. Distal biceps tendon tears have a predilection to occur in middle-aged male patients, particularly those who participate in manual labor, weightlifting, or sporting activities. Additional risk factors for distal biceps tendon ruptures include smoking, anabolic steroids, age, and overuse.1,2

Several identifying features obtained during a patient’s history and physical examination can suggest a distal biceps tendon tear. Patients may report feeling a “pop” in their proximal forearm while lifting a heavy object. On inspection of the injured arm, a palpable gap may be seen in the antecubital fossa where the tendon’s native attachment exists, along with a reverse Popeye sign.3,4 In the presence of a complete tear of the distal biceps tendon, the examiner’s finger will be unable to “hook” the tendon from lateral to medial at the elbow flexion crease.5 Although the diagnosis of a complete tendon tear may be made on these findings alone, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is useful to confirm the diagnosis, assess the degree of tendon retraction, and evaluate adjacent neurovascular structures.6

Direct anatomic surgical repair to the radial tuberosity remains the most effective means to restore maximal strength of forearm supination and elbow flexion with relief of antecubital pain.7,8 Numerous surgical techniques have been devised, including 1- and 2-incision approaches and tendon fixation with suture anchors, transosseous sutures, interference screws, and cortical buttons.9 To date, no consensus exists on the optimal fixation method for the rupture of the distal biceps tendon.10, 11, 12 However, recent biomechanical studies have demonstrated enhanced fixation strength and increased load to failure with cortical button fixation when compared to other fixation methods.13

In this Technical Note, we demonstrate a distal biceps tendon repair technique using a single-incision approach with bicortical button fixation. The video highlights key pearls to perform the procedure in an efficient, reproducible manner while minimizing the risk of surgical complications (Video 1).

Preoperative Evaluation and Surgical Decision-Making

Patients who present after an acute injury to the elbow should undergo thorough history and physical examination. History of distal biceps injuries commonly occur in patients eccentrically contracting the biceps tendon, usually with a heavy resistance. Passive and active range of motion of the elbow should be elicited in addition to varus and valgus constraint. One of the critical components of the examination in distal biceps injures is to perform the hook test. This test is performed with the elbow in 90° of flexion and supination while palpation and “hooking” of the finger from lateral to medial to assess for disruption of the tendon distally on the radial tuberosity. Performing this test from medial to lateral can result in a false positive due to the orientation of the lacertus fibrosis. Standard imaging requires anteroposterior (Fig 1A) and lateral radiographs (Fig 1B) to assess for associated fractures, large osteochondral fragments, and joint space. In patient presentations with a high concern for distal biceps injury, we obtain an MRI (Fig 1C). Superficial Dissection

An L-shaped incision is made, with a shorter transverse limb at the level of the radial head ending laterally at the brachioradialis (about 1.5-2 cm in length), with the longitudinal limb starting at the radial border of the transverse limb (3-4 cm in length). The transverse component should be kept distally at the level of the radial head to allow more distal access to the radial tuberosity. If needed, the ulnar border of the transverse limb can be extended longitudinally, proximal toward the biceps muscle belly. Importantly, throughout the dissection, the arm is kept in supination to protect the posterior interosseous nerve.

After incision, Metzenbaum scissors are used to spread longitudinally through the subcutaneous fat. Radial and ulnar skin flaps are developed to create a mobile window. Dissection is carried down between the brachioradialis and pronator teres. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve is very superficial and typically adjacent to the cephalic vein (Fig 2B). Care must be taken during the approach to identify and protect this nerve throughout the case. Nerve exposure and dissection is carried out both distally and proximally to allow adequate visualization and protection during the case.

Branches of the cephalic and median cubital vein often cross the surgical field during this step. These can be tied off with sutures to maintain a bloodless field. Similarly, branches of the radial recurrent artery can be cauterized with a bipolar device (Fig 2C). A blunt self-retainer is then placed, and, depending upon the amount of retraction, the biceps stump may be found here. One may encounter a pseudotendon around the actual tendon, which consists of scar and fibrous tissue formed by the patient’s attempt to heal the injury in cases where repair is delayed. If this is found, it should be debrided sharply, to find the true tendon. If the distal end of the tendon has retracted proximally, it can be retrieved with a milking maneuver, compressing the biceps muscle belly to distalize the tendon. Other techniques include using an Army-Navy retractor proximally and bluntly performing dissection to identify the stump and grabbing it with a nonpenetrating clamp. If the tendon cannot be safely identified, the incision is extended proximally from the ulnar border of the transverse limb. Tendon Preparation

Once the distal stump of the tendon is secured, it is freed circumferentially of any adhesions to gain length so that a near native length–tension relationship can be restored. The pseudotendon should be debrided, as well as any poor-quality tissue, with a goal tendon diameter of 6 to 8 mm, and a target of 7 mm to avoid overdrilling the radial tuberosity tunnel. The stump can become bulbous after injury, necessitating considerable debulking. Next, the distal 2.5 cm of the distal tendon is whipstitched using the FiberLink number 5 suture (Arthrex, Naples, FL) (Fig 3B) and then the tendon is sized (Fig 3C). The prepared tendon is then covered and retracted proximally.

Footprint Preparation

Distal dissection is carried out bluntly with hemostasis as described previously. Our preference is to use 3 Army-Navy retractors on the radial and ulnar side of the wound, as well as distally, to expose the radial tuberosity. If necessary, a small pointed Hohmann retractor can be used on the ulnar side of the radial tuberosity but should be avoided on the radial side to protect the posterior interosseous nerve. A stump of tendon and bursa often is encountered and can be debrided with a rongeur. The radial tuberosity is identified, using a freer to bluntly dissect down onto the tuberosity with the forearm in supination. The bony surface is exposed, initially with the freer, then a rongeur until it is cleared of soft tissue. An anteroposterior radiograph using a mini fluoroscan placed under the hand table is obtained, using the tip of the Hohmann retractor as a point of reference to help identify the center of the tuberosity (Fig 4 A and B).Fig 4Radiographic determination of the anatomic insertion of a right elbow distal biceps tendon. Tendon Fixation

A spade-tipped guidewire is drilled bicortically through the center of the radial tuberosity, using the Hohmann as a reference, and ultimately confirmed under fluoroscopic guidance. The near cortex is then overreamed based on the size of the measured tendon (typically 7 mm) (Fig 5 A and B). The FiberLink suture is then loaded into the button of the TensionTight device (Arthrex). The authors prefer this implant, as the one-way locking mechanism allows the suture to slide in one direction to help reduce the tendon into the reamed tunnel while preventing the suture from sliding in the opposite direction, which maintains tension on the construct. This allows docking of the tendon, cycling of the elbow to remove creep, and then repeat tensioning. The tendon is then reduced into the tunnel by pulling on the free suture limb. It is helpful to have the elbow slightly flexed while docking the tendon, as this vector provides an easier insertion with less tension. Once the tendon is docked, the elbow can be extended. The loop of the suture is then released, and the suture is passed through the distal tendon 1 cm proximal to the tunnel aperture and tied to provide secondary fixation (Fig 6C).

Wound Closure and Rehabilitation

The wound is closed with three 3-0 VICRYL stitches (one for each limb and one at the corner) and then simple 3-0 Nylon sutures, including a corner stitch. A sterile dressing is applied, and the arm is splinted in 60° to 90° of flexion with neutral rotation. Postoperatively, a splint is maintained for 10 to 14 days, and then is removed at the first postoperative visit. A removable extension block splint is then fabricated, and active assist range of motion is begun, with a plan to advance to full extension by 4 weeks. The splint is discontinued at this point.

Discussion

Distal biceps tendon tears are common injuries. Direct surgical repair can restore maximum strength of forearm supination and elbow flexion. A single-incision approach holds several advantages, including decreased risk of heterotopic ossification and radioulnar synostosis, with improved cosmesis as compared with a 2-incision technique.14, 15, 16 A disadvantage of the single-incision approach is the risk of neurovascular injury, particularly to the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve and posterior interosseous nerve.17

A multitude of options exist for the surgical fixation of distal biceps tendon tears. Biomechanical data suggest that cortical button fixation confers the highest load-to-failure of contemporary fixation devices.18, 19, 20 The technique herein described uses a knotless cortical button for fixation, allowing the surgeon to titrate the tension of the repair while using a single 4.0-mm drill hole for inlay fixation. This technique also avoids the use of an interference screw, which decreases the risk of fracture.14

Distal biceps repair using cortical button fixation has been associated with excellent patient-reported outcomes and satisfaction with minimal complications.21, 22, 23 In a review of 102 patients who underwent distal biceps tendon repair with cortical button fixation with a mean follow-up of 5 years, Carter et al.21 reported the median abbreviated Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (i.e., QuickDASH) score, EuroQol 5-dimension 3-level (i.e., EQ-5D-3 L) score, Oxford Elbow Score (i.e., OES), and satisfaction scores were 1.2, 0.8, 48, and 100/100, respectively. In this cohort, 97.6% of patients were able to return to work and 2.9% of patients required subsequent surgery.

Our technique provides an efficient and reproducible method for distal biceps repair with strong fixation that minimizes the risk of neurovascular injuries.
