Preclinical evaluation of AAV2 anti-VEGF gene therapy using scFv for choroidal neovascularization
Authors: Xin Xu, Guangzuo Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-chain variable fragments (scFvs) have been proposed as efficient therapeutic cargoes for gene therapy. However, their safety and efficacy in clinical settings remain to be fully established. In this study, we constructed a recombinant adeno-associated virus 2 (AAV2) vector encoding an anti-VEGF scFv to inhibit choroidal neovascularization (CNV), and evaluated its efficacy, biodistribution, and safety in non-human primates (NHPs) and chinchilla rabbits. Pharmacological studies demonstrated sustained inhibition of neovascularization and vascular leakage both in vivo and in vitro. Pharmacokinetic and biodistribution analyses further showed that a single subretinal injection of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv resulted in prolonged retention of the transgene product in the retina and choroid, with minimal distribution to non-target tissues. These results indicate that AAV2-antiVEGFscFv may provide a safe and durable therapeutic approach for wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD). Collectively, our results support the potential of scFvs as efficient and well-tolerated therapeutic payloads for in vivo gene therapy, with the capacity to overcome key limitations of conventional antibody-based therapies.Keywords: AAV, scFv, choroidal neovascularization, efficacy, safety, biodistribution

Introduction

A growing body of research has highlighted the significant potential of single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) for use in the diagnosis and treatment of various disease.1 Their small size makes scFvs easier and more cost-effective to produce, as they can be expressed in bacterial systems, unlike monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), which typically require mammalian expression systems. In addition, scFvs can be readily screened using in vitro display technologies such as phage display.2 Compared with other types of antibodies, scFvs offer several advantages, including rapid blood clearance, improved tissue penetration for both therapeutic and diagnostic purposes,and reduced immunogenicity due to the absence of the fragment crystallizable (Fc) region.3 Furthermore, their rapid clearance from the bloodstream, resulting in a short half-life, can limit their therapeutic efficacy.4 Consequently, these challenges have restricted the clinical development of scFv-based therapeutics, with only two scFv-based drugs, Blincyto and Beovu, currently approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

Recently, scFvs have gained prominence in chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) and CAR-natural killer (CAR-NK) therapies, where they guide T cells or NK cells to recognize and eliminate tumor cells through antibody-antigen interactions. Sustained expression of scFvs in engineered immune cells mitigates some of their intrinsic limitations, including short half-life and limited long-term stability.5 Moreover, the broader affinity threshold of scFvs in CAR-T cells allows them to function effectively despite their lower affinity.6 As of December 2024, six CAR-T cell therapy products have been approved by the FDA, demonstrating the clinical safety and efficacy of scFvs in ex vivo gene therapy. Beyond ex vivo applications, scFvs have also been explored in preclinical studies for in vivo gene therapy. For instance, Hughes et al. showed that adeno-associated virus 8 (AAV8)-mediated delivery of anti-angiogenic scFvs effectively suppresses choroidal neovascularization (CNV) in murine models.7 Similarly, Selles et al. reported that AAV-mediated neuronal expression of an scFv targeting Aβ oligomers preserves synaptic integrity and restores memory in mouse models of Alzheimer’s disease, highlighting the therapeutic potential of viral vector-mediated scFv delivery for neurodegenerative disorders.8 In our previous research, AAV2-antiVEGFscFv effectively treated retinal neovascularization in both mouse and rabbit models.9 However, the safety and efficacy of AAV-delivered scFvs have not yet been evaluated in non-human primates (NHPs), a critical step for advancing AAV-scFv toward clinical translation.

Retinal neovascular diseases (RNDs) are among the leading causes of visual impairment and blindness worldwide, such as neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), diabetic retinopathy (DR), diabetic macular edema (DME), and retinal vein occlusion (RVO).10,11,12 Among these, nAMD is the primary cause of vision loss in the elderly and accounts for approximately 9% of global legal blindness.13,14 The hallmark pathological feature of these conditions is aberrant neovascularization. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is widely recognized as the principal driver of this process.15,16 Currently, intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy represents the first-line treatment for nAMD, center-involving DME, and RVO. Commonly used agents include ranibizumab, aflibercept, conbercept, brolucizumab, and faricimab.17,18,19 However, despite their proven efficacy, the need for frequent intravitreal injections imposes additional risks, as well as significant financial and psychological burdens on patients.20 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop long-acting anti-VEGF therapies.

AAV has emerged as a widely recognized gene delivery platform due to its long-term stability, high transduction efficiency, low immunogenicity, and non-pathogenic nature.21,22 Gene therapy offers a promising strategy for the treatment of RNDs, enabling sustained therapeutic effects following a single administration. Recent clinical studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of AAV-mediated delivery of anti-VEGF agents. A single administration of candidates such as RGX-314, ADVM-022, or 4D-150 has been shown to achieve long-term intraocular expression of anti-VEGF proteins, thereby reducing the need for repeated intravitreal injections. However, these approaches typically rely on relatively large transgene cargoes and require high vector doses, which may increase the risk of immune responses.23,24,25 scFvs offer several advantages, including improved tissue penetration and reduced immunogenicity.26 As an scFv antibody, brolucizumab has been shown to achieve higher concentrations in retinal and RPE/choroidal tissues compared with other anti-VEGF agents.27 In our previous work, we developed AAV2-antiVEGFscFv, a recombinant AAV2 vector encoding an anti-VEGF scFv comparable to brolucizumab. This construct demonstrated effective inhibition of neovascularization and vascular leakage in both mouse and rabbit models.9

In this study, we evaluated the efficacy, biodistribution, and safety of an AAV2-mediated anti-VEGF gene therapy encoding an scFv for the treatment of CNV. Our findings demonstrate that scFvs represent efficient and safe therapeutic payloads for in vivo gene therapy, effectively overcoming key limitations associated with conventional antibody-based approaches.

Results

Transgene product of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv showed high binding affinity to VEGF

AAV2-antiVEGFscFv vectors were generated using a triple-plasmid transient co-transfection system, followed by purification via ion-exchange chromatography and ultracentrifugation. The viral particles consist of an AAV2 capsid encapsulating an inverted terminal repeat (ITR)-flanked transgene cassette, which includes the chicken β-actin (CBA) promoter, a DNA sequence encoding the anti-VEGF scFv, and bGH polyA elements (Figure 1A). The potency of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv was confirmed in ARPE-19 cells. Dose-dependent transgene DNA was detected in the cells transduced with AAV2-antiVEGFscFv while no DNA was detected in the cells transduced with the AAV2 control (empty capsid without single-strand DNA; Figure 1B). Consistently, transgene mRNA and protein expression were detected only in ARPE-19 cells transduced with AAV2-antiVEGFscFv (Figures 1C and 1D). Together, these results confirm the efficient in vitro transduction and expression capability of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv.

We also utilized the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) biosensor-based assay to measure the binding kinetics for the transgene product and bevacizumab to VEGF. As shown in Table S1, anti-VEGF scFv bound human VEGF165, cynomolgus VEGF165, rabbit VEGF164, rat VEGF164, and mouse VEGF164, while bevacizumab bound human VEGF165, cynomolgus VEGF165, and rabbit VEGF164. Notably, the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of anti-VEGF scFv for human VEGF165 (<1 nM) was lower than that of bevacizumab (1.88 nM). These results indicate that the transgene product is specific for human VEGF and binds with high affinity.

To further evaluate the inhibiting activity of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv in vitro, cell proliferation, wound-healing and tube formation assays were conducted using VEGF-dependent human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs). As expected, the VEGF-induced HREC proliferation and migration were significantly inhibited in the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv groups, as well as in the aflibercept group (positive control; Figures 2A–2C). Consistently, the tube-forming capacity of HRECs was markedly reduced in both the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv and aflibercept groups (Figures 2D and 2E). Furthermore, the inhibitory effects of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv were dose-dependent. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the transgene product of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv exhibits potent anti-VEGF activity and holds strong potential as a gene therapy strategy for RNDs.

AAV2-antiVEGFscFv effectively inhibited neovascularization in a laser-induced cynomolgus monkey model

Our previous studies demonstrated that pathological neovascularization and vascular leakage were significantly suppressed by a single injection of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv in multiple models, including the oxygen-induced retinopathy (OIR) mouse model, Tet/opsin/VEGF transgenic mouse model, and VEGF-induced rabbit model.9 Given the close similarity in ocular size and anatomical structure between NHPs and humans, monkey models are widely regarded as highly relevant in ophthalmic research. In the present study, we employed a laser-induced CNV model in cynomolgus monkeys, which is one of the most widely accepted and well-established models for studying CNV.

Cynomolgus monkeys were randomly divided into four groups, including the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv low-dose group (1.5E+09 vg/eye), AAV2-antiVEGFscFv high-dose group (1.5E+10 vg/eye), solvent control group (abbreviated as control), and aflibercept group (2 mg/eye; positive control). As shown in Figure 3A, the solvent and AAV2-antiVEGFscFv vectors were subretinally injected at the designated volume and dose on day 0, whereas the positive control (aflibercept) was intravitreally injected on day 28. CNV was induced in all animals by laser photocoagulation on day 28. Subsequently, the severity of neovascular lesions and retinal structural changes were evaluated by fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) on days 42, 56, 70, and 91. The appearance of a vaporization bubble in the fundus confirmed disruption of Bruch’s membrane (Figure 3B). In the control group, the proportions of grade IV lesions were 70.37 ± 24.003%, 42.59 ± 28.475%, 25.92 ± 21.847%, and 20.37 ± 19.136% on days 42, 56, 70, and 91, respectively. Correspondingly, the retinal fluorescein leakage areas were 7.58 ± 4.455 mm², 5.45 ± 4.772 mm², 5.17 ± 3.705 mm², and 5.25 ± 3.693 mm2 (Figures 3C and 3D). In contrast, no grade IV lesions were detected in the aflibercept group at any time point, and fluorescein leakage areas were 0.06 ± 0.159 mm2, 0.07 ± 0.171 mm2, 0.00 ± 0.000 mm², and 0.00 ± 0.000 mm2 on days 42, 56, 70, and 91, respectively. In the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv 1.5E+9 vg/eye group, the percentage of grade IV lesions was 4.17 ± 8.266% on day 42 and 0.00 ± 0.000% at the remaining time points. The fluorescein leakage areas were 0.52 ± 0.506 mm2, 0.10 ± 0.171 mm2, 0.04 ± 0.113 mm², and 0.00 ± 0.000 mm2. In the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv 1.5E+10 vg/eye group, neither grade IV lesions nor detectable fluorescein leakage were observed on days 42, 56, 70, and 91 (Figures 3C and 3D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that AAV2-antiVEGFscFv effectively and durably suppresses laser-induced neovascularization and vascular leakage.

Following laser injury, OCT revealed disruption of the RPE at the lesion sites. In the control group, retinal neuroepithelium layer edema and local bulge were observed at the photocoagulation site in some eyes of animals, likely resulting from pathological neovascular growth. Quantitative analysis showed a significant increase in retinal thickness after laser injury in the control group, peaking on day 42 and gradually declining thereafter. In contrast, no evident retinal edema was observed in either AAV2-antiVEGFscFv group or the aflibercept group. Retinal thickness in these groups was apparently reduced compared with the control group on days 42, 56, 70, and 91(Figure 3E).

To further assess transgene expression, aqueous humor samples were collected on days 0, 42, and 91 and analyzed using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) specific for anti-VEGF scFv protein. No anti-VEGF scFv was detected in the control group at any time point. In contrast, in the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv-treated groups, no anti-VEGF scFv protein was detected on day 0, whereas robust and sustained protein expression was observed in most animals on days 42 and 91, with the exception of animal 3F001 (Table S2). Moreover, the protein levels appeared to be dose-dependent.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that administration of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv at doses of 1.5E+9 and 1.5E+10 vg/eye results in sustained intraocular expression of the therapeutic protein and persistent inhibition of laser-induced neovascularization, exudation, and retinal edema in cynomolgus monkeys, highlighting the excellent therapeutic efficacy of the expressed anti-VEGF scFv.

Pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv

Beyond efficacy, pharmacokinetics and biodistribution are critical preclinical considerations. To evaluate these parameters for the anti-VEGF scFv expressed by the AAV2 vector, cynomolgus monkeys and chinchilla rabbits received subretinal injections of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv at designated doses.

Cynomolgus monkeys were divided into five groups: three AAV2-antiVEGFscFv dose groups (3.0E+09, 3.0E+10, and 1.0E+11 vg/eye), a solvent control group, and a vehicle group (1.0E+11 vp/eye). Following a single subretinal injection, transgene concentrations were measured at 4- and 13 weeks post-administration. Four weeks post-injection, the transgene was primarily detected in the aqueous humor of monkeys across all three AAV2-antiVEGFscFv groups. In the high-dose group (1.0E+11 vg/eye), the transgene was also identified in the spleen (6/6), inguinal lymph nodes (5/6), cervical lymph nodes (4/6), liver (3/6), blood (1/6), and mesenteric lymph nodes (1/6). The transgene was detected in the blood of one male in the 3.0E+09 vg/eye group, whereas it remained below the limit of detection (BLD) in all monkeys within the 3.0E+10 vg/eye group (Table 1). At 13 weeks post-injection, the transgene persisted in the aqueous humor of monkeys in the 3.0E+09 (2/4), 3.0E+10 (4/4), and 1.0E+11 (3/4) vg/eye groups. In the high-dose (1.0E+11 vg/eye) group, the transgene was also identified in the spleen (4/4), inguinal lymph nodes (2/4), cervical lymph nodes (1/4), and mesenteric lymph nodes (1/4; Table 1). Transgene levels in all other tissues were either below the BLD or below the lower limit of quantification (BLQ). Furthermore, AAV2-antiVEGFscFv transcripts were BLD in the aforementioned tissues (data not shown). These results suggest that AAV2-antiVEGFscFv vectors are primarily localized to ocular tissues, with only minimal systemic distribution following subretinal injection.

Blood concentrations of the transgene product (anti-VEGF scFv protein) were determined at multiple time points (Table S3). In the high-dose group (1.0E+11 vg/eye), protein concentrations for animal 5F004 were 8.44 and 8.34 ng/mL on days 57 and 92 post-injection, respectively. For animal 5M005, the concentration was 8.27 ng/mL on day 57. In all other samples, transgene product concentrations remained below the BLQ. These results indicate that only a very small amount of anti-VEGF scFv protein entered the systemic circulation. Based on the present data, the product of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv was largely sequestered within the ocular tissue following a single subretinal injection at doses ranging from 3.0E+09 to 3.0E+10 vg/eye. Only negligible amounts entered the circulatory system at the highest dose of 1.0E+11 vg/eye.

To further investigate pharmacokinetic and biodistribution characteristics, AAV2-antiVEGFscFv was also subretinally injected at a single dose of 1.5E+10 vg/eye in chinchilla rabbits. Consistent with the findings in cynomolgus monkeys, the vector was primarily localized within ocular tissues, as evidenced by transgene concentrations across various tissues and organs. Furthermore, transgene concentrations in the posterior segment were significantly higher than those in the anterior segment (Table S4). These findings indicate that the anti-VEGF scFv gene therapy product diffuses from the posterior to the anterior segment of the eye. Transgene concentrations were relatively high in the cervical lymph nodes and spleen, while being only transiently detected in the brain, liver, trigeminal nerve, and medulla oblongata. In all other organs, concentrations were BLD (Table S4). These results suggest that AAV2-antiVEGFscFv does not readily cross the blood-retinal barrier and could not enter most systemic organs.

Taken together, these pharmacokinetic and biodistribution results demonstrate that the transgene product of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv was mainly retained within ocular tissues and only a small amount entered the circulatory system and a few organs. Consequently, a single subretinal injection of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv is expected to have negligible systemic effects on non-target organs in humans.

Both mAb and gene therapies can elicit anti-drug antibodies (ADAs), potentially leading to adverse effects and reduced therapeutic efficacy. To assess the immunogenicity of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv, we measured serum ADAs directed against the anti-VEGF scFv, as well as anti-AAV2 neutralizing antibodies. Serum ADA levels were evaluated in all animals on days 0, 7, 14, 28, and 91. The ADA positivity rate was 2/10 in the control group and 4/10 in the vehicle group. In the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv treatment groups (3.0E+09, 3.0E+10, and 1.0E+11 vg/eye), the ADA positivity rates were 1/11, 1/10, and 3/10, respectively (Table 2). These results indicate that a single subretinal administration of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv does not induce a significant ADA response, suggesting that anti-VEGF scFv is a low-immunogenicity and safe therapeutic payload for gene therapy.

Safety and tolerability of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv following a single subretinal injection

To investigate the safety and tolerability of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv following a single subretinal injection, eyes and serum were further assessed along with the pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies in cynomolgus monkeys. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was measured at baseline (predose) and 30 min, 3 days, 1 week, 6 weeks, and 13 weeks post-injection (Table 4). IOP remained within the normal range in the control group. A transient decrease in IOP was observed in the aflibercept group as well as in the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv 3.0E+10 and 1.0E+11 vg/eye groups. Notably, a more pronounced reduction in IOP was observed from day 3 to week 6 in the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv 3.0E+9 vg/eye group. As this reduction was not dose-dependent, we speculated that the abnormal reduction was caused by the individual variation among cynomolgus monkeys.

Retinal functional integrity was assessed using electrophysiology (ERG) in all cynomolgus monkeys. Dark-adapted (DA) 3.0 and light-adapted (LA) 3.0 responses were recorded at predose, 2 weeks, and 13 weeks post-injection. The a-wave and b-wave amplitudes remained within the normal range in the control group and were largely preserved in the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv 3.0E+9 vg/eye group. In contrast, some eyes in the vehicle group and the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv 3.0E+10 and 1.0E+11 vg/eye groups showed transient reduction in the a-wave and b-wave amplitudes at 2 weeks post-injection. Notably, a clear trend toward recovery was observed by 13 weeks post-injection (Table S5 and S6). These findings suggest that the transient functional changes may be associated with mechanical effects of the subretinal injection procedure and a mild, temporary immune response to the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv vector.

To further evaluate immune responses following subretinal injection with AAV2-antiVEGFscFv, levels of complement (C3 and C4), circulating immune complex (CIC), and inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, and IL-10) were measured at predose, 1 week, 4 weeks, and 13 weeks post-injection (Tables S7 and S8). ELISA results showed no significant differences among the control group, vehicle group and AAV2-antiVEGFscFv groups. Moreover, T cell differentiation analyses indicated comparable immune profiles across all groups (Tables S7 and S8). Taken together, these results suggest that subretinal injection of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv does not elicit a detectable systemic immune response in NHPs. Therefore, AAV2-antiVEGFscFv appears to be safe and well tolerated following a single subretinal injection, supporting its potential for clinical translation in humans.

Discussion

ScFvs offer several advantages over mAbs, including their smaller size, lower production costs, and the ability to be expressed in bacterial systems, whereas mAbs typically require more complex mammalian expression platforms. scFvs are also well suited for in vitro screening approaches such as phage display. In addition, they exhibit rapid systemic clearance, enhanced tissue penetration, and reduced immunogenicity due to the absence of an Fc region. However, scFvs also have inherent limitations, including lower binding affinity, shorter half-life, and a greater tendency to aggregate, which have constrained their broader application in drug development. Despite these challenges, scFvs have demonstrated considerable promise in CAR-T and CAR-NK therapies for cancer, where sustained intracellular expression can mitigate issues related to poor stability and lower affinity. Emerging preclinical studies further suggest their potential as therapeutic payloads in AAV-based gene therapies, including applications in retinal diseases, Alzheimer’s disease, and other conditions. Nevertheless, comprehensive evaluations of safety and efficacy in NHPs remain essential prior to clinical translation.

Intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy is currently the first-line treatment for RNDs, significantly improving visual outcomes and slowing disease progression. Several anti-VEGF agents have been approved by regulatory agencies, including ranibizumab, aflibercept, conbercept, brolucizumab, and faricimab.28,29,30 However, these treatments require frequent intraocular injections due to their relatively short half-lives, imposing substantial burdens on patients.18,31,32 Reducing dosing frequency therefore remains a major priority in the development of next-generation anti-VEGF therapies.33 In this context, AAV-based gene therapy holds considerable promise, as it can enable long-term, potentially lifelong, intraocular expression of anti-VEGF proteins, thereby addressing key limitations of current treatment paradigms.23,24 In recent years, both preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of rAAV-mediated delivery of anti-VEGF agents, including RGX-314, ADVM-022, and 4D-150. Following a single subretinal administration of RGX-314, the annual number of intravitreal injections was significantly reduced, while stable expression of the transgene product was maintained for at least four years.34 In addition, a single intravitreal injection of ADVM-022 has been shown to reduce the need for annual injections while maintaining long-term, stable transgene expression for up to 4.5 years.35 In theory, supplemental intravitreal administration of anti-VEGF agents may no longer be necessary if sufficient levels of anti-VEGF protein are continuously produced by the rAAV vector. However, increasing the vector dose to achieve adequate expression may elevate the risk of intraocular inflammation and immune responses. Accordingly, in this study, we aimed to develop an optimized rAAV-based gene therapy employing an scFv as the therapeutic payload, with the goal of achieving sufficient anti-VEGF expression while minimizing immunogenicity.

Among currently approved anti-VEGF agents, brolucizumab has been approved for the treatment of nAMD and DME. Previous studies have shown that brolucizumab binds all VEGF-A isoforms with higher affinity than bevacizumab, ranibizumab, or aflibercept.36 To generate an optimized gene therapy vector, the gene encoding the anti-VEGF scFv protein (similar to brolucizumab) was cloned into our expression cassette. Consistent with previous reports, SPR assays confirmed that the anti-VEGF scFv produced by AAV2-antiVEGFscFv binds VEGF-A with higher affinity than bevacizumab (Table S1).

Following subretinal injection, the therapeutic vectors were delivered into the potential space adjacent to RPE cells. Accordingly, ARPE-19 cells were used to evaluate the transduction efficiency and expression profile of the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv vector. In vitro results demonstrated dose-dependent transgene expression in transduced ARPE-19 cells (Figures 1B–1D). Neovascularization is the process of generating abnormal new blood vessels mediated mainly by progenitor and/or endothelial cells, which is the hallmark pathology of RNDs. Therefore, HRECs were used to explore the pharmacodynamics of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv vectors. Our results showed that VEGF-induced HREC proliferation, migration, and tube formation were significantly inhibited by the transgene product (Figures 2A–2E). Consistent with our previous findings, we also observed high transduction efficiency in HEK293T cells and potent anti-VEGF activity in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs).9 Taken together, these data demonstrate that the anti-VEGF scFv produced by AAV2-antiVEGFscFv exhibits strong biological activity and represents a promising therapeutic payload for gene therapy targeting pathological neovascularization.

Following a single intravitreal injection in cynomolgus monkeys, the ocular half-lives of scFv, Fab, Fc-fusion proteins, and IgG were 2.28, 2.62, 3.13, and 3.26 days, respectively.37 In patients, the ocular half-life of ranibizumab is approximately 7.2 days, whereas that of aflibercept is 9.1 days.31,32 It could be inferred that brolucizumab has a shorter duration in the eye compared with ranibizumab. According to the model-based meta-analysis, Caruso et al. estimated the half-life of brolucizumab to be 4.3–5.1 days.38 The molecular weights of brolucizumab, ranibizumab, and aflibercept are approximately 26, 48, and 115 kDa, respectively. Notably, according to their package inserts, the recommended dosage of brolucizumab (6 mg) was higher than that of ranibizumab (0.5 mg) and aflibercept (2 mg), which may be caused by its shorter half-life. Our study showed that this limitation could be overcome by rAAV-based gene therapy using brolucizumab as a therapeutic cargo. Following a single subretinal injection of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv, the formation and development of neovascularization and exudation were significantly suppressed for at least 91 days in the laser-induced NHP model (Figures 3A–3E). Although intravitreal aflibercept treatment confirmed the responsiveness of the CNV model to VEGF inhibition, direct comparisons with AAV2-mediated gene therapy are of limited translational value. Differences in routes of administration result in distinct drug distribution profiles, while dosing regimens lead to divergent pharmacokinetics. Moreover, the immunogenicity profiles of exogenously administered protein therapeutics and AAV-mediated gene therapies differ substantially. To better isolate the effects of the therapeutic molecule itself, future studies will incorporate AAV2-Eylea as a comparator. This will enable a more rigorous head-to-head evaluation and remains a key focus of our ongoing research.

Following subretinal administration of AAV-based gene therapy, the transgene product is expected to diffuse from the subretinal space to neovascular lesions at therapeutically relevant levels. Due to the absence of heavy chain and light chain constant regions, the small size of anti-VEGF scFv confers enhanced tissue penetration and reduced immunogenicity.26 In addition, a previous study has demonstrated that brolucizumab showed higher concentrations in retinal tissues and RPE/choroid tissues than other anti-VEGF drugs.27 These properties may help explain why AAV2-antiVEGFscFv achieves robust transgene expression (Table S2) and sufficient anti-VEGF activity (Figures 3A–3E) at doses as low as 1.5 × 109 vg/eye following a single subretinal injection. Notably, subretinal administration of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv induced a dose-dependent increase in anti-AAV2 neutralizing antibodies but not in ADAs (Tables 2 and 3). Although a large number of ADAs can be detected in the vehicle, similar observations have been reported in studies using AAV empty capsids. This phenomenon may be attributed to the intact capsid structure of empty particles, which more readily exposes antigenic epitopes and directly activates the immune system, thereby triggering innate immune responses such as microglial activation and infiltration. In contrast, administration of genome-containing AAV2 vectors appears to exhibit reduced immunogenicity, potentially due to genome-induced alterations in capsid conformation.39 Furthermore, optimization of vector production processes has been shown to reduce the proportion of empty capsids and increase genome packaging efficiency, which may further mitigate ADA formation.40 Based on the analysis of the complement, CIC, inflammatory factors, and T cell differentiation, our results confirmed that no obvious immune responses were induced following administration of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv (Tables S7 and S8). We speculated that the intrinsically low immunogenicity of the scFv contributes substantially to this favorable safety profile. The primary safety finding of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv was dose-dependent ocular adverse reactions, such as inflammation, pigmentary changes, hierarchy disorders, and thinning at the injection site of the retina. In addition, the retinal morphology of eyes in the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv 3.0E+10 and 1.0E+11 groups and the vehicle group recovered over time. The retinal morphology of eyes in the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv 3.0E+9 group was comparable to that of the control group at week 13. Analysis of retinal functional integrity showed similar results using ERG. Eyes in the control group and the low-dose group remained almost normal, while eyes in the middle-dose and high-dose groups and the vehicle group showed a transient reduction in the a-wave and b-wave amplitudes. These functional changes are likely attributable, at least in part, to mechanical stress associated with the surgical procedure. Building upon the whole preclinical results, we concluded that the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv is 3.0E+9 vg/eye for ocular toxicology, while that is 1.0E+11 vg/eye for systemic toxicology. Nevertheless, given the limited sample size and the detection of low-level ADA responses, potential clinical risks cannot be fully excluded and warrant further investigation.

In conclusion, AAV2-antiVEGFscFv demonstrated strong efficacy, good tolerability, and favorable pharmacokinetic properties in NHPs. Furthermore, no serious adverse events, vision impairment, or detrimental immune responses were observed. These findings support the potential of scFvs as efficient and safe therapeutic payloads for in vivo gene therapy, effectively addressing key limitations of conventional antibody-based approaches.

Study design

Subretinal injection

Following mydriasis using Mydrin-p (Compound Tropicamide Eye Drops; one to two drops/eye), the animals were anesthetized with 2.5% pentobarbital sodium (25 mg/kg) via intravenous injection. The eyes to be administered were disinfected with povidone iodine solution. The eyelids of the animals were opened using an eyelid opener to expose the operating site. At 2 mm from the corneoscleral limbus toward the center of the eyeball, the scleral puncture needle was inserted, and the scleral tunnel nail was retained. Using the microinjection device with a surgical microscope and a retinoscope, the drug was delivered into the potential space between the RPE and neuronal retina after slight puncture at a proper site of the retina. Then, ofloxacin eye ointment (one to two drops) was used to keep the cornea wet and prevent infection. Each eye received the subretinal injection at a single site. The day of subretinal injection was defined as day 0.

rAAV production and titration

The AAV2-antiVEGFscFv vector contains a transgene cassette encoding an anti-VEGF scFv protein. The vehicle vector consists of empty AAV2 capsids without a transgene cassette. Both rAAV vectors were produced using a transient three-plasmid transfection system in HEK293 cells. Briefly, HEK293 cells were cultured in cell factories and co-transfected with pHelper, pAAV Rep-Cap, and the transgene plasmid (pAAV2-antiVEGFscFv) at a molar ratio of 1:1:1 using polyethyleneimine (PEI) as the transfection reagent. At 48–72 h post-transfection, cells were harvested and lysed to release intracellular AAV particles. The lysate was treated with Benzonase nuclease to degrade residual host nucleic acids. Following clarification by filtration, the crude viral lysate was subjected to cation-exchange chromatography for primary purification of rAAV particles. The chromatography column (BPG 100/500) and POROS™ 50 HS Strong Cation Exchange Resin were sourced from Cytiva and Thermo Fisher, respectively. Bound vectors were washed and then eluted sequentially using PBS buffer containing NaCl at concentrations of 400, 300, 200, and 100 mM. The vectors were subsequently concentrated using a TFF KrosFlo KR2i system (Repligen). Further purification was performed by ultracentrifugation using a density gradient to separate AAV particles from protein impurities and empty debris. Purified vectors were buffer-exchanged and sterilized by filtration through a 0.22-μm filter. Vector genome titers were determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR). To determine the physical titer of AAV vectors using qPCR, purified viral samples were first subjected to a DNase I treatment at 37°C for 15 min to eliminate any residual, non-encapsidated plasmid DNA from the production process. Then viral genomes were released from the capsid via heat denaturation. The quantification of AAV vectors relies on a standard curve generated from a series of 10-fold dilutions of a linearized plasmid standard containing the target sequence. The qPCR reaction utilizes specific primers (forward primer: 5′-GCCTTCCTTGACCCTGGA-3′; reverse primer: 5′-ACTCAGACAATGCGATGCAA-3′) and a fluorescently labeled TaqMan probe (5′-6-FAM-AGGTGCCACTCCCACTGTCCT-TAMRA-3′) targeting the 3′ end of the transgene to determine the cycle threshold (Ct) values. By comparing the Ct values of the DNase-resistant viral samples to the plasmid standard curve, the final genome titer (vg/mL) was calculated. Capsid particle levels were assessed by SDS-PAGE followed by silver staining to visualize the AAV capsid proteins (VP1, VP2, and VP3). The AAV vehicle control, which is the empty vector obtained from the ultracentrifugation step during production, does not contain a vector genome. Therefore, its particle titer was determined by measuring capsid protein levels with the AAV2 Titer Capsid ELISA Kit (GenScript, L00942). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the capture plate (Part No. G1-80) provided in the kit was used. For quantification, the empty AAV2 capsids, AAV2 control (Part No. G1-10), were used as the standard. The lyophilized standard was reconstituted with 500 μL PBS, diluted 1:10 with sample dilution buffer to prepare the top standard, and then serially diluted 1:2 to generate an 8-point standard curve. 100 μL of the diluted samples or standards was added to the corresponding wells. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 60 min. The detection antibody was the biotin Anti-AAV2 antibody (Part No. G1-20), which was added to the wells and incubated at 37°C for 60 min. Then, 100 μL of the streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase (HRP; Part No. E1-30) was added and incubated at 37°C for 10 min. Following incubation and washing steps, binding was detected by adding TMB solution (Part No. B-A1-40), incubating for 15 min at room temperature in the dark, and then stopping the reaction with stop solution (Part No. B-A1-50). Absorbance was read immediately at dual wavelengths of 450 nm and 650 nm, with the 650 nm reading used to subtract background signal.

The impurities in the vectors were screened, including mammalian DNA, plasmid DNA, bovine serum albumin (BSA), HEK293 protein, and Benzonase. Residual mammalian DNA was quantified by qPCR as previously described with modification.41 Briefly, human genomic DNA was digested with EcoRI (NEB, R0101S), followed by two phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) extractions, ethanol precipitation, and dissolution in TE buffer (Fisher Scientific, BP2473100). DNA concentration was determined by OD260, then diluted to prepare standard stock (SS) at 2.0 × 107 pg/mL. Seven serial 4-fold dilutions of SS were prepared to obtain standards S1–S7, ranging from 5.0 × 106 (S1) to 1,220 pg/mL (S7). Purified AAV vector samples were thawed and kept on ice, then diluted independently three times in Q-PCR dilution buffer (0.01% Pluronic F68 in nuclease-free water) to achieve 10-, 30-, and 100-fold dilutions. A qPCR master mix was prepared per well: 12.5 μL TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4304437), 1.25 μL of 20 × 18S rRNA Assay Reagent (Eurogentec, RT-CKFT-18S), and 6.25 μL nuclease-free water. Then, 5 μL of each standard, 5 μL of each diluted test article, or 5 μL of Q-PCR dilution buffer as no-template controls was added to each well. The plate was sealed, centrifuged at 2,000×g for 2 min, and run on an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system under the following conditions: 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 min, then 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. The average concentration from the three independent dilutions was calculated from the standard curve.

Residual plasmid DNA was quantified by qPCR as previously described with modification.41 The production plasmid containing the kanamycin resistance gene (KanR) was linearized by restriction digestion with EcoRV (NEB, R0195S), and serial 10-fold dilutions were prepared to generate a standard curve ranging from 1.0×106 to 64 copies per 5 μL. AAV vector samples (test article) were diluted 1,000-, 3,000-, and 10,000-fold in Q-PCR dilution buffer. Each qPCR reaction mix (20 μL) contained 12.5 μL of TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4304437), target-specific forward and reverse primers and probe for KanR (forward: 5′-TTGATGGTCGGAAGAGGCAT-3′; reverse: 5′-GGTAGCGTTGCCAATGATGT-3′, and a fluorescently labeled TaqMan probe (5′-6-FAM-CCGTCAGCCAGTTTAGTCTGACCATCT-TAMRA-3′), and 5 μL of sample or standard. Reactions were performed in triplicate in a 96-well optical reaction plate using an ABI 7500 real-time PCR system under the following conditions: 50°C for 2 min, 95°C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. The copy number of residual plasmid DNA was calculated from the standard curve.

Residual BSA was quantified using BSA ELISA Kit (Cygnus Technologies, F030) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 50 μL of BSA standards (0, 0.5, 2, 8, and 32 ng/mL), quality controls, and test samples were pipetted into anti-BSA-coated microtiter strips, followed by the addition of 100 μL of HRP-conjugated anti-BSA antibody. Quality control samples at low (approximately 2 ng/mL) and high (approximately 12 ng/mL) BSA concentrations were included in each assay to monitor assay performance. The strips were covered and incubated on an orbital shaker at 400–600 rpm for 1 h at room temperature (24°C ± 4°C). After incubation, the wells were washed four times with diluted wash buffer, ensuring that each wash completely filled the wells and was removed promptly. Then, 100 μL of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 30 min without shaking. The enzymatic reaction was stopped by adding 100 μL of 0.5 M sulfuric acid (stop solution). The absorbance was read at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm using a microplate reader. BSA concentrations in test samples were determined by interpolation from a standard curve generated using the kit-provided BSA standards, employing a four-parameter logistic method.

Residual HEK293 host cell proteins (HCPs) were quantified using HEK293 HCP ELISA Kit (Cygnus Technologies, F650S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of HRP-conjugated anti-HEK293 antibody was pipetted into anti-HEK293-coated microtiter strips, followed by the addition of 50 μL of HEK293 HCP standards (0, 2, 10, 25, 75, and 200 ng/mL), quality controls, and test samples. Quality control samples at low (8 ng/mL), medium (20 ng/mL), and high (125 ng/mL) HCP concentrations were included in each assay to monitor assay performance. The strips were covered and incubated on an orbital shaker at 400–600 rpm for 1.5 h at room temperature (24°C ± 4°C). After incubation, the wells were washed four times with diluted wash buffer. Then, 100 μL of TMB substrate was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 30 min without shaking. The enzymatic reaction was stopped by adding 100 μL of 0.5 M sulfuric acid (stop solution). The absorbance was read at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm using a microplate reader. HEK293 HCP concentrations in test samples were determined by interpolation from a standard curve generated using the kit-provided standards, employing a four-parameter logistic method.

Residual benzonase endonuclease was quantified using Endonuclease GTP ELISA Kit (Cygnus Technologies, F960) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of HRP-conjugated anti-endonuclease antibody was pipetted into anti-endonuclease-coated microtiter strips, followed by the addition of 50 μL of endonuclease standards (0, 0.31, 0.63, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 ng/mL), quality controls, and test samples. Quality control samples at low (0.75 ng/mL), medium (3 ng/mL), and high (12 ng/mL) endonuclease concentrations were included in each assay to monitor assay performance. The strips were covered and incubated on an orbital shaker at 400–600 rpm for 1 h at room temperature (24°C ± 4°C). After incubation, the wells were washed four times with diluted wash buffer. Then, 100 μL of TMB substrate was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 30 min without shaking. The enzymatic reaction was stopped by adding 100 μL of 0.5 M sulfuric acid (stop solution). Finally, the absorbance was read at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 650 nm using a microplate reader. Endonuclease concentrations in test samples were determined by interpolation from a standard curve generated using the kit-provided standards, employing a four-parameter logistic curve-fitting method.

Cell culture

ARPE-19 cells were purchased from ATCC and cultured in DMEM/F-12 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). HRECs were purchased from Guangzhou Gineo Biotechnology Co., Ltd and cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM) (ScienCell Research Laboratories, 1001) supplemented with 5% FBS (ScienCell Research Laboratories) and 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ScienCell Research Laboratories). All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Potency assays: In vitro transgene product expression

Following infection of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv with the assigned multiplicity of infection (MOI), ARPE-19 cells were collected at the appointed time. DNA and RNA were isolated from ARPE-19 cell samples using the AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 80204) as described in the instruction manual. The concentration of isolated DNA and RNA was measured using a NanoDrop Microvolume Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For RNA samples, cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (Takara, RR047B). Primers were designed based on the transgene sequence of AAV2-antiVEGFscFv. The copy numbers of transgene DNA and RNA were determined using TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4304437). The qPCR reaction utilizes specific primers (forward primer: 5′-GCCTTCCTTGACCCTGGA-3′; reverse primer: 5′-ACTCAGACAATGCGATGCAA-3′) and a fluorescently labeled TaqMan probe (5′-6-FAM-AGGTGCCACTCCCACTGTCCT-TAMRA-3′). The in vitro expression of the AAV2-antiVEGFscFv transgene product was determined by ELISA. To isolate the total protein, the infected cells were harvested and lysed, while the supernatants were collected after centrifugation. For ELISA analysis, plates were coated with recombinant human VEGF165 protein (Genscript, Z02689). Following blocking with 2% BSA, the reference standard and samples were added into the wells and incubated with the rabbit antiVEGFscFv antibody (Zoonbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd; 1:3,000) and the goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-2; 1:5,000). The reference standard was VEGF-scFv synthesized by Zoonbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Finally, TMB solution was added for color development, and the reaction was stopped with termination solution. The absorbance at 450 nm was recorded and then analyzed.

Potency assay: In vitro VEGF inhibition of transgene products

For in vitro VEGF inhibition assays, ARPE-19 cells were cultured and then transfected with the assigned rAAV vector when the cell density reached 80%–90%. At 48 h post-transfection, supernatant was collected for the subsequent experiments.

For the cell proliferation assay, HRECs in the logarithmic growth phase were used and digested with 0.25% trypsin. Following resuspension in ECM medium, the HREC suspension was incubated in 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. After starvation for 4 h, the medium was replaced with conditional medium supplemented with VEGF (25 ng/mL) and/or aflibercept (600 ng/mL) and/or supernatant of transfected ARPE-19 cells according to the study design. At 72 h after the addition of ARPE-19 cell supernatant, the 96-well plate was removed from the incubator, and the cell supernatant was discarded. Then, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (NCM Biotech, C6005) and 100 μL of culture medium were added to each well. The plate was returned to a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator for an additional 3 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell proliferation rate was calculated according to formula 1: proliferation rate (%) = (ODtest − ODno cell)/(ODbalnk − ODno cell) × 100%.

To investigate the migration ability of HRECs, the wound healing assay was conducted. Similarly, HRECs in the logarithmic growth phase were used and incubated in a 6-well plate at a density of 6 × 105 cells/well. Following incubation for 24 h, cells were scratched using a sterile pipet tip to generate a linear cell-free area and then incubated with conditional medium supplemented with VEGF (25 ng/mL) and/or Aflibercept (600 ng/mL) and/or supernatant of transfected ARPE-19 cells according to the study design. Images were obtained at 0 and 32 h post-scratching, and ImageJ software was utilized to calculate the wound area.

For the tube formation assay, HRECs were starved overnight when the cell density reached approximately 80%. The 96-well plate was placed on ice, and 50 μL/well of Corning Matrigel (Corning, 354230) was added vertically and evenly into the plate to avoid bubble formation. After gentle shaking to ensure uniform coverage, the plate was incubated in a cell culture incubator for 30 min to allow solidification. Following resuspension, HRECs were seeded into Matrigel-coated 96-well plates at a density of 6,000 cells/well and incubated with conditional medium supplemented with VEGF (50 ng/mL) and/or aflibercept (1,000 ng/mL) and/or supernatant of transfected ARPE-19 cells according to the study design. After 4 h of incubation, images were captured using an inverted microscope (ECLIPSE TS2, Nikon) and analyzed using ImageJ software.

SPR

All SPR measurements were performed using a Biacore 8K instrument (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB) at 25°C. Human VEGF165 (ACROBiosystems, VE5-H82QO), cynomolgus VEGF165 (Leading Biology, PHK0002HO1), rabbit VEGF164 (Abcam, ab189228), rat VEGF164 (Cell Signaling, 5874SC), and mouse VEGF164 (Cell Signaling, 5211SC) were immobilized onto CM5 chips (Cytiva, 10343798) via amine coupling using an Amine Coupling Kit (Cytiva, BR100050) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HBS-P buffer (0.01 M HEPES, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.005% surfactant P20, pH 7.4) was used as both the running and dilution buffer at a flow rate of 20 μL/min. Subsequently, a human antibody capture kit, type 2 (Cytiva, 29234600) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol. 25 μg/mL anti-VEGF scFv and bevacizumab (MCE, HY-P9906A) were captured onto the CM5 chip surface at a flow rate of 10 μL/min for 360 s. Then, serial dilutions of the target VEGF proteins, prepared in HBS-P buffer, were injected over the captured antibodies at a flow rate of 20 μL/min, with an association phase of 300 s and a dissociation phase of 900 s. After each cycle, the CM5 chip surfaces were regenerated using a 30-s injection of 10 mM glycine (pH 2.5) at a flow rate of 30 μL/min. The association and dissociation curves for antigen-antibody interactions were recorded in real time by the instrument, and blank sensorgrams were subtracted for double referencing.

Quantification of transgene and transgene product

The aqueous humor and blood of monkeys and rabbits were collected according to the study design. Rabbits were euthanized at designated time points, and tissues including bilateral eyelid, bulbar conjunctiva, eyeball with optic nerve, optic chiasma, and optic tract were collected. Ocular tissues including cornea, iris, ciliary body, lens, vitreous body, retina, choroid, and optic nerve were subsequently dissected. All tissues were rinsed twice with normal saline and blotted dry with filter paper.

For transgene (DNA and RNA) detection, DNA and RNA were extracted using the AllPrep DNA/RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, 80204) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, solid tissue samples were thawed on ice and homogenized. Approximately 25–100 mg of each tissue sample was transferred to a 2.0 mL grinding tube with ceramic beads (3-mm diameter). An appropriate volume of Buffer RLT Plus from the kit was added based on tissue weight (25 mg: 200 μL; for <25 mg, 200 μL was added). Samples were homogenized at 8,000 rpm for 20 s using a homogenizer for three repeated cycles with a 20 s interval between each cycle, repeated once or twice if homogenization was incomplete, and then immediately centrifuged. Liquid samples (aqueous humor, vitreous humor, blood) did not require homogenization; 200 μL of liquid sample was mixed directly with 600 μL of Buffer RLT Plus. Then, the homogenate or liquid sample lysate was transferred to an AllPrep DNA spin column and centrifuged. The DNA bound to the column, while the flow-through containing RNA was collected. The DNA column was washed, and genomic DNA was eluted in 50–100 μL of Buffer EB. The RNA in the flow-through was processed by adding ethanol, transferred to a RNeasy spin column, washed, and treated with DNase I to remove residual genomic DNA. Total RNA was then eluted in 30–50 μL of RNase-free water. DNA and RNA concentrations were measured using a spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was performed using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Perfect Real Time) (Takara, RR047B) to synthesize cDNA from total RNA according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 1 μg of total RNA was treated with gDNA Eraser at 42°C for 2 min to eliminate any residual genomic DNA, followed by reverse transcription using PrimeScript RT Enzyme Mix at 37°C for 15 min and 85°C for 5 s. The resulting cDNA was stored at −20°C until use. Quantification was performed based on standard curves generated from serial 5-fold dilutions of plasmid standards containing the target sequence. Each standard and sample were tested in duplicate. The lower limit of quantitation (LLOQ) and limit of detection (LOD; 95%) for transgene DNA were 4.00 copies/μL and 1.00 copies/μL, respectively. Transgene RNA levels were determined by quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) using cDNA as template. The primers and probe used were the same as the vector genome assay above. Standard curves were generated using serial 5-fold dilutions of the same plasmid standard. The LLOQ and LOD (95%) for transgene RNA detection were 10 copies/μL and 5.00 copies/μL, respectively.

For transgene protein detection, solid tissues were homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) supplemented with protease inhibitors at a ratio of 5 mL PBS per 1 mg tissue. The homogenates were centrifuged at 5,000×g and 4°C for 5 min. The obtained supernatant was aliquoted (approximately 100 μL/tube), and the remaining supernatant was stored in another tube. Liquid samples, including aqueous humor, vitreous humor, and serum, were directly aliquoted (approximately 50 μL in one tube and the rest in another tube) for downstream analyses. The concentrations of transgene protein were determined by a validated ELISA method as previously described.9 Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with 100 μL of recombinant human VEGF165 protein (Genscript, Z02689) at 1 μg/mL overnight at 4°C. Then, the plate was blocked with 200 μL of 2% BSA in PBS for 1 h at 37°C. The reference standard was VEGF-scFv synthesized by Zoonbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Next, 100 μL of reference standards and study samples were added to the wells in triplicate and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Following five washes, 100 μL of rabbit anti-VEGF scFv antibody (Zoonbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., 1:3,000 dilution in blocking buffer) was added and incubated for 2 h at room temperature. Then, 100 μL of goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:5,000 dilution in blocking buffer) was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The plate was washed five times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 after each incubation step. Finally, 100 μL of TMB solution was added and incubated for 30 min at 37°C in the dark for color development. The reaction was stopped with 50 μL of 2 NH2SO4, and the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a microplate reader (Thermo, Varioskan LUX). Protein concentrations were calculated based on a four-parameter logistic standard curve generated using serial dilutions of purified anti-VEGF scFv protein. The LLOQ for the ELISA assay was 7.81 ng/mL.

Slit lamp and fundus examination

The animals were anesthetized with ketamine (4.5–8.5 mg/kg) or Zoletil 50 (2.4–5.1 mg/kg) via intramuscular injection, which can be adjusted according to the anesthesia condition of animals, and dilated with Mydrin-p (Compound Tropicamide Eye Drops, one to two drops/eye). Slit lamp microscopy was utilized for the examination of the anterior segments of monkey and rabbit eyes, including the conjunctiva, cornea, anterior chamber, iris, and lens. The vitreous body, optic disc, macula, retina, and retinal vessels of the eyes of animals were assessed by slit lamp microscopy and slit lamp indirect ophthalmoscopy or a binocular indirect ophthalmoscope.

Measurements of anti-drug antibodies and anti-AAV2 neutralizing antibody

Blood samples were collected on days 0, 7, 14, 28, and 91 for the detection of ADA and on days 0, 28, and 91 for determining the anti-AAV2 neutralizing antibody. After centrifugation, serum samples were aliquoted and stored at −60°C until analysis. ADA levels were measured using a validated electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA). Pooled drug-naïve cynomolgus monkey serum was used as a negative control (NC). Quality control samples were prepared on the day of use by diluting the positive control antibody, a VEGF-scFv custom synthesized by Zoonbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., from a stock concentration of 0.7 mg/mL with drug-naïve cynomolgus monkey serum to the desired concentrations. Briefly, serum samples were heated at 56°C for 30 min to inactivate complement components and cooled to room temperature. Subsequently, samples were diluted 10-fold with assay buffer. 60 μL of the diluted sample was incubated with 60 μL of labeled anti-VEGF scFv antigen for 1 h at 37°C to allow the formation of immune complexes with ADA present in the samples. The immune complexes were captured on assay plates and detected using a hypersensitive multi-factor electrochemiluminescence MESO QuickPlex SQ120MM (Meso Scale Discovery). The emitted signal was measured using an electrochemiluminescence detection system, and ADA levels were determined based on the signal intensity relative to assay controls. Anti-AAV2 neutralizing antibodies were determined using a cell-based fluorescence assay. Briefly, serum samples were heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 min and cooled to room temperature and diluted 10-fold with complete culture medium. Next, 60 μL of the diluted sample was mixed with 60 μL of AAV2 reporter vectors (2 × 1011 vg/mL) in a 96-well plate. The same volume of complete medium was added. The plate was placed in a CO2 incubator (37°C, 5% CO2) for 1 h. Then, 50 μL of the co-incubated solution was added to target cells (e.g., 1 × 104 cells/well) in a 96-well cell culture plate, which was placed in the CO2 incubator for 24 h. Each sample was tested in duplicate. The culture plate was removed from the incubator and brought to room temperature. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Varioskan Lux multimode microplate reader (Thermo Scientific). NAb levels were determined based on the inhibition of reporter expression compared with control samples.

Investigation of immune indices, inflammatory factors, and T cell differentiation

Blood samples were collected from cynomolgus monkeys on study days 0, 7, 28, and 91. Following collection, the samples were aliquoted and stored at a temperature below −60°C to preserve sample integrity. Quantitative determination of immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, IgA), complement components (C3 and C4), and circulating immune complexes (CIC) was performed using immunoturbidimetric assays. Specifically, the immunoglobulins and complement factors were analyzed using a cobas 8000 modular analyzer (Roche Diagnostics), while CIC levels were measured with a UV-2600i spectrophotometer (SHIMADZU CORPORATION). T lymphocyte subsets and their respective subtypes were identified and quantified via flow cytometry using a CytoFLEX LX instrument (Beckman Coulter), following the manufacturer’s standard operating procedures. Inflammatory cytokine levels were assessed using electrochemiluminescence immunoassays (ECLIA) according to the provided instruction manuals. The panel of inflammatory markers included interleukin-2 (IL-2), IL-5, IL-6, IL-10, and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) using the detection kit from Meso Scale Discovery (K156A0H-1/2, MSD), as well as IL-4 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) using another detection kit (K15068M-1, MSD).

Laser-induced neovascularization model

On day 28, neovascularization was induced in the area surrounding the macula via laser photocoagulation. The monkeys were anesthetized with 2.5% pentobarbital sodium (25 mg/kg) by intravenous injection. Bilateral eyes received ofloxacin eye ointment to keep the cornea wet during anesthesia and were dilated with Mydrin-p (Compound Tropicamide Eye Drops). The head of each monkey was fixed in front of the ophthalmic laser photocoagulation instrument, and panretinal photocoagulation was performed for the perimacular area. Nine laser spots were created around the macula of each eye while avoiding the macular central fovea. The spot diameter was approximately 50 μm with a power of 0.5–0.7 W and an exposure time of 0.05 s.

Following laser photocoagulation, retinal thickness was evaluated using Spectralis OCT (Heidelberg). Retinal thickness was defined as the thickness from the internal limiting membrane (ILM) to Bruch’s membrane.

Colorful fundus photography and fundus fluorescein angiography

For colorful fundus photography (CFP) and fundus fluorescein angiography (FFA) examination, the monkeys were anesthetized with ketamine (10 mg/kg) via intravenous injection and dilated with Mydrin-p (Compound Tropicamide Eye Drops; one to two drops/eye). Then, CFP was obtained using Spectralis HRA (Heidelberg). Following quick injection of fluorescein sodium (20 mg/kg) into the saphenous veins of the lower limb, early-phase (within about 1 min) and late-phase (5–10 min) fundus angiograms were obtained using Spectralis HRA (Heidelberg). Finally, ofloxacin eye ointment (one to two drops) was used to prevent infection.

Grading scoring was conducted for angiograms collected on days 42, 56, 70, and 91 using the Heidelberg Eye Explorer (HEYEX) software. Fluorescein leakage was graded by a masked technician according to the following grading scale:

Grade I: the spot showed no hyperfluorescence; Grade II: the spot showed hyperfluorescence, but without fluorescein leakage; Grade III: the spot showed hyperfluorescence and slight fluorescein leakage within the spot edge; Grade IV: the spot showed hyperfluorescence and significant fluorescein leakage beyond the spot edge.

The ratio of grade IV lesions in each group was calculated according to formula 2:

Ratio of grade IV lesions = the total number of grade IV lesions/the total number of evaluated lesions.

Statistical evaluation

In this study, all quantitative data were displayed as mean ± SD. Significance analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA and subsequent Dunnett’s t test for significance between groups. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 or Stata/IC 15.0 for Windows. The significance threshold of p < 0.05 was applied to all comparisons. ns (no significance) means no statistical difference. ∗ means significant difference (p < 0.05).
