A spatiotemporal atlas of senescence-associated secretory phenotype in fracture healing: stage-dependent mechanisms and therapeutic windows
Authors: Binyu Chen, Gan Li, Qihang Fang, Peng Ding, Youshui Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFracture repair remains a significant clinical challenge in orthopedics, particularly in aging populations. Accumulating evidence indicates that cellular senescence critically modulates the fracture microenvironment via the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). The SASP constitutes a complex secretory program of senescent cells that releases pro-inflammatory cytokines, growth factors, and matrix-modifying enzymes to reshape the surrounding microenvironment. Rather than being a random collection of molecules, the SASP represents a coordinated signaling network that can either promote tissue repair or drive chronic inflammation depending on its context. In this review, we apply a systematic SASP classification to the canonical four phases of fracture healing to clarify their distinct roles across these stages. During the inflammatory phase, SASP-associated (but non-exclusive) inflammatory cytokines and chemokines potentiate innate immune cell recruitment and early host defense, thereby initiating the repair cascade; in the soft-callus phase, chemotactic and angiogenic SASP components (such as CCL2, PDGF, VEGF) position mesenchymal progenitors and support chondrogenesis and neovascularization; in the hard-callus phase, osteoinductive growth factors and matrix-acting proteases (such as TGF-β, IGFBPs, MMP-9/13) promote cartilage-to-bone conversion and early mineral deposition; and in the remodeling phase, regulated SASP-mediated matrix changes couple osteoclast resorption with osteoblast formation, while sustained pro-inflammatory or profibrotic signals may hinder the fine remodeling of the structure. This stage-resolved framework elucidates the dual nature of SASP, with transient activation facilitating repair and sustained overproduction leading to impaired remodeling. This framework provides a conceptual basis for developing stage-specific interventions to enhance bone repair. Notably, many mediators discussed here overlap with the broader injury-induced inflammatory secretome and are not senescence-exclusive; where possible, we emphasize senescent-cell-enriched contributions and regulatory circuits rather than implying unique cellular origins.Keywords: Fracture healing, Bone regeneration, Cellular senescence, Senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), Spatiotemporal atlas, Senolytics

Background

Fracture healing is a regenerative process of bone after injury, which aims to restore the structural integrity and biomechanical function of the injured bone [1]. This process progresses through four overlapping yet distinct stages: inflammation, soft callus formation, hard callus formation, and remodeling [2]. Within these stages, two osteogenic pathways—endochondral and intramembranous ossification—contribute to new bone formation depending on the local biomechanical and vascular conditions: intramembranous ossification predominates in well-stabilized and well-perfused regions, whereas endochondral ossification occurs in unstable or hypoxic sites where cartilage first forms and is later replaced by bone [1, 3]. During the hard callus and remodeling phases, osteoclasts play indispensable roles in coupling bone resorption with new bone formation to restore cortical architecture. Despite the intrinsic regenerative capacity of bone, the success of fracture repair is profoundly influenced by systemic factors, most notably aging. The aging process not only increases fracture incidence but also delays healing, largely due to age-related cellular alterations, among which cellular senescence has emerged as a critical determinant of impaired bone regeneration [4].

Cellular senescence (CS) is a fundamental biological process involved in tissue remodeling, regeneration, and aging. Cellular senescence was initially defined as permanent growth arrest in proliferating cells [5]. However, recent studies show that transient senescence contributes to normal development and tissue repair, whereas its chronic persistence promotes inflammation and degeneration [6]. The accumulation of senescent cells is implicated in diverse age-related diseases such as osteoarthritis, atherosclerosis, pulmonary fibrosis, diabetes, and neurodegeneration [4, 7]. A defining feature of senescent cells is the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)—a complex and dynamic secretory program comprising cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases [8]. The SASP acts as a key mediator linking cellular senescence to tissue-level outcomes: it can promote regeneration and immune clearance of senescent cells in the short term, yet chronic or excessive SASP signaling drives sustained inflammation, matrix degradation, and fibrosis [9]. Thus, the biological impact of senescence largely depends on the balance between transient, adaptive SASP activation and long-term, maladaptive persistence, a concept particularly relevant in tissues with high regenerative demands such as bone.

Emerging research has established a pivotal role for the SASP in modulating the bone repair microenvironment [10, 11]. Transcriptomic analyses indicate that SASP-related factors are dynamically upregulated during the healing process, and the presence of senescent cells varies across different stages of callus formation [10]. The timing, localization, and composition of SASP components have been shown to correlate with the progression of bone repair, suggesting that senescence-associated signaling exerts distinct effects at different healing phases [10, 11]. Notwithstanding these advances, a systematic understanding of the spatiotemporal regulation of SASP across the distinct healing phases remains elusive. Current evidence is fragmented, often focusing on isolated pathways or cell types without integrating their temporal and spatial context. Given the dual nature of SASP—beneficial when transient yet detrimental when persistent—deciphering its stage-specific mechanisms is essential for identifying therapeutic windows to modulate senescence-driven processes. Therefore, this review constructs a spatiotemporal atlas of SASP in fracture healing, summarizing current findings on its dynamic regulation and functional diversity across the four canonical phases.

Senescence and SASP

Cellular senescence is a state of permanent cell-cycle arrest induced by various stressors, such as DNA damage, metabolic alterations, and oncogene activation. During cellular senescence, telomere shortening, oxidative stress, oncogene activation, and DNA damage trigger stress-response pathways, leading cells to enter a largely irreversible cell-cycle arrest and evade apoptosis. Accordingly, telomere length has emerged as a key biomarker of cellular aging, and a broad spectrum of methodologies has been developed to quantify human telomeres in experimental and clinical samples [12] (Fig. 1).

Diverse stresses—including DNA damage and telomere attrition, oxidative and mitochondrial stress, metabolic and inflammatory stress (for example, via NLRP3 inflammasome activation and gasdermin D [GSDMD]-mediated pyroptosis), oncogenic signals, mechanical injury and fracture, and therapy-induced damage—drive cells into a stable senescent state. Senescent cells accumulate in multiple tissues and secrete SASP factors composed of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases. In acute and well-controlled settings, SASP exerts beneficial effects, such as wound repair and tissue remodeling, early callus remodeling after fracture, and tumor immunosurveillance mediated by cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs). In contrast, persistent senescent cells and chronic SASP production promote detrimental outcomes, including chronic inflammation, delayed or impaired fracture healing, osteoporosis and bone loss, fibrosis with increased tissue stiffness, and tumor growth and dissemination. Together, the figure highlights that therapeutic targeting of senescent cells or SASP must balance preservation of beneficial functions with prevention of long-term tissue damage

Despite the arrest of cell cycle and proliferation, senescent cells maintain an active metabolic state and secrete a wide range of factors that affect themselves and their surrounding environment. These secretory factors form the so-called SASP, which was first introduced in 2008 as a phenotype in which genotoxic stress-induced aging in human cells results in the secretion of substances associated with inflammation and malignancy [8]. Early studies defined the SASP as a set of secreted proteins. Researchers measured these proteins mainly in the conditioned media of cultured senescent cells using antibody arrays and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) [8, 13–15]. The SASP is now recognized as a complex and dynamic secretory program. It includes hundreds of proteins and non‑protein signaling molecules, including pro‑inflammatory factors, proteases, and growth factors. Nevertheless, the full spectrum of SASP components remains incompletely characterized [16]. The components and levels of the SASP can differ based on the stimulus that induces senescence.

SASP composition and levels also vary depending on the cell type and the duration of the senescent state [8, 17]. The SASP comprises a range of bioactive factors that can reinforce senescence in their source cells and influence both the local microenvironment and even the organism as a whole [9, 18]. The SASP factors enhance growth arrest in an autocrine manner and alters the behavior of surrounding cells in a paracrine manner [18]. Hundreds of individual SASP factors have been documented, among which the most common factors include interleukin-1α (IL-1α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1 (IL-1), and others such as interferon-γ (IFN-γ), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) [19–21], and a subset are packaged within extracellular vesicles, such as exosomes, for release [20, 22]. The SASP exhibits considerable heterogeneity across tissues (e.g., skin, bone, liver) and under diverse stress conditions [23–25]. Furthermore, the SASP factors exert dual regulation on bone tissue. In the context of fracture healing, SASP can either accelerate repair by promoting inflammation or delay bone formation if secretion becomes excessive [26]. Many studies have shown that SASP is heterogeneous and highly dynamic, and it has various beneficial and harmful biological functions [27]. To facilitate research, SASP components are frequently categorized according to functional categories [22, 27]. In this framework, SASP factors are grouped into eight major functional categories: (1) inflammatory cytokines; (2) chemokines; (3) growth or hormone-like factors; (4) proteases and their inhibitors; (5) extracellular matrix/tissue-remodeling proteins; (6) complement and coagulation-related mediators; (7) soluble receptors and binding proteins; (8) stress-related, metabolic, or damage-associated signals (Table 1).

Spatiotemporal dynamics of SASP factors during fracture healing

Biological framework of fracture healing

To precisely map the dynamic roles of SASP outlined above onto the process of bone repair, a detailed understanding of the spatiotemporal progression of fracture healing is necessary. Bone is a highly specialized connective tissue composed of a mineralized extracellular matrix and diverse resident cell populations that collectively maintain skeletal integrity and mobility [65]. Following injury, the bone undergoes a multicellular, temporally coordinated regenerative process that restores both structure and function [1, 66]. Rather than a simple ossification event, fracture healing represents a complex orchestration of inflammation, callus formation, and remodeling, in which immune, mesenchymal, endothelial, and skeletal lineage cells interact within a changing biochemical and biomechanical microenvironment [67]. Depending on the mechanical stability and vascularization at the fracture site, healing may occur through direct (primary) or indirect (secondary) mechanisms. Direct healing, which is relatively uncommon, requires precise anatomical reduction and proceeds via intramembranous ossification without callus formation. In contrast, indirect healing is the predominant pathway, characterized by the coordinated occurrence of endochondral and intramembranous ossification, resulting in the formation and remodeling of a cartilaginous and bony callus [1, 66]. This regenerative sequence is conventionally divided into four overlapping phases: inflammatory, soft callus, hard callus, and remodeling. Each stage is defined by distinct cellular activities, molecular signaling, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Successful fracture repair depends on the proper temporal resolution of inflammation, spatial coordination of osteogenic and chondrogenic programs, and balanced bone resorption and formation during remodeling. Failure of this regulation leads to delayed healing or nonunion [2]. Understanding fracture healing as a spatiotemporally dynamic process provides the biological foundation for this review.

Coordinated endochondral and intramembranous ossification in fracture healing

Fracture healing involves two different developmental modes of bone formation: endochondral ossification and intramembranous ossification. In a typical long bone fracture under moderate stability, both modes work together: soft callus forms between the fracture ends (endochondral ossification). Meanwhile, osteoblasts in the periosteum directly form new woven bone around the periphery (intramembranous ossification) [1, 68, 69]. Endochondral ossification plays a major role in connecting unstable fracture spaces, as mesenchymal progenitor cells differentiate into chondrocytes, which form fibrocartilage matrix that is subsequently replaced by bone tissue [70–72]. In contrast, intramembranous ossification occurs at the well-vascularized periosteal surfaces, where osteoprogenitor cells directly form woven bone without a cartilage intermediary, contributing to the early mechanical stabilization of the callus [73, 74]. This intramembranous bone deposition contributes to the early increase of callus hardness. Meanwhile, endochondral ossification occurs in regions with poor stability or low oxygen content. In summary, the mechanical milieu (such as interfragmentary strain and stability) governs the relative contribution of each osteogenic pathway. High strain and micromotion tend to induce the formation of cartilage intermediates, while good rigid stability conditions allow direct intramembranous bone formation [75, 76]. Regions experiencing poor mechanical stability and disrupted perfusion become locally hypoxic, which makes mesenchymal precursor cells more inclined to differentiate into cartilage, thus filling the larger gap through endochondral osteogenesis [75, 77, 78]. The interaction of intramembranous osteogenesis and endochondral osteogenesis provides mechanical continuity and stability for the fracture site [69, 79, 80] (Fig. 2).

Inflammatory phase: initiation of repair and cytokine networks with SASP-overlapping mediators

Non-SASP

Immediately after fracture, infiltrating neutrophils and monocyte-derived macrophages, together with mast cells and activated stromal cells, generate a high-amplitude inflammatory and pro-angiogenic secretome that initiates debris clearance, immune recruitment, and early vascular activation. Below, we outline the stage-specific secretory milieu across the temporal sequence of repair, beginning with the inflammatory phase. The inflammatory phase marks the onset of fracture healing. Within the first 24 h after injury, vascular rupture triggers hematoma formation at the fracture site, creating a hypoxic, acidic, and calcium-rich microenvironment that serves as both a scaffold and a signaling hub for immune and stromal cell recruitment [1, 81]. The hematoma formed immediately after fracture serves as a provisional scaffold for the recruitment of inflammatory cells and the accumulation of cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and C–C motif chemokine ligand 2 (CCL2) [29, 82]. Within the first 24–48 h, neutrophils rapidly infiltrate the hematoma, initiating debris clearance and antimicrobial defense [83]. These cells undergo apoptosis soon after, followed by the sequential recruitment of resident and circulating macrophages [84]. In parallel, a smaller population of lymphocytes—including T cells, B cells, and mast cells—also migrates into the hematoma [85, 86]. These cellular components, together with platelets and erythrocytes, are concentrated within the fracture hematoma [77, 87]. Moreover, this phase is characterized by edema, fibrin deposition, and the accumulation of cellular debris [88, 89] As the inflammation subsides, apoptotic neutrophils and activated macrophages within the hematoma are gradually replaced by granulation tissue, marking the transition from inflammation to repair [90]. The fracture hematoma itself is indispensable for successful healing, as it serves as a rich source of inflammatory mediators and extracellular substrates that facilitate cell migration and tissue regeneration [87, 91]. Additionally, some studies have shown that the absence of fracture hematoma delays healing [87, 91, 92]. During this phase, activated macrophages accumulate at the fracture site and secrete high levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and other cytokines [93]. These factors can stimulate local fibroblast proliferation, promote the migration of muscle-derived stromal cells, and attract lymphocytes to the region [94–96]. At this stage, macrophage-derived IL-1β and TNF-α orchestrate the recruitment and proliferation of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) and regulate angiogenesis [90]. Macrophages also produce CCL2/monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1). CCL2 is a potent chemokine that recruits circulating monocytes and is essential in fracture repair [41]. Furthermore, CCL2 expression polarizes macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype [97]. In addition to macrophages, mast cells promote the formation of granulation tissue and early callus by releasing histamine (increasing vascular permeability) and VEGF (driving angiogenesis) [98, 99]. Beyond their cytokine and chemokine output, macrophages also undergo profound metabolic reprogramming that links their inflammatory phenotype to glycolytic and oxidative pathways, critically shaping the transition from pro-inflammatory to pro-resolving states during tissue repair [100]. Importantly, the acute inflammatory phase is age-sensitive. Age-associated chronic inflammation (“inflammaging”) compromises skeletal stem/progenitor function and can prolong pro-inflammatory cytokine exposure in the fracture milieu [68]. The fracture callus in the inflammatory stage is filled with pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which work together to coordinate the healing process [77]. For example, IL-1α/IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 are markedly upregulated at the site of injury [28, 101]. Within a few hours to a day after fracture, elevated levels of these cytokines, together with chemokines such as CCL2 and CXCL1/KC, can be detected in the hematoma [29, 45, 86]. During cell necrosis, IL-1α is passively released into the extracellular environment, where it rapidly activates neighboring cells and amplifies the inflammatory cascade [102]. Additionally, TNF-α and IL-1β, primarily secreted by infiltrating macrophages and neutrophils, further reinforce cytokine production and leukocyte recruitment at the fracture site [83]. The synergistic effect of TNF-α and IL-1β increases the expression of adhesion molecules and chemokines, achieving a positive feedback loop in white blood cells [103]. These two cytokines also affect mesenchymal cells at the fracture site. For example, IL-1 directly drives fibroblast proliferation and promotes matrix production to initiate granulation tissue formation [95, 104]. TNF-α can also promote the proliferation of these cells and participate in matrix remodeling, but it has dual effects on collagen synthesis: Most in vitro studies have shown that it inhibits type I/III collagen and promotes collagen degradation, while it has also been shown that TNF-α promotes the synthesis of type III/VI collagen under long-term stimulation [105–107]. Moreover, IL-6 is regulated very early after fracture and gradually falls back in the following days [101]. Acting as a multifunctional cytokine, IL-6 promotes neutrophil and monocyte recruitment through trans-signaling, bridges inflammation and bone turnover, and enhances angiogenesis at the repair site via the signal transducer and activator of transcription-3 (STAT3)–VEGF axis [108–110]. Consistently, studies have shown that IL-6 deficiency results in delayed fracture healing, impaired callus formation, and reduced osteoclast activity [111]. Meanwhile, activated mesenchymal cells and immune cells secrete CXCL1/IL-8 to attract neutrophils [112, 113]. Recruited neutrophils not only clear necrotic debris but also release growth factors such as VEGF-A, thereby stimulating angiogenesis and tissue regeneration [114]. Together, these factors establish a cytokine-rich microenvironment, which is essential for initiating the healing process. In murine models, Il6 mRNA shows an early peak within the first few days after injury; however, in some data, Il1b is downregulated at these initial time points [10]. In contrast, other studies report Il1b upregulation at day 3 [115]. By contrast, in murine fracture models, Il17a rises rapidly and peaks in the very early phase (day 2–3), then diminishes as healing progresses—consistent with the inflammatory phase [116, 117]. Inflammatory cytokines initiate and sustain immune activation, stimulating resident stromal cells to participate in repair. Chemokines orchestrate immune cell recruitment and influence their functional polarization. Proteases remodel the extracellular matrix, removing necrotic components and releasing bioactive fragments that facilitate tissue turnover. Meanwhile, growth factors drive angiogenesis and the proliferation and differentiation of progenitor cells, ultimately replacing the hematoma with reparative tissue. Together, these mediators generate a robust yet self-limiting inflammatory response that establishes the foundation for subsequent phases of bone regeneration.

SASP

Superimposed on this acute injury-induced milieu, a transient senescent-cell compartment contributes a senescence-restricted secretory layer (SASP); for shared mediators, attribution should be tied to senescence markers rather than mediator identity alone. Consistently, postmenopausal/aging-mimicking models show altered macrophage dynamics and inflammatory cytokine expression during early fracture healing [101], which may amplify early SASP-like signaling and delay the transition to reparative granulation tissue. As an upstream cytokine, IL-1α is a major regulator of SASP [118]. Even a minimal amount of IL-1α can trigger nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling, promoting the production of other SASP factors such as IL-6 and CXCL1 [30]. During the inflammatory phase, a subset of local cells undergoes cellular senescence, leading to transient enrichment of SASP factors at the fracture site [119]. Recent studies suggest that even in young mice, senescent cells can transiently accumulate in the fracture callus during healing, with levels peaking during the inflammatory phase and early cartilaginous callus formation [10]. These senescent cells include MSCs, some osteoblast lineage and inflammatory cells, remain metabolically active and secrete a series of SASP factors that largely overlap with the pro-inflammatory cytokines of the inflammatory phase [10]. Furthermore, single-cell RNA analyses of murine fracture calluses have found that SASP-associated (but non-exclusive) genes are highly enriched in the MSC population within the callus [115, 119]. This indicates that local mesenchymal progenitor cells become an important source of a large number of injury-induced cytokines that overlap with canonical SASP signatures after being stimulated to enter the aging state. The dynamic expression of SASP factors during fracture healing is complex. The regular increase or decrease of these SASP mediators ensures a strong but self-limiting inflammatory response. In summary, the various categories of SASP factors act in a coordinated manner to shape the inflammatory milieu during early fracture healing.

Soft callus phase: chondrogenic differentiation and transient senescence of osteochondral progenitors

Non-SASP

As acute inflammation resolves, chondrocytes, fibroblasts, stromal cells, and residual immune populations sustain a reparative secretome that supports fibrocartilage deposition, cell recruitment, and early neovascular patterning within the developing soft callus. When inflammation subsides, the fracture transitions into the soft callus formation phase, characterized by the development of a fibrocartilaginous matrix that bridges the fracture ends [2]. During this stage, mesenchymal progenitor cells migrate to the injury site and differentiate into chondrocytes, establishing a cartilaginous scaffold that stabilizes the fracture [2, 65]. Among these, transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) is highly expressed, functioning as a potent inhibitor of late osteoblast differentiation and contributing to the fine-tuning of callus maturation [120]. Moreover, the regulatory effect of transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) on bone metabolism is concentration-dependent: low levels of TGF-β promote osteoclast differentiation, whereas high levels exert an inhibitory effect [121, 122]. At this stage, the microenvironment progressively shifts from an inflammatory to a reparative state. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, which peak in the early phase, gradually decline as anti-inflammatory signaling increases and granulation tissue matures [123]. The growth of new capillaries into the callus is another sign of this stage, ensuring an adequate blood supply for the expansion of the callus and later ossification [123]. Collectively, the soft callus phase is characterized by a hypercellular and biosynthetically active tissue, because chondrocytes and fibroblasts produce type II collagen and proteoglycans to form cartilage callus, while the biological characteristics of the fracture site shift from inflammation to repair. These events form a soft tissue scaffold connecting the fracture ends, paving the way for subsequent hard callus formation and bone healing. Collagen composition and turnover are tightly stage-coupled. Early granulation tissue is enriched in type III collagen, providing a compliant provisional matrix that is subsequently remodeled into type I collagen-rich woven bone. During the soft callus stage, chondrocytes synthesize type II collagen as the principal cartilaginous scaffold, whereas hypertrophic chondrocytes express type X collagen to promote cartilage mineralization and vascular invasion during endochondral transition [69–72]. Type VI collagen forms a pericellular network that supports cell–matrix anchorage and mechanotransduction during callus maturation and remodeling [105–107]. For instance, VEGF-A, IGFBP-3, and IGFBP-5 increase within the first week after fracture and remain elevated through approximately day 15, supporting angiogenesis and cell survival within the largely avascular callus. This upregulation of Serpine1 and PDGF-A suggests active remodeling and cell migration in the soft callus. In terms of interleukins, IL-6 has an early peak and remains at a low level. In contrast, IL-17A shows a gradual decline after an initial increase, being much lower by the end of the soft callus period [10]. CCL2-knockout mice exhibit deficient macrophage/MSC infiltration, reduced callus vascularization, and significantly delayed fracture union [124]. TGF-β and platelet-derived growth factor-AA (PDGF-AA) released in the callus stimulate mesenchymal cell proliferation and differentiation into chondrocytes and osteoblasts, thereby expanding the callus tissue and initiating its maturation [2, 119]. For example, MMP-13, produced by hypertrophic chondrocytes and osteoblasts in the callus, is required for proper resorption of cartilage matrix and the transition from soft callus to hard callus [60, 72]. Besides, mice lacking MMP-13 or MMP-9 show accumulation of cartilage and delayed ossification in their fracture calluses [10, 60]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines recruit and activate essential immune and stromal cells; growth factors drive angiogenesis and chondrogenic–osteogenic differentiation; and proteases remodel the extracellular environment to enable the progression from fibrocartilaginous to mineralized callus. Through this tightly regulated network, the soft callus evolves into a structurally competent template that supports the formation of mature lamellar bone in the subsequent healing stage.

SASP

In the soft callus, a discrete population of senescent osteochondral/progenitor cells contributes a spatially restricted SASP program that evolves as chondrogenesis and early osteogenesis proceed. Although overt inflammation has resolved, senescent cells transiently accumulate within the developing callus during this period [26]. A subset of osteochondral progenitor cells in the periosteal callus rapidly enters senescence, as indicated by upregulated p16 and p21 expression and cell-cycle arrest. These p21 + osteochondral progenitor cells (OCH cells) appear within the first week after fracture. They express typical senescence markers (such as DNA damage foci) and acquire the characteristics of SASP. In their studies, transcriptome analysis revealed that p21 + OCH cells had a large number of secreted factors known to hinder osteoblast maturation and bone formation [119]. During the soft callus phase, numerous SASP-associated (but non-exclusive) molecules exhibit dynamic and stage-dependent changes. In contrast, chemokines such as CCL2 and platelet-derived growth factor-A (PDGF-A) peak in the early soft callus and subsequently decline as the hard callus phase approaches, while another SASP component, Serpine1, rises markedly during this period, indicating ongoing matrix remodeling and cell migration [10]. Notably, the distribution of SASP-producing cells is spatially heterogeneous. For example, P21 + cells (such as OCH cells) are mainly found in periosteal callus and cartilage periphery [10, 119]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (like IL-1α/β, IL-6, TNF-α) are prominently elevated in the early fracture callus as part of the SASP and inflammatory response [29, 119]. IL-1β and IL-6, released from senescent and inflammatory cells, enhance the infiltration of macrophages and lymphocytes and promote the migration of MSCs to the fracture site [29]. Besides, mice lacking IL-6 or TNF signaling exhibit impaired callus formation and delayed endochondral bone repair, underscoring the pivotal signaling role of these injury-induced cytokines that overlap with canonical SASP signatures in early-stage healing [29]. In addition to cytokines, chemokines commonly included in SASP catalogs direct cell trafficking within the callus. CCL2, secreted by senescent callus cells and tissue macrophages, potently recruits monocytes/macrophages and bone marrow–derived MSCs into the fracture microenvironment [83, 125, 126]. Likewise, neutrophil-attracting chemokines such as CXCL1/IL-8, another component of the SASP, promote the recruitment of neutrophils that not only remove necrotic debris but also release pro-angiogenic factors facilitating tissue repair [119]. The growth factors in SASP are crucial for promoting the regeneration process of cartilage callus. Meanwhile, VEGF, one of the most prominent SASP mediators, induces robust angiogenesis within the cartilage callus, forming a vascular network that delivers oxygen and nutrients essential for tissue survival and subsequent mineralization [123, 127]. Moreover, although these growth factors usually contribute to healing, excessive or sustained output of SASP can be counterproductive [119]. For example, TGF-β1, which is persistently overexpressed by senescent cells in older fracture callus, inhibits bone formation [120]. Finally, proteases and inhibitors released as part of SASP play a key role in callus remodeling. Collectively, the diverse SASP mediators coordinate a finely tuned balance between inflammation, tissue renewal, and matrix turnover.

Endochondral ossification and structural stabilization

Non-SASP

At the ossification front, non-senescent cells at the cartilage–bone transition zone produce a remodeling and angiogenic secretome that promotes vascular invasion and matrix turnover required for endochondral ossification. The hard callus stage represents the transition from a fibrocartilaginous to a mineralized callus through endochondral ossification, during which the cartilage template is replaced by woven bone bridging the fracture gap [2, 128]. This stage typically begins within 2–3 weeks post-fracture in rodent models—corresponding to a proportionally longer period in humans—and overlaps with the late soft callus stage [2, 128]. During the formation of hard callus, hypertrophic chondrocytes in the cartilage callus calcify the surrounding matrix, and then either undergo apoptosis or transdifferentiate into osteoblast-like cells. At the same time, vascular and osteoblast lineage cells invade and absorb the calcified cartilage [2, 26]. Osteoprogenitor cells originating from the periosteum and endosteal surfaces proliferate and differentiate into osteoblasts, depositing new woven bone along the cartilage remnants. Simultaneously, chondroclasts and osteoclasts participate in the removal of calcified cartilage and immature bone, thereby facilitating callus maturation [2, 129]. By the end of this stage, the fracture ends are connected by hard woven bone callus, conferring considerable mechanical stability [2]. Meanwhile, around day 14, the transcription levels of MMP-9 and MMP-13 in the callus rise sharply, at which time hypertrophic chondrocytes are being cleared and woven bone is being deposited. Consistent with this, the expression of VEGF-A remains elevated throughout the hard callus stage, sustaining angiogenesis within the developing callus. Meanwhile, several chemokines display delayed peaks corresponding to the timing of hard callus formation; for instance, by day 14, CCL7 expression increases more than 70-fold relative to baseline [10]. Meanwhile, the aging mesenchymal stromal cells in the periosteum and callus secrete inflammatory cytokines and growth factors, regulating the behavior of nearby bone progenitor cells and inflammatory cells [26, 120]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines within the callus, such as IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, promote endochondral ossification by recruiting and activating osteoclast-lineage cells and modulating osteoblast and chondrocyte activity [119, 128]. For example, IL-1α and IL-1β not only stimulate osteoclastogenesis but also induce apoptosis of osteoblasts and hypertrophic chondrocytes, thereby facilitating the removal of mineralized cartilage and preventing premature or excessive bone deposition [130]. Interestingly, moderate TNF-α is beneficial to the progression of hard callus. Paradoxically, too much TNF-α or its presence for too long may be harmful [96, 131]. For instance, CXCL1 (and its human homolog CXCL8/IL-8) is markedly upregulated in the early callus, serving as a potent chemoattractant for neutrophils and osteoclast precursors, which clear debris and remodel the nascent bone matrix [119]. Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) and FGFs promote the proliferation and migration of mesenchymal progenitors and endothelial cells, facilitating both callus expansion and vascular integration [2]. At physiological levels, it enhances chondrocyte hypertrophy and extracellular matrix synthesis, whereas sustained or excessive expression inhibits osteoblast differentiation and callus mineralization [120]. Mice deficient in MMP-13 or MMP-9 exhibit impaired cartilage resorption and delayed mineralization of the hard callus, ultimately leading to defective fracture healing [26, 77]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines establish a transient inflammatory milieu that recruits and activates immune and stromal cells; growth factors drive angiogenesis and osteogenic differentiation; and matrix proteases reshape the provisional cartilage matrix to permit the replacement of soft callus with woven bone.

SASP

During hard callus formation, senescent-cell burden and SASP output peak and become enriched at the cartilage–bone transition zone, aligning senescence-linked secretion with vascular invasion and matrix turnover. The abundance of senescent cells and the expression levels of SASP factors peak during the early part of this stage [2, 69, 74]. In murine fracture models, p21 and p16 expression, along with the abundance of senescent cells, maximizes approximately 2 weeks post-fracture. Notably, the number of SADS-positive senescent cells within the callus increases nearly sixfold—peaking on days 8 and 14 compared with uninjured bone, which coincides with the period of active woven bone formation and extensive hard callus formation. Spatially, senescent cells and SASP activity concentrate near the vascular invasion front (cartilage–bone transition zone), where calcified cartilage is progressively replaced by newly deposited bone. These senescent chondrocytes and/or invading osteoclast lineage cells produce proteases that can digest cartilage matrix to enable vascular ingress and osteoblastic bone deposition. At this interface, senescent hypertrophic chondrocytes and osteochondral progenitor cells accumulate and release SASP factors that drive matrix remodeling and vascular infiltration, both prerequisites for ossification [129]. The early senescent neutrophils are mostly cleared by the hard callus stage, when a later wave of p16^Ink4a + senescent cells (including osteoprogenitors and chondrocytes) dominates the SASP profile in the callus [119]. Together, as cartilage callus is replaced by hard callus, the expression of SASP factors is enhanced at the site of tissue renewal, coordinating cell recruitment and matrix remodeling to achieve successful bone regeneration. A key function of SASP during the hard callus phase is to orchestrate the balance between bone formation and resorption. In parallel, chemokines within the SASP direct immune and progenitor cell trafficking to the fracture site. Other chemokines like CCL2 (MCP-1) are also elevated and recruit monocytes/macrophages to the callus, which in turn support angiogenesis and osteogenesis or, if senescent, contribute their own SASP factors to the environment [26, 119]. Growth factors within the SASP milieu exert pivotal control over cell proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis during hard callus formation. VEGF, secreted by senescent chondrocytes and stromal cells, drives neovascularization toward the callus, ensuring sufficient delivery of oxygen, nutrients, and osteoprogenitor cells to support active bone formation [2, 132]. Among SASP components, TGF-β1 serves as a key yet context-dependent regulator. Notably, neutralizing TGF-β activity or selectively eliminating TGF-β–producing senescent cells alleviates osteogenic suppression in aged fracture models, underscoring the necessity of a balanced growth factor milieu within the callus [120]. Finally, matrix proteases and other degradative enzymes are prominent SASP factors that drive the structural turnover of the callus. Senescent cells in the hard callus secrete enzymes like MMP-13 and MMP-9, which degrade type II collagen and other components of the cartilage matrix, thus clearing the way for new bone deposition [129]. Genetic evidence further underscores the importance of SASP-linked proteases in callus maturation. Similarly, MMP-12, another SASP-associated (but non-exclusive) protease, contributes to extracellular matrix remodeling and is co-upregulated with inflammatory injury-induced cytokines that overlap with canonical SASP signatures during the repair process [26]. These coordinated proteolytic and inflammatory activities ensure the timely transition from soft callus to mineralized bone, whereas dysregulated or persistent SASP signaling can result in aberrant remodeling and delayed union. In summary, the hard callus phase represents a highly integrated stage of SASP-mediated regulation, in which distinct molecular classes act synergistically. Through this coordinated network, SASP activity facilitates the structural consolidation of the fracture, setting the stage for subsequent bone remodeling and functional restoration.

Remodeling phase: structural consolidation and long-term SASP modulation

Non-SASP

During remodeling, non-senescent osteo-immune coupling signals produced by osteoblast/osteoclast lineages and supporting immune/stromal cells coordinate resorption–formation balance and architectural refinement of the callus. The final and longest stage of fracture healing is the remodeling phase, during which the hard callus undergoes gradual structural reorganization to restore the bone’s pre-injury architecture. In rodent models, this phase typically begins around 3 weeks post-fracture and can persist for months to years, depending on the extent of injury and systemic conditions. During this period, the hard callus undergoes gradual structural reorganization to restore the bone’s pre-injury architecture. The woven bone formed during earlier stages is progressively resorbed and replaced by lamellar bone, while the marrow cavity and cortical structure are re-established [2, 65, 133]. At the cellular level, remodeling is achieved through the coupled activity of osteoclasts and osteoblasts: osteoclasts remove excess callus and resorb immature bone, whereas osteoblasts deposit organized lamellar bone, reconstructing both cortical and trabecular compartments to restore mechanical integrity [2, 69, 77]. In this stage, bone remodeling proceeds through a resorptive cascade, during which the hard callus is progressively remodeled into lamellar bone with reconstitution of the central medullary cavity, thereby completing the process of skeletal repair. This remodeling is orchestrated by multiple cytokines and morphogens, primarily IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, with contributory effects from bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) [28, 77, 96, 134]. Bone morphogenetic protein-3/−4/−6/−7/−8 (BMP-3/−4/−6/−7/−8) exhibit marked upregulation around the third week post-fracture, promoting cartilage resorption, osteoclast activation, and osteoblastic recruitment to the remodeling front [134, 135]. Concurrently, activation of the Wnt signaling pathway favors osteogenesis while suppressing chondrogenic differentiation. In late healing, however, sclerostin expression increases and competitively binds BMP receptors, thereby attenuating BMP signaling and inhibiting chondrogenic differentiation of subperiosteal MSCs [136]. TNF-α promotes the apoptosis of osteocytes [83], while some chondrocytes become apoptotic or necrotic in the remaining soft callus. Multiple soluble factors are liberated in this process, including TGF-β, Wnt family member 10B (WNT10B), BMP-6, slit guidance ligand-3 (SLIT-3), MMP-13, and MMP-9, which are crucial for subsequent osteogenesis [77]. A prolonged low-grade inflammatory state, often referred to as “inflammaging,” extends the remodeling timeline and impairs the restoration of normal bone architecture [129]. In aged animals, the remodeling process is typically prolonged and incomplete. Compared with young mice, older mice exhibit extended callus remodeling, residual woven bone, and inferior biomechanical properties [129, 137]. These changes are closely associated with an aging callus microenvironment. Analogous observations in pathological bone marrow niches show that stromal cells can modulate miRNAs such as miR-21 and miR-15a/16 in plasma cells via adhesion, cytokine secretion and exosome transfer, underscoring microRNAs as mediators of bone marrow microenvironmental crosstalk [138]. While a transient surge of such cytokines early in healing is important for callus formation, their persistent elevation during remodeling leads to bone resorption outpacing bone deposition, ultimately jeopardizing the restoration of bone mass and strength [139, 140]. These chemokines attract immune cells and osteoclast precursors to the fracture site, facilitating debris clearance and the initiation of remodeling. During this phase, they also help position osteoclast-lineage cells for localized resorption and may indirectly recruit mesenchymal progenitors via secondary paracrine signaling [1, 26]. However, sustained chemokine signaling prolongs inflammation and disrupts the finely regulated cellular crosstalk required for balanced bone turnover, predisposing the callus to chronic catabolic remodeling. At physiological levels, these mediators promote angiogenesis and the recruitment and differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into osteoblasts or chondrocytes, thereby coupling resorption and new bone formation [10, 17, 26]. In contrast, excessive TGF-β1 has an inhibitory effect on bone formation. It blocks osteoprogenitor maturation and skews remodeling toward fibrosis or non-union, as evidenced by the improved healing outcomes when TGF-β activity is reduced in aged models [10, 120]. These proteolytic enzymes degrade the provisional collagenous and cartilaginous matrix within the callus, an essential step that permits vascular invasion and enables osteoclast-osteoblast-mediated remodeling [26, 60]. For example, MMP-13, produced by hypertrophic chondrocytes and immature osteoblasts, is indispensable for normal remodeling; its absence leads to persistent calcified cartilage and aberrant bone architecture during repair [60]. However, unregulated protease activity could also cause collateral tissue damage. Pro-inflammatory cytokines give a short early boost to callus formation, but if they stay high during remodeling they drive receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)-dependent osteoclast activity and suppress osteoblasts, leading to net bone loss. Chemokines recruit immune cells and osteoclast precursors to resorption sites and then can draw in MSCs or osteoprogenitors, but persistent signals prolong inflammation and disrupt balanced remodeling. Growth factors support angiogenesis and osteogenesis and help couple resorption to formation at physiological, well-timed levels, whereas excess TGF-β1 blocks osteoprogenitor maturation and favors fibrosis or non-union. Matrix metalloproteinases remove the provisional collagenous and cartilaginous matrix to allow vessel ingrowth and coordinated osteoclast–osteoblast remodeling, but unchecked proteolysis risks collateral damage. However, deviations from this pattern can adversely affect healing outcomes.

SASP

In the remodeling phase, overt inflammation wanes, yet a small pool of long-lived senescent cells can sustain low-level SASP signaling that modulates turnover and, in aged settings, may impair recovery. Although inflammation has largely resolved, SASP-related signaling continues to exert subtle regulatory influence during this extended phase. Most transient senescent or inflammatory cells generated during earlier healing have been cleared; however, a small population of long-lived senescent cells—predominantly derived from resident bone cell lineages—persists within the bone microenvironment, where they may contribute to matrix turnover and tissue homeostasis [129, 137]. Although a certain degree of SASP is necessary for remodeling, persistent senescent cells during this phase can adversely affect bone mass and structural recovery. Studies have shown that in aged mice, senescent immune cells—particularly macrophages—persist within the callus and secrete granulocalcin (GCA), which induces senescence in skeletal stem/progenitor cells (SSPCs), thereby diminishing the regenerative potential of remodeling bone [137]. Thus, if left unchecked, SASP factors from persisting senescent cells can impair fracture healing. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (like IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), which are prominent SASP components, generally drive catabolic processes in remodeling: they enhance osteoclast activity and can simultaneously inhibit osteoblast-mediated bone formation. Chemokine-associated SASP factors, including CXCL8/IL-8 and CCL2, play critical roles in cell recruitment and spatial organization within the remodeling callus. Within the SASP growth factor network, TGF-β1 plays a central but dose- and context-dependent role, alongside PDGF, IGF-1, and VEGF. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)—particularly MMP-9 and MMP-13—are prominently upregulated by senescent and inflammatory cells during remodeling. Thus, their activity is normally kept in check by inhibitors (like TIMPs), which are part of the regulatory aspect of the SASP [26, 60]. In summary, a transient and well-regulated SASP during the remodeling phase promotes cell recruitment, matrix turnover, and scar-free bone regeneration. Conversely, when SASP factors exceed physiological thresholds or remain chronically elevated, the microenvironment becomes pro-inflammatory and proteolytic, disrupting the coupled balance of bone resorption and formation and ultimately impairing the integrity of skeletal regeneration. Overall, the SASP factors present during the remodeling stage can be classified based on their functions, and each factor plays a unique role in this stage. In conclusion, each stage of fracture healing presents different spatiotemporal patterns of SASP activity. During the inflammatory phase, injury-induced cytokines that overlap with canonical SASP signatures are concentrated in the fracture hematoma and periosteum to drive cell recruitment [46]. At the soft callus stage, SASP signals appear in the nascent cartilage and periosteal callus, where senescent progenitor cells regulate tissue formation [119]. During the hard callus stage, SASP expression peaks at the cartilage bone interface (vascular front) to promote matrix remodeling and angiogenesis [10]. Finally, the decrease in SASP expression throughout the callus contributes to remodeling [119]. This dynamic regulation of SASP in time and space seems to be a conventional mode of normal fracture healing. For example, overexpression or persistence of SASP in elderly or diseased conditions can affect fracture healing [10, 119]. By delineating the insights gained from the dynamic changes of SASP at various stages of fracture repair, this article aims to lay the foundation for potential therapeutic strategies, such as the timely use of therapies that clear senescent cells (Fig. 3).

Senolytic interventions in fracture healing: mechanistic insights, experimental evidence, and therapeutic perspectives

Given the spatiotemporal duality of SASP in fracture healing, strategies to selectively target its detrimental aspects while preserving beneficial functions have garnered significant interest. Accumulating evidence demonstrates the broad benefits of eliminating senescent cells, sparking a surge in research on therapeutic approaches to achieve this, collectively termed senotherapy [141]. Several drugs have been identified that can eliminate senescent cells. Senotherapeutic agents can be broadly divided into senolytics, which selectively eliminate senescent cells, and senomorphics, which suppress SASP signaling without necessarily reducing the senescent cell burden [18]. A variety of senolytic compounds have been developed, including both synthetic small molecules and natural products. These agents induce apoptosis of senescent cells by inhibiting the pro-survival pathways that senescent cells upregulate [142]. Some researchers have classified senolytics into three categories: Class I senolytics are B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2) family protein inhibitors (such as ABT-737 and navitoclax (ABT-263)), and their inhibitory effects finally lead to selective apoptosis of senescent cells [18, 143]; Class II senolytics can inhibit pro-survival signals upstream of senescent cells to resist cell death. For example, FOXO4–DRI acts by interfering with the binding of FOXO4 and p53 [144], dasatinib and quercetin act by downregulating protein kinase B (AKT) signaling [145], and the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor rapamycin acts by affecting NF-κB [146]; Class III senolytics (such as piperlongumine, GL-V9 and procyanidin C) can interfere with the intracellular homeostasis of senescent cells [147]. On the natural product side, flavonoids have attracted great interest for their senolytic activity. Quercetin—a plant-derived polyphenol—was one of the first senolytics identified, especially in combination with dasatinib and quercetin (D + Q) [145]. Quercetin on its own can eliminate certain senescent cell types (e.g., endothelial cells) and has mild osteoprotective effects, but its bioavailability is limited [145, 148]. A closely related flavonoid, fisetin, was later found to be an even more potent senolytic in preclinical tests. Intermittent high-dose fisetin reduced senescent cell markers in multiple tissues and significantly extended the healthspan and lifespan of aged mice [149] (Table 2).

Senolytic or senomorphic agents related to bone fracture healing and osteoporosis

The relationship between senolytic intervention and fracture healing remains an active area of investigation, with dose, timing, and duration of treatment yet to be standardized. At the cellular level, senolytics modulate bone repair primarily by targeting senescent osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and stromal progenitors that accumulate during aging. By eliminating these dysfunctional cells or attenuating their SASP output, senolytics can restore the anabolic–catabolic balance critical for effective remodeling. These cells—particularly senescent osteoclasts and mesenchymal stem cells—accumulate with aging and secrete high levels of injury-induced cytokines that overlap with canonical SASP signatures that impair osteogenesis and prolong inflammation. Clearance of these cells or attenuation of their SASP output enhances osteogenic differentiation, promotes matrix mineralization, and reduces inflammatory burden within the fracture microenvironment. This reprogramming of the cellular niche underlies the mechanistic rationale for applying senolytics to improve bone repair [10, 160, 170]. Moreover, studies treating aged mice with established bone loss using senolytics (dasatinib and quercetin) found a reduction in the number of senescent osteoclasts [152]. In animal models, preclinical studies in rodents have provided direct evidence linking senolytic treatment to improved bone regeneration. In aged mice with bone loss, treatment with D + Q significantly reduced the number of senescent osteoclasts and partially restored bone remodeling efficiency [160]. Interestingly, the response to senolytic therapy appears to be age-dependent. Intermittent D + Q administration in young fracture models downregulated senescence markers in callus tissue and promoted faster union [10]. However, when the same treatment was limited to short-term exposure (days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-fracture), accelerated healing was observed only in aged mice [120]. These results suggest that the timing, duration, and cellular composition of senescent populations within the callus critically determine therapeutic outcomes. Although no standardized clinical protocols currently exist for senolytic therapy in bone repair, ongoing translational research emphasizes both efficacy optimization and safety concerns. Synthetic senolytics such as navitoclax exhibit broad activity against anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family proteins, leading to hematologic toxicities including thrombocytopenia and neutropenia [160, 170] Moreover, excessive apoptosis of senescent cells may result in local tissue atrophy or delayed regeneration [171]. Although senolytic therapy is expected to promote fracture repair, it must be treated with caution. Senescent cells do play some beneficial roles (such as in fighting cancer and acute wound healing), so indiscriminate clearance may have drawbacks. Moreover, some senolytic drugs have known toxicities. A notable example is navitoclax, which not only causes thrombocytopenia in patients, but also adversely affects bone in animal studies [160, 172]. Short-term administration of navitoclax to 24-month-old mice resulted in approximately 50% reduction in trabecular bone volume and more than 80% reduction in osteoblast progenitor function, although senescent cells were cleared [160]. This finding underscores that not all senolytic agents are suitable for orthopedic applications—the ideal drug for orthopedics should effectively remove harmful senescent cells from the callus without damaging the stem and progenitor cells required for new bone formation. In this regard, senolytics like D + Q or fisetin which are given short term and have relatively mild transient side effects may be better suited than continuous BCL-2 inhibitors. Recent studies are examining optimized dosing schedules (to minimize side effects while maximizing senescent cell clearance) and exploring local delivery approaches (e.g., senolytic biomaterials) to confine the effects to the fracture site [137, 173].

Clearing senescent cells has been shown to delay or prevent osteoporosis in animal models [161], a condition that is traditionally assessed in clinical practice using bone mineral density together with panels of bone turnover biomarkers of bone formation and resorption [174]. In estrogen-deficient rodents, intermittent D + Q therapy preserved bone microarchitecture and prevented postmenopausal bone loss [175]. ABT-263 similarly reversed osteoporosis in vitamin D–insufficient mice by selectively eliminating senescent bone marrow cells and enhancing bone formation [159]. The natural senolytic fisetin significantly attenuated bone loss in a progeroid mouse model, whereas D + Q had minimal effect in that model [153]. These findings show that senolytic treatments can mitigate age-related bone loss and improve bone density in preclinical models. Senolytics work by inducing apoptosis in senescent cells and reducing their pro-inflammatory SASP signals that hinder new bone formation. For example, ABT-263 triggers apoptosis in senescent mesenchymal stem cells (via Bcl-2 downregulation and Bax upregulation), which restores osteoblast-driven bone growth while suppressing resorption [159]. However, broad senolytics like navitoclax can harm healthy bone cells: a 2-week navitoclax course in aged mice caused approximately 50% trabecular bone loss and severely impaired osteoblast function [160]. By contrast, intermittent D + Q or fisetin regimens improved bone mass in old animals without evidence of bone toxicity [175]. Thus, senolytic therapy holds promise for osteoporosis and fracture healing; however, optimizing agent selection, dosing, and delivery remains crucial to maximize benefit and minimize risk.

Conclusion and future directions

Fracture healing is a dynamic and precisely orchestrated regenerative process involving complex interactions among immune, stromal, and skeletal cells. Recent research has elucidated the context-dependent, dual role of cellular senescence and the SASP in this process. Transient senescence facilitates early repair by promoting inflammation resolution, progenitor recruitment, and extracellular matrix remodeling, whereas sustained senescence leads to chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and delayed bone union. The spatiotemporal profiling of SASP components across the inflammatory, soft callus, hard callus, and remodeling phases reveals that the timing, localization, and composition of SASP factors determine whether senescence acts as a regenerative cue or a pathological barrier. Deciphering this temporal dichotomy is paramount for harnessing the beneficial functions of the SASP while mitigating its detrimental effects on bone repair.

At the cellular and molecular level, SASP regulates key processes including immune cell infiltration, angiogenesis, and matrix turnover. Single-cell and transcriptomic studies have shown that senescent mesenchymal progenitors, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes exhibit distinct SASP signatures that shape the fracture microenvironment. However, excessive or prolonged SASP signaling perturbs the osteogenic–osteoclastic balance and hinders remodeling. Preclinical data demonstrate that selective removal of senescent cells or modulation of SASP intensity can shorten fracture healing time and restore tissue homeostasis. Yet, these benefits are highly context-dependent—varying with age, injury site, and senescent cell subtype—highlighting the need for stage-specific senescence modulation rather than global ablation.

Collagen composition and turnover are tightly stage-coupled. Early granulation tissue is enriched in type III collagen, providing a compliant provisional matrix that is subsequently remodeled into type I collagen-rich woven bone. During the soft callus stage, chondrocytes synthesize type II collagen as the principal cartilaginous scaffold, whereas hypertrophic chondrocytes express type X collagen to promote cartilage mineralization and vascular invasion during endochondral transition [69–72]. Type VI collagen forms a pericellular network that supports cell–matrix anchorage and mechanotransduction during callus maturation and remodeling [105–107].

Translational relevance and limitations

Most causal evidence for stage-specific senescent-cell dynamics and SASP-like programs in fracture healing derives from genetically tractable murine models and experimental perturbations. In humans, available data are comparatively limited and often observational, and direct cell-type-resolved mapping of senescent populations and their secretory programs across healing stages remains incomplete. Therefore, extrapolation to patients should explicitly consider interspecies differences in healing dynamics and immune responses, as well as the higher and more heterogeneous baseline senescence burden in aged or comorbid settings. These constraints underscore the need for well-powered human tissue studies, harmonized biomarkers, and carefully timed local delivery strategies when evaluating senotherapeutic approaches in orthopedic contexts.

From a translational perspective, senotherapeutic approaches, including senolytics and senomorphics, have emerged as promising strategies to rejuvenate skeletal repair. Pharmacological elimination of senescent cells using dasatinib and quercetin accelerates callus formation and improves bone strength, particularly in aged models, while excessive senolytic activity or improper dosing (e.g., navitoclax) may cause hematologic toxicity and bone loss. The development of natural or biomimetic senotherapeutics—such as plant-derived flavonoids, procyanidins, and other low-toxicity compounds—offers a promising strategy to modulate the SASP without disrupting its essential, transient functions during repair. Furthermore, integrating senotherapy with biomaterials, stem cell therapy, or localized delivery systems may enhance drug precision and minimize systemic side effects.

Evidence strength varies by model system. Most mechanistic insights into senescent cell sources and causal SASP functions derive from genetically tractable rodent fracture models and single-cell/spatial-omics datasets. In humans, available evidence is primarily observational but supports an early inflammatory and hypoxic fracture hematoma enriched in cytokines/growth factors and osteogenic signals [36, 91], and highlights dynamic systemic biomarker signatures associated with healing outcomes and non-union risk [32]. Direct, cell-type-resolved mapping of senescent populations and stage-specific SASP programs in clinical callus samples remains limited, underscoring the need for paired multi-omics analyses of human specimens.

Given that fragility fractures frequently co-occur with frailty and sarcopenia, extra-skeletal senescence and muscle–bone crosstalk may influence the fracture niche. Muscle-derived stromal cells can contribute to early repair in a TNF-α-dependent manner [96]. Aging skeletal muscle accumulates senescent stromal populations with chemokine-rich secretomes (e.g., CCL2/SPP1) that recruit and polarize macrophages [176], raising the possibility that muscle-derived SASP signals could reinforce inflammatory circuits in the hematoma and delay the inflammatory-to-reparative transition. Senolytic regimens (e.g., dasatinib + quercetin) can reduce senescent cell burden and improve physical function/frailty phenotypes in aged mice and human tissue explants [177, 178], and sarcopenia is epidemiologically associated with higher fall and fracture risk in older adults [179]. These observations motivate future fracture studies that incorporate frailty/sarcopenia endpoints to test whether systemic senotherapy benefits the integrated muscle–bone unit.

Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular networks that govern SASP heterogeneity across healing stages and identifying biomarkers that reflect senescence dynamics in vivo. Multi-omics and spatial transcriptomic approaches are instrumental for mapping senescent cell lineages and signaling trajectories within the fracture callus. Moreover, standardized animal models and robust clinical trial frameworks are critical for translating senescence-targeted strategies into orthopedic practice. Ultimately, precise regulation of SASP—preserving its transient regenerative functions while preventing its chronic persistence—may open new avenues for accelerating fracture healing, combating age-related bone degeneration, and advancing personalized regenerative medicine.
