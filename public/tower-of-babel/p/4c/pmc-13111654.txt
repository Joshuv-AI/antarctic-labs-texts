The conserved human astrovirus-receptor interface reveals a targetable vulnerability for antiviral development
Authors: Wei Wang, Ying Xu, Zehou Li, De Liu, Ke Ma, Rui Wang, Rong Chen, Bo Shu, Xiaoman Sun, Yue Liu, Xianliang Ke, Shuijun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human astrovirus (HAstV) is a major cause of viral enteritis in children and the older adults worldwide. The eight serotypes of classical HAstVs (HAstV1-8), employ the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) as an entry receptor. To elucidate the receptor binding mechanism of HAstVs, we determined the crystal structure of the HAstV8 spike protein complexed with human FcRn. The structure reveals that the HAstV8 spike protein engaged FcRn via a conserved surface depression, which is also present across the other seven classical HAstVs serotypes. The binding interface of HAstV8 spike protein on FcRn largely overlaps with the footprints of three clinically approved FcRn blockers to treat an autoimmune disease. Accordingly, these FcRn inhibitors potently suppress astrovirus infection, significantly reducing viral RNA levels in astrovirus permissive Caco2 cells. Therefore, our study reports a conserved receptor recognition mechanism among human astroviruses and suggests the potential of repurposing clinically approved therapeutics to treat astrovirus infection.

Introduction

Human astrovirus (HAstV) is a significant cause of acute diarrhea, accounting for up to 10% of non-bacterial gastroenteritis worldwide, particularly affecting young children, the elderly and immunocompromised individuals1–3. It is considered as the third most common viral agent of acute diarrhea, following rotavirus and norovirus4. Classical HAstVs include eight serotypes, HAstV1 through HAstV8, with HAstV1 being the most frequently detected serotype globally2. There are also two groups of non-classical HAstVs, Virginia/human-mink-ovine (VA/HMO) and Melbourne (MLB), both of which are unexpectedly associated with extra-enteric infections2. HAstV VA/HMO and HAstV MLB have been reported to spread to the central nervous system, leading to severe outcomes such as fatal encephalitis and meningitis in immunocompromised patients4–7. Elucidation of the cell entry mechanisms of HAstVs would provide critical insights into the tissue tropism and pathogenesis of these viruses. Currently, there is a lack of vaccines or antiviral drugs for the prevention or treatment of HAstV infections.

HAstVs belong to the Astroviridae, a family of non-enveloped viruses with a single-stranded positive sense RNA8. The capsid of immature HAstV particles exhibits T = 3 icosahedral symmetry, composed of 180 copies of the capsid protein precursor (VP90). The mature capsid consists of the inner core protein (VP34) and the outer spike protein (VP27), both of which are derived from proteolytic processing of VP90 during the viral maturation process9–12 (Fig. 1a). The cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of immature and mature HAstV particles, both determined at a resolution of ~25 Å, reveal that the spike protein dimers are displayed as globular protrusions on the viral surface13. Recently, three studies have independently identified the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) as a cellular receptor for classical HAstVs15–17. FcRn, initially discovered for its role in transfer of maternal IgG from milk to neonatal intestines, indeed functions throughout life as a receptor responsible for IgG transport across epithelial cells and protects circulating IgG from degradation18. Therefore, FcRn is also a therapeutic target for IgG-mediated autoimmune disease. Three FcRn blockers, which disrupt the interaction between IgG and FcRn, have been approved by the US FDA for the treatment of myasthenia gravis, a chronic autoimmune disease that causes weakness in the voluntary muscles18–21. In addition to HAstVs, FcRn also serves as an uncoating receptor for echoviruses, which belong to Enterovirus genus in the Picornaviridae family22. Therefore, FcRn blockage could also represent a broad-spectrum antiviral strategy.

When HAstV infects a host cell, the virus is internalized via clathrin mediated endocytosis following receptor binding on the cell surface22. Subsequently, HAstV is delivered to late endosomes, where the virus uncoats and releases its genome into cytosol17,22. As a receptor for HAstVs, FcRn has been shown to directly bind to the viral spike proteins and facilitates subsequent virus internalization and genome release15–17. Consistently, knockout of FcRn in HAstV susceptible cell lines or human enteroids significantly inhibits virus infection15,16. Although FcRn is crucial for HAstVs entry, the precise mechanism by which HAstV interacts with FcRn remains unknown. Deciphering this interaction would provide a more complete understanding of the astrovirus entry process and facilitate antiviral development.

To elucidate the molecular basis of HAstV binding to FcRn, we determined here the crystal structure of FcRn in complex with HAstV8 spike protein. The structure showed that HAstV8 spike protein binds to the α2 domain of FcRn via a depression formed by a four-stranded β sheet. This depression is conserved across classical HAstV serotypes, indicating that these viruses likely share a common mechanism for binding the FcRn receptor. We also found that the footprint of HAstV8 spike on FcRn largely overlaps with those of three approved FcRn blockers for the treatment of myasthenia gravis. Consistently, two of these FcRn blockers potently interfere with the interaction between HAstV8 spike protein and FcRn and inhibited astrovirus infection, suggesting that these drugs could potentially be repurposed to treat HAstV infection via receptor blocking. Therefore, we find a potential druggable target on HAstV-receptor binding interface.

Results

Overall structure of HAstV8 spike-FcRn complex

HAstVs bind to the FcRn receptor via the viral capsid spike (Fig. 1a). Therefore, to characterize the mechanism of receptor recognition by HAstVs, we solved the crystal structures of HAstV8 spike protein in complex with FcRn, as well as the structures of HAstV1 and HAstV8 spike proteins in their apo forms. HAstV1 spike (residues 429-645) and HAstV8 spike (residues 475-706) were expressed in E. coli, while FcRn heavy chain (residues 24-297) and light chain (β2-microglobulin, β2m) (residues 21-110) were co-expressed in Hi5 insect cells. The recombinant proteins were purified by Ni-NTA affinity purification and gel filtration. The peak corresponding to HAstV8 spike-FcRn complex from gel filtration (Supplementary Fig. 1a) was pooled and used for protein crystallization trials. The crystal structures of HAstV8 spike-FcRn complex, HAstV8 spike and HAstV1 spike were determined to resolutions of 2.65 Å, 1.44 Å and 1.35 Å, respectively (Fig. 1b–d and Supplementary Table 1). There was one HAstV8 spike-FcRn complex in the asymmetric unit of the complex structure. However, crystal packing revealed that one heterocomplex formed a dimer with its twofold crystal symmetry-related counterpart (Fig. 1b). This is in agreement with our gel filtration chromatography result, which demonstrated that HAstV8 spike-FcRn predominantly existed as dimers of approximately 160 kDa in solution (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

HAstV8 spike adopted a three-layered β-sandwich fold (Fig. 1b, c), with four β strands (β2, β3, β5 and β6) directly engaging the α2 domain of FcRn (Fig. 1b). Superimposition of the structures of receptor-bound and receptor-free HAstV8 spikes showed a high degree of similarity, with a root mean square deviation (RMSD) value of 0.29 Å for 214 aligned Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. 1b), indicating receptor binding did not induce significant conformational changes on HAstV8 spike. The structure of HAstV1 spike also shared a high similarity with that of HAstV8 spike, with an RMSD of only 0.54 Å over 214 equivalent Cα pairs (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1c). This suggested that HAstV1 probably bound to FcRn receptor in a similar way. Since the HAstV8 spike-FcRn binding interface was positioned opposite to the spike protein dimer interface (Fig. 1b), FcRn engagement did not disrupt the dimeric assembly of spike protein, as demonstrated by both our work and previous studies9,14,23 (Supplementary Fig. 1b–e). FcRn, like class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC-I), consisted of a heavy chain and a β2m light chain18,24. The heavy chain comprised three domains, α1, α2 and α3 domains, with α3 domain interacting with the β2m (Fig. 1b). α1 and α2 domains together formed an eight-stranded antiparallel β sheet topped by two α helices (Fig. 1b). β6-β8 strands and the second helix (α2 helix) in the α2 domain were involved in binding to the spike of HAstV8 (Fig. 1b).

The HAstV8 spike-FcRn binding interface

The surface area buried at the interface of HAstV8 spike protein and FcRn was 1,079.9 Å2 (539.6 Å2 on spike and 540.3 Å2 on FcRn), which was relatively smaller than that of echovirus 6-FcRn complex (1,188.5 Å2 on viral VP1/VP2/VP3 and 1,196.2 Å2 on FcRn)22. The β6-β8 strands and α2 helix of the FcRn α2 domain were inserted into a depression formed by two β-hairpins (β2-β3 and β5-β6) on the HAstV8 spike surface (Fig. 2a). The residues located on the HAstV8 spike-FcRn interface were identified using an inter-atomic distance cutoff of 4.0 Å. Since the HAstV8 spike residues interacting with FcRn were exclusively localized to the β2-β3 and β5-β6 hairpins, we divided the binding interface into two distinct sites based on the two hairpins (site I and II, Fig. 2a, b). In site I, L532 and Y535 of HAstV8 were hydrogen bonded to E156 and E138 of FcRn, respectively (Fig. 2b, c and Supplementary Table 2). In site II, D153 of FcRn formed multiple hydrogen bonds / salt bridges with R569, N572 and K574 of HAstV8 (Fig. 2b, d and Supplementary Table 2). FcRn W154, located between the two sites, packed into a pocket formed by HAstV8 spike residues D531, L532, R569 and N572 (Fig. 2a, b, d and Supplementary Table 3). Sequence alignment of the spike proteins from classical astroviruses revealed that six out of the nine FcRn-interacting residues were either conserved or exhibited similar chemical properties over half of the classical HAstV serotypes, with Y535 and K574 being strictly conserved. Previous studies proposed three potential receptor binding sites (P site, S site and a β-turn) on HAstV spike proteins9,14. However, we found that the FcRn binding regions on HAstV8 spike only marginally overlapped with the S site (Supplementary Fig. 2a). As a shared receptor for both HAstV8 and echovirus 6, FcRn bound to a “canyon” formed by VP1/VP2/VP3 proteins of echovirus 622. However, FcRn utilizes different regions to interact with these two viruses. FcRn engaged HAstV8 spike via its lateral surface, whereas it primarily utilized the top surface to bind echovirus 6 VP1/VP2/VP3 proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d). We then assessed the impact of mutations of the aforementioned interface residues on the interaction between HAstV8 and FcRn using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays. The spike proteins of wild-type HAstV8, HAstV1 and HAstV2 bound to FcRn with comparable affinities (651 nM, 793 nM and 324 nM, respectively) (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3a–c), which were also close to those reported in previous studies15,17,25. The L532A, Y535A, R569A and N572D mutations in HAstV8 spike, lowered binding affinities to FcRn by 6-13 fold. Specifically, the L532A mutation likely disrupts the van der Waals contacts formed by the side chain of L532 with residues L135, E138, F140, W154 and E156 on FcRn (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Table 3). Consistent with our finding, a recent study demonstrated that the corresponding T474A mutation in the HAstV6 spike protein nearly abolished its binding to FcRn25. While R569 in HAstV8 contributes substantially to FcRn binding, this residue is not conserved across other HAstV serotypes and is instead replaced by L, M or Y (Fig. 2e). Based on recently solved structures of HAstV1 spike-FcRn and HAstV6 spike-FcRn complexes, the leucine or methionine residue at this position maintains hydrophobic stacking with FcRn residue W15425,26. Similarly, in our HAstV8 spike-FcRn complex structure, the apolar moiety of R569 also packs against the hydrophobic side chain of W154. Therefore, although the hydrogen bonding between HAstV8 spike R569 and FcRn D153 is absent in other serotypes, the conserved hydrophobic packing between the corresponding viral residue and the FcRn W154 probably sustains virus receptor binding. In contrast, the K574A, D531S and D531S/N572D mutations only marginally affected binding (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3d–k). The effect of D531S/N572D mutation was inconsistent with a previous study, which reported that this dual mutant completely abolished binding17. Reciprocally, single FcRn mutations (E138A, D153A, W154A and E156A) did not significantly alter binding affinities (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 3l–o), suggesting that these residues might contribute collectively to binding. The FcRn-spike binding interface as a potential drug target

Since HAstV spike protein is crucial for virus entry into cells, it is also the important target of neutralizing antibodies. Multiple neutralizing antibodies against different HAstV serotypes have been identified, including 2D9 and 3E8 for HAstV827, 4B6 and PL2 for HAstV228,29, 3B4 and 3H4 for HAstV128, which all bind to HAstV spike proteins. Structural analysis revealed that these antibodies bound to sites that partially overlapped with or near the region on viral spike that is engaged by the FcRn receptor (Supplementary Fig. 4a–g). Therefore, these antibodies (except for 3B4) would sterically hinder FcRn-spike protein interactions (Supplementary Fig. 4h–m), suggesting they probably neutralized HAstV infection by blocking virus attachment to the cellular receptor.

Antibodies like 3E8 and 3H4, whose footprints on HAstV spikes partially overlap with that of FcRn (Supplementary Fig. 4b, d), have been shown to effectively inhibit HAstV-FcRn interaction17. Similarly, FcRn binders that occupy regions overlapping with HAstV spike binding sites on FcRn would be expected to impede viral spike-FcRn interaction through steric hindrance, potentially inhibiting HAstV infection as well. FcRn, best known for its role in IgG transport, has been extensively explored as a therapeutic target for IgG-mediated autoimmune diseases. Several FcRn antagonists, including efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab and nipocalimab were clinically approved for the treatment of myasthenia gravis19–21. These inhibitors disrupt FcRn-IgG binding and reduce the levels of circulating pathogenic IgG, thus alleviating the symptoms of autoimmune diseases18–21,30–32. Efgartigimod is an IgG1-derived Fc fragment mimicking the natural ligand of FcRn, while rozanolixizumab and nipocalimab are anti-FcRn monoclonal antibodies19–21. Unexpectedly, the binding sites of these three FcRn blockers on FcRn largely overlapped with that of HAstV8 spike protein (Fig. 3a–d). We then performed ELISA assays to test whether these FcRn blockers could inhibit astrovirus binding to FcRn. The scFv of rozanolixizumab and nipocalimab effectively inhibited the binding of HAstV1 virus or HAstV8 spike protein to FcRn, while efgartigimod had much weaker inhibition effects on virus-receptor binding (Fig. 3e, f). We further evaluated whether soluble FcRn protein and FcRn blockers could inhibit HAstV infection. Pre-treatment of HAstV1 with FcRn protein reduced viral binding to Caco2 cells by 30%-40% (Fig. 3g). In line with the competitive ELISA binding result, pre-incubation of Caco2 cells with FcRn blockers prior to HAstV1 infection, including rozanolixizumab (scFv/IgG), nipocalimab (scFv/IgG) and the engineered human IgG1 Fc fragment (efgartigimod), significantly decreased viral loads in Caco2 cells. The IgG forms of rozanolixizumab and nipocalimab showed the strongest inhibitory effect, which reduced intracellular viral levels by approximately 90% (Fig. 3h). Consistently, a recent study revealed that nipocalimab inhibited HAstV infection of susceptible Caco2 cell lines or human intestinal enteroids15. Discussion

In this study, we presented the crystal structure of HAstV8 spike protein in complex with FcRn, the cellular receptor for classical HAstVs. FcRn binds to a concave depression on the HAstV8 spike surface (Fig. 2a), reminiscent of its interaction with a “canyon” on the surface of echovirus 622 (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Using previously solved cryo-EM structures of both immature and trypsin-digested mature HAstVs13, we modeled FcRn binding to the viral surface, which demonstrated no clashes between the receptor and neighbouring viral spikes (Fig. 4a, b). This is consistent with our ELISA assay and with a recent study showing that FcRn efficiently binds HAstV4 regardless of trypsin-induced viral maturation16. While FcRn binding triggers echovirus 6 uncoating under acidic conditions22, whether astrovirus similarly undergoes subsequent uncoating and genome release upon FcRn binding remains to be investigated. In addition to FcRn, gene knockout and antibody blocking experiments revealed that dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4), the functional receptor for Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS-CoV), was required for HAstV entry into cells15,17. However, we did not detect direct binding between DPP4 and HAstV1/HAstV8 spike proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), consistent with prior findings15,17. Although the spike proteins of classical HAstVs exhibit much lower sequence conservation compared to the capsid core10, their structures are highly conserved. Despite sharing only ~50% sequence identity, the structures of HAstV1, HAstV2 and HAstV8 spikes display close resemblance, with RMSD values of ~0.5 Å9,14,23. Structural comparisons further demonstrate that similar depressions where FcRn binds are present on all three spike proteins (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). In addition, the electrostatic potential distribution around the corresponding receptor-binding regions also shows a similar pattern among these spike proteins (Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). Therefore, the conserved structural features strongly suggest that classical HAstVs employ FcRn as a common cellular receptor. This was supported by our SPR assay, which showed comparable binding affinities between FcRn and the spike proteins of HAstV1, HAstV2 and HAstV8 (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). During the preparation of this manuscript, two studies reported the crystal structures of FcRn in complex with the spike proteins of HAstV1, HAstV2 and HAstV625,26. Although the overall structure of HAstV8 spike-FcRn complex resembles recently published structures of FcRn bound to spike proteins from other HAstV serotypes, notable differences exist at the virus-receptor binding interface. Key viral residues involved in receptor binding are not fully conserved across serotypes (Fig. 2e). For instance, residue R569 in the HAstV8 spike protein, which is critical for FcRn binding, is substituted to L, M or Y in other HAstV serotypes. This substitution would disrupt its hydrogen bonding with FcRn D153. Such variations likely contribute to serotype specific binding affinities25. Our work systematically evaluated the antiviral potential of all three approved FcRn blockers-efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab (scFv/IgG) and nipocalimab (scFv/IgG) from multiple perspectives: competitive binding with viral spike protein, blockage of virus attachment and inhibition of virus replication, while the two related structural studies focused primarily on nipocalimab IgG. Given the global circulation of diverse HAstV serotypes, elucidating the FcRn binding interfaces across multiple serotypes offers valuable insights for the development of both serotype-specific and broad-spectrum anti-astrovirus strategies. In contrast, the spike proteins of non-classical HAstV-VA1 and HAstV-MLB1 share only 12.1% and 11.5% sequence identity with that of HAstV8, respectively. Additionally, the conserved FcRn-binding depression present in classical HAstVs has not been observed in HAstV-VA1 or HAstV-MLB133,34 (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e), suggesting these two non-classical HAstVs may not be able to use FcRn as an entry receptor. Nevertheless, we could not rule out the possibility that HAstV-VA1 or HAstV-MLB1 engages FcRn via alternative regions on the spike protein-a mechanism demonstrated in phylogenetically divergent α and δ coronaviruses, which employ structurally distinct different receptor binding domains within their spike proteins to interact with the same host receptor35–38.

The binding regions of HAstV spike protein on FcRn almost completely overlap with the FcRn-IgG interaction interface39. In line with this structural observation, our ELISA result showed two of the three approved FcRn inhibitors designed to block FcRn-IgG interaction potently inhibited the binding of HAstV to FcRn (Fig. 3f). Unexpectedly, although efgartigimod has the best structural overlap with the HAstV8 binding footprint on FcRn among the three FcRn blockers, it exhibited the weakest competition with the spike protein for FcRn binding. We propose that this discrepancy likely arises from the low binding affinity between efgartigimod and FcRn at neutral pH (pH 7.4). In contrast, the other two antibodies, rozanolixizumab and nipocalimab, bind FcRn with high affinity in a pH-independent manner31,32. As an IgG transport receptor, FcRn is well known to bind the Fc portion of IgG in a pH-dependent fashion, displaying high affinity in acidic endosomes (pH <6.5), and markedly reduced affinity at neutral or basic pH to facilitate IgG release at the cell surface30. This canonical pH-sensitive binding profile is retained by efgartigimod, an engineered human IgG1 Fc fragment optimized for FcRn engagement, which still shows approximately 40-fold stronger binding at pH 6.0 than at pH 7.4 (33 nM vs 1220 nM)30. Correspondingly, our ELISA data confirm the pH-dependent interaction between efgartigimod and FcRn across a pH range of 6.0 to 7.4 (Supplementary Fig. 7a). In contrast, both rozanolixizumab and nipocalimab bind FcRn via their Fab regions, and their binding affinity remains largely pH-independent31,32. To further investigate this pH dependency, we performed competitive ELISA assays under three additional pH conditions (6.0, 6.5 and 7.0), alongside the originally reported pH 7.4. While efgartigimod exhibited stronger binding to FcRn at lower pH, we did not observe a comparably enhanced competitive inhibition against HAstV8 spike under the same acidic pH conditions (Supplementary Fig. 7b). This result is likely attributable to a concomitant increase in the binding affinity between HAstV8 spike and FcRn at lower pH (Supplementary Fig. 7c). Such pH-dependent binding of HAstV spike protein to FcRn has been similarly reported in recent studies25,26. Consistent with the competitive ELISA results, efgartigimod also demonstrated significantly weaker inhibition of HAstV infection in Caco2 cells than rozanolixizumab and nipocalimab when cells were pre-incubated with these FcRn blockers (Fig. 3h). On the one hand, this finding suggests the possibility of repurposing FcRn blockers for treating HAstV infection. On the other hand, HAstV infection may interfere with IgG transport, including transplacental transfer from maternal bloodstream to the fetus and intestinal absorption from breast milk to newborns. While FcRn interacts with IgG in a pH dependent manner in which these two proteins binds at low pH but dissociates at neutral pH40,41, binding between FcRn and HAstV spike could be detected at neutral pH conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). However, whether pH affects FcRn interaction with intact HAstV virions and subsequently induces viral uncoating needs to be further investigated.

In summary, our study elucidates the structural basis of human astrovirus receptor recognition. The shared binding interface between the astrovirus spike protein and clinically approved FcRn inhibitors for autoimmune disease on FcRn presents a promising target for anti-astrovirus drug development.

Methods

Cell lines and viruses

Hi5 and Sf9 insect cells were maintained in the SIM HF medium and SIM SF medium (Sino Biological Inc., Beijing, China) at 27 °C. Caco2 cells were maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM Earle’s, Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, PAN). The fecal samples containing HAstV1 and HAstV8 were kept in the department of viral diarrhea from National Institute for Viral Disease Control and Prevention, Chinese CDC. The fecal samples were diluted with PBS, vortexed, and centrifuged at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected as the first-generation wild-type virus (P1). Viral P1 RNA was extracted and typed by Reverse Transcription PCR (RT-PCR) using virus-specific primers (AV177-F: TCCTGTRCTAYCAGTTGCT; AV180-R: TCCTGTRCTAYCAGTTGCT). For HAstV1 propagation, Caco2 cells in T25 flasks were inoculated with virus P1 that had been pre-activated with trypsin (125 μg/mL, 37 °C, 1.5 h). Trypsin was then inactivated by FBS. Following virus adsorption (1.5 h), the inoculum was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Subsequently, the cells were maintained in serum-free MEM medium supplemented with 10 µg/mL trypsin for 96 h. The infected cells and culture medium were subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles to release the virus, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected as the second-generation virus (P2). The virus was passaged further using the same method. Viral gene copy number was quantified using a standard curve generated from a constructed DNA plasmid. HAstV1 P6 was used in both the subsequent virus-binding and FcRn blockade assays.

Protein expression and purification

The spike proteins of HAstV1 (residues 429-645, GenBank accession number: AAC34717.1), HAstV2 (residues 429-644, GenBank accession number: AAA62427.1) and HAstV8 (residues 475-706, GenBank accession number: AAF85964.1) were expressed in E.coli. Briefly, the genes encoding HAstV1 and HAstV2 spike were cloned into the pET-52b (Beijing Tsingke Biotech) vector with a C-terminal 10×His tag, while the HAstV8 spike gene was cloned into the pET-28a vector (Beijing Tsingke Biotech) with a C-terminal 6×His tag. The resulting plasmids were transformed into E.coli strain BL21(DE3) for expression. After cell lysis by sonication, the proteins were then purified to homogeneity using Ni-NTA affinity (HisTrapTM FF, Cytiva) chromatography and gel filtration (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column, Cytiva). FcRn and human DPP4 (hDPP4, residues 39-766, GenBank accession number: NP_001926) were expressed in Hi5 insect cells using the Bac-to-Bac baculovirus expression system (Invitrogen). Briefly, the genes encoding FcRn heavy chain and β2m light chain were cloned into the pACEBac1 (Invitrogen) vector at the Pst I and Xho I restriction sites while hDPP4 was cloned into the pFastBac1 (Invitrogen) vector at the Pst I and EcoR I restriction sites. Both FcRn and hDPP4 constructs contained an N-terminal gp67 signal peptide and a C-terminal 8×His tag. Transfection and virus amplification were performed in Sf9 cells, and recombinant proteins were expressed in Hi5 cells. The target proteins secreted in the Hi5 cell culture supernatants, were purified similarly as HAstV spike proteins.

Protein crystallization

HAstV8 spike protein complexed with FcRn was concentrated to a concentration of 3 mg/mL. The protein complex was crystallized by the sitting-drop vapor diffusion method at 18 °C with 1 μL of protein solution mixed with 1 μL of reservoir buffer. The crystal of the HAstV8 spike and FcRn complex was obtained in 0.1 M BICINE pH 8.5, 8% w/v Polyethylene glycol monomethyl ether 5000. The individual spike proteins of HAstV1 and HAstV8 were crystallized similarly. HAstV1 spike protein (3 mg/mL) crystals were obtained in 0.2 M Lithium sulfate, 0.1 M Sodium acetate, pH 4.5, 50% w/v PEG 400, while HAstV8 spike (3 mg/mL) was crystallized in 0.1 M HEPES pH 7.5, 30% w/v Polyethylene glycol 1000.

Data collection and structure determination

Crystals were cryoprotected in reservoir solution containing 20% (v/v) glycerol before flash freezing in liquid nitrogen. The diffraction data were collected at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility beamline BL02U1 and BL18U42. All data were indexed, integrated and scaled with XDS43. The structures were solved by molecular replacement in Phaser program44, using HAstV8 spike (PDB code: 3QSQ) and FcRn (PDB code: 6C98) as the search models. Further rounds of iterative model building and refinement were performed using phenix.refine45 and COOT46, respectively. The stereochemical quality of the final model was assessed with the Molprobity47. Data collection, processing, and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Buried surface areas of different complexes were calculated by the PISA program48. The structural figures were generated using PyMOL (http://www.pymol.org).

Surface plasmon resonance assay

The SPR assays were carried by BIAcore T200 system at room temperature (25 °C). FcRn wild-type or mutant proteins were immobilized on the CM5 sensor chips (Cytiva). Serially diluted wild-type or mutant HAstV1/HAstV2/HAstV8 spike proteins dissolved in HEPES buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% (v/v) Tween-20) were flowed over the chip surface. After each cycle, the sensor surface was regenerated using 15 mM NaOH. The binding kinetics were analyzed with the BIAevaluation software (Version 4.1) using the 1:1 Langmuir binding model.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

HAstV1 virus or recombinant spike protein (diluted to 4 μg/mL, 100 μL/well) was coated on a 96-well plate. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sangon Biotech), FcRn either alone or pre-incubated with different inhibitors was added to the plate and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing unbound FcRn, the plates were sequentially incubated with primary anti-FcRn rabbit monoclonal IgG (Abcam, Cat: AB228975, lot: 1110453-11, 1:5000 dilution in PBST) for 1 h at 37 °C and then with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abmart, Cat: M21002-s, lot: 324176, 1:5000 dilution in PBST) for another 1 h at 37 °C. After each antibody incubation step, the plates were washed three times with PBST buffer. The binding signal was detected by adding a 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) chromogenic substrate, and the reaction was stopped with 2 M H2SO4. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using SpectraMax ® Absorbance Reader CMax Plus (Molecular Devices).

Neutralization assay

To evaluate the neutralizing effect of soluble FcRn on HAstV binding to cells, 200 μL of HAstV1 (7.74 × 104 copies/μL) was first treated with trypsin (125 μg/mL) at 37 °C for 1.5 h. The activated virus was then incubated with either PBS or FcRn (15 μg/mL or 30 μg/mL) at 4 °C for 1 h. The virus-FcRn mixtures were subsequently added to Caco2 cells pre-chilled on ice and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h to allow surface adsorption. After incubation, the supernatant was removed and the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove unbound virus. Total cellular RNA was extracted using TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TRAN). The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using HiScriptR II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit ( + gDNA wiper) (Vazyme). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using QiankunTM PlatinumTM SYBRTM Green Premix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the housekeeping gene to normalize samples. HAstV1-specific primers were used in RT-qPCR to quantify the viral RNA levels. Results were compared against the control group (without FcRn). Primer sequences for RT-qPCR were as follows: HAstV1-F: TGGAGGGGAGGACCAAAGAA; HAstV1-R: ACTGACTTATCTCGGCCCCT; GAPDH-F: GCACCGTCAAGGCTGAGAAC; GAPDH-R: TGGTGAAGACGCCAGTGGA.

FcRn blocking assay

To assess the inhibitory effect of FcRn blockers on viral infection, Caco2 cells were first pre-incubated with rozanolixizumab (scFv/IgG), nipocalimab (scFv/IgG) or an engineered human IgG1 Fc fragment (efgartigimod) at a final concentration of 50 μg/mL at 37 °C for 1 h. Trypsin activated HAstV1 was then added to the monolayer of pre-treated Caco2 cells and allowed to adsorb at 37 °C for 1 h. Following adsorption, the viral supernatant was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were then maintained in medium containing the same FcRn blocker (final concentration 50 μg/mL). Total cellular RNA was extracted at 24 hpi, and the viral load was quantified by RT-qPCR.
