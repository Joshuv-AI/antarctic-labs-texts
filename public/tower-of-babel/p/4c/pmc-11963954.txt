Lipids, Apolipoproteins, Lipid‐Lowering Drugs, and the Risk of Cerebral Small Vessel Disease: A Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Yi Xie, Shuai Liu, Xinyue Wang, Hao Huang, Minghuan Wang, Wensheng Qu, Zhiyuan Yu, Wei Wang, Xiang Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSerum lipids are causally involved in the occurrence of atherosclerosis, but their roles in cerebral small vessel disease remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the causal roles of lipid or apolipoprotein traits in cerebral small vessel disease and to determine the effects of lipid‐lowering interventions on this disease.Methods and ResultsData on genetic instruments of lipids/apolipoproteins, as well as characteristic cerebral small vessel disease manifestations, including small vessel stroke (SVS) and white matter hyperintensity (WMH), were obtained from publicly genome‐wide association studies. Through 2‐sample Mendelian randomization analyses, it was found that decreased levels of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (odds ratio [OR], 0.85, P=0.007) and apolipoprotein A‐I (OR, 0.83, P=0.005), as well as increased level of triglycerides (OR, 1.16, P=0.025) were associated with a higher risk of SVS. A low level of high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (OR, 0.93, P=0.032) was associated with larger WMH volume. Specifically, the genetically determined expressions of lipid fractions in various size‐defined lipoprotein particles were more closely related to the risk of SVS than WMH. Moreover, it was found that the hypertension trait ranked at the top in mediating the causal effect of hyperlipidemia on SVS and WMH by using Mendelian randomization‐based mediation analysis. For drug‐target Mendelian randomization, the low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol‐reducing genetic variation alleles at HMGCR and NL1CL1 genes and the high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol‐raising genetic variation alleles at the CETP gene were predicted to decrease the risk of SVS.ConclusionsThe present Mendelian randomization study indicates that genetically determined hyperlipidemia is closely associated with a higher risk of cerebral small vessel disease, especially SVS. Lipid‐lowering drugs could be potentially considered for the therapies and preventions of SVS rather than WMH.Keywords: cerebral small vessel disease, mediation analysis, Mendelian randomization, microvascular ischemic disease, serum lipids, small vessel stroke, white matter hyperintensitySubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke, Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke, Lipids and Cholesterol

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) represents a cluster of pathologies with heterogeneous pathogeneses affecting the brain vasculature, such as small arteries, arterioles, capillaries, and venules. 1 CSVD‐induced small vessel stroke (SVS) accounts for 25% of all strokes, and CSVD itself more than doubles the odds of recurrent stroke and contributes to 45% of dementia cases and to global functional decline. 2 , 3 The CSVD‐related brain parenchymal lesions, including white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, enlarged perivascular spaces, and brain atrophy can be observed with neuroimaging and serve as imaging markers for CSVD. Manifestations of CSVD on magnetic resonance imaging are highly prevalent in the aging population, with figures reaching 90% for WMH in individuals aged 65 years and above. 4 However, the pathogeneses underlying CSVD are poorly understood, impeding the development of effective strategies for disease prevention.

Serum lipids, including low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and triglyceride, are established causal factors in the development of stroke. 5 The amounts of cholesterol and triglyceride vary largely in different sized‐defined lipoprotein particles. 6 As for apolipoproteins, the level of apolipoprotein B (ApoB) is proportional to the number of circulating atherogenic lipoprotein particles in the blood, 7 and apolipoprotein A‐I (ApoA‐I) is a major constituent of high‐density lipoprotein. 8 Several common dyslipidemias are causally associated with ischemic stroke (IS) subtypes, particularly large artery atherosclerotic stroke. 9 It has been shown that high concentration of LDL‐C or triglyceride increases the risk of IS, 10 , 11 whereas high concentration of HDL‐C possibly decreases the risk of IS, particularly SVS. 10 At present, lipid‐lowering therapies have shown benefits in reducing the risk of both coronary artery disease and stroke. 12 , 13 However, most of the lipid‐related randomized clinical trials provided no subanalyses for IS subtypes according to TOAST (Trial of Org 10 172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) criteria. 14 , 15 Until now, the pathogenic role and therapeutic value of dyslipidemia in CSVD remain largely controversial and elusive.

Confined by methodological limitations, such as residual confounding and reverse causality, traditional observational study designs are unable to infer causality regarding the role of serum lipids in the development of stroke. These limitations may be overcome by the use of Mendelian randomization (MR), an epidemiological approach that assesses the causal effect of a risk factor on an outcome, replacing the metaphorical coin toss in the randomized trial. 16 Individuals randomly inherit gene variants that may affect risk factors at birth and are then tracked to determine whether an outcome of interest develops. 17 Recently, more derivative MR analyses have been developed, such as MR‐based mediation analysis and drug‐target MR, etc. MR‐based mediation analysis combines the advantages of MR and mediation analysis, which facilitates the identification of intermediaries. 18 In addition, MR can provide vital information for drug use, including predicting the efficacy and revealing target‐mediated adverse effects, which is also called drug‐target MR. 19 Drug‐target MR can show the impact of modifying biomarkers through specific therapeutic targets on long‐term health outcomes. 19

In the current study, we leveraged data from the largest and latest large‐scale genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) about the levels of serum lipids and various lipoprotein components, 20 to explore the causal effects of the aforementioned lipid traits on 2 characteristic manifestations of CSVD, namely, SVS 21 and WMH. 22 We aimed to (1) examine the genetically determined causal effects of serum lipid levels (LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglyceride, ApoB, and ApoA‐I) on different manifestations of CSVD; (2) explore the associations between genetic determinants of size‐defined lipoprotein particle fractions with these 2 CSVD manifestations; (3) identify the key intermediaries of causal associations between serum lipid traits and CSVD manifestations; and (4) determine the effects of genetic predisposition to LDL‐C lowering, HDL‐C raising, and triglyceride lowering through variants in genes encoding targets of lipid‐modifying drugs on CSVD manifestations.

METHODS

Study Design

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. This study followed the guidelines of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization. 23 This genetic association study used published summary data from studies involving human participants and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. The informed consent was not required in this MR study using a public database. All data and supporting materials in this study are publicly available and can be accessed from the GWAS data cited within the article.

An overview of the study design is shown in Figure 1, and the data sources are presented in Table S1. We performed 3 types of MR analysis. First, univariable MR analysis was conducted to determine the overall effect of different serum lipid or apolipoprotein traits on the outcome (different CSVD phenotypes, including SVS and WMH volume). Second, MR‐based mediation analysis was applied to explore significant mediators mediating the causal effect of serum lipid trait on the outcome. Finally, the effect of lipid‐lowering drug on the outcome was analyzed through drug‐target MR.

Overview of study design.

In this study, genetic variants associated with various serum lipid levels were identified based on results from their corresponding GWAS. These genetic variants were then used as proxies for the serum lipid levels to test their relationship with different CSVD outcomes (SVS and WMH volume) using data from the MEGASTROKE consortium and UK Biobank (univariable MR analysis). Then, MR‐based mediation analyses were performed for those CSVD risk factors (mediators) that were potentially causally associated with both serum lipid levels and CSVD outcomes. Ultimately, drug‐target MR analyses were conducted to determine the effects of genetic predisposition to LDL‐C lowering, HDL‐C raising, and TG lowering through variants in genes encoding targets of lipid‐modifying drugs on CSVD outcomes. GWAS Data Source

The publicly available GWAS summary statistics used in this study were extracted from corresponding authoritative consortiums. Summary statistical data for LDL‐C, HDL‐C, triglycerides, ApoA‐I, ApoB, and different lipid fractions in size‐defined lipoprotein particles were obtained from the UK Biobank. 20 The data for SVS defined according to the TOAST criteria 14 were obtained from the MEGASTROKE consortium. 21 The WMH volume data were generated from the image‐processing pipeline developed by the UK Biobank. 22 An overview of the data sources used is displayed in Table S1.

Genetic Instrument Selection

The following criteria were used to select appropriate genetic variants that preferably satisfy 3 core assumptions for MR analysis (Figure S1). Specifically, genetic variants, in this case single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs), associated with serum lipid levels were extracted as instrumental genetic variants at the genome‐wide significance level of P<5e‐8. SNVs that reached genome‐wide significance were clumped on the basis of a clumping window of 10 000 kb, and linkage disequilibrium r2<0.001. 24 F‐statistics for each SNV were calculated to quantify the strength of the instruments, and the weak ones were excluded (F‐statistics <10). Next, the SNVs were excluded if they were significantly (P<5e‐8) associated with the confounding factor hypertension (checked on the PhenoScanner website: www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk).

The instrumental variants at or near the HMGCR, NPC1L1, PCSK9, PPARA, APOB, LPL, LDLR, CETP, APOC3, and APOA5 regions were selected according to the reported methods. 25 , 26 , 27 All variants were chosen based on their associations with the relevant serum lipid trait (either LDL‐C, HDL‐C, or triglyceride), and they were not strongly correlated (r2<0.3, clumping window <100 kb).

MR Analysis

The instrumental variants identified were harmonized with the summary data of outcome to confirm the direction of estimates by effect alleles. The coefficients of SNV exposure and SNV outcome were combined using random‐effects inverse‐variance weighted (IVW) meta‐analyses to estimate the overall causal effect. 28 MR‐Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode tests were further performed to control for horizontal pleiotropy. 28 We also used Cochran's Q statistic and MR‐Egger test (intercept) to test for heterogeneity and pleiotropy, 27 respectively.

In drug‐target MR, estimates for the HMGCR, NPC1L1, PCSK9, PPARA, APOB, LPL, and LDLR gene regions were scaled to a 1‐SD increase in LDL‐C (mmol/L); estimates for the CETP, PPARA, and LPL gene regions were scaled to a 1‐SD increase in HDL‐C (mmol/L); and estimates for the HMGCR, PPARA, LPL, APOC3, and APOA5 gene regions were scaled to a 1‐SD increase in triglyceride (mmol/L). The IVW method was used to determine the effect of each drug target on SVS or WMH volume.

Mediation Analysis

MR‐based mediation analysis was carried out using a 2‐step approach 18 : (1) the effect of serum lipid on the candidate mediator (a) was estimated using univariable MR analysis; (2) the effect of candidate mediator on SVS or WMH (b), and the total effect of serum lipid on SVS or WMH (c) were estimated using univariable MR analysis; and (3) the indirect effect of serum lipid on SVS or WMH through the mediator was calculated by a×b, and the direct effect of serum lipid on SVS or WMH (c') was calculated by c‐a×b. The proportion of mediation (mediated percent) was calculated as the indirect effect divided by the total effect (a×b/c).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the “TwoSample MR” (version 0.5.6) and “MendelianRandomization” (version 0.5.1) packages in the statistical program R (version 4.1.1). The statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

RESULTS

Genetically Determined Causal Effects of Serum Lipid Levels on CSVD Manifestations

The primary results of the IVW MR analyses of the associations between genetic determinants of serum lipid levels (HDL‐C, LDL‐C, triglyceride, ApoA‐I, and ApoB) and SVS or WMH volume are presented in Figure 2 and Figures S2 and S3. Genetic predispositions to decreased HDL‐C level (odds ratio [OR], 0.85 [95% CI, 0.75–0.96], P=0.007), elevated triglyceride level (OR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.02–1.32], P=0.025), and decreased ApoA‐I level (OR, 0.83 [95% CI, 0.73–0.95], P=0.005) were associated with a higher risk of SVS. Among these 5 serum lipid traits, only genetic predisposition to lower HDL‐C level was found to be related to larger WMH volume (OR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.86–0.99], P=0.032). Moreover, genetic predisposition to elevated LDL‐C level was associated with a tendency toward a higher risk of SVS (OR, 1.07 [95% CI, 0.91–1.25], P=0.426) and larger WMH volume (OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 0.90–1.23], P=0.516) although without statistical significance.

The effect value based on the MR‐Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode tests was consistent with the IVW estimate (Figure 2 and Table S2). The Cochran's Q statistic was calculated to test for heterogeneity (Table S3). No horizontal pleiotropy (no significant difference between intercept and 0 in the MR‐Egger test) was detected (Table S3). Together, the results of MR analysis indicated the genetically determined causal effects of serum lipid levels on different CSVD outcomes.

Genetically Determined Causal Effects of Size‐Defined Lipoprotein Particle Fractions on CSVD Manifestations

To obtain a deeper understanding of the observed associations, we next selected genetic instruments for cholesterol, triglyceride, and total lipid concentrations in size‐defined lipoprotein particles and examined their associations with SVS or WMH volume. As shown in Figure 3 and Table S4, the genetic predisposition toward higher cholesterol level in very low‐density lipoprotein (VLDL) particles, including small and large VLDL particles, was associated with a higher risk of SVS. In addition, genetically determined lower cholesterol level and cholesterol ester level in HDL particles, including medium and large HDL particles, were also related to a higher risk of SVS. As for triglyceride, it was found that the genetic predisposition toward higher triglyceride level in intermediate‐density lipoprotein (IDL) and LDL particles (especially medium and large LDL particles) led to a higher risk of SVS. In outcome of WMH, lower cholesterol levels in small and medium HDL particles tended to be associated with larger WMH volume although not reaching statistical significance. Apart from lipoprotein particle traits, we also explored the genetically mediated causality between lipid metabolism‐related traits and outcomes. We found genetically determined lower levels of acetoacetate, acetone, and 3‐hydroxybutyrate (3‐Hb), as well as a higher level of lactate increased the risk of SVS. Sensitivity analysis demonstrated the robustness of the results, where similar causal effects with the IVW method were observed (Table S5). In brief, our univariable MR analyses further revealed genetically determined causal effects of size‐defined lipoprotein particle fractions on different CSVD outcomes, especially on SVS.

Intermediaries of Causal Associations Between Lipid Traits and CSVD Manifestations

Based on the results of the univariable MR analyses, we further explored and identified the intermediaries of causal associations between HDL‐C (or triglyceride) and SVS (or WMH volume) using MR‐based mediation analysis. In these situations, the pathways from lipid trait to the mediator and from the mediator to outcome should both be significant. We therefore examined the significance of the 2 pathways separately using MR analysis. Using MR‐based mediation analysis, 7 traits were identified as independent mediators for the association between HDL‐C and SVS, 3 traits were identified as independent mediators for the association between HDL‐C and WMH volume, and 16 traits were identified as independent mediators for the association between triglyceride and SVS (Tables 1 and 2; Table S6).

Gene‐Specific Analyses of Drug Proxy Variants on CSVD Manifestations

After determining the causal associations between serum lipids and SVS or WMH volume, we next selected genetic variants in genes encoding known LDL‐C‐lowering, HDL‐C‐raising, and triglyceride‐lowering drug targets and examined their associations with SVS and WMH. With respect to SVS, IVW‐MR analyses provided a suggestive association between HMGCR‐mediated or NPC1L1‐mediated LDL‐C level (equivalent to a 1 mmol/L increase) and the risk of SVS (OR, 2.43 [95% CI, 1.57–3.75], P=6e‐5; OR, 6.09 [95% CI, 2.13–17.44], P=0.001), respectively. Moreover, suggestive evidence was obtained for the association of CETP‐mediated HDL‐C level (equivalent to a 1 mmol/L increase) with the risk of SVS (OR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.81–0.99], P=0.036). No significant association was found between the expression of genes encoding known triglyceride‐lowering drug targets and SVS outcomes. With respect to WMH volume, the results of IVW‐MR analyses did not indicate any evidence for the association between the level of LDL‐C (or HDL‐C) mediated by some known drug‐target genes and the risk of WMH. However, the ApoA5‐ and ApoC3‐mediated triglyceride level (equivalent to a 1 mmol/L increase) was predicted to a larger WMH volume (OR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.06–1.25], P=0.0005; OR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.03–1.22], P=0.007), suggesting their potential therapeutic value. No horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity were detected in the drug‐target MR analysis (Tables S9 and S10). Collectively, the results of drug‐target MR analysis indicated that lipid‐lowering strategies such as LDL‐C‐lowering, triglyceride‐lowering, and HDL‐C raising treatments could be considered for the prevention of CSVD.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we analyzed the genetically determined causal effects of serum lipids on different CSVD manifestations by MR analysis. The key findings can be summarized as follows: (1) we found significant causal associations of genetic predisposition to lower HDL‐C level, lower ApoA‐1 level, and higher triglyceride level with a higher risk of SVS, as well as lower HDL‐C level with larger WMH volume; (2) the levels of cholesterol in VLDL and small/medium‐sized HDL, and the levels of triglyceride in IDL and large/medium‐sized LDL had notable causal effects on the risk of SVS; (3) hypertension mediated the genetically determined causal effects of key serum lipid traits on the risk of both SVS and WMH; and (4) we found LDL‐C‐lowering variants in the HMGCR and NPC1L1 loci and HDL‐C‐raising variants in the CETP locus to be associated with a lower risk of SVS, although no lipid‐modifying variants to be related to WMH volume.

Observational studies and randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that LDL‐C is one of the most important modifiable and causal risk factors for coronary heart disease and IS. 29 , 30 The roles of other serum lipids, such as HDL‐C and triglyceride, in the pathogenesis of IS subtypes are not so well established. 5 Moreover, most of lipid‐related randomized clinical trials provided no subanalyses of IS subtypes. 15 , 31 The J‐STARS (Japan Statin Treatment Against Recurrent Stroke) trial, the only study providing subanalyses, found that statins reduced the recurrence of large artery stroke but not small vessel stroke. 32 Currently, the relationship between serum lipids and CSVD remains controversial and unclear. In 1 report, the levels of LDL‐C and TC were found to be independent predictors for the occurrence of large‐artery atherosclerotic stroke rather than small vessel stroke. 33 In a recent study involving 623 participants, a new prediction model based on LDL‐C3 and LDL‐C4 (LDL‐C subtypes) could help clinicians identify high‐risk CSVD, even in people with normal LDL‐C levels. 34 Moreover, in a case–control study from China involving 333 and 503 patients with and without WMH, respectively, risk factors such as age, smoking, diabetes, and hypertension, but not hyperlipidemia, were associated with the prevalence of WMH. 35 Therefore, in the current study, we performed MR‐analyses to explore the genetically determined causal effects of key serum lipid and apolipoprotein traits on different manifestations of CSVD including SVS and WMH. It was found that genetic predisposition to decreased HDL‐C level, decreased apolipoprotein A‐I level, and elevated triglyceride level were significantly associated with higher risk of SVS. However, only genetic predisposition to decreased HDL‐C level led to larger WMH volume. Overall, it was strongly indicated that the level of HDL‐C and triglyceride rather than LDL‐C potentially play an important role in the pathology and incidence of CSVD.

Conventional measures of circulating lipids (eg, LDL‐C and HDL‐C) fail to distinguish among the size, density, concentrations, and compositions of lipoproteins, which may have contrasting relevance to risk of IS. 5 , 36 Therefore, a more detailed investigation of the association of individual lipoprotein particle subclasses with risk of stroke subtypes could be informative. Our research revealed that genetic predisposition to an elevated level of cholesterol in various sizes of VLDL particles was significantly associated with higher risk of SVS. Besides, genetic predisposition to decreased level of cholesterol in medium‐ and large‐sized HDL particles, an elevated level of triglyceride in IDL particles, and medium‐ and large‐sized LDL particles also could increase the risk of SVS, respectively. It is acknowledged that in the lipid metabolism, the LDL is transformed from IDL and IDL is transformed from VLDL in plasma. 37 Our subanalyses of size‐defined lipoprotein particle subfractions indicated the elevated level of VLDL‐C rather than LDL‐C played an important part in the risk of SVS. We also determined the triglyceride in specific size of lipoprotein particles to be essential in the occurrence of SVS. With the development of clinical testing technology, the classification of lipoprotein particle subclasses has great potential for aiding diagnosis or evaluating risk of varied subtype of cerebrovascular disease. As such, our findings highlight the importance of subanalyses stratifying by lipoprotein particle size, but investigation of the potential underlying mechanisms necessitates further studies.

This present study also involved the MR‐based mediation analysis to identify intermediate variables and provide clues to possible mechanisms of causal pathway from key significant lipid traits to different CSVD manifestations. Remarkably, we found genetically predicted hypertension ranked top in mediating the causal effect of HDL‐C on SVS (MP=32.4%), HDL‐C on WMH (MP=18.3%), and triglyceride on SVS (MP=52.2%). It is widely recognized that hypertension is a leading independent risk factor for IS. 38 Additionally, hypertension also mediated the causal association between hyperlipidemia and CSVD; thus, one may conclude, controlling hypertension might help control the hyperlipidemia‐induced susceptibility of SVS and WMH. Furthermore, compared with WMH, which had only 1 significant mediator trait with hyperlipidemia, there were other traits mediating the relationship between hyperlipidemia and SVS, such as type 2 diabetes, sex hormone binding globulin, agouti‐related protein, waist‐to‐hip ratio, and forced vital capacity. Specifically, of the several mechanistic stroke subtypes, individuals with diabetes were especially susceptible to the consequences of cerebral small vessel diseases. 39 A prospective cohort of postmenopausal women revealed a statistically significant inverse association between serum sex hormone binding globulin level and IS risk, which supported the notion that sex hormone binding globulin could be used as a risk stratification tool for predicting IS in women. 40 The current findings highlight the direct and indirect causal pathways from hyperlipidemia to CSVD manifestations, as well as potentially contribute to a better understanding of the pathogenic mechanism by which hyperlipidemia affects other IS types.

Strong clinical trial evidence supports a range of lipid‐modifying therapies for lowering the risk of IS, particularly those that lower LDL‐C level. However, most published randomized clinical trials provided no subanalyses for IS subtypes. 41 , 42 , 43 In the present study, the results of drug‐based MR analyses showed significant association of HMGCR‐mediated, NPC1L1‐mediated LDL‐C level, and CETP‐mediated HDL‐C level (equivalent to a 1 mmol/L increase) with the risk of SVS. The findings seemed partially contradictory to our foregoing MR analyses that it was genetic predisposition to elevated VLDL‐C rather than LDL‐C associated with higher risk of SVS. It could be explained that statin drugs (targeting HMGCR) and ezetimibe (targeting NPC1L1) were able to decrease both the level of VLDL‐C and LDL‐C, through inhibiting endogenous cholesterol synthesis and exogenous cholesterol absorption, respectively. These results indicate that, similar to large artery atherosclerotic stroke, genetic predisposition to the effects of lipid‐modifying drugs also had great potential in decreasing the risk of SVS. However, it was found that the risk of WMH, another manifestation of CSVD, was neither influenced by genetic predisposition to LDL‐C‐lowering targets nor HDL‐C‐raising targets. Our determinations basically agreed with findings from previous observational studies and randomized controlled trials. The SPARCL (Stroke Prevention by Aggressive Reduction in Cholesterol Levels) trial reported that the use of 80‐mg atorvastatin daily was similarly efficacious for preventing ischemic stroke in the small vessel disease and large vessel disease groups. 44 Administration of statins to patients with WMH was shown to decrease the risk of stroke, WMH progression, and cognitive decline. 45 Overall, our drug‐based MR analyses revealed that lipid‐lowering strategies could be considered for the prevention of SVS rather than WMH, but the benefits of such an approach would need to be tested in further more randomized controlled trials.

There were other similar MR studies investigating the causal effect of hyperlipidemia on IS and coronary artery disease, some of which were consistent with and some of which were controversial with our findings. Hindy et al demonstrated that genetic predisposition to higher LDL‐C was associated with the risk of large‐artery atherosclerosis stroke but not small vessel and cardioembolic strokes, whereas higher HDL‐C led to lower risk of small vessel stroke. 10 White et al found that genetically determined elevation in LDL‐C and triglycerides was associated with higher coronary artery disease risk. 46 Different from previous studies, we further explored the associations between varied components of serum lipoprotein with 2 characteristic CSVD manifestations (SVS and WMH volume). Moreover, in the present study, MR‐based mediation analysis and drug‐target MR were applied to identify key intermediary factors and provide a theoretical basis for the use of lipid‐lowering drugs in the disease of CSVD.

Meanwhile, there were several limitations in this study as well. First, we used only genetic data to find a possible causal relationship between serum lipid and different CSVD manifestations, the mechanism of which was uncertain and still needed to be confirmed through large‐sampled randomized controlled trials and basic research. Second, the GWAS data we used in our MR analyses were from a population of European descent. The present results should be generalized to other populations with different races with caution. Third, due to lack of available GWAS data, our outcome index involved only SVS and WMH (2 major manifestations of CSVD). Other CSVD manifestations like microbleeds, lacunar, and perivascular space could be included for analysis in further studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this MR study indicates that for different CSVD manifestations (SVS and WMH), genetic predispositions to an elevated level of triglyceride and VLDL‐C as well as decreased level of HDL‐C are associated with a higher risk of SVS, whereas only a decreased level of HDL‐C might lead to larger WMH volume. Genetically predicted hypertension ranks top in mediating the causal effect of hyperlipidemia with SVS and WMH. Furthermore, lipid‐lowering drugs could be considered for the therapies and prevention of SVS rather than WMH. The reliable causal relation between serum lipids and CSVD, and its potential mechanisms require further investigation, so as to provide a theoretical basis for the effective therapeutic strategies in clinic.
