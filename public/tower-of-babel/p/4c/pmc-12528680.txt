Phytochemical analysis and enzyme inhibition of Origanum dictamnus L essential oil through in vitro and in silico methods
Authors: Giuseppe Amato, Laura De Martino, Mejdi Snoussi, Vincenzo De Feo, Anouar El Hassane, Emira Noumi, Lucia Caputo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOriganum dictamnus L., is an aromatic plant belonging to Lamiaceae family. The aim of this study was to evaluate whether and how O. dictamnus essential oil (EO) showed inhibitory activity against enzymes associated with the Central Nervous System disorders and human metabolism. The EO was analyzed by GC/MS. Enzyme inhibitory activity against cholinesterases, tyrosinase, α-amylase, α-glucosidase and lipase were investigated. Lineweaver-Burk plots were constructed to study the kinetic parameters of the inhibitory activity. The interactions between the main constituents of the EO and enzymes were further analyzed through an in silico molecular docking study. The EO was mainly composed by carvacrol and p-cymene. The enzymatic assays showed inhibitory activities dependent on the EO concentrations. The type of inhibition observed was mixed for acetylcholinesterase, α-glucosidase, and lipase; competitive for butyrylcholinesterase; and non-competitive for tyrosinase. O. dictamnus EO could be used as supplement for management of metabolic and neurological disease.Keywords: O. dictamnus, Essential oil, Anti-enzymatic activity, Enzyme kinetic, Molecular dockingSubject terms: Enzyme mechanisms, Secondary metabolism

Introduction

The plants belonging from Origanum genus have been propagated by different cultures over the years for their exclusive culinary and therapeutic properties1. From ancient times, about 15 species of the Origanum genus are used to treat digestive, nervous, kidney, and respiratory disorders, as well as diabetes, hypertension, insomnia, epilepsy, toothache, and headaches1,2. Nowadays, the main use is as a spice known all over the world under the name “oregano” or “pizza-spice”1.

Origanum dictamnus L. (dittany) is native to the island of Crete, where it grows mainly on the sides of the mountains. In traditional medicine, this plant has been used against convulsions, tonsillitis, cold, stomach discomforts, liver diseases, diabetes and obesity as infusions and against epilepsy as compresses, powder or plant parts mixed with water3. In an ethnopharmacological study, Hanlidou and coworkers4 investigated the origin, preparation, and traditional medicinal uses of 172 plant species from 72 families found in the local market of Thessaloniki, Greece. According to their findings, Origanum dictamnus was traditionally used in folk medicine in the form of an infusion or applied externally as a compress or wash. It was employed to treat a variety of conditions, including endocrine, metabolic, neurological, and skin disorders, as well as ailments of the gastrointestinal and urogenital systems4. Traditional medicinal plants and their essential oils (EOs) are reported as sources of health-promoting bioactive compounds5. In general, as nutraceuticals EOs showed several health benefits in the prevention of metabolic syndrome-related disorders and as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiatherosclerosis and anti-angiogenesis agents6.

The essential oil (EO) of O. dictamnus is known for its antioxidant, antimicrobial, and antiproliferative activities3,7. However, other properties well known for essential oils distilled from other Origanum species8–10remain unexplored for dittany, volatile fraction.

The known activities are mainly attributed to carvacrol, a phenolic compound, which in most cases is the main constituent of the volatile fraction of many Origanum species EO, among which O. dictamnus11. Moreover, the antioxidant, antimicrobial, neuroprotective of carvacrol have been demonstrated in several studies, suggesting its use as nutraceutical ingredient in the development of novel functional foods12,13.

In recent years, the attention of the food, pharmaceutical and cosmetics sectors in natural plant-based products has increased significantly14,15.

In particular, the pharmacological activities of traditionally used plants have been investigated to develop new potential nutraceutical ingredients or coadjutants to classical synthetic drugs for treating neurodegenerative diseases and metabolic disorders6,14–17.

Enzyme inhibitors represent an important class of drugs widely used in the treatment of various diseases. As a result, enzymes have become key targets in drug discovery. For example, cholinesterase and tyrosinase are investigated for their roles in neurodegenerative diseases, while α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and lipase are targeted for managing metabolic disorders18–21. Cholinesterases are implicated in Alzheimer’s disease due to their role in regulating acetylcholine levels18; whereas tyrosinase is associated with Parkinson’s disease through its involvement in neuro-melanin synthesis within neuronal cells19. Moreover, α-amylase and α-glucosidase are important for carbohydrate digestion21and lipase has a fundamental role in the absorption of triacylglycerols20.

Kinetic studies are essential for understanding how enzymes interact with their substrates and how inhibitors disrupt these interactions22. The rate of enzymatic reactions can serve as a proxy for active enzyme present, providing valuable insights beyond what is obtained from standard spectrophotometric inhibition assays23 These kinetic data help clarify the underlying mechanisms of enzyme inhibition.

In addition, computational approaches such as molecular docking have significantly accelerated drug discovery efforts. These methods enable the prediction of binding affinity and interaction strength between small molecules (potential inhibitors) and their protein targets (enzymes), offering further insight into their potential efficacy24.

In light of these findings and considering that O. dictamnus L. is one of the less studied Origanum species, the purposes of this research were: (I) to study the volatile fraction of O. dictamnus, obtained through hydrodistillation and analysed by GC-MS, (II) to evaluated the nutraceutical potential of the Origanum dictamnus essential oil evaluating potential inhibitory activities against enzymes involved in neurodegenerative diseases (acetyl- and butyrylcholinesterase, tyrosinase) and metabolic disorders (amylase, glucosidase, lipase); (III) to clarify the mechanism of action of this EO against the above cited enzymes through the study of the kinetics; and (IV) to validate the data obtained from in silico approaches, particularly regarding the molecular interactions of the main EO compounds and the targeted enzymes.

Results and discussion

This result was similar to that obtained by Mitropoulou and collaborators11who reported a yield of 2.53%, while Ntalli et al.25 and Liolios et al.26 obtained yields of 0.94% and 0.46%, respectively, lower compared to our sample.

Oxygenated monoterpenes represented 69.4% of the essential oil (EO), while sesquiterpenes accounted for only 4.9%. The major compound was carvacrol (57.7%), followed by p-cymene (20.2%) and p-cymene-2,5-diol (2.5%). Caryophyllene was the most abundant sesquiterpene, present at 1.4%.

The chemical composition of this sample closely resembled that of Origanum dictamnus EOs previously studied by Liolios26 and Ntalli25 where carvacrol was also the predominant constituent, followed by p-cymene. Our data partially agreed with the available literature: in fact, carvacrol was consistently the main component11,27,28but the amounts of other components often differed from our sample. In these studies, the authors found a lower percentage of p-cymene and a higher amount of γ-terpinene and linalool, compared to the EO here reported11,27–29.

Marrelli et al.30 reported a markedly different chemical composition for O. dictamnus essential oil. In their study, monoterpene hydrocarbons dominated the oil, with p-cymene as the major component (32.7%), followed by γ-terpinene (12.4%) and carvacrol (14.7%). Additionally, an essential oil of O. dictamnus analyzed by Daferera and colleagues31 exhibited a different chemical composition compared to that reported here. Specifically, thymol was the predominant constituent at 78%, followed by p-cymene at 10.8%, while carvacrol was absent.

The variation in yield and chemical composition can be influenced by several factors, including geo-climatic conditions (such as soil fertilization), the duration of the drying process, and the timing of collection32. For example, Olivas and colleagues32 examined how harvesting time affects the chemical composition of O. dictamnus collected from Mexico: they found that the essential oil content was more diverse during the flowering stage, with the percentage of the main compound, carvacrol, varying between 47.99% and 79.84%.

The IC50 value represents the concentration required to inhibit 50% of enzymatic activity. Among the tested enzymes, the essential oil (EO) showed the strongest inhibitory effect against acetylcholinesterase (AChE), followed, in order of increasing IC50, by lipase, butyrylcholinesterase (BChE), α-glucosidase, and tyrosinase involved in the monophenolase reaction. Conversely, the EO was not active against tyrosinase in the diphenolase reaction or against α-amylase, as the IC50 values for these enzymes exceeded 1 mg/mL (Table 2).

The IC50values obtained in this study for both cholinesterases indicate strong inhibitory activity. While several studies have described cholinesterase inhibition by essential oils from the Origanum genus, none have specifically investigated the EO of Origanum dictamnus. Notably, the EO analyzed in this study showed a good inhibitory effect on acetylcholinesterase, with an IC50 value lower than those reported for O. vulgare subsp. glandulosum Salzm. ex Benth. (IC50 = 139.5 µg/mL) and O. onites L. (IC50 = 519 µg/mL), and O. rotundifolium Boiss. (IC50 = 32 µg/mL)33. However, our sample was less active respect to O. syriacum L. (IC50 = 1.7 µg/mL) and O. ehrenbergii Boiss. (IC50 = 0.3 µg/mL)34. In any case, the IC50 value obtained in this study was higher than that of galantamine, which was used as a reference compound (0.7 µg/mL).

The EO of O. dictamnus also showed inhibitory activity against BChE with an IC50 comparable to those reported for other Origanum species in previous studies. In fact, literature data reported IC50 values ranging from 0.3 to 538 µg/mL33–35, for BChE inhibition by Origanum EOs.

O. dictamnus EO was active against tyrosinase only in the monophenolase reaction. Few studies investigated the tyrosinase inhibitory effects of EOs from Origanum species, and none specifically addressed O. dictamnus EO. The IC50 value of O. dictamnus EO in the monophenolase reaction was higher than those reported for O. ehrenbergii (IC50 = 44 µg/mL) and O. syriacum L. (IC50 = 25 µg/mL) by El Khoury and coworkers36. In contrast, a separate study reported that the essential oil (EO) of Origanum compactum Benth. was effective against tyrosinase, but specifically in the diphenolase reaction. This difference in activity is probably related to variations in chemical composition. In particular, the EO of O. compactum was rich in carvacrol, as well as thymol, β-pinene, o-cymene, and 3-carene. Additionally, the EO tested in the present study showed lower inhibitory activity in the monophenolase reaction compared to kojic acid, the reference standard, which had an IC50 value of 14.4 µg/mL37.

The essential oil (EO) was also tested for its inhibitory activity against enzymes involved in metabolic processes, specifically α-amylase, α-glucosidase, and lipase. The results showed that the EO had the strongest inhibitory effect on lipase, followed by moderate activity against α-glucosidase, while it exhibited no inhibitory activity against α-amylase. To date, only two studies reported the potential inhibitory effects of Origanum essential oils on α-amylase and α-glucosidase, and none investigated their activity against lipase. Bustanji and co-workers38 tested the activity of methanolic extract of O. syriacum against lipase: the data obtained reported an IC50 of 254 µg/mL38. According to the literature, O. compactum EO demonstrated IC50 values ranging from 119 to 150 µg/mL, indicating it was more effective than our sample against both α-amylase and α-glucosidase39. In contrast, the study by Ya’ni et al.40 found that O. majorana EO had no activity against α-glucosidase, whereas our sample exhibited an IC50 value lower than that of acarbose, which was used as a reference standard for α-glucosidase inhibition.

Overall, the analysis of Lineweaver–Burk plots, presented in Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4, demonstrated that O. dictamnus EO inhibits acetylcholinesterase (AChE), α-glucosidase, and lipase in a mixed inhibition mechanism, while it acts as a competitive inhibitor on butyrylcholinesterase (BChE) and as a non-competitively on tyrosinase. Furthermore, the Ki values indicated that AChE was the most sensitive enzyme to the action of the EO, followed by BChE, α-glucosidase, lipase, and tyrosinase.

For AChE, the Lineweaver–Burk plot analysis (Fig. 1, A) displayed lines intersecting both X and Y axes at different points; moreover, the lines in LB plot were not parallel, indicating a mixed-type inhibition. For BChE, the LB plot (Fig. 1, B) showed lines intersecting at same point on the Y- axes, while the Vmax remained constant and the Km increased: these findings indicated a competitive-type inhibition.

Galantamine, the reference standard for cholinesterase inhibitors, was reported to be a competitive inhibitor42; in comparison, O. dictamnus EO exhibited a mixed-type inhibition against AChE, differing from competitive inhibition shown by galantamine, while displying competitive inhibition against BChE.

In Fig. 2, the Lineweaver-Burk plot for tyrosinase shows that the lines intersect at a point on the left side of the X-axis, while intercepting the Y-axis at different points. This pattern indicates that the essential oil (EO) reduced the rate of the monophenolase reaction catalyzed by tyrosinase without affecting the Michaelis constant (Km), suggesting a non-competitive mode of inhibition. In contrast, kojic acid, which was used as a reference standard, acts as a competitive inhibitor of tyrosinase43.

The Lineweaver–Burk plot (Fig. 3) for α-glucosidase showed that the control and inhibitor lines intersected at a point on the left side of the X-axis, with both lines intercepting the axes at different points. Additionally, there was a decrease in the Vmax value and an increase in the Km value, indicating a mixed-type inhibition, unlike acarbose that is a competitive inhibitor44.

For lipase (Fig. 4), the Lineweaver–Burk plot showed that the lines of control and inhibitor coincided on a point on the right side of the X-axis and intercepted both the axes at different points. Also in this case, the inhibition is of mixed-type, whereas the standard Orlistat acted as a competitive inhibitor of lipase45.

In general, mixed and/or non-competitive inhibitor can be advantageous because they are able to bind both the free enzyme and the enzyme-substrate complex, providing a broader mechanism of action and allowing for therapeutic effects at lower drug concentrations41. No previous studies reported kinetic data for these enzymes inhibited by O. dictamnus EOs. In our previous study, the possible anti-enzymatic activity of carvacrol an p-cymene against AChE, BChE and α-amylase activity was studied: the results showed IC50 values ranging from 3.8 to 171 µg/mL for carvacrol, and from 15.2 to 1456.0 µg/mL for p-cymene46.

Additionally, Taslimi and coworkers47 reported that carvacrol exhibited a higher affinity for AChE, BChE, and α- glucosidase with Ki values of 22.93, 23.87 and 2880.88 nM respectively, compared to those reported for their reference standard tacrine (Ki = 225.31 for AChE, Ki = 112.87 for BChE and acarbose (Ki = 12,600 nM for α-glucosidase).

They fit relatively well into the binding sites of these enzymes, leading to the formation of stable complexes with binding energies ranging from − 4.99 to − 6.26 kcal mol− 1 (Table 4). Methods

Materials and methods

Reagents

All used reagents were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Milan, Italy).

Plant material

The aerial parts of Origanum dictamnus L. were purchased in May 2023 from Minardi A. & Sons (Ravenna, Italy) through a licensed supplier, rather than collected from the wild. All required permits and licences were obtained, and handling of the material fully complied with the WHO Guidelines on Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP) for medicinal plants48. The specie was identified by Prof. Vincenzo De Feo (University of Salerno) based on Flora d’Italia49 and a voucher specimen (DF/738/2023) was deposited in the herbarium of the Chair of Medical Botany of the University of Salerno. The plants were subsequently dried in the absence of light at room temperature until hydrodistillation.

Extraction and analysis of EO

Three hundred forty-five g of dried aerial parts of the sample were subjected to hydrodistillation for 3 h, following the procedure described in the European Pharmacopoeia50. The resulting essential oil were solubilized in n-hexane, filtered over anhydrous sodium sulphate, and stored under N2 at + 4◦C in the dark until tested and analyzed.

GC/MS analyses were performed on an Agilent 6850 Ser. II apparatus (Agilent, Roma, Italy), fitted with a fused silica DB-5 capillary column (30 m, 0.25 mm i.d., 0.33 μm film thickness, Agilent, Roma, Italy), coupled to an Agilent Mass Selective Detector MSD 5973; Ionization energy was set to 70 eV and the electron‑multiplier voltage to 2000 V. Mass spectra were scanned in the range 40–500 amu, scan time 5 scans/s. Cholinesterases inhibition assay

Cholinesterase inhibitory activity was assessed by the colorimetric assay of Ellman et al.54 with some modifications. Briefly, 10 µL of the EO dissolved in MeOH, at various concentrations (100, 10, 1 and 0.1 mg/mL), was combined with 25 µL of 0.28 U/mL acetyl cholinesterase AChE (or butyryl cholinesterase, BChE) solution and 415 µL of Tris-HCl solution (0.1 M, pH 8). The mixture was put for 15 min at 37 °C. Following a further 30 min incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance was read at 405 nm using a UV spectrophotometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vantaa, Finland Varian, CARY 100 Bio, USA). Tyrosinase Inhibition assay

The inhibitory effects on tyrosinase (TYR) were determined by evaluating both the monophenolase and diphenolase activities of the enzyme, using the method of Khatib et al.55, with minor modifications. The reaction mixture was prepared in a 96 multiwell plate: in each well, aliquots 8 µL of EO (0.1–1 mg/mL) were mixed with 400 µL of substrate, 120 µL of tyrosinase (200 U/ml) and 280 µL of sodium acetate buffer (50 mM, pH 6.8). The reaction mixture was incubated for 5 min at 37 °C; thereafter, 400 µL of L-DOPA or L-tyrosine (2 mM) were added. α-Amylase inhibition assay

Jaradat’s approach, with minor modifications, was used to assess the amylase activity56. The mixture, composed by 100 µL of different concentrations of the EO (previously dissolved in methanol to obtain solution of 5–1000 µg/ml), 200 µL of 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH = 6.9), and 100 µL of amylase solution (10 U/mL), were placed in a block heater at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, 180 µL of a 1% substrate solution was added and placed in a block heater at 37 °C, for 20 min. Following this, 180 µL of a 3,5 dinitrosalycyclic acid (DNSA) water solution (96 mM) was added to the mixture and boiled in a block heater at 100 °C for 10 min. Then, the absorbance of the solution was read at 540 nm using a UV Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vantaa, Finland Varian, CARY 100 Bio, USA) All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and the results were expressed as IC50 value.

α-Glucosidase inhibition assay

α-Glucosidase inhibitory activity was assessed following the method proposed by Si et al.57 with some modifications. Lipase inhibition assay

Lipase inhibition was evaluated according to Bendicho et al.58, with some modification. Briefly, porcine pancreas type 2 lipase (Sigma product L3126, 200 U/g) was dissolved in ultrapure water at a concentration of 5 mg/mL and then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min; the supernatant was used for the assay. A 5 mM solution of 4-nitrophenyl decanoate (NPD) in dimethylsulphoxide was prepared. The composition of the reaction mixture included: 20 µl of 5 mM NPD, 800 µl of Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5), 20 µl of EO at different concentrations (5–1000 µg/ml, obtained by dissolving the EO in methanol), and 20 µl of the enzyme solution. The mixture was pre-incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, before substrate addition, and then incubated for an additional 30 min at 37 °C. In the control, the EO was replaced with the same volume of distilled water. The absorbance was measured at 412 nm (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vantaa, Finland Varian, CARY 100 Bio, USA) and a blank sample, without the enzyme, was also measured. Orlistat was used as positive control. All experiments were carried out in triplicate, and the results were expressed as IC50 value.

Enzyme kinetics

Enzyme‑kinetic studies were undertaken only when the essential oil (EO) displayed an IC₅₀ value < 1 mg/ml.

Lineweaver-Burk plots were used to linearize the substrate-velocity data and to investigate the kinetic parameters determining the type of inhibition, as well as the Michaelis-Menten constant (Km), maximum velocity (Vmax) values. Additionally, the inhibition constant (Ki) was determined to express the affinity of the EO for binding to enzymes59. For the kinetics study, the experiments were carried out as described in previous paragraphs with the exception that several substrate concentrations were used.

For cholinesterases, the concentration of two enzyme was kept constant at 0.28 U/mL, while various concentrations of acetylthiocholine iodide (AChI) or butyrylcholine iodide (BChI) was increased from 0.250 to 2.5 mM, in absence (control) and in the presence of 5 µg/mL of essential oil. The reaction was started by the addition of substrate, and monitored with UV spectrophotometer at 412 nm (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vantaa, Finland Varian, CARY 100 Bio, USA), at 1 min intervals over a period of 30 min. All experiments were carried out in triplicate.

For tyrosinase, the concentration of the enzyme was kept constant at 200 U/ml and different concentrations of tyrosine (0.5–2.5 mM) in the absence (control) and in the presence of 400 µg/mL of EO were used. The reaction was started by the addition of substrate, and monitored with UV spectrophotometer at 412 nm (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vantaa, Finland Varian, CARY 100 Bio, USA), at 1 min intervals over a period of 30 min. All experiments were carried out in triplicate.

For α-glucosidase, the concentration of the enzyme was kept constant at 1 U/mL, and different concentrations of 4- pNPG (0.5–4 mM) were used in the absence (control) and in the presence of 200 µg/mL of EO. The reaction was started by the addition of substrate, and monitored with UV spectrophotometer at 415 nm (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vantaa, Finland Varian, CARY 100 Bio, USA), at 1 min intervals over a period of 6 min. All experiments were carried out in triplicate.

For lipase, the concentration of the enzyme was kept constant at 5 mg/mL, while various concentrations of 4-nitrophenyl decanoate (0.5–50 mM) in the absence (control) and in the presence of 30 µg/mL of EO were used. The reaction was started by the addition of substrate, and monitored with UV spectrophotometer at 412 nm (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Vantaa, Finland Varian, CARY 100 Bio, USA), at 1 min intervals over a period of 30 min. All experiments were carried out in triplicate.

GraphPad (GraphPad Prism version 8.0 https://www.graphpad.com) for Windows was used to do analyses of data obtained with t-test analysis (three times for each determination, at p level < 0.05).

Conclusion

The present study highlights the inhibitory potential of Origanum dictamnus essential oil, primarily due to its major constituents, carvacrol and p-cymene, as confirmed by molecular docking analysis. Notably, this work provides, for the first time, new insights into the type of enzyme inhibition exerted by the EO: mixed-type inhibition of acetylcholinesterase, α-glucosidase, and lipase; competitive inhibition of butyrylcholinesterase; and non-competitive inhibition of tyrosinase. Whereas earlier investigations focused mainly on general antioxidant or antimicrobial effects of O. dictamnus EO, our research specifically elucidates its multi-target enzyme inhibition relevant to both metabolic (e.g., diabetes, obesity) and neurodegenerative disorders (e.g., Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s). This dual action represents a novel contribution to the field, positioning O. dictamnus EO as a promising candidate for development into nutraceuticals or functional food ingredients. These findings could pave the way for safer and more effective adjunct therapies with fewer side effects compared to conventional drugs. Further studies are needed to go into detail the O. dictamnus EO interaction mechanisms with enzymes and to improve EO stability in food systems.
