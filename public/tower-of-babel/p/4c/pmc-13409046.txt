Esculeoside A attenuates aortic remodeling and early atherosclerosis-like changes, with protective effects strongly associated with Nrf2 activation in high-cholesterol-fed rats
Authors: Talal Abdulaziz Alfurayh, Abu ElGasim A. Yagoub, Ghedeir M. Alshammari, Rajapandiyan Krishnamoorthy, Nawal A. Albadr, Laila Naif Al-Harbi, Mohammed Abdo Yahya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Atherosclerosis is a progressive inflammatory and oxidative vascular disease, and the discovery of safe, natural therapeutic agents is becoming increasingly important. This study investigated the vascular protective effects of esculeoside A (ESA), a steroidal glycoside from Solanum lycopersicum, against atherosclerosis induced by a high-cholesterol diet (HCD) in rats, with a focus on the Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1/nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Keap1/Nrf2) signaling axis. Male Wistar rats (n = 8/group) were assigned to control, ESA, HCD, HCD + ESA, or HCD + ESA + brusatol (an Nrf2 inhibitor, intraperitoneally) groups and treated daily for 8 weeks. ESA (100 mg/kg) was given orally, and brusatol (2 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally. Treatment with ESA significantly mitigated increases in serum cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL-c, and oxidized low-density lipoprotein (Ox-LDL-c) in HCD-fed rats. It also reduced aortic Ox-LDL-c levels in HCD rats by 51.3%. ESA also mitigated oxidative stress in these aortas, decreasing malondialdehyde (− 33.2%) and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (− 63.1%), while enhancing antioxidant defenses, including superoxide dismutase (+ 111.2%), catalase (+ 185.1%), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) (+ 177.1%), and reduced glutathione (+ 144.7%). Aortic inflammation in HCD rats was significantly reduced, with tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-6, and nuclear factor kappa B expression decreasing by 66.5%, 69.6%, and 49.2%, respectively. ESA also attenuated aortic apoptosis in these HCD rats by reducing levels of Bcl-2-associated X protein, caspase-3, and cytochrome c, while upregulating Bcl-2. Mechanistically, ESA enhanced nuclear translocation of Nrf2 (+ 129.1%) in aortic tissue without affecting Keap1 expression. Within this HCD rat model, these vascular benefits occurred without significant changes in fasting glucose, insulin levels, or body weight. Notably, co-administration of brusatol abolished these effects, findings that are consistent with and strongly associated with Nrf2 activation; however, this mechanistic inference is based on pharmacologic inhibition. Collectively, ESA confers robust vascular protection against HCD-induced aortic remodeling and early atherosclerosis-like changes, with these effects being strongly associated with Nrf2 activation.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for over half of all mortalities1. Atherosclerosis, a central contributor to CVDs, is a chronic, lipid-driven, and immune-mediated inflammatory disorder affecting large and medium-sized arteries2–4. It begins with the subendothelial retention and oxidation of apolipoprotein B-containing lipoproteins, especially LDL-c, resulting in the formation of pro-inflammatory oxidized LDL (Ox-LDL-c)2. This process initiates monocyte recruitment, their differentiation into macrophages, and subsequent foam cell formation, thereby promoting plaque development within the vascular intima4,5. As these plaques progress, they can impair blood flow through luminal narrowing or, more critically, rupture, exposing thrombogenic material, which triggers platelet activation and leads to thrombus formation3. These pathophysiological events are fundamental to the onset of ischemic heart disease, myocardial infarction, and stroke, underscoring atherosclerosis as a vital therapeutic target1.

Major modifiable risk factors contributing to the onset and progression of atherosclerosis include hypercholesterolemia, obesity, cigarette smoking, diabetes mellitus (DM), and hypertension6. A substantial body of evidence accumulated over recent decades has implicated oxidative stress as a pathological imbalance between pro-oxidant and antioxidant systems, a central mechanism in the development of atherogenesis. All aforementioned risk factors are associated with excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly in the vascular endothelium. Key enzymatic sources of ROS include nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases (NOX), xanthine oxidase (XO), lipoxygenase, myeloperoxidases (MPO), and uncoupled endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS). Elevated ROS levels are frequently accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction and increased expression of NOX, XO, and eNOS within early atherosclerotic lesions7,8. Concurrently, antioxidant defenses, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase, glutathione peroxidase, and glutathione (GSH), are markedly reduced within atherosclerotic plaques8. ROS potentiate vascular damage by promoting endothelial dysfunction, oxidizing LDL-c, and enhancing the expression of scavenger receptors such as cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36) and lectin-like oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor-1 (LOX-1)9. Additionally, ROS trigger vascular inflammation through activation of the nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) and the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules8,9. ROS also contribute to plaque instability by upregulating matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-1, MMP-2, MMP-9). While the direct use of antioxidants, such as vitamin E, vitamin C, B vitamins, carotenoids, and polyphenols, has shown protective effects in preclinical models7,10,11, clinical efficacy remains limited, emphasizing the urgent need for more targeted antioxidant interventions9.

In most mammalian cells, the transcription factor NF–E2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) serves as a master regulator of cellular antioxidant defense, coordinating the expression of a broad array of genes involved in reactive oxygen species (ROS) detoxification and redox homeostasis. Nrf2-mediated signaling is well recognized for its protective roles against oxidative stress–related pathologies, including neurodegenerative, renal, and cardiovascular disorders such as diabetes, stroke, myocardial infarction, and ischemia12–15. While Nrf2 can be activated endogenously in response to oxidative stress, its activity can also be boosted through dietary phytochemicals and drugs16. Under basal conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm by its repressor Keap1, which facilitates its ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation17. Oxidative stressors—such as ROS, Ox-LDL-c, lipid peroxides, and electrophiles—modify Keap1, releasing Nrf2, which translocates into the nucleus and induces the transcription of antioxidant and cytoprotective genes13,18. Beyond its antioxidant role, Nrf2 attenuates inflammation by suppressing NF-κB activation and reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules19. Notably, a growing body of evidence suggests that Nrf2 activation exerts potent anti-atherogenic effects, particularly in endothelial cells and macrophages. These effects include enhanced antioxidant defenses, improved endothelial function, reduced ROS generation, inhibited lipid peroxidation, and suppressed vascular inflammation20–31. In this context, identifying more effective Nrf2-targeting compounds from medicinal plants could offer novel, translationally relevant therapeutic options for atherosclerosis management, thereby making them more applicable and effective in humans.

Plant-derived compounds are potent activators of Nrf2 signaling, offering protective effects against atherosclerosis through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Several flavonoids, such as acacetin, berberine, and lunasin, have demonstrated anti-atherogenic effects in animal models by activating the Nrf2 pathway32–34. Similarly, adherence to the Mediterranean diet has been associated with a reduced risk of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and thrombosis35–37, likely due to its reliance on unprocessed foods, including tomatoes and olive oil. Tomatoes are a vital dietary component, containing bioactive compounds such as lycopene, quercetin, and kaempferol, which are associated with a reduced risk of cardiovascular disease38,39. Among tomato-derived compounds, esculeoside A (ESA), a spirosolane steroidal glycoside formed during the oxidation of tomatine, shows emerging promise. ESA has demonstrated antidiabetic effects by inhibiting hepatic gluconeogenesis and enhancing insulin sensitivity40, as well as potent hypolipidemic activity in apolipoprotein E-deficient (apoE)-deficient mice4. Recently, ESA has been shown to protect against diabetic cardiomyopathy in rats by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway42. Independent of its hypoglycemic and hypolipidemic effects, ESA also attenuates retinal damage in diabetic rats by activating the Nrf2/antioxidant system43.

Although ESA and structurally related tomato sapogenols have been reported to exert hypolipidemic and antioxidant effects, including attenuation of atherosclerosis in ApoE-deficient mice and Nrf2-dependent protection in cardiac, retinal, and hepatic tissues, their direct impact on vascular atherosclerosis and aortic redox/inflammatory signaling has not been fully characterized. To date, no study has examined whether ESA directly modulates aortic atherosclerotic remodeling in diet-induced hypercholesterolemia or whether such effects are mechanistically dependent on Nrf2 activation within vascular tissue. Therefore, the present study aimed to extend this emerging body of evidence by investigating the protective effects of ESA against aortic atherosclerosis-like changes in high-cholesterol diet (HCD) fed rats and to delineate the specific contribution of the Keap1/Nrf2 signaling axis in mediating its vascular actions.

Materials and methods

Rats

Adult male Wistar rats (180 ± 15 g, ~ 7 weeks old) were obtained from the Animal Research Facility at King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. Upon arrival, animals were housed in individually ventilated cages with clean bedding under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 20–22 °C; humidity: 40–60%) and maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Standard laboratory chow and filtered water were provided ad libitum throughout the acclimatization and study periods.

Ethical statements

All protocols conducted on these animals were approved by the Research Ethics Committee, King Saud University, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia (Ethics Reference No: KSU-SE-25-13). The authors confirm that all methods were performed in accordance with the full ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Furthermore, the study followed the US National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH publication no. 85 − 23, revised 1996) and strictly adhered to the Research Ethics Committee’s ethical guidelines and regulations.

Diets

The atherogenic diet developed by Paigen, characterized by its high cholesterol (5%) and cholic acid (0.5%) content, has been widely used to model hypercholesterolemia and atherosclerosis in rodents44,45. In the present study, both control and atherogenic diets were sourced from Research Diets, Inc. (New Brunswick, NJ, USA) (Table 1). The atherogenic formulation (D12336) was designed to mimic the original Paigen high-cholesterol diet (HCD), providing an energy density of 4.1 kcal/g, with macronutrient contributions of 20% protein, 45% carbohydrate, and 35% fat (kcal). In contrast, the control diet (D12337) was low in fat and cholesterol, offering 3.6 kcal/g with energy distribution of 20% protein, 70% carbohydrate, and 10% fat. The whole composition of both diets is detailed in Table 1. These formulations have been widely used in experimental models to induce not only hypercholesterolemia but also lipid accumulation in the coronary arteries and the aortic wall after 8 weeks of chronic feeding, thereby reliably replicating the early pathological features of atherosclerosis46–50.

ESA preparation

ESA was extracted from fresh, ripe tomatoes following the methodology described in earlier studies51. The tomato fruits were procured fresh from a local farm in Saudi Arabia throughout the cultivation period. The fruits were sliced, rinsed, diced into fragments, and then crushed, filtered, and centrifuged at 500× g for 10 min. Initially, we purified the ESA fraction using column chromatography. The freshly isolated supernatants were passed through filtration using a Diaion HP20 (Sigma Aldrich, # 13616, St. Louis, MO, USA) and then washed with 40% and 60% methanol. ESA was hydrolyzed with 2 N HCl and then extracted using ethyl acetate. The organic layer was extracted under vacuum, and the resulting pellet was purified using silica gel chromatography using chloroform/methanol/H₂O (9:10:1 v/v). The final product’s identity was inferred by comparison with previously published analytical data for ESA obtained using the same extraction and purification strategy, including reported chromatographic and spectroscopic characteristics42,51,52. This protocol has been validated in prior studies; however, in the present study, no batch-specific confirmation of ESA identity or purity was performed51,52. In particular, no HPLC or LC-MS purity percentage, chromatogram, or equivalent pharmaceutical-grade quality-control documentation was generated for the administered batch. Therefore, the compound preparation used here should be regarded as literature-validated for proof-of-concept experimental use rather than pharmaceutical-grade batch-verified material. The purified compound was stored at − 4 °C and used for all experimental procedures.

Experimental procedure

A total of 40 rats were randomly assigned to five experimental groups (n = 8 per group) and treated for eight weeks. Group one: (Control) received the control diet and daily oral administration of the vehicle (0.5% carboxymethylcellulose, CMC). Group two: (Control + ESA) received the control diet and ESA at 100 mg/kg body weight (BW) via oral administration. Group three (HCD) was fed HCD and received the vehicle (0.5% CMC) orally daily. Group four (HCD + ESA) received HCD and oral ESA (100 mg/kg body weight). Group five (HCD + ESA + Brusatol)received HCD, followed by an intraperitoneal injection of the Nrf2 inhibitor brusatol (2 mg/kg) 2 h before ESA (100 mg/kg) was given orally. All treatments were conducted daily for 8 continuous weeks. The ESA dosage was selected based on its effectiveness in reducing diabetic cardiomyopathy and retinopathy in streptozotocin (SZ)-treated diabetic rat models. Meanwhile, the i.p. administration of brusatol has been shown to inhibit Nrf2 activation in various tissues42,43.

Blood and tissue collection

At the end of week 8, all animals were fasted for 12 h and then anesthetized. Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine/xylazine (80/10 mg/kg). The anesthetic mixture was freshly prepared by combining 0.8 mL ketamine (100 mg/mL) and 0.5 mL xylazine (20 mg/mL) with 2.7 mL sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) to a final volume of 4.0 mL (final concentrations: ketamine 20 mg/mL; xylazine 2.5 mg/mL) and administered at 4 mL/kg using a 1 mL syringe with a 23–25G, 5/8-inch needle, 10–15 min before tissue collection. Vital signs, including respiratory rate and pattern, were monitored intraoperatively and postoperatively until the animal recovered from general anesthesia. Blood was collected directly from the heart, with 1 mL drawn into both plain and EDTA tubes (0.5 mL each). Then, animals were deeply anesthetized using ketamine/xylazine before the terminal procedures. Euthanasia was completed by cervical dislocation as a secondary method to confirm death, performed only after loss of consciousness had been fully achieved. The abdominal cavity was accessed through a midline incision. The abdominal aorta was excised, cleaned of surrounding tissue, and processed immediately. For each animal, the aorta was divided into portions: one segment was used for nuclear protein extraction, and the remaining tissue was used to prepare a total homogenate for biochemical and ELISA analyses. All assays were performed on tissue derived from individual animals without pooling, and the same tissue allocation strategy was applied across all experimental groups. After allowing the blood to clot at room temperature for 30 min, the samples were centrifuged at 500 ×g for 10 min. Plasma and serum fractions were then carefully separated and stored at −20 °C for later analysis. The isolated aorta was then prepared, as shown later, for further examination in accordance with established experimental protocols.

Biochemical analysis in plasma and serum

Plasma insulin was quantified using the Insulin ( rat) EIA Kit (Cayman Chemical, #589501, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Glucose levels were measured using the Glucose Colorimetric Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical, #10009582, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Serum total cholesterol levels were determined using the EnzyChrom™ Cholesterol Assay Kit (BioAssay Systems, #ECCH-100, Hayward, CA, USA). Quantification of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c) and triglycerides (TG) were performed using the Rat ELISA Kit (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA), Rat Low Density Lipoprotein (LDL) ELISA Kit (MyBioSource, #MBS702165, San Diego, CA, USA), and Rat Triglyceride ELISA Kit (MyBioSource, #MBS726298, San Diego, CA, USA), respectively. In addition, serum Ox-LDL-c levels were measured using the Rat Oxidized LDL-cholesterol ELISA Kit (Colorimetric) (Novus Biologicals, #NBP2-79692, Centennial, CO, USA). All biochemical assessments were conducted in duplicate using samples from each group (n = 8), strictly following the manufacturers’ protocols.

Preparation of total aortic homogenates

Total cell homogenate soluble proteins were extracted using GB-181 lysis buffer (GoldBio, MO, USA). Briefly, frozen aorta samples were weighed before lysis, and the appropriate volume of lysis buffer was prepared accordingly: 1 mL per 100 mg tissue. Homogenization was carried out on ice using a high-efficiency mechanical homogenizer (Q-Sonic homogenizer, CT, USA) set at 30 Hz for 20 s, with brief resting intervals (1–2 min) on ice to maintain low temperatures throughout the process. All procedures were performed at 4 °C with ice to maintain protein stability and prevent heat damage. Additionally, a protease inhibitor cocktail was added during homogenization at a 1:50 ratio to avoid protein degradation (GoldBio, # ProBlock™ Gold Protease Inhibitor Cocktail, MO, USA). Following homogenization, the samples were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The resulting clear supernatants, containing solubilized proteins, were carefully collected and stored at −70℃ until further use.

Preparation of cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins

Nuclear proteins were isolated from frozen aortic tissues using the Nuclear Extract Kit (Active Motif, #40010, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, aorta samples were thawed on ice and finely chopped to facilitate efficient lysis. Each sample was suspended in 500 µL of pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), supplemented with 1 mM sodium fluoride and 1 mM sodium orthovanadate to inhibit phosphatase activity and preserve protein function. Homogenization was followed by the addition of 500 µL of hypotonic lysis buffer containing 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM KCl, and 0.5% NP-40. Samples were incubated on ice for 15 min to ensure complete membrane disruption. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 1,000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C to separate cytoplasmic contents from nuclear material. The supernatants (cytoplasmic fractions) were aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C. The remaining nuclear pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of a high-salt lysis buffer devoid of detergents, consisting of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.9), 400 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, and a broad-spectrum protease inhibitor cocktail. This mixture was incubated on ice for 30 min with periodic vortexing to facilitate protein solubilization. Final centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C yielded nuclear protein extracts in the supernatant. Protein concentrations in nuclear and cytoplasmic extracts were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentrations fell within the expected range for aortic tissue extracts, consistent with previously published protocols. For all ELISA assays, equal amounts of total protein were loaded per well for each analyte to ensure comparability across samples.

Analysis of oxidative stress, inflammatory, and apoptotic markers in total aorta homogenates

Quantitative analyses of key inflammatory, oxidative stress, and apoptotic markers were conducted using a series of enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits for rat samples. Total tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) concentration was determined using the Rat TNF alpha ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #BMS622, Vienna, Austria). Interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels were assessed using the Rat IL-6 ELISA Kit (Elabscience, #E-EL-R0015, Wuhan, Hubei, China). Markers of oxidative stress and DNA damage, including malondialdehyde (MDA) and 8-hydroxy-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), were quantified using the Rat MDA (Malondialdehyde) ELISA Kit (FineTest, #ER1878, Wuhan, Hubei, China) and the Rat 8-Hydroxy-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) ELISA Kit (AFG Scientific, #EK720424, Arlington Heights, IL, USA), respectively. Antioxidant-related markers, including superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase, were measured using the Rat SOD1 (Superoxide Dismutase [Cu-Zn]) ELISA Kit (FineTest, #ER0332, Wuhan, Hubei, China) and the Rat Cat (Catalase) ELISA Kit (FineTest, #ER0264, Wuhan, Hubei, China), respectively. In addition, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) protein and glutathione (GSH) levels were measured using the Rat Heme Oxygenase 1 (HO-1) ELISA Kit (AFG Scientific, #EK720658, Arlington Heights, IL, USA) and the Rat Glutathione (GSH) ELISA Kit (AFG Scientific, #EK720391, Arlington Heights, IL, USA), respectively. Apoptosis-related proteins, including B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax), caspase-3, and cytochrome c, were quantified using the Rat BCL-2 ELISA Kit (FineTest, #ER0762, Wuhan, Hubei, China), the Rat Bax ELISA Kit (FineTest, #ER0512, Wuhan, Hubei, China), the Rat CASP3 (Caspase 3) ELISA Kit (Elabscience, #E-EL-R0160, Wuhan, Hubei, China), and the Rat Cyt-C (Cytochrome C) ELISA Kit (FineTest, #ER0893, Wuhan, Hubei, China), respectively. Limits of quantification and assay precision (intra-/inter-assay CVs) for the primary oxidative stress assays (MDA and 8-OHdG) were based on manufacturer validation data. All assays were performed according to kit instructions and within the linear range. All assays were performed in duplicate using samples from each group (n = 8/group) to ensure accuracy and reproducibility.

Analysis of transcription factor activity in the cytoplasmic and nuclear aorta extracts

Nuclear levels of transcriptional regulators involved in oxidative and inflammatory signaling pathways were quantitatively assessed using species-specific ELISA assays. The concentration of NF-κB in nuclear extracts was determined using a rat-validated ELISA kit (MyBioSource, #MBS752046, San Diego, CA, USA). Nrf2-related signaling was evaluated using the Nrf2 Transcription Factor Assay Kit (Colorimetric) (Abcam, #ab207223, Cambridge, UK), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In the present study, the mechanistic interpretation of Nrf2 activation was based on the nuclear Nrf2 readout together with HO-1 induction and downstream antioxidant markers. Meanwhile, total cytoplasmic levels of Keap1, a negative regulator of Nrf2, were measured using an ELISA kit (MyBioSource, #MBS7218529m, San Diego, CA, USA). All procedures were performed in duplicate, following the manufacturer’s standardized protocols, for eight samples per group.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

To determine the transcriptional profiles of components of the Nrf2 pathway, total RNA was extracted from the aorta using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, #74104, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s standard protocol. RNA purity and yield were measured spectrophotometrically using the NanoDrop system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed in a 20 µL reaction using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems/Thermo Fisher Scientific, #4368814, Foster City, CA, USA). The reverse transcription protocol included incubation at 37 °C for 60 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 5 min. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was carried out using the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL of SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), 0.5 µM of forward and reverse primers, and 2 µL of diluted cDNA template. The remaining volume to reach 20 µL is made up with distilled water. Amplification was performed using the following thermocycling parameters: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s. Quantification of mRNA levels was performed using the comparative threshold cycle (ΔΔCt) method, with β-actin as a reference (housekeeping) gene. The specific primer sequences and the corresponding PCR product sizes for each target gene are detailed in Table 2. Primer efficiency was validated using a serial dilution standard curve, and only primer sets with acceptable amplification efficiency (approximately 90–110%) and strong linearity (R² ≥ 0.99) were used for quantification. All runs were performed in duplicate of eight samples/group.

Hematoxylin and eosin general histology

For general histological evaluation of aortic structure, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue. Aortic segments were immediately immersed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and fixed for 24–48 h at room temperature. After fixation, tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4–5 μm using a rotary microtome, and the sections were mounted onto glass slides. The slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a series of descending ethanol concentrations to distilled water. For staining, sections were immersed in Mayer’s hematoxylin for 5–8 min, rinsed in running tap water to develop the nuclear stain, and then counterstained in eosin Y for 1–2 min to visualize cytoplasmic and extracellular components. The stained slides were then dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and cover-slipped using a permanent mounting medium. Microscopic evaluation was performed to assess tissue integrity, cellular morphology, and structural changes within the vascular wall.

Histology morphological analysis

Microscopic evaluation and morphometry were performed by an observer blinded to group allocation. For each animal, three non-consecutive cross-sections of the abdominal aorta were analyzed, and representative photomicrographs were captured at consistent magnification (scale bar as indicated in the figure). Tunica media (TM) thickness was quantified using ImageJ by measuring the distance between the internal and external elastic lamina at multiple equidistant points per section, and values were averaged to obtain one mean value per animal for statistical analysis. Qualitative descriptors were applied using standardized criteria as follows: endothelial disruption/denudation was defined as focal loss of the continuous endothelial lining with surface irregularity; elastic lamellae disorganization/fragmentation was defined as visible disruption, waviness, or discontinuity of elastic layers within the media; and adventitial inflammatory cell infiltration was defined as increased density of nucleated inflammatory cells in the adventitia/perivascular region relative to control sections.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism software, version 8.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Before conducting inferential analyses, the distribution of each dataset was evaluated for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. After confirming Gaussian distribution, intergroup differences were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For post hoc comparisons between individual groups, Tukey’s multiple comparison test was applied to control for type I error across pairwise comparisons within each endpoint. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), unless otherwise noted. The sample size (n = 8 animals per group) was determined a priori based on prior published studies employing similar HCD models and comparable biochemical endpoints, which have demonstrated sufficient power to detect biologically meaningful intergroup differences. All outcome measures and group comparisons were prespecified before data collection, and no post hoc exploratory endpoints were added after unblinding.

Results

Metabolic effects of ESA on final body weight, fasting plasma glucose, insulin levels, and hemoglobin A1c in HCD-fed rats

Compared to control animals, ESA administration did not cause any significant alterations in final body weight (450.0 ± 19.9 g vs. 451.0 ± 21.6 g), fasting plasma glucose (111.8 ± 10.0 mg/dL vs. 113.0 ± 11.9 mg/dL), fasting insulin levels (4.52 ± 0.61 µIU/ml vs. 4.65 ± 0.49 µIU/ml), or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels (4.89 ± 0.44% vs. 5.31 ± 0.92%) (Fig. 1A-D), indicating that ESA is metabolically inert under normolipidemic conditions. In contrast, feeding an HCD caused significant increases in body weight (586.7 ± 22.9 g), fasting plasma glucose (194.5 ± 19.1 mg/dL), insulin levels (7.32 ± 0.43 µIU/ml), and HbA1c (7.63 ± 0.50%), indicating a state of metabolic dysregulation (Fig. 1A-D). Notably, administering ESA to HCD-fed rats did not significantly change any of these parameters, with glucose (186.0 ± 26.5 mg/dL), insulin (7.58 ± 0.59 µIU/ml), HbA1c (7.77 ± 0.73%), and body weight (584.5 ± 24.4 g) remaining statistically similar to the untreated HCD group (Fig. 1A-D), indicating a lack of metabolic effect. Similarly, the HCD + ESA + brusatol group exhibited glucose, insulin, HbA1c, and body weight values (190.8 ± 17.0 mg/dL, 7.53 ± 0.79 µIU/ml, 7.87 ± 0.97%, and 580.0 ± 32.3 g, respectively) that did not differ significantly from either the HCD or HCD + ESA groups (Fig. 1A-D). Collectively, these findings indicate that under the experimental conditions of this HCD model, ESA did not significantly alter fasting glucose, insulin, HbA1c, or body weight.

ESA modulates serum lipids and aortic oxidized LDL-c via Nrf2 pathway

Compared to control rats, ESA-treated animals exhibited marked reductions in plasma triglycerides (↓10.6%), total cholesterol (↓22.2%), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-c: ↓30.4%), and oxidized LDL-c (Ox-LDL-c: ↓28.3%), along with a substantial elevation in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c: ↑66.2%) (Table 3), confirming its lipid-modulating impacts under normolipidemic conditions. However, treatment with ESA did not significantly alter aortic levels of Ox-LDL-c (↓8.1%) compared to control rats. HCD-fed rats exhibited severe dyslipidemia, with plasma triglycerides, cholesterol, LDL-c, and Ox-LDL-c levels elevated by 43%, 257.4%, 304.3%, and 210.9%, respectively, and HDL-C levels reduced by 59.1% compared to controls (Table 3). They also experienced a 158.3% increase in aortic levels of Ox-LDL-c. However, treating HCD-fed rats with ESA attenuated these abnormalities by reducing serum levels of triglycerides (↓29.2%), cholesterol (↓67.9%), LDL-c (↓63.9%), and Ox-LDL-c (↓57.5%), and increasing HDL-c levels (↑109.5%). ESA also reduced levels of Ox-LDL-c in the aorta of HCD-fed rats by 51.3% compared with HCD (Table 3). Co-administration of brusatol with ESA negated these improvements, as serum triglycerides, cholesterol, LDL-c, and Ox-LDL-c levels nearly returned to the levels seen in the HCD group (variations < 4% in each), and HDL-c was once again significantly reduced (↓55.3% compared to HCD + ESA) (Table 3). In contrast, aortic levels of Ox-LDL-c increased substantially by 85.8% in the HCD + ESA + brusatol group as compared to the HCD + ESA group (Table 3). These findings highlight that ESA’s anti-atherogenic effects, which affect lipid metabolism and LDL-c oxidation, rely on Nrf2 signaling.

ESA attenuates oxidative stress and restores antioxidant defense in HCD-fed rats through Nrf2 dependence

Compared to control rats, ESA administration led to significant improvements in aorta redox balance, evidenced by decreased aortic homogenate levels of MDA (↓41.1%) and 8-OHdG (↓32.8%), along with substantial increases in SOD (↑52.0%), CAT (↑101.7%), HO-1 (↑60.9%), and GSH (↑47.9%), indicating a robust enhancement of the antioxidant defense system under normolipidemic and normoglycemic conditions (Fig. 2A-D). Conversely, aortas of HCD-fed rats exhibited pronounced oxidative stress, with elevated MDA (↑60.4%) and 8-OHdG (↑265.9%), along with marked reductions in SOD (↓57.6%), CAT (↓59.4%), HO-1 (↓61.1%), and GSH (↓62.3%) compared to controls (Fig. 2A-D). ESA treatment in HCD-fed animals effectively counteracted these disturbances by lowering aortic levels of MDA and 8-OHdG by 33.2% and 63.1%, respectively, while restoring normal baseline levels of SOD (↑111.2%), CAT (↑185.1%), HO-1 (↑177.1%), and GSH (↑144.7%) compared to the HCD group (Fig. 2A-D). Notably, when compared to the HCD + ESA group, co-treatment with brusatol, the Nrf2 inhibitor, reversed ESA-induced antioxidant improvements and led to significant increases in MDA (↑51.5%) and 8-OHdG (↑185.1%) while decreasing levels of SOD (↓48.9%), CAT (↓72.1%), HO-1 (↓62.8%), and GSH (↓64.8%). However, there were no significant differences in the levels of these markers between the HCD and HCD + ESA + brusatol groups of rats (Fig. 2A-D).

ESA suppresses aortic inflammation in HCD-fed rats via Nrf2-mediated inhibition of NF-κB activation

In comparison to control rats, ESA administration had negligible effects on aortic inflammation, with minor changes in TNF-α (↑5.8%), IL-6 (↓14.2%), NF-κB mRNA (↓2.4%), and nuclear NF-κB levels (↑1.1%), indicating no proinflammatory activation under basal conditions (Fig. 3A-D). In contrast, HCD feeding significantly increased TNF-α (↑330.3%), IL-6 (↑657.6%), NF-κB mRNA (↑160.1%), and nuclear NF-κB (↑182.5%) levels in rats, demonstrating robust activation of inflammatory signaling. ESA co-treatment in HCD-fed rats significantly reversed these responses, reducing TNF-α (↓65.2%), IL-6 (↓67.4%), NF-κB mRNA (↓50.2%), and nuclear NF-κB levels (↓59.4%) compared to the HCD group (Fig. 3A-D). However, co-treatment with brusatol reversed the anti-inflammatory effects induced by ESA, resulting in restoration of inflammatory marker levels with increasing homogenate levels of TNF-α (↑198.9%), IL-6 (↑229.9%), NF-κB mRNA (↑104.2%), and nuclear NF-κB (↑160.2%) (Fig. 3A-D). These findings emphasize Nrf2 signaling as a key pathway through which ESA mitigates vascular inflammation in atherosclerosis. Notably, compared to HCD alone, the HCD + ESA + brusatol group exhibited no significant changes in TNF-α (↑4.1%), IL-6 (↑7.6%), NF-κB mRNA (↑1.7%), or nuclear NF-κB (↑5.6%) levels (Fig. 3A-D).

ESA attenuates aortic apoptosis in HCD-fed rats through Nrf2 activation

Bcl-2 levels in the aortic cytoplasm did not significantly change among all treated groups (Fig. 4B). Also, compared to control animals, ESA administration did not markedly alter cytoplasmic levels of apoptotic protein expression, with minimal decreases in Bax (↓4.0%), caspase-3 (↓0.8%), and cytochrome-c (↓2.6%), indicating no significant basal pro- or anti-apoptotic modulation (Fig. 4A, C, & D). In contrast, HCD-fed rats exhibited significant pro-apoptotic activation, as evidenced by elevated Bax (↑179.1%), caspase-3 (↑529.4%), and cytochrome-c (↑193.9%), confirming that an apoptotic milieu exists within the aortic tissue (Fig. 4A, C, & D). However, ESA treatment effectively suppressed these changes, significantly reducing Bax (↓61.0%), caspase-3 (↓69.5%), and cytochrome c (↓61.7%) compared with HCD rats (Fig. 4A, C, & D). In contrast, co-treatment with brusatol reversed ESA-mediated anti-apoptotic effects, resulting in reinstatement of pro-apoptotic signaling, as evidenced by significantly higher levels of Bax (↑150.2%), caspase-3 (↑227.1%), and cytochrome c (↑145.0%) (Fig. 4A and C, & D). These findings demonstrate that the anti-apoptotic effect of ESA is strongly associated with Nrf2 activation, as indicated by the minimal changes in Bax (↓2.4%), caspase-3 (↓0.3%), cytochrome-c (↓6.2%), and Bcl-2 (↑0.8%) in the HCD + ESA + brusatol group compared to the HCD group (Fig. 4A, C, & D).

ESA activates the Keap1/Nrf2 antioxidant pathway in aortic tissue

In normolipidemic rats, ESA administration had no significant effect on Keap1 mRNA (↑5.5%), cytoplasmic Keap1 protein (↑1.4%), and cytoplasmic Nrf2 (↑13.6%) levels, but it significantly increased nuclear Nrf2 levels (↑64.7%), indicating activation of Nrf2 transcriptional activity (Fig. 5A-D). In contrast, HCD-fed rats demonstrated a significant upregulation in Keap1 mRNA (↑220.4%) and protein (↑150.3%), along with robust cytoplasmic Nrf2 accumulation (↑180.0%) but a significant reduction in nuclear Nrf2 translocation (↓60.3%), suggesting Nrf2 cytoplasmic sequestration and pathway dysfunction (Fig. 5A-D). ESA treatment in HCD-fed rats did not significantly alter Keap1 mRNA (↓6.9%), Keap1 protein (↓1.7%), or cytoplasmic Nrf2 (↑1.1%) levels but significantly increased nuclear Nrf2 (↑129.1%) compared to HCD, reflecting enhanced nuclear translocation and transcriptional activation (Fig. 5A-D). However, despite unchanged mRNA and protein levels of Keap1 and Nrf2, the aortas of HCD + ESA + brusatol showed significantly lower nuclear Nrf2 levels (↓55.2%) compared to HCD + ESA. These findings are consistent with ESA enhancing Nrf2 signaling, potentially by relieving Keap1-mediated repression and promoting nuclear accumulation of Nrf2; however, this interpretation remains pharmacology-based rather than genetically definitive (Fig. 5A-D).

Aortic histology

In control and ESA-alone rats, the abdominal aorta exhibited normal vascular architecture, characterized by an intact endothelial lining, circumferentially organized smooth muscle cells (SMCs) within the tunica media (TM), well-preserved elastic lamellae, and small, uniformly sized perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) adipocytes (Fig. 6A–B). In contrast, rats fed an HCD for 8 weeks demonstrated pronounced aortic structural remodeling consistent with early atherosclerosis-like changes, including endothelial disruption/denudation, significant thickening of the TM, disorganization and fragmentation of elastic fibers, adventitial inflammatory cell infiltration, and marked PVAT adipocyte hypertrophy (Fig. 6C). Co-treatment with ESA markedly attenuated these HCD-induced structural alterations. ESA-treated aortas showed improved preservation of endothelial integrity, more organized medial SMC alignment, partial restoration of elastic lamellae architecture, and reduced PVAT adipocyte hypertrophy compared with HCD alone (Fig. 6D). However, pharmacological inhibition of Nrf2 with brusatol abolished these protective effects. Aortas from the HCD + ESA + brusatol group exhibited structural features comparable to HCD alone, including endothelial damage, medial thickening, elastic fiber disorganization, adventitial inflammation, and enlarged PVAT adipocytes (Fig. 6E).

Morphometry corroborated with the histology

TM thickness increased with HCD versus control and was significantly reduced by ESA; brusatol reversed this improvement (one-way ANOVA with post-hoc testing; groups with different superscript letters differ at P < 0.05) (Fig. 6F). These data indicate that ESA mitigates HCD-induced aortic remodeling, at least in part via an Nrf2-dependent mechanism.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrate that ESA significantly attenuates the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis in HCD-fed rats, thereby extending previous reports on tomato-derived steroidal glycosides to diet-induced vascular atherosclerosis and aortic Nrf2 signaling. ESA treatment resulted in a significant reduction in lipid deposition in the aorta, an improvement in histological architecture, and marked decreases in serum total cholesterol levels and in serum and aortic Ox-LDL-c. These protective effects included decreased lipid peroxidation, increased GSH and antioxidant enzyme activity, reduced inflammatory markers, and enhanced nuclear translocation of Nrf2 in aortic tissues. Notably, ESA exerted these beneficial effects without significantly altering fasting glucose or insulin levels in this HCD-fed rat model, suggesting that the observed vascular protection is not secondary to measurable changes in systemic glycemic parameters under these conditions. Importantly, inhibiting Nrf2 with brusatol abolished the hypolipidemic and other protective effects of ESA, supporting a pharmacology-based inference that these actions are consistent with, and strongly associated with, Nrf2 activation (Fig. 7; Graphical abstract).

The HCD induced increases in body weight gain, fasting glucose, and insulin levels, reflecting early metabolic dysregulation rather than overt diabetes53. Diets enriched in cholesterol and cholic acid promote intestinal lipid absorption, hepatic lipid accumulation, and systemic inflammation, which collectively impair insulin signaling and increase compensatory insulin secretion53. These metabolic changes are well described in Paigen-like diet models and are consistent with the development of insulin resistance secondary to dyslipidemia and hepatic metabolic stress. Hyperglycemia and hyperinsulinemia contribute to atherosclerosis. Persistent hyperglycemia promotes endothelial activation and can accelerate plaque development54. It increases oxidative stress and promotes advanced glycation end-products (AGEs)2,55. AGEs activate RAGE and stimulate NF-κB–dependent transcription of pro-inflammatory genes2,54,55. Hyperglycemia also reduces endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability56,57. This promotes vasoconstriction and endothelial dysfunction56,57. It also increases leukocyte adhesion and vascular inflammation56,57. Hyperglycemia is linked to increased myelopoiesis and microbiota-driven dysbiosis, which can further amplify inflammation56,57. Insulin resistance (IR) and the resulting hyperinsulinemia can further exacerbate atherogenesis2,58–61. Hyperinsulinemia promotes de novo lipogenesis via SREBP-1c signaling and altered acetyl-CoA carboxylase regulation2,58–61. It increases hepatic LDL-c secretion and enhances LDL-c uptake in the vessel wall2,58–61. It also stimulates vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, upregulates collagen synthesis, and induces angiopoietin-2 expression2,58–61. Collectively, these effects increase oxidative stress and inflammation and promote vascular remodeling during.

Despite the marked improvements in lipid profiles and inflammatory markers observed in our study, ESA treatment did not alter plasma glucose, insulin, HbA1c levels, or body weight in either control or HCD-fed rats. Taken together, these findings indicate that within the context of an HCD-induced metabolic disturbance, ESA improved vascular and lipid parameters without concomitant changes in fasting glucose or insulin, rather than demonstrating definitive independence from glycemic modulation across all metabolic states. Our results are consistent with previous reports by AlTamimi et al.42, who demonstrated significant cardioprotective effects of ESA in streptozotocin-induced diabetic rats without alterations in blood glucose or insulin levels. Similarly, ESA was shown to attenuate non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in rats fed a high-fat diet and to mitigate diabetic retinopathy in streptozotocin-treated rats without significantly impacting fasting glucose, insulin, or HbA1c43,62. Collectively, these findings support the idea that ESA primarily benefits the vasculature through local cellular mechanisms that reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, rather than directly affecting systemic glucose metabolism or insulin release. However, our observations diverge from those reported by Yan et al.11, who demonstrated that ESA exerted an AMPK-dependent insulin-sensitizing and hypoglycemic effect in db/db mice. These discrepancies could be attributed to differences in diabetic models employed (Type 1 versus Type 2 diabetes), variations in ESA dosing regimens, and treatment durations, highlighting the importance of context-specific factors when interpreting ESA’s metabolic effects.

Inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, promoting endothelial activation, monocyte recruitment, foam cell formation, and plaque formation4,63. Excessive retention and oxidative modification of LDL-c within the arterial intima initiate a cascade of inflammatory responses, including increased expression of endothelial adhesion molecules (ICAM-1, VCAM-1) and the release of chemokines such as MCP-1. These actions collectively promote monocyte adhesion and transmigration4,63,64. Upon infiltration, monocyte-derived macrophages internalize oxidized lipids and release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, further amplifying the inflammatory milieu2. Additionally, excessive ROS production enhances NF-κB activation in endothelial cells, leading to further synthesis and release of inflammatory cytokines8. This self-perpetuating inflammatory loop promotes plaque expansion, destabilization, and eventual rupture, culminating in thrombus formation and the growth of a necrotic core8. Consequently, chronic inflammation not only initiates lesion formation but also critically contributes to plaque instability and the risk of acute atherothrombotic events65.

In our study, ESA treatment markedly suppressed the expression of NF-κB and reduced the homogenate levels of TNF-α, and IL-6 in the aorta of HCD-fed rats, indicating potent anti-inflammatory effects. Notably, ESA did not alter the expression, levels, or activities of any of these inflammatory markers in control rats, suggesting that its anti-inflammatory effects are predominantly disease-activated rather than constitutive. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of Nrf2 with brusatol completely abolished ESA’s regulatory effects on these inflammatory markers, resulting in the re-emergence of heightened inflammation in HCD-fed rats despite ongoing ESA treatment. These findings strongly suggest that Nrf2 activation is a critical upstream mechanism mediating the anti-inflammatory effects of ESA, likely through suppression of NF-κB signaling. Mechanistically, it is well established that Nrf2 functions as a master regulator of the cellular antioxidant response and acts as an upstream inhibitor of NF-κB-driven transcription66. The interplay between oxidative stress and inflammation is evident, as ROS are potent activators of NF-κB. Thus, upregulation of antioxidant systems through Nrf2 activation, including glutathione, glutaredoxins (Grxs), glutathione-S-transferase, SOD, and heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), can attenuate NF-κB activity and downstream cytokine production67,68. Our findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the anti-inflammatory effects of ESA across multiple experimental models, which consistently implicate activation of the Nrf2/antioxidant axis as a central mechanism. In diabetic retinopathy models, ESA significantly attenuated retinal inflammation by Nrf2-mediated suppression of NF-κB activation and the subsequent downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines43. Similarly, chronic ESA treatment in diabetic cardiomyopathy models reduced cardiac inflammation and inhibited NF-κB signaling in an Nrf2-dependent manner42. Furthermore, in a rat model of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) induced by a high-fat diet, ESA conferred hepatic protection by suppressing NF-κB nuclear translocation. This response was associated with enhanced nuclear accumulation of Nrf262.

Along with its well-known role in metabolic dysregulation, hyperlipidemia, together with oxidative stress, constitutes a pair of interconnected and key factors in the development of atherosclerosis. Both contribute to endothelial dysfunction, foam cell formation, and chronic vascular inflammation. In this context, hyperlipidemia elevates circulating levels of LDL-c and its oxidized derivative form, Ox-LDL-c, which accumulate within the arterial intima, initiating endothelial injury, recruiting inflammatory cells, and leading to the development of lipid-laden foam cells, key features of early plaque formation. Concurrently, Ox-LDL-c exacerbates oxidative stress by stimulating ROS production via NADPH oxidase activation, a process mediated by its interaction with scavenger receptors, such as CD36 and LOX-18,9. This oxidative stress is further amplified by Ox-LDL-c-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and NO depletion, both of which impair endothelial integrity8,69. Moreover, Ox-LDL-c increases intracellular iron availability, thereby intensifying ROS generation through Fenton chemistry8,9. The resulting ROS not only sustains elevated Ox-LDL-c levels but also perpetuates a self-amplifying loop of oxidative injury, inflammation, and plaque growth, significantly increasing the risk of rupture, thrombosis, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes.

In this context, our findings reveal a robust hypolipidemic and antioxidant effect of ESA in both HCD-fed and control rats. ESA administration significantly reduced serum total cholesterol, LDL-c, aortic Ox-LDL-c content, and serum Ox-LDL-c levels. These changes were paralleled by significant elevations in key antioxidant markers, including GSH, SOD, CAT, and HO-1, suggesting that ESA enhances endogenous antioxidant capacity within the vasculature; these dual lipid-lowering and antioxidative effects position ESA as a multi-targeted modulator of atherogenesis. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have shown that ESA reduces serum triglycerides, cholesterol, and LDL-c in both control and STZ-induced diabetic rats, while simultaneously increasing cardiac and retinal levels of GSH, SOD, and HO-142,43. Furthermore, esculeogenin A, a compound structurally related to ESA, has been demonstrated to attenuate hyperlipidemia and atherosclerosis in ApoE-deficient rats by inhibiting acyl-CoA: cholesterol acyltransferases (ACAT-1 and ACAT-2), key enzymes in cholesterol esterification and lipoprotein assembly41,70. Collectively, these data highlight ESA’s capacity to regulate lipid metabolism and augment antioxidant defenses under both normal and atherogenic conditions. Notably, we also demonstrated that the ESA’s antioxidant effects in HCD were mediated by Nrf2 activation. Indeed, Nrf2 is the primary antioxidant transcription factor that stimulates GSH synthesis and the transcription of antioxidant enzymes31. In addition, Nrf2 also regulates lipid metabolism by modulating genes such as CD36, PPAR-γ, and SREBP-1c, influencing lipid uptake, efflux, and synthesis71,72.

Now it is evident that intrinsic cell apoptosis, mediated by Bax upregulation, cytochrome c release, and caspase activation, plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of atherosclerosis73. Endothelial cell (EC) apoptosis contributes to the early formation of plaque. In contrast, vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) apoptosis in the later stages undermines the integrity of the fibrous cap, promoting plaque rupture and thrombotic events73–76. Macrophage apoptosis is context-dependent: it may limit lesion expansion in early stages but becomes deleterious in advanced plaques due to impaired efferocytosis, which leads to a necrotic core and increased inflammation73–76. Mechanistically, oxidative stress, cholesterol overload, and p53-mediated Bax upregulation trigger mitochondrial cytochrome c release and activate caspase-3, culminating in intrinsic apoptosis73,75. Moreover, apoptotic cells expose phosphatidylserine, which amplifies tissue factor activity and increases plaque thrombogenicity75. In our study, rats fed the HCD exhibited elevated pro-apoptotic markers (Bax, caspase-3, cytochrome-c) and decreased Bcl-2 in their aortas, aligning with mitochondrial-driven apoptosis. ESA treatment effectively reversed these apoptotic markers and selectively restored Bcl-2 levels in atherosclerotic tissues, suggesting a protective and stimulus-dependent effect. These effects were observed only in HCD rats and not in control rats fed ESA, indicating that ESA inhibits intrinsic apoptosis in aortic cells through its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. In addition, the anti-apoptotic effect was diminished in the aorta of these HCD after Nrf2 suppression, confirming that oxidative stress is the major mechanism causing apoptosis during atherosclerosis. The protective effect of ESA is mainly due to its antioxidant properties. Collectively, ESA confers vascular protection and mitigates atherosclerotic progression by disrupting the oxidative stress–apoptosis axis. These findings align with other diabetic disease models, where ESA activates Nrf2 signaling, enhances antioxidant defenses, and suppresses inflammation and apoptosis in the left ventricle and kidneys42,43.

In addition, our findings provide compelling evidence that the vascular protective effects of ESA against HCD-induced aortic remodeling and early atherosclerosis-like changes in hypercholesterolemic rats are strongly associated with activation of Nrf2 signaling. This interpretation is supported by increased nuclear Nrf2 accumulation, coordinated induction of antioxidant defenses including the canonical Nrf2-regulated enzyme HO-1, and consistent reversal of ESA’s protective effects by pharmacological inhibition with brusatol. Additionally, ESA-induced Nrf2 activation was associated with the attenuation of key Nrf2 antioxidant targets as discussed above. Mechanistically, Nrf2 is tightly regulated by both Keap1-dependent and -independent repressors, such as glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl reductase degradation 1 (Hrd1) and regulated in development and DNA damage responses 1 (REDD1)17,77. Prior studies have proposed plausible routes through which ESA modulates this axis. AlTamimi et al.42demonstrated that ESA disrupts the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction, likely via direct covalent modification of key cysteine residues within the Keap1 sensor domain. Building on this, Alsabaani et al.43 elucidated that ESA downregulates REDD1 levels, thereby diminishing GSK-3β activity and enhancing Nrf2 nuclear transactivation. Our current data reinforce these mechanistic insights by showing that ESA significantly promotes nuclear accumulation of Nrf2 in vascular tissues, particularly in the aortic wall of both control and HCD-fed rats. Notably, this activation occurred without changes in Keap1 or Nrf2 transcript levels, or in total Keap1 protein expression, further supporting the hypothesis that ESA acts post-translationally to disrupt Keap1-Nrf2 sequestration. These findings expand the systemic understanding of ESA’s Nrf2-activating capacity, which was previously characterized in neural and cardiac tissues, to include vascular tissue compartments. The apparent dissociation between Keap1 and Nrf2 mRNA expression and their corresponding protein levels reflects the post-transcriptional nature of Keap1–Nrf2 regulation. Nrf2 activity is primarily controlled through Keap1-mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, rather than transcriptional induction. Accordingly, increases in Nrf2 protein—particularly within the nuclear compartment—can occur without parallel changes in Nrf2 or Keap1 mRNA expression. The enhanced nuclear accumulation of Nrf2 observed following ESA treatment, in the absence of transcriptional changes, is therefore consistent with post-translational stabilization and activation of Nrf2, a mechanism widely described for electrophilic and antioxidant-inducing compounds. While the precise molecular interactions between ESA and Keap1 remain to be elucidated, our results identify ESA as a potent, broad-spectrum activator of Nrf2, with significant therapeutic potential in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ESA attenuated HCD-induced aortic remodeling and early atherosclerosis-like changes through a combination of hypolipidemic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects. These protective responses were consistent with, and strongly associated with, Nrf2 activation, based on the nuclear Nrf2 readout, HO-1 induction, restoration of antioxidant status, and pharmacologic reversal by brusatol. Nevertheless, this mechanistic interpretation remains pharmacology-based and should not be considered genetically definitive, especially given the recognized off-target and broader translational effects of brusatol. Furthermore, because ESA was prepared using literature-validated extraction procedures without batch-specific pharmaceutical-grade purity and identity confirmation, the present findings should be regarded as proof-of-concept rather than compound-validated translational evidence. Collectively, these results support ESA as a promising natural molecule for further investigation in hypercholesterolemia-associated vascular injury, pending additional studies with analytically verified batches and more definitive mechanistic validation.

Study limitations

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although our data demonstrate robust activation of Nrf2 signaling and functional reversal by the pharmacological inhibitor brusatol, direct molecular interrogation of the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction was not performed. Specifically, binding assays or mass spectrometry–based analyses to confirm direct disruption of the Keap1–Nrf2 complex by ESA were not conducted. In addition, while protein expression was quantified using validated ELISA-based assays, complementary validation by Western blotting or immunohistochemistry was not feasible. Although ELISA provides a sensitive, quantitative assessment of protein levels and is supported by consistent downstream functional readouts and pharmacological inhibition, future studies incorporating protein localization and visualization techniques would further refine mechanistic resolution. Relatedly, Nrf2 activation was inferred from increased nuclear Nrf2 accumulation and induction of downstream antioxidant defenses, but direct transcriptional activity assays and broader profiling of canonical Nrf2 target genes (e.g., NQO1, GCLC, GCLM) were not performed. Moreover, while nuclear protein extraction followed validated protocols and optimized controls, marker-based validation of nuclear–cytoplasmic purity (e.g., lamin B1 or α-tubulin) by Western blot was not conducted. Accordingly, the mechanistic conclusions should be interpreted as Nrf2-associated and functionally consistent, rather than genetically definitive. In addition, brusatol was used here as a pharmacologic tool to probe pathway involvement; however, it has recognized off-target actions, including broader inhibition of protein synthesis/global translational effects, and therefore its reversal of ESA responses should not be interpreted as pathway-exclusive proof of Nrf2 dependence. From a chemical and pharmacokinetic perspective, ESA was prepared using previously published and spectroscopically validated extraction protocols; however, batch-specific spectroscopic confirmation (e.g., NMR, MS, or HPLC) and batch-to-batch consistency testing were not performed in the present study. In addition, plasma and tissue concentrations of ESA were not directly measured, limiting insight into bioavailability, tissue exposure, and translational dose equivalency. Regarding disease modeling, the Paigen-like high-cholesterol diet in Wistar rats primarily induces hypercholesterolemia and early atherosclerosis-like vascular remodeling, rather than advanced plaque pathology. Consequently, lesion assessment was limited to H&E-based structural evaluation and tunica media thickness of the abdominal aorta, without quantitative plaque burden, lipid deposition, inflammatory cell infiltration, or plaque stability metrics. The 8-week duration, use of a single ESA dose and time point, and reliance on a non-diabetic model further constrain extrapolation to advanced or metabolically complex atherosclerosis. Finally, the study was conducted exclusively in male rats, limiting generalizability to females, in whom sex-specific differences in lipid metabolism, atherosclerosis progression, and Nrf2 signaling are well documented. Potential inflation of type I error due to multiple endpoints is also acknowledged; however, all analyses were prespecified and appropriately controlled, and the consistency of effect direction across mechanistically related outcomes supports the robustness of the findings.

Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Superscript symbols indicate statistical significance as follows: A vs. control, B vs. ESA, C vs. HCD, and D vs. HCD + ESA (p < 0.05).

Fig. 6Abdominal aorta histology and tunica media thickness in experimental groups. Representative H&E-stained sections of the abdominal aorta (scale bar, 50 μm). A–B, Control and ESA alone: normal architecture with a continuous endothelium (black arrows), circumferentially oriented smooth-muscle cells in the tunica media (TM) (black arrowheads), well-formed elastic lamellae in a lamellar pattern (yellow arrows), and perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) containing small adipocytes (green arrows). C, High-cholesterol diet (HCD): endothelial injury with apoptotic/denuded endothelial cells (blue and red arrows), thickened tunica media (TM), reduced/disorganized elastic fibers (yellow arrows), hypertrophied PVAT adipocytes (green arrows), and inflammatory-cell infiltration in the tunica adventitia (light-blue arrows). D, HCD + ESA: near-normal endothelium (black arrows), orderly smooth medial muscle, restored elastic lamellae (yellow arrows), and smaller PVAT adipocytes (green arrows). E, HCD + ESA + brusatol (Nrf2 inhibitor): recurrence of HCD-like lesions with endothelial apoptosis/denudation (blue/red arrows), loss of elastic fibers, adventitial inflammation (light-blue arrows), and PVAT adipocyte hypertrophy (green arrows). F: Quantification of TM thickness for all groups. HCD increased TM thickness, ESA attenuated this effect, and brusatol blunted ESA protection. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 8/group). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Superscript symbols indicate statistical significance as follows: A vs. control, B vs. ESA, C vs. HCD, and D vs. HCD + ESA (p < 0.05).

Fig. 7The graphical abstract shows that ESA attenuates HCD-induced aortic remodeling and early atherosclerosis-like changes in high-cholesterol-fed rats through an Nrf2-associated mechanism. Under hyperlipidemic conditions, Nrf2 remains bound to Keap1 in the cytoplasm and is continuously degraded. ESA disrupts the Keap1–Nrf2 interaction without altering Keap1 levels, allowing free Nrf2 to accumulate and translocate into the nucleus. Once inside the nucleus, Nrf2 binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE) and upregulates the expression of antioxidant genes, including GSH, SOD, and HO-1. The enhanced antioxidant capacity lowers ROS and MDA, suppresses inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-6), and shifts apoptosis toward a protective profile (↓Bax, ↓caspase-3, ↑Bcl-2). The improvement in lipid abnormalities is likewise mediated through Nrf2 activation, leading to reductions in triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL-c, and Ox-LDL-c.
