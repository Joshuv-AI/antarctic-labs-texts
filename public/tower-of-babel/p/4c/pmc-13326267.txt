Background

Acute type B aortic dissection (aTBAD) is a life-threatening emergency that significantly affects long-term morbidity and mortality.1 Aggressive blood pressure (BP) control is the cornerstone of management of aTBAD.1,2 Refractory hypertension (RH) in aTBAD is a critical complication associated with significantly higher in-hospital mortality.3 International guidelines recommend blood pressure (BP) control as a first-line strategy in all aTBAD, with β-blockers as the preferred agent and treatment targets set at systolic BP < 120 mm Hg and heart rate ≤60 beats/min (Class I Level B).2 Uncontrolled BP is also associated with late aortic-related adverse events during long-term follow-up. Achieving sustained, long-term BP control remains unfulfilled in approximately 40% of patients.4 Renal denervation (RDN) is an established technique for chronic RH that acts by interrupting sympathetic nerve activity.5 Given the role of sympathetic overdrive in aTBAD-associated RH, RDN presents a biologically plausible targeted intervention.Take-Home Messages•Renal denervation is a feasible adjunctive tool for achieving sustained blood pressure control in acute type B aortic dissection.•Larger prospective studies are needed to confirm its safety and efficacy and its impact on long-term aortic outcomes.

We present a series of 2 patients successfully treated with catheter-based RDN for RH in the acute phase of aTBAD to optimize pressure control during follow-up.

First-In-Human

Case 1

A 57-year-old man presented with acute chest and abdominal pain. His medical history included poorly controlled hypertension and active smoking. At admission, BP was 250/125 mm Hg. A full-body computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed an aTBAD, with the proximal entry tear located just distal to the left subclavian artery. No signs of rupture or visceral malperfusion were observed. The right renal and right polar arteries arose from the false lumen. The patient was first admitted to the intensive care unit for medical management. However, aggressive medical therapy failed to achieve adequate BP control. Therefore, thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) was performed in landing zone 2 using a single-branch off-the-shelf thoracic endograft (Gore TAG Thoracic Branch Endoprosthesis; W. L. Gore & Associates). Postoperatively, the patient developed acute respiratory distress syndrome caused by acute heart failure and pneumonia, successfully managed with diuretics and antibiotics. Severe hyperkinetic delirium occurred, and magnetic resonance imaging revealed recent cerebral ischemic lesions, likely secondary to hypertensive injury. Despite maximal doses of 7 antihypertensive agents, the patient presented severe RH (BP >140/90 mm Hg). After a multidisciplinary discussion, an interventional approach to the renal arteries was indicated to detect silent chronic malperfusion and optimize BP control. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia using a single percutaneous femoral access. An angiographic study confirmed normal renal perfusion, with no signs of a lack of contrast opacification of the renal arteries or renal parenchyma. Subsequently, the ostium of the renal arteries was engaged using a 7-F steerable introducer (TourGuide; Medtronic) and a 0.035-inch STORQ guidewire (Cordis). Polygraphic measurements were then used to monitor pressure levels in the true aortic lumen, measured through the steerable introducer, and renal arteries, measured through a Terumo GLIDECATH angiographic catheter (Terumo). No hemodynamically significant pressure gradient was measured (aortic systolic BP – renal arteries systolic BP <10 mm Hg). Given the absence of angiographic or hemodynamic evidence of renal malperfusion, the patient underwent RDN. A Hi-Torque Iron Man guidewire (Abbott) was used to navigate into the distal extraparenchymal segmental renal arteries. RDN was performed using the Symplicity Spyral Multi-Electrode RDN Catheter (Medtronic). Thirty ablations were applied to the main right renal artery and its distal branches, 12 to the right polar renal artery, and 44 to the main left renal artery and its distal branches. Intraoperative technical success of the procedure was defined as a combination of: 1) efficient catheterization and treatment delivery in all the main distal branches of the renal arteries; 2) absence of complications (occlusion and/or dissection) in the treated vessels with preserved parenchymal opacification at final angiography. No complications were observed during the procedure. After the procedure, the patient was transferred to the Internal Medicine Department to continue respiratory rehabilitation and adjustment of the antihypertensive therapy. The patient was discharged 46 days after admission on 5 antihypertensive agents. At the 3-month follow-up, the antihypertensive regimen was simplified to 4 molecules with office and home BP values consistently <130/80 mm Hg.

Case 2

A 61-year-old man was admitted for intense chest pain during a hypertensive crisis (BP 230/130 mm Hg). CTA revealed a TBAD extending from the left subclavian artery to the common iliac arteries. No signs of rupture or malperfusion were observed. The left renal artery originated from the false lumen. During intensive care unit stay, optimal pressure control was not achieved despite maximal medical therapy. High doses of sodium nitroprusside were needed to control BP, and the patient started to experience clinical signs of neurologic toxicity. Therefore, TEVAR in landing zone 2 with a single-branched off-the-shelf device (Gore TAG Thoracic Branch Endoprothesis; W. L. Gore & Associates) was indicated. After a multidisciplinary evaluation, invasive management of the renal arteries was indicated using the same strategy as that used for the first procedure. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with a single percutaneous femoral access and left radial access. TEVAR was performed as the first step. The above-mentioned algorithm to detect silent kidney malperfusion was then performed, with no evidence of malperfusion. RDN was then performed using the same surgical techniques as presented in case 1. RDN was successfully performed in both the left and right renal arteries and distal branches, with a total of 104 ablations (Figure 2). Final angiography and postoperative CTA confirmed no complications in the treated renal arteries and persistent good perfusion of the renal parenchyma. The complete procedure is available in Video 1. After the procedure, the patient progressively recovered from the neurologic symptoms linked to sodium nitroprusside intoxication. Antihypertensive therapy was progressively eased from 7 to 5 molecules at the time of discharge. Discussion

RH in aTBAD represents a clinical challenge, as uncontrolled BP exacerbates the risk of aortic rupture.3 Effective BP control remains crucial in chronic TBAD as well. Aortic-related reinterventions during follow-up occur at high rates, mainly due to aneurysmatic degeneration of the aorta, with substantial morbidity and mortality.6 Evidence suggests that optimal BP control during follow-up limits disease progression and favors positive aortic remodeling.7 However, this may be difficult to achieve during the chronic phase of the disease, and patients often require more than 3 different molecules, hindering therapeutic compliance.4

To date, the literature on the impact of renal function and BP control on long-term outcomes of aortic dissection is scarce and fragmented. Renal involvement may play a key role in the persistence of hypertension during the chronic phase of TBAD. Chronic silent renal malperfusion may be difficult to identify because of the absence of initial radiological or laboratory indicators, yet it may ultimately affect renal function and BP control over time.8 In a single-center experience, angiographic renal malperfusion was detected in almost 40% of patients with no clinical suspicion.8 In this experience, the authors followed a standardized strategy to detect silent chronic kidney malperfusion. First, an angiographic study was performed to detect a deficit of vessel or parenchymal opacification. Pressure gradient measurement between the renal arteries and the visceral aorta was then performed. In both cases, no signs of chronic malperfusion were detected, and RDN was performed, assuming renal artery stenting as inappropriate in this scenario. RDN is an invasive strategy for the management of chronic RH.5 It acts by interrupting sympathetic nerves in the adventitia and perivascular tissue of the renal arteries, resulting in sustained BP reduction during the entire 24-hour circadian cycle. Although renal artery dissection is generally considered a contraindication for RDN, in most patients with aortic dissection, the renal arteries are not directly involved. Moreover, the efficacy of RDN is higher when it is performed in the most distal branches, which are intact in most cases. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this is the first-in-human report of RDN used in the setting of aTBAD. The use of RDN in TBAD has only been reported anecdotally, with the larger experience counting 6 treated patients.9 However, all cases refer to patients treated in the chronic phase of TBAD.9,10 This experience suggests that RDN represents a feasible and effective adjunct tool to achieve sustained BP control in carefully selected patients during aTBAD. Optimizing BP control starting from hospitalization may improve long-term outcomes and ultimately reduce disease progression. Larger prospective clinical trials focusing on the impact of long-term renal function and pressure control strategies to improve BP control may be a game changer in the chronic management of patients with TBAD, and RDN may be a possible interventional strategy to achieve this goal.

Conclusions

This experience suggests that RDN is a feasible and effective adjunctive tool for achieving sustained BP control in carefully selected patients during aTBAD. Larger prospective studies are needed to confirm its safety and efficacy and its impact on long-term aortic remodeling and aortic-related outcomes.
