A cross-disciplinary hands-on genomics curriculum adaptable for high school to undergraduate education
Authors: Sophie Zaaijer, Simon C. Groen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDNA literacy is becoming increasingly essential for navigating healthcare, understanding pandemics, and engaging with biotechnology—yet genomics education remains limited at the secondary level of education. We present a modular, hands-on curriculum designed for high school and early undergraduate students (ages 14–21) that introduces key genomics concepts through an experiment on fermentation, a process that is key to food preservation and medicine. Students follow a complete scientific process: exploring what DNA is and how microbial succession works, analyzing real DNA sequencing data, and writing a formal scientific report. The course integrates molecular biology, bioinformatics, and data analysis obtained from portable nanopore sequencing technology (Oxford Nanopore MinION), giving students access to authentic data sets. Activities such as microbial species identification using taxonomic IDs foster skills in observation, experimental design, and quantitative reasoning. The curriculum aligns with Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) and Vision and Change (V&C) frameworks, supporting interdisciplinary learning and scientific literacy. By making molecular biology visible and relatable, this curriculum equips diverse learners with the tools to engage meaningfully in a genomics-driven world.

INTRODUCTION

In today’s rapidly advancing scientific landscape, genomics has become a fundamental pillar of biological understanding, with far-reaching implications for society. This innovative curriculum seeks to address the pressing need to introduce genomics education at an earlier stage in students' academic journeys, positioning it alongside core subjects like mathematics, geography, and computer science.

Three key societal shifts highlight the importance of this initiative:

The democratization of DNA analysis, transforming it from a specialized procedure into a consumer product (e.g., 23andMe, Ancestry.com).

These developments underscore the need for a population that is well-versed in fundamental genetic principles.

Current curricula often focus on foundational scientific principles and basic experiments but typically lack the integration of advanced genomics and bioinformatics. While programs such as OpenSciEd offer hands-on activities (1), they generally do not provide opportunities for students to work with real genomic data or practice bioinformatics. On the other hand, the NetSci High program connects high school students from underrepresented minority backgrounds and their teachers with regional university research labs (2), offering valuable research experiences. However, such programs may not be accessible or feasible for all schools. Our scalable and accessible curriculum addresses these gaps by providing students with precollected DNA sequencing data from the Oxford Nanopore MinION device, allowing them to engage in genomics, bioinformatics, and scientific report writing. Building on previous work in genomics education (3, 4), our curriculum offers an approachable, hands-on experience with sequencing and bioinformatics, even in classrooms with limited resources. While genomics education has been explored at the higher education level, few curricula focus on high school students or emphasize student-driven research culminating in the production of a formal scientific report or manuscript.

Teaching genomics presents unique challenges, particularly due to its highly interdisciplinary nature, which demands a synthesis of concepts across diverse scientific domains. Additionally, genomics is inherently abstract—students cannot directly observe the molecular processes at play, which can make it difficult for them to connect theoretical concepts to real-world applications. From a neurobiological perspective, this lack of observable stimuli can hinder engagement and retention, as active learning relies on meaningful interactions with content that can be perceived and manipulated. To overcome these challenges, we developed a novel curriculum that emphasizes experiential learning and cognitive engagement of students, with the goal of culminating in a high-quality report students write that details their scientific analysis on the process of pickling cucumbers. The curriculum is entitled “The Secret Life of Pickles”.

Ultimately, this approach aims to prepare the next generation for a world in which genetic literacy is essential. Even for students who will not pursue careers in biology, this course will provide foundational knowledge that will be increasingly relevant as genomics and precision medicine continue to shape the future.

Our curriculum adopts a “Lego-building” pedagogical model, providing students with a clear endpoint—a scientific report about the biological process of pickling—while they are being guided to inquire about and explore the steps necessary to achieve this goal (Fig. 1). Pickling of fruit and vegetables, a familiar and relatable process for many students, serves as an accessible gateway to complex biological concepts. This context, paired with the neurobiological principle of “active recall” learning (5) enhances students' ability to transfer knowledge from short-term to long-term memory.

Each lesson is designed to incorporate hands-on activities that stimulate active cognitive processes, minimizing passive information intake and fostering deeper understanding. By promoting continuous engagement and facilitating knowledge construction, we aim to ensure that learning is retained.

Intended audience

This manuscript presents a hands-on genomics educational course module designed for high school or undergraduate students (aged 14–21). Although a pilot was previously conducted with slightly younger students (ages 12–14), we found that certain elements of the curriculum are more effectively absorbed and executed by students who are more mature. Therefore, we recommend offering the curriculum to older students, particularly those in high school and early undergraduate courses. The curriculum provides a flexible, classroom-based approach that can either supplement existing biology classes or replace specific sections, depending on the educational context.

Each lesson emphasizes hands-on, interactive learning, an approach consistently shown to enhance engagement across age groups (6). The curriculum employs applied problem-solving methodologies, which have been shown to increase engagement, particularly among female students (7). Studies indicate that girls often demonstrate greater interest in fields like computer programming when framed as a means to address personally relevant problems rather than as an end in itself (8).

Prerequisite student knowledge

The curriculum blends biology, mathematics, and introductory computer science, making it adaptable for a range of educational levels. It can serve as an entry point for students new to these topics or as a way to reinforce and connect knowledge for more advanced learners.

For high school students, the course introduces new interdisciplinary concepts and may require additional instruction time. For early undergraduate students, it deepens understanding and highlights the integration of disciplines they have already encountered. Educators can adjust the pacing and emphasis to align with students’ backgrounds and goals, making the curriculum flexible and effective across diverse classroom settings.

Safety

The fermentation process primarily involves the growth of Lactobacillaceae species, which are widely regarded as safe and typically classified under biosafety level 1 (BSL-1). However, it is important to recognize that Enterobacteriaceae species and other microorganisms may be present in the cucumber microbiome, especially during the early stages of fermentation. These microbes could pose potential risks, necessitating careful biohazard management. To address these concerns, best practices for handling and mitigating biohazard risks are provided in the Biohazard Considerations section of File S1. These guidelines ensure that the experiment remains safe for both students and instructors, while still allowing for an authentic, hands-on scientific experience.

Possible modifications

While the curriculum is currently distributed in PDF format, its structure and interactivity make it exceptionally well suited for transformation into a digital, interactive learning module. A digital platform would allow for immediate feedback, automated evaluation, and guided learning pathways, enabling students to deepen their understanding independently—even outside of classroom hours. This format would not only enhance student engagement but also reduce reliance on teacher availability during homework or self-paced study.

The modular structure of the curriculum allows for flexible implementation across educational levels. In resource-rich environments or advanced classrooms, teachers may choose to perform DNA extraction and sequencing in class, using devices like the Oxford Nanopore MinION sequencer. Several groups have successfully piloted this approach (3, 4, 9), offering students a unique opportunity to operate cutting-edge molecular tools.

CONCLUSION

Preliminary results of field testing

As part of our pilot implementation, we taught the course to a class of 17 secondary-level education students (aged 12–14). They participated in the genomics curriculum in person in Spring 2017.

We conducted an anonymous survey among participating students. Their feedback provided valuable insight into both the strengths of the course module and areas for improvement. Students consistently praised the curriculum’s hands-on, inquiry-driven components—particularly the pickling experiment and use of DNA sequencing data generated with the MinION sequencer. These activities made the experience feel authentic, with many students remarking that they felt like “real scientists.” Several students expressed excitement about sequencing DNA from unconventional samples such as chocolate bars or their own saliva. The curriculum’s use of real-world technology contributed to its high perceived value. Most students gave top marks and indicated they would eagerly participate again in “The Secret Life of Pickles.”

Beyond its genomics content, this curriculum emphasizes scientific thinking, collaboration, and students’ ability to work across disciplines. Students learn not just what science is, but how it is done—by participating in it. Even those students who do not end up pursuing STEM careers gain transferable skills in data interpretation, evidence-based reasoning, and written communication.

This model affirms that complex biological concepts and real-world data sets can be made accessible to high school learners—without compromising scientific authenticity. As sequencing technologies continue to evolve, so too can classroom experiences that prepare students to understand and shape the biological sciences of the future.
