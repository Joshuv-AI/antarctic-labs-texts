Gastrointestinal apicomplexan parasites of captive reptiles: diversity, causes, consequences
Authors: Jakub Žahourek, Jana Kvičerová
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCaptive reptiles frequently suffer from gastrointestinal parasites, which stem from their breeding conditions such as limited space and frequent contact with faeces, high numbers of individuals in inadequate conditions, stress, but also from extensive exports and imports in recent years. In particular, parasites with single-host life cycles are common as they can easily complete their development. This study focused on extensive sampling across a variety of captive reptiles groups including lizards (agamas, chameleons, geckos, iguanas, monitors, skinks), snakes, tortoises, and also crocodiles, originating from various resources (pet trades, private breeders, and zoos).ResultsExamination of faecal samples revealed that 17.9% (55/308) of captive reptiles were infected with coccidia, namely Eimeria (7.1%), Choleoeimeria (5.5%), Isospora (3.9%), and Caryospora (0.3%), and that 3.2% (10/308) were infected with Cryptosporidium. These apicomplexans, which have monoxenous life cycles, may easily be maintained in breedings, and commonly transmitted to the breedings of new owners via trades and expos. While Choleoeimeria spp. were most prevalent in snake hosts, Isospora amphiboluri was recorded as the most common parasite of bearded dragons; however, its host specificity was shown to be relatively low, as it was also able to infect veiled chameleons. In addition, other representatives of coccidia, as well as pseudoparasites from prey items, were also recorded. Although the treatment of coccidiosis in reptiles remains debatable, our findings revealed that it was more effective to treat infected animals with anticoccidials than to leave them untreated.ConclusionsThis is a comprehensive study on a variety of genera of gastrointestinal apicomplexan protists in captive reptiles that considers not only their detection and identification based on a robust combination of morphological and molecular data, but also their host specificity, evolutionary relationships, clinical status, and possible pseudoinfections with parasites of prey items that cannot be proven without the use of molecular methods. The study highlights the frequent occurrence and importance of gastrointestinal parasites in the health of captive reptiles, the need for their examination and consideration, and the application of proper management strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05029-8.

Background

Reptiles are among the most common pets worldwide, not only in Europe and North America. This is evidenced by the huge number of animals imported into or exported from Europe [1]. For example, 24,611 animals protected by CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) were exported from the Czech Republic in the course of 2021–2022. During the same period, 16,323 animals were imported to the Czech Republic, primarily from Madagascar, Indonesia, and the USA [1]. However, these numbers represent only a fraction of the total trade volume; the total numbers cannot be easily quantified due to shared border areas, but mainly due to the significant role of illegal trade [2–4]. Exports and imports allow animals to travel around the world together with their parasites, which may persist primarily in farmed animals that exhibit considerably higher parasitaemia than their wild counterparts [5]. Primarily, this is the case of monoxenous parasite species that do not require an intermediate host to complete their life cycles, and their developmental stages released into the environment quickly become infective to the new host/s.

Although infections with parasites are usually asymptomatic in the wild, parasitaemia often increases in captivity and can affect animal health due to the high risk of autoinfection caused by limited space, high stocking densities, frequent contact with faeces, and inadequate housing conditions, all of which are associated with higher stress [6]. Typical symptoms of intestinal parasitic infection include apathy, inappetence, anorexia, diarrhoea, and, in the final stages, the death of the host individual [7].

The most common parasites detected in captive reptiles are coccidia (Apicomplexa: Coccidia), and pinworms of the family Pharyngodonidae (Nematoda: Oxyurida). Coccidia, represented mainly by the genera Choleoeimeria, Eimeria, and Isospora, can damage the gut wall and other organs of reptiles, and subsequently reduce the overall fitness of the animals. On the other hand, pinworms are usually considered not much pathogenic, even in cases of high parasitaemia [8, 9]. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the degree of parasitism across various families and genera of captive reptiles of different origins, as well as to assess the identity, evolutionary relationships, and host specificity of the parasites, to obtain a complex view of the current situation.

Results

During 2021–2022, faecal samples were obtained from 144 lizards (agamas, geckos, iguanas, skinks), 131 snakes, 30 tortoises, and 3 crocodiles (for more details, see Additional file 1). The samples originated from 12 private breeding facilities, 4 zoological gardens, and 2 pet stores of 1 chain (for more details, see Additional file 2). Of the 308 examined individuals, 55 individuals (17 lizards, 38 snakes, 0 tortoises, and 0 crocodiles) were microscopically positive for coccidian parasites, and 10 individuals (9 lizards, 1 snake, 0 tortoises, and 0 crocodiles) for Cryptosporidium (Additional files 1, 3, 4). Four genera of coccidian parasites were recorded: Caryospora, Choleoeimeria, Eimeria, and Isospora. Photomicrographs of selected oocysts are presented in Additional file 5.

All isosporans detected in samples from bearded dragons (P. vitticeps) – samples numbered 125, 141, 206, 265, 287, and 304 (Additional file 5) – were morphologically identical to I. amphiboluri described by [10] from P. vitticeps (Additional file 7). This was also congruent with the results of phylogenetic analyses (Figs. 1, 2).

In veiled chameleons (Ch. calyptratus), the spectrum of coccidia was not so uniform. The sample 276 (Additional file 5) also morphologically corresponded to I. amphiboluri. The sample 110 (Additional file 5) morphologically corresponded to I. jaracimrmani described by [11] from Ch. calyptratus. The sample 267 (Additional file 5) was also morphologically similar to I. jaracimrmani, however, the oocyst size was smaller than in the original description. The sample 108 (Additional file 5) most closely resembled Isospora wildi described by [12] from flapneck chameleon (Chamaeleo dilepis), however, the oocyst size was smaller compared to its original description. In addition, this sample was also similar to Isospora brygooi [13], described from the panther chameleon (Furcifer pardalis) (Additional file 7).

Only one sample (number 002) containing Isospora was obtained from snakes at all, namely from the western rat snake (Pantherophis obsoletus). However, this Isospora did not correspond to any previously described coccidia of the genus Isospora from snakes. Based on morphology, it most closely resembled Isospora cenchoae described by [14] from the Amazon Basin tree snake (Imantodes lentiferus), however, its oocysts were considerably smaller (Additional file 8). This sample was phylogenetically most closely related to Isospora albogularis (Fig. 1), however, both the oocysts and sporocysts were smaller than its original description. Similarly, it was placed on a common branch with the sample number 110 (Fig. 1), which morphologically corresponded to I. jaracimrmani, however, these two samples also differed in oocyst and sporocyst size.

The oocysts in the sample 302 (Additional file 5) from Acanthosaura capra (Squamata: Agamidae) most closely resembled Eimeria cameronensis, which was described from the great anglehead lizard (Gonocephalus grandis) by [15] and shares the same geographical range with A. capra. However, they were also similar to oocysts of the Choleoeimeria glawi [13], which are slightly larger (Additional file 9), and its sequence also clustered within Choleoeimeria (Fig. 1). In the phylogenetic tree, sample 302 was most closely related to Choleoeimeria gallotiae, Choleoeimeria scincorum, and Choleoeimeria wiegmanniana; however, all of these species have larger oocysts and differ in shape [16].

Coccidia included in the samples numbered 001 (Lampropeltis triangulum sinaloae), 002 (P. obsoletus), 011 (L. t. sinaloae), 021 (Pituophis catenifer sayi), 022 (L. getula californiae), 106 (L. alterna blairi), 129 (L. calligaster calligaster), and 213 (L. t. hondurensis) (Additional file 5) were morphologically most similar to Eimeria zamenis described from Coluber sp. by [17], and also recorded in Coluber constrictor flaviventris, L. calligaster calligaster, L. getula helbrooki, L. triangulum triangulum, Masticophis flagellum flagellum, and Masticophis flagellum testaceus [18]. However, oocysts of Eimeria detected in the above mentioned samples were also similar to several other Eimeria species, such as E. leptophis described from Leptophis mexicanus, E. oxybelis described from Oxybelis aenes, E. papillosum described from Salvadora grahaminae lineatus, E. scaphiodontophis described from Scaphiodontophis annulata, E. coniophanes described from Coniophanes fissidens, or to E. coniophis described from Conophis lineatus [19–21] (Additional file 10). In addition to the species mentioned above, the oocysts also resembled those of Choleoeimeria ghaffari described by [22] from Eryx jayakari, but they were slightly larger than the original description. Phylogenetically, all sequences of coccidia from the above mentioned samples clustered within Choleoeimeria spp. from the GenBank database (Fig. 1).

Sequences of coccidia included in samples 134 (Trimeresurus trigonocephalus), 145 (Boaedon fuliginosus), 154 (Acrantophis dumerili), 160 (Morelia spilota), and 264 (Boa constrictor) (Additional file 6) originated from unsporulated oocysts and clustered with Eimeria species described from rodents and rabbits (Fig. 1). Therefore, they most likely represented a passage from ingested (fed) rodents and rabbits. The sequence from the sample 154, obtained from A. dumerili, clustered together with sequences from domestic rabbits; the owner confirmed that the snake was frequently fed rabbits. Oocysts from the sample 264 (Additional file 5) were morphologically identical to Eimeria ferrisi described from the house mouse (Mus musculus) by [23]. Samples 134, 145, and 160 (Additional file 5) clustered with Eimeria papillata also described from M. musculus [24]. However, they were also morphologically similar in shape and size to Eimeria nieschulzi and E. vermiformis described from M. musculus by [24, 25]. All of the above mentioned snakes were routinely fed mice. As all these sequences originated from unsporulated oocysts, it was not possible to assign them morphologically to a particular species.

Regarding clinical manifestations observed in the examined animals, three unrelated individuals of P. vitticeps from different breeds exhibited reduced food intake, decreased activity, and suppressed growth compared to other animals within the same breeds. One of them was infected with I. amphiboluri, and two harboured a coinfection of I. amphiboluri with Ch. pogonae. Two individuals were treated with anticoccidials; their condition improved, and these animals survived. The third individual was not subjected to any therapy and died two months after the onset of clinical signs. Other individuals in this study that tested positive for coccidia showed no apparent clinical signs.

Cryptosporidium

Of the total number of examined faecal samples, oocysts of Cryptosporidium were microscopically detected in 10 samples (10/308; 3.2%) using the centrifugation-flotation concentration method (for more details, see Methods chapter and Additional file 1). DNA was isolated from all such microscopically positive samples, and amplified using three pairs of primers, each pair targeting a different gene. The final alignment of the actin gene was 822 bp Long and contained 29 sequences of Cryptosporidium; four of them originated from reptiles (Additional file 11). The final alignment of gp60 was 939 bp Long and contained 17 sequences of Cryptosporidium; one of them originated from reptiles (Additional file 11). Compared to other alignments analyzed in this study, the gp60 alignment contained so few sequences as only a limited number of sequences of this gene were available in the GenBank database. Unfortunately, amplification of the 18S rRNA gene was not successful.

Only two Cryptosporidium sequences of sufficient quality were obtained. One was for the actin gene, originating from the green pricklenape A. capra (sample 302), which clustered together with the sequence of Cryptosporidium varanii from the GenBank database (EF502043) (Fig. 3). The morphology of the oocysts found in the faeces also corresponded to this species. The second sequence, targeting the gp60 gene, originated from the Honduran milksnake coral (L. triangulum hondurensis; sample 146). It clustered together with sequences from murine cryptosporidia (HM234176 and MT265683) (Fig. 4), which also corresponded with the oocyst size consistent with that of Cryptosporidium tyzzeri. A single individual of a leopard gecko (Eublepharis macularius), newly introduced to an established breeding group, presented with diarrhoea, apathy, and rapid weight loss despite continued food intake. A severe infection with Cryptosporidium was recorded, which was then introduced into the breeding group by this new animal due to the absence of proper quarantine. This individual, as well as four others from the infected breeding, was euthanised. No clinical symptoms were observed in the other individuals found to be infected with Cryptosporidium.

Discussion

The highest prevalence of coccidia (almost 50%) was observed in animals originating from pet trades. Approximately 20% of animals from private breeders were infected. The fewest infections (less than 10%) were recorded in zoo animals (Additional files 3, 4). These results are consistent with current trends and methodologies used in different types of breeding [26, 27]. Zoos implement parasite screening protocols, facilitated by veterinary care and appropriate treatment. This trend has also being adopted in private breedings, although the level of implementation varies across breeders (Additional file 4). However, prevention is often underestimated in pet stores and import companies; in particular, measures against parasitic protists are frequently neglected because they require specialized drugs.

The question remains whether and how protistan infections in reptiles should be treated. Little is known about this problematics, there are no standard guidelines, and all currently available anticoccidial drugs are off-label for reptiles. Moreover, many of these drugs are effective against only a limited range of coccidian genera. In addition, the longevity of coccidian oocysts in the environment, their ease of transmission, and growing resistance to antiprotozoal drugs further complicate the situation [18, 28]. The disease known as coccidiosis (usually manifested by diarrhoea, malabsorption, dehydration, anorexia, weight loss, lethargy, and emaciation; in the case of infection with Choleoeimeria, also with a dilated gall bladder, cholangitis, choleostasis, and liver degeneration) develops as a result of hyperinfection caused by overcrowding of reptiles in captivity, intensive breeding, stress associated with captivity, handling or shipping, and exposure to oocyst contamination in breeding areas. For this reason, prevention is no less important than therapy. Preventative measures include optimal husbandry, facility sanitation, careful handling of animals to prevent contamination with coccidia, veterinary care, and a strict quarantine of newly arrived animals. Folate antagonists (sulfonamides and diaminopyrimidines) and symmetric triazines (toltrazuril, ponazuril) are available to veterinarians for the treatment of coccidiosis [29]. Recommended doses of these anticoccidials for reptiles have been published, for example in [29–34]. It is always advisable to treat all animals within a group. Cryptosporidium, which is genetically distinct from Coccidia and its infection is not self-limiting, can result in severe disease. To date, no universally effective treatment has been developed, including for reptiles. According to [35], the mortality rate associated with Cryptosporidium in reptiles may be as high as 50%. Based on a couple of examples, this study demonstrated that it is worth to administer any anticoccidial to agama lizards exhibiting clinical signs of coccidiosis than not to treat them. Both infected animals treated with anticoccidials survived, while the untreated individual died. A similar outcome was reported by [36], who recorded a treatment success rate of 74% in affected individuals. Using the example of green iguanas in captivity, [37] also found that treating cryptosporidiosis with anticoccidials is worthwhile, as it reduced mortality risk and mitigated the adverse effects of the disease.

The prevalence of coccidia and cryptosporidia in captive reptiles varies in published articles, however, it cannot be generalized, as it depends on a number of factors, particularly the species, number, and origin of the individuals examined. For example, a similar prevalence of Isospora (127/748; 17%) and Cryptosporidium (2/748; 0.5%) in agamid lizards, as in our study, was reported by [36]. These authors also reported a few cases of Choleoeimeria pogonae (0.5%; 4/748), while Choleoeimeria was more prevalent in our samples (Additional file 1). However, high prevalence of Ch. pogonae (100%; 24/24) in P. vitticeps was recorded by [38]. A high prevalence (25.39%) of coccidia in captive bearded dragons was also reported by [39]. However, [40] examined an even larger set of reptiles (949 individuals), and recorded the presence of coccidia and cryptosporidia only in a few samples. In contrast to our findings, [41] detected high prevalence of Cryptosporidium sp. in 56.8% (25/44) and 38.6% (17/44) of captive lizards and snakes, respectively [42] recorded Cryptosporidium infection in elapid and viperid snakes suffering from hypertrophic gastritis, regurgitation, and midbody swelling. A rapidly progressing cryptosporidiosis in leopard geckos (E. macularius), similar to that observed by us, was detected by [43], who reported severe proliferative enteritis in the small intestine of 23/23 individuals.

This study, further underlines the need to combine morphological methods with molecular ones. The oocysts in several isolates clearly corresponded morphologically to particular species (e.g. E. zamenis, I. jaracimrmani), however, no molecular confirmation/comparison was available as the original isolates from which the species were described had not been sequenced. This fact further opens the question of the host specificity of the members of Coccidia, which have long been considered highly host-specific. For example, we recorded I. amphiboluri, which was supposed to be specific to agamas (P. vitticeps), also in the veiled chameleons (Ch. calyptratus) originating from a different breeding source than the positive agamas (both flotation and molecular analyses were performed at a different time than those for agamas). This may suggest that I. amphiboluri possesses a broader host specificity than previously expected. This finding is, therefore, important not only from a taxonomic but also from an ecological-evolutionary point of view.

No less important is the question of passage/pseudoinfection. Doubts have already been raised in the past that some oocysts found in faeces may only merely pass through the gastrointestinal tract of a non-specific host or of prey, and therefore represent only a passage [18, 44–47]. Most species of Coccidia have been described based solely on the morphology of their most readily available developmental stage, the sporulated oocyst; knowledge on their life cycles and endogenous development remains unknown. Several attempts to verify the host specificity such as experimental cross-transmission studies have been made in the past, however, these often yielded conflicting results [23, 48–50]. Further insight was provided by [51], who pointed out the need to combine the morphological, phylogenetic, and experimental analyses to demonstrate that the Isospora oocysts recorded in bank voles (Clethrionomys glareolus) represent only pseudoparasites of the bank voles, most likely of the avian origin. Similarly, in our study, we recorded oocysts of Eimeria in several species of snakes. The phylogenetic analyses placed these Eimeria among eimerians described from mice and/or rabbits (Figs. 1, 2). As these snakes were confirmed to have been fed mice and rabbits, the recorded eimerians represent only a passage of oocysts from the fed animals through the gastrointestinal tract of snakes. A similar case also involves the gp60 sequence of Cryptosporidium sp. isolated from L. t. hondurensis, clustering within the murine cryptosporidia (Fig. 4). Therefore, the combination of morphological and molecular methods enables, among other benefits, to reveal pseudoinfections – otherwise difficult to identify – which may potentially lead to erroneous descriptions of new species. On the other hand, in future studies, more focus should be placed on the parasites of endangered and endemic species of reptiles, as well as on the design of primers better matching the 18S rRNA of reptile cryptosporidia. Definitely, attention should also be paid to helminth parasites.

Conclusion

The presented study demonstrates that captive reptiles—particularly those from pet trades—are frequently infected with protistan parasites. Due to the nature of these parasites, they can be easily transmitted within breeding facilities and the surrounding environment. We also emphasized that examination of the faecal samples of pet reptiles, which is a non-invasive procedure, followed by targeted treatment of infected individuals, is better for their health and survival compared to untreated individuals. In addition, the detected parasites were identified based on both morphology and sequencing. This also enabled to assess the host specificity of the recorded parasites and its flexibility of some of them, as well as the pseudoinfections with parasites of the prey (mice, rabbits), which cannot be confirmed without the use of molecular methods. This study, based on large-scale sampling across diverse host genera and families from various sources, highlights the importance of not neglecting parasites in captive reptiles. On the other hand, in future studies, more focus should be placed on the parasites of endangered and endemic species of reptiles, as well as on the design of primers better matching the 18S rRNA of reptile cryptosporidia. Definitely, attention should also be paid to helminth parasites.

Methods

Sampling

Faecal samples of reptiles were collected from live captive animals from multiple sources – zoos, private breeding facilities, and pet trades (for individual species and their numbers, see the Results chapter and Additional file 1). Pet trade refers to a pet store or an animal exchange. Private breeding refers to breeding by an owner who has kept the reptile in permanent care for more than one year as a pet, or who has bred its offspring. The faecal sampling was carried out in accordance with the valid Czech legislation, i.e. Act on Veterinary Care and Amendment of Certain Related Acts (Veterinary Act) No. 166/1999 Coll.; the animals were not part of any experimental work. The faecal samples were collected as fresh as possible in sterile sampling tubes, and preserved and stored in 2.5% potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) solution at 4 °C. For small-volume samples (e.g., geckos, chameleons, skinks), the entire sample was collected for analyses; for large-volume samples (e.g., snakes, tortoises, crocodiles), three hazelnut-sized subsamples from different parts of the dropping were collected for analyses. Half of each collected sample was used for morphological examination, and the other half for molecular analysis.

Coprological examination, morphological analyses

The faecal samples were examined by the standard centrifugation-flotation concentration method with modified Sheather’s sucrose solution (specific gravity 1.30) [52] followed by light microscopy. Samples positive for coccidian and cryptosporidian oocysts were analysed morphologically and morphometrically according to the guidelines and criteria published by [40, 53] using an Olympus BX53 light microscope equipped with a DP-73–1-51 high-resolution, cooled digital camera, and Olympus cellSens Standard v.1.13 imaging software (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Molecular and phylogenetic analyses

Genomic DNA was extracted from faecal samples using the FastDNA® Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, LLC, Santa Ana, California, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For coccidia, the isolated DNA was amplified with primers targeting partial sequences of the 18S rRNA and COI genes as described in [54, 55], respectively. In the case of Cryptosporidium, PCR amplification was performed with primers targeting partial sequences of 18S rRNA [56], actin [57], and 60-kDa glycoprotein (gp60) [58] genes. Positive controls (Eimeria apionodes for coccidia, Cryptosporidium sciurinum for cryptosporidia) and negative controls (PCR-grade H2O instead of extracted DNA) were included in each PCR reaction set. Moreover, in most cases, samples from different animals and different origins were examined and amplified at different times, which further reduced the risk of cross-contamination. The PCR products were visualized by electrophoresis in 1% agarose gel stained with GelRed® Nucleic Acid Stain (50 ml/2 µl; Biotium). Amplicons of expected sizes were purified using FastAP and Exo I enzymes (Thermo Scientific, USA), and sent to a commercial company (Macrogen Europe, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) for Sanger sequencing.

The obtained sequences were verified by BLAST [59], assembled and edited with SequenceScanner v.1.0 (Applied Biosystems) and DNASTAR v.5.05 program package (DNASTAR, Inc., Madison, Wisconsin, USA), and deposited in the GenBank database under the following accession numbers: PQ137460-PQ137468, PQ144684-PQ144689, PQ145972, PQ284145-PQ284150 (18S rDNA); PQ146480, PQ146481, PQ376569-PQ376577 (COI); PQ287448 (actin); and PQ287449 (gp60). Alignments were performed and adjusted using BioEdit v.7.0.5.3 program [60]. The 18S rDNA sequences were aligned in nucleotide mode; the COI, actin, and gp60 sequences were aligned in the amino acid mode, then switched to nucleotide mode, and used for the analyses. Phylogenetic analyses were reconstructed using two approaches: maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) computed in PhyML v.2.4.3 [61] and MrBayes v.3.2.2 [62], respectively. The most suitable model of evolution was determined using the SMS: Smart Model Selection software [63, 64]. ML analysis was performed using the GTR + Γ + I evolutionary model and the non-parametric bootstrap analysis of 1,000 replicates. BI was performed with parameters corresponding to the selected model (GTR + Γ + I), MCMC run for 10 million generations, and tree sampling every 100 generations; the trees were summarized after removing a 25% burn-in. Final trees were visualized and exported using TreeView v.1.6.6 [65], and further adjusted in Adobe Illustrator 2020 (Adobe Systems, Inc.).
