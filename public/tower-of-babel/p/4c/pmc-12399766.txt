Multimodal dataset for wind turbine blade monitoring during lightning strikes
Authors: Tao Li, Chenxi Li, Yuxin Qin, Long Tan, Bang Jiang, Hang Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLightning strikes pose a significant threat to the structural integrity and operational performance of wind turbine blades. Due to the high probability of lightning strikes but the difficulty in capturing their dynamic data, obtaining comprehensive data on blades subjected to lightning strikes is challenging. This study presents a rare multimodal dataset for wind turbine blade monitoring during lightning strikes (MDWTBM-LS). It includes vibration, load, and strain measurements from fiber optic load sensors and fiber optic accelerometer sensors. It provides real-time records of blade operational status and damage assessments after strikes. The dataset is intended to promote the development of predictive models for lightning strike detection and blade damage assessment, ultimately enhancing the monitoring, maintenance, and operational optimization of wind energy facilities. The dataset is publicly available for researchers to conduct in-depth studies in fields such as machine learning, condition monitoring, and fault prediction, contributing to the resilience and reliability of wind turbine operations.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Energy access, Technology

Background & Summary

Wind energy is one of the most important sustainable and renewable energy sources. The ongoing expansion of wind power generation not only significantly enhances its strategic position in modern power systems but also serves as a key driver in the global transition to a low-carbon energy structure1–3. Lightning strikes pose a highly destructive threat to wind turbine blades4–6. The probability of blade lightning strikes is relatively high, and such events can cause significant damage, including cracks, delamination, fractures, and structural failures, leading to substantial repair costs and downtime losses7–9. As a result, effective monitoring and predictive maintenance strategies are essential. In response, international technical standards and guidelines have been proposed for lightning protection of wind power equipment10, along with research on composite material improvements, down-conductor designs, and receptor layouts to mitigate potential lightning damage to wind power systems11–13. Due to the high frequency of lightning strikes but the difficulty in obtaining related data, damage data from lightning strikes on wind turbine blades is relatively scarce. Effective lightning monitoring and fault prediction are critical for ensuring the optimal operation and maintenance of wind energy equipment.

Operation and maintenance monitoring of wind turbine equipment primarily focuses on parameters such as load, vibration and stress. Through these parameters, professional assessments and equipment analyses are conducted to determine the operational status of wind power14. Currently, most wind-power-related datasets on popular platforms like Kaggle and Our World in data emphasize wind power prediction. For example, the Wind Turbine SCADA15 and Wind Power Generation Data16 datasets provide operational status records for wind turbine equipment, but data on extreme environmental impacts such as lightning strikes is exceedingly rare.

To address this issue, multiple datasets in the wind power field have been developed and widely applied, providing strong support for the fault diagnosis, performance evaluation, and operational maintenance management of wind power equipment. The dataset in Table 1 not only includes the basic operational parameters of wind turbines but also covers the performance of wind energy equipment under various environmental conditions. These datasets are widely used in the fields of health monitoring, fault prediction, and operational maintenance management of wind turbines. However, data related to extreme weather events such as lightning strikes on wind turbine blades is relatively scarce, posing a challenge to the long-term stability and reliability of the equipment. Therefore, the continuous optimization and expansion of such datasets are crucial for enhancing the reliability and safety of wind energy equipment.

In the context of data-driven approaches, machine learning methods can utilize the aforementioned parameters to predict the operational state and diagnose faults in wind turbine equipment. For example, Jinjing An et al.17 used SCADA data and employed tree-based models along with unsupervised statistical learning techniques for fuzzy reliability assessment and fault prediction in wind turbines. Kuan-Cheng Lin et al.18 applied deep learning to a five-year dataset of wind turbines provided by their company to achieve early-stage fault prediction. Rahimilarki et al.19 proposed a convolutional neural network-based fault classification method for wind turbines using time-series analysis, achieving high classification accuracy in simulations on a 4.8 MW benchmark system by converting sensor and actuator data into 2D grayscale images. Colone et al.20 proposed a method that relies solely on signal frequency to detect mass changes in wind turbine blades, integrating statistical pattern recognition methods. In addition, previous studies21–23 have employed various types of wind power datasets or simulated data in combination with machine learning techniques to investigate fault diagnosis and wind power prediction. However, due to the difficulty of acquiring lightning strike data, research on fault diagnosis of wind turbine blades under lightning conditions remains limited, leaving a gap in fault diagnosis under extreme operating scenarios within the wind power domain.

Wind power systems are mainly deployed in complex, high-altitude mountainous regions. Limited monitoring conditions make it extremely difficult to obtain complete state information and multimodal data during lightning attachment events. To address this challenge, this study provides a rare and valuable dataset of wind turbine blade condition monitoring during lightning strike events, with the following key contributions:

Methods

This study utilizes an online monitoring system installed on wind turbine blades, aiming to continuously measure and record various operational parameters of the wind turbine blades, including wind speed, power, angle of variable propeller and more. After the incident, the blade monitoring data was extracted, with particular focus on the period before the incident, during which the blade load exceeded the limit and exhibited abnormal fluctuations. Through further trace-back analysis of the blade monitoring data, wind speed, power, pitch angle, and turbine operation were recorded at different time intervals. Specifically, attention was given to several critical time periods when abnormal blade load and strain were observed. During these abnormal periods, mismatches between wind speed, pitch angle, and power, as well as abnormal blade load data, were observed. These anomalies may indicate potential damage to the blade structure. Such monitoring data can serve as a valuable foundation for machine learning-based fault diagnosis and intelligent operation and maintenance strategies.

In order to ensure the safe and stable operation of the wind turbine and to detect potential fault risks in a timely manner, multiple advanced sensors were installed on the wind turbine before the incident to conduct comprehensive monitoring. Real-time acquisition of key operational parameters, precise tracking of various components of the turbine can be achieved, allowing for early warning of potential faults. The main methods employed are as follows:

Strain Sensor Monitoring. Strain sensors monitor the bending moment at different sections of the blades in real-time. By converting strain data into load data, the loads on different parts of the blades are continuously monitored, enabling the detection of any abnormal loads at various locations.

Vibration Sensor Monitoring. Vibration sensors are employed to capture the actual operating acceleration values, accumulating the normal operating acceleration levels. By obtaining vibration signals during operation, high-order blade mode information can be acquired. Combined with the design model analysis, this allows for high-order feature recognition. Additionally, under the same operating conditions, by analyzing the load and low-order natural frequency differences, it is possible to assess whether there is significant stiffness damage to the blades.

Sensors

Three sections of the selected blade were chosen for load and vibration monitoring purposes. At the root of the blade, strain sensors were installed to monitor the strain in both the spanwise and chordwise directions. At the 1/3 and 2/3 positions along the blade length, strain sensors and accelerometers were deployed to monitor strain and vibration in the spanwise and chordwise directions, respectively. Take the installation process of the load sensor as an example. Before installing the load sensor, the performance of the sensor and the data acquisition channel should be verified to ensure that the sensor and the system are in good condition, thereby guaranteeing the accuracy of the lightning strike response data. The installation process followed a standardized protocol, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Firstly, by determining the positioning line and grinding the target area to remove surface impurities, ensuring that the attachment surface of the sensor is flat. Secondly, draw contour lines of the position to ensure consistent installation height and reduce measurement errors caused by local undulations. After completing the position processing, use high-strength adhesive materials to bond the sensor, ensuring its close contact with the blade surface. Finally, after the bonding is completed, perform installation and thermal curing treatment to enhance the bonding strength and installation stability. This process can effectively improve the durability and data reliability of the sensor in complex environments. The installation methods for other types of sensors are similar, and adjustments are made according to specific working conditions.

Transfer system

The blade condition monitoring system, based on fiber-optic MEMS sensing technology, primarily consists of fiber optic load sensors, MEMS fiber optic accelerometers, a fiber optic sensor analyzer, a diagnostic host, a back-end server, and other components. Data is transmitted and communicated via a bus. Through data fusion and integration, the diagnostic system generates standardized, application-specific data. After multilayer processing, which includes the data layer, application layer, and presentation layer, the server ultimately provides production management and operational guidance and displays the equipment’s health status. The blade’s last recorded change took place between 11:13 and 11:19 on August 16, during which the load exceeded its limit and showed abnormal fluctuations. From the blade monitoring data, it can be observed that the turbine underwent multiple start-stop cycles in different time periods, with wind speed, power, and angle of variable propeller all exhibiting irregular variations. Specifically, the turbine transitioned from normal operation to a fault state, going through repeated self-start attempts until a complete shutdown. Notably, in the critical time window, load and strain displayed anomalies, indicating that the turbine had entered a fault condition.

Failure time period analysis

Reviewing the data from 3:20 to 4:20, the blade’s operating parameters showed no significant changes under normal conditions, and the relationships among power, rotational speed and angle of variable propeller appeared normal. At this point, the blade was likely in a stable state, with no evident damage to the main structure or the airfoil. Comparison with data prior to 4:20 indicates no marked anomalies. Although the possibility of minor inaccuracies in the monitoring system cannot be ruled out, the likelihood of severe blade damage before that time was relatively low.

At 4:20, the blade load data, acceleration data, and vibration data all exhibited pronounced irregularities, suggesting that the blade was subjected to an abnormal external force. An emergency shutdown of the blade ensued. Following this abnormal shutdown, the turbine experienced multiple start-stop cycles at 4:30, 4:49, 7:42, and 8:54, which further exacerbated the blade’s damage and progressively reduced the correlation between power, rotational speed and angle of variable propeller. By 10:10, large-scale damage had begun to manifest, as evidenced by noticeably abnormal load measurements. At 11:13, the blade suffered a complete fracture. Combined with on-site inspection, it was confirmed that the blade was struck by lightning at 4:20. The subsequent frequent start-stop cycles caused the lightning damage to continue spreading, ultimately culminating in total blade failure at 11:13. Around 4:21, load levels oscillate violently, with amplitudes quickly exceeding normal operational limits. These anomalous readings strongly suggest that the turbine encountered severe interference caused by the lightning strike, resulting in abnormal load data. Continued monitoring and analysis reveal that neither the anomalous load nor the strain fluctuations subside over a short timeframe, implying that the turbine did not revert to normal operating conditions following the impact. This persistence of irregular behavior raises the possibility of serious structural damage.

Fig. 5(g–i) present the vibration, load, and strain data recorded from 7:42 to 8:30, capturing further structural damage sustained by the wind turbine blades following a lightning strike. During this period, all three parameters display substantial fluctuations. Vibration measurements at both 19.5 m and 41 m show pronounced oscillations, indicating severe structural harm and vigorous vibration responses. In particular, the 19.5 m position shows larger amplitudes, possibly due to a stronger localized impact from the lightning strike, leading to uneven oscillations across the blade.

Load data also reveal significant volatility, indicating abnormal mechanical stress likely linked to sudden wind speed variations and blade damage induced by the lightning. The synchronized fluctuations in load and vibration imply that the compromised blade structure hampers the turbine’s ability to adapt to changes in wind speed and load, thereby heightening operational instability. Moreover, strain readings at 19.5 m and 41 m undergo marked and rapid changes, especially in the minutes immediately following the lightning event, when strain values spike sharply. This abrupt escalation indicates pronounced blade deformation driven by the transient high temperatures and electrical discharge characteristic of the lightning strike, highlighting the extent of structural damage incurred.

Fig. 5(j–l) present data collected between 11:13 and 11:19, during which the turbine blade’s vibration, load, and strain readings show intense fluctuations. The interval from 11:15 to 11:17, in particular, features abrupt changes in all three parameters, highlighting the severe operational consequences of blade fracture. Vibration signals at 19.5 m and 41 m spike dramatically, showing brief yet pronounced surges. In parallel, load measurements become increasingly erratic, with sudden jumps indicating that the blade fracture caused an unstable load distribution. The turbine’s control system could not adjust swiftly to these abrupt load changes, as evidenced by the close synchronization of load and vibration fluctuations.

Strain data likewise escalate rapidly from 11:13 to 11:15, highlighting marked blade deformation. The 19.5 m sensor records especially pronounced strain oscillations, suggestive of higher external stress at this position. Collectively, these findings reveal how blade fracture compromises overall turbine operation. The sharp shifts in vibration, load, and strain indicate a loss of structural stability post-fracture, preventing uniform load redistribution and triggering further mechanical damage that ultimately leading to complete blade failure.

Data Records

The MDWTBM-LS dataset includes a comprehensive set of wind turbine blade sensor measurements and operational parameters, covering the period from 00:00 on the day of the incident to 15:13 after the fault occurred, available at 10.6084/m9.figshare.28879679.v5. This dataset is publicly available in csv format24. As shown in Fig. 6, the dataset is organized into a structured directory to ensure ease of access and interpretation. It consists of six primary folders: Vibration data, Load data, Extended Load data, Strain data, Extended Strain data, and Temperature. Each data file is formatted with the following columns: Sensor Type, Deployment Location, Sensor Value, Time, Wind Speed, Rotate Speed, Angle of Variable Propeller, and Wind Power.

The vibration data was collected from two directions at the 1/3 and 2/3 positions of the wind turbine blade, including the response information in the flapping direction and the pitching direction. In the Vibration data section, there are a total of four sets of vibration sensor data. This part of the data reflects the vibration changes of the blade at different positions before and after lightning strikes, providing valuable support for dynamic characteristic analysis research.

The load data is mainly divided into the sensor positions on the blade tip and the sensor positions at the blade root. Specifically, the Load data includes four sets of data for the pitch and heave directions at the 1/3 and 2/3 positions of the blade. The Extended Load data additionally provides two sets of load sensor data for the pitch and heave directions at the blade root, which is helpful for in-depth research on the load correlation between the blade and the blade root.

Technical Validation

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the wind turbine monitoring data presented in this study, a technical verification was performed by comparing the fault-day records with the historical operational data of the same turbine. Fig. 7 illustrates the trends in vibration, load, and strain measurements recorded on the fault day as well as the preceding and following days. Since environmental factors, such as wind speed, are highly correlated with the blade’s mechanical characteristics, the figure is subdivided into distinct wind speed intervals.

Trends of vibration, load, and strain monitoring data on the fault day, the day before, and the day after.

Within the same wind speed range, the vibration, load, and strain data exhibit comparable fluctuations and oscillations, indicating a consistent pattern between the normal operating data which is recorded prior to the fault and historic baseline measurements. Moreover, post-fault data captured from the onset of the incident until the blade was completely damaged closely mirror the readings obtained the day after the fault, confirming the consistency of the fault data type. These findings affirm that the recorded data accurately reflect both normal and fault conditions of the wind turbine, thus validating their use for the analysis presented in this study.

To further validate the representativeness of data under normal operating conditions, we compared the same wind speed intervals across different datasets, as shown in Fig. 8. The box plots indicate that, within the same wind speed range, the distribution of historical data is essentially aligned with measurements obtained on the day of the fault. In contrast, the data recorded during the fault interval remain nearly constant, a consequence of blade damage from the lightning strike that prevented the turbine from functioning normally. These observations agree with the broader trend in Fig. 7, highlighting that our dataset accurately reflects both routine and fault scenarios. Consequently, the analyses in this study demonstrate high accuracy, generalizability, and applicability, offering robust support for intelligent monitoring and maintenance of wind turbines.

This study focuses on the state response and data monitoring of wind turbine blades under lightning strike conditions, and constructs a lightning strike monitoring dataset with multimodal and temporal consistency. This dataset not only fills the gap in the field of wind power lightning strike research but also provides a data basis for the fault assessment and intelligent diagnosis of wind power equipment. The main conclusions are as follows:

The recorded lightning strike data were obtained from natural lightning events during real wind farm operations, covering the entire process response pre-strike, strike, and post-strike. This dataset possesses strong scarcity and representativeness. It features high-quality acquisition, event integrity, and temporal continuity, providing reliable support for modeling event responses under extreme operating conditions.

The recorded lightning strike data were obtained from natural lightning events during real wind farm operations, covering the entire process response pre-strike, strike, and post-strike. This dataset possesses strong scarcity and representativeness. It features high-quality acquisition, event integrity, and temporal continuity, providing reliable support for modeling event responses under extreme operating conditions.

The data collected by multimodal sensor sensors show obvious modal differences. It exhibits significant nonlinear and time-varying change trends in the load, strain, and vibration channels before and after lightning strikes. The data synchronization processing achieved through a unified acquisition platform ensures temporal alignment and structural consistency among variables, providing a solid foundation for extracting system features and studying state evolution under complex working conditions.
