Clinical outcomes of paediatric home ventilation in South Korea: liberation and mortality
Authors: Jae Won Moon, Ji Eun Jang, Min Jeong Lee, Myung Yeon Lee, Kyunghoon Kim, June Dong Park, Ji Weon Lee, Min Sun Kim, Ji Soo Park, Dong In Suh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe number of children receiving home ventilation (HV) has substantially increased, many with neuromuscular diseases (NMDs). Understanding outcomes in this population is critical for guiding care and policy decisions. However, real-world data from South Korea are scarce, limiting evaluation of the 2016 national insurance expansion.ObjectivesWe aimed to investigate characteristics and outcomes of paediatric HV after insurance expansion, focusing on differences by NMD status and to identify key factors associated with HV liberation and mortality.MethodsWe conducted a single-centre retrospective cohort study at Seoul National University Children’s Hospital. We included children aged <19 years who received a Health Insurance or Medical Aid Ventilator Prescription between 1 January 2016 and 30 September 2024. Primary outcomes were HV liberation and all-cause mortality. Kaplan-Meier estimates with log-rank tests were used for between-group comparisons. Multivariable Cox regression was applied to identify factors associated with outcomes, reporting adjusted HRs (aHRs) and 95% CIs.ResultsAmong 316 children, 89 (28.2%) had NMDs. During follow-up, 71 (22.5%) were liberated and 81 (25.6%) died. Reduced likelihood of liberation was associated with NMD, home nursing care and gastrostomy, whereas oxygen use at HV initiation increased likelihood. NMD was not significantly associated with mortality (aHR 0.97 (95% CI 0.48 to 1.96)). Mortality risk was reduced with preterm birth, home nursing care and airway anomalies, but increased with antiseizure medication and continuous ventilation.ConclusionNMD was linked to lower liberation likelihood but not mortality. Several other clinical factors were associated with outcomes, underscoring the need for individualised care strategies for paediatric HV.Keywords: Paediatric Lung Disease, Assisted Ventilation, Patient Outcome Assessment

Introduction

Use of home ventilation (HV) in children has increased markedly with technological advances in the past few decades.1,7 The type and goal of ventilatory support vary according to the pathophysiological mechanisms of respiratory failure.8 Accordingly, the clinical indications for HV in children are heterogeneous, including respiratory muscle weakness, impaired ventilatory drive, chronic lung disease, upper airway obstruction or the need for prolonged support following acute respiratory failure. While ventilatory support in potentially reversible conditions may serve as temporary bridging towards recovery or liberation, in progressive or severe disorders it often aims to provide prolonged or life-prolonging assistance. Neuromuscular diseases (NMDs), which are primarily characterised by respiratory muscle weakness,9 10 are among the most common indications for HV in children.411,13 Given their prevalence and distinct clinical profiles, it is imperative to investigate differences in HV-related outcomes between children with and without NMDs.

As paediatric HV becomes more widely used, the associated healthcare costs and caregiving demands have imposed a substantial burden on families.14 15 In January 2016, South Korea implemented expanded National Health Insurance (NHI) coverage to include HV prescriptions, increasing the paediatric HV population. However, data on the outcomes of expanded paediatric HV in Korea remain limited.

Previous studies have described baseline characteristics, including age at HV initiation, sex, diagnostic categories, ventilator interface type and mortality, in children using HV.1,47 8 12 13 16 17 Nonetheless, factors associated with HV outcomes and the relationship between clinical characteristics and long-term outcomes in paediatric HV remain unclear. Additionally, current guidelines primarily provide general recommendations for clinical management and eligibility criteria, with limited guidance on prognostic factors or individualised decision-making.18 19

To address these research gaps, we aimed to investigate the real-world characteristics and clinical outcomes of children receiving HV in Korea in accordance with their NMD status. Furthermore, we aimed to identify key factors associated with HV liberation and mortality to inform the development of individualised paediatric HV care strategies. To our knowledge, this is the largest cohort study on the outcomes of paediatric HV in Asia.

Methods

Study design and population

This single-centre retrospective cohort study was conducted at Seoul National University Children’s Hospital in South Korea. Data were extracted from the institutional clinical data warehouse. We included children who received Health Insurance Ventilator Prescription or Medical Aid Ventilator Prescription between 1 January 2016 and 30 September 2024. Patients aged <19 years at the time of their first prescription were considered eligible. We excluded patients who had initiated HV before 1 January 2016 and those who did not actually receive HV following the prescription. Patients who were prescribed a home ventilator but using continuous positive airway pressure or continuous flow modes only were also excluded. Included patients were divided into two groups based on their NMD status, which was determined using International Classification of Diseases 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes documented before HV initiation (online supplemental figure 1).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design or conduct of the study.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcomes were cumulative probabilities of HV liberation and all-cause mortality at 1, 3, 5 and 7 years after HV initiation, estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods. HV liberation was defined as discontinuation of HV without any reapplication during the follow-up period. All-cause mortality included any death occurring after HV initiation, regardless of cause. Mortality data were obtained from the national death registry, including both in-hospital and out-of-hospital deaths. Secondary outcomes included time to HV liberation and time to death. Additionally, we compared changes in the length of hospital stay (total admission days), number of respiratory-related hospitalisations and number of respiratory-related emergency department visits in the 1-year periods before and after HV initiation. For the hospitalisation during which HV was initiated, admission days were assigned to the pre-HV or post-HV period according to the initiation date. We assessed pre-HV and post-HV measurements of end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) obtained from arterial or venous samples, with pre–post comparisons using the same sampling method within each patient. All follow-up and outcome data were retrospectively collected through 30 September 2024, which served as the cut-off date for all analyses.

Definitions of variables

Baseline characteristics included age, weight and height at HV initiation, as well as sex, gestational age, birth weight and the HV initiation date. Weight and height Z-scores were calculated using WHO growth standards (<36 months) and the 2017 Korean National Growth Charts (≥36 months). Gestational age was categorised as preterm (<37 weeks) or term (≥37 weeks). Birth weight was categorised as low birth weight (<2500 g) or normal birth weight (≥2500 g) according to the WHO criteria. Comorbidities such as cardiac disease, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and airway anomalies, including laryngomalacia, tracheomalacia, bronchomalacia and subglottic stenosis were documented. Cardiac disease was defined as any condition classified in the ‘I’ range of ICD-10 codes. Any pre-HV aspiration events were recorded

In addition, we collected data regarding home nursing care (hospital-affiliated home nursing teams or home medical clinics), mechanical insufflation-exsufflation (MIE), gastrostomy and antiseizure medication (ASM). Baseline home nursing care and MIE were defined as being prescribed before discharge from the hospitalisation in which HV was initiated. Contrastingly, baseline gastrostomy and ASM were defined as those present at HV initiation.

Clinical factors related to HV initiation included interface type (tracheostomy or mask (nasal or facial)), ventilator use pattern (continuous, indicating 24 hours use or intermittent), oxygen supply and ventilator settings (mode, inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP), expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP), tidal volume (TV) and respiratory rate (RR)). Ventilator mode was categorised as pressure control when IPAP exceeded EPAP, indicating the presence of pressure support (PS); volume control when a TV setting was present; and combined mode when both PS and TV settings were present.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and comparative analyses

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables are described as medians with IQRs. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Pearson χ² and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. When expected cell counts were <5, Fisher’s exact test was used. Within-group pre–post HV comparisons were performed using the McNemar and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for categorical and continuous data, respectively. For continuous variables, the median of the paired differences with IQRs was reported. Between-group comparisons of pre–post changes were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

Survival analysis

The cumulative probabilities of the primary outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with between-group comparisons using log-rank tests. These estimates were visualised using cumulative incidence plots. Censoring for HV liberation occurred at death, follow-up loss or study end, whichever came first; for mortality, at follow-up loss or study end. For sensitivity analysis, the Fine and Gray model was used to account for death as a competing risk. The cumulative incidence function curves from this model are presented.

Factors related to primary outcomes

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards (PHs) regression models were used to estimate HRs and 95% CIs for liberation and mortality, with adjustment for potential confounders. Patients with missing data or covariates recorded after the outcome events were excluded from each analysis. Multivariable models included the NMD group as the primary variable of interest; further, they were used to identify other independent factors associated with each outcome. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance, prior literature2,416 and statistical significance in univariate analysis (p value <0.05). Multicollinearity was assessed by calculating variance inflation factors (VIFs). Linearity of age at HV initiation was assessed using Martingale residuals. The PHs assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals. In the HV liberation model, gestational age (in weeks) violated the PHs assumption and was included as a stratification variable. In the mortality model, MIE violated the PHs assumption and was modelled as a time-varying covariate using an interaction term between MIE and log(time+1).

All statistical tests were two-sided, with statistical significance set at p value <0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Characteristics of the study cohort

We included 316 children (median (IQR) age at HV initiation, 3.0 (0.0–10.0) years; 180 boys (57.0%), 136 girls (43.0%)). Among them, 89 (28.2%) had NMDs and 227 (71.8%) did not. Within the NMD group, spinal muscular atrophy (32.6%) and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (20.2%) were the predominant diagnoses. In the non-NMD group, central nervous system diseases (30.1%), including hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy and central nervous system tumours, were most common, followed by genetic or congenital disorders (19.0%) and respiratory diseases (18.7%). There was no significant between-group difference in the distribution of the year of HV initiation (p=0.75; online supplemental figure 2).

There was significant between-group difference in age, weight and height at HV initiation, as well as gestational age and birth weight. The NMD group had a higher median age at HV initiation than the non-NMD group (9.0 years vs 2.0 year; p=0.003). Preterm birth and low birth weight (<2500 g) were more common in the non-NMD group (35.2% vs 14.6% and 34.4% vs 13.5%, respectively; both p<0.001). Furthermore, there were significant between-group differences in comorbidities and the use of supportive interventions, including home nursing care, gastrostomy, MIE and ASM, at baseline. After the follow-up period, these differences remained significant for all interventions except gastrostomy (table 1).

Primary outcomes

The cumulative probability of liberation from HV was significantly lower in the NMD group than in the non-NMD group (log-rank p<0.001; figure 1A). The estimated cumulative probabilities of liberation at 1, 3, 5 and 7 years were 0.0%, 4.4%, 10.1% and 14.8% in the NMD group, compared with 23.6%, 35.0%, 37.9% and 37.9% in the non-NMD group (table 3). In contrast, the cumulative probability of death was significantly higher in the non-NMD group over the same period (log-rank p=0.02; figure 1B), with corresponding estimates of 14.2%, 25.9%, 34.4% and 53.4%, compared with 11.1%, 16.1%, 21.0% and 23.1% in the NMD group. Overall, 71 (22.5%) patients were liberated from HV and 81 (25.6%) died during follow-up.

In the sensitivity analysis of the entire cohort accounting for death as a competing risk, the 1-year cumulative incidences of liberation and death were 16.5% and 11.5%, respectively (online supplemental figure 3). Given the limited number of liberation events in the NMD group, stratified competing risk analyses by NMD status were not performed. Online supplemental table 1 describes the clinical characteristics of the eight patients with NMD who successfully achieved liberation from HV.

Secondary outcomes

In the time-to-event analysis, both the median time to liberation and death showed no significant between-group difference. The length of hospital stay significantly decreased during the 1-year period after HV initiation in the non-NMD group (82.0 vs 23.0 days, p<0.001), whereas no significant change was observed in the NMD group (28.0 vs 22.0 days, p=0.07). Furthermore, the frequency of respiratory-related admissions and emergency department visits did not differ significantly between the groups. There were also no significant post-HV changes in EtCO2, PaCO2 and partial pressure of venous carbon dioxide (PvCO2) (table 3).

Factors associated with HV liberation and mortality

The multivariable Cox regression models for liberation and mortality included 296 and 304 patients, respectively. In these models, all VIFs for multicollinearity tests were <2, indicating low multicollinearity among the involved variables. In the HV liberation multivariable model, NMD group was negatively associated with liberation compared with non-NMD group (adjusted HR (aHR) 0.32 (95% CI 0.14 to 0.77); p=0.01; figure 2A). Home nursing care (aHR 0.48 (95% CI 0.27 to 0.85); p=0.01) and gastrostomy (aHR 0.38 (95% CI 0.19 to 0.74); p=0.004) were associated with a decreased likelihood of liberation. Contrastingly, initial oxygen supply was associated with an increased likelihood of liberation (aHR 2.63 (95% CI 1.29 to 5.39); p=0.01).

In the adjusted mortality model, NMD group was not significantly associated with mortality (aHR 0.97 (95% CI 0.48 to 1.96); p=0.94; figure 2B). In a time-interaction analysis, the effect of MIE on mortality demonstrated a shifting pattern over time. Initially, MIE use at HV initiation showed a non-significant tendency towards an increased mortality risk (aHR 9.37 (95% CI 0.40 to 219.93), p=0.16). However, this association appeared to attenuate as the follow-up period progressed, as suggested by the interaction with log-transformed time, although it did not reach statistical significance in this analysis (aHR 0.45 (95% CI 0.19 to 1.06); p=0.07). Regarding gestational age, mortality was lower in preterm (12.9%) than in term children (29.9%), with preterm birth being associated with a significantly lower mortality risk (aHR 0.33 (95% CI 0.17 to 0.64); p=0.001). Additionally, home nursing care (aHR 0.53 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.89); p=0.02) and airway anomalies (aHR 0.54 (95% CI 0.30 to 0.94); p=0.03) were associated with a decreased mortality risk. Contrastingly, ASM (aHR 1.83 (95% CI 1.01 to 3.33); p=0.046) and continuous ventilation (aHR 14.12 (95% CI 3.21 to 62.16); p<0.001) were associated with a significantly increased mortality risk.

Discussion

Our findings indicated that NMD was associated with significantly decreased probability of HV liberation and mortality. After adjustment, NMD remained independently associated with a lower likelihood of liberation. However, the lower mortality risk initially observed in the NMD group was no longer significant in the multivariable model, likely reflecting underlying differences in baseline disease profiles and clinical complexity between the NMD and non-NMD groups.

The observed between-group difference in the likelihood of liberation is consistent with previous reports of lower liberation rates in children with NMDs than in those with chronic respiratory diseases.1 17 The progressive and irreversible nature of NMDs, marked by respiratory muscle weakness, likely limits the possibility of liberation.20 While disease-modifying therapies may affect the disease course in selected neuromuscular conditions, their long-term impact on ventilation outcomes has yet to be fully established. Given the limited likelihood of liberation in the NMD group, it is important to confirm that families fully understand the anticipated clinical course during the shared decision-making process to start HV.

Notably, the non-NMD group showed a significant post-HV decrease in length of hospital stay over the 1-year period following HV initiation compared with the preceding year, whereas the NMD group demonstrated a similar but non-significant reduction. This may reflect improved clinical stability following HV initiation in the non-NMD group. The decreased length of hospitalisation in the NMD group, although not statistically significant, is broadly consistent with previous reports of favourable respiratory outcomes associated with HV use in patients with NMD.21 22

Additionally, the need for gastrostomy and home nursing care were independently associated with a reduced likelihood of liberation. These findings may reflect the underlying disease severity and vulnerability of patients rather than a direct effect of the interventions. The association between gastrostomy and lower liberation likelihood may reflect the greater clinical complexity.23 The proportion of children receiving home nursing care increased from 22.8% in 2016 to 36.0% in the current study, reflecting increasing caregiver burden for technology-dependent children.16 24 Home nursing care was associated with a decreased mortality risk, highlighting its clinical utility in supporting ventilator-dependent children and their families.

MIE showed a complex association with mortality. While MIE use itself had a tendency towards higher mortality risk at initiation, time interaction analysis revealed a potential long-term survival benefit as this risk attenuated over time. This supports the role of MIE in enhancing airway clearance and preventing respiratory complications when consistently applied throughout the disease course.9 10 25 26 In South Korea, national insurance coverage for MIE began in 2017, leading to its increased use, often in conjunction with HV support. However, the long-term outcomes of MIE in paediatric HV populations remain unclear. Our findings suggest a time-dependent trend in survival benefit and highlight the need to establish the optimal protocol for MIE use in children requiring HV support, including long-term compliance with the prescribed appliance.

Preterm birth was associated with a lower mortality risk despite the inherently vulnerable nature of prematurity. This may partly reflect the relatively high proportion (13.1%) of patients with bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), which is a chronic lung disease of prematurity. Many children with BPD require prolonged respiratory support and are often discharged from the neonatal intensive care unit with home oxygen or a ventilator support.27 28 A previous study reported that 67.6% of children with BPD enrolled in a home ventilator programme were successfully liberated.29 Accordingly, the observed association may be attributed to these favourable outcomes among children with BPD, who are predominantly born preterm.

In our study, mortality risk was negatively associated with the presence of airway anomalies, including airway malacia and subglottic stenosis, which can be congenital or acquired.30 31 Airway malacia may increase susceptibility to respiratory infections and necessitate positive pressure ventilator support; however, it generally follows a benign course and often resolves spontaneously.31 32 Subglottic stenosis typically requires surgery but is usually reversible with appropriate care.30 The favourable prognosis of these conditions may partly explain the observed association.

In South Korea, the typical monthly rental cost for home ventilators is 535 000 KRW (385 USD) for pressure-volume hybrid models and 356 000 KRW (256 USD) for pressure-based or volume-based models. Since 2016, the NHI has covered 90% of these costs for insured individuals and provides full coverage for recipients of Medical Aid. This has substantially reduced cost barriers and may have facilitated broader access to children receiving HV nationwide.33 Furthermore, the improved financial accessibility may have broadened the clinical spectrum of children considered eligible for HV, including those with higher baseline medical complexity or limited disease reversibility, potentially influencing observed outcomes. While our findings reflect a general national population, our results should be generalised with caution with respect to healthcare systems with different reimbursement structures.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, it included a relatively large and well-characterised cohort. Second, data were obtained from the nation’s largest prescriber of paediatric HV, which allowed inclusion of patients with a wide range of complex conditions. Third, to our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively describe initial home ventilator settings and clinical risk factors in South Korea. Although some international studies have described home ventilator settings, most have been limited to non-invasive ventilation.3 34 Moreover, few studies have compared home ventilator settings among paediatric users according to NMD status. Our findings highlight the importance of distinct strategies for HV across diagnostic groups and may inform evidence-based paediatric HV guidelines.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-centre retrospective study, limiting generalisability to the broader population. Second, although we adjusted for key demographic and clinical variables, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be ruled out. Third, only 9.0% of patients in the NMD group achieved successful liberation, which impeded subgroup analyses in this population.

Conclusion

In this cohort study, NMD was associated with decreased likelihood of HV liberation; however, it was not associated with mortality risk. Moreover, markers of clinical complexity, including the need for supportive care interventions such as gastrostomy and home nursing care, were associated with clinical outcomes. These findings highlight the need to develop individualised, trajectory-based HV care strategies. Given the increasing number of children with medical complexities receiving HV under the expanded national insurance coverage in South Korea, further efforts are warranted to optimise long-term outcomes by establishing sustainable, policy-driven and multidisciplinary care systems.
