A multicenter analysis revisiting the PLASMIC score: performance limitations and the impact of age, MCV, and LDH
Authors: Rishabh Singh, Ashley Khouri, Ho Jun Lee, Sanjana Kalvehalli Kashinath, Mukul Singal, Cecilia Peterson, Simon Bakhos Bechara, Aniket-Vijay Rao, Carley Mitchell, Chong Zhang, Peter Kouides, Lalitha Nayak, Ming Y. Lim, Mouhamed Yazan Abou-Ismail
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•PLASMIC score performance declined with age; MCV lacked predictive value, whereas LDH was a strong predictor.•Replacing MCV with LDH modestly improved accuracy, highlighting structural limits of equally weighted PLASMIC variables.

Introduction

Immune thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (iTTP) is a rare, life-threatening thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in which early recognition is essential for improving outcomes.1, 2, 3, 4 Confirmatory diagnosis requires ADAMTS13 activity testing; however, limited test availability may delay result turnaround. Given that rapid early clinical diagnosis is essential for best outcomes, the PLASMIC score (Table 1) was developed as a clinical tool for the prediction of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%).5 The PLASMIC score has been externally validated, remains the most widely used prediction model, and is recommended by the 2020 International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis guidelines.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 Initial studies reported high diagnostic performance, with sensitivity and negative predictive value (NPV) approaching 100%.5 However, subsequent validation studies have shown variable results, some of which demonstrated suboptimal performance, such as specificity of 58% to 63%,9,11 NPV of 67% to 78%,9,12 positive predictive value (PPV) of 51% to 67%,11,13 and decline in specificity with older age.14Table 1.PLASMIC score components and definitionsVariableCriteriaPointsPPlatelet count of <30 × 109/L1LHemolysis variable: reticulocyte count of >2.5%, or haptoglobin undetectable, or indirect bilirubin of >2.0 mg/dL1ANo active cancer1SNo history of solid organ or stem cell transplant1MMCV of <90 fL1IInternational Normalized Ratio of <1.51CCreatinine of <2.0 mg/dL1One point is assigned for each criterion met (range, 0-7).5

Several limitations likely contribute to this variability, including that key components of the score are susceptible to confounding. Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) can be influenced by iron deficiency, liver disease, or hypothyroidism, all of which are common conditions. Chronic kidney disease is common in older adults and can elevate creatinine, falsely lowering the score. The definition of “active cancer” as any malignancy treated in the past year lacks clinical granularity, because it does not distinguish, for instance, aggressive hematologic cancers from indolent or definitively cured solid tumors.

The hemolysis criterion is also prone to misclassification. It is considered fulfilled if any 1 criterion of the following is met: reticulocytosis (>2.5%), indirect bilirubin (>2.0 mg/dL), or undetectable haptoglobin. However, a single isolated abnormality may not reflect true hemolysis (eg, elevated reticulocytes due to bleeding). Moreover, haptoglobin detection thresholds vary widely by laboratory (eg, <10 mg/dL vs <45 mg/dL), and no specific cutoff is defined by the score’s definition of “undetectable.” Lastly, despite being a robust marker of hemolysis albeit nonspecific, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is notably absent from the score.

Collectively, these issues may affect the PLASMIC score’s predictive performance and clinical utility, potentially explaining the variable performance results in subsequent validation studies. In the case of a life-threatening condition such as iTTP, even modest improvements in diagnostic accuracy are clinically meaningful; missed cases may lead to avoidable morbidity or mortality, whereas false positives may result in unnecessary therapeutic plasma exchange, which carries its own risks. In this study, we reassessed the performance of the PLASMIC score as well as its individual components across different age groups in a multicenter cohort, and evaluated whether modification of its variables, by omission of select variables and/or addition of LDH, could improve upon its diagnostic precision.

Methods

We identified all ADAMTS13 testing performed between 2012 to 2022 across 3 institutions: University of Utah, Case Western Reserve University, and Rochester General Hospital. All patients with ADAMTS13 activity testing performed were included. iTTP was defined as ADAMTS13 activity of <10%, or of 10% to 20% with a clinical diagnosis of iTTP, along with confirmed presence of an ADAMTS13 inhibitor and/or antibody. Non-iTTP controls were defined as ADAMTS13 activity of >20%. Patients with ADAMTS activity between 10% to 20% without a clear clinical diagnosis of iTTP were excluded from the study to minimize misclassification. Patients with congenital TTP were excluded.

Patient demographics and clinical variables, including all components of the PLASMIC score, were descriptively summarized and stratified by iTTP status. Haptoglobin detection limits varied by institution, and the highest cutoff of 30 mg/dL was used as a uniform standard, below which levels were counted as “undetectable,” because the PLASMIC score did not define the haptoglobin threshold. Continuous variables were compared between iTTP cases and non-iTTP controls using independent t tests for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for nonnormally distributed data. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 tests, or the Fisher exact test when expected cell counts were <5.

We validated the PLASMIC score using our data set and calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) obtained via bootstrap resampling (2000 replications). Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were reported based on the high-risk cutoff (PLASMIC score of ≥6) defined in the original study.5

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to evaluate each scoring element as an independent predictor of iTTP and to assist in selection of score variants.

We also explored predefined variants of the PLASMIC score by either excluding one of the components and/or introducing a new variable, LDH. Exclusion of “A” (active cancer) yielded PLSMIC, exclusion of “S” (history of transplant) yielded PLAMIC, and exclusion of “M” (MCV) yielded PLASIC. Because PLASMIC is a binary scoring system, LDH was dichotomized to preserve the structure of the original score. In contrast to previous approaches that used the upper limit of normal of LDH,15 we applied an optimized LDH threshold derived from our data to preserve consistency across laboratory reference ranges and maximize diagnostic performance. The optimal LDH threshold was identified using the Youden J index, which selects the cutoff that maximizes the combined sensitivity and specificity for iTTP discrimination. LDH values were analyzed in their reported units across participating institutions; although assay platforms differed, values were not normalized to institutional upper limits of normal to avoid introducing additional variability. For each PLASMIC variant, we reported the AUC, optimal cutoff, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV at the identified threshold. We further stratified performance by age, comparing patients aged <60 years and those aged ≥60 years, a cutoff selected based on previous literature14,16 and to preserve adequate sample size within each age group. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.2 [2], all tests were 2-sided.

This study was approved by the institutional review boards of the University of Utah, Case Western Reserve University, and Rochester General Hospital. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Validation of the PLASMIC score

The PLASMIC score yielded an AUC of 0.854 (95% CI, 0.809-0.855). At the high-risk cutoff of ≥6, the specificity was 0.81 (95% CI, 0.78-0.85), sensitivity was 0.78 (95% CI, 0.69-0.86), PPV was 0.45 (95% CI, 0.40-0.52), and NPV was 0.95 (95% CI, 0.93-0.97).

LDH, as a continuous variable, was by itself significantly associated with iTTP (Table 2). To implement it into the PLASMIC score, we used the Youden Index and determined the optimal threshold to be 762.5 U/L. LDH, dichotomized at this threshold, was found to be an independent predictor of iTTP (OR, 3.2; 95% CI, 1.7-6.1) in the multivariable logistic regression model (Table 3). It maintained its predictive ability with and without the inclusion of MCV in the multivariable regression model.

Performance of PLASMIC score variants

Based on the low predictive performance of the MCV component, we excluded it and created the “PLASIC” variant of the score. Compared with PLASMIC, the PLASIC variant (using a cutoff of ≥5) resulted in an increase in the AUC from 0.854 to 0.881, respectively, and an increase in sensitivity from 78% to 91%, respectively. Due to the strong, independent association between LDH and iTTP diagnosis, we created additional model variants by incorporating LDH into them. Adding LDH to the PLASIC model (PLASIC+L) resulted in change in AUC from 0.881 to 0.883, respectively, and a trade-off between improving its specificity and decreasing its sensitivity. Compared with the PLASMIC score, the PLASIC+L model yielded a higher AUC (0.883 vs 0.854), specificity (85% vs 81%), and PPV (51% vs 45%), whereas maintaining similar sensitivity and NPV. Adding LDH to the original PLASMIC score (PLASMIC+L) only improved AUC from 0.854 to 0.859, and it was outperformed by both the PLASIC and PLASIC+L models (Table 4). The results were similar when using either ≥7 or ≥6 cutoffs. Comparisons of score performances at intermediate range cutoffs are shown in supplemental Table 2.

Other attempts at score variants by excluding alternative components, PLSMIC (excluding “A”) and PLAMIC (excluding “S”), did not improve the score, consistent with an independent association of iTTP with both the individual components of active cancer and history of transplant. Overall, the PLASIC variation appeared to be the most effective, considering its improved AUC, enhanced sensitivity, and simplicity. Age stratification

Age stratification analysis demonstrated that among older adults (aged >60 years), the sensitivity of the PLASMIC score dropped from 83% to 62%, as did the AUC (0.864 vs 0.821) and PPV (51% vs 33%). Across all model variants created, the AUC declined with older age consistently, as did most diagnostic metrics except specificity (Table 5; Figure 1). Notably, within both age groups (age <60 and age >60 years), the PLASIC and PLASIC+L variants still outperformed all other variants, achieving the highest AUCs. Discussion

In this largest multicenter external validation of the PLASMIC score and its components to date to our knowledge, we evaluated the impact of age on the PLASMIC score, the predictive ability of each individual PLASMIC component and LDH, and the performance of PLASMIC score variants. The PLASMIC score retained good overall discriminatory ability for identifying iTTP, with an AUC of 0.854. However, its sensitivity declined notably with age. Among patients aged ≥60 years, sensitivity dropped from 83% to 62%, raising concerns about potential missed diagnoses in this population. These findings align with previous data showing reduced sensitivity of the PLASMIC score in older adults, likely due to the confounding effects of age-associated comorbidities.14 This highlights a significant limitation of the PLASMIC score’s ability to confidently rule out iTTP among older adults.

We also examined the individual predictive value of each PLASMIC score variable. In multivariable regression, all components were independently associated with iTTP diagnosis, except for MCV, which showed no significant association (OR, 1.1; P = .68). This is in line with findings suggested by previous studies.15,17 Of note, some PLASMIC components had very low prevalence, resulting in wide CIs of the estimated ORs. Although these variables demonstrate statistically significant associations with iTTP, the magnitude of their effects should be interpreted with caution given the imprecision of the estimates. Given its lack of independent predictive value, we excluded MCV to create the “PLASIC” variant. Compared with PLASMIC, PLASIC demonstrated improved overall performance (AUC of 0.881 vs 0.854), and higher sensitivity (91% vs 78%). However, specificity declined, in contrast to a previous attempt to exclude MCV, in which specificity improved.17 The reduction in specificity observed with PLASIC reflects the use of a relatively more liberal threshold (≥5/6 points vs ≥6/7 points) rather than a limitation of the score itself; the improved AUC confirms better overall discrimination across all thresholds. Overall, these findings suggest that MCV is a low-yield variable whose equal weighting with other variables may reduce overall score performance.

In contrast, LDH, a well-established marker of hemolysis, was not included in the PLASMIC score. We investigated the role of LDH given its wide availability in acute-care settings, its ability to reflect the extent of acute hemolysis, as well as previous data that suggested a potential benefit with its use.15,18,19 LDH showed strong independent association with iTTP in our study. It remained predictive both as a continuous and dichotomized variable, and its predictive ability was independent of other variables including the hemolysis variable in the multivariable regression. This is in line with previous studies that have suggested improved diagnostic performance when incorporating LDH in some capacity.15,18,19 We identified 762.5 U/L as the optimal LDH threshold and incorporated it into model variants. Adding LDH to the PLASIC model (PLASIC+L) resulted in the highest overall AUC observed among all variants (0.883), which was a modest increase from PLASMIC’s AUC of 0.854. Adding LDH to the original PLASMIC score (PLASMIC+L) produced smaller benefits and was outperformed by both PLASIC and PLASIC+L.

Although the improvements observed with PLASIC+L were modest (AUC increase from 0.854 to 0.883), they highlight a more important issue: traditional scoring models such as PLASMIC harbor structural limitations. Specifically, the PLASMIC score assigns equal weight to variables with differing predictive strengths, preventing it from accurately reflecting each variable’s true influence. Although the observed improvement when substituting LDH for MCV was relatively small, even small gains may be clinically meaningful in a disease as morbid as iTTP. The limited magnitude of improvement is likely related to the constraints within the PLASMIC framework, in which all variables contribute equally regardless of their predictive value. The inclusion of MCV, a low-yield variable lacking independent association with iTTP, and exclusion of LDH, a robust and readily available marker of hemolysis, may contribute to its diminished diagnostic performance, particularly among older adults.

As a future direction, our findings highlight the potential value of developing alternative modeling strategies that prioritize high-yield variables and allow differential weighting based on true predictive contribution. Advances in machine learning may enable the development of newer models that treat clinical variables on a continuous scale rather than as binary cutoffs and assign influence based on individual predictive strength. Such models may also capture complex nonlinear relationships and dynamic interactions between predictors. These approaches may complement existing methods such as PLASMIC, with the potential to refine diagnostic accuracy across all age groups and provide greater flexibility in real-world settings, including when certain variables are missing. Consistent with this direction, our group has developed and externally validated a novel machine learning model for iTTP prediction, with preliminary results suggesting improved diagnostic performance that is maintained even in the presence of missing variables, and with further analyses currently underway.20,21

Our study has several limitations. Its retrospective design is subject to inherent biases, including variability in clinical recognition and documentation of iTTP. The multicenter setting introduced heterogeneity in clinical practice and laboratory testing methodologies. Most notably, haptoglobin detection thresholds varied across institutions. To address this, we applied a uniform cutoff of 30 mg/dL for the definition of “undetectable” haptoglobin, because the PLASMIC score does not specify a threshold. Another limitation is that inclusion was contingent upon ADAMTS13 testing, which presumes clinical suspicion for TMA. This may have excluded unrecognized TMA cases or included patients with low suspicion. However, this mirrors the methodology by which PLASMIC itself was originally developed, and reflects the real-world context in which it is used. Additionally, formal calibration of predicted probabilities was not assessed, and further external validation in independent populations may be warranted. Lastly, our sample size may have limited the accuracy of performance analyses of the different models.

Conclusions

In this multicenter validation, the PLASMIC score remained a useful prediction tool for suspected iTTP, but its performance declined in older adults, and its MCV component lacked predictive value. In contrast, LDH emerged as a strong, independent predictor. Replacing MCV with LDH improved diagnostic performance, although only modestly. This may reflect the limitations of traditional scoring models that assign equal weight to variables regardless of their true predictive strength. Our findings highlight structural weaknesses in the PLASMIC model that may constrain its accuracy, and provide potential avenues for refinement. This underscores the need for future models to incorporate high-yield markers such as LDH, optimize weighting based on each variable’s actual diagnostic contribution, and account for age-related performance shifts. Our findings support continued efforts to optimize iTTP risk stratification, including the use of advanced modeling or machine learning approaches.
