Micronutrient deficiency detected by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry and its association with clinical outcome of cancer patients—a prospective cohort study
Authors: Wenjie Zhu, Chenan Liu, Keqing Yao, Xi Zhang, Jiaxin Huang, Maoliang Cheng, Xinyi Wang, Wei Wei, Minghua Cong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVitamin deficiency may be associated with cancer development and poor prognosis. Data on vitamin levels in patients with cancer are lacking owing to the limitations of traditional analytical methods. This study assessed the serum levels of vitamins and the prevalence of vitamin deficiency in patients with cancer using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). The study also analysed the association between vitamin deficiency and clinical outcomes in patients with cancer.MethodsPatients diagnosed with malignant diseases at the largest cancer centre in China were prospectively enrolled. Nutritional risk and status were evaluated using standardised evaluation tools. Blood samples were collected upon entry and analysed for vitamin concentrations using LC–MS/MS. Survival analysis was conducted for patients with stage IV disease (N = 510). The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), and the secondary endpoints included a 1-year OS rate and cancer-specific survival.ResultsA total of 1257 patients were included. Vitamin deficiency was common in the study, with deficiencies in vitamin D (89.4%), vitamin B1 (80.9%), folic acid (53.5%), and vitamin C (VC, 41.4%). Vitamin deficiency was more prevalent in the cancer cohort than in the healthy controls. Vitamin status was not directly related to the tumour, node, metastasis stage, or nutritional status. Patients with VC deficiency had a significantly shorter OS than those with normal VC levels (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.410, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.008–1.972, p = 0.044). After adjusting for covariates, VC status was an independent prognostic factor for patients with stage IV disease (HR = 1.451, 95% CI 1.037–2.033, p = 0.030). No significant association was found for the other measured vitamins.ConclusionsScreening for micronutrient deficiency is crucial in hospitalised patients with cancer. VC deficiency is associated with increased mortality risk in metastatic malignancies. A prospective interventional study is warranted to confirm the effects of VC on clinical outcomes in patients with cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04370-x.Keywords: Micronutrients/deficiency, Vitamins, Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry, Neoplasms, Survival

Background

Malnutrition is common in patients with cancer, with an incidence rate ranging from 25 to 80% [1, 2]. According to the 2015 European Society of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition consensus statement, malnutrition is a condition resulting from protein–energy catabolism and deficiencies in both macro- and micronutrients [3]. Oncologists often focus on the insufficiency of macronutrients and energy, whereas micronutrient deficiencies have long been overlooked. Vitamins are vital micronutrients, which support normal biological functions and are generally classified as water- or fat-soluble. Vitamins play critical roles in maintaining genomic stability, modulating immune responses, and regulating tumour microenvironment dynamics, all of which are pivotal in cancer progression and therapeutic efficacy [4]. For instance, vitamin D deficiency is linked to dysregulated apoptosis and increased metastasis via epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [5], while vitamin C acts as a cofactor for collagen synthesis, influencing stromal integrity and metastatic potential [6]. Clinically, low serum levels of these micronutrients correlate with poorer survival rates and heightened chemotherapy toxicity [7, 8]. Patients with cancer are at a high risk for vitamin deficiency owing to insufficient dietary intake stemming from disease- and therapy-related factors, as vitamins must be obtained through the diet. Clinical and laboratory requests for vitamin level assessments have risen. Despite this, routine screening for deficiencies remains inconsistent, partly due to conflicting guidelines and methodological variability in assessment.

The concentrations of vitamins in the blood are typically very low and variable, making accurate measurements in biological fluids challenging [9]. Common analytical techniques for vitamins include immunoassays and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled with UV/Vis or photodiode array detection [10–12]. However, these methods have yielded inconsistent results across studies. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) is a new technique used for measuring small molecular metabolites [13, 14]. LC–MS/MS offers simultaneous quantification of multiple vitamins in biological matrices with high throughput, improved efficiency and accuracy—advantages that make it particularly suitable for clinical applications [15, 16]. Expert panel consensus has recommended LC–MS/MS as the preferred method for vitamin analysis [16].

Breakthroughs in analytical technologies have opened new avenues for exploring the relationship between vitamins and cancer. We conducted a prospective study using a large cohort of over 1200 Chinese patients with cancer. The primary objective was to obtain cross-sectional data on serum vitamin levels and the prevalence of vitamin deficiencies using LC–MS/MS. The secondary aim was to assess the association between vitamin deficiencies and clinical outcomes and to evaluate their prognostic significance in cancer.

Methods

Study setting

The current study (VitaCan study) was based on a single-centre prospective cohort enrolled from Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College, which is a leading oncology institution in China, renowned for its clinical care, research, and public health initiatives, with more than 1,200,000 outpatient visits, 110,000 hospital admissions, and 35,000 surgical procedures per year. The clinical cohort predominantly consists of malignancies, encompassing high-incidence cancers such as oesophageal cancer, lung cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and liver cancer, as well as rare entities (e.g. neuroendocrine tumours and soft tissue sarcomas), with late-stage diagnoses requiring combinatorial therapeutic approaches.

Study population and data collection

Adult patients admitted to the Comprehensive Oncology Unit of the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College, for anticancer treatment or supportive care were enrolled. Participant recruitment spanned August 2021 to July 2022 to ensure a representative sample of the department’s annual caseload and minimise seasonal biases in micronutrient levels. This period also provided sufficient follow-up time to assess treatment outcomes. Eligibility criteria included age > 18 years, a pathologically confirmed diagnosis of cancer, and the cognitive ability to understand the study and provide written informed consent. Patients who had taken vitamin supplements in any form within 3 months before enrolment were excluded. The study also excluded patients who had been receiving regular treatment with the following medications that significantly affect vitamin metabolism and excretion, including broad-spectrum antibiotics (≥ 7 days), metformin (≥ 3 months), proton-pump inhibitors (≥ 8 weeks), H2 receptor inhibitors (≥ 8 weeks), antiepileptic drugs (≥ 3 months), isoniazid (≥ 2 weeks), glucocorticoids (≥ 2 weeks), diuretics (≥ 7 days), and bile acid sequestrants (≥ 3 months). In addition, patients with chronic diseases that severely interfere with vitamin metabolism cannot be included in the current study, including celiac disease, Crohn’s disease, chronic pancreatitis, chronic kidney disease (≥ G3), liver cirrhosis, heart failure, hyperthyroidism, and sepsis.

Participants’ demographic data and disease-related information (including histological type, tumour, node, metastasis [TNM] stage at enrolment, previous anticancer treatment administered, and best response achieved) were collected from electronic medical records. Immediately after enrolment, participants were asked to complete a predesigned questionnaire about their tobacco/alcohol consumption, medical complications, recent weight loss, food intake, and cancer-specific symptoms. This information, along with their blood test results (e.g. levels of (pre)albumin, haemoglobin, and C-reactive protein), was recorded in case report forms. Nutrition risk was assessed using the Nutrition Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002), whereas nutritional status was evaluated according to the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria [17]. Fasting blood samples were collected at the study entry and processed for vitamin concentration analysis within 2 h of sampling. After discharge from our unit, participants were followed up by telephone until death, withdrawal, or loss of follow-up.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (number of approval, 24/484–4764). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant for study enrolment, use of their data, and venous sampling.

Biospecimen LC–MS/MS analysis

Biospecimens collected from each participant were analysed for serum vitamin levels. LC–MS/MS analysis was performed using the Qlife Lab 9000 Plus system, comprising an HPLC coupled with a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer equipped with an ESI source (Qlife, Jiangsu, China). For water-soluble vitamins (vitamins B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, B7, folate, B12, and C), 50 μL of serum was transferred to a 1.5-mL centrifuge tube. Subsequently, 200 μL of protein precipitant containing internal standard (IS) was added, followed by shaking for 5 min and centrifugation at 20,810 × g at 15 °C for 5 min. A total of 80 μL of supernatant was collected for injection.

For fat-soluble vitamins (vitamins A, D, E, and K, among them, the content of vitamin D is defined as the sum of vitamin D2 and vitamin D3), 100 μL of serum was transferred to a 1.5-mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 20 μL of IS working solution and 1000 μL of ethyl acetate–hexane (1:1, v/v). After shaking for 5 min and centrifugation at 20,810 × g at 15 °C for 5 min, 800 μL of supernatant was transferred to another centrifuge tube, evaporated under nitrogen, reconstituted in 100 μL of 90% methanol, and centrifuged again before LC–MS/MS analysis. The LC–MS/MS methods were validated, and detailed information about the methods can be found in the additional files.

Sample size calculation

We calculated the minimum required sample size using the PASS (version 11.0) software. The sample size calculation of this study is based on the following criteria. Considering that the analysis is primarily based on a cross-sectional cohort study, we use the sample size formula for cross-sectional studies: n = Z2 * p(1 − p)/e2, where Z = 1.96 (at a 95% CI), p = 0.5 (since there have been no large-scale reports on vitamin deficiencies in the domestic cancer population previously, p is set to 0.5, at which point the variance is the largest), and e = 0.03 (the allowable error is 3%). By calculation, n ≈ 1067. In reality, 1257 cases were included, which meets the basic requirements.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive design was used to determine the baseline characteristics of enrolled patients. Vitamin levels were examined by the Shapiro–Wilk test and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to determine whether they conformed to a normal distribution. For data that did not conform to a normal distribution, we used the median and quartiles (the 25th percentile, P25 and the 75th percentile, P75) for representation. The measurements of each vitamin were arbitrarily defined as deficient or normal, using the lower limit of the normal range as the cutoff value. The normal range of each vitamin detected is decided according to established standards or methods which have been described in Additional file 1. The prevalence of vitamin deficiency was calculated as the proportion of patients with vitamin levels below the respective lower limit within the analysis set.

In survival analysis, the primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from enrolment to death from any cause or the last follow-up. Secondary endpoints included the 1-year OS rate and cancer-specific survival (CSS), defined as the time from enrolment to cancer-related death or the last follow-up. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) curve was used to explore the nonlinear relationship between vitamin levels and the risk of prognosis. Survival curves were derived using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariable analyses were performed to determine the prognostic value of vitamin status. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was constructed to evaluate the association between serum vitamin deficiency and overall survival in patients with stage IV diseases. The model adjusted for age, sex, treatment type (surgery, radiotherapy, or systemic therapy), comorbidities (e.g. diabetes, hypertension), BMI, and nutritional status. Variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior evidence linking these factors to micronutrient metabolism or cancer outcomes. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF < 2 for all variables). Missing data (< 8%) were addressed via multiple imputation. All p values reported were two-sided, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 1356 consecutive patients were assessed. During the screening phase, 28 patients were excluded because of prior vitamin supplementation, and 71 patients declined venous sampling or questionnaire interviews. Ultimately, 1257 patients were included in the final analysis, all of whom provided complete data and were prospectively followed up until June 2023 (Additional file 2: Fig. S1). Among the cohort, 62.3% (n = 783) were males, and the mean age was 59 years. The most common cancer types were lung (26.4%), colorectal (20.6%), and oesophageal (15.6%). Nutritional risk (NRS-2002 score ≥ 3) was identified in 35.9% of patients, while 22.1% were diagnosed with malnutrition according to the GLIM criteria.

The percentage of patients with a deficiency in each vitamin is also presented in Table 1. These data indicate that micronutrient deficiencies were common in our study cohort. Deficiencies in vitamins D, B1, folic acid, and C were particularly prevalent, with 89.4% of patients showing vitamin D deficiency, 80.9% for vitamin B1, 53.5% for folic acid, and 41.4% for vitamin C (VC). In addition, we also included 380 healthy individuals without a history of malignant tumours or chronic diseases and who had not taken vitamin supplements in the past 6 months as the control group (mean age: 58.58 ± 14.47 years old, with 62.6% being male). A higher prevalence of vitamin B1 and VC deficiency was observed among patients with cancer than in the control cohort (80.9% vs. 71.3% for vitamin B1, p < 0.001; 41.4% vs. 27.1% for VC, p < 0.001, respectively).

We also investigated whether vitamin deficiency was correlated with the nutritional status of the patients (Additional file 2: Tables S2, S3). For most of the vitamins measured, no significant differences in vitamin deficiency rates were observed between patients with and without nutritional risk. Similarly, no association was found between vitamin deficiency and malnutrition.

Micronutrient deficiency correlated to the clinical outcome of cancer

By the date of data cutoff (30 July 2023), the median duration of follow-up was 20.9 months (95% CI 20.6–21.2 months). Only patients with stage IV cancer (n = 510) were included in the survival analysis because of the immaturity of follow-up data for stage I–III disease. First, we examined whether a deficiency in major vitamins, namely vitamins D, B1, folic acid, and C, had an impact on patient survival. Patients with VC deficiency had a markedly shorter OS than patients with normal VC levels (Fig. 2A, HR = 1.410, 95% CI 1.008–1.972, p = 0.044). Based on this result, we further explored the nonlinear relationship between VC levels and all-cause mortality. As VC levels increased, the risk of all-cause mortality in patients gradually decreased (HR = 0.813, 95% CI 0.657–0.980) (Additional file 2: Fig. S2, Table S4). Compared with patients with normal VC levels, patients with VC deficiency have a higher 1-year mortality rate (OR = 1.148, 95% CI 1.041–1.290, p = 0.006) (Additional file 2: Table S5). VC deficiency appeared to be related to hyperhomocysteinaemia and folate deficiency rather than to other clinical factors and comorbidities (Additional file 2: Table S6). Univariate analysis indicated that normal VC level, BMI ≥ 24, and absence of nutritional risk were related to better OS (Table 2.). After correcting for other covariates, VC status was determined to be an independent prognostic factor in patients with stage IV disease (Table 3). No definite association with patient survival was identified for the other measured vitamins (Fig. 2B–D). We further explored the relationship between vitamin deficiencies and CSS and found that VC deficiency is associated with an increased risk of CSS (HR = 1.543, 95% CI 1.091–2.180, p = 0.014) (Additional file 2: Table S7).

Discussion

Micronutrient deficiency, especially vitamins, has long been overlooked in patients with cancer. Vitamin deficiency may be associated with cancer development and poor prognosis [18, 19]. Well-designed clinical studies are crucial to elucidate the relationship between vitamin and cancer. To date, data on vitamin levels in patients with cancer are lacking, mainly because of the limitations of traditional analytical methods.

LC–MS/MS technology has been gradually recognised as the standard analytic technique for small molecular weight metabolites since its first introduction. Here we utilised this new method to obtain primary data on the serum level of 13 vitamins in hospitalised patients with cancer from the largest cancer centre in China, and based on this cross-sectional measurement data of vitamins we further evaluated the association of vitamin deficiency with the clinical outcome of patients with cancer. A major finding of this study was the high prevalence of micronutrient deficiency among patients with cancer. Over 40% of the enrolled patients were deficient in 4 out of 13 vitamins, including vitamins D, B1, folate, and VC, and patients with cancer were more likely to have vitamin deficiency than healthy control subjects. For example, using the serum level < 11 µmol/L as the cutoff value, the prevalence of VC deficiency was 41.4% in our study, while it was reported as 21% in rural areas of China [20]. In a recent meta-analysis of mainland China studies, the estimated prevalence of vitamin D deficiency was 63.2% [21] which was 89.4% in the current cancer cohort. Moreover, vitamin deficiency seemed to be a relatively independent condition which was not affected by the disease stage or nutritional status of the patients, indicating that vitamin deficiency screening should be performed even in patients with early-stage cancer and normal nutritional status. A counterintuitive finding was that vitamin deficiency was more pronounced in patients with stage I disease. Most stage I patients included in the analysis were perioperative patients. Surgery-related factors such as fasting, physiological stress, and tissue trauma may exacerbate vitamin deficiencies, particularly VC depletion. Furthermore, the small sample size (66 patients with stage I disease) may have introduced bias and compromised the accuracy of the findings. Future studies should expand the cohort and conduct prospective investigations to validate these findings.

Epidemiological studies performed in healthy populations in economically developed countries have indicated that VC intake or status may be related to overall or cancer-related mortality [22, 23]. Here our study adds further evidence as to the association between serum VC status and long-term survival in a large cohort from a developing country. More importantly, after adjusting for potential confounders, the VC status was found to be a prognostic factor in patients with metastatic cancer in our study. It may seem counterintuitive that micronutrient status could influence the survival of severely ill patients, as shown in our study. The Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES III; 1988–1994) involving 16,008 adult participants suggested that higher serum concentrations of VC, rather than other micronutrients, were related to a decreased risk of all-cause and cancer mortality [24]. Consistent with this finding, our study revealed a robust effect of VC status on the overall mortality of patients with cancer, which was not observed for the other vitamins examined.

According to our study, VC (ascorbate) plays multifaceted roles in cellular processes, and maintaining physiologically adequate blood concentrations seems to be important for patients with malignancies. Its deficiency may exacerbate cancer progression via several interconnected biological pathways. An important mechanism involves its antioxidant/pro-oxidant dual role. By donating electrons, VC acts as an antioxidant and helps eliminate reactive oxygen species (ROS) to protect against DNA damage and lipid peroxidation, which may slow tumour progression [25]. Deficiency can lead to the accumulation of ROS which causes DNA damage, changes in cancer cell metabolism, tumour metastasis, and tumour drug resistance [26–28]. VC at pharmacological concentrations (high doses, intravenous) acts as a pro-oxidant and induces oxidative stress selectively in cancer cells rather than in normal cells by generating hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and very high levels of ROS, which have direct cytotoxic activity on cancer cells [29, 30]. In addition to this ‘redox crisis’, the ‘energy crisis’ caused by metabolic rewiring, which is marked by severe disruption of metabolic homeostasis (e.g. inhibited glycolysis), was also suggested as a potential mechanism behind the selective cancerous cytotoxicity of pharmacological ascorbate [31, 32]. Besides, VC is a cofactor of collagen-synthesising enzymes (prolyl hydroxylases), which are essential for collagen maturation [6]. Impaired collagen synthesis during VC deficiency can weaken the extracellular matrix, facilitating tumour invasion and metastasis [33]. VC also supports hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) hydroxylation, promoting HIF-1α degradation. Deficiency of VC may stabilise HIF-1α, enhancing tumour adaptation to hypoxia and driving angiogenesis/metastasis [34, 35]. Additionally, VC enhances T-cell differentiation, natural killer cell activity, and cytokine production, and deficiency might impair immune surveillance, enabling tumour immune evasion [36, 37]. As a cofactor for ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes and histone demethylases, VC is involved in genetic and epigenetic regulation by enhancing the demethylation of DNA, RNA, and histones. An in vitro study revealed that VC directly inhibits proliferation, induces apoptosis, and suppresses the migration of human pancreatic cancer cells by targeting epigenetic alterations [38]. Deficiency may hypermethylate tumour suppressor genes [39, 40]. While the antitumour effect of VC is probably mainly due to its pro-oxidative effect in supraphysiological concentrations, it was observed that low doses of VC remarkably facilitated stemness reduction in colorectal cancer stem cells (CR-CSCs) via β-catenin retention in the cell membrane, accompanied by a mesenchymal to epithelial transition, which enhanced chemosensitivity of CR-CSCs and potentially reduced tumour recurrence [41]. Although extensive exploration has been conducted in this field, most mechanistic evidence has been derived from preclinical models, with limited human data directly linking VC levels to molecular pathways involved in cancer progression. Whether the mechanisms differ in solid tumours (e.g. colorectal) vs. haematologic cancer or early vs. advanced stages, and how VC deficiency affects responses to chemotherapy, radiation, or immunotherapy remains underexplored. It is important to test whether VC supplementation in VC-deficient patients improves clinical outcomes or synergises with existing therapies in interventional trials. Future studies should be designed to correlate VC levels with HIF-1α activity, oxidative stress markers, or immune profiles (e.g. PD-L1 expression, T-cell infiltration) in patient cohorts and explore synergies or antagonisms between VC and other micronutrients (e.g. iron, vitamin D) in cancer biology.

Although this study preliminarily revealed the correlation between vitamin C deficiency and the prognosis of patients with stage IV cancer, we did not observe such an association in the analysis of other vitamins prone to deficiency (including vitamin D, B1, and folic acid). As a key coenzyme in energy metabolism, vitamin B1 mainly participates in maintaining nerve conduction (acetylcholine synthesis) and myocardial function, and B1 deficiency can exacerbate lactic acidosis, cardiac insufficiency, and neuropathy. Studies on its correlation with tumour prognosis are relatively scarce, and its role is more reflected in the association with treatment tolerance and complications rather than directly driving cancerous behaviour. Folic acid mainly participates in DNA synthesis and repair, maintains genomic stability, acts as a methyl donor to mediate DNA/RNA methylation, and regulates gene expression. In the precancerous stage, long-term folic acid deficiency may cause DNA synthesis disorders, DNA repair defects, and hypomethylation of tumour-related genes, thereby increasing the risk of cancer (especially colorectal cancer) [42–44]. However in advanced diseases, data is limited regarding the correlation of folate deficiency with survival. It is inferred that the impact of cancer-related factors (tumour burden, metastases, multidrug resistance, cachexia, etc.) on survival far exceeds that of the folate status. Vitamin D exerts its effects through the binding of its active metabolite (calcitriol) to the vitamin D receptor (VDR), which may affect tumour development through multiple pathways: inhibiting cancer cell cycle progression and promoting differentiation; inducing programmed death of cancer cells; reducing tumour neovascularisation and limiting nutrient supply; weakening cancer cell migration and invasion abilities; inhibiting pro-inflammatory factors; enhancing innate immunity (such as macrophage and NK cell functions); and regulating adaptive immunity [45, 46]. Overall, in early or locally advanced stages, higher vitamin D levels are stably but not causally associated with better cancer prognosis [47, 48]. The strength of this association, causality, and specific mechanisms vary across studies and may become blurred or non-significant in metastatic cancers. Current evidence does not support that vitamin D supplementation can generally improve the survival of all cancer patients. The overall results of existing large randomised controlled trials are generally neutral [49, 50]. The huge tumour burden, severe systemic disorders, and high heterogeneity of previous treatment in advanced cancer patients might obscure the weak protective effect that vitamin D itself may have, making it statistically difficult to detect. Vitamin D deficiency is largely a reflection of advanced disease status. More precise studies (targeting specific subtypes and baseline deficient populations) are needed in the future to explore its value as adjuvant therapy. Lastly, diversity within our stage IV cohort should be acknowledged. It is plausible that the biological relevance of deficiency in these vitamins varies substantially by tumour origin (e.g. vitamin D pathways in colorectal cancer vs. lung cancer). The heterogeneity of treatments received, line of therapy, and nutrient interventions could have masked any underlying association. Different treatments might alter vitamin metabolism, create deficiencies acutely, or independently dominate the survival signal, making it harder to detect the specific contribution of vitamin status measured at baseline.

Despite the prevalence of vitamin deficiency observed in our study cohort, confounders including systemic inflammation and other factors inherent to advanced disease may significantly influence micronutrient levels. Chronic inflammation in stage IV disease, characterised by elevated cytokines (e.g. IL-6, TNF-α), may directly impair vitamin metabolism. For instance, inflammation-driven oxidative stress depletes antioxidants such as VC. Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP), a marker of systemic inflammation, correlates with hypoalbuminaemia and reduced circulating levels of fat-soluble vitamins (e.g. vitamin D) [51]. Cancer cachexia, which is prevalent in the advanced stages, leads to reduced dietary intake and malabsorption of micronutrients. This finding may confound the association between vitamin deficiency and clinical outcomes. Additionally, hepatic or renal impairment, which is common in metastatic diseases, can alter vitamin storage (e.g. vitamin A in the liver) and activation (e.g. vitamin D hydroxylation in the kidneys). Concurrent therapies, such as corticosteroids (reducing vitamin D absorption) or proton-pump inhibitors (impairing B12/iron uptake), may further perturb micronutrient homeostasis [52]. Our study did not prospectively assess dietary habits, inflammatory cytokine profiles, or medication history, which may partially explain the variability in vitamin levels. Future prospective studies should incorporate serial measurements of CRP, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and nutritional intake to disentangle inflammation-specific effects from true deficiencies.

The aetiology of vitamin deficiency can be multiple, including unhealthy dietary habits, smoking, alcohol abuse, surgery, and diseases of the digestive tract leading to malabsorption. Malabsorption in certain types of cancer deserves attention. Pancreatic insufficiency due to tumour obstruction or surgical resection disrupts pancreatic enzyme secretion, leading to the impaired digestion of fats and fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K). This is compounded by reduced bile production from liver metastases or biliary obstruction, which further hinders lipid absorption. Gastric tumours and subtotal gastrectomies reduce intrinsic factor production, which is essential for vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum. Tumours or surgeries that affect the terminal ileum directly impair vitamin B12 absorption. Additionally, chemotherapy-induced mucositis or radiation enteritis may damage the intestinal villi and reduce the surface area available for vitamin uptake. Cancer-associated cachexia alters gut permeability and enzyme activity, exacerbating deficiency of vitamins A, C, and E [18].

This study has several limitations. First, micronutrients were measured only once at patient enrolment, under the assumption that blood concentrations at this single time point could reflect levels over a certain period. Although mild seasonal variations in VC levels were observed (high in summer and low in winter), we performed season-stratified analyses (data not shown) and derived similar results to those in the overall cohort. Future studies will further explore vitamin variation, especially during systemic anticancer treatment, using serially collected biospecimens. Second, the association between micronutrient status and clinical outcomes was evaluated only in patients with stage IV disease owing to immature mortality data for patients with early-stage malignancies. While the overall sample size was substantial, stratifying the cohort into smaller subgroups may reduce statistical power, thereby increasing the risk of type II errors. Certain subgroups may have been underpowered to identify significant associations, and caution is needed when interpreting these findings. Future research should validate these findings through larger multicentre studies or pooled meta-analyses aggregating data across comparable cohorts. Such efforts would enhance the statistical power and enable more definitive insights into subgroup-specific outcomes. Finally, the association between vitamin status and cancer outcomes identified in this observational study may be a residual confounding factor. Prospective vitamin supplementation trials should be performed in selected vitamin-deficient patients with cancer to confirm its benefits on the survival of patients with cancer.

Conclusions

Using LC–MS/MS analysis, we identified high rates of micronutrient deficiency in hospitalised patients with cancer. Deficiencies in vitamins D, B1, folate, and C were more prevalent than those of other vitamins. Vitamin status was not directly associated with the TNM stage or the nutritional status of patients with cancer. VC deficiency was associated with an increased mortality risk in patients with metastatic malignancies. A prospective intervention study is warranted to evaluate the potential benefits of VC on the clinical outcomes in patients with cancer.
