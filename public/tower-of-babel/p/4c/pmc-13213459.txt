Current understanding and future directions in severe asthma through artificial intelligence-integrated multi-omic approaches
Authors: Sundhas Rafeeq Valappil, Mohammed Uddin, Saba Al Heialy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe asthma remains a challenging, heterogeneous condition, despite significant advances in therapeutic strategies. A subset of patients continues to experience poor control, frequent exacerbations and a high burden of disease. The advent of multi-omic technologies, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics, has opened new avenues for understanding the molecular underpinnings of asthma. When combined with artificial intelligence (AI), these approaches hold the potential to transform and augment the management of severe asthma by identifying key biomarkers, refining disease endotypes and enabling personalised treatment strategies. This review explores the role of AI-integrated multi-omic approaches in asthma research, highlighting how AI-driven models can analyse vast datasets to uncover patterns often missed by traditional methods. These insights can improve diagnostic precision, predict therapeutic responses and guide the development of novel, targeted therapies. Key areas of focus include genetic loci associated with asthma severity, single-cell RNA sequencing to uncover cellular heterogeneity, and proteomic profiles that differentiate asthma phenotypes. Through this review, we aim to provide readers with a clear understanding of the current landscape in severe asthma research, highlighting the breakthroughs achieved through AI-integrated multi-omic approaches. Additionally, we aspire to guide the future direction of the field by addressing the challenges that remain in translating these discoveries into clinical practice. By fostering a deeper understanding of the potential of these technologies, we hope to inspire further innovations that will pave the way for precision medicine to transform severe asthma management, ultimately leading to more personalised and effective treatment options for patients.

Introduction

Asthma is a non-communicable disease marked by chronic inflammation and hyperresponsiveness, leading to airway remodelling, that affects approximately 262 million people globally and causes over 1000 deaths per day [1]. Despite therapeutic advances [2–6], many individuals continue to experience severe symptoms, with persistent airflow obstruction, lung function loss and frequent exacerbations [7]. European Respiratory Society/American Thoracic Society guidelines [8] define severe asthma as uncontrolled, persistent asthma despite the use of high-dose inhaled corticosteroids or systemic corticosteroids with a second controller like long-acting β-agonists of at least 6 months’ duration [9, 10]. Common comorbidities, such as rhinitis, gastro-oesophageal reflux, obesity and psychological conditions [11], worsen asthma control by altering respiratory mechanics, increasing airway and systemic inflammation, and reducing treatment efficacy [12, 13]. However, the heterogeneity of asthma [14] necessitates personalised treatment strategies tailored to individual patient profiles [14]. Researchers are using multi-omics approaches, including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics, to provide deeper mechanistic insights [15–17], but efficient use of these platforms in addressing and understanding the complexity of asthma is still nascent.

Emerging technologies like artificial intelligence (AI), including deep learning (DL) [18], demonstrate potential for improving diagnosis and optimising treatment efficiency [19]. Their convergence with multi-omics data paves the way for personalised treatment and offers a deeper understanding of asthma pathogenesis and management. This review aims to explore and synthesise insights into how the combined potential of AI and multi-omics approaches can transform our understanding, management and treatment of severe asthma.

Relevant articles were sourced from PubMed and Google Scholar, focusing on studies related to severe asthma, multi-omics and AI applications. Peer-reviewed articles, systematic reviews, meta-analyses and clinical guidelines were prioritised. Additional references were identified through citation tracking to ensure a comprehensive review.

Heterogeneity of severe asthma across omics layers

Rackemann [20] initially classified asthma as intrinsic or extrinsic, based on age at onset. He considered those diagnosed before 30 years old as having allergic asthma, and those diagnosed after 30 years old as having non-allergic asthma. Later, the broad classification of T-helper 2 (Th2)-high (eosinophilic) and Th2-low (non-eosinophilic) endotypes revealed a more complex system encompassing a multitude of factors, such as clinical presentations, triggers and pathophysiological cues, reflecting a spectrum of severity ranging from mild, intermittent symptoms to severe, persistent forms, often blurring the phenotype boundaries [21–23]. The availability of biological therapies targeting immunoglobulin E and interleukin (IL)-4/IL-13 signalling has enabled effective disease control in many patients with eosinophilic asthma [24, 25]. By contrast, the Th2-low endotype encompassing neutrophilic, mixed and paucigranulocytic subtypes lacks eosinophilic inflammation. They are characterised by CD8+ T-cell and Th17 immune responses with increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-17, IL-23, tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and CXCL-1 [26–28]. In neutrophilic asthma, the nucleotide-binding oligomerisation domain-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3), activated by TNF-α and IL-1β, plays a key role by enhancing caspase-1, IL-1β and IL-18 levels, notably in severe cases [29, 30]. Clinically, Th2-low endotypes show more heterogeneity, including obesity-associated, smoking-related and very late-onset asthma [23], with obesity linked to heightened systemic inflammation [31, 32]. This suggests an even greater degree of complexity at the molecular level that cannot be resolved without omics-based understanding and analyses.

Extensive research has aimed to identify asthma-related genetic variants through linkage analyses, candidate gene polymorphisms and genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Through these studies, asthma has been established as a complex polygenic disease in which genetic susceptibility interacts closely with environmental exposures to shape disease onset, severity and progression [33]. The numerous loci identified across multiple GWAS and candidate gene analyses have been linked to asthma, including immune regulation, airway remodelling and epithelial barrier function. However, a potential variant for predicting disease development remains elusive while significantly influencing severity modulation, treatment responsiveness and exacerbation risk [34–43]. Genetic loci associated with asthma risk and severity. This comprehensive mapping of genetic loci emphasises the polygenic nature of asthma, highlighting the complex interplay between genetics, immune response and environmental factors in shaping asthma's clinical manifestations and severity. The ADAM33 gene, located on chromosome (Chr) 20, has been implicated in airway remodelling and asthma progression, with several polymorphisms linked to early-onset and adult-onset asthma. Variants in the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) family, including those on chromosomes 1 and 14, are crucial immune modulators associated with asthma severity and control. Chromosome 6 harbours the AHI1 gene, while chromosome 3 includes CD200R1; both play important roles in immune activation, particularly in T-helper 1 (Th1) and T-helper 2 (Th2) asthma endotypes. Additionally, polymorphisms at the IL1RL1/IL18R1 loci on chromosome 2 and the ORMDL3/GSDMB region on chromosome 17 have been consistently linked to asthma susceptibility and exacerbations across various studies. FLJ22447 (on chromosome 14) is a long noncoding RNA significantly associated with severe asthma exacerbations in Latino populations, increasing risk by over 1.5 times. Moreover, the interaction between obesity and asthma is evident through the downregulation of MSR1, PHLDA1 and CPA3, genes influencing macrophage function and mast cell activity, thus contributing to the pathophysiology of severe asthma in obese individuals.

Transcriptomic profiling across the genome captures the molecular dynamics of gene expression in patient groups. They reflect the dynamic molecular states of gene expression and inflammatory processes in disease tissues. In the sputum of patients with neutrophilic asthma, upregulation of the IL-1 receptor accessory protein signalling pathway revealed the coordinated activation of IL-33 through the Janus kinase–signal transducer and transcription activator pathway, linking innate immune activation to airway injury, exacerbation, oxidative stress and disease severity [44–46]. A network-based analysis of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets highlighted a neutrophil-centric module of differentially expressed genes, which underscored reperixin's therapeutic promise, targeting CXCR1, CXCR2 and MMP9 and offering a potential strategy to alleviate the severity of asthma by curbing neutrophil-induced airway epithelial damage [47]. These differentially expressed genes further enabled molecular stratification of severe asthma into biologically distinct subtypes defined by shared regulatory pathways such as neural signalling, REDOX processes, apoptosis and O-glycan pathways [48]. Importantly, this gene regulation is highly compartment-specific, with distinct transcriptional programmes observed, as noted in studies of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid cells, sputum, bronchial epithelium and even fibroblasts from patients with severe asthma [49, 50]. Yet, a single unifying transcriptional driver for asthma pathogenesis remains elusive.

The advent of single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) has further revolutionised our understanding of severe asthma by resolving gene expression at the individual cell level and revealing rare, disease-defining transcriptional states across immune and structural compartments, as summarised in figure 2. The scRNA-seq analyses have identified shared cell clusters in asthma models exacerbated by respiratory syncytial virus, cockroach extract and house dust mite, with each showing distinct variations in gene regulatory networks [51]. This technology has also enabled comprehensive mapping of the lung immune landscape, providing deeper insights into the activation states and phenotypes of immune cells, especially in steroid-resistant asthma [52]. Together, these molecular findings underscore the critical role of airway cells in asthma pathogenesis and highlight the power of single-cell technologies to uncover previously unrecognised cellular heterogeneity and identify novel targets to guide future therapies.

Serum, plasma and sputum proteomics have identified distinct protein signatures associated with specific asthma endotypes and inflammation, reinforcing the persistence of disease heterogeneity beyond gene expression alone [53–55]. The Italian PROXIMA study investigated the clinical relevance of protein profiling by identifying responders to omalizumab in severe allergic asthma, highlighting the potential of protein-level markers to inform treatment stratification [56]. Collectively, these findings indicate that asthma heterogeneity extends across molecular layers and varies with disease severity and inflammatory context, which is further supported by emerging peptidomics and metabolomics data [57, 58]. However, challenges with a lack of protocol standardisation, technical variability and incomplete proteome coverage continue to limit direct clinical translation. To date, no omics-driven endotypes have been fully implemented in routine asthma management. Although analytical and statistical challenges remain, particularly for untargeted approaches, advances in targeted approaches and machine learning (ML) are improving biomarker discovery and interpretation [59, 60]. Integrating multiple omics data with ML holds substantial potential to help our understanding of the clinical relationships between biological molecules and their corresponding phenotypes, as well as to refine clinical endotypes and enable personalised treatment plans for this heterogeneous disease.

AI in severe asthma management

AI supports and augments biomedical research and healthcare by advancing disease diagnosis and management. Since its inception in 1956 by John McCarthy and Marvin Minsky, AI has evolved into sophisticated systems that mimic human cognition by analysing vast datasets, identifying patterns and supporting informed decision-making. In asthma management, AI integrates data from clinical research, patient records, radiological images, omics, environmental reports and medical histories to uncover intricate patterns often missed by traditional methods, enabling precise phenotyping, targeted therapies, effective biomarker development and improving disease management.

AI encompasses ML, DL and generative models. ML improves performance by learning from data, inferring patterns and adapting to changes [61], often using supervised models like random forests, logistic regression, decision trees, support vector machines (SVM) and gradient-boosting machines (e.g. XGBoost). Supervised models use input data and corrected outputs to train models that minimise errors and generalise to new data and are often used in multi-omics to predict asthma endotypes or treatment responses. Unsupervised learning via algorithms such as K-means, hierarchical clustering and non-negative matrix factorisation uncovers hidden patterns or subtypes in omics data. Semi-supervised methods combine small, labelled datasets with large, unlabelled data to improve accuracy, using techniques like label propagation, semi-supervised SVM and graph-based models, particularly when labelled clinical data are scarce. Reinforcement learning, with methods like Q-learning or deep Q networks, treats problem-solving as a sequential process, where an agent learns optimal actions by interacting with its environment to maximise rewards, and is used for treatment optimisation, including adaptive therapy or personalised drug recommendations based on evolving patient omics profiles [62].

DL uses multi-layer artificial neural networks to model highly complex, nonlinear relationships in large and high-dimensional datasets and to extract raw input data, such as gene expression, imaging files or unstructured clinical notes. DL is particularly powerful in analysing multi-omics data, where the number of features vastly exceeds the number of samples. Different neural network architectures tailored to specific data types include convolutional neural networks, for spatial data-like imaging and spatial transcriptomics, and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory RNNs, which handle time-series inputs such as longitudinal clinical or metabolic data. Autoencoders compress high-dimensional omics data into meaningful latent features. Emerging models like transformers and variational autoencoders (VAEs) support multi-modal integration and synthetic data generation, helping address sample size limitations. Although highly predictive, these DL models often lack transparency, driving the development of explainable AI tools to improve clinical interpretability [63].

In a study by Li et al. [64], two transparent ML algorithms were applied, multivariate logistic regression and decision-tree analysis, to routine clinical and laboratory data to predict moderate-to-severe asthma attacks in a paediatric population. In 805 paediatric cases, logistic regression quantified seven predictors, most notably rhinovirus and Mycoplasma pneumoniae infections, allergy and family history, age ≥6 years, comorbidities and neutrophil percentage. The logistic model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.733, while the decision tree ranked M. pneumoniae, C-reactive protein and allergy as top factors, with an AUC of 0.694. Together, these algorithms facilitate early risk stratification, enabling targeted monitoring and personalised treatment [64]. In 2016, a group of biomedical engineers trained an artificial neural network on 1800 individuals using Global Initiative for Asthma guidelines, lung function results, medical histories and genetic data, achieving 97% accuracy in diagnosing asthma and 99% for healthy individuals [65]. Integrating RNA-seq data with ML algorithms identified patterns associated with various asthma subtypes and uncovered significant genes linked with the disease. Advanced techniques such as ANOVA, feature selection and association rule mining identified key genes from 54 715 mRNAs, pinpointing PTBP1, RAB11FIP3, APH1A and MYD88 as highly significant in the context of severe asthma. Refinement by DL isolated polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 (PTBP1) as a candidate gene associated with disease severity and correlated with elevated airway inflammation, mucus production and remodelling [66]. Knockout studies of PTBP1 in dendritic cells within asthmatic mouse models have shown dysregulation in both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell populations, underscoring the gene's pivotal role in the immune response within asthma [67]. More broadly, K-means clustering, K-nearest neighbour, random forest, decision trees, LASSO logistic regression and gradient-boosting machines predict asthma exacerbations risk from clinical data, enabling early targeted interventions [68].

Generative AI (GenAI) creates novel data rather than merely analysing or classifying existing datasets. The core architectures include generative adversarial networks (GANs), VAEs and diffusion models. GANs train a generator against a discriminator pair to improve the fidelity of outputs, and have been used to augment scarce medical imaging datasets, e.g. producing synthetic computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging slices to bolster training cohorts without compromising patient privacy [69]. VAEs learn continuous latent representations of complex data, allowing controlled sampling and interpolation of molecular fingerprints for drug discover [70]. More recently, score-based diffusion models have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in generating high-resolution medical images and even realistic patient avatars for educational simulations [71]. In multi-omics, these models augment rare endotypes, impute missing transcriptomic values and predict gene or drug perturbation effects on cellular phenotypes [72]. Large language models (LLMs) are a specialised subset of GenAI based on transformer architectures and self-attention, which model the distribution of natural language tokens. Trained on massive corpora spanning scientific articles, clinical guidelines and electronic health records, LLMs such as GPT-3 and GPT-4 predict the next token in a sequence, capturing syntax, semantics and domain-specific knowledge [73]. Through fine-tuning or prompt-based adaptation, these models can be specialised for tasks in biomedicine: automated summarisation of lengthy patient histories, generation of structured clinical trial protocols, or even synthesising research insights across thousands of publications. Retrieval-augmented generation grounds output in up-to-date factual databases to reduce hallucinations. Despite the impressive fluency of LLMs, explainable AI techniques such as attention-weight visualisation and integrated gradients help elucidate which tokens drive a prediction, thereby increasing client trust when LLMs assist in drafting pathology reports or interpreting multi-omics study findings. As both GenAI and LLMs improve, their convergence promises powerful platforms for accelerating biomarker discovery, automating literature curation and ultimately enhancing personalised disease management.

AI-powered mobile health applications are transforming asthma management by offering customised treatment plans through ML, which can improve asthma control and patient outcomes. For instance, Android apps utilise TensorFlow and XML files to process user data and generate treatment recommendations, integrating sophisticated AI into user-friendly platforms [74]. Such digital tools reduce direct patient-provider interactions, enabling remote health monitoring and informed clinical decision-making. Despite some patients’ preference for face-to-face care, these apps have decreased office visits while providing clinicians with real-time insights for treatment adjustments. Applications like MASK-Air monitor symptoms and medication adherence, particularly in patients on biologicals; they use patient-reported outcomes to feed back into treatment plans and can identify risk of non-adherence, despite the observed variability in patient adherence rates [75]. Similarly, Awaretag integrates the SMART Asthma Monitoring and Management System to monitor indoor air quality to proactively manage environmental triggers [76]. This represents a significant advance in AI-driven prevention strategies [75]. While average adherence to these apps is 51.8% within 6 months, no clear correlation between app use and medication adherence has been established. This variability, influenced by asthma severity and control, underscores the need for further research on improving adherence and optimising outcomes.

AI-enabled at-home asthma monitoring shows promising results through the analysis of breath sounds. Models trained on extensive auscultation data were able to distinguish asthmatic from non-asthmatic individuals based on an individual's wheezing sounds. Their evaluation, usually reported as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, has highlighted AI's ability to identify acoustic signatures of asthma. Notably, classifiers also differentiate patients with asthma from those with other abnormal breath sounds, offering a promising home-based screening and ongoing monitoring tool for improving care [77–79]. The broader implications of AI in asthma treatment are transformative, driving toward personalised, prevention-focused care. Predictive algorithms analyse environmental triggers and medical histories, enabling pre-emptive interventions that reduce the frequency and severity of asthma attacks. By integrating genetic predispositions with environmental factors, AI fosters customised management plans, improving outcomes and alleviating healthcare burdens. This advancement marks a move from generic protocols to treatment strategies that are as unique as the patients they serve.

Clinical translation of AI-incorporated omics data

The integration of multi-omics data with AI marks a transformative shift in understanding and managing asthma, where it outperforms single-omics by capturing cross-layer hidden data as shown in figure 3. AI models, such as logistic regression and multi-omics graph convolutional networks (MOGONET), enhance the predictive power of these datasets, aiding in early diagnosis, endotyping and targeted therapy development. In practice, ML analyses variations in gene expression, protein levels and metabolic products to generate the precise outputs [80]. Integrating GWAS hits with gene expression (expression quantitative trait locus analysis) can identify the targeted genes regulated by risk variants in asthma [81]. In severe asthma, dysregulation of the interferon-γ (IFN-γ)/secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor axis skews immunity towards Th1 responses, while impairing Th2 signalling, leading to compromised lung function and increased airway hyperresponsiveness [82]. Advanced murine models replicating human asthma dynamics, combined with mechanistic insights into IL-17 and Wnt pathways, are driving the development of novel therapies aimed at correcting this dysregulation [83, 84]. The emergence of biological therapies, such as omalizumab, mepolizumab and benralizumab, signals a pivotal shift towards precision medicine and refining the management of asthma [85]. Furthermore, proteomic profiles and identification of biomarkers within a patient's serum, which correlate with exacerbation rates and vulnerability to bacterial infections, suggest the potential for devising targeted interventions. For instance, addressing the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 pathway alongside Th2 cytokines might offer therapeutic advantages in endotypes characterised by poor exacerbation outcomes. Similarly, for endotypes exhibiting inadequate control over bacterial infections, targeting deficiencies in proteins such as interferon-stimulated gene-15, IFN-γ and C3 may be warranted.

Beyond patient education and clinical workflow support, GenAI, particularly transformer-based LLMs like ChatGPT, is being explored as a bridge between complex multi-omics datasets and clinical insights. In a study by Høj et al. [86], ChatGPT's performance on 26 asthma-related queries, spanning definitions, risk factors, diagnostics, treatment options, lifestyle interventions and clinical scenarios, each followed by targeted follow-up questions, was independently rated on a 1–5 accuracy scale. Impressively, over 80% scored ≥4, indicating high reliability for core educational and clinical topics. Nevertheless, five responses contained minor inaccuracies that could potentially mislead, and the overall Fleiss’ kappa reflected only moderate agreement among expert raters. The model's lack of real-time updates and source citation limits its role to an adjunct for patient education and information retrieval rather than a standalone clinical decision tool [86]. Another study assessed ChatGPT's capability to answer 30 common asthma “frequently asked questions” twice, to evaluate consistency, reliability and readability. Among the 60 total responses, 93.3% were deemed reliable by five board-certified physicians, with only 6.7% showing inconsistencies, yet readability scores far exceeded recommended patient-friendly levels. The authors concluded that, although ChatGPT provides quality information comparable to online resources, its outputs need to be simplified for direct patient education [87]. In a study on AI responses in asthma, researchers compared responses from ChatGPT, Google Bard and Microsoft Copilot to 15 patient questions about asthma [88]. Responses were rated by 21 asthma experts for accuracy and completeness, while 16 patients assessed clarity. ChatGPT delivered the most accurate and comprehensive responses, achieving perfect consistency and the highest median accuracy score, whereas Bard excelled in readability. These results demonstrate that AI chatbots reliably address asthma queries, but differences in performance and the need for the latest clinical guidelines highlight the importance of professional oversight [88]. Because AI chatbots may not consistently reflect the most current evidence, ensuring alignment with real-time clinical updates remains essential to guarantee accuracy, patient safety and clinical applicability. Early evaluations indicate that, when prompted with validated guidelines and real-world evidence, ChatGPT effectively synthesises pathophysiology, risk factors and lifestyle interventions, with reproducible accuracy across core educational domains [89].

Challenges and future perspective in AI-omics integration

Despite the potential of AI-integrated omics data in asthma management, several challenges need to be tackled to fully harness its benefits. One of the primary challenges is heterogeneity of omics datasets generated across diverse omics platforms, which makes it difficult to combine and analyse them effectively. The inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity in clinical data can also profoundly impact AI-omics integration. Poorly labelled exacerbation dates, medication adherence and phenotype fluctuation over time, depending on the nature of the triggers, lead to clinical variabilities that confound supervised learning approaches [90]. Developing standardised protocols for data collection, preprocessing and annotation is crucial to ensure reliability and reproducibility, while the complexity and lack of transparency in DL models raise concerns about model transparency in interpretation and clinical trust [91]. Genomics translation is also challenging, because clinicians must reconcile large variant sets with workflows, while many variants have uncertain clinical relevance [92]. For such situations, the regulatory bodies demand robust evidence of analytical validity, clinical utility and safety before approving AI-based diagnostics or companion tools. Unfortunately, many healthcare centres still lack the high-performance computing resources and bioinformatics expertise for routine AI inference. However, efforts are underway to enhance the cost-effectiveness of omics analyses, given that generating comprehensive multi-omics profiles remains both costly and computationally intensive. Privacy, security and ethics are foundational when linking omics with clinical data. Medical histories and protected health information, including comorbidities, medication regimens and hospital encounters, are governed by strict laws like the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the US and General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the EU, which demand clear patient consent, minimal data collection and strong protections (e.g. encryption or data anonymisation) to uphold privacy. Integration of omics data with electronic health records and protected health information requires full disclosure of how data are handled, must guarantee individuals the right to review and correct their information, and must enforce tight access controls along with mandatory breach notifications. Accordingly, any secondary use of health data, including for research, must follow rigorous de-identification and consent procedures to prevent unauthorised access or re-identification [93–96]. Algorithmic bias poses a significant challenge in the integration of AI for severe asthma care, because models trained on non-representative or imbalanced datasets may yield inaccurate or inequitable outcomes. Underrepresentation of specific phenotypes, such as neutrophilic or obesity-associated subtypes, can lead to misclassification, suboptimal treatment recommendations and widened health disparities in severe asthma. Bias can stem from limited geographic diversity in training data, reliance on subjective clinical labels and the use of surrogate end-points that do not reflect true pathophysiology. To ensure equitable AI-driven care, future models must incorporate diverse, well-annotated datasets, apply fairness metrics across subgroups, and prioritise transparency and clinical relevance throughout development and deployment [97]. Challenges with GenAI chatbots providing medication-specific advice mean they remain less reliable. The model's fixed training data means it cannot automatically include the latest guideline updates or new biomarker studies. Moving forwards, adding real-time literature feeds and improving the model's explainability, so clinicians can trace each recommendation back to related molecular features and primary sources, will be essential for safe use of GenAI as an explanatory tool in multi-omics-based respiratory care.

AI applications in respiratory care continue to grow, but patient safety concerns, in terms of data privacy and algorithm limitations, and risks such as data breaches underscore the need for global security standards and ethical guidelines for safe implementation in healthcare.

Conclusion

The integration of multiple omics data with AI is transforming our understanding of severe asthma by uncovering complex molecular interactions. This approach facilitates the identification of precise biomarkers and supports the development of personalised treatment strategies. Leveraging extensive datasets and advanced analytics enables early diagnosis, comprehensive endotyping and targeted therapies tailored to individual patients. However, translating these findings into clinical practice is challenging owing to the inherent complexity of asthma, the variability in patient responses and the need for AI systems that seamlessly integrate with healthcare frameworks. The computational demands and ethical considerations associated with large-scale omics data further complicate their application. Yet, the ongoing advancement in data integration and the interpretability of complex models continue to inspire hope within the medical research community. These tools are indispensable for differentiating the molecular profiles unique to each patient, enabling a shift from a one-size-fits-all treatment approach to diagnosis and treatment of asthma. This paradigm shift highlights the potential and promise of AI technologies in the management of severe asthma, though achieving widespread clinical adoption will require further refinement and validation of these technologies.
