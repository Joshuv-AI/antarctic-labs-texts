Association of IGF-1 with hypertension among children and adolescents
Authors: Zhenghao Zhao, Ting Huang, Pengzhe Xie, Wen Yue, Xinyi Zhang, Zhongze Fang, Yunfeng Cao, Yan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension among children and adolescents is an escalating global health issue, significantly linked to cardiovascular risks and long-term complications in adulthood. Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) is recognized for its role in growth and metabolism, yet its relationship with pediatric hypertension remains poorly understood. This study aimed to explore the association between IGF-1 levels and hypertension in children and adolescents.MethodsParticipants were categorized into Normal and Hypertension groups based on hypertension criteria. Demographic, anthropometric, and laboratory data were collected via medical records. Logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsData from 586 participants were analyzed. The prevalence of hypertension was 22.3% among the participants. Lower IGF-1 levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of hypertension. Compared to the highest quartile of IGF-1, the adjusted odds ratios (OR) for hypertension were 6.99 for quartiles 1 (p < 0.05). The association was more pronounced in females, children aged 6–12 years, and those with abdominal obesity.ConclusionsLower IGF-1 levels are significantly associated with an increased risk of hypertension in children and adolescents. This association is evident across different subgroups, including sex, age, and obesity status. These findings highlight Maintaining the balance of these factors is critical for hypertension health, especially during adolescence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06007-7.

Introduction

In recent years, the increasing prevalence of hypertension among children and adolescents has become a significant public health challenge globally. It is reported that approximately 4% of children and adolescents worldwide have hypertension, with a rising trend; furthermore, the prevalence of pre-hypertension has reached 9.67%, which will lead more children and adolescents to progress to hypertension [1]. Hypertension not only increases the risk of multiple diseases affecting the heart, kidneys, blood vessels, eyes, and nervous system in childhood, but is also closely associated with target organ damage [2], early coronary and aortic atherosclerosis [3], and the development of metabolic syndrome [4]. Importantly, childhood blood pressure is closely related to adult blood pressure, offering some predictive value for later-life hypertension [5]. Hypertension in childhood and adolescence significantly increases the risk of adult hypertension, atherosclerosis [3], and target organ damage [6, 7], thereby exacerbating the potential threat of cardiovascular diseases. Hence, hypertension in children and adolescents not only affects their current health but also has long-term impacts on their future health as adults.

Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) plays a crucial role in the growth and metabolic processes of children and adolescents, promoting the growth of bones [8] and muscles [9], as well as participating in the development of the nervous system [10] and immune regulation [11], all of which are vital to overall health. Existing studies have shown that IGF-1 levels are generally higher in adults with hypertension [12], and high IGF-1 status is associated with reduced systolic and diastolic blood pressure in adults [13], suggesting that elevated IGF-1 levels may help in controlling hypertension. Studies have also found that certain gene variants associated with IGF-1 receptors (e.g., rs1815009 and rs2654981) are linked to hypertension [14]. Currently, studies on IGF-1 levels in children and adolescents mainly focus on aspects of growth and nervous system development, with little attention paid to the relationship between IGF-1 and their hypertension. This study aims to explore the association between IGF-1 and hypertension in children and adolescents, thereby filling the knowledge gap in metabolic research concerning the role of IGF-1 in pediatric hypertension.

Methods

Study populations

This was a cross-sectional study. Between January 2022 and March 2024, we recruited 607 participants from the Department of Pediatrics, Tianjin Medical University General Hospital, Tianjin, China, and collected anthropometric and biochemical information. Among the recruited participants, 21 were excluded due to missing IGF-1 or blood pressure data. Finally, 586 participants were included in the analysis. (Fig. 1).

Measurement of IGF-1

Serum levels of Insulin-like Growth Factor-1 (IGF-1) was measured using standardized enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. Blood samples were collected from participants after an overnight fast and immediately processed. The serum was separated by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C until analysis. The assays were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and each sample was measured in duplicate to ensure accuracy. The concentrations of IGF-1 was reported in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL). Quality control procedures were followed to minimize variability, and intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation were maintained within acceptable ranges.

Hypertension definitions

Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (SBP) and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ the 95th percentile for age and sex [15]. Blood pressure was measured using a Yuwell electronic sphygmomanometer (Yuwell, Jiangsu, China), a device validated for use in children. Cuff sizes (small, medium, or large) were chosen based on each participant’s mid-upper arm circumference following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Three consecutive readings were obtained at 1-minute intervals after 5 min of rest, and the average of the second and third readings was used for analysis.

Abdominal obesity definitions

General obesity was defined using BMI-for-age Z scores, which were calculated based on the World Health Organization (WHO) Growth Reference for children and adolescents. According to WHO criteria, individuals with a BMI Z-score > + 1 SD were classified as overweight, and those with a BMI Z-score > + 2 SD were classified as obese. Abdominal obesity was determined in accordance with the Chinese national standard WS/T 611–2018: Screening criteria for overweight and obesity among school-age children and adolescents [16]. Specifically, abdominal obesity was defined as a waist circumference at or above the 90th percentile for sex and age, applicable to individuals aged 7 to 18 years.

Data collection

Each participant underwent anthropometric measurements, including weight, height, SBP, DBP, waist circumference, and hip circumference. Participants wore light clothing and were barefoot during the measurements, with weight recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg. Obesity was assessed using body mass index (BMI, kg/m²). Height was measured in the morning to the nearest 0.1 cm. Waist circumference was measured in a standing position using a non-elastic measuring tape placed at the narrowest point of the waist or at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the lower rib margin. The tape was applied without compressing the skin, and measurements were recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm at the end of natural exhalation. Hip circumference was measured similarly at the widest part of the hips. All measurements were taken by trained personnel under standardized conditions to ensure data reliability, with the average of multiple measurements recorded. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was calculated as waist circumference divided by hip circumference (WHR = waist circumference [cm]/hip circumference [cm]).

We collected biochemical data from participants, including lipid levels such as total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), all expressed in mmol/L. Fasting blood glucose (FBG) and fasting insulin levels were used to calculate the Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) using the formula: HOMA-IR = fasting insulin level (mU/L) × fasting blood glucose level (mmol/L)/22.5, with the constant 22.5 used to calibrate HOMA-IR to match reference levels in a healthy population. FBG was measured in mmol/L and insulin in mU/L.Liver function tests included alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST), both measured in U/L.All biochemical measurements were conducted using automated biochemical analyzers, strictly following the manufacturer’s guidelines to ensure accuracy and reproducibility. Laboratory test results were compared with age- and sex-specific reference ranges to assess the participants’ physiological and metabolic status.

Statistical analysis

The chi-square test for categorical variables and t-test for continuous variables were used to compare the characteristics of the study participants based on hypertensionk.

For variables following a normal distribution, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For skewed variables, a natural logarithm transformation was used to approximate normality, and the transformed mean ± SD was reported. For variables that could not be normalized, data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). To evaluate the strength of associations, odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and logistic regression models were employed to explore the relationship between hypertension risk and IGF-1 levels. Potential confounding factors were adjusted for in these models. Stratified analysis was also conducted by sex, age, and abdominal obesity to evaluate the relationship between IGF-1 and hypertension risk across subgroups.

Two-tailed P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R Version.4.3.2.

Results

There were 455 participants (77.64%) in the normal blood pressure group and 131 (22.36%) in the hypertension group. The hypertension group had significantly higher age (11.4 ± 2.3 years vs. 10.8 ± 2.2 years), height (154.0 ± 16.7 cm vs. 147.0 ± 15.3 cm), weight (61.7 ± 23.4 kg vs. 45.2 ± 18.5 kg), BMI (25.14 ± 6.22 kg/m² vs. 20.11 ± 5.31 kg/m²), waist circumference (82.0 [70.0, 90.0] cm vs. 66.0 [58.0, 73.5] cm), and hip circumference (95.0 [82.0, 106.0] cm vs. 78.0 [69.0, 88.0] cm) compared to the normal group. Additionally, SBP (126 [120, 137] mmHg vs. 106 [100, 112] mmHg) and DBP (80 [74, 86] mmHg vs. 67 [63, 72] mmHg) were significantly higher in the hypertension group. Triglycerides, insulin levels, and HOMA-IR were also higher in the hypertension group, while IGF-1 and IGFBP-3 levels were significantly lower. There were no statistically significant differences in the other variables (Table 1).

Association of IGF with hypertension

We evaluated the association between IGF-1 and hypertension in children and adolescents. The results indicated a significant association between lower IGF-1 levels and hypertension risk. Compared to the highest quartile (Q4) of IGF-1, the adjusted model (Model 3) showed that the OR for hypertension was 6.99 (95% CI: 3.57, 14.29, p < 0.001) for Q1, 3.82 (95% CI: 1.96, 7.73, p < 0.001) for Q2, and 2.46 (95% CI: 1.28, 4.90, p = 0.008) for Q3 (Table 2).

Discussion

While the relationship between IGF-1 levels and aspects of growth and neurological development in children and adolescents has been extensively studied, data on the relationship between IGF-1 and hypertension are limited. In this study, we used a cross-sectional design to analyze the association between IGF-1 and hypertension in children and adolescents. The results showed that lower IGF-1 levels were significantly associated with hypertension in this population, even after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and other potential confounders.

IGF-1 plays an important role in the cardiovascular system [17, 18], as it is involved in cardiac signaling [19] and vascular wall structure [18], and may be linked to the development of hypertension. In our study, the prevalence of hypertension among children and adolescents was 22.3%. We observed that hypertensive children and adolescents had significantly lower IGF-1 levels compared to their normotensive peers. Subgroup analysis revealed sex differences in the association between IGF-1 and hypertension risk, with female participants exhibiting higher OR values compared to males. This may be because IGF-1 levels rise and peak earlier during puberty in females, with overall levels higher than in males [20]. Additionally, the relationship between IGF-1 levels and hypertension risk showed clear age-related stratification. In all age group analyses, low IGF-1 levels were consistently associated with a higher likelihood of hypertension. We also found a significant association between IGF-1 levels and hypertension risk in children with abdominal obesity. In both children with abdominal obesity and those without abdominal obesity were significantly associated with higher odds of hypertension. These findings indicate that low IGF-1 is associated with a higher likelihood of hypertension in children regardless of abdominal obesity status.

Findings from other epidemiological studies have confirmed a link between IGF-1 and the risk of hypertension. One study found that circulating free IGF-1 levels were significantly higher in men newly diagnosed with hypertension compared to normotensive controls [21]. Colao conducted a cross-sectional study involving 404 adult participants and reported a significant inverse association between serum IGF-1 levels and both systolic and diastolic blood pressure, suggesting a potential protective role of IGF-1 in blood pressure regulation [22]. Similarly, Erlandsson demonstrated that, in women with rheumatoid arthritis, lower IGF-1 levels were significantly associated not only with a higher prevalence of hypertension but also with an increased risk of early cardiovascular events [23]. Unfortunately, there is limited direct epidemiological evidence linking IGF-1 with hypertension, although indirect evidence and biochemical studies have confirmed an association.

IGF-1 is a peptide hormone that shares high homology with insulin’s amino acid sequence and, like insulin, is composed of alpha and beta chains linked by disulfide bonds [24]. The similarity between IGF-1 and insulin endows it with insulin-like activity, allowing it to intervene in the development of hypertension through multiple mechanisms. Specifically, IGF-1 and insulin can affect vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration via the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [25], thereby playing key roles in the pathophysiology of hypertension. From a mechanistic perspective, low IGF-1 levels may lead to decreased nitric oxide synthase activity, resulting in impaired vasodilation and subsequent elevation in blood pressure [26]. Additionally, IGF-1 deficiency may activate inflammatory pathways and enhance oxidative stress responses, further contributing to vascular dysfunction [27]. Serum IGF-1 levels are positively correlated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [28], which is related to blood volume and pressure. Additionally, IGF-1 levels are associated with renin-angiotensin system activity [29], a key hormonal system that regulates blood pressure and fluid balance [30]. IGF-1 is also involved in the inflammatory response [31], an important factor in hypertension development. Studies have shown that IGF-1 helps maintain vascular structure and function by inhibiting excessive inflammation, including reducing the release of inflammatory mediators such as cytokines and chemokines and regulating immune cell behavior [32, 33], thus reducing hypertension risk.

IGF-1 plays a vital role in the growth and development of children and adolescents. As the primary mediator of growth hormone (GH), IGF-1 directly affects height and weight by promoting bone and soft tissue growth. While IGF-1’s role in promoting growth has been well established, current research has mainly focused on its effects on height [34], weight, and overall development [35]. Its potential role in cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension has been relatively overlooked. Although existing research suggests that IGF-1 may influence blood pressure through the regulation of vascular function and metabolic status, there has been insufficient exploration of these mechanisms, and no systematic evaluation of their potential role in pediatric hypertension. Therefore, this study specifically selected children and adolescents as subjects, partly because existing research lacks focus on this specific age group and partly because hypertension, as a chronic condition, may have serious implications for health in adulthood. Our study aims to fill this research gap and provide deeper scientific evidence for the prevention and treatment of pediatric hypertension.

One of the strengths of this study lies in redefining the role of IGF-1 in children’s hypertension. Traditionally, these biomarkers have been primarily associated with growth and development; however, our findings indicate that IGF-1 are also significantly linked to hypertension in children, suggesting that their roles extend beyond growth regulation to encompass blood pressure health. Our study has several limitations to consider: The cross-sectional design does not allow us to infer causality. Therefore, although we observed an association between IGF-1 levels and hypertension, we cannot determine whether changes in IGF-1 levels lead to changes in hypertension status or vice versa. Second, because we did not perform clinical assessments and stratification of pubertal status (e.g., Tanner staging), this study could not further explore the potential mediating or confounding effects of pubertal development on the relationship between IGF-1 and hypertension. Future research should include data on pubertal status to enhance our understanding of the mechanisms driving changes in IGF-1 and metabolic markers in children and adolescents.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study investigated the association between IGF-1 levels and hypertension risk in children and adolescents. Our analysis revealed a significant association between lower IGF-1 levels and an increased risk of hypertension, even after adjusting for multiple potential confounders. Future research should comprehensively evaluate the multiple roles of IGF-1, and further studies are needed to monitor changes in IGF-1 levels and their health effects in specific age groups, thus enhancing our understanding of the role of growth factors in pediatric health.
