Opening the Flood Gates: Epigenetic Regulators Safeguard Against Rogue Lineage Plasticity in Cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Paula Olaizola, Luke Boulter
Published: 2026

Cellular plasticity is a defining feature of liver biology, underpinning both regeneration and malignant transformation. A long-standing challenge has been to understand how mature hepatocytes, normally locked into a stable transcriptional identity, can be coerced into alternative lineages such as cholangiocytes, and why this process is so tightly linked to intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA). A recent study by Chiou and colleagues, “Pbrm1 Loss Induces a Permissive Chromatin State for Cholangiocytic Differentiation and Cholangiocarcinoma Formation,” provides compelling mechanistic insight by placing chromatin remodeling at the center of hepatocyte fate control.1

PBRM1 encodes a defining subunit of the PBAF SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling complex and is among the most frequently mutated tumor suppressors in iCCA. Yet whether PBRM1 loss is simply permissive for tumorigenesis or actively instructive in lineage choice has remained unclear. Using liver-specific Pbrm1 knockout mice, Chiou et al show that Pbrm1-loss does not immediately disrupt hepatocyte identity under homeostatic conditions, despite widespread reductions in chromatin accessibility at hepatocyte and metabolic gene loci. This uncoupling of chromatin accessibility from transcriptional output is striking, implying that hepatocytes tolerate a “latent” epigenetic erosion that only becomes consequential when the system is stressed.

That stress arrives in the form of injury. Upon cholestatic challenge or metabolic stress, Pbrm1-deficient hepatocytes fail to maintain hepatocyte-specific transcriptional programs and instead mount an exaggerated ductular reaction with features of cholangiocyte differentiation.1 Thus, PBRM1 does not merely sustain transcription but safeguards the epigenetic infrastructure that allows hepatocytes to respond appropriately to injury. Loss of this safeguard effectively lowers the barrier to lineage reprogramming.

The oncogenic implications of this epigenetic priming are made explicit when Pbrm1 loss is combined with oncogenic KRAS. Whereas KRAS activation alone predominantly yields hepatocellular neoplasms, concomitant Pbrm1-loss redirects tumorigenesis towards aggressive iCCA. These data resonate strongly with a growing body of work reframing iCCA not as a cancer of fixed biliary origin, but as the product of plastic epithelial states shaped by genetic lesions and microenvironmental cues. Recent morphomolecular analyses and lineage-tracing studies have emphasized that hepatocytes, hepatic progenitors, and cholangiocytes can all serve as cells of origin for iCCA, depending on context.2

Importantly, recognizing that Pbrm1 (and by extension, other chromatin regulators) contribute to lineage fidelity challenges the transcription-factor–centric views of plasticity. For example, PROX1 has been identified as a hepatocyte-specific “safeguard repressor” that actively suppresses alternative lineage programs and prevents hepatocyte-to-cholangiocyte conversion; loss of PROX1 enables fate switching and favors iCCA development.3 Together, these studies suggest a layered system in which transcription factors such as PROX1 operate atop a chromatin landscape maintained by remodelers like PBRM1. Disruption at either level destabilizes hepatocyte identity, but chromatin-level changes may be particularly insidious, as they silently license plasticity before transcriptional collapse becomes evident.

Beyond chromatin (re)modelers, metabolic mutations further reinforce this paradigm. IDH1/2 mutations, prevalent in iCCA, generate the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate, which perturbs α-ketoglutarate–dependent dioxygenases and enforces aberrant epigenetic states. These mutations block hepatocyte differentiation and expand progenitor-like populations susceptible to malignant transformation.4,5 Thus, diverse genetic insults: chromatin remodeling defects, transcriptional deregulation, and metabolic rewiring converge on a common outcome—erosion of epigenetic barriers that normally restrain cell fate plasticity.

The translational implications of Chiou et al’s work are intriguing. The sensitivity of PBRM1-deficient organoids to EZH2 inhibition hints at a synthetic vulnerability arising from imbalanced chromatin regulation. Although in vivo responses were modest, this observation dovetails with broader efforts to exploit epigenetic dependencies in iCCA, an urgent priority highlighted in recent consensus statements on the field’s unmet needs.6

Understanding the role of Pbrm1-loss in cholangiocarcinoma moves us towards a unifying model in which epigenetic architecture, rather than lineage per se, dictates the trajectory of liver tumorigenesis. By maintaining chromatin accessibility at hepatocyte identity genes, PBRM1 functions as a gatekeeper against inappropriate plasticity. Its loss does not force a fate change outright but creates a permissive chromatin environment that allows injury and oncogenic drivers to redirect hepatocytes toward a cholangiocytic, tumor-prone identity. This work underscores the notion that in liver cancer, as in development, epigenetic control of cell state is not a passive backdrop but an active determinant of disease.
