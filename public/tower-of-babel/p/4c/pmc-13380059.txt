Neurogenic heterotopic ossification: from molecular mechanisms to clinical treatment
Authors: Zhengqiang Yuan, Linbin Xu, Juehong Li, Cunyi Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurogenic heterotopic ossification (NHO), a disabling complication of severe central nervous system injuries, involves ectopic periarticular bone formation that causes profound functional impairments and reduced quality of life. This review highlights recent advances in NHO, especially focusing on the neuro-immune-osteogenic axis. Rather than a merely local disorder, NHO is a systemic response driven by neural, inflammatory, and interorgan signals, notably involving BMP/Smad, macrophage activation, and neuropeptide pathways. While combined-injury animal models advance mechanistic insights, they incompletely replicate human disease heterogeneity. Clinically, early diagnosis is hindered by nonspecific symptoms and insensitive radiography. Current treatments are mainly preventive or symptomatic, lacking efficacy for mature lesions. We highlight that addressing controversies in prophylaxis, surgical timing, and biomarker utility is essential. Future progress relies on validated early biomarkers, standardized imaging, mechanism-based therapeutics, and multidisciplinary precision management.The Translational Potential of this Article.This review highlights clinical mechanisms and emerging targets for NHO, which may enable earlier diagnosis, individualized prevention, and targeted treatment for patients with central nervous system injury.Keywords: Clinical treatment, Ectopic bone formation, Molecular mechanisms, Nervous system injury, Neurogenic heterotopic ossification, Neuroimmune signalling

Introduction

Neurogenic Heterotopic Ossification (NHO) refers to the aberrant bone formation in the muscle tissue around joints following severe central nervous system (CNS) injuries, such as spinal cord injury (SCI), traumatic brain injury (TBI), stroke, or cerebral hypoxia [1]. NHO primarily occurs around the joints, leading to significant limitations in joint mobility, which in turn causes substantial functional impairment, pain, and a decline in quality of life. The prevalence of NHO in patients with SCI ranges from 10% to 53%, while in individuals with TBI, the prevalence varies between 4% and 37% [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]].

In addition, NHO may also cause severe pain and muscle spasms, posing significant challenges during the rehabilitation process [8,9]. Although the pathogenesis of NHO remains incompletely understood, studies suggest that neurogenic factors play a key role in its development. Following nerve injury, local disruption of neural signaling leads to abnormal ossification in muscle tissue, a process that may be closely associated with factors such as local inflammation, activation of osteogenic cells, and neuro-bone interactions [[10], [11], [12]].

Currently, the diagnosis of NHO relies on clinical manifestations, imaging studies, and histological analysis. Common imaging tools such as ultrasound, X-ray, computed tomography (CT), and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) are used to assess the extent and range of ossification [13,14]. However, early symptoms of NHO are often subtle, and imaging findings can resemble those of other diseases, making early diagnosis challenging. Regarding treatment, the management of NHO typically involves pharmacological therapy, physical therapy, surgical intervention, and preventive measures [15]. Although no definitive cure exists, many studies focus on mitigating or preventing the occurrence of NHO through anti-inflammatory, anti-ossification, and neuro-regulation strategies [1,11]. For instance, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), bisphosphonates, and certain novel molecular targeted therapies have shown potential efficacy in clinical practice [16,17]. Additionally, physical therapy has been demonstrated to help reduce the incidence and severity of NHO(1, 18, 19).

Neurogenic heterotopic ossification is a significant complication affecting patients with neural injuries. Over the past decade, several narrative reviews have summarized the clinical features, diagnostic approaches, treatment methods, and general mechanisms of NHO and soft-tissue heterotopic ossification, particularly emphasizing inflammation, BMP signaling, rehabilitation management, and conventional therapeutic strategies [8,11,17,18]. Despite progress in research, a deeper understanding of its mechanisms, diagnostic methods, and therapeutic strategies remains a central focus of current investigations. This review will summarize the pathogenesis, animal models, diagnostic approaches, and treatment strategies for NHO, aiming to provide theoretical support for clinical treatment and guide future research directions. This review will first discuss the pathogenic mechanisms of NHO, including neurogenic inflammation, immune regulation, neuro–bone crosstalk, neurotrophic factors, neuropeptides, and extracellular vesicles. It will then summarize representative animal models of NHO associated with spinal cord injury and traumatic brain injury. Subsequently, current diagnostic approaches, including imaging modalities and potential biomarkers, will be reviewed. Finally, existing treatment strategies and future research directions will be addressed, with the aim of providing theoretical support for clinical management and guiding future investigations.

The pathogenic mechanism of neurogenic heterotopic ossification

The pathogenesis of NHO is driven by a neural injury-initiated cascade involving neuroinflammation, immune-cell activation, progenitor-cell recruitment, and aberrant osteochondral differentiation. Unlike physiological bone remodeling, NHO develops within injured soft tissues under a distinct neuroimmune microenvironment, where disrupted neurovascular barriers, macrophage-derived cytokines, and osteoinductive signals converge to promote ectopic bone formation. This section therefore focuses on NHO-specific mechanisms, including neural injury-induced inflammatory remodeling, immune regulation, progenitor activation, and neurogenic signaling pathways (Fig. 1).

Neural injury-induced neuroinflammatory and osteogenic responses

Inflammatory response

Neural injury is a critical initiating event in NHO. After central or peripheral nervous system damage, injured neural tissues release pro-inflammatory mediators and neurogenic signals that remodel the local microenvironment and create conditions permissive for ectopic osteogenesis [11,19,20]. Disruption of the blood–nerve barrier (BNB) further increases neurovascular permeability, allowing immune cells and inflammatory mediators to infiltrate neural and perineural tissues [11,21].

Bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling is a key osteoinductive pathway activated after neural injury. BMP-2 is rapidly induced in sensory neurons and activates transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels at sensory nerve terminals, promoting the release of substance P (SP) and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP). These neuropeptides amplify neurogenic inflammation, induce mast cell degranulation, and further disrupt BNB integrity [12,[22], [23], [24]]. Increased barrier permeability may facilitate the migration of neural crest-derived progenitor cells into injured stromal tissues, where elevated BMP-2 drives their osteochondral differentiation [25,26].

Mechanistically, BMP ligands activate BMP receptor type I/II complexes and downstream Smad1/5/8 signaling, leading to Runx2 upregulation and osteochondrogenic commitment. This process promotes cartilage template formation, angiogenesis, mineralization, and maturation of heterotopic bone [[27], [28], [29]]. Consistently, BMP antagonists such as Noggin and BMP receptor inhibitors such as LDN-193189 attenuate pathological ossification, supporting BMP signaling as a potential therapeutic target in NHO [30,31].

Immune regulation

Macrophages are key immune effectors in NHO. They accumulate within NHO lesions and injured muscle, where they promote ectopic ossification by secreting inflammatory cytokines and regulating progenitor-cell fate [32,33]. Depletion of F4/80-positive resident tissue macrophages significantly reduces spinal cord injury-induced NHO volume, indicating that macrophages actively drive heterotopic bone formation [34]. Their function is also stage-dependent: M1-like macrophages contribute to early inflammation, whereas later M2-like phenotypes are associated with tissue repair, matrix remodeling, and osteogenic progression [35].

Oncostatin M (OSM), a macrophage-derived cytokine, links inflammation to ectopic osteogenesis. OSM induces osteoblastic differentiation and mineralization of human muscle-derived stromal cells [36] and promotes NHO formation in periarticular muscles after spinal cord injury [37]. In spinal cord injury combined with muscle injury, macrophage-derived OSM activates the OSMR/JAK1/2/STAT3 pathway in fibro-adipogenic progenitors, inducing osteogenic and chondrogenic transcriptional programs and accelerating aberrant ossification [32].

Although immune activation is essential for NHO, neutrophils, T cells, and B cells appear to contribute less than macrophages [38,39]. Thus, NHO is primarily driven by selected innate immune populations and their crosstalk with osteogenic progenitors rather than by a broad nonspecific immune response.

Together, neural injury-induced BNB disruption, BMP activation, macrophage-derived cytokine signaling, and progenitor-cell osteogenic commitment constitute a disease-specific neuroimmune–osteogenic axis in NHO, distinguishing it from physiological bone remodeling and providing a focused framework for therapeutic targeting.

Neurogenic regulation of osteogenic progenitors

Neural crest–derived progenitor cells

Neural crest–derived progenitor cells are also released into injury sites, where BMP-2 stimulation activates Wnt/β-catenin and cAMP/PKA pathways to promote bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) osteogenic differentiation [12]. This finding underscores the tight association between nerve injury and ectopic ossification and may explain the clinical co-occurrence of traumatic brain injury or spinal cord injury with heterotopic ossification.

Mesenchymal progenitor cells

Multiple mesenchymal progenitor populations, including mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and FAPs, are now recognized as key responders to neurogenic cues.

MSCs are multipotent adult stem cells widely distributed across multiple tissues and characterized by self-renewal and multilineage differentiation capacities, including osteogenic, chondrogenic, and adipogenic lineages. Under normal physiological conditions, the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs is precisely regulated by various endogenous and exogenous signals, including the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, TGF-β/BMP pathway, PTH/PTHrP pathway, and mechanical signaling pathways [40,41]. Under pathological conditions, the local inflammatory state can activate MSCs and drive their differentiation toward the osteogenic lineage, thereby promoting the formation of bone-like tissue [42]. Meanwhile, hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) is activated under hypoxic conditions, and by upregulating angiogenic and osteogenesis-related genes, it further enhances the osteogenic differentiation capacity of MSCs [43].

FAPs are PDGFRα+ mesenchymal progenitors residing in skeletal muscle stroma with adipogenic, fibrogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic potential [44,45]. Following neurogenic or inflammatory injury, inflammatory mediators, neuropeptides, endocrine factors, and infectious signals converge to induce FAP lineage reprogramming, which is subsequently reinforced by microRNA- and epigenetic-mediated regulation, ultimately promoting ectopic bone formation through endochondral ossification. Endoneurial progenitors likewise serve as important osteogenic precursors in HO, migrating from peripheral nerves to sites of new bone formation where they contribute to osteogenesis [46]. Alexander et al. further demonstrated that a transient glucocorticoid surge occurring shortly after SCI acts as an upstream systemic trigger, independently inducing HO and amplifying osteoinductive gene expression, whereas glucocorticoid receptor blockade suppresses NHO(49). These findings collectively suggest that neurogenic endocrine stress functions as a systemic switch for pathological reprogramming. Similarly, Salga et al. found that in the presence of lipopolysaccharide, FAPs derived from human NHO-associated muscle exhibited markedly elevated levels of osteogenic markers and enhanced calcification, providing mechanistic insight into why co-infection increases clinical NHO risk [47]. At the epigenetic level, Gueguen et al. showed that the osteoinhibitory miRNA profile is systematically downregulated in NHO, which derepresses upstream osteogenic and chondrogenic genes. Restoration of these microRNAs in patient-derived FAPs effectively inhibited mineralization and reduced lineage marker expression in vitro [48].

The cross-talk between nerves and osteogenesis

Neurogenic interventions comprise various factors originating from the nervous system and the system under its control. Studies have shown that these signals can regulate bone metabolism and homeostasis either directly or indirectly [49,50]. Among them are neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors released by the nervous system, which play crucial roles in the regeneration, motility, proliferation, and differentiation of bone marrow-derived cells [50,51]. In addition, the release of neurogenic signals significantly contributes to bone healing [52,53]. However, the role of neurogenic signaling in HO remains complex, and the involvement of neurotrophic factors and neurotransmitters in modulating the ossification process warrants further investigation. Thus, a deeper understanding of neurogenic ligand–receptor interactions between neural signal-producing cells and bone-associated recipient cells may help clarify how neural inputs shape the local osteogenic microenvironment in HO and identify previously underappreciated mechanisms driving ectopic bone formation.

Neural injury reshapes the local tissue niche by inducing the release of neurotrophic factors, neuropeptides, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), which function as key molecular intermediates between the injured nervous system and osteogenic recipient cells. Rather than acting as isolated mediators, these signals establish a neuroimmune–osteogenic communication network involving neurons, glial cells, immune cells, endothelial cells, fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs), bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), and other stromal progenitors. Through receptor-specific signaling, neurogenic mediators coordinate inflammatory amplification, vascular remodeling, progenitor-cell recruitment, lineage commitment, and osteochondral matrix formation, thereby providing a mechanistic link between neural injury and ectopic ossification. Neurotrophic factors

Neurotrophic factors (NFs) are injury-responsive mediators mainly derived from neurons, glial cells, and activated immune cells, including macrophages and mast cells. Following neural injury, inflammatory activation enhances NF production, enabling these factors to participate not only in neural repair but also in the regulation of neuroimmune signaling and osteogenic cell fate [57,58]. Among them, nerve growth factor (NGF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and neurotrophin-3 (NT-3) have been implicated in neuro–osteogenic coupling through their cognate receptors, including TrkA, TrkB, TrkC, and P75NTR [[59], [60], [61]].

Nerve growth factor

NGF represents one of the best-characterized neurotrophic mediators linking neural remodeling to ectopic osteogenesis. Through TrkA and P75NTR, NGF regulates both neural ingrowth and osteogenic differentiation [62,63]. TrkA-positive nerves have been shown to facilitate HO formation, as pharmacological TrkA activation enhances neurotrophin signaling, hyperinnervation, cartilage-associated antigen expression, and subsequent heterotopic bone deposition after injury [64]. In parallel, NGF–P75NTR signaling promotes osteogenic commitment through JNK-dependent epigenetic remodeling. Specifically, NGF-activated c-Jun upregulates KDM4B, which removes the repressive H3K9me3 mark and activates DLX5 expression, thereby facilitating osteoblast differentiation [65,66]. P75NTR may also promote osteoblast differentiation through the PI3K/Akt/β-catenin pathway [67].

However, the effects of NGF signaling appear to be context-dependent. Excessive NGF expression can increase P75NTR levels and activate the NF-κB–NLRP3 inflammasome axis, leading to GSDMD- and Caspase-1-associated pyroptosis, membrane rupture, and inflammatory cytokine release in BMSCs [[68], [69], [70], [71]]. Thus, NGF may exert dual effects by promoting osteogenic differentiation while also amplifying inflammatory injury under pathological conditions. In addition, NGF directly enhances BMSC osteogenic differentiation through TrkA-mediated PI3K/Akt and MAPK/ERK signaling, resulting in Runx2 activation, osteogenic matrix production, and mineralization [72,73]. NGF may further support ossification indirectly by promoting endothelial-cell proliferation, migration, and VEGF expression, thereby improving the angiogenic microenvironment required for ectopic bone formation [[74], [75], [76]].

Brain-derived neurotrophic factor

BDNF is another neurotrophic mediator involved in neural repair and skeletal remodeling. Its expression is regulated by the Gsα/cAMP pathway and is frequently increased after traumatic brain injury, suggesting a role in injury-induced neural regeneration and tissue repair [[77], [78], [79]]. Through TrkB activation, BDNF promotes MSC osteogenic differentiation and regulates osteogenesis-related gene expression [[80], [81], [82]].

Exogenous BDNF has also been shown to reduce senescence-associated markers in human periodontal ligament stem cells by inhibiting Notch3 signaling, thereby enhancing osteogenic differentiation [83]. Conversely, miR-210-3p suppresses osteogenic differentiation by targeting BDNF [84]. These findings indicate that BDNF-related neurogenic signaling may contribute to bone regeneration by modulating stem-cell senescence, lineage commitment, and osteogenic gene programs [85].

Neurotrophin-3

NT-3, primarily acting through TrkC, participates in cell survival, lineage specification, and bone repair [86,87]. NT-3 and its receptor TrkC are closely associated with bone repair. NT-3 promotes the proliferative capacity of BMSCs and enhances the expression of bone morphogenetic proteins (particularly BMP-2) and VEGF in mineralizing cells via the phosphorylation of Erk1/2 and Akt signaling pathways [88]. Moreover, NT-3 confers protective effects on BMSCs by attenuating inflammation-induced cellular damage and apoptosis triggered by lipopolysaccharide exposure [89].

In HO-related models, interestingly, Zhang et al. discovered that NT-3 plays a crucial role in promoting HO by driving endothelial-mesenchymal transition, as demonstrated in both in vivo and in vitro models [90]. In HO-induced rats and cultured tendon-derived stem cells (TDSCs), NT-3 upregulated mesenchymal and osteogenic markers such as osteocalcin, Runx2, while suppressing endothelial markers such as Tie-1, VE-cadherin, effects that were reversible upon treatment with TrkC or EndMT inhibitors. Further studies revealed that macrophages, particularly the M1 and M2 subtypes, serve as the primary source of NT-3 at injury sites, and macrophage depletion reduced both HO formation and NT-3 secretion [91]. Mechanistically, NT-3 enhances the osteogenic differentiation of TDSCs via the ERK1/2 and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways, suggesting macrophage-derived NT-3 as a potential therapeutic target for HO. Notably, Jiang et al. conducted a pivotal study demonstrating that NGF stimulates mast cell activation to enhance NT3 secretion, which subsequently drives aberrant stem cell differentiation and ultimately leads to HO development. These findings establish NT3 as a crucial downstream effector mediating NGF-induced HO pathogenesis [92].

Neuropeptides

Neuropeptides are a class of signaling molecules synthesized and released by neurons, typically functioning as co-transmitters alongside classical neurotransmitters [93]. Unlike small-molecule transmitters, neuropeptides exert their effects primarily through binding to G protein–coupled receptors, thereby mediating relatively slow but sustained signal transduction with a broader range of influence [94]. In the context of neural injury, neuropeptides serve as important mediators of neurogenic inflammation and neuro–bone communication. By acting on immune cells, endothelial cells, osteoblast-lineage cells, BMSCs, and FAPs, they regulate vascular permeability, inflammatory-cell recruitment, cytokine release, and osteochondral differentiation [[95], [96], [97]]. Among them, substance P (SP), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), and neuropeptide Y (NPY) represent major neuropeptide systems potentially involved in NHO pathogenesis.

Substance P (SP)

SP is a neuropeptide distributed throughout both the central and peripheral nervous systems, and accumulating evidence highlights its roles in neurogenic inflammation, bone metabolism, and the pathophysiology of TBI and SCI [[98], [99], [100], [101], [102], [103]]. By binding to neurokinin-1 receptor (NK1R) on immune cells, endothelial cells, and osteogenesis-related cells, SP promotes neutrophil and monocyte–macrophage infiltration, pro-inflammatory cytokine production, vascular leakage, plasma protein extravasation, and hematopoietic-cell migration [[104], [105], [106]]. These events generate a permissive inflammatory niche for BMSC recruitment and engraftment [107].

Beyond its inflammatory effects, studies suggest that SP may participate in regulating the differentiation of BMSCs toward osteogenic and chondrogenic lineages through its interaction with the NK1R receptor. This process is associated with key signaling pathways commonly involved in osteogenic differentiation, such as upregulation of Runx2 and COL1A1, as well as activation of the BMP-2/Smad pathway, thereby potentially accelerating the formation and mineralization of the cartilage template [40,108]. Notably, in the early stages of SCI, this process often coincides with marked activation of adrenergic signaling [109]. Catecholamines and glucocorticoids released by the adrenal glands not only drive the acute release of SP but also potentiate immune cell recruitment and pro-osteogenic signal transduction, further amplifying the neuro–immune–bone positive feedback loop and accelerating pathological bone formation in a short time frame [110]. Studies indicate that pharmacological blockade of the SP–NK1R axis can significantly reduce the incidence and volume of HO and slow lesion progression, suggesting that the SP–NK1R pathway is not only a pivotal node in NHO pathogenesis but also a promising therapeutic target for its prevention and treatment [34,111].

Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)

CGRP is predominantly released by sensory neurons and functions as a neurovascular and osteogenic regulatory peptide [8,59,112]. Following neural injury, the release of CGRP not only influences the local immune milieu within bone tissue but may also facilitate heterotopic ossification through the modulation of cytokine concentrations in the microenvironment [113,114]. Mechanistically, CGRP activates receptors on the osteoblast membrane, initiating downstream signaling cascades—most notably the cyclic adenosine monophosphate/protein kinase A pathway—which subsequently promotes extracellular matrix deposition and the formation of trabecular bone [115,116]. Beyond its vasodilatory and immunomodulatory functions in the context of nerve injury, CGRP significantly contributes to bone homeostasis and skeletal remodeling via neuro–bone interactions [117]. Xu et al. found that CGRP released by dorsal root ganglion neurons enhances the expression of the key osteogenic transcription factor Runt-related transcription factor 2 in MC3T3-E1 cells, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation [118]. Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that CGRP may enhance bone regenerative capacity by modulating the lineage commitment and differentiation potential of mesenchymal stem cells in the bone marrow [119,120]. Notably, Zhao et al. found that CGRP enhances the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through the PKA/CREB/JUNB signaling pathway, suggesting that neurogenic signals can directly regulate the osteogenic potential of BMSCs and may play a pivotal role in the development of neurogenic heterotopic ossification [121]. In addition, CGRP released from peripheral sensory nerves can directly promote the chondrogenic differentiation of FAPs, thereby accelerating the formation of the early cartilage template in NHO(114).

These findings indicate that CGRP functions not only as an essential local immune regulator in the formation of neurogenic heterotopic ossification but also as a potential key mediator in skeletal regeneration. As such, targeting CGRP signaling pathways may provide novel therapeutic opportunities for the prevention and treatment of NHO and for the enhancement of bone repair following neural injury.

Neuropeptide Y (NPY)

NPY is a 36-amino-acid neuropeptide widely expressed in the central and peripheral nervous systems [122]. Its receptors, including Y1, Y2, Y4, and Y5, are mainly Gi/o-coupled receptors that inhibit adenylate cyclase and regulate MAPK signaling [123]. NPY plays key roles in appetite and energy homeostasis, stress responses, vascular tone and blood flow regulation, as well as pain modulation [[124], [125], [126], [127], [128]].

The Y1 receptor is expressed in osteoblasts and mesenchymal stem/progenitor cells (MSCs), and both global and osteoblast-specific Y1 receptor deficiency in mice result in a high–bone–mass phenotype [129]. Pharmacological studies have demonstrated that Y1 receptor antagonists increase bone mass and promote osteogenic differentiation, suggesting that the NPY–Y1 signaling pathway functions as an inhibitory brake on bone formation [130,131]. In vitro and in vivo studies further indicate that changes in NPY levels influence the expression of osteogenic transcription factors such as Runx2 and Osterix, as well as osteoblast markers including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Osteocalcin [132].

At present, direct evidence from human studies or animal models demonstrating that NPY signaling is required for the initiation or progression of NHO remains lacking. Thus, rather than being regarded as a definitive causative or driving factor, NPY should be interpreted as a potential modulatory component within the neuro-osteogenic microenvironment of NHO. Its biological effects may be context-dependent and influenced by spatiotemporal expression patterns, receptor subtype distribution, cellular specificity, and local microenvironmental conditions, including perfusion and hypoxia status.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs)

EVs provide an additional mechanism for long-range communication between injured neural tissues and peripheral osteogenic sites. These cell-derived vesicles transport bioactive cargo, including neurotransmitters, cytokines, proteins, and nucleic acids, and thereby regulate target-cell proliferation, differentiation, inflammatory responses, and osteogenesis [[133], [134], [135], [136], [137]]. Because of their biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and stability, EVs are increasingly recognized as important regulators of bone development, homeostasis, and repair [[138], [139], [140]].

In NHO, brain-derived EVs may directly convey injury-related signals from the central nervous system to peripheral soft tissues. After traumatic brain injury, brain-derived EVs can cross the blood–brain barrier, enter the circulation, and be taken up by fibroblasts at injured peripheral sites. These EVs induce fibroblast pyroptosis, increase local calcium and phosphorus concentrations, and promote osteoprogenitor proliferation and differentiation, thereby facilitating HO formation [141]. This evidence supports an EV-mediated brain–periphery communication axis in which neural injury can remotely influence the osteogenic microenvironment and accelerate pathological ossification.

NHO model

Spinal cord injury

Unlike traditional studies on rare genetic heterotopic ossification using gene-modified mouse models [142,143], the SCI-induced heterotopic ossification model in unmodified wild-type mice induces ossification by combining SCI with localized muscle injection of cardiotoxins to trigger inflammation, thereby stably inducing heterotopic bone formation (Fig. 2A) [34]. This combined injury model is based on the “central nervous system injury plus local inflammatory insult” paradigm and recapitulates an important clinical feature of NHO, namely that neurological injury alone is usually insufficient and that a permissive inflammatory microenvironment in soft tissue is required for ectopic osteogenesis. In this SCI-induced NHO model, early mineralized calcium deposits associated with muscle injury can be detected by micro-computed tomography μCT as early as day 4 after SCI. The volume of ectopic mineralization increases rapidly and reaches a peak between days 7 and 14, indicating a relatively short and reproducible time window for evaluating early NHO formation. As the lesions mature, Osterix-positive osteoblasts surrounding the developing NHO deposit collagen I and osteocalcin-containing bone matrix, leading to the formation of small ectopic bone nodules. These nodules subsequently undergo remodeling by multinucleated osteoclasts, suggesting that this model recapitulates key histological features of endochondral bone formation and bone remodeling observed during NHO maturation [33].

A key advantage of the SCI plus cardiotoxin model is its high reproducibility and mechanistic clarity. Ectopic bone formation spatially overlaps with inflammatory cell infiltration, particularly macrophage accumulation, supporting a central role for macrophage-mediated inflammation in establishing the osteogenic niche. Consistently, selective depletion of phagocytic macrophages with liposome-encapsulated bisphosphonates reduces NHO volume by up to 90%, highlighting the therapeutic relevance of targeting macrophages or downstream inflammatory mediators [35]. However, the cardiotoxin-induced muscle injury is artificial and may not fully recapitulate the complex soft-tissue trauma, immobilization, spasticity, vascular alterations, and systemic inflammatory responses observed in patients with SCI. Moreover, clinical SCI is highly heterogeneous, including complete, incomplete, contusive, and compressive injuries. Thus, although this model is valuable for mechanistic studies and preclinical screening, validation in more clinically representative models and human samples remains necessary.

Traumatic brain injury

Currently, three representative animal models are used to study HO induced by TBI (Fig. 2B). These models consistently indicate that TBI alone is generally insufficient to induce HO, but can act as a systemic enhancer that amplifies ectopic bone formation at sites of local musculoskeletal injury. They differ in anatomical relevance, injury complexity, and mechanistic focus: the TBI combined with hip trauma model closely mimics hip-associated neurogenic HO; the triple-injury model reflects a broader polytrauma setting involving brain injury, fracture, and muscle damage; and the repetitive mild TBI combined with local injury model provides mechanistic insight into hypoxia-mediated activation of perivascular progenitors. Together, these models provide complementary platforms for studying how central nervous system injury interacts with local tissue trauma, inflammation, hypoxia, and progenitor cell activation to promote HO formation.

TBI combined with hip trauma model

The first is the TBI combined with hip trauma model, which simulates the clinical scenario where the incidence of HO is significantly increased in cases of TBI with hip injuries [144]. In this rat model, hip trauma was induced by femoral canal reaming and gluteal muscle injury, while TBI was generated using a controlled cortical impact system with either moderate or severe injury parameters. HO formation was assessed by μCT 12 weeks after injury. This model showed good induction efficiency after local hip trauma, as HO developed in almost all animals that underwent hip surgery, whereas no HO was detected in animals subjected to severe TBI alone. Importantly, severe TBI significantly increased the amount and severity of HO compared with hip trauma alone or moderate TBI combined with hip trauma, indicating a dose-related enhancement of local osteogenesis by brain injury.

The major strength of this model is its high clinical relevance, as it reproduces a common anatomical site of NHO and demonstrates that TBI acts as a systemic enhancer rather than an independent inducer of HO. It is therefore useful for investigating how brain injury creates a permissive osteogenic environment that amplifies local musculoskeletal trauma-induced ossification. However, the model is technically demanding because it requires both standardized controlled cortical impact and reproducible hip bone/soft-tissue injury. Its reproducibility may be affected by variations in TBI severity and the extent of local muscle trauma. In addition, HO was evaluated mainly at a 12-week endpoint, limiting information on the early temporal dynamics of ossification. The study also used only male rats, and quantitative analysis was based on bone-to-tissue ratio within a defined cylindrical region rather than whole-lesion longitudinal assessment. Thus, this model is particularly valuable for studying the neurogenic enhancement of hip-associated HO after severe TBI, but further refinement with longitudinal imaging and broader biological validation may improve its translational utility.

Triple-injury polytrauma model

The second model is the multiple injury combined TBI triple injury model [145]. Rhys et al. established a polytrauma model by combining TBI, femoral fracture, and muscle crush injury in Sprague–Dawley rats. At 6 weeks after injury, HO developed in approximately 70% of rats in the triple-injury group, compared with 20% in the peripheral injury-only group and 0% in the sham group. The ossification severity score was also significantly increased, supporting a synergistic effect between central nervous system injury and peripheral musculoskeletal trauma.

Compared with the TBI combined with hip trauma model, which is anatomically relevant to hip-associated NHO, the triple-injury model better reflects the polytrauma setting in which TBI coexists with long-bone fracture and severe muscle injury. Therefore, it is useful for investigating how brain injury, fracture repair, and local soft-tissue inflammation jointly promote HO formation. Its 6-week endpoint and approximately 70% induction rate also provide a practical window for evaluating established ossification.

However, this model is technically more complex because it requires simultaneous induction of three injuries. Variations in TBI severity, fracture generation or stabilization, and muscle crush intensity may affect reproducibility. Moreover, although it has strong clinical relevance for polytrauma-associated NHO, the contribution of each injury component is difficult to isolate. Thus, compared with the hip trauma model, the triple-injury model offers broader polytrauma relevance but lower mechanistic specificity and greater technical complexity.

Repetitive mild TBI with local injury and hypoxic signaling activation model

The third model is the repetitive mild TBI combined with local soft tissue injury and hypoxic signaling activation in mice [146]. In this study, Kesavan et al. induced repetitive mild TBI in mice using a weight-drop method, followed by local Achilles tendon or fibular injury. HO formation was evaluated mainly 8 weeks after local injury using X-ray, DEXA, and micro-CT. Compared with non-TBI controls, mice subjected to mild TBI showed an approximately four-fold increase in ectopic bone density after Achilles tenotomy, whereas no HO was detected in the uninjured limb. These findings indicate that mild TBI alone is insufficient to induce HO, but can enhance ectopic bone formation at sites of local musculoskeletal injury.

A distinctive feature of this model is its focus on hypoxia-mediated progenitor activation. To test the role of hypoxic signaling, the authors used IOX2, a PHD2 inhibitor that stabilizes HIF signaling, after fibular injury. IOX2 markedly increased HO volume at the injury site and promoted the accumulation of NG2-and PDGFRβ-positive perivascular cells. In vitro, IOX2 upregulated hypoxia-responsive genes, including Vegf and Epo, and chondrogenic markers, including Col2a1 and Col10a1. Serum from mild TBI mice also enhanced the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of perivascular cells, suggesting that systemic factors released after brain injury may cooperate with the local hypoxic microenvironment to promote endochondral ossification.

Compared with the TBI combined with hip trauma model, this model has lower anatomical specificity for hip-associated NHO, but provides clearer mechanistic insight into the role of hypoxia and perivascular progenitor cells. Compared with the triple-injury model, it is less representative of severe polytrauma, but is technically more accessible and better suited for studying mild or repetitive TBI. Its limitations include the requirement for local injury, the use of pharmacological hypoxia activation, and the lack of a fully spontaneous NHO phenotype. Therefore, this model is most useful for dissecting hypoxia-dependent mechanisms of TBI-enhanced HO, while validation in more clinically representative trauma models remains necessary.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of NHO generally relies on imaging findings and clinical manifestations, supplemented by biomarker assessment (Fig. 3). However, due to early symptoms being atypical, such as joint swelling, fever, and limited movement, it is easy to confuse with infections and deep vein thrombosis (DVT) [147,148]. Clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that male sex, injury level, prolonged immobilization, spasticity, deep vein thrombosis, and pressure ulcers are associated with an increased likelihood of NHO development [149,150]. Based on these observations, patients may be stratified into different risk categories. Individuals with complete SCI or severe TBI accompanied by prolonged immobilization, marked spasticity, recurrent infections, or DVT may represent a high-risk population requiring closer surveillance and earlier imaging assessment. In contrast, patients with incomplete neurological injury and rapid functional recovery generally appear to have a lower risk of developing clinically significant NHO. In addition, based on these factors, they developed an effective predictive model to assess the risk of NHO occurrence. The application of this model enables clinicians to identify high-risk patients at an early stage, allowing for the implementation of appropriate preventive measures to reduce the incidence of NHO, thereby improving patients' quality of life.

As shown in Fig. 3, different imaging modalities have complementary roles during the diagnostic process. Clinical symptoms may precede radiographically detectable mineralization, and repeated or alternative imaging may be required when clinical suspicion persists despite initially negative radiographs. Conventional X-ray films are commonly used for the diagnosis of NHO, but they lack specificity in the early stages of the disease [151]. Radiographs usually become informative only after mineralization has occurred, often several weeks after symptom onset, and are therefore more useful for detecting mature ossified lesions than for early diagnosis. In recent years, with the advancement of high-resolution imaging technologies, the diagnostic accuracy of NHO has significantly improved. CT scans and MRI can clearly display the morphology, size, and maturity of ossification foci, which are crucial for assessing the relationship between the lesions and surrounding tissues, as well as determining surgical indications [152,153]. CT is particularly sensitive for mineralized lesions and is preferred for defining lesion volume, anatomical extension, joint involvement, and neurovascular proximity before surgery. CT evaluation of NHO's compression of the sciatic nerve can provide a basis for surgical decompression [154]. MRI is potentially more effective than CT in assessing the relationship between NHO and surrounding structures (such as nerves and blood vessels). It can show soft tissue edema and inflammation, which helps identify ossification foci that are still in the maturation stage [152,153]. MRI and ultrasound may be useful in the early inflammatory phase because they can detect soft-tissue edema, hyperemia, and immature lesion formation before mature mineralization is visible on radiographs; however, their specificity remains variable. Bone scintigraphy can detect increased osteoblastic activity at an early stage but lacks specificity and is now less commonly used as a first-line diagnostic modality. Some studies have indicated that early pelvic MRI in spinal cord injury patients can detect ectopic ossification in up to 15% (6/40) of cases within 6 months post-injury, providing a window for early intervention [155]. Furthermore, for patients with prolonged disorders of consciousness, the diagnosis of NHO requires a comprehensive application of various diagnostic methods, including imaging examinations as well as tools such as the Coma Recovery Scale and the Disability Rating Scale. Through these integrated assessments, NHO can be detected at an early stage, thereby maximizing the effectiveness of rehabilitation and improving the patient's quality of life [156].

Several imaging-based classification systems have been used to describe the severity and anatomical extent of HO. The Brooker classification remains the most widely used radiographic grading system for hip HO, ranging from isolated ectopic bone islands to apparent ankylosis [157]. However, this classification was originally developed for post-arthroplasty HO and may not fully reflect the anatomical complexity of NHO lesions. Therefore, CT-based anatomical assessment is particularly important in NHO because it provides more precise information on lesion location, maturity, volume, joint encasement, and the relationship with adjacent vessels and nerves. MRI may further help characterize early inflammatory activity and soft-tissue involvement, although standardized MRI grading criteria specifically validated for NHO remain limited.

In recent years, researchers have been exploring a range of potential biomarkers to achieve non-invasive early diagnosis (Fig. 3). Although elevated serum ALP is commonly regarded as a marker of bone formation, its specificity is low, and it can be influenced by other factors such as fractures [158]. Furthermore, researchers found that serum osteocalcin has limited value in confirming the diagnosis of NHO and lacks utility in assessing the maturity of ossification [159]. To improve diagnostic accuracy, Matteo et al. focused on bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (B-ALP), a subtype of ALP, which is believed to more accurately predict the occurrence of HO following spinal cord injury, especially when fractures are excluded [158]. In addition to these traditional biomarkers, researchers are also investigating proteins more closely related to the mechanisms of NHO. For example, Min et al. found that after TBI, the expression levels of Gc protein, RBP4, and troponin were lower, which may be closely associated with the development of NHO. These proteins are promising candidates as potential biomarkers for secondary NHO after TBI(158). However, no universally accepted or clinically validated cutoff values are currently available for ALP, B-ALP, osteocalcin, or emerging serum biomarkers in the diagnosis, staging, or maturity assessment of NHO. Their sensitivity and specificity remain insufficiently established, and available findings are influenced by injury type, fracture status, disease stage, and sampling time. Therefore, biomarkers should not be used as standalone diagnostic criteria; instead, they should be interpreted in combination with clinical manifestations and imaging findings. Importantly, the absence of biomarker elevation should not exclude early NHO when clinical suspicion remains high.

Recent studies have shifted the biomarker concept from single bone-turnover markers toward integrated risk prediction and mechanism-related molecular signatures. Clinical nomograms incorporating neurological severity, spasticity, pressure ulcers, infection, and deep vein thrombosis may help identify high-risk SCI patients before radiographic mineralization. Meanwhile, EV-associated cargo, pyroptosis-related molecules, inflammatory cytokines, and FAP-derived miRNAs have emerged as candidate mechanistic biomarkers [48,141]. However, none of these biomarkers has yet been validated with standardized cutoffs for early diagnosis, staging, or maturity assessment of NHO.

Treatment

NHO is a common and complex complication following SCI or TBI, and its management often requires a combination of pharmacological interventions, rehabilitation-based therapies, surgical treatment, and multidisciplinary care (Fig. 3). Given the heterogeneity of disease presentation and progression, treatment strategies should be individualized according to the stage of NHO, clinical manifestations, and functional goals. To provide a more clinically oriented overview, the available treatment options have been stratified into preventive, therapeutic, and salvage approaches, with their indications, mechanisms of action, and levels of supporting evidence summarized (Table 3). Given that preventive therapies are generally more effective before mature ossification develops, a risk-stratified approach to management may be considered. Patients presenting with factors that have been associated with an increased likelihood of HO development—such as greater neurological severity, substantial spasticity, prolonged immobilization, or a history of inflammatory complications—could be regarded as a relatively higher-risk subgroup and may therefore warrant earlier consideration of prophylactic measures, including optimized rehabilitation strategies, NSAID use, and closer radiological surveillance. In contrast, routine prophylactic treatment for individuals without these features should be individualized, taking into account the potential adverse effects, costs, and the current uncertainty regarding overall cost-effectiveness.

Pharmacological treatment

NSAIDs, which reduce inflammation by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis, are currently the most widely used preventive medications (Fig. 3) [1,160]. Although its accessibility and safety are relatively good, its effects are mainly focused on symptomatic treatment of pain and inflammation during joint lesions, and its efficacy in treating mature ossification remains controversial [161]. On the other hand, pyrophosphates, as natural mineralization inhibitors, have been shown in animal models to block the osteogenesis process, but their instability in the human body limits widespread application [162]. In contrast, bisphosphonates, such as alendronate, which have a more stable structure, have been used clinically for years. Some studies suggest that bisphosphonates may have potential effects in preventing NHO, although other research indicates that their efficacy is limited for already-formed heterotopic bone tissue, suggesting that their action may be influenced by the maturity of the ossification and the timing of intervention [163,164].

While the efficacy of anti-ossification drugs remains controversial, novel therapeutic targets related to the inflammatory osteogenic mechanisms of NHO have gradually emerged (Fig. 3). For instance, the Caspase-1 inhibitor Ac-YVAD-cmk has been shown to significantly reverse the ossification process in mice, suggesting the potential for targeting inflammasome-related pathways in treatment [141]. Additionally, the JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway is closely associated with heterotopic ossification following spinal cord injury, providing a new direction for immune modulation-based therapies [32]. These strategies remain at the preclinical or early investigational stage, lacking high-quality clinical evidence to support widespread application. Beyond inflammation-related targets, infection control is increasingly recognized as an important strategy in the intervention of NHO. Marjorie et al. found a significant correlation between infections with Gram-negative bacteria, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and heterotopic ossification in TBI patients, suggesting that infection may indirectly promote ossification progression by activating systemic inflammatory responses [47]. Therefore, early identification and control of infection sources have become a critical and indispensable aspect of comprehensive NHO management.

Physical therapy

In addition to pharmacological treatments, physical therapies such as radiation therapy and extracorporeal shock wave therapy (ESWT) are also involved to some extent in the treatment sequence of NHO (Fig. 3). Radiation therapy helps prevent new or recurrent ossification and is commonly used for perioperative or high-risk populations for prevention [165,166]. While there is preliminary evidence supporting the efficacy of radiation therapy, and it is still used as an adjunct in some clinical practices, its potential side effects have raised increasing concerns. A study by Thibaud et al. found that preoperative or postoperative combined radiation therapy does not effectively prevent recurrence and may, in fact, be significantly associated with an increased risk of sepsis [167]. Based on these findings, researchers suggest that radiation therapy should not routinely be combined with surgery, but rather, individualized decisions should be made based on the patient's overall condition. Similarly, some case reports indicate that long-term application of ESWT can help alleviate pain and functional impairments caused by NHO, particularly in patients with severe neurogenic disorders following bridge protein hemorrhage, showing certain therapeutic efficacy [168,169]. These physical interventions are often used for patients who have poor responses to or cannot tolerate pharmacological treatments, and as supplementary tools to the treatment strategy, they still hold exploratory value.

Surgical excision

When pharmacological and non-invasive therapies are ineffective, and ossification significantly impacts function or quality of life, surgical excision becomes a necessary treatment approach (Fig. 3). Surgical treatment of NHO faces multiple challenges. First, the ossified bone often fuses with adjacent skeletal structures, requiring precise identification of normal bone contours during excision to avoid fractures [170]. Furthermore, intraoperative bleeding risk is particularly high, especially in the hip area, due to the dense surrounding vasculature. Preoperative arterial embolization and enhanced CT evaluation can effectively reduce intraoperative bleeding and postoperative complications [171,172]. In recent years, innovative surgical techniques, such as the “sashimi technique,” have been proposed, which involve layered dissection to reduce nerve damage and intraoperative bleeding, demonstrating superior safety and recovery potential compared to traditional approaches [173]. Beyond these technical considerations, several prognostic factors have been associated with postoperative outcomes. Careful preoperative planning based on three-dimensional CT imaging, meticulous surgical technique, and the establishment of patient-specific functional goals are critical determinants of successful recovery. Rather than pursuing complete excision of all ectopic bone, current evidence supports a functional resection strategy aimed at achieving the desired range of motion while preserving surrounding structures [174]. Excessive resection may increase the risks of bleeding, hematoma formation, infection, fracture, and joint instability, all of which can adversely affect postoperative function and quality of life. Conversely, targeted resection guided by functional requirements, combined with appropriate patient selection and postoperative rehabilitation, is generally associated with better restoration of joint mobility and improved long-term outcomes. Surgical timing alone appears to be less predictive of recurrence than previously believed, whereas thorough preoperative assessment and individualized surgical planning remain essential for optimizing prognosis [175,176].

Multidisciplinary integrated management

Furthermore, the treatment of NHO should not rely on a single specialty but should be organized through a multidisciplinary collaborative model involving orthopedics, neurology, rehabilitation medicine, pain management, and radiology (Fig. 3) [177,178]. Within such a multidisciplinary framework, the roles of different specialties may be defined according to the stage of NHO, neurological status, functional impairment, and therapeutic goals. Orthopedic assessment is generally important for characterizing the anatomical extent, joint involvement, mechanical limitation, and radiological maturity of ossified lesions, as well as for considering the indication and optimal timing of surgical excision when conservative treatment fails or severe functional restriction occurs. Neurological evaluation may help characterize the type, severity, and evolution of the underlying central nervous system injury. Relevant assessments include motor and sensory examination, muscle tone and spasticity evaluation, deep tendon reflexes, pathological reflexes, voluntary motor control, and autonomic dysfunction. Findings such as spasticity, hyperreflexia, clonus, abnormal posturing, impaired motor control, and autonomic instability may influence joint mobility, rehabilitation tolerance, pain severity, and the risk of secondary complications. Rehabilitation physicians and therapists are typically involved in preserving joint range of motion, preventing contractures, optimizing positioning and mobility training, and developing individualized rehabilitation programs according to lesion activity, pain level, and postoperative status. Radiological expertise is also essential for selecting appropriate imaging modalities, supporting early detection, assessing lesion distribution and maturation, and monitoring disease progression or recurrence using X-ray, CT, MRI, ultrasound, or bone scintigraphy where clinically indicated. Therefore, individualized and stratified management should integrate lesion characteristics, neurological severity, functional limitation, pain burden, and overall medical condition, with the aim of improving clinical outcomes and reducing treatment-related complications [179].

Notably, the management of NHO in certain rare circumstances requires special attention. For instance, while HO is rare in patients with Parkinson's disease or stroke, when it does occur, it often severely interferes with the rehabilitation process. Given the limited treatment options available for these patients, studies and case reports have suggested that peripheral nerve blocks, as a pain-relieving method, may improve the feasibility and adherence to motor function training, making it a valuable adjunctive treatment [180]. Additionally, in patients with traumatic brain injury, paroxysmal sympathetic hyperactivity (PSH) is not uncommon. Takeaki et al. found that such patients may be associated with vitamin D metabolism abnormalities, which could trigger or exacerbate HO. Conducting metabolic assessments in these patients and providing targeted vitamin D supplementation or metabolic intervention pharmacotherapy could represent a novel intervention for PSH-related HO[181]. This finding broadens the traditional treatment approach centered on bone metabolism or inflammation, highlighting the potential value of metabolic regulation in specific subgroups of patients.

In fact, there remains a significant gap in the treatment of NHO that still needs to be addressed. Key future research directions include how to accurately identify the early stages of ossification, how to establish a standardized imaging grading system, and how to quantify the effectiveness of pharmacological and physical therapies [182]. At the same time, inflammation and immune-related targets, such as IL-1β, Caspase-1, and the JAK/STAT3 pathway, offer potential breakthroughs. With the aid of modern imaging, molecular diagnostics, and digital rehabilitation assessment techniques, the future diagnosis and treatment of NHO are expected to achieve more individualized, precise, and systematic closed-loop management.

Summary and future directions

NHO is characterized by ectopic bone formation in periarticular soft tissues after central nervous system injury, including spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury, and stroke, and may result in pain, joint stiffness, functional impairment, and reduced quality of life. Despite the availability of conventional imaging and current interventions such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, bisphosphonates, rehabilitation, and surgical excision, early diagnosis remains difficult because initial manifestations are nonspecific, while therapeutic efficacy is limited once mature ossification has developed. Future research should therefore shift toward biomarker-guided, mechanism-based, and technology-enabled strategies.

Inflammatory mediators, particularly IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and OSM, should be systematically evaluated and integrated with advanced imaging modalities to improve pre-radiographic detection, risk stratification, and longitudinal monitoring. In parallel, multi-omics approaches may help delineate the cellular heterogeneity and molecular networks underlying NHO by defining the dynamic interactions among immune, stromal, vascular, neural, and osteogenic components within the pathological microenvironment. Therapeutic development should prioritize key regulatory pathways, including BMP/Smad signaling, macrophage activation and polarization, OSM–OSMR/JAK–STAT3 signaling, neurogenic inflammation, hypoxia-related mechanisms, and extracellular vesicle-mediated intercellular communication. Organoid and organ-on-chip platforms may provide physiologically relevant systems for modeling neuro-immune-osteogenic crosstalk and evaluating candidate interventions, whereas nanomedicine-based delivery systems may enhance local targeting, controlled release, and therapeutic safety in high-risk periarticular tissues. Moreover, artificial intelligence and machine-learning models integrating clinical characteristics, neurological injury profiles, inflammatory biomarkers, imaging features, and rehabilitation-related variables may enable early risk prediction, progression assessment, and individualized preventive strategies. Collectively, an integrated translational framework combining biomarker validation, mechanistic investigation, engineered disease modeling, targeted drug delivery, and AI-assisted prediction is expected to advance earlier diagnosis, mechanistic precision, and more effective prevention and treatment of NHO.
