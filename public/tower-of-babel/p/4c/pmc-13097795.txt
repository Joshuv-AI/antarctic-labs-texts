Temporal bone mass as the initial presentation of peripheral T-cell lymphoma in an infant: a case report
Authors: Aranxa-Sofia Forero-Sanchez, Nathalie Yepes-Madrid, José-Fernando Gómez-Urrego, Romel-Alberto Segura-Galvis, Carlos-Alberto Caicedo-Estrada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPeripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) is an exceptionally rare diagnosis in infants, particularly under one year of age. The exceptional nature of the case in patients under one year of age and the limited cancer-specific treatment protocols pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case presentationWe report the case of an 11‑month‑old male infant presenting with a rapidly enlarging right temporozygomatic mass that developed over a two‑month period, accompanied by ipsilateral exophthalmos and B symptoms, specifically weight loss. Physical examination demonstrated marked facial asymmetry, ptosis, exophthalmos, a secondary parieto‑occipital lesion, and ipsilateral submandibular lymphadenopathy. Imaging studies demonstrated multiple infiltrative lesions characterized by lytic involvement of bone and extension into the central nervous system. The initial differential diagnosis encompassed fibrous dysplasia, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, neuroblastoma. Morphological evaluation and immunohistochemistry confirmed a diagnosis of mature peripheral T-cell lymphoma. The patient was treated with bridging chemotherapy (carboplatin and etoposide), followed by six cycles of CHOP protocol, and subsequently underwent autologous hematopoietic progenitor cell transplantation. He achieved complete remission and remains disease-free at the time of publication.DiscussionThis case highlights the diagnostic complexity of PTCL in infants, where clinical features can mimic more common pediatric conditions. The lack of standardized pediatric treatment protocols necessitates the extrapolation of adult regimens, which may not account for pediatric pharmacodynamics. Despite these limitations, the patient responded favorably to the tailored therapy, underscoring the importance of early morphological and immunohistochemical diagnosis and multidisciplinary management.ConclusionsPeripheral T-cell lymphoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of aggressive soft tissue masses in infants. Morphological evaluation and immunohistochemistry is essential for accurate diagnosis, and adult-based treatment protocols may offer effective outcomes when tailored to pediatric needs.

Introduction

Lymphomas are the third most common childhood cancer, with a prevalence of 11.8%, following leukemia (35.1%) [1]. They are classified into two broad groups: Hodgkin lymphomas and non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL). The latter accounts for 6% of all childhood cancers worldwide [2].

In Colombia, Childhood Cancer Epidemiological System of Cali, Colombia (VIGICANCER) recorded 9,838 patients and 3,184 events, including 2,728 deaths, between 2009 and 2023. The median age was 8.6 years. Regarding tumor type distribution, 54% were hematological malignancies, 14.6% were central nervous system (CNS) tumors, and 31.1% were solid tumors [3]. These findings align with data reported by the national high‑cost account for 2023–2024, which identified acute lymphoblastic leukemia (28.6%), central nervous system tumors (9.6%), and non‑Hodgkin lymphoma (8.5%) as the most prevalent pediatric cancers, with the latter occurring more frequently in adolescents. Importantly, these malignancies represent a prototypical group of High‑Need, High‑Cost (HNHC) patients, whose complex treatment requirements, prolonged hospitalizations, and intensive resource utilization generate a disproportionate financial impact on insurance systems and public health budgets. In the context of a low‑income country, the concentration of HNHC cases underscores the urgent need for strategic allocation of limited resources, strengthening of pediatric oncology infrastructure, and policies that ensure equitable access to high‑cost therapies [4].

NHL comprises T-cell and B-cell lymphomas, with T-cell lymphomas constituting less than 15% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas [5].

T‑cell lymphomas exhibit marked heterogeneity in clinical presentation, with anatomical distribution varying according to histological subtype. While nodal involvement is common, extranodal manifestations most frequently affecting the skin, gastrointestinal tract, mediastinum, or soft tissues—are also observed. In pediatric patients, non‑Hodgkin lymphomas demonstrate significant differences in subtype distribution compared to adults, and the occurrence of these extranodal sites is considered atypical. The rarity of such presentations in children complicates timely diagnosis and underscores the absence of standardized, evidence‑based management protocols tailored to the pediatric population [6].

Almost all NHL in the pediatric population belong to one of these types: lymphoblastic lymphoma (LBL), Burkitt lymphoma (small non-cleaved cell lymphoma), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), or anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL). The least frequent are peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs), of which more than 22 distinct subtypes have been reported according to the most recent WHO classification of 2022 [7]. Peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs) account for approximately 1% of adult non-Hodgkin lymphomas and are exceptionally rare in infancy. In children and adolescents, PTCL represents less than 2% of pediatric NHL, with an estimated incidence of 0.34 cases per million per year in the United States. PTCL shows a male predominance and is typically diagnosed in late childhood in pediatric populations and around 60 years of age in adults. Ongoing advances in disease classification continue to complicate interpretation of incidence trends over time [8–10].

Diagnostic and therapeutic experience in the pediatric population is limited. Currently, there is no oncospecific treatment for the management of this type of neoplasia in children, and treatment protocols are often extrapolated from those used in adults [11]. This can have important clinical repercussions due to the physiological, immunological, pharmacokinetic, and pharmacodynamic differences between adults and children. These differences significantly influence toxicity, tolerance, and treatment efficacy, thereby affecting the patient’s prognosis and mortality.

There is a clear need to develop specific and validated therapeutic approaches in the pediatric population. Documenting and critically analyzing these types of cases will allow us to promote future studies aimed at standardizing the management of this neoplasia and thereby impact the morbidity, mortality, and prognosis of pediatric patients diagnosed with this type of lymphoma.

Case report

We report the case of an 11‑month‑old male infant presenting with a rapidly enlarging right temporozygomatic mass that developed over a two‑month period, accompanied by ipsilateral exophthalmos and B symptoms, specifically weight loss. Physical examination revealed facial asymmetry, ptosis, exophthalmos, a second parieto-occipital lesion, and ipsilateral submandibular adenopathy.

Initial laboratory evaluation showed leukocytosis (26.34 × 10³/µL) with lymphocytosis (18.10 × 10³/µL) and neutrophil counts within normal limits. Hemoglobin was decreased (9.1 g/dL) with a hematocrit of 30.2%, consistent with anemia, and marked thrombocytosis was observed (849 × 10³/µL). Red blood cell indices demonstrated microcytosis with increased red cell distribution width. Lactate dehydrogenase levels were markedly elevated (1,173 U/L), placing the patient at high risk for tumor lysis syndrome according to the Cairo–Bishop classification (2004) [12]. Other tumor lysis parameters, including uric acid (3.40 mg/dL) and electrolytes, chlorine (102.80 mmol/L), potassium (4. 94 mmol/L), phosphorus (5.3 mg/dL), sodium (134 mmol/L), calcium (10.2 mg/dL), were within normal limits. Liver enzymes showed mild elevation of aspartate aminotransferase (52 U/L), while alanine aminotransferase levels were within normal range (12 U/L).

Inflammatory markers were elevated, with an increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate (33 mm/h), whereas C-reactive protein (Less than 5 mg/L) remained low. Renal function was preserved. Infectious workup, including serologic testing for Epstein–Barr virus and human T-lymphotropic virus types I and II, was negative. Peripheral blood flow cytometry, bone marrow aspirate, bone marrow biopsy, and lymph node biopsy showed no evidence of neoplastic involvement.

Head CT and brain MRI revealed a total of four infiltrative-appearing lesions with lytic areas at the skull base, temporal bone and brain parenchyma (Fig. 1). Radiographs of the long bones also revealed lytic lesions. Abdominal computed tomography demonstrated diffuse hepatosplenic involvement, characterized by multiple lesions suggestive of a myeloproliferative process affecting the entire hepatic parenchyma, none exceeding 2 cm in diameter and exhibiting peripheral enhancement. Similar lesions were also identified within the spleen.

Given the aggressiveness and spread of the disease, multiple diagnoses were considered, including polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, Langerhans cell histiocytosis and neuroblastoma.

Due to the presence of a skull mass, the patient initially underwent a skull biopsy; however, the results were non-diagnostic. Subsequently, the patient experienced rapid and progressive clinical deterioration with neurological findings consistent with intracranial hypertension. In light of the urgent clinical course and in the absence of a definitive diagnosis, induction therapy with dexamethasone and bridging chemotherapy consisting of carboplatin and etoposide was initiated for three days, in accordance with institutional protocols.

Histologic examination of the liver biopsy revealed extensive infiltration by malignant, discohesive lymphoid cells with high-grade features, including marked nuclear pleomorphism, brisk mitotic activity, abundant apoptotic bodies, and areas of tumor necrosis (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated strong positivity for T-lymphoid markers CD2, CD3, CD4, and CD7, with an aberrant loss of CD5 expression. The proliferation index, assessed by Ki-67, was markedly elevated, approaching 95%.

Markers of immaturity, including TdT, CD1a, and CD34, were negative. In addition, markers associated with T follicular helper cell differentiation (CD10, BCL6, and PD-1), as well as CD56, CD30, Epstein–Barr virus latent membrane protein (LMP-1), and ALK, were negative.

Biopsy of the right orbital lesion demonstrated similar high-grade malignant histopathological features and an identical immunophenotypic profile, with evidence of tumorous bone infiltration and associated necrosis. Bone marrow and lymph node biopsies showed no evidence of neoplastic involvement. Taken together, these findings supported the diagnosis of peripheral T-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS).

Based on these findings and 2015 St Jude NHL staging classification for pediatric NHL, a diagnosis of stage IV peripheral mature T-cell lymphoma was established, given evidence of central nervous system involvement with multifocal meningeal infiltration, despite acellular cerebrospinal fluid on cytological and cytochemical analyses. The patient completed bridging chemotherapy with a favorable clinical response, demonstrated by an approximately 70% reduction in facial mass size and proptosis on physical examination, with follow-up PET/CT showing no evidence of residual disease.

Following confirmation of the diagnosis, treatment was continued with the CHOP chemotherapy protocol. This approach was consistent with Colombian institutional and national guidelines, which recommend CHOP, CHOEP, CHOP-R, or BFM-based regimens for pediatric patients with peripheral or anaplastic T-cell lymphomas [10, 13]. Given the aggressive clinical presentation, treatment was initiated whit a CHOP based chemotherapy regimen (Table 1). The patient received a total of six cycles of CHOP protocol without complications and subsequently underwent autologous hematopoietic progenitor cell transplantation with BEAM protocol (Table 2).

At the time of publication, the patient is 2 years and 7 months old and remains alive and in complete remission, 1 year after undergoing autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Disease status was assessed by positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) performed both prior to transplantation and at 1-year post-transplant follow-up, with no evidence of residual or recurrent disease. No high-grade lymphadenopathy, splenic involvement, or extranodal tumor activity attributable to the primary lymphoma was identified (Fig. 3).

Physiological radiotracer distribution is observed in the encephalic structures, with no hypermetabolic cervical lymphadenopathy. The abdomen and pelvis show no morphometabolic abnormalities in the liver, spleen, pancreas, adrenal glands, or kidneys, and no hypermetabolic abdominal, retroperitoneal, pelvic, or inguinal lymphadenopathy. A moderate increase in metabolic activity is noted in Waldeyer’s ring, bilateral laterocervical lymph nodes (level II), thymus, spleen, and inguinal lymph nodes, findings consistent with lymphoid reconversion/reactive immune activity.

Discussion

Peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs) comprise a heterogeneous group of rare and typically aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphomas derived from mature T or NK cells. Although they primarily arise in lymphoid tissues, extranodal involvement is common [7]. More than 22 distinct subtypes have been described in the 2022 WHO classification, reflecting their biological diversity.

In pediatric patients, and particularly in infants, PTCL is exceptionally rare. Most reported cases occur in older adults, making diagnosis in early childhood both diagnostically and therapeutically challenging. The limited number of pediatric cases described in the literatura mostly isolated case reports or small series demonstrates marked clinical and therapeutic heterogeneity. Reported presentations have included abdominal masses, orbital involvement, central nervous system infiltration, and craniofacial disease. Treatment approaches have varied widely, ranging from CHOP-based chemotherapy regimens extrapolated from adult protocols to immunosuppressive therapy in selected subtypes such as subcutaneous panniculitis-type T-cell lymphoma [14–19]. Clinical outcomes have likewise ranged from complete remission to rapid progression and early mortality, underscoring the absence of standardized pediatric management strategies.

Certain viral infections, particularly Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) and human T-lymphotropic virus type 1 (HTLV-1), have been associated with specific PTCL subtypes. These infections are endemic in parts of South America and represent a relevant regional consideration [8, 11, 20–22]. In our patient, serologic studies for EBV and HTLV-1 were negative; however, EBV-encoded RNA in situ hybridization (EBER-ISH), the preferred diagnostic method for detecting latent EBV infection in tissue, was not performed. While viral studies did not support an infectious association in this case, their evaluation remains essential in regions with high prevalence [14–17].

The diagnosis of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma in infancy requires a high index of suspicion, as clinical findings may mimic more common pediatric conditions. In our patient, the initial differential diagnoses included polyostotic fibrous dysplasia, Langerhans cell histiocytosis, and neuroblastoma, reflecting the diagnostic complexity posed by such an unusual presentation. Furthermore, peripheral T-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS), is largely considered a diagnosis of exclusion and requires integration of clinical, morphologic, immunophenotypic, and molecular findings after ruling out defined entities and reactive processes.

Therapeutic decision-making in pediatric PTCL remains challenging due to the absence of standardized protocols. Current guidelines from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommend enrollment in clinical trials whenever posible [11]. In practice, treatment often relies on extrapolation from adult regimens, most commonly CHOP with or without etoposide (CHOEP) [17, 18, 23, 24]. Evidence from adult studies suggests that the addition of etoposide may improve outcomes in selected patients, although pediatric data are scarce. Other combinations have been explored in adults, including CHOP with alemtuzumab or bortezomib-based regimens, but their role in children remains undefined.

High-dose chemotherapy followed by autologous stem cell transplantation has been proposed as consolidation therapy in first remission for aggressive T-cell lymphomas [25, 26]. Adult series, including reports from the Nordic Lymphoma Group, describe long-term survival rates approaching 40–45% in selected patients undergoing transplantation. Although these data cannot be directly extrapolated to pediatric populations, they provide a rationale for considering intensive consolidation strategies in high-risk cases. In our patient, autologous transplantation following CHOP-based induction was associated with sustained metabolic remission on follow-up PET/CT imaging.

This case highlights the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of peripheral T-cell lymphoma in infancy. The favorable outcome achieved with combination chemotherapy followed by autologous transplantation suggests that individualized, intensive strategies may be effective even in the absence of standardized pediatric protocols. Given the extreme rarity of PTCL in this age group, each additional well-documented case contributes valuable information to guide future clinical decision-making.

Patient perspective

The patient’s perspective was not directly obtained due to the patient’s age. The parents were willing and cooperative throughout the diagnostic and therapeutic process.

Informed consent

Informed consent was obtained from the parents for the publication of this clinical case for academic and scientific purposes.

Conclusion

Peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL) can mimic other more common neoplasms in children, making its diagnosis challenging. Immunohistochemistry is essential for establishing a definitive diagnosis and differentiating it from other neoplasms. The lack of standardized protocols for diagnosis and management in pediatrics poses a risk in terms of morbidity, mortality, and patient prognosis. In this case, evidence-based management extrapolated from adult patients proved effective in our patient.
