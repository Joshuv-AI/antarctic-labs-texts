Logistic and patient preference-driven therapeutic stance in an opioid-dependent pregnant female: A case report
Authors: Rajkumar Sanahan, Shreya Pourush, Swati Tomar, Piyali Mandal, Siddharth Sarkar
Published: 2026

Dear editor,

Current international guidelines recommend opioid agonist treatment (OAT) as the standard of care for opioid dependence during pregnancy and generally discourage medically supervised withdrawal because of concerns regarding relapse, fetal distress, and adverse obstetric outcomes.[12] However, translating these recommendations into real-world practice can be challenging. Patients and families may resist maintenance therapy because of stigma, fear of neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS), concerns about prolonged neonatal monitoring, reluctance to engage in long-term pharmacotherapy, or difficulty accessing OAT services, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Consequently, treatment decisions often require individualization based on patient preference, clinical stability, feasibility of treatment delivery, and contextual factors. Psychosocial interventions remain an integral component of care regardless of the pharmacological strategy.

We managed a 25-year-old woman at 8 weeks’ gestation who was in her fourth gravida, with a history of three pregnancy losses before 20 weeks of gestation. She presented with opioid withdrawal symptoms following daily heroin use by the chased route for 3 years. She tested positive for HCV antibodies and was referred for further viral testing to the gastroenterology department. She had a history of multiple prior pregnancy losses and was highly motivated to maintain abstinence. After multidisciplinary consultation including that with obstetrics and gynecology, three management options were discussed: gradual buprenorphine taper with antagonist transition, immediate discontinuation with drug-free discharge, or continuation of agonist therapy until delivery. The patient expressed a strong preference for complete detoxification followed by relapse-prevention therapy. Psychological counseling focusing on relapse-prevention strategies, psychoeducation regarding pregnancy-related risks of opioid use, motivational enhancement, and family involvement was provided during admission. She underwent supervised tapering using buprenorphine starting at 6 mg per day in divided doses over approximately 3 weeks and was discharged on oral naltrexone, but she took only for 15 days and did not follow up subsequently.

At around 24 weeks’ gestation, she was readmitted due to concerns regarding relapse risk. This was in view of the husband having a relapse, and she took heroin once along with him. Initiation of OAT was considered; however, she was unable to attend the center for daily supervised dispensing of medication because of logistical constraints. The husband was also an opioid user and could not be given proxy dispensing for her, especially given the fact that he was not willing to engage into OAT. Also, she did not intend to have OAT as a long-term management plan. In view of these practical barriers and personal choice, withdrawal management was provided and she was discharged. Subsequently, she could only be contacted telephonically and did not continue addiction treatment at our center, although she remained engaged in routine antenatal care elsewhere.

The patient’s expressed desire to carry the pregnancy successfully and deliver a healthy child appeared to be a strong motivational factor for cessation of heroin use and maintenance of abstinence. This intrinsic motivation, along with family support and continued antenatal follow-up, likely contributed to the avoidance of relapse despite discontinuation of pharmacotherapy later in pregnancy. She delivered a healthy term female infant through normal vaginal delivery without peripartum complications. The baby cried immediately at birth and breastfeeding was initiated without any problems, and these were no significant irritability noted in the baby. She and the baby were discharged after a day of delivery. She maintained abstinence with exclusive breastfeeding at 3-month postpartum follow-up.

Current evidence does not support detoxification as a recommended treatment intervention as a result of the low completion rates of detoxification, high relapse rates, and limited data pertaining to the effect of detoxification on maternal and neonatal outcomes beyond delivery.[3] Systematic review data suggest that naltrexone use in pregnancy is a reasonable treatment option with reassuring perinatal outcomes but with limited evidence as most studies had a moderate to high potential for selection bias primarily driven by small sample size and lack of controlling for confounders. Current medical evidence indicates that naltrexone is a reasonable and promising medication option for managing opioid use disorder (OUD) during pregnancy, but data remain limited compared to standard agonist therapies. Clinical guidelines do not recommend initiating naltrexone during pregnancy due to detoxification risks, but they support continuing it if a patient was already stable on it pre-pregnancy. The intrinsic motivation and close obstetric monitoring have contributed to the successful outcome in our case.[4] In fact, there is suggestion for longer and proxy dispensing of medications to family members for pregnant and puerperal women,[56] though concerns of securing responsible family members for supervision remain.

In real-world settings, several barriers can affect the feasibility of OAT. These include the requirement for daily supervised dispensing in many programs, limited availability of treatment centers, transportation difficulties, stigma associated with attending addiction clinics, and concerns among families regarding neonatal abstinence syndrome and prolonged neonatal monitoring. Coordination with obstetric services and ensuring continuity of treatment postpartum can further complicate care delivery in low-resource contexts.

This case underscores the importance of contextualizing guideline recommendations. In settings where access to structured OAT programs and supervised dosing is inconsistent, individualized treatment decisions incorporating patient preference, feasibility of follow-up, family supervision, and service availability may be necessary. Rather than replacing OAT as standard care, such experiences highlight the need for flexible and patient-centered approaches when maintenance therapy is declined or impractical.
