Impact of music intervention on endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography patients’ pain, anxiety, and vital signs: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Emine Çakır, Yeliz Ciğerci, Sezgin Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of a music intervention on anxiety, pain, and vital signs in patients undergoing Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). This randomized controlled trial included 70 patients who underwent ERCP at a university hospital in Türkiye between November 20, 2020, and February 11, 2021. Patients were randomly assigned to a music intervention group (Group 1, n = 35) or a control group (Group 2, n = 35). Anxiety and pain were defined as primary outcome measures, while vital signs were secondary outcome measures. Anxiety and pain were assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Anxiety scores significantly decreased in Group 1 following ERCP compared to Group 2 (p = 0.003). Pain scores were lower in Group 1 at the 1st and 2nd hours after ERCP; however, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups (p > 0.05). No significant differences were found between the groups in heart rate, systolic blood pressure, or diastolic blood pressure at any time point (p > 0.05). Oxygen saturation levels were statistically higher in Group 1 than in Group 2 at the 1st hour after ERCP (p = 0.007). These findings suggest that music intervention may be associated with reduced anxiety levels in patients undergoing ERCP and with a transient increase in oxygen saturation in the early post-procedural period. Further large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled studies are needed to confirm these findings and to clarify the role of music intervention in clinical practice.Clinical trial number: NCT04641052 (19 November 2020).Keywords: ERCP, Music, Anxiety, Pain, Vital signsSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is an invasive procedure widely used today1. ERCP is a gastrointestinal endoscopic procedure. It is used in the management of pancreaticobiliary diseases2. It has a higher risk of complications than other endoscopic procedures1.

Endoscopic procedures such as ERCP can cause patients to experience anxiety3, 4, 5. A significant proportion of patients undergoing gastroscopy have been reported to experience high levels of anxiety3. High levels of anxiety may negatively affect the endoscopy process and the patient6. Anxiety may be related to the procedure causing pain, complications that may occur, or fear of being diagnosed with a life-threatening disease or a fatal disease3, 4, 7, 8.

Pain is a common symptom following ERCP9. This symptom can reduce satisfaction10 and is one of the main reasons for increased anxiety3. Currently, holistic approaches are becoming increasingly popular in reducing anxiety and enhancing the quality of life during invasive procedures5. Music, a simple and cost-effective intervention, stands out as a prominent holistic approach5, 11, 12. Furthermore, music increases endorphin secretion, helping to relax the individual and can reduce anxiety by positively affecting pain tolerance11, 13. Nurses can use complementary methods, such as music, as part of holistic nursing care14.

The effects of music intervention on pain and anxiety levels in patients undergoing endoscopic procedures have been investigated in the literature. Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of music in reducing anxiety and pain during gastrointestinal endoscopy. Kannan et al. (2020) reported significantly lower post-intervention anxiety levels in patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy who received receptive music therapy15. Aksu (2023) showed that listening to music 45 min before the procedure reduced pain and anxiety in patients undergoing upper endoscopy16. Bay et al. (2021) reported that adding music to sedation in pediatric endoscopy reduced anxiety and pain17. Demirci et al. (2023) demonstrated that binaural beats reduced anxiety during upper endoscopy without sedation, although they did not affect heart rate or blood pressure18. Çakır et al. (2023) found that music and audiovisual distraction reduced procedural pain and increased satisfaction during colonoscopy19. Collectively, these studies suggest that music is a promising non-pharmacological intervention for reducing anxiety and pain during endoscopic procedures.

However, a comprehensive review of the literature did not identify any study investigating the effects of music on anxiety, pain, and vital signs in patients undergoing ERCP. ERCP differs substantially from routine endoscopy. It is typically longer and more complex and commonly requires deeper sedation or general anaesthesia20. In addition, it carries procedure-specific adverse event risks, such as post-ERCP pancreatitis21, as well as respiratory risks, including hypoxemia during deep sedation22. These differences underscore the need for dedicated studies evaluating the effectiveness and safety of music intervention in the ERCP population. Moreover, existing studies have generally assessed anxiety, pain, or vital signs separately; none of these studies has evaluated all three outcomes together. This limits a comprehensive understanding of the psychological and physiological effects of music. Therefore, this study was designed as the first randomized controlled trial to simultaneously evaluate the effects of music intervention on anxiety, pain, and vital signs in patients undergoing ERCP.

Aim

The study’s objective was to examine music’s effect on these factors in patients undergoing ERCP. In line with this purpose, the hypotheses of the study;

H1: Music intervention before ERCP effectively reduces patients’ anxiety levels.

H2: Music intervention before ERCP effectively reduces the pain intensity of patients.

H3: Music intervention before ERCP has a positive effect on patients’ vital signs.

Methods

Study design

This study is a two-group randomised controlled trial.

Study setting and sample

Sample size was determined by a power analysis conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.2, based on pilot data obtained from 10 patients. To detect the expected difference between the groups, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 36 participants (18 per group), assuming an effect size of 0.82, an alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95. To account for potential missing or incomplete data, the sample size was increased to 70 participants, with 35 patients allocated to each group (Fig. 1).

Randomised Controlled Study Flow Chart.

Participants

The study population consisted of 128 patients who underwent ERCP at the ERCP unit of a university hospital general surgery clinic between 20 November 2020 and 11 February 2021.

Inclusion criteria

Patients above 18 years old, scheduled for ERCP, classified as ASA I-II, without speech or hearing difficulties, without any diagnosed psychiatric disorders, and who volunteered to participate were included.

Excultion citeria

Patients were excluded if they were under 18 years of age, had a diagnosed psychiatric disorder, had hearing or speech impairment, did not volunteer for the study, or underwent emergency ERCP.

Randomization and blinding

Following the initial interview, the randomization process was carried out using the sealed envelope method (EÇ). Papers with odd and even numbers were placed in envelopes. Patients who received a paper with an odd number (1,3,5,7,9,….) were placed in Group 1 (n = 35) and patients who received an even number (2,4,6,8,10….) were placed in Group 2 (n = 35) (the first author). There was no blinding in the study.

Data collection

Outcome measures

“Demographic Information Form” and “Patient Follow-up Form” were the data collection tools.

Demographic information form It, developed by the authors, included 12 items covering demographic characteristics (age, gender, marital and educational status, height, and weight) and medical characteristics (chronic diseases and hospitalization history). It was completed during the initial interview during the admission of the participants to the clinic.

Patient follow-up form This form included assessments using the Visual Analog Scale for Anxiety (VAS-A), the Visual Analog Scale for Pain (VAS), as well as vital signs such as systolic/diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, and SpO₂ levels.

Primary outcomes

The VAS-A was used to assess anxiety, one of the primary outcome measures in the study. The VAS-A is a 10 cm (0–10) scale, where “0” indicates “no anxiety” and “10” reflects “a lot of anxiety.” Higher scores reflect higher levels of anxiety. It has demonstrated concurrent validity with the Spielberger State Anxiety Inventory, showing a correlation of r = 0.4916 to r = 0.821923, indicating its reliability and validity. Patients’ anxiety levels were measured immediately before ERCP (after the music intervention for Group 1) and again 1 h after the procedure. Pre-procedural anxiety is clinically important because the level of anxiety experienced immediately before ERCP may influence patient cooperation, procedural experience, and early recovery. For this reason, anxiety was assessed immediately before ERCP in both groups (after the 15-minute music intervention in Group 1 and at a comparable pre-procedural time point without intervention in Group 2). This design was intended to evaluate the effect of music on the anxiety level that is most clinically relevant, namely, the anxiety present at the time of entering the procedure. Similar measurement timings have also been used in the literature15, 23, 24. Participants indicated their anxiety levels by placing a vertical mark on the VAS-A.

The VAS is a widely used unidimensional tool for assessing pain severity. Its validity and reliability were confirmed in our population. On the VAS, pain intensity is scored from “no pain” at 0 points to “the most severe pain imaginable” at 10 points. A score below 3 points indicates mild pain, 3 to 6 moderate pain, and above 6 severe pain23.

In this study, patients’ pain intensity was measured at the 1st and 2nd hours after the ERCP procedure. Patients reported their pain levels by placing a vertical mark on the VAS.

Secondary outcome

In this study, heart rate (HR), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP-DBP), and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) (measured using finger pulse oximetry, BEURER PO40) were analyzed. They were recorded before the ERCP procedure and again at 1 and 4 h post-ERCP.

Intervention

The study data were collected by first author. Group 1 participants received the music intervention, while control group (Group 2) participants did not receive any intervention. Patients scheduled for ERCP were informed about the study during admission to the General Surgery Clinic. Informed consent was obtained from voluntary participants. Participants were then randomized into the groups by the enrollment coordinator (the first author). Data collection was carried out in the same manner for both groups, and all patients received standard care throughout the study.

Standard care All patients scheduled for ERCP were monitored in the General Surgery Clinic. Vital signs of hospitalized patients were checked every six hours from admission until they were transferred to the ERCP unit. Continuous monitoring was also carried out in the ERCP unit. The ERCP procedures for all patients were performed under general anaesthesia by the same team, following a standardized protocol. Each procedure was conducted by an experienced surgeon (the third author) specialized in ERCP, assisted by a team of skilled and trained nurses and technicians. After the procedure, patients were returned to their rooms in the General Surgery Clinic. Post-procedural pain management followed the standard clinical protocol of the General Surgery department, where analgesics were administered on an as-needed (PRN) basis. The administration of these medications was independent of study groups and based solely on the clinical needs of the patients. Patients were normally discharged either on the first or the second day after the procedure.

Group 1

A music intervention was carried out in the intervention group. Patients were offered a selection of Turkish music genres, including pop, folk songs, hymns, Turkish classical music, arabesque, and traditional dance music. After the admission procedures were completed, each patient’s music preference was determined through an interview. After being transferred to the ERCP unit, patients listened to their preferred music using an MP3 player and headphones (set at 50–60 dB) for 15 min before the ERCP procedure. Disposable headphone covers were used to ensure hygiene.

Group 2

The control group did not listen to music and received only standard health care.

Data analysis

The SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) for Windows 25.0. was used for the Data analysis. Data evaluation was carried out using descriptive statistical methods (number, percentage, mean, standard deviation). Normality tests and kurtosis and skewness values were used to test whether the data were distributed normally.

A chi-square analysis was performed to examine the relationship between the demographic characteristics, chronic disease status, and hospitalization status of the patients in the study according to the groups. Demographic characteristics, chronic disease status, and hospitalization status of participants were tested using chi-squared analysis. Within-group comparisons of anxiety and pain scores were analyzed by dependent samples t-test. Independent samples t-test was used for between-group comparisons. Within-group comparisons of patients’ vital signs were performed with the Friedman test and the Mann-Whitney U test for between-group differences.

The effect size was measured with Cohen’s d, calculated in the G-power program. The d value obtained as a result of the calculations indicates a small effect size if it is 0.20 and below, a medium effect size if it is 0.50 and above, and a large effect size if it is 0.80 and above25.

Secondary outcome

No statistically significant differences were observed between Group 1 and Group 2 in SBP at baseline, at the 1st hour, or at the 4th hour after ERCP (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). Within-group analyses showed no significant changes in SBP over time in Group 1 (p = 0.121), whereas a significant change was observed in Group 2 (p = 0.013), primarily between baseline and the 1st hour after ERCP.

There were no statistically significant differences in DBP or HR values between or within the groups at any time point (p > 0.05).

SpO₂ levels did not differ significantly between the groups at baseline or at the 4th hour after ERCP (p > 0.05). However, SpO₂ levels were statistically higher in Group 1 compared to Group 2 at the 1st hour after ERCP (p = 0.007). Within-group analyses showed no significant change in SpO₂ levels over time in Group 1 (p > 0.05), whereas a significant change was observed in Group 2 across all time points (p = 0.017) (Table 4).

Discussion

ERCP is an invasive procedure performed through side viewing duodenoscope26. Before endoscopic procedures, 46.8% of patients experience procedure-related anxiety3. Music, a non-pharmacological intervention27, is widely used across various patient groups to reduce anxiety28, 29, 30. Studies have shown that music interventions positively affect anxiety in patients undergoing upper gastrointestinal endoscopy15, 16, 18, 31. Our findings are consistent with the recent literature on music interventions in gastrointestinal endoscopy. In our study, the music intervention group demonstrated a significant reduction in post-ERCP anxiety levels (p < 0.001), with significantly lower post-procedural anxiety compared to the control group (p = 0.003, d = 0.75). The level of anxiety reduction was substantially greater in the music group (large effect size, d = 1.14) compared to the control group (medium effect size, d = 0.49). These findings are comparable to those reported in recent endoscopy studies. Aksu (2023) reported significant anxiety reduction (STAI scores, p = 0.006) in upper endoscopy patients receiving music 45 min before the procedure16. Demirci et al. (2023) found reduced state anxiety (STAI scores, p = 0.005) with binaural beats during unsedated upper endoscopy18. Kannan et al. (2020) reported significantly lower post-intervention state anxiety in the music group (p < 0.05)15. ] Bay et al. (2021) demonstrated reduced anxiety (m-YPAS, p < 0.001) in pediatric endoscopy with music as an adjunct to sedation17. The present study extends these findings to the ERCP population, which represents a more complex procedural setting characterized by longer duration, deeper sedation, and higher complication risks20–22. Nevertheless, baseline anxiety scores were relatively low in both groups. This may reflect the use of general anesthesia in our ERCP setting, which differs from most endoscopy studies using conscious sedation or no sedation. Despite the low baseline levels, the observed anxiety reduction was statistically significant and accompanied by a large effect size (d = 1.14), suggesting a significant intervention effect. However, the clinical significance of these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the low baseline anxiety levels. Music intervention may be considered a supportive, non-pharmacological approach for reducing pre-procedural anxiety in ERCP patients.

Pain, defined as an unpleasant experience related to actual or potential tissue damage, is common after ERCP9. Music is known to reduce pain perception by stimulating endorphin release16. Patients who listened to music before and during gastroscopy have been shown to experience less pain after the procedure12, 16, 32. In our study, no significant between-group difference in pain scores was observed after ERCP (1st hour: p = 0.551; 2nd hour: p = 0.099). Although pain scores decreased over time in both groups (p < 0.001), the level of pain reduction was greater in the music group (d = 0.82, large effect size) compared to the control group (d = 0.40, medium effect size), though this difference was not statistically significant between groups. This finding is not consistent with some previous studies reporting a beneficial effect of music on post-procedural pain. Aksu (2023) reported significant pain reduction (VAS scores, p = 0.006) in upper endoscopy patients receiving music16. However, other studies have reported mixed results; Sun et al. (2022) found no significant pain reduction with music during colonoscopy33. Several factors may explain the lack of a significant between-group difference in our study. First, pain scores remained low in both groups and stayed within the mild pain range (VAS < 3), suggesting a possible floor effect that may have limited the ability to detect a clinically meaningful between-group difference. Second, because all ERCP procedures were performed under general anesthesia, procedural pain may have been substantially attenuated, thereby masking the potential effect of music on pain perception. This contrasts with studies reporting greater pain reduction in endoscopic procedures performed under conscious sedation or without sedation16, 17, 19, where music’s effects on pain perception may be more apparent. Third, differences in study design, sample characteristics, and music intervention protocols (including timing, duration and music type) may also have contributed to the observed results. Therefore, the effect of music on post-ERCP pain in the present study does not demonstrate a clinically significant improvement. This finding should be interpreted particularly in the context of general anesthesia, which may mask the subtle analgesic effects of music. Future studies should investigate the effects of music on pain in ERCP patients undergoing conscious sedation and examine whether these effects vary according to sedation depth, procedural complexity, and intervention protocol.

Procedures like ERCP can cause deviations from normal values of vital signs due to increased stress hormones11. Music, on the other hand, can lead to relaxation by increasing endorphin release and positively changing BP, HR, respiratory rate and oxygen consumption by decreasing catecholamine values27. In the literature, the effects of music on vital signs have been studied with varying results. Some studies have found that music positively affects heart rate34, 35, 36, blood pressure34, 35, 36, 37, 38, and SpO₂ levels38. However, other studies, such as Sun et al., reported no significant effects33. These different outcomes might be due to differences in sample characteristics, music type, and the duration of the procedures. In the present study, no statistically significant effects of the music intervention on SBP, DBP, or HR were observed. These findings suggest that the impact of music on most vital signs, including SBP, DBP, and HR, may be limited in patients undergoing ERCP. In our study, SpO₂ levels were statistically higher in Group 1 at the first hour after the procedure. However, the absolute between-group difference was small, and both groups remained within the normal oxygenation range. Therefore, the clinical relevance of this finding appears limited. The small increase in SpO₂ may be related to more relaxed breathing patterns associated with reduced anxiety; however, it should be interpreted as a limited physiological correlate of the intervention rather than as an independent clinically meaningful improvement.

These findings suggest that music intervention may serve as a supportive, non-pharmacological approach for patients undergoing ERCP. However, the findings of the present study should also be interpreted in light of several factors that may have influenced the outcomes beyond the music intervention itself. In the literature, anxiety, pain, and physiological responses during ERCP may be affected by both patient-related factors, such as baseline anxiety and previous procedural experience, and procedure-related factors, including procedural difficulty, anesthesia-related factors, and post-procedural complications15, 18, 21, 39, 40, 41. Furthermore, procedure-related factors, particularly procedural difficulty and duration, may represent important determinants of post-procedural pain and stress response. Although we applied a standardized protocol with a single expert team, variations in procedural complexity may still have influenced individual pain scores. In addition, the use of general anesthesia likely influenced pain perception and physiological responses and may have masked the subtle physiological effects of music, potentially explaining the lack of significant differences in heart rate and blood pressure between groups. Therefore, the observed effects of music, particularly on anxiety, should be interpreted within this clinical context. Future studies should consider adjusting for these covariates through stratified analyses or more stringent inclusion criteria to better isolate the specific therapeutic contribution of music.

Limitations and implications

This study has several limitations. First, although a power analysis was performed and the required sample size for the primary outcome was achieved, the overall sample remained relatively small. This may have limited the ability to detect smaller differences, particularly for secondary outcomes. In addition, the study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, blinding of participants was not feasible due to the nature of the music intervention, which may have introduced performance bias. In addition, outcome assessment was not blinded to group allocation, introducing the possibility of detection bias. This is particularly important because anxiety and pain were assessed using self-reported subjective scales and may therefore have been influenced by individual perception and patient expectations. Furthermore, because no sham intervention was used, the specific effects of music could not be fully distinguished from non-specific effects such as distraction or expectation. Future studies should consider blinded outcome assessment and active control conditions to reduce potential bias and better isolate the specific effects of music intervention. In addition, because a true pre-intervention baseline anxiety score was not obtained in the intervention group, the full magnitude of change in anxiety could not be assessed. Furthermore, the short follow-up period limited the evaluation of longer-term effects of the intervention. Additionally, the administration of rescue analgesics during the recovery period was not systematically recorded. While these were provided on an as-needed basis according to standard clinical protocol, their potential influence on post-procedural pain scores cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, although patients were allowed to select their preferred type of music, the use of different music genres may have contributed to variability in individual responses.

Conclusion

This study is among the first randomized controlled trials to evaluate the effects of music intervention on anxiety, pain, and vital signs together in patients undergoing ERCP. The findings suggest that music may serve as a supportive, non-pharmacological approach for reducing pre-procedural anxiety in this setting. In contrast, no clear effect was observed on pain intensity or most vital signs. These results indicate that the benefits of music intervention in ERCP may be more evident for psychological outcomes than for physiological parameters. However, the single-center design, relatively small sample size, and short follow-up period of this study should be taken into consideration during the interpretation of our findings.

Recommendations

From a clinical perspective, music intervention may be considered a supportive, non-pharmacological approach to help reduce pre-procedural anxiety in patients undergoing ERCP. Given that it is low-cost, easy to implement, and safe, it may be used as an adjunct to standard care rather than as a replacement for routine clinical management, particularly in patients with marked pre-procedural anxiety and in accordance with patient preference. However, because this recommendation is based on findings from a single-center study with a relatively small sample, larger multicenter randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and clarify the role of music intervention in routine clinical practice.
