MNDA promotes immunosuppression in microsatellite instability-high colorectal cancer by facilitating PMN-MDSC infiltration via H3K18 lactylation
Authors: Xuan Zhang, Wen-bin Wang, Xin-Yi Cai, Xiao-qiong Chen, Qing Feng, Quan Yang, Ji-biao Li, Wei Xiong, Tao Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting programmed death-1 (PD-1) have demonstrated significant clinical benefit in colorectal cancer (CRC) with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) status, yet a substantial subset of patients remains resistant to therapy. Understanding the mechanisms of resistance and identifying new therapeutic targets are urgently needed.MethodsWe established a murine MC38-based MSI-H CRC model to investigate the variability in PD-1 treatment response and performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on tumors from patients with PD-1-sensitive and PD-1-resistant CRC. Immune cell subsets and signaling pathways were analyzed using CellChat and AUCell, and gene expression enrichment was performed. Functional assays, including RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, qRT-PCR, and Co-IP, were employed to characterize the role of MNDA and its downstream targets.ResultsscRNA-seq analysis revealed a significant enrichment of polymorphonuclear neutrophil myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs) in PD-1-resistant MSI-H CRC, accompanied by increased lactylation activity and high MNDA expression. Further investigations revealed that MNDA recruits the histone acetyltransferase EP300 to the CXCR2 promoter, mediating H3K18 lactylation and enhancing CXCR2 transcription. This promotes PMN-MDSC infiltration and the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, thereby impairing PD-1 efficacy. Inhibition of the MNDA/EP300-CXCR2 axis reversed H3K18la modification, reduced PMN-MDSC infiltration, remodeled the immune microenvironment, and restored sensitivity to PD-1 therapy.ConclusionsOur study reveals a novel mechanism by which MNDA-driven H3K18 lactylation of the CXCR2 promoter facilitates immune evasion and PD-1 resistance in MSI-H CRC. Targeting the MNDA/EP300-CXCR2 regulatory axis represents a promising strategy for overcoming ICI resistance and enhancing the therapeutic benefit in CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07097-8.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related death globally [1]. Current clinical treatment strategies for CRC include surgical resection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapies, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies [2]. However, the efficacy of ICIs is largely restricted to a small subset of patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) tumors, whereas the majority of microsatellite-stable (MSS) patients show limited clinical benefit [3, 4]. This highlights the pressing need to explore the molecular mechanisms underlying immune evasion and resistance to immunotherapy and to identify novel therapeutic targets that can enhance antitumor immune responses in CRC. With the advancement of molecular biology, especially in the fields of epigenetics, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, new approaches have emerged for understanding CRC progression and overcoming treatment resistance.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a transformative technology for dissecting the complex tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) at unprecedented resolution. By enabling the high-throughput profiling of transcriptomes from thousands of individual cells, scRNA-seq provides comprehensive insights into intratumoral heterogeneity, immune cell composition, and dynamic cell-cell interactions. In CRC, scRNA-seq has revealed diverse populations of immunosuppressive cells, including polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and exhausted CD8⁺ T cells, all of which contribute to immune evasion and resistance to ICIs [5]. Similar findings have been reported in other malignancies, such as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), where scRNA-seq revealed novel tumor-associated macrophage subsets, T-cell dysfunction states, and intercellular signaling networks that drive immune suppression and tumor progression [6, 7]. Moreover, scRNA-seq has facilitated the discovery of new therapeutic targets, such as LAMP3⁺ dendritic cells in HCC or CXCL13⁺ T cells in melanoma, which are associated with immunotherapy responses [8]. These findings underscore the indispensable role of scRNA-seq in understanding the TIME and guiding precision immunotherapy across multiple cancer types.

Histone lactylation is a newly identified posttranslational modification (PTM) derived from lactate, a metabolic byproduct abundantly produced by tumor cells undergoing aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect). First described by Zhang et al. in 2019, histone lactylation, particularly at lysine residues such as H3K18la and H4K12la, has been shown to regulate gene transcription directly in response to metabolic cues [9]. In various cancers, including CRC, HCC, and breast cancer, aberrant histone lactylation promotes oncogene expression, tumor growth, and immune evasion [10, 11]. In the TIME, lactylation modulates immune cell behavior by promoting the M2-like polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), enhancing polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cell function, and suppressing cytotoxic T-cell activation [12, 13]. For example, the lactylation of histones in myeloid cells upregulates arginase-1 (ARG1) and CXCR2, leading to increased recruitment of immunosuppressive cells and decreased antitumor immunity. Furthermore, targeting lactate metabolism or lactylation writers has shown promise in reprogramming the immune microenvironment and sensitizing tumors to ICIs [14]. These findings highlight histone lactylation not only as a metabolic sensor but also as a critical epigenetic regulator of tumor-immune crosstalk, offering novel opportunities for therapeutic intervention in immunotherapy-resistant tumors.

The chemokine receptor CXCR2, a member of the G protein-coupled receptor family, is a central regulator of leukocyte trafficking and tumor-immune interactions. In humans, it binds seven CXC chemokines CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL3, CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL7, and CXCL8, which collectively orchestrate neutrophil recruitment, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling [15]. The ligands of CXCR2 are produced by a diverse set of cells. CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL3 are secreted by tumor cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and endothelial cells, especially under NF-κB-activating stimuli. CXCL5 originates from tumor cells, CAFs, neutrophils, and macrophages, strongly promoting neutrophil infiltration. CXCL6 is expressed by epithelial and endothelial cells and upregulated in inflammatory conditions [16–19]. CXCL7 is derived primarily from platelets, whereas CXCL8 (IL-8) is broadly secreted by tumor cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, neutrophils, and endothelial cells in response to IL-1β, TNF-α, and hypoxia [20]. Thus, while most CXCR2 ligands are inducible and abundant in tumor microenvironments, CXCL7 represents a constitutive source.

Overexpression of CXCR2 or persistent production of its ligands profoundly alters the immune landscape. In tumors, CXCR2 signaling drives the accumulation of neutrophils and PMN-MDSCs, which produce reactive oxygen species, arginase-1, and other suppressive mediators that inhibit cytotoxic T lymphocyte and NK cell activity [21]. This results in an immunosuppressive milieu characterized by T-cell exclusion and impaired responsiveness to checkpoint blockade therapies. CXCR2-dependent infiltration of myeloid cells also promotes neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and enhanced metastatic dissemination [22]. Conversely, disruption of CXCR2 signaling-by genetic deletion or pharmacologic inhibition-reduces suppressive myeloid cell trafficking, restores CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and synergizes with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in preclinical models [23].

In this study, we identified MNDA as a key regulator of histone lactylation-mediated immune suppression in CRC. Mechanistically, MNDA recruits the histone acetyltransferase EP300 to the promoter region of the chemokine receptor gene CXCR2, where it can catalyze H3K18 lactylation and upregulate CXCR2 transcription. This axis facilitates the abnormal infiltration and activation of polymorphonuclear neutrophil myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs), fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and ultimately leading to resistance to PD-1 blockade. Importantly, pharmacological or genetic inhibition of the MNDA/EP300-CXCR2 axis reverses histone lactylation in PMN-MDSCs, remodels the immune landscape, and restores sensitivity to PD-1 therapy. Our findings reveal a novel epigenetic mechanism of immune evasion and suggest a potential combinatorial strategy to overcome ICI resistance in CRC.

Materials and methods

Clinical tissues

Twenty pairs of colorectal cancer samples and corresponding surrounding normal tissues were procured from The Third Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University between 2020 and 2021. All the tissues were promptly preserved in liquid nitrogen after surgery. All the experiments conducted in this study were performed in strict accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Approval Number: KYCS2025-109) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given that this was a retrospective study, the requirement for obtaining patient informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee.

Data collection

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing data were obtained from three patients with MSI-H CRC who were sensitive to PD-1 therapy and three patients who were resistant to PD-1 therapy. Spatial transcriptomic sequencing data of colon cancer were obtained from the 10x Genomics database (https://www.10xgenomics.com). We downloaded RNA-Seq (PTM) data and clinical data from the TCGA-COAD project from the TCGA database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga) and obtained 360 RNA-Seq files of colon cancer tissues. We downloaded the mRNA expression profiles of 213 patients with colon cancer from the GSE17538 project in the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and used them as the validation dataset.

Single-cell sequencing data acquisition, integration and processing

All analyses in this study were conducted using R software (version 4.3.1). We analyzed the scRNA-seq data using the Seurat package (version 4.3.0) and removed data from low-quality and dead cells, retaining 32,135 cells for further analysis. The FindVariableFeatures function from the Seurat package was used to identify the top 2000 highly variable genes using default parameters. After standardization, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on highly variable genes. We used the first 30 principal components for dimensionality reduction and clustering.

Spatial transcriptomic analysis

To study the distribution of cell types, we performed deconvolution analysis on the samples using RCTD. The single-cell data we used were previously annotated, and we ultimately obtained the distribution of 8 cell types in the spatial transcriptome.

Intercellular communication analysis

We utilized the CellChat package (version 1.6.1) (http://www.cellchat.org/) to explore intercellular communication based on “Secreted Signaling”, “ECM-Receptor”, and “Cell‒Cell Contact” ligand-receptor patterns.

Identification of histone emulsification-related genes and aucell analysis

We collected the same set of genes related to lactation as in previous studies. To evaluate the activity of the aforementioned gene sets at the single-cell level, we utilized the AUCell package (version 1.24.0). This package calculates gene expression rankings based on the area under the curve values of model genes using default parameters. Finally, the AUC scores for each cell were embedded into UMAP for visualization using the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.0).

Analysis of immune cell infiltration characteristics

We constructed a lactylation score for TCGA-COAD patients based on RNA-seq data of 330 genes related to lactylation from the TCGA-COAD cohort. To evaluate the immune cell status in patients with colon cancer, we utilized CIBERSORT, a deconvolution method that employs gene expression profiling. Furthermore, we utilized CIBERSORT to investigate the distribution of 28 immune cell types and evaluated their infiltration levels in patients with high and low lactate scores.

GSVA enrichment analysis

We retrieved human gene sets from 50 hallmark pathways using the msigdbr package (version 7.5.1). We subsequently applied GSVA with standard settings to assign pathway activity scores to individual samples through the GSVA package (version 1.40.0).

Plasmid and transfection

In our in vitro experiments, control shRNA (shNC) and MNDA-targeting shRNA (shMNDA) were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Unless otherwise stated, transfections were carried out in PMN-MDSCs seeded at 1 × 106 cells per well in 6-well plates. Cells were transfected with shRNA or overexpression plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24 h of culture, the transfection complexes were applied, and the cells were incubated for 48 h before subsequent assays. MNDA was overexpressed using pcDNA3.1 MNDA (pcDNA/MNDA). ShRNAs or plasmids were transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

RNA sequencing

PMN-MDSCs were added to a 6-well culture plate at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. After 24 h, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, shMNDA was transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen). Cell samples were collected 48 h after transfection and sent to Aksomics Company (Shanghai, China) for RNA sequencing analysis. The samples were sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 in PE 150 mode, and the results were analyzed using DESseq2 software (accomplished by LC Sciences, Hangzhou, China).

Co-IP assay

Briefly, the lysed cells were mixed with nonspecific A/G agarose beads to generate a supernatant. After their removal, the A/G agarose beads, precoupled with EP300 and MNDA antibodies (Abcam), were added to the supernatant. Following the incubation period, the beads were washed, and SDS‒PAGE was used to assess the bound proteins.

ChIP-seq

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed with a High-Sensitivity ChIP Kit (Abcam) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Lysed cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde and then incubated with glycine for 20 min. Following cell lysis, the DNA was sonicated to produce 400–800 bp chromatin fragments. Following sonication, the DNA fragments were subjected to an overnight immunoprecipitation process using magnetic Protein A beads coated with either rabbit nonimmune IgG (as a negative control) or H3K18la antibody (Abcam), and the final product was sent to Aksomics Company (Shanghai, China) for sequencing.

Coimmunoprecipitation and LC‒MS/MS analysis

For the coimmunoprecipitation assay, the cells were lysed and incubated with MNDA antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. The magnetic beads were subsequently added to the protein lysate and incubated at 4 ℃ for 2 h. The supernatant was discarded after centrifugation. The magnetic bead mixture was washed with PBS, and buffer containing SDS was added. The samples were subsequently subjected to LC‒MS/MS analysis. Rabbit IgG was used as a negative control. The LC‒MS/MS analysis was carried out by PTM Bio Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). Finally, we screened out the substrate proteins that could bind to MNDA according to the score and the mass of the detected proteins.

qRT‒PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA with a QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (Qiagen). Quantitative real-time PCR was conducted using SYBR Green PCR mix (Takara), and the gene expression levels were normalized relative to those of GAPDH. The primers used in qRT-PCR analysis are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Abcam) was used to lyse the cells. The extracted proteins were then separated on a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel and subsequently transferred to Immobilon-P membranes (Merck Millipore). After the membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dried milk in PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 for an hour at room temperature, they were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, the membranes were washed three times with Tris-buffered saline containing Tween and then incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotech) for two hours at room temperature. Ultimately, an Amersham Biosciences ECL western Blotting Kit was used to detect the bands. β-actin served as the internal reference. The primary antibodies used in Western blot analysis are listed in Table S2.

Xenograft animal studies

All animal research in the study adhered to the ARRIVE criteria. The relevant guidelines and regulations were strictly followed during all the experiments conducted in this study. All animal research in the study adhered to ARRIVE principles and received approval from the animal ethics committee of Kunming Medical University (Approval Number: KMMUX2025020626).

A xenograft animal study was conducted to assess in vivo cell growth. In summary, three one-month-old female nude mice were generated, and approximately 1 × 106 cells were injected subcutaneously into the axilla of the nude mice. After the mice were euthanized, the tumors were excised, photographed, and measured; the tumor volume was calculated as 0.5 × (length × width2).

One month later, the mice were euthanized in compliance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020) via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) to induce profound anesthesia, which was subsequently confirmed by the cessation of heartbeat and respiration.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 18.0 was used for statistical analysis, and GraphPad Prism 9 was used for mapping. As previously stated, the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). To compare the two groups, an unpaired, two-sided t test was applied; a P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Analysis of tumor immune microenvironment composition in patients with MSI-H CRC with immune resistance

To explore the mechanism underlying resistance to PD-1 therapy, we collected tumor tissues from 3 patients with PD-1-sensitive CRC and 3 patients with PD-1-resistant MSI-H CRC and performed single-cell transcriptome sequencing. After quality control, the transcriptome data from 32,135 cells were retained. Through clustering analysis, eight cell subpopulations were identified, namely, fibroblasts (COL1A1/COL1A2/ACTA2), epithelial cells (KRT18/KRT19/EPCAM), endothelial cells (VWF/PECAM1/CD34), T cells (CD3D/CD3E/CD3G), macrophages (CD14/CD68/C1QC), B cells (CD79A/MS4A1/MZB1), mast cells (TPSAB1/TPSB2/MS4A2), and neutrophils (FCGR3B/G0S2/CXCL8) (Fig. 1A and B). Based on the expression profiles of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), a heatmap was constructed to visualize the top 5 differentially expressed genes for each cell type (Fig. 1C). Next, we used CellChat and scRNA-seq data to analyze the communication between eight cell clusters and identify the intercellular interactions among the eight cell subtypes. We detected various correlations in “cell-cell contact” patterns among these cell subtypes, and further analysis revealed that neutrophils exhibited the most significant numerical changes between samples responsive and resistant to PD-1 therapy (Fig. 1D and E). CellChat analysis revealed that neutrophils and endothelial cells interact the most strongly in the “Secreted Signaling” mode but were most strongly correlated with T cells in the “Cell-Cell Contact” mode (Fig. 1F). We detected a total of 6,207 endothelial cells, which were reclustered into three clusters. Based on their marker gene expression profiles, Clusters 0 and 1 were designated as PMN-MDSCs (S100A8/S100A9/S100A6/CXCR2), and Cluster 2 was designated as CCL3+ neutrophils (CCL3/CCL3L1/CSTB/CCL20) (Fig. 1G). Analysis of neutrophil subsets revealed that the proportion of PMN-MDSCs was significantly greater in patients with drug-resistant cancer than in the drug-sensitive group (Fig. 1H). To evaluate the activity of neutrophil lactylation-related pathways in patients with colorectal cancer treated with PD-1 therapy, we employed the AUCell software package for analysis. The results revealed that the activity of lactylation-related pathways in PMN-MDSCs was significantly greater than that in CCL3+ neutrophils (Fig. 1I). To further identify genes associated with lactylation, we performed an intersection analysis between 167 PMN-MDSC marker genes and 330 lactylation-related genes and identified five key genes: MNDA, S100A6, S100A11, JPT1, and HMGN2 (Fig. 1J and K).

Screening of key genes involved in lactylation modification in PMN-MDSCs

Single-cell data analysis revealed significant correlations between lactylation-related genes (MNDA, S100A6, S100A11, JPT1, and HMGN2) and PMN-MDSC subtypes and immunosuppression in patients with MSI-H CRC. Furthermore, we integrated public databases to screen key lactylation-related genes. Based on RNA-seq data and clinical information of 330 lactylation-related genes from TCGA-COAD, we constructed a lactylation score for the TCGA-COAD cohort. To explore the immune characteristics of patients with differential lactylation scores, we estimated the immune infiltration characteristics by assessing the proportions of 28 immune cell types in groups with different lactylation scores. Immunosuppressive tumor cells, including neutrophils and M2 macrophages, were more abundant in the high-lactate scoring group, suggesting that patients with the high-lactate subtype exhibited an immunosuppressive tumor progression microenvironment (Fig. 2A). We subsequently explored immune function activity and reported that the different groups exhibited completely different immune activation states. The high-lactate group exhibited increased immune activity. Further analysis revealed that MNDA, which is highly expressed by PMN-MDSCs, was strongly correlated with immune function (Fig. 2B). GSVA revealed that patients with high MNDA expression were significantly enriched in activated signaling pathways, mainly the T-cell receptor, B-cell receptor, chemokine, cell adhesion molecule, and JAK-STAT signaling pathways (Fig. 2C). Similar results were obtained from the GEO database (GSE17538) (Fig. 2D and F). The above results indicate that the lactylation-related gene MNDA in PMN-MDSCs may promote CRC progression by affecting immune cell function.

NMDA promotes PMN-MDSC infiltration into tumor tissues

To explore the individual differences in PD-1 expression in the treatment of MSI-H CRC, we constructed a subcutaneous tumorigenesis model of MSI-H CRC using a murine-derived cell line, MC38, which was inoculated into C57BL/6 mice to form an immune-infiltrating tumor microenvironment. After 7 days, when the tumors reached an appropriate size, anti-PD-1 therapy (tislelizumab, 2 mg/kg, intravenous injection every other day) was administered, and the body weight and tumor size of the mice were measured regularly. As shown in Fig. 4A and C, there were significant differences in the sensitivity of anti-PD-1 therapy for MSI-H CRC. Previous studies have shown that MNDA and lactylation modifications are highly expressed in PMN-MDSCs. To determine the expression level of MNDA in PMN-MDSCs and its impact on PMN-MDSC infiltration into the tumor microenvironment, we conducted animal model experiments. As shown in Fig. 5A and E, overexpression of Mnda significantly increased tumor weight and volume, increased PMN-MDSC infiltration in the tumor immune microenvironment, and reduced the efficacy of PD-1 therapy. However, inhibiting the PMN-MDSC chemokine receptor Cxcr2 significantly improved the efficacy of PD-1 therapy. Furthermore, through qRT‒PCR analysis of clinical tissue samples, we found that the expression of the chemokine CXCL2 tended to increase in tumor tissues (Fig. 5F). These results suggest that the high expression of the CXCR2 receptor in PMN-MDSCs and the high expression of the chemokine CXCL2 in tumor cells may be important factors promoting PMN-MDSC infiltration and creating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. These findings indicate that MNDA may promote PMN-MDSC infiltration into tumor tissues and form an immunosuppressive microenvironment by regulating the CXCL2/CXCR2 activation axis.

MNDA recruits EP300 to promote H3K18 lactylation at the CXCR2 promoter region

To explore the molecular mechanism by which MNDA promotes PMN-MDC infiltration in the TIME, we suppressed MNDA expression and analyzed differentially expressed genes before and after MNDA interference through RNA-seq (Fig. 6A and B). Next, we performed KEGG and GSEA enrichment analyses on the differentially expressed genes. After MNDA interference, several important signaling pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and EP300 histone acetylation, were significantly enriched (Fig. 6C and D). To identify potential targets of lactylation modification, we performed ChIP-seq experiments targeting H3K18 lactylation combined with high-throughput sequencing in PMN-MDSCs isolated from murine MC38 models. As shown in Fig. 6E and F, H3K18la modification was enriched in the promoter regions of 2019 genes. KEGG enrichment analysis also revealed that multiple important biological processes, including signaling molecule interactions and signal transduction, are regulated by H3K18la modification (Fig. 6G). To explore the specific molecular mechanism through which MNDA regulates PMN-MDSC infiltration through lactylation modification, 10 potential target genes were identified through the intersection of the RNA-seq and ChIP-seq datasets (Fig. 7A). The ChIP-seq data also revealed that H3K18la modification was enriched in the CXCR2 promoter region (Fig. 7B). In addition, after treatment with the lactate inhibitor 2-DG and MNDA knockdown, the level of H3K18la modification was significantly reduced (Fig. 7C). Western blot analysis revealed that the protein expression of CXCR2 gradually decreased with increasing concentrations of 2-DG. Moreover, after MNDA knockdown, both the mRNA and protein expression levels of CXCR2 were significantly reduced (Fig. 7D and F). To explore the specific molecular mechanism through which MNDA regulates CXCR2 lactylation, we conducted IP-mass experiments in PMN-MDSCs isolated from murine MC38 models. The results showed that MNDA interacts with the H3K18la modification enzyme EP300, which was further validated by molecular docking software (Fig. 7G and H). The results of endogenous protein coimmunoprecipitation also demonstrated the interaction between MNDA and EP300 (Fig. 7I). In addition, EP300 knockdown significantly reduced the mRNA expression level of CXCR2 (Fig. 7J). MNDA knockdown inhibited the enrichment of EP300 in the CXCR2 promoter region, whereas MNDA overexpression significantly increased the enrichment of EP300 in the CXCR2 promoter region (Fig. 7K). The above results suggest that MNDA promotes CXCR2 transcription by recruiting EP300 to mediate H3K18la modification in the CXCR2 promoter region (Fig. 8).

Discussion

CRC remains a leading global health challenge, ranking third in incidence and second in cancer-related mortality worldwide [24]. Despite the advent of immunotherapy, particularly ICIs targeting PD-1/PD-L1, durable clinical benefits are predominantly confined to a subset of patients with dMMR or MSI-H tumors [25]. However, emerging clinical data reveal considerable heterogeneity in treatment response even among patients with MSI-H CRC. Although the study by Cercek et al. (2022) revealed remarkable complete response rates in patients with localized dMMR/MSI-H rectal cancer treated with PD-1 blockade [26], other reports suggest that a significant proportion of patients with MSI-H CRC still fail to respond effectively [27]. Mechanistic insights summarized by Sharma et al. (2017) highlight the multifactorial nature of immune resistance, including the presence of immunosuppressive cell subsets, aberrant metabolic programming, and epigenetic remodeling [28]. These findings underscore the need to explore novel targets that modulate the TIME to overcome resistance and optimize immunotherapeutic efficacy.

In our study, using both murine MSI-H CRC models and scRNA-seq data from clinical samples, we revealed a distinct immunosuppressive signature in PD-1-resistant tumors characterized by enrichment of PMN-MDSCs. Subsequent AUCell and pathway enrichment analyses revealed heightened activity of lactylation-related genes, notably MNDA, in these cells. Through transcriptomic and chromatin profiling, we established that MNDA recruits the histone acetyltransferase EP300 to the CXCR2 promoter, mediating H3K18 lactylation and driving the transcriptional upregulation of the expression of CXCR2, a key chemokine receptor involved in PMN-MDSC trafficking [29, 30]. Functional experiments confirmed that MNDA overexpression increased PMN-MDSC infiltration and diminished PD-1 therapeutic efficacy, whereas targeted inhibition of MNDA, EP300, or CXCR2 reversed these effects and sensitized tumors to PD-1 blockade [31]. This mechanistic cascade delineates a novel epigenetic-metabolic axis contributing to immune resistance and provides a potential intervention strategy to reshape the TIME.

Lactylation is a recently identified epigenetic modification derived from lactate metabolism and was first characterized in 2019 by Zhang et al., who demonstrated that histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) can stimulate gene transcription [32]. Tumor cells, characterized by aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), produce large amounts of lactate that can diffuse into immune cells, modulating their function via lactylation. Histone lactylation has emerged as a critical modulator of the tumor immune landscape, promoting immune suppression through effects on TAMs, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and dendritic cells [33, 34]. Several studies have reported the immunoregulatory role of lactylation. Gao et al. (2022) reported that lactate-induced histone lactylation enhanced the transcription of genes involved in angiogenesis and immune evasion. Yang et al. (2023) further demonstrated that lactylation promoted an immunosuppressive phenotype in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, with H3K18la driving the expression of immunosuppressive mediators such as ARG1 and CCL2 [35]. These studies underscore the functional relevance of lactylation in reprogramming immune cells within the TIME [36, 37]. However, none of these works addressed the role of lactylation specifically in PMN-MDSCs in colorectal cancer, leaving a significant gap in our current findings.

MNDA, traditionally viewed as a nuclear factor involved in myeloid cell development and inflammatory responses, has not previously been implicated in epigenetic remodeling [38, 39]. Our study reveals that MNDA is a novel chromatin adaptor that recruits EP300 to the CXCR2 promoter in PMN-MDSCs, thereby linking inflammatory signaling with epigenetic modification. This interaction facilitates H3K18la enrichment and transcriptional activation of CXCR2, positioning MNDA at the interface of immune signaling and chromatin remodeling. To date, there are no reports associating MNDA with lactylation or PD-1 resistance in CRC, indicating that our study is the first to reveal this mechanistic role.

EP300 has long been studied as a histone acetyltransferase and coactivator in multiple oncogenic pathways [40]. Recent studies have expanded its enzymatic repertoire to include histone lactylation, particularly catalysis of H3K18la and H4K12la [41]. EP300 is often upregulated in tumors and is known to coactivate transcription factors such as HIF-1α, STAT3, and MYC. In the context of tumor immunity, EP300 has been shown to regulate FoxP3 expression in regulatory T cells and modulate dendritic cell activation [42, 43]. However, no previous study has explored EP300 recruitment to specific chemokine gene promoters by immune-specific transcription factors such as MNDA or its role in MDSC-driven immunosuppression in CRC. Our work thus provides new insight into how EP300 activity is spatially and functionally directed within suppressive immune subsets.

CXCR2 is a chemokine receptor known to mediate neutrophil and MDSC chemotaxis in response to ELR+ CXC chemokines such as CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL5 [44, 45]. Increased CXCR2 expression is associated with increased MDSC infiltration, angiogenesis, and therapeutic resistance in multiple cancers, including lung and pancreatic cancers [46]. Pharmacological blockade of CXCR2 has shown promise in preclinical models because it enhances CD8+ T-cell infiltration and improves ICI efficacy [47]. However, to our knowledge, the transcriptional regulation of CXCR2 via histone lactylation has not been described in CRC. Our findings uniquely demonstrate that H3K18la deposition at the CXCR2 promoter, mediated by MNDA-EP300, is critical for its upregulation in PMN-MDSCs and that disrupting this axis reverses PD-1 resistance. Thus, CXCR2 joins a growing list of chemokines and receptors whose expression is epigenetically regulated by metabolic cues within the TIME.

Our study provides compelling evidence for the functional relevance of the MNDA/EP300-CXCR2 axis in PD-1 resistance; however, certain limitations should be noted. Pharmacological inhibitors of MNDA and EP300 remain experimental; thus, clinical translation will require further development and toxicological assessment. Despite these limitations, our study introduces several notable innovations. It expands the functional repertoire of histone lactylation and establishes its role in immune evasion within the CRC TIME. It identifies MNDA as a critical upstream regulator of lactylation-specific chromatin modification, a function not previously attributed to this gene. It reveals CXCR2 transcription as a lactylation-dependent process, linking metabolic cues to chemokine-driven immune suppression. Most importantly, this study provides a rationale for targeting this epigenetic-metabolic axis as a therapeutic strategy to enhance ICI efficacy.

In summary, we provide comprehensive molecular and functional evidence that MNDA, by recruiting EP300, promotes H3K18 lactylation at the CXCR2 promoter, thereby driving PMN-MDSC infiltration and establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Targeted disruption of this axis remodels the TIME and restores responsiveness to PD-1 therapy. These findings not only deepen our understanding of immune resistance mechanisms but also offer translational promise for designing combination therapies that integrate metabolic and epigenetic modulation with immunotherapy in CRC.
