Finding Catalyst Design Principles for Oxygen Evolution using High‐Throughput Optimizations and Electrochemical Symmetry
Authors: Nerea Azcona‐Aliende, Paramaconi Rodriguez, Federico Calle‐Vallejo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The global‐scale use of water electrolyzers for sustainable hydrogen production is currently limited by the lack of efficient, abundant, and stable catalysts for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Unfortunately, OER models based on adsorption energies of the intermediates and their scaling relations have led to the customary but unsafe use of heuristic rules as design principles for catalyst design. Conversely, guidelines derived from the concept of electrochemical symmetry have yet to be exploited despite their simplicity and quantitative nature. Here, a high‐throughput analysis on a sizable dataset is performed using a variety of scaling‐free, scaling‐based optimizations, and combinations thereof, while keeping track of the material's degree of electrochemical symmetry. This analysis provides an important conclusion: a visible improvement in the OER activity is statistically linked to an increase in the number of electrochemical steps larger than 1.23 eV. This is not a mere rule of thumb but a quantitative criterion to safely guide the design and enhancement of OER catalyst materials.

Introduction

The conundrum of climate change, finite resources, and ever‐growing global energy demand can be solved by developing and deploying technologies that fulfill our energy requirements while safeguarding the environment.[ 1 , 2 , 3 ] In this context, green hydrogen produced from carbon‐decoupled renewable sources is a promising energy carrier, fuel, and chemical feedstock.[ 4 , 5 ] Therefore, technologies associated with its production, such as water electrolyzers, currently receive considerable attention.[ 6 ]

Water electrolysis (2H2O→O2+2H2) is the electricity‐driven generation of hydrogen and oxygen from water. The process involves two half reactions: the anodic oxygen evolution reaction (OER, in acid media: 2H2O→O2+4H++4e−) and the cathodic hydrogen evolution reaction (HER, 4H++4e−→2H2). At least one of these processes exhibits slow kinetics, requiring high overpotentials. In particular, the OER is sluggish because it involves four proton–electron transfers.[ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ] The most active OER catalysts are based on Ir and Ru oxides, which are scarce on the Earth's crust and have elevated market prices.[ 2 , 11 ] Arguably, the lack of efficient and cost‐effective OER electrocatalysts is a major obstacle for the global‐scale use of water electrolyzers.[ 12 ] Hence, substantial improvements in the activity, stability, and durability of OER electrocatalysts are urgently needed.[ 7 , 13 , 14 ]

To partially or totally avoid expensive and time‐consuming trial‐and‐error experiments, computational electrocatalysis models can be used. One of the cornerstones of theory‐guided electrocatalyst design is the use of descriptors tightly linked to predicted OER activities. Descriptors are easily quantifiable energetic, electronic, or structural properties of materials that capture trends in their electrocatalytic activity, allowing researchers to swiftly make predictions via interpolation.[ 15 , 16 ] If descriptors and activities are strongly correlated, experimental researchers can be provided with a set of promising new candidates or guidelines for the optimization of existing materials.[ 17 ]

Descriptors can be divided at least into two categories: those based on bulk properties and those based on surface properties.[ 18 ] Some examples of bulk‐based descriptors are bulk energies of formation,[ 19 , 20 ] eg orbital filling,[ 21 ] number of outer electrons,[ 22 , 23 ] and p‐band center of bulk O ions.[ 24 , 25 ] In turn, surface‐based descriptors include but are not restricted to surface d‐band center,[ 26 ] coordination numbers,[ 16 ] work function, electron localization function, and (integrated) crystal orbital overlap population (coop) and crystal orbital Hamilton population (cohp).[ 22 , 27 , 28 ] Importantly, there have also been attempts to provide manifold descriptions to correlate the OER activity of materials with assortments of physicochemical features, involving bulk, surface, and/or energy‐related properties.[ 18 , 29 ] In particular, linear adsorption‐energy scaling relations fall in the category of energetic surface descriptors and play a fundamental role in the search for new electrocatalysts, as they help capture activity trends in a simple manner.[ 30 , 31 ]

In recent years, numerous attempts have been made to break scaling relations to improve the electrocatalyst performance for a number of reactions.[ 32 , 33 ] Specifically for the OER, computational and experimental studies have sought to stabilize *OOH without stabilizing *OH,[ 7 , 34 ] and other studies have suggested to stabilize the transition state leading to *OOH formation.[ 35 ] This has led to an exaggerated focus on a single intermediate (*OOH) and a single scaling relation (*OOH vs *OH). However, various studies suggest that breaking this relationship does not guarantee that electrocatalysts will achieve higher electrocatalytic activity.[ 36 , 37 ] Alternatively, a class of procedures called “delta‐epsilon optimization” provides catalyst‐specific, quantitative guidelines to enhance known catalysts,[ 38 ] while providing the option of breaking scaling relations and/or following them. In spite of their convenience, these optimizations remain underexplored, such that their full potential might not have been realized yet, and their underlying principles remain elusive.

The aim of this work is to show that the main net effect of catalyst optimizations breaking and/or preserving scaling relations is an increase in the electrochemical symmetry of OER catalytic materials. We illustrate this by means of high‐throughput delta, epsilon, delta‐then‐epsilon, and simultaneous delta‐epsilon optimizations applied to a sizable adsorption‐energy dataset. As a result, a useful and statistically sound design principle is obtained to guide electrocatalyst optimization: increasing the number of OER electrochemical steps exceeding 1.23 eV from 1 to 2–3 or from 2 to 3 appreciably reduces the OER overpotential.

Experimental Section

OER Modeling

The curated dataset collected from literature is shown in Table S1 of the Supporting Information. It includes 159 adsorption energies of *O, *OH, and *OOH on 11 families of materials: single‐atom catalysts (SACs, 8% of the total),[ 39 , 40 ] monoxides (MO, 6%),[ 20 , 23 , 41 ] dioxides (MO2, including RuO2 and IrO2, 3%),[ 41 ] doped and undoped TiO2 (35%),[ 42 ] perovskite oxides (BaNiOx (3%),[ 43 ] SrxNayRuO3 (3%),[ 44 ] LaMO3 (8%) and SrMO3 perovskites (8%)[ 20 , 23 , 41 ]), double perovskites (Sr2MIrO6 (3%) and La1.5Sr0.5NiMn0.5Ru0.5O6 (LSNMR), 2%),[ 45 , 46 ] and metalloporphyrins (23%).[ 47 ] While we do not consider carbides, chalcogenides, or pnictogenides, our dataset contains a broad spectrum of materials. In many cases, the data come from studies where good agreement was found between density functional theory (DFT) data and experiments. Under anodic polarization conditions, surface reconstructions may occur that form new active sites.[ 48 , 49 , 50 ] Although our dataset includes some reconstructed electrodes and oxides with oxygen vacancies to reflect this (e.g., see Sr x Na y RuO3 and BaNiO x in Table S1, Supporting Information), our main interest is to observe broad trends in materials optimization as a function of electrochemical symmetry and the data in Table S1 (Supporting Information) are suitable for that purpose.

OER Overpotential and Electrochemical Symmetry

A widespread metric for the OER activity within this framework is the thermodynamic overpotential (ηOER),[ 41 , 59 ] which is calculated from the free energy of the potential‐limiting step (PLS), namely the largest positive reaction energy of Equations ((1), (2), (3))–(4). (9) ηOER=max(ΔG1,ΔG2,ΔG3,ΔG4)/e−−E0

For the ideal catalyst, ηOER=0 V, as ΔG 1 = ΔG 2 =ΔG 3 = ΔG 4 = 1.23 eV at 0 V vs RHE. Equivalently, ΔG 1 = ΔG 2 =ΔG 3 = ΔG 4 = 0 eV at E 0 = 1.23 V vs RHE. Hence, from a thermodynamic perspective, the ideal catalyst is fully symmetric. In fact, highly efficient enzymes display approximately symmetric free‐energy profiles for various reactions.[ 60 , 61 , 62 ] In this context, the electrochemical step symmetry index (ESSI) was defined to quantify how much real catalysts approach the ideal one.[ 37 , 63 , 64 ] (10) ESSI=1n∑i=1n(ΔGi+e−−E0) where ΔGi+ are the free energies of the reaction steps in Equations ((1), (2), (3))–(4) equal to or larger than 1.23 eV at 0 V vs RHE. Numerical examples on the assessment of ESSI are given in Section S1. We note that n in Equation (10) is the number of steps larger than 1.23 eV and provides a classification method for OER materials.[ 64 ] Besides, the causality relationship between ηOER and ESSI has been demonstrated.[ 65 ] Briefly, ESSI is the average excess free energy of the OER steps that are likely to be rate‐limiting. If ESSI is low, the reaction steps are balanced, resulting in low ηOER. If ESSI is large, energetic asymmetry leads to high ηOER. A joint analysis of ESSI, n , the number of outer electrons (NM), and eg filling is given in Section S7. Briefly, optimal perovskites for the OER display eg slightly larger than 1.[ 10 ] A high‐spin perovskite with eg=1 has NM=4, while a low‐spin perovskite with eg=1 has NM=7. These values correspond to the lower and upper limits for n=3 observed in Figure S1 (Supporting Information).

Delta‐Epsilon Optimization

Delta‐epsilon optimization is a set of quantitative strategies to optimize OER materials by breaking or following scaling relations.[ 38 ] Scaling‐based optimization is driven by a parameter δ that tunes adsorption energies maintaining the scaling relations between *O, *OH, and *OOH. Consequently, modifying ΔGOH by δ corresponds to an identical change in ΔGOOH and a change of 2δ in ΔGO. These ratios stem from the theoretical slopes of the scaling relations between *O, *OH, and *OOH. In principle, δ can be positive or negative, which weakens or strengthens ΔGOH, respectively.[ 38 ] Experimental implementation of δ optimization can be achieved through, e.g., lattice strain, coordination, ligand effects, etc. These effects are more clearly observed and predictable on metals and, hence, have so far been more widely exploited for oxygen reduction.[ 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 ]

Scaling‐free optimization is based on a parameter ε that only affects the adsorption energy of *OOH. By definition, ε is a stabilization, so it is equal to zero or negative, and it does not change *OH or *O adsorption. ε optimizations have been schematized extensively,[ 32 , 34 , 70 , 71 ] but in our view, their experimental implementation remains challenging. An interesting case is the breaking of the *OH vs *O scaling relation on IrO2 by means of electrolyte effects,[ 72 ] which modify the adsorption of *OH without significantly affecting that of *O.

In previous work,[ 38 ] δ and ε were respectively constrained to [−0.3, 0.3] and [−0.3, 0] eV to ensure alignment with state‐of‐the‐art experimental active‐site engineering techniques, which offer changes within those ranges without drastically changing the nature and spatial configuration of the active sites. For numerous materials, it was found that both δ and ε reached the extremes of those ranges (i.e., ±0.30 eV),[ 38 ] suggesting that they might be further optimized. Hence, we intendedly removed those constraints in this work to illustrate that OER materials optimization 1) is tightly linked to electrochemical symmetry, and 2) generally increases n.

The effects of δ and ε on the reaction energies are shown in Equations ((11), (12), (13))–(14) and additional details are given in Section S2. Note that δ=ε=0 makes Equations ((11), (12), (13))–(14) identical to Equations ((5), (6), (7))–(8). In addition, because delta‐epsilon optimization is energy‐conserving, the sum of Equation ((11), (12), (13))–(14) is equal to ΔGO2=4.92 eV. (11) ΔG1=ΔGOH+δ (12) ΔG2=ΔGO−ΔGOH+δ (13) ΔG3=ΔGOOH−ΔGO−δ+ε (14) ΔG4=ΔGO2−ΔGOOH−δ−ε

In the following, we will show the results of unconstrained scaling‐based (δ), scaling‐free (ε), sequential (first δ then ε, denoted δ+ε), and simultaneous (denoted δε) high‐throughput optimizations on the 159 materials in Table S1 (Supporting Information) to deduce materials design guidelines based on electrochemical symmetry. δ and ε were found using MS Excel Solver by minimizing Equation (9). Because δ and ε are addends in Equations ((11), (12), (13))–(14) and each material has its own δi and εi, we found no dependence on the ansatz given to Solver. This approach enables a swift, consistent, simultaneous, high‐throughput evaluation of electrocatalysts.

Results and Discussion

Impact of Materials Optimization on OER Free‐Energy Diagrams

Energy diagrams illustrate the changes in the reaction coordinate of the four‐electron OER. Let us consider two materials: a porphyrin with Co centers and –BH2 ligands (Co‐BH2) and TiO2 doped with Ta (Ta‐TiO2). Figure 1 shows the OER free‐energy diagrams at 1.23 V vs RHE for these two materials before and after delta‐epsilon optimizations. Initially (in black, Figure 1a), Co‐BH2 exhibits two steps (ΔG 1 ≈ ΔG 2 = 1.24 eV) slightly exceeding 1.23 eV, while ΔG 3 = 1.81 eV. δ optimization (Figure 1a, red, δ = 0.29 eV) and δ+ε optimization (Figure 1b, blue, δ = 0.29 eV, ε=0) locate the three steps at 1.53 eV. Figure 1b shows the behavior of the material optimized with ε = −0.57 eV, which significantly reduces ΔG3, enhances electrochemical symmetry, and maintains all energies close to 1.23 eV for all steps. A nearly ideal free‐energy landscape is reached through simultaneous optimization (δ = −0.01 eV, ε = −0.59 eV, Figure 1b, orange).

Free‐energy diagrams for the OER at 1.23 V vs RHE for a,b) Co‐BH2 and c,d) Ta‐TiO2 (6 cM refers to the sixfold coordination of Ti atoms in doped TiO2(110)).[ 43 ] For comparison, the initial diagrams are shown in black (left and right) together with those after δ (pink, left), ε (green, right), δ+ε (purple, right), and δε (orange, right) optimizations.

At an applied potential of 1.23 V vs RHE, all intermediates of the OER on Ta‐TiO2 exhibit negative free energies. We see that δ, δ+ε, and δε (Figure 1c–d) optimizations increase the symmetry of ΔG2, ΔG3 , and ΔG4, at the expense of lowering ΔG1. ε optimization (Figure 1d, green) alone has no effect, as the PLS of Ta‐TiO2 is Step 2. Consequently, δ, δ+ε, and δε lead to similar free‐energy diagrams. All in all, Co‐BH2 seems easy to optimize in different ways, while Ta‐TiO2 is not.

Besides, let us consider two practical examples of δ optimization. First, SrRuO3 has ΔG OH = 1.37 eV, ΔG O − ΔG OH = 1.23 eV, n=2, and ηOER=0.75 V. We predict that δ = 0.31 eV should lead to a catalyst with ΔG OH = 1.67 eV, ΔG O − ΔG OH = 1.54 eV, n=3, and ηOER=0.44 V. In experiments, Na was added during synthesis to produce Sr0.90Na0.10RuO3, which showed an important activity and stability enhancement with respect to SrRuO3. Calculations on Sr0.88Na0.12RuO3 displayed ΔG OH = 1.56 eV, ΔG O − ΔG OH = 1.60 eV, n=3, and ηOER=0.37 V.[ 44 ] Therefore, Na doping induces a scaling‐based optimization of SrRuO3 and increases n from 2 to 3. Second, consider a Ni porphyrin with —H as the sole ligand species in the porphyrin ring (denoted Ni—H). This material has ΔG OH = 1.82 eV, ΔG O − ΔG OH = 1.84 eV, n=2, and ηOER=0.61 V. We predict that δ = −0.24 eV should lead to a porphyrin with ΔG OH = 1.58 eV, ΔG O − ΔG OH = 1.60 eV, n=3, and ηOER=0.37 V. Indeed, replacing —H by —OH in the porphyrin ring leads to ΔG OH = 1.58 eV, ΔG O − ΔG OH = 1.63 eV, n=3, and ηOER=0.40 V.[ 47 ] Hence, the ligand effect of —OH induces a scaling‐based optimization of Ni porphyrins and increases n from 2 to 3.

Unfortunately, there are no obvious examples of ε optimization in our dataset. While there are some computational examples in the literature,[ 71 ] the main problem is often to stabilize only *OOH and not *O and *OH, as pointed out by Hellman and coworkers.[ 73 ]

High‐Throughput Optimization of the OER Catalytic Activity

Apart from the case‐by‐case analysis in Figure 1, one can make high‐throughput delta‐epsilon optimizations. For example, to perform simultaneous δε optimization, one needs:

1) An electrochemical‐step matrix of dimension m×4, where m is the total number of catalysts in the dataset (m=159 in this work). (15) ES¯=|ΔG11⋮ΔG1m ΔG21⋮ ΔG2m ΔG31⋮ ΔG3m ΔG41⋮ ΔG4m|

2) A matrix of free parameters of dimension m×4. (16) FP¯=|δ1⋮δm δ1 ⋮δm −δ1+ε1⋮−δm+εm −δ1−ε1⋮−δm−εm|

If one is only interested in δ optimization, all εi are zero in Equation (16). Similarly, for ε optimization all δi are zero.

3) An algorithm to find in each case the values of δi and εi that minimize the overpotential (see Tables S2–S5, Supporting Information). The output is a matrix of optimized parameters and overpotentials of dimension m×3. (17) O¯=|δ1⋮δm ε1⋮εm ηOER1⋮ηOERm|

With this output matrix one can calculate the final adsorption energies of *OH, *O, and *OOH, and the new ESSI and n.

The initial and final trends in ηOER can be assessed by means of volcano‐type activity plots using different descriptors: ΔGO, ΔGO−ΔGOH, ΔGO−ΔGOOH, structural parameters, bulk energies of formation, etc.[ 20 , 41 , 47 , 52 , 74 ] Among them, ΔG2=ΔGO−ΔGOH is arguably the most widespread OER descriptor. Materials that bind the OER intermediates strongly are found on the left (PLS: Step 3), whereas those on the right side (PLS: Step 2) bind weakly. Figure 2 shows the volcano plots for the initial and optimized data, and the materials within them are classified depending on n.

Sabatier‐type activity plot showing −ηOER as a function of ΔGO−ΔGOH for a) initial data, and data after b) δ, c) ε, d) δ+ε, and e) δε optimizations. The data are classified depending on the number of electrochemical steps larger than 1.23 eV at 0 V vs RHE: blue (n=1), red (n=2), and white (n=3). The ideal catalyst (n=4, ηOER=0), is shown in yellow. The volcano lines of the initial data are shown in all panels.

For the initial data in Figure 2a, materials with n=3 are close to the summit and span a narrow range of ΔG2 around 1.60 eV. In contrast, materials with n=1, 2 are spread on both sides of the volcano. Razzaq and Exner[ 36 ] have shown that the Butler–Volmer equation in the Tafel regime (η>0.1 V) can be written in terms of the descriptor Gmax(η) for the OER as (18) jOER(η)≈4kBTheΓe−Gmax(η)+ckBT where kB is the Boltzmann constant, T the absolute temperature, h the Planck constant, e the electron charge, Γ the number of active sites per surface area, and c a constant relating the OER kinetics and thermodynamics. In Equation (18), small values of Gmax(η) are beneficial for jOER(η). Interestingly, previous work[ 64 ] showed that Gmax(η) decreases rapidly as a function of η when n=3. Besides, for η>0.1 V, the average Gmax(η) of materials with n=3 is visibly smaller than those with n=1, 2.[ 64 ] The material with the lowest ηOER before optimization is SrCoO3 (0.31 V) and n=3 (steps 1, 2, 3). In turn, the catalyst with the highest ηOER is LaTiO3 (2.82 V) and n=2 (steps 3, 4).

In Figure 2b, δ optimization leaves no materials with n=1, such that the procedure makes all materials display at least two steps above 1.23 eV. Besides, δ optimization displaces all materials with n=2,3 toward the top of the volcano. Materials with n=3 are mostly around the top or to the right, while a few materials with n=2 are on the left. The most active material is now Ni‐doped TiO2, with ηOER=0.15 V and n=3 (steps 2, 3, 4). The highest ηOER corresponds to Cr‐doped TiO2 and is 0.92 V, with n=2 (steps 1, 3).

Furthermore, ε optimization decreases ηOER for materials limited by Step 3 (Figure 2c). Materials for which the PLS is Step 1 or 2 generally lie at the right side of the volcano plot and are not affected by ε optimization, as *OOH is an intermediate only in steps 3 and 4. Materials limited by Step 4 are negatively affected by stabilizing *OOH. Interestingly, Figure 2c shows various materials on the left side approaching the ideal catalyst, while the materials on the right side tend to remain unchanged. The material with the lowest ηOER is in this case Co‐BH2, with a nearly null overpotential (0.01 eV) and n=2 (steps 1, 2). It is worth noting that despite having n=2, the four OER steps of Co‐BH2 are close to 1.23 eV. The highest overpotential (2.14 eV) is displayed by LaTiO3, which has n=2 (steps 3, 4).

Sequential δ+ε optimization leads to a volcano plot similar to ε optimization, see Figure 2c–d. However, δ+ε optimization leads to lower average overpotentials in the dataset compared to ε optimization. Besides, δ+ε optimization leads to an increase in the number of materials with n=3 from 20% (ε optimization) to 39% (δ+ε optimization). Consequently, the final number of materials with n=1,2 is lower after δ+ε optimization. A double perovskite (LSNMR@Ni) is the only optimized material with n=1 (ηOER=0.71 V). The material undergoing the largest activity change is Mn‐functionalized graphitic material ((FGM), a type of SAC), consisting of graphitic layers functionalized with MnN4 sites, with a final overpotential of 0.08 eV. This material has n=2 (steps 2, 3) and high symmetry of the four OER steps. The material with the highest overpotential is again LaTiO3, displaying ηOER=1.84 V and n=2 (steps 3, 4).

In turn, simultaneous δε optimization drastically increases the electrochemical symmetry of all materials. In fact, most optimized electrocatalysts end up having n=3 and are concentrated near the ideal catalyst in Figure 2e, that is far from the initial summit. On the right side of the volcano the data points seem to follow the volcano line. However, on the left the catalysts rearrange with a lower slope, suggesting a change in the nature of the scaling relation of the species involved in Step 3, namely *O and *OOH.[ 38 ] The material with the lowest overpotential is again Co‐BH2, which exhibits virtually no overpotential and all steps are close to 1.23 eV. The least benefited material after optimization is Ta‐doped TiO2 with an overpotential of ηOER=0.80 V and n=3. Before closing this subsection, we note that this study is framed within the “first generation” of volcano plots, which focuses on the conventional H2O→*OH→*O→*OOH→O2 pathway and is not potential‐dependent. More advanced activity plots are available in the state of the art.[ 75 ]

Effect of Increasing n on Electrocatalytic Symmetry

Figure 3 illustrates the proportionality between ηOER and ESSI for the initial and optimized data. In Figure 3a two notable aspects are observed in the initial data: 1) ηOER≥ESSI for all materials and ESSI=ηOER when n=1, which follows from Equation (10). 2) The dispersion of the data decreases alongside ηOER and ESSI,[ 63 ] which indicates that ESSI is particularly good at predicting OER active materials. As shown in Figure 3a, materials with n=1,2 exhibit a wide range of ESSI values, whereas materials with n=3 are in a narrow range where both ESSI and ηOER are low. This confirms the connection between high electrocatalytic symmetry and enhanced OER activity put forward in previous works.[ 65 ]

Correlation between ηOER and ESSI for a) the initial data, and data upon b) δ, c) ε, d) δ+ε, and e) δε optimizations. The data points are classified depending on the number of steps larger than 1.23 eV: blue (n=1), red (n=2), and white (n=3). The ideal catalyst (ηOER=ESSI=0 and n=4) is shown in yellow.

Figure 3b shows that δ optimization significantly narrows down the range of both ESSI and ηOER compared to the initial data. After δ optimization, numerous materials align close to the ideal catalyst without entirely behaving like it as a result of scaling relations. Furthermore, the resulting data upon ε and δ+ε optimizations exhibit similar trends in Figure 3c–d: n=1 tends to disappear (six materials left after ε optimization and only one after δ+ε optimization), the scattering decreases for n=2, and various materials approach the ideal catalyst. In addition, after these optimizations, numerous materials with n=2 fall over the ηOER=ESSI line, which means that their two steps larger than 1.23 eV are identical and numerically equal to the PLS. Materials with n=3 remain close to each other and not far from the ideal catalyst after ε and δ+ε optimizations. However, as ε only affects catalysts for which Step 3 is the PLS, several catalysts are unchanged with respect to the initial dataset.

Upon simultaneous δε optimization, all OER catalysts become highly symmetric, as shown in Figure 3e. Now, 92% of the materials have n=3 and the data points are close to the parity line (ηOER=ESSI) with only a few and minor departures.[ 65 ] Here, the gap between real and ideal catalysts is closed. The clearest example is again Co‐BH2, which has an overpotential of 0.00(02) V and the energies of the four OER steps are practically equal to 1.23 eV.

Statistical Analysis of Optimization Parameters

Now that we have correlated the optimization of OER catalysts with an increase in n, we can use it to provide further insight. In Figure 4 , we classify the mean absolute values of δ and ε (MAV(δ) and MAV(ε)) depending on n and the corresponding error bars are the standard deviations. δ and δε optimizations have comparable MAV(δ) and error bars for n=2,3. This suggests that in both strategies the thermodynamic limits for optimization imposed by scaling relations are reached, and the differences between the final results of the two methods are due to the scaling‐free parameter ε. In the sequential δ+ε optimization, MAV(δ) is around 0.25 eV because we restricted δ to the range of [−0.3, 0.3] eV to allow for a beneficial scaling‐free ε optimization afterwards (see Section S2). Consequently, the error bars are narrower compared to the other methods.

Mean absolute values (MAVs) of a) δ and b) ε after δ, ε,δ+ε, and δε optimizations as a function of the number of electrochemical steps greater than 1.23 eV after the optimizations. The standard deviations of the data are included as error bars.

Moreover, the mean values of ε vary depending on the method in Figure 4b. Indeed, δ+ε optimization displays the lowest MAV(ε), followed by ε optimization. Simultaneous δε optimization displays the highest MAV(ε), indicating that this method makes full use of δ to approach the top of the volcano, and then ε helps in reaching the ideal catalyst, which is some 0.37 V above the top.[ 76 ]

In sum, Figure 4 indicates that simultaneous optimization is the best method to capitalize on the scaling‐based and scaling‐free features of catalysts, as will be statistically shown in the next section. However, other potentially effective approaches were not explored here, such as ε then δ optimization, as they might be overly challenging to implement experimentally. Additionally, alternative constraints and optimization targets could be considered, such as optimizing symmetry in terms of n or ESSI. Finally, breaking scaling relations other than that of *OOH vs *OH or implementing scaling‐free parameters for intermediates other than *OOH has been shown to be a viable strategy.[ 72 ]

Moreover, Figure 5a shows that before optimization, only 18% of materials have n=3 and their average ηOER is 0.60 ± 0.2 V. After δ optimization, 62% of catalysts have n=3 and the average ηOER is 0.40 ± 0.1 V. After ε and δ+ε only 20 and 39% of materials end up with n=3 and the average ηOER is 0.51 ± 0.2 and 0.48 ± 0.2 V, respectively. In turn, δε optimization induces n=3 in 92% of the materials, and the average ηOER is as low as 0.15 ± 0.2 V.

Comparing Figure 5a,b, it is clear that ηOER and ESSI exhibit similar patterns as a function of n before and after the optimizations. In general, the average values of ηOER and ESSI follow the trend: Initial>ε>δ+ε>δ>δε (Figure 5c). This indicates that simply breaking the *OOH vs *OH scaling relation improves materials performance, but the ultimate performance is reached by a combination of scaling‐free and scaling‐based optimization. In fact, n=3 exhibits similar average values of ηOER and ESSI for initial and δ, ε, and δ+ε optimized materials in Figure 5a–b. To go beyond that limit, a viable option is δε simultaneous optimization, which gives average ηOER of 0.15 ± 0.2 V and average ESSI of 0.15 ± 0.1 V.

Figure 5c shows the correlation between the mean values of ηOER and ESSI for initial and optimized materials as a function of the final n for initial and optimized datasets. ηOER =ESSI for materials with n=1, which can be deduced from Equation (10). This correlation extends to materials with n=3, for which ηOER ≈ESSI, especially close to the ideal catalyst. Notably, the enhancement in structural symmetry induced by simultaneous δε optimization yields comparable ηOER and ESSI values, regardless of the final value of n. Conversely, materials with n=2 show greater dispersion, and in general, higher values.

How Delta‐Epsilon Optimizations Affect the Reaction Steps as a Function of n

Figure 6a shows the percentage of materials with a specific value of n, and the electrochemical steps associated with them. Before optimization, Figure 6a shows that 20% of materials display n=1 and are limited either by steps 2 (4%) or 3 (16%). No catalysts are limited by steps 1 or 4. In turn, 62% of the catalysts display n=2 initially and the positive steps are mainly 1 and 2 (31%), and 3 and 4 (18%). Initially, n=3 occurs only in 18% of the materials in the dataset, and the most common steps over 1.23 eV are 1, 2, 3, amounting to 14% of the catalysts. Interestingly, when n=3, steps 2 and 3 are always above 1.23 eV.

Proportion of catalysts with various combinations of OER steps larger than 1.23 eV as a function of n. a) Before optimization and after b) δ, c) ε, d) δ+ε, and e) δε optimizations. The optimizations affect electrochemical symmetry by increasing n, tending toward n=3 in most cases, and modifying the combinations of steps larger than 1.23 eV.

Scaling‐based δ optimization alters the distribution of steps substantially, as shown in Figure 6b. This optimization affects all materials, regardless of their PLS. After optimization, all materials initially displaying n=1 end up having 2 or 3 steps greater than 1.23 eV. This confirms that δ optimization increases the electrochemical symmetry of the OER steps. Around 38% of materials have now n=2 and nearly 84% of these have steps 2 and 3 over 1.23 eV. 50% of materials now have n=3 and the most common steps coincide with those of the initial dataset: steps 1, 2, 3. In sum, δ optimization increases the electrochemical symmetry of materials and most of them end up having steps 1, 2, 3 over 1.23 eV. Interestingly, only 6% of the materials involve Step 3 without Step 2, and only 1% involve Step 2 without Step 3. These results suggest that an extra optimization of Step 3 applying epsilon is likely beneficial.

Figure 6c shows that ε optimization does not imply a significant change of symmetry or combinations of steps with respect to the initial one in Figure 6a. This is in line with the fact that ε only affects materials limited by Step 3. Around 17% of materials initially with n=1 corresponding to Step 3 display after ε optimization n=2 corresponding to steps 3 and 4. This combination corresponds to 34% of the catalysts in Figure 6c.

The effects of δ+ε optimization are similar to those of ε optimization with the caveat that the percentage of catalysts in each category is different. According to Figure 6e, simultaneous δε optimization leads to n=3 for 92% of materials. The most representative combinations are steps 2, 3, 4 (69%) and 1, 3, 4 (21%). No catalyst is observed to display n=1, and only 8% of the analyzed materials have n=2 after this optimization. Evidently, this method produces the most electrochemically symmetric materials.

In sum, Figure 6 shows that each optimization leads to different combinations of steps with free energies larger than 1.23 eV. However, electrochemical symmetry, as quantified by n, always increases after all procedures. In other words, OER optimization is statistically connected to an increase of catalysts with n=3, thereby decreasing those with n=1,2.

Conclusions

The search for active OER electrocatalysts ought to rely more on statistically verified guidelines than on incidental correlations, speculations, unverified hypotheses, or rules of thumb. We presented here a high‐throughput statistical analysis of 159 materials subjected to four optimization methods, including a scaling‐based parameter (δ) and a scaling‐free parameter (ε) and combinations thereof. In all cases, the optimizations led to increased symmetry of the electrochemical steps, which can be easily measured by an increase in the number of electrochemical steps exceeding 1.23 eV (n). Importantly, simultaneous optimization using scaling‐free and scaling‐dependent parameters yields by far the best results, with some materials lying remarkably close to the ideal catalyst.

In perspective, this work leaves two statistically sound conclusions: first, increasing the number of electrochemical steps larger than 1.23 eV can confidently be used as a principle to enhance OER electrocatalysts. Second, in contrast with the widespread notion advocating only for the breaking of the *OOH vs *OH scaling relation, we have shown that the key to achieving ideal OER electrocatalysis from a thermodynamic standpoint lies in a simultaneous combination of scaling‐free and scaling‐based optimizations, guaranteeing a high number of n and ηOER≈ESSI.
