Introduction

While true extracranial internal carotid aneurysms (tECAAs) are rare and often incidental and asymptomatic, they can lead to serious complications, including rupture, thrombosis, or embolization [1]. Traditionally, surgery has been the standard of care, even for asymptomatic patients, due to its established safety and favorable long-term outcomes. However, advancements in technology have introduced endovascular interventions and observation as viable therapeutic options. Multiple case series suggest elevated perioperative risks after open repair and the combined risk of mortality and major stroke risk for open surgery is approximately 9 %. The technical advantages of endovascular procedures may decrease that rate. There are multiple endovascular modalities including covered stenting, bare metal stenting, stent-assisted coil embolization, and ligation, but no clear guideline as to which modality is superior exist [2]. Therefore, we report a successful case of a true, internal carotid aneurysm treated endoscopically. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [3].

Presentation of case

A 32-year-old obese man presented with sudden right-sided facial numbness lasting 3 h. Personal, family, social and trauma history were unremarkable. Cardiac and neurological examinations were normal. Computed tomography angiography revealed a non-flow-limiting stenosis and a left internal carotid artery saccular aneurysm (15 × 16 mm). No intraluminal thrombus was noted (Fig. 1). Remaining imaging workup was also unremarkable. After thorough shared decision-making, left carotid stenting using a 6 × 25 mm ViabahnGORE stent was performed (Video). Patient received Clopidogrel for 2 days preoperatively. The postoperative course was uneventful and the patient was discharged on the postoperative day 2 with Clopidogrel. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic and adherent to the medication.

Technical aspects of endovascular intervention

The lower extremity groins were prepped and draped in a normal sterile fashion. Ultrasound was used to gain access to the right femoral artery with an 18 g needle under direct visualization. J - wire was advanced and fluoroscopy confirmed adequate access over the femoral head and wire placement in the right iliac artery. A 6Fr. sheath was placed and needle removed. It was then flushed with heparinized saline. IV Heparin 5000 Units were administered to achieve an activated coagulation time > 250 s. The wire and glide catheter were then advanced over the wire into the aorta and up the arch and the left common carotid artery was cannulated. Angiography revealed the distal common carotid lesion; the glide wire was placed at this segment. Then catheter was removed and the short 7Fr sheath was replaced with a 90 cm 6Fr sheath which was placed at the proximal common carotid artery. Then the distal embolic protection spider device was placed in the mid to distal internal carotid artery and the wire kept in place. The aneurysm in the mid internal carotid artery was identified on angiography and marked on the fluoroscopy screen. A 6 mm × 25 mm ViabahnGORE stent was then placed to cover the aneurysm, as needed for complete coverage. Post-angiography revealed good flow throughout the internal carotid artery and no longer any leak into the aneurysm. Glide catheter was used to retrieve the distal embolic protection device and then removed. The glide wire was placed and the sheath removed over the wire. The Angio-seal closure device was used successfully and then a dry sterile dressing was placed on the right groin access site after it was washed and dried successfully.

Discussion

The choice of treatment approach for tECAAs depends on various factors, including patient characteristics (e.g. comorbidities, symptoms, etiology, and location of the carotid aneurysm), surgeon experience, and equipment availability. In general, symptomatic aneurysms (regardless of diameter), expanding aneurysms, and larger aneurysms (>20 mm), and those with identified thrombus are typically indications for repair.

Open repair is the standard, involving either carotid artery ligation with or without bypass, or aneurysm excision and reconstruction. A recent systematic review revealed that 85 % of patients with type I ECAAs underwent open repair, while 19 % endovascular repair [4]. However, endovascular techniques are gaining traction due to their potential advantages, including avoidance of cranial nerve injury and general anesthesia, enhanced access and control of lesions that may be difficult to navigate surgically, and lower rates of perioperative morbidity and mortality. Another recent systematic review revealed that open and endovascular repair of extracranial carotid artery aneurysms is associated with similar low early mortality and cerebrovascular event rates, although open repair showed a greater risk of cranial nerve injuries. An endovascular approach could be more appropriate when the aneurysm is located distally or requires extensive dissection [5]. Nonetheless, studies investigating outcomes after endovascular repair for ECCAs remain limited. In this respect, there is still debate regarding the use of covered versus bare metal stents, with covered stents showing decreased complications rates [6]. Cornwall et al. suggest a useful algorithm for navigating the various endovascular modalities [7]. Regarding to the postoperative care, these patients may benefit from dual antiplatelet perioperatively. Some authors recommend 100 mg/day Aspirin indefinitely and 75 mg/day Clopidogrel for at least three months, specially for bare metal stents [8]. However, more studies are required [9]. Duplex ultrasound is recommended following carotid artery stenting at least 3 months after intervention [10].

Observation is safe for select cases involving small (<10–20 mm), non-expanding, and asymptomatic aneurysms with no intraluminal thrombus. Notably, a study conducted at the Mayo Clinic found that none of the true aneurysms managed nonoperatively (40 %) progressed to necessitate treatment during a mean follow-up of 77 month (mean diameter of 12 mm) [11]. However, historically, nonoperative treatment carried a substantial stroke risk as high as 50 %, given the propensity of aneurysms for continued expansion [12]. If observation is the chosen treatment modality, these patients ideally should be treated with antiplatelets therapy and have frequent follow-ups with serial imaging.

Conclusion

Given the lack of definitive guidelines and the concerns regarding the safety and long-term outcomes of different treatment modalities for true extracranial internal carotid aneurysms, surgeons should continue to carefully weigh the risks and benefits on a case-by-case basis, considering the patient's overall health. Our patient is recovering successfully after endovascular intervention.
