A canine case of multiple intrahepatic portosystemic shunts
Authors: Shinichiro YODA, Hideki KAYANUMA, Takuya MARUO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An elderly, spayed female Maltese dog (10 years 3 months, 2.2 kg) presented with hyporexia, weight loss, polydipsia, and a staggering gait. Serum biochemistry analysis revealed increased aspartate aminotransferase, alanine transaminase, ammonia (226 μg/dL), and fasting total bile acids (130 μmol/L). Contrast-enhanced CT showed multiple clusters of contrast enhancement within the hepatic parenchyma; however, discrete shunt vessels could not be clearly identified, and a definitive diagnosis could not be established based on CT alone. Intraoperative jejunal venous portography revealed numerous microvascular clusters connecting portal branches to the hepatic vein and caudal vena cava. The portal pressure was 7 mmHg. Based on these findings, multiple intrahepatic portosystemic shunts (MIPSS) were diagnosed, and medical management was selected.

A portosystemic shunt (PSS) is a vascular anomaly that forms a direct connection between the portal and systemic circulation, causing blood to circulate without passing through the liver [17]. When blood flowing from the gastrointestinal tract bypasses the liver, increased levels of gastrointestinal substances such as ammonia develop in the systemic circulation, causing clinical symptoms, including neurological signs [4, 11].

PSS is classified by etiology (congenital/acquired), anatomic location (intrahepatic/extrahepatic), and number of shunts (single/multiple) [2]. Congenital PSS arises from the abnormal closure of fetal vessels, whereas acquired PSS develops as a compensatory response to portal hypertension [2]. Extrahepatic shunts are more common in small breed dogs, whereas intrahepatic shunts are more common in large breeds [7, 13].

Multiple intrahepatic portosystemic shunts (MIPSS) are rare, with only a few reported cases in humans [3, 14]. They are extremely rare in veterinary medicine, with few canine cases reported to date [5, 15]. Here, we report a canine case of MIPSS in which contrast-enhanced CT suggested the diagnosis, which was subsequently confirmed by intraoperative portography. A 10-year-3-month-old spayed female Maltese presented to the Animal Hospital, Azabu University, with complaints of hyporexia, weight loss, polydipsia, and a staggering gait. Hyporexia and weight loss had been present for 5 months. Body weight at presentation was 2.2 kg, and the body condition score (BCS) was 2/5. All procedures were performed as part of routine clinical care. Written informed consent was obtained from the owner for the use of the animal’s clinical information and images for research and publication.

Past medical history included bacterial cystitis with calcium oxalate urolithiasis at 4 years of age, managed with dietary therapy, and ureteral obstruction due to ureterolithiasis at 9 years of age, treated with a right ureterotomy and stone removal performed by our soft-tissue surgery service. Additional medical history included medial patellar luxation (Grade II), intervertebral disc herniation (Grade II), spondylosis deformans, myxomatous mitral valve disease (ACVIM Stage B2), pancreatitis, and a periapical abscess. The dog was receiving benazepril hydrochloride, spironolactone, and camostat.

Neurological examination revealed no abnormalities in gait, postural reactions, or spinal reflexes. Hematology/biochemistry results revealed aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 306 U/L, alanine transaminase (ALT) 624 U/L, total cholesterol 66 mg/dL, total protein 4.6 g/dL, albumin 3.0 g/dL, glucose 78 mg/dL, calcium 8.7 mg/dL, ammonia 226 μg/dL, and fasting total bile acids (TBA) 130 μmol/L, indicating hepatic abnormalities. In contrast, blood tests performed by the primary veterinarian 3 months earlier showed AST 50, ALT 89, total protein 6.7, albumin 3.4, and glucose 109, with no hepatic abnormalities noted. Abdominal radiography revealed marked emaciation and mild microhepatia (Figs. 1Fig. 1.Lateral plain radiograph. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed no parenchymal abnormalities or obvious shunting vessels; however, given the markedly elevated TBA level, a diffuse hepatic disorder or PSS was suspected. The patient was referred to our diagnostic imaging service, and a CT scan was scheduled. Levetiracetam (20 mg/kg) and lactulose (0.5 mL/kg) were prescribed, and the diet was changed to a hepatic low-protein diet. One clonic seizure occurred while awaiting CT.

Anesthesia was induced with intravenous propofol (6 mg/kg) and maintained with isoflurane following endotracheal intubation. In addition to a non-contrast series, a nonionic iodinated contrast medium (2.5 mL/kg) was injected intravenously over 15 sec, and a portal-venous phase scan was performed 40 sec later. Images were obtained with 0.5 mm slices under soft-tissue settings.

Routine hematoxylin and eosin staining was supplemented by immunohistochemistry, including cytokeratin AE1/AE3 and α-SMA, and special stains, including Masson’s trichrome and Elastica-van Gieson. The spacing between the portal tracts and central veins was uneven, and many central veins were indistinct. Conversely, some large dilated veins with perivascular fibrosis were observed. In the portal tracts, the basic triad architecture (interlobular vein, interlobular artery, and interlobular bile duct) was largely preserved, although mild fibrosis was accompanied by the proliferation of the interlobular bile ducts and interlobular arteries. Mild pericellular collagen deposition was observed in the sinusoids associated with small duct-like structures, identified by cytokeratin AE1/AE3 positivity, and vascular-like structures. These changes reflected hypoperfusion secondary to anomalous portal and/or arterial vascular courses. Most of the luminal structures proliferating with collagen demonstrated negative immunostaining for von Willebrand factor; when combined with the results of cytokeratin AE1/AE3 staining, the majority of these were concluded to be ductal epithelium, proliferating interlobular bile ducts. Because there were no definite findings of cirrhosis or portal hypertension, the lesion was suspected to be congenital.

Based on these findings, MIPSS was diagnosed. We also sought the opinion of a veterinarian who is a U.S. specialist in veterinary diagnostic imaging; they independently reviewed the CT and intraoperative portography findings and agreed with the diagnosis of MIPSS. Because no single dominant shunt vessel that could be treated surgically to improve the condition was identified, medical management with lactulose (0.5 mL/kg) and dietary measures aimed at seizure control was continued.

Canine MIPSS, as in the present case, has rarely been reported [5, 15]. In a recent series of 100 intrahepatic PSS cases, only 10 had multiple or numerous shunts [17]. In the present case, CT revealed clustered contrast enhancement within the hepatic parenchyma, and portography from the jejunal vein confirmed an unusual pattern of multiple microvascular clusters [17]. Previously reported canine MIPSS cases involved much younger dogs (7 and 17 months old), and because the shunting vasculature originated from a single vessel, surgical intervention was possible, unlike in the present case. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no prior reports of MIPSS in dogs like this case [5].

In veterinary medicine, intrahepatic PSS has been classified not only by the traditional left/central/right division but also by interlobar/intralobar categories; however, no classification has been established to include multiple shunts [12, 16]. In human medicine, intrahepatic portosystemic venous shunts are categorized according to the Park classification (Types 1–4); Type 4 is defined as “numerous portal-hepatic venous communications diffusely distributed in both lobes” [8]. In this case, shunts were observed in multiple hepatic lobes; therefore, under this classification scheme, the findings would be most consistent with Type 4. In our diagnostic process, PSS was suspected on the basis of clinical findings; however, it was difficult to clearly identify the shunt vessels using contrast-enhanced CT alone. Multiple intrahepatic shunts were also identified using intraoperative jejunal venous portography. For PSS diagnosis, contrast-enhanced CT is a valuable non-invasive tool for delineating portal branching and course and for surgical planning [7]. However, normal intrahepatic portal branches may not always be visualized on CT. In MIPSS presenting as clusters of microcommunications, identifying orifices and defining vessel courses can be difficult, potentially necessitating intraoperative angiography [9, 18].

The timing of contrast infiltration into the intrahepatic portal branches may differ between typical PSS and MIPSS, as in this case. Because this rare condition was not anticipated, we used our standard contrast-enhanced CT protocol, administering 2.5 mL/kg of contrast over approximately 15 sec and acquiring only a single portal venous phase image at 40 sec after injection. Given the dog’s small body weight, the total contrast volume was limited, and the iodine delivery rate may have been relatively low, potentially resulting in a blunted enhancement peak. Consequently, vascular-to-parenchymal contrast may have been insufficient to delineate microvascular communications or multiple shunt vessels, which may have appeared as nonspecific parenchymal enhancement rather than distinct vascular structures. Therefore, the difficulty in clearly identifying the shunt vessels on CT in this case may represent a limitation related to the contrast injection conditions and CT acquisition protocol, and the diagnostic performance of CT may have been reduced under these conditions. Fluoroscopic portography, which allows temporal and continuous assessment of portal flow, may play an important role in diagnosis.

Histopathology showed proliferation of interlobular bile ducts and arteries, mild fibrosis, and sinusoidal capillarization, and immunostaining demonstrated that the small tubular structures were AE1/AE3 positive and vWF negative, indicating the presence of bile duct epithelium. Proliferation of interlobular bile ducts with collagen has also been reported in human MIPSS and is considered a secondary change due to portal hypoperfusion caused by congenital MIPSS. Histologic findings in this case likewise suggested congenital MIPSS [14]. However, despite sampling the parenchyma containing grossly abnormal mottled vascular clusters, abnormal vascular communication was not identified histologically. This underscores the fact that histopathologic confirmation of shunt vessels can be difficult in MIPSS because the shunting channels in MIPSS can be microscopic, multifocal, and unevenly distributed within the liver parenchyma; therefore, imaging findings are crucial for diagnosis [14]. Portal microvascular dysplasia (MVD), or a possible coexistence of MVD and portosystemic shunting, was also considered in the differential diagnosis; however, histopathology did not provide sufficient evidence to support a diagnosis of MVD in this case. Therefore, the diagnosis of MIPSS was made based on the imaging findings. However, because biopsies were obtained from only a limited portion of the liver and neither whole-liver histopathological evaluation nor postmortem examination was performed, the pathological assessment in this case may have been limited.

Dogs with PSS may show neurological signs, including hepatic encephalopathy and seizures, gastrointestinal abnormalities, including hyporexia, diarrhea, vomiting, and weight loss, and urinary tract issues, including polyuria and urolithiasis [2, 6, 10]. Common hematologic and biochemical changes include microcytic, hypochromic anemia, elevated liver enzyme activities, decreased glucose, blood urea nitrogen, albumin, and cholesterol levels, hyperammonemia, and increased pre- and postprandial bile acids [2, 10]. Similar findings were observed in this case; notably, elevated liver values and clinical signs were observed only at an advanced age.

In this case, the portal pressure was 7 mmHg, and there were no clinical signs of portal hypertension such as ascites. MIPSS without portal hypertension is considered congenital and is exceedingly rare in humans [14]. The manifestation of hepatic encephalopathy in congenital PSS depends on the shunt fraction, brain sensitivity to ammonia, and hepatic function. Thus, with a low shunt fraction, young individuals may remain asymptomatic, whereas age-related decreases in cerebral tolerance and hepatic function can increase the risk of symptoms in middle-aged and older individuals [1, 14]. In humans, Type 4 MIPSS cases analogous to this case have been reported, in which hepatic encephalopathy and other symptoms developed at an advanced age without evidence of portal hypertension or cirrhosis, showing a clinical course similar to that of our case, albeit in a different species [3]. However, given the scarcity of veterinary reports on MIPSS, it is difficult to conclude definitively whether this is a congenital disease.

For intrahepatic PSS, endovascular management centered on staged luminal narrowing using caudal vena cava stents and coils has been reported to yield good outcomes [13, 17]. In recent case reports of multiple shunts, the feasibility of surgical intervention depended on whether a single shunt orifice could be identified [5, 15]. In multiple shunts lacking a single orifice, as in this case, surgical options have been limited, and medical management focused on quality of life improvement, including lactulose, dietary management, and seizure control, has been a pragmatic choice [15]. In our case, surgical intervention was not performed, and medical management was instituted.

In conclusion, this report describes a rare case of canine MIPSS. A definitive diagnosis could not be achieved with abdominal ultrasonography and contrast-enhanced CT alone, and intraoperative fluoroscopic portography was essential for delineating the vascular pathways and establishing the diagnosis. Because histopathology may not confirm portosystemic shunts in such cases, imaging findings play a pivotal role in the diagnosis. However, this case has several limitations, including constraints related to the CT acquisition protocol, the limited availability of archived intraoperative fluoroscopic portography images, and the fact that histopathology evaluation of only a partial liver sample may not be sufficient to establish the diagnosis. The accumulation of similar case reports will help standardize diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
