Intraneural Topography and Branching Patterns of the Common Peroneal Nerve: Studying the Feasibility of Distal Nerve Transfers
Authors: Elliot L.H. Le, Taylor H. Allenby, Marlie Fisher, Ryan S. Constantine, Colin T. McNamara, Caleb Barnhill, Anne Engemann, Orlando Merced-O’Neill, Matthew L. Iorio
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Common peroneal nerve (CPN) disruption is the most common lower extremity nerve injury and results in gait disturbances and sensory paresthesias. The goal of this study is to describe branching patterns and the intraneural topography of the CPN to facilitate operative planning in CPN reconstruction.Methods:The CPN and its distal motor and sensory branches were dissected in 6 lower leg cadavers. Branching patterns and distances were recorded with the fibular head as the landmark. Histological review of the nerve cross sections helped characterize the intraneural topography within the fibular tunnel.Results:The CPN distal branching patterns were highly variable. The tibialis anterior motor branch was found on average 9.6 cm distal to the fibular head. Despite the variable branching patterns, the fascicular topography of the CPN within the fibular tunnel was consistent. Proximal to the tunnel, the nerve has 3 major fascicles, which include the superficial peroneal motor, common sensory, and deep peroneal motor (DPN) fascicles from lateral to medial. Within the tunnel, the topography consolidates into the superficial peroneal motor and DPN major divisions—motor axons anteriorly and sensory axons posteriorly.Conclusions:The data presented provide clinically relevant information for the peripheral nerve surgeon where fascicular reconstruction of the nerve and neurolysis should focus on the anterior half of the nerve to restore ankle dorsiflexion. The nerve proximally is divided into 3 major fascicles compared with 2 distally. Surgeons may consider distal nerve transfers from the tibial nerve motor branches to the DPN or tibialis anterior motor branch.

Takeaways

Question: What is the intraneural topography of the common peroneal nerve for reconstruction and nerve transfers?

Findings: There is significant variability in distal branching patterns, though the topography through the fibular tunnel proximally demonstrates a consistent topography between 2 motor groups and a central sensory group.

Meaning: There is reliable topographic anatomy of the common peroneal nerve that the peripheral nerve surgeon will find useful in planning motor nerve reconstruction of the common peroneal nerve. Distal nerve transfers should be a consideration in the treatment of peroneal nerve injury.

INTRODUCTION

The common peroneal nerve (CPN) is a major mixed motor sensory nerve innervating the lower extremity. This nerve originates from L4, S1, and S2 and continues as a terminal division of the sciatic nerve at the posterolateral knee, deep to the biceps femoris tendon, and posterior to the fibular head. It then courses anteriorly along the fibular neck, where it terminates by dividing into the deep (DPN) and superficial peroneal nerves (SPN).1 Based on the superficial location around the knee and fibula, it is susceptible to both traumatic (eg, knee dislocations and fibula fractures) and nontraumatic injuries (eg, compressive neuropathy) that can significantly impact motor function and sensation. In cases refractory to nonoperative management, surgical nerve decompression and neurolysis, nerve reconstruction, nerve transfers, and/or tendon transfers may be indicated.1,2 Although CPN decompression is generally well described, there are limited data on the intraneural topography and branching patterns which would improve the precision of CPN reconstruction techniques such as intrafascicular neurolysis, fascicular repair with autograft or allograft, or nerve transfer from the tibial nerve.3–6 The aim of this study was to delineate the intraneural topography and fascicular groups of the CPN, DPN, and SPN, as well as to explore the various branching patterns of the CPN to provide a roadmap for operative management and reconstruction of CPN injuries.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Specimens from 3 anatomic donors were utilized for matched lower extremity dissection, for a total of 6 legs. Dissections started at the fibular head along the trajectory of the CPN as it coursed from lateral to medial around the fibula. The dissection was extended proximally 10 cm from the fibular head. The CPN was identified within the fibular tunnel and followed distally along the anterolateral aspect of the lower leg. The fascia of the anterior compartment was sharply released, and proximal branches were tagged. Motor nerve branches to the lateral compartment and anterior compartment muscles were identified and tagged.

A pin was placed at the fibular head as the reference point for measurements—where distal to this point would be positive and proximal would be negative. Pins were also placed at the point of each motor branch’s exit point from the major peripheral nerve (CPN, DPN, and/or SPN). Two measurements were then obtained for each pin placement: (1) the incisional distance, defined as the straight-line distance between the reference point and the branch, and (2) the true nerve length, defined as the precise length of nerve between the reference point and the motor branch. The first measurement identified the approximate distance the motor branch could be found from a known landmark. The second measurement consisted of the length of each major motor branch from its branch point to the neuromuscular junction. All measurements are reported as median lengths and interquartile ranges.

The CPN was marked along its anterior surface at the level of the fibular head and fibular tunnel to maintain orientation during histologic processing. The nerve was then removed from the specimen en bloc, careful to maintain all branches in continuity (Fig. 1). The branches were followed to the neuromuscular junction, and the distal nerve was removed with a piece of muscle for later re-identification (Fig. 2). Each specimen was sectioned into 10 different portions of the nerve—CPN proximal to the fibular tunnel; CPN within the fibular tunnel; CPN at the bifurcation; SPN; DPN; and the proximal aspects of the 5 muscle branches—peroneus longus (PL), peroneus brevis (PB), extensor hallucis longus (EHL), extensor digitorum longus (EDL), and tibialis anterior (TA).

Example of the in situ dissection of the CPN and its motor and sensory branches. Dissection and measurements were completed in situ before removal of the nerve from each specimen.

CPN dissection through the superficial and deep terminal divisions along the anterior and lateral lower leg. The nerve was traced to muscle targets which was sharply elevated in continuity and removed en bloc for sectioning and pathology review.

The 60 specimen sections were labeled and sent to the pathology laboratory for preparation and staining. The formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned at 5 µm and mounted onto charged slides. The slides were dried overnight, baked at 60°C, deparaffinized in xylene, rinsed in alcohol, rehydrated in water, and equilibrated in a wash buffer. Slides were put through a heat-induced epitope retriever with a pH of 9 for 20 minutes at 95°C. Four specialty stains were performed—goat anticholine acetyltransferase (ChAT), calcitonin gene-related protein, laminin 111, and neurofilament 2F11—along with hematoxylin and eosin staining. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was used for gross highlighting of nerve tissue from other tissue. Laminin was primarily used for characterizing nerve structure in each sample. Neurofilament staining highlighted the axons. Calcitonin gene-related protein staining identified sensory axons and fascicles. ChAT staining identified motor axons and fascicles.

The slides were converted into digital images on Aperio ImageScope (Leica Biosystems, Deer Park, Ill.). The orientation of the motor and sensory fascicles was then corroborated by the orientation on gross examination of the nerves and their branches before sectioning to create intraneural topographic maps of the CPN, SPN, and DPN.

RESULTS

Nerve Anatomy and Branching

The bifurcation of the CPN into the deep and superficial branches always occurred distal to the PL origin and a median distance of 2.8 cm [interquartile range (IQR): 2.1–3.8 cm] distal to the fibular head (Table 1). This bifurcation was distal to the posterior crural intermuscular septum in all 6 specimens. Within the fibular tunnel, we found some variation in branching patterns, including the recurrent articular branch, which was found within the tunnel in 5 of the specimens and on average 2.0 cm (SD = 1.2) distal to the fibular head. Only 1 specimen had 2 recurrent articular branches, which were also found within the fibular tunnel. The only other branch identified at the level of the fibular tunnel was a branch to the PL. Five of the 6 specimens had a PL branch within the fibular tunnel and a median distance of 1.9 cm (IQR: 1.1–2.8) distal to the fibular head (Table 1 and Fig. 3).

The bifurcation of the CPN occurred at varying lengths, ranging from 1 to 5.1 cm distal to the fibular head. At the bifurcation, 3 specimens had additional motor branches exiting at the bifurcation including to PL, PB, and recurrent articular branches (cadavers 4, 5, and 6). The PL and PB branches off the SPN were among the most redundant motor branches with an average of 1.8 and 1.5 branches, respectively (Table 1). The first branch from the SPN was within 2 cm of the bifurcation in 4 of the specimens, highlighting the high and proximal exit points of these branches (Table 1). The most distal branch observed in the SPN was a primary PB motor branch 10 cm from the fibular tunnel (Fig. 3). Median distances of PL and PB branches were 3 cm (IQR: 1.5–4.0) and 5.0 cm (IQR: 3–8.8) distal to the fibular head, respectively (Table 1). The SPN had a variable number of motor branches to the lateral compartment muscles, ranging from 2 to 4 branches.

The DPN branching pattern was also variable. The EDL branch was found proximal to the bifurcation in 1 specimen (Fig. 3, cadaver 5). The remaining 5 specimens’ DPN branches were distal to the bifurcation. The EDL branches were the most redundant of the DPN branches, with a median of 1.5 (IQR: 1–2) branches—1 specimen with 3 branches and 2 others with 2 branches (Table 1). The EDL branch exit point ranged from 2.5 to 8.5 cm from the fibular head and was the most proximal branch in 5 of the specimens. The EHL branch was the least redundant with a median of 1 branch—1 specimen with 2 branches. The exit point of the EHL branch ranged from 4 to 10 cm from the fibular head (Fig. 3). The TA had a median of 1 branch—1 specimen with 3 branches. The exit point of the TA branch ranged from 6 to 14 cm from the fibular head and a median distance of 8.8 cm, making it the most distal of the branches (Table 1). Median EHL, EDL, and TA motor branch distances were 9.0 cm (IQR: 7.8–9.0), 6.3 cm (IQR: 4.3–7.0), and 8.8 cm (IQR: 7.6–10.3) distal to the fibular head respectively (Table 1). In total, the DPN had a variable number of motor branches to the anterior compartment muscles, ranging from 3 to 6.

Motor Branch Lengths

Median lengths of all 5 motor branches were less than 3 cm. In the lateral compartment, the PL and PB branches were 1.53 cm (IQR: 1.39–2.58) and 1.84 cm (IQR: 1.26–2.91) in median length, respectively (Table 2). In the anterior compartment, the TA, EHL, and EDL branches were 2.25 cm (IQR: 0.85–2.49), 2.16 cm (IQR: 1.40–3.58), and 1.52 cm (IQR: 0.63–3.02) in median length, respectively (Table 2). The longest branch recorded for each muscle was found in the same specimen, except for the EHL.

Nerve Histology and Topography

The fascicular content and topography were consistent across the 6 specimens. The analysis was primarily focused on the CPN at 3 levels adjacent to and within the fibular tunnel—proximal to the fibular tunnel, within the fibular tunnel, and just proximal to the bifurcation of the nerve—as this is the most common location of CPN injury. Proximal to the tunnel, the fascicles were organized into motor and sensory components with the anterior compartment motor branches medial in relation to the lateral compartment motor branches. Between the DPN and SPN was a common sensory fascicle, completing a set of 3 major fascicular groups—2 motor and 1 common sensory (Figs. 4, 5). Within the tunnel, the topography begins to diverge into its superficial and deep nerve counterparts (Figs. 4, 6). The group of 3 major fascicles decussates into a group of 2, with the DPN medial and the SPN lateral. Within these 2 groups, the anterior half of each is primarily motor axons, whereas the posterior half is primarily sensory axons.

Proximal to the fibular tunnel, the nerve is arranged with motor groups of the DPN medially, shared sensory fascicles centrally, and motor groups of the SPN laterally. Within the tunnel, the sensory fascicles divide into their superficial and deep components and move posterior while the motor fascicles migrate anteriorly. Distal to the tunnel, the individual motor branches divide anteriorly.

Finally, just proximal to the bifurcation of the deep and superficial branches, the individual motor branches begin to distinguish themselves while maintaining their medial-lateral orientation (Figs. 4, 7). Branches exit the nerve anteriorly, and we did not observe any unexpected or aberrant crossover (eg, SPN branches exiting from the medial half of the nerve or DPN branches exiting from the lateral half of the nerve). As the motor fascicles divided and exited the nerve anteriorly, the posterior aspect of the nerve was composed of the main SPN or DPN trunk.

DISCUSSION

This study provides a topographical description and branching pattern of the CPN, DPN, and SPN in the lower leg and surgical anatomy relevant to the peripheral nerve surgeon. Currently, there are no publications in the literature describing the fascicular topography or the approximate location of specific motor branches of the CPN. The results of our observational study clarify the relevant anatomy for surgeons performing CPN decompression, planning fascicular reconstruction of the CPN, and designing tibial nerve to CPN transfers.

Fascicular Topography of the CPN

Despite the variability in branching patterns in our 6 specimens, fascicular topography was consistent, especially through the fibular tunnel. At the proximal extent of the fibular tunnel, the CPN orientation from lateral to medial is motor, sensory, and then motor fascicles. As they pass through the tunnel, the sensory fascicles divide into their SPN and DPN counterparts to form 2 major divisions. The motor fascicles migrate anteriorly while the sensory fascicles migrate posteriorly. At the bifurcation level, this motor and sensory orientation is maintained, but the individual motor branches divide from the main nerve in a variable pattern depending on which branches exit first. The bifurcation is a busy segment with DPN and SPN splitting and several other branches, exiting the main nerve(s) all within a 1- to 2-cm segment. This topographic knowledge is important when performing decompression of the nerve and when planning reconstruction of the nerve and its motor branches.

CPN Entrapment and Decompression

CPN entrapment within the fibular tunnel is a known pathology. Release of the surrounding fascia and neurolysis of the CPN has demonstrated reliable recovery of motor and sensory deficits. This is especially important in the subacute period after CPN palsy with motor and/or sensory deficits. Maalla et al7 found that early intervention within 3–4 months of symptom onset led to functional recovery in 2.5 months on average. Others have demonstrated benefit even in more chronic settings on the order of years after possible injury.8 Ken Wamsley and Mackinnon9 described 4 fascial bands that should be released when decompressing the CPN: Lateral to the nerve are 3 fascial bands—the posterior crural intermuscular septum, anterior crural intermuscular septum, and innominate intermuscular septum. Medial to the nerve is the deep tendinous fascia.9,10 Given the fascicular topography of the CPN proximal and within the tunnel, the DPN fascicles are adjacent to the deep tendinous fascia and need to be released in cases of foot drop. The remaining lateral septums also need to be released but are more directly compressing the SPN fascicles rather than the DPN fascicles.

Feasibility of Distal Nerve Transfers

If the CPN injury is extensive and unlikely to improve in a timely manner with decompression and neurolysis alone, distal nerve transfers are an option to restore DPN and SPN function.11–17 The redundancy of motor functions in the deep and superficial posterior compartments, along with the proximity of the tibial nerve to the DPN and SPN distally, make tibial nerve motor branches the most common donor nerves. Bodily et al18 performed a cadaveric dissection in 10 specimens of the tibial nerve and its motor branches and the most proximal TA motor branch and found the most reliable transfers were with the flexor hallucis longus (FHL) and flexor digitorum longus (FDL) muscles. Both branches were found on average 7.6 and 7.2 cm distal to the tibial plateau, respectively, and had an average branch length of 13.9 and 12.2 cm, respectively. These were of sufficient length in all 10 specimens to reach the TA branch around the proximal fibula, though the authors did not consider dissection through the interosseous membrane, which would shorten the distance necessary for nerve transfer.18 Importantly, though, their work determined that only 4 of the tibial nerve motor branches exit the tibial nerve near the level of the fibular head—tibialis posterior (TP), distal soleus, FHL, and FDL.

Apaydin et al19 evaluated the tibial nerve in 36 cadaver specimens and focused on the deep posterior compartment motor branches. The FHL, FDL, and TP were on average found 6.7, 7.6, and 19.8 cm distal to the fibular head. The FHL was dually innervated with a proximal and distal branch in ~60% of their specimens, whereas the remaining 40% of specimens had only 1 branch. The TP was dually innervated with a proximal and distal branch in all specimens, whereas the FDL was innervated by a single branch in all specimens. These findings suggest that, although the FHL is an optimal donor from a length perspective, there is a near 50% risk of denervation, as at least 38% of patients will only have 1 major motor branch.19 The TP and FDL branches are the most appropriate donors when integrating the findings of the studies by Bodily et al and Apaydin et al.

Other feasibility studies have focused on the superficial posterior compartment muscles in the setting of high nerve injury.20,21 The branches to the gastrocnemius muscles are found at the level of the popliteal fossa and range in length between 2 and 5 cm, with the lateral gastrocnemius branch being longer. Unfortunately, these donors can be too proximal and short to accommodate distal nerve transfers and would require coaptation at the level of the fibular tunnel or interposition nerve grafting.20 Yilmaz et al22 performed a cadaveric study of tibial nerve branching patterns and compatibility of DPN transfers. They found that the soleus was most suited for transfers when considering length and level of branching.22 The soleus is dually innervated with an anterior and posterior branch, with the anterior branch being more distal. Bodily’s results demonstrated that the anterior soleus branch was found approximately 7.5 cm distal to the tibial plateau and was 4.7 cm in length.18,22 The posterior soleus branch is found more proximal and has been described in high CPN injury nerve transfers.23,24 An advantage of the soleus branch transfer is that it weakens but does not sacrifice ankle plantarflexion, resulting in a favorable trade for dorsiflexion when neurotization of the TA is successful with the nerve transfer.11,22,25

Finally, if the level of injury is distal to the branch point of the CPN into its deep and superficial nerves, an SPN to DPN transfer is possible. Nath et al13 described 5 cases of SPN to DPN transfers via an intraneural dissection of the SPN. They found that 2 fascicle groups from the SPN were sufficient to reinnervate the DPN.13

In summary, there are many options for distal nerve transfers, and based on the cadaveric studies and case series in the literature, the ideal nerve transfers for DPN reinnervation include the anterior soleus branch (ie, distal branch), TP branch, and FDL branch. The results of our branch measurements indicate that motor branches are short and intraneural dissection may be necessary to facilitate tensionless coaptation.13,26

Study Limitations

There were several limitations to our study, as based upon anatomic variability, it would take a massive number of specimens to determine frequency of all branching patterns.27–30 Follow-up cadaveric studies will focus on performing tibial to peroneal transfers in situ and identify possible obstacles and associated maneuvers to facilitate the transfers. However, given the incisional distances we recorded and corroborated with other studies identifying the branching patterns of the tibial nerve, we are confident that these transfers are attainable.

CONCLUSIONS

Surgical interventions for CPN injury range from fibular tunnel release and neurolysis to nerve transfers and nerve grafting. The data presented provide clinically relevant information for the peripheral nerve surgeon for fascicular reconstruction of the nerve. Proximal to the fibular tunnel, fascicles are divided into 3 major groups (motor-sensory-motor) compared with 2 distally (anterior motor-posterior sensory). Injuries at the level of the fibular tunnel can be reconstructed with distal nerve transfers from the tibial nerve motor branches to the DPN or TA motor branch through an interosseous septal window.
