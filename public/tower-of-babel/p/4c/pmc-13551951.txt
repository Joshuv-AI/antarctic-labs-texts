Is clinical experience associated with lower perceived resuscitation stress in emergency nurses? Psychometric validation of the Chinese Perceived Stress during Resuscitation Scale and comparison of novice, intermediate, and expert practitioners
Authors: Haixia Zhao, Yiming Lu, Xuan Qiao, Chun Wang, Xinwei Zhang, Yujia Liu, Yue Ma, Chuang Li, Yang Zheng, Yanyan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResuscitation imposes substantial psychological stress on emergency nurses, potentially impairing clinical performance and patient outcomes. Whether cumulative clinical experience is associated with lower perceived resuscitation stress remains empirically unresolved, and no validated Chinese instrument exists to measure resuscitation-specific perceived stress. This study aimed to translate the Perceived Stress during Resuscitation Scale (PSDRS) into Chinese, psychometrically evaluate its properties, and test its known-groups validity by comparing scores across novice, intermediate, and expert emergency nurses.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across seven tertiary hospitals in Liaoning Province, China, from June 2025 to February 2026. Following the Brislin translation model, the original PSDRS was translated, back-translated, and culturally adapted through expert panel review and pilot testing. A convenience sample of 434 emergency nurses completed the Chinese PSDRS. Participants were stratified by clinical experience: novice (< 2 years, n = 52), intermediate (2–10 years, n = 202), and expert (≥ 10 years, n = 180). Psychometric evaluation included content validity (S-CVI), construct validity via exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) on randomly split subsamples, internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α), test-retest reliability (ICC), and known-groups validity tested through one-way ANOVA.ResultsThe Chinese PSDRS demonstrated robust psychometric properties. Content validity was excellent (S-CVI = 0.926). A randomly split subsample for EFA (n = 210) extracted a seven-factor structure explaining 75.17% of total variance. CFA on the validation subsample (n = 224) confirmed satisfactory model fit (χ²/df = 1.295, RMSEA = 0.036, CFI = 0.958, TLI = 0.951). Internal consistency was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.815) and test-retest reliability was acceptable (ICC = 0.774). Known-groups validity was supported, with expert nurses reporting lower PSDRS scores (M = 2.31, SD = 0.42) than intermediate (M = 2.48, SD = 0.45) and novice nurses (M = 2.67, SD = 0.49; F = 24.36, P < 0.001, η²=0.102). Post-hoc analyses confirmed all pairwise comparisons were statistically significant (all P < 0.01).ConclusionsThe Chinese PSDRS is a reliable and valid instrument. A significant dose-response gradient was observed, with greater clinical experience associated with lower perceived resuscitation stress. This pattern is consistent with theoretical perspectives such as the stress-inoculation hypothesis and professional development frameworks; however, these mechanisms were not directly tested, and causal inferences cannot be drawn given the cross-sectional design. This tool can facilitate early identification of at-risk novice nurses and inform experience-tailored stress management interventions.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05030-y.Keywords: Clinical experience, Emergency nursing, Known-groups validity, Perceived stress, Psychometric validation, Resuscitation

Introduction

Resuscitation is among the most critical interventions in emergency healthcare; any delay in initiating cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) exponentially reduces a patient’s chance of survival [1, 2]. Emergency nurses are typically the first to identify cardiac arrest and coordinate the team’s response [3, 4], frequently performing under conditions of extreme urgency that render resuscitation one of the most stressful clinical scenarios [5].

Perceived stress during resuscitation carries significant consequences. For nurses, chronic exposure is associated with compassion fatigue, emotional exhaustion, burnout, moral distress, and secondary traumatic stress [6–9], with psychological sequelae that can persist for months or years after particularly distressing events [10]. For patients, elevated provider stress impairs cognitive function, situational awareness, and decision-making, potentially degrading CPR quality and outcomes [11, 12]. Consequently, accurate assessment is a prerequisite for identifying at-risk nurses and developing targeted interventions.

Despite this importance, a validated instrument has been lacking in the Chinese context. Existing studies predominantly rely on generic scales like the Maslach Burnout Inventory, which cannot differentiate resuscitation-specific stressors from routine occupational demands [13]. A recent national survey documented high workplace stress among Chinese emergency nurses but used a general occupational stress scale incapable of capturing scenario-specific pressures, while a large-scale multicenter study identified a 26.2% prevalence of effort-reward imbalance using a generic questionnaire [14], further underscoring the need for a context-specific instrument. The Perceived Stress during Resuscitation Scale (PSDRS), recently validated among Turkish emergency nurses, addresses this gap by capturing multidimensional stress through seven sub-dimensions [15]. However, cross-cultural validation is essential because perceived stress is shaped by cultural norms and healthcare system structures that differ substantially between countries [16]. Without rigorous adaptation, direct application in Chinese settings could yield misleading results.

A further unresolved question concerns the relationship between clinical experience and resuscitation stress. The stress-inoculation hypothesis posits that repeated, controlled exposure to stressors, coupled with effective coping skills training, can reduce subsequent stress reactivity [17, 18]. This process theoretically unfolds through a three-phase pathway: (1) cumulative clinical exposure provides repeated “inoculation” doses; (2) progressive skill acquisition and successful coping experiences build psychological resilience; and (3) enhanced resilience buffers acute stress responses. A recent study demonstrated that emergency nurses undergoing progressive real-rescue training showed greater improvements in stress resistance than those receiving traditional simulation-based training alone [19], and work experience has been significantly associated with higher resilience and lower occupational stress among new nurses [20]. The theoretical framework illustrating a potential association between clinical experience and perceived resuscitation stress is presented in Fig. 1, which informed the known-groups validity hypothesis; the cross-sectional design of the present study does not permit causal inference regarding the depicted pathways.

However, empirical evidence regarding the association between experience and perceived stress remains inconsistent. Qualitative findings suggest that even experienced clinicians can be profoundly affected, with participants averaging over seven years of emergency department experience nonetheless reporting significant moral distress and secondary trauma [10]. This complexity suggests that while experience may be associated with lower stress on certain dimensions, other factors—such as moral conflict or the emotional intensity of individual cases—may exert effects independent of professional tenure. Known-groups validity testing, which examines whether an instrument discriminates between groups expected to differ on the measured construct [21], offers a rigorous methodological approach to addressing this question while generating validity evidence for the PSDRS [22, 23].

The present study therefore had two objectives:

To translate the PSDRS into Simplified Chinese, culturally adapt it, and evaluate its psychometric properties, including content, structural, and construct validity, and reliability.

We hypothesized that if greater clinical experience is associated with lower perceived resuscitation stress—as would be expected under theoretical frameworks such as the stress-inoculation hypothesis—expert nurses would demonstrate significantly lower PSDRS scores than novice nurses, following a dose-response pattern.

Methods

Study design

A multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted across seven tertiary hospitals in Liaoning Province, China, from June 2025 to February 2026. The study followed the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional studies [24]. Psychometric evaluation was guided by the COSMIN methodology, assessing content validity (including face validity through pilot testing), structural validity (EFA and CFA), internal consistency (Cronbach’s α), reliability (test-retest), and construct validity (convergent, discriminant, and known-groups validity).

Participants

A convenience sample of emergency department nurses from seven tertiary grade A hospitals was recruited. These hospitals comprised three municipal central hospitals, two university-affiliated general hospitals, one specialized emergency hospital, and one maternal and child health hospital with an independent emergency department. Inclusion criteria were: (1) practicing nurses with ≥ 6 months of emergency department clinical experience; (2) currently working in the emergency department; and (3) voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria were: (1) nurses from other departments; and (2) nurses on extended leave (maternity, sick, or annual leave exceeding three months).

Sample size was estimated using the Kendall method (5–10 participants per item) [25]. With 27 PSDRS items, 135–270 participants were required; EFA requires a minimum of 100 cases and CFA at least 200 [26]. Accounting for a 20% invalid response rate, a minimum of 338 participants was targeted. A total of 450 questionnaires were distributed; after excluding duplicates (n = 5), incomplete (n = 8), and contradictory entries (n = 3), 434 valid questionnaires were retained (effective response rate 96.44%). The total sample was randomly split using the SPSS random number generator (seed: 20250601) into subsample 1 for EFA (n = 210) and subsample 2 for CFA (n = 224). Demographic homogeneity between the two subsamples was confirmed via chi-square and t-tests (all P > 0.05). Both subsamples met the minimum threshold for factor analysis stability [27].

Instruments

General information questionnaire

A researcher-designed questionnaire collected demographic and work-related characteristics including gender, age, educational level, years of clinical experience, technical title, weekly working hours, monthly night shifts, and self-rated health condition.

Perceived Stress during Resuscitation Scale (PSDRS)

The PSDRS was originally developed by Şener et al. [15], comprising 27 items across seven dimensions: inadequate working conditions (items 1–6), ethical difficulties (items 7–9), insufficient authority (items 10–13), unsuccessful resuscitation effort (items 14–17), difficulties caused by patient relatives (items 18–21), emotional pressure caused by patient/patient relatives (items 22–24), and unexpected situations (items 25–27). Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = never to 4 = always). Higher scores indicate greater perceived stress. The original scale demonstrated robust psychometric properties [15]. Permission for translation was obtained from the corresponding author.

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

Following the Brislin model [16], the process comprised five stages. The complete translation and cross-cultural adaptation process is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Brislin translation and cross-cultural adaptation process of the Chinese Perceived Stress during Resuscitation Scale (PSDRS)

Stage 1 – Forward translation

Two bilingual nursing postgraduates independently translated the PSDRS into Simplified Chinese (versions P1, P2).

Stage 2 – Reconciliation

A third postgraduate synthesized P1 and P2, resolving discrepancies through discussion to create a unified version (P).

Stage 3 – Back-translation

Two doctoral students and one master’s student in medical English, blinded to the original, back-translated P into English (versions PP1, PP2).

Stage 4 – Expert review

An emergency medicine expert and the research team compared PP1/PP2 with the original. Items with semantic discrepancies (< 90% consistency) underwent iterative forward and back-translation. Specifically, the panel evaluated whether culturally bound expressions—such as family management stress dynamics unique to Chinese hierarchical medical teams, the impact of filial piety expectations on family interactions during resuscitation, and communication challenges arising from the prevalent practice of family-witnessed resuscitation in Chinese emergency departments—were equivalently captured.

Stage 5 – Pilot testing and finalization

A pilot test with 40 nurses (data not included in analysis) assessed item clarity and comprehensibility. The scale was confirmed to have clear semantics and a reasonable structure, requiring no item modifications. The panel integrated all feedback to establish the final Chinese PSDRS. A representative item from each of the seven dimensions is provided in Supplementary Material (Table S4).

Content validity

A six-member expert panel (two emergency physicians, three senior emergency nursing specialists, one psychometrics methodologist) independently rated each item’s relevance (1 = not relevant to 4 = highly relevant) [28]. All panelists held senior professional titles and had at least 10 years of clinical or research experience. I-CVI was calculated as the proportion rating 3 or 4; S-CVI/Ave was the mean of all I-CVIs. I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 indicated excellent validity [28].

Data collection

A pilot survey with 40 nurses from one tertiary grade A hospital confirmed clarity and an approximate 8-minute median completion time. For the formal investigation, participants completed the electronic questionnaire via Questionnaire Star (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd.). The survey was optimized for both desktop and mobile completion. Quality controls included: (1) IP address and device binding restrictions to prevent repeat submissions; (2) mandatory settings for all items to prevent missing data; and (3) embedded logical consistency checks for key demographic variables (e.g., working years and professional title). Given the mandatory settings, no missing data imputation was performed; implications of forced-response design are addressed in the Limitations. Data were transmitted via encrypted HTTPS protocol and stored on password-protected servers. The homepage presented an electronic informed consent form detailing the purpose, confidentiality, and right to withdraw. A separate sample of 30 participants completed the PSDRS again at a 14-day interval for test-retest analysis. The survey followed the CHERRIES checklist for electronic surveys.

Data analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 25.0. Significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed).

Item analysis

The critical ratio method compared the top 27% and bottom 27% scorers using independent-samples t-tests; critical ratio > 3.000 and P < 0.001 indicated satisfactory discrimination [29]. Item-total Pearson correlations ≥ 0.400 were required [29].

Validity analysis

Content validity was assessed via I-CVI and S-CVI/Ave. For construct validity, EFA on subsample 1 (n = 210) used principal component analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation. PCA was selected for the following reasons: (1) to maintain methodological consistency with the original Turkish PSDRS development study, which also employed PCA; (2) PCA has been widely used in cross-cultural scale adaptation research when the primary aim is to examine whether the factor structure identified in the source instrument replicates in a new cultural context; and (3) the seven-factor structure was subsequently cross-validated through CFA on an independent subsample. Criteria for factor retention were: KMO > 0.700, Bartlett’s test of sphericity P < 0.050 [30], factor loadings ≥ 0.400, eigenvalues > 1.0. CFA on subsample 2 (n = 224) used maximum likelihood estimation; model fit thresholds: χ²/df < 3.000, RMSEA < 0.080, CFI > 0.900, TLI > 0.900 [31]. Convergent validity required CR > 0.700 and AVE > 0.400 [32]; discriminant validity used the Fornell-Larcker criterion.

Reliability analysis

Internal consistency (Cronbach’s α ≥ 0.700) [33], split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability (ICC; two-way mixed-effects, absolute agreement) with ICC ≥ 0.700 indicating acceptable stability [34] were calculated. The 14-day interval and a sample of 30 were chosen to balance minimizing recall bias against ensuring stability of trait-like stress perceptions [34].

Known-groups validity

Participants were categorized by clinical experience, informed by Benner’s novice-to-expert framework [35]: novice (< 2 years, n = 52), intermediate (2–10 years, n = 202), and expert (≥ 10 years, n = 180). One-way ANOVA compared total and subscale PSDRS scores; Welch’s ANOVA was applied if Levene’s test was significant. Post-hoc comparisons used Tukey’s HSD or Games-Howell tests. Effect size was quantified via η² (0.01 = small, 0.06 = medium, 0.14 = large) [36]. The hypothesized dose-response pattern predicted: expert < intermediate < novice.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jinzhou Medical University (Approval No.: JZMULL2025333). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participant anonymity was ensured through de-identification of all questionnaire data, and responses were stored on encrypted servers accessible only to the research team.

This dose-response gradient was consistently observed across all seven subscales. Discussion

This study translated and validated the Chinese PSDRS among 434 emergency nurses, providing evidence of its reliability and validity, and demonstrating significant differences in PSDRS scores across experience groups, with greater clinical experience associated with lower perceived resuscitation stress.

Psychometric properties

The Chinese PSDRS demonstrated excellent content validity (S-CVI = 0.926), confirming its relevance to Chinese clinical settings. The seven-factor structure aligns with the original [15], supporting cross-cultural dimensional stability. The high explained variance (75.17%) and absence of salient cross-loadings may partially reflect the rigorous translation process; however, a structure this “clean” also warrants cautious interpretation, as it might indicate high within-factor homogeneity—a point that future studies should investigate with alternative extraction methods or bifactor modeling. CFA fit indices met established thresholds [31] and were favorable compared to the original development study [15]. Internal consistency and test-retest stability were satisfactory, supporting the scale’s utility for longitudinal assessments.

Convergent and discriminant validity evidence supports construct validity. All dimensions met the relaxed AVE threshold [32], indicating items share adequate common variance despite capturing heterogeneous facets of their respective constructs. The Fornell-Larcker criterion confirmed dimensional distinctiveness. A critical limitation, however, is the absence of criterion-related validity evidence against established measures of general stress (e.g., Perceived Stress Scale) or psychological distress (e.g., Depression Anxiety Stress Scales, DASS-21). This omission precludes definitive demonstration that the PSDRS captures a construct distinct from general occupational stress and limits the ability to position it within the broader nomological network of psychological distress. Future validation studies must incorporate concurrent criterion measures to address this fundamental gap.

Clinical experience differences and potential explanatory perspectives

The observed experience-dependent gradient in total and subscale PSDRS scores supports the known-groups validity of the instrument by demonstrating its ability to distinguish between groups that were theoretically expected to differ. The medium effect size for the total score (η²=0.102) suggests meaningful differences across levels of clinical experience. These findings are consistent with—but do not directly test—theoretical perspectives such as the stress-inoculation hypothesis [17, 18] and Benner’s novice-to-expert framework [35]. One possible explanation, consistent with stress inoculation theory, is that repeated exposure to resuscitation situations may contribute to the development of technical competence, situational awareness, and emotional coping strategies that are associated with lower perceived stress [17, 18, 20]. In addition, the observed association may reflect broader processes of professional adaptation described in Benner’s framework [35], whereby nurses progressively integrate crisis management capabilities into their professional practice. However, we emphasize that professional identity was not directly measured in this study, and the specific mechanisms through which experience may relate to stress perception remain to be empirically tested in longitudinal designs. The present known-groups validity evidence should therefore be interpreted as supporting the discriminative capacity of the PSDRS, rather than as confirmatory evidence for any particular theoretical mechanism.

Several alternative explanations for the observed association must be considered. First, the cross-sectional design cannot distinguish experience-related changes in stress perception from healthy worker survivor effects, whereby nurses most susceptible to severe stress may have already left the emergency nursing workforce, resulting in an apparent but artifactual decline in stress among the remaining experienced cohort. Second, resilience-related selective retention may operate such that nurses with higher baseline psychological resilience are more likely to remain in emergency settings and accumulate years of experience. Third, unmeasured organizational variables—including nurse-to-patient ratios, team psychological safety, and the availability of structured post-resuscitation debriefing—may confound the relationship between experience and perceived stress. Future longitudinal research is necessary to disentangle these competing explanations and establish whether clinical experience exerts a genuine stress-attenuating effect.

Notably, the effect size was medium, suggesting that a large proportion of variance remains attributable to unmeasured factors. Potential confounders include: (1) organizational variables such as nurse-to-patient ratios, team psychological safety, and availability of structured post-resuscitation debriefing; (2) individual traits such as baseline resilience, personality, and prior psychological training; and (3) exposure intensity—an emergency nurse with two years’ experience in a high-volume trauma center may have participated in more resuscitations than a nurse with ten years in a low-acuity setting. Therefore, “years of experience” is an imperfect proxy for “cumulative stress inoculation dosage.” Additionally, the observed linear association could be shaped by a healthy worker effect, whereby nurses most susceptible to severe stress may have already left the profession, leading to an apparent difference in the surviving cohort. Furthermore, stress inoculation theory itself posits that inoculation occurs only with controlled, successful exposures; repeated traumatic or failed resuscitation experiences could theoretically lead to stress sensitization, a trajectory our cross-sectional design cannot distinguish. Longitudinal studies that track individual stress trajectories and account for actual resuscitation frequency and outcomes are essential to disentangle these competing mechanisms.

Differential effect sizes across subscales support the premise that specific skill sets are honed by experience. The strongest effects for “insufficient authority” and “difficulties caused by patient relatives” suggest that navigating hierarchical relationships and complex family interactions are strongly experience-dependent skills. In the Chinese sociocultural context, filial piety expectations and family-centered decision-making amplify these interactions, whereas the hierarchical medical structure may intensify authority-related stress. Conversely, smaller effect sizes for “emotional pressure” and “unexpected situations” suggest that certain stressors are less amenable to experiential differences. The fear accompanying pediatric codes or the shock of sudden unexpected deterioration may represent fundamental human responses that no amount of clinical experience can entirely override. This interpretation aligns with qualitative evidence showing that even seasoned nurses report significant moral distress [10] and highlights boundary conditions of the stress-inoculation hypothesis, suggesting that complementary psychological support mechanisms—such as structured peer debriefing and access to professional counseling—remain essential even for the most experienced clinicians. While the present study focused on lower perceived resuscitation stress among more experienced nurses, repeated exposure to resuscitation may also foster posttraumatic growth, compassion satisfaction, and heightened existential awareness among some clinicians. Whether the PSDRS could help identify dimensions of stress that are more or less amenable to such positive adaptation remains an empirical question for future longitudinal research.

Cultural and contextual considerations

Distinct response patterns emerged that reflect the Chinese healthcare system. “Insufficient authority” scores were elevated across all groups, possibly reflecting the physician-led hierarchy where nurses operate within a circumscribed scope of practice and physician confirmation is typically required for time-critical decisions. “Difficulties caused by patient relatives” captured culturally specific stressors intensified by filial piety, strained doctor-patient relationships, and the evolving practice of family-witnessed resuscitation in Chinese emergency departments. These culturally salient dimensions underscore the unique contribution of the PSDRS beyond generic occupational stress scales. However, these interpretations remain tentative and should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory; cross-national comparative studies using the PSDRS would be necessary to directly test whether these dimensional elevations are culturally specific or reflect universal characteristics of resuscitation-related stress.

A notable incidental finding was the discrepancy between clinical experience and technical title (23.3% held primary-level nurse titles despite ≥ 10 years of experience). This career advancement stagnation may independently influence stress perception and represents an important covariate for future research, as the psychological impact of blocked career progression could confound or moderate the experience-stress relationship.

Implications for nursing practice and education

The Chinese PSDRS offers nursing managers a validated, multidimensional tool for routine screening. Given the significantly higher stress among novices, early identification can facilitate timely, stage-specific interventions mapped to distinct phases of professional development:

For novice nurses (< 2 years)

Simulation-based psychological preparedness training with progressive stress exposure, structured post-simulation debriefing to promote cognitive reframing, and mentorship pairing with experienced nurses who model effective coping behaviors. These interventions align with the core components of stress inoculation training and can be integrated into existing standardized residency programs.

For intermediate nurses (2–10 years)

Monthly ethics case conferences addressing resuscitation-specific ethical dilemmas (e.g., decisions to terminate efforts, managing family presence) and standardized communication workshops for complex family interactions. These interventions target stressors that intensify with increasing clinical responsibility.

For expert nurses (≥ 10 years)

Biannual psychological resilience assessments using the PSDRS, combined with access to confidential peer support groups and Employee Assistance Programs. Although expert nurses reported the lowest overall stress, their persistent vulnerability in emotional pressure and unexpected situations domains suggests that even experienced clinicians benefit from ongoing psychological support.

Crucially, PSDRS-based screening requires robust ethical safeguards. Results should be communicated confidentially with personalized feedback, avoiding coercive intervention or mandatory disclosure to supervisors. Organizational policies must prohibit the use of PSDRS scores for performance evaluation or promotion decisions, thereby fostering a psychologically safe environment that encourages honest self-reporting and help-seeking behavior.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged when interpreting these findings.

First, the sample was recruited exclusively from tertiary grade A hospitals in Liaoning Province. This limits generalizability to nurses in primary or secondary hospitals, or to other Chinese regions with different healthcare resource allocations. Future multicenter studies encompassing diverse geographic regions and hospital tiers are warranted to establish nationally representative normative data.

Second, the online convenience sampling method may have excluded nurses with limited internet access or excessive workloads, introducing selection bias. Furthermore, the mandatory completion design of the e-survey, while preventing missing data, may risk low-quality or “straight-lined” responses. The absence of cognitive interviewing during the adaptation process represents an additional methodological limitation; while expert and pilot reviews supported item clarity, direct probing of target users’ understanding of culturally nuanced items was not conducted.

Third, and most critically, the study lacks criterion-related validity evidence. As the primary objective was initial psychometric validation following translation and cultural adaptation, established measures of psychological distress (e.g., Depression Anxiety Stress Scales, DASS-21), general perceived stress (e.g., Perceived Stress Scale, PSS-10), or burnout (e.g., Maslach Burnout Inventory, MBI) were not included in the study protocol. Consequently, we cannot confirm the extent to which the Chinese PSDRS converges with or diverges from these related constructs. Although self-rated health was collected as a demographic variable, it is a single-item global assessment and does not constitute adequate criterion validity evidence. This omission fundamentally limits understanding of whether the PSDRS captures a unique dimension of resuscitation-specific stress distinct from general occupational stress, anxiety, or burnout, and represents the single most important psychometric gap to be addressed in future research.

Fourth, clinical experience (years of employment) served as a proxy for cumulative resuscitation exposure; however, actual frequency and intensity of resuscitation participation were not measured and may vary substantially within experience groups across hospitals with differing patient volumes, introducing potential misclassification bias in the known-groups comparison.

Fifth, unmeasured confounders—including personality traits, baseline psychological resilience, formal psychological training, social support, and organizational variables such as nurse-to-patient ratios, team safety culture, and availability of psychological support resources—may influence the experience-stress relationship and preclude causal conclusions.

Sixth, the cross-sectional design cannot distinguish stress attenuation due to experience from alternative explanations such as healthy worker survivor effects (differential attrition of stress-vulnerable nurses), nor can it rule out stress sensitization trajectories among those with predominantly traumatic resuscitation experiences.

Seventh, exploratory factor analysis employed principal component analysis (PCA) rather than a common factor extraction method (e.g., principal axis factoring or maximum likelihood). PCA analyzes total variance including unique and error variance, whereas common factor methods partition shared variance to identify latent constructs. While PCA was chosen to maintain consistency with the original scale development study and the resulting structure was supported by CFA, future replications using common factor extraction methods would provide additional psychometric evidence regarding the latent structure of the Chinese PSDRS.

Eighth, test-retest reliability was assessed in a relatively small subsample (n = 30), and the 14-day interval, while designed to balance recall and stability considerations, may have allowed for genuine fluctuation in state-like stress perceptions influenced by recent shift patterns or specific resuscitation events.

Finally, the known-groups hypothesis was specified a priori based on theoretical grounding in the stress-inoculation hypothesis and Benner’s framework; however, the study was not prospectively registered in an observational study registry, and the possibility of selective outcome reporting cannot be completely excluded.

Future research directions

Building on these findings, several research directions emerge:

Longitudinal cohort studies tracking novice emergency nurses from career entry through professional development should be conducted to elucidate the trajectory of perceived resuscitation stress over time, identify critical periods for intervention, and disentangle the effects of experience from healthy worker survivor effects using appropriate statistical methods (e.g., growth mixture modeling, survival analysis). These studies should incorporate objective exposure-related variables to more accurately quantify cumulative resuscitation exposure, including the number of CPR events attended, frequency of caring for critically ill patients, experience working in trauma centers, and the ratio of successful versus unsuccessful resuscitation outcomes. Incorporating such variables would allow researchers to determine whether the observed association between years of experience and lower perceived stress is driven by cumulative exposure dosage rather than by chronological tenure alone.

Conclusion

The Chinese version of the Perceived Stress during Resuscitation Scale demonstrates satisfactory reliability and validity, confirming its suitability as a multidimensional tool for assessing resuscitation-specific stress among emergency nurses in China. The seven-factor structure aligns with the original instrument, and the scale effectively discriminates between nurses at different stages of professional development. Significant differences in PSDRS scores were observed across experience groups, with greater clinical experience associated with lower perceived resuscitation stress. This pattern is consistent with theoretical perspectives such as the stress-inoculation hypothesis and Benner’s novice-to-expert framework. However, because of the cross-sectional design, alternative explanations—including healthy worker survivor effects, resilience-related selective retention, and unmeasured confounding factors—cannot be excluded. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal and causal relationships underlying this association. The finding that novices report the highest stress levels underscores the importance of early career support and stage-specific interventions. The Chinese PSDRS offers nursing managers and educators a validated instrument to identify at-risk novice nurses, guide targeted stress management programs, and evaluate intervention effectiveness, ultimately contributing to improved nurse well-being and resuscitation quality. By making resuscitation-specific stress visible and measurable, the Chinese PSDRS provides a foundation for both individual-level support and further research on stress management in emergency nursing.
