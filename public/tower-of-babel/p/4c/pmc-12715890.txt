Infectious mononucleosis complicated with Kawasaki disease: Two case reports and literature review
Authors: Mengjia Liu, Lingyun Guo, Qinjing Li, Dilara Dilmurat, Gang Liu, Zhengde Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionInfectious mononucleosis (IM) and Kawasaki disease (KD) can present with overlapping clinical features, occasionally complicating the differential diagnosis. Given the significant differences in their management, accurate distinction is critical. It is exceptionally rare for a single patient to simultaneously suffer from both IM and KD in clinical practice.Case presentationWe report two cases of children over five years of age who initially presented with clinical manifestations suggestive of IM and serological evidence consistent with primary Epstein‐Barr virus (EBV) infection, supporting an initial diagnosis of IM. Subsequently, both patients developed clinical features meeting the diagnostic criteria for KD. The final diagnosis was co‐occurrence of IM and KD.ConclusionWe describe two cases of concurrent IM and KD, offering further insight into a possible association between primary EBV infection and KD. Clinicians should be mindful of the potential co‐occurrence of these disorders, strive to avoid conflicts in treatment strategies, and take proactive measures to prevent complications.

INTRODUCTION

Primary Epstein‐Barr virus (EBV) infection may present asymptomatically or as infectious mononucleosis (IM). 1 In China, the peak incidence of IM occurs among children aged 4–6 years. 2 Typical clinical features of IM include lymphadenopathy, fever, and pharyngitis, 3 which overlap with those of Kawasaki disease (KD). KD primarily affects children under 5 years of age. 4 The clinical manifestations of classic KD include fever, lymphadenopathy, strawberry tongue, among others. 5 Some affected children may develop coronary artery lesions. Clinically, the management strategies for IM and KD differ significantly. 4 , 5

Due to the overlap in early clinical manifestations between IM and KD, differentiating between the two diseases can be challenging. This report describes two pediatric patients who were initially diagnosed with IM and subsequently developed KD. Additionally, relevant literature cases are reviewed and analyzed to highlight this rare yet clinically significant phenomenon.

CASE REPORT

Case 1

A previously healthy 6‐year‐8‐month‐old girl was admitted with fever (Tmax 39.5 °C), eyelid edema, sore throat, cervical lymphadenopathy, and snoring for 2 days. Physical examination revealed scattered grayish‐white flaky exudates on the tonsils, pruritic erythematous polymorphic rashes on both legs, and splenomegaly. Laboratory tests revealed white blood cell count (WBC) of 18.2 × 109/L, platelet count (PLT) of 198 × 109/L, lymphocytes percentage (L) of 50.2%, C‐reactive protein (CRP) level of <10.0 mg/L, and procalcitonin (PCT) of 0.7 µg/L. Laboratory evaluation revealed elevated levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) at 88.3 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 123.2 U/L. Serological tests were positive for anti‐EBV viral capsid antigen (VCA) IgM and low‐affinity anti‐EBV‐VCA‐IgG antibodies, and negative for anti‐EBV nuclear antigen (EBNA) IgG antibodies. EBV‐DNA in plasma was 6.78 × 103 copies/mL, while EBV‐DNA in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) was 1.57 × 106 copies/mL. Abdominal Doppler ultrasound revealed splenomegaly, with the lower edge of the spleen located 1.0 cm below the costal margin. Based on the proposed diagnostic criteria of IM, 3 the patient was diagnosed with IM. Treatment consisted of symptomatic and supportive care, combined with acyclovir therapy.

On the second day of admission, her temperature peaked up at 39.0 °C, accompanied by bilateral non‐exudative conjunctival congestion, strawberry tongue, red lips, and spreading polymorphic rash on both legs and the waist. Laboratory tests showed CRP level of 31.0 mg/L, WBC of 16.1 × 109/L, L of 35.8%, neutrophil percentage (N) of 53.1%, atypical lymphocyte percentage (AL) of 8%, PLT of 117 × 109/L. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 31 mm/h. Flow cytometry analysis revealed the following lymphocyte subsets: CD19+ B cells accounted for 4.4%, CD16+CD56+ NK cells comprised 4.4%, CD4+ T cells constituted 5.5%, and CD8+ T cells represented 68.3%. These findings resulted in an inverted CD4:CD8 ratio. Cardiac Doppler ultrasound indicated right coronary artery dilation (proximal diameter 3.1–2.7 mm), and the left coronary artery and cardiac structure and function were normal. According to the proposed diagnostic criteria of KD, 6 the girl was diagnosed with KD and treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) (2 g/kg). Due to thrombocytopenia and petechiae, aspirin was not given. On day 5, her fever persisted, peaking at 38.5 °C, and she was given another IVIG of 1 g/kg. By day 6, symptoms such as nasal congestion, snoring, pharyngitis, red lips, and cervical lymphadenopathy had alleviated; the rash had faded, and conjunctival congestion had resolved. The fever subsided by day 7. Laboratory results showed WBC of 11.1 × 109/L, PLT of 288 × 109/L, L of 78.8%, N of 14.5%, and AL of 8%. Aspirin combined with dipyridamole was initiated for anti‐platelet therapy, but was discontinued due to new facial rash on the ninth day.

On the 10th day of admission, the patient's clinical condition significantly improved. Physical examination revealed dark red papules and maculopapules on the face and neck, and mild hepatosplenomegaly. No desquamation was observed on the fingers or toes. Laboratory investigations revealed an AST of 83.7 U/L, and ALT of 42.7 U/L. Cardiac ultrasonography still showed right coronary artery dilation (proximal diameter 3.1–2.7 mm). The patient was discharged and followed‐up.

One month after discharge, the patient's condition had improved significantly, although mild cervical lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly persisted. AST and ALT levels had returned to normal. Plasma EBV‐DNA was undetectable (<500 copies/mL), while EBV‐DNA in PBMCs was measured at 5.82 × 103 copies/mL. Cardiac ultrasound revealed normalized coronary artery dimensions and normal cardiac function (Figure S1).

Case 2

A previously healthy 5‐year‐4‐month‐old boy was admitted with a 16‐day history of fever (Tmax 39.0 °C), eyelid edema, cervical lymphadenopathy, sore throat, and snoring. Physical examination revealed grade II tonsillar hypertrophy. Laboratory tests revealed the following results: WBC 7.8 × 109/L, PLT 309 × 109/L, L 74.7%, N 19.9%, AL 8%, hemoglobin (Hb) 82 g/L, AST 44.9 U/L, ALT 47.5 U/L, CD4:CD8 ratio 0.21, and D‐dimer 1.13 mg/L. Serological tests were positive for anti‐EBV‐VCA‐IgM and low‐affinity anti‐EBV‐VCA‐IgG antibodies, and negative for anti‐EBV‐EBNA‐IgG antibodies. EBV‐DNA in PBMCs was 3.00 × 104 copies/mL. Abdominal ultrasound revealed splenomegaly. Echocardiography showed a dilated left coronary artery (proximal diameter 3.4 mm and Z = +2.43) with otherwise normal cardiac structure and function. Additionally, the parents reported that laboratory tests performed on the second day of illness showed a WBC of 3.5 × 109/L, PLT of 198 × 109/L, L of 66.4%, N of 24%. By day 5 of the disease course, the patient developed conjunctival congestion. According to the diagnostic criteria for both IM and KD, 3 , 4 a diagnosis of IM and incomplete Kawasaki disease (IKD) was established and ganciclovir was initiated as antiviral therapy. On the second day after admission, IVIG (2 g/kg) was administered.

On the seventh day after admission, the patient was afebrile. Laboratory tests showed WBC of 4.4 × 109/L, PLT of 289 × 109/L, L of 77.9 %, and N of 14.8 %, AST of 62.0 U/L, and ALT of 41.9 U/L. The echocardiographic results were normal. The patient was discharged and followed‐up.

One month after discharge, the child continued to experience fatigue and poor appetite. Physical examination showed that cervical lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly had improved. EBV‐DNA in plasma was undetectable, while EBV‐DNA in PBMCs was 8.39 × 103 copies/mL. AST and ALT in serum were 32.6 U/L and 13.5 U/L, respectively. Abdominal ultrasound still indicated splenomegaly, with the spleen extending 3.4 cm below the left costal margin. (Figure S2).

DISCUSSION

We reported two cases of children over 5 years of age who developed KD during the clinical course of IM. Both patients initially presented with the classic clinical manifestations of IM, and then developed the clinical features of classic KD or IKD. These cases revealed a rare occurrence of KD comorbid with IM, which is different from previous reports suggesting that KD typically manifests prior to the detection of EBV infection. These findings provide evidence supporting EBV as one of the potential initiative factors in the pathogenesis of KD.

Currently, the pathogenesis of KD remains unclear. It is widely hypothesized that KD is triggered by infectious agents. 11 Potential pathogens include EBV, adenovirus, hepatitis B virus, and others. 12 However, there is no clear evidence directly linking EBV infection to the pathogenesis of KD. Kikuta et al. 13 reported serological evidence of primary EBV infection in 49 of 57 KD patients (86%) and in 15 of 22 patients with recurrent KD (68%) within the first month after disease onset, suggesting that EBV may act as a trigger rather than a direct cause. However, Stijin et al. 14 observed a lower seroprevalence of EBV among children under 10 years of age with KD. Therefore, the relationship between EBV infection and KD requires further investigation.

In China, the peak incidence of IM has been reported in children aged 4–6 years, with a trend toward older age groups in recent years. 2 KD while capable of occurring at any age, is most common in children under 5 years old, with incidence peaking between 18 and 24 months. 15 Pediatricians should maintain a suspicion for atypical co‐occurrence of these conditions when school‐age children diagnosed with either KD or IM present with unusual clinical features. Pavone et al. 16 recommended that in febrile children with confirmed EBV infection, differential diagnosis should include evaluation for possible concurrent KD. Therefore, when a child with KD displays symptoms typical of IM—such as eyelid edema, snoring, or hepatosplenomegaly—concurrent IM should be considered. Similarly, if a child diagnosed with IM develops signs suggestive of KD, such as rash, strawberry tongue, or conjunctivitis, the possibility of comorbid KD should be carefully assessed. In such cases, further diagnostic and therapeutic actions should adhere to relevant clinical guidelines. Moreover, since many KD patients are evaluated in cardiology or rheumatology settings, clinicians in these specialties should also be aware of potential EBV co‐infection. Early recognition and appropriate management are essential to minimize the risk of complications.

The treatment strategies for IM and KD have some differences, and their co‐occurrence may lead to therapeutic conflicts. KD is typically managed with IVIG and oral moderate‐dose aspirin to prevent complications. 17 Aspirin is not recommended in patients with severe liver dysfunction. 4 However, most children with IM have liver dysfunction. This underscores the importance of timely diagnosis, close monitoring for comorbidity, and individualized adjustment of treatment regimens. Notably, the administration of IVIG may interfere with EBV serological testing, potentially causing false‐positive results. Therefore, in cases with clinical suspicion of EBV co‐infection, EBV antibody testing should be performed prior to IVIG infusion. Timely and accurate diagnosis, followed by appropriate intervention, is essential for reducing the risk of complications—particularly cardiac involvement in KD—and improving overall clinical outcomes.
