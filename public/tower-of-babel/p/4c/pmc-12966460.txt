CRMP2 inhibits metastasis formation by impairing ILF3-dependent stabilization of CXCL10 mRNA in breast cancer
Authors: Binyan Lin, Mei Luo, Yanqing Zhou, Xin Liu, Qin Zhu, Zebin Weng, Lei Li, Tao Cao, Jing Sun, Dawei Yang, E-Hu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor-derived elements contribute to the formation of the pre-metastatic niche (PMN) and facilitate cancer metastasis, but much less is known about the key molecular mechanisms. Here, we demonstrate that collapsin response mediator protein 2 (CRMP2), a critical regulator of the cytoskeleton, is associated with metastasis in breast cancer. CRMP2 overexpression inhibits both lung metastasis and PMN formation in breast cancer. Mechanistically, CRMP2 overexpression leads to downregulation of CXCL10. We also found that the correlation between CRMP2 and CXCL10 is mediated by interleukin enhancer-binding factor 3 (ILF3). The D-hydantoinases (D-HYD) fragment of CRMP2 specifically interacts with the second double-stranded RNA binding motif (dsRBM2) of ILF3. Overexpressed CRMP2 reduces the expression of ILF3 in proteasome-dependent degradation via Lys 48-linked polyubiquitination at Lys257, Lys332 and Lys413. In addition, ILF3 directly binds to CXCL10 mRNA, thereby increasing CXCL10 mRNA stability. Finally, we found that psoralen interacts with CRMP2 and suppresses the development of lung metastases in breast cancer. In conclusion, our findings uncover a critical CRMP2-related mechanism behind breast tumor metastasis, and the CRMP2-ILF3-CXCL10 axis may provide a potential therapeutic strategy for controlling breast cancer metastasis. Subject terms: Breast cancer, Metastasis, Tumour-suppressor proteins

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent form of cancer globally and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women [1, 2]. In 2022, breast cancer accounted for ~670,000 global deaths, comprising approximately 12–15% of cancer fatalities among females [2]. In the United States, breast cancer accounted for 32% of all new diagnoses for women and caused approximately 42,170 female deaths in 2025 [3]. Up to 50–60% of patients with high-grade breast cancer have a high metastatic potential [4]. Partly due to the lack of approved therapies for metastasis, breast cancer patients with high metastatic potency often experience relapse, resulting in an inferior 3-year overall survival rate.

More than 90% of cancer-related deaths continue to be caused by cancer metastasis, highlighting the importance of distinguishing key molecular elements at each step of tumor metastasis and developing new strategies to control this process. The spread of cancer cells from the primary tumor, their evasion of immunosurveillance, and their ability to thrive in unfamiliar tissue microenvironments are necessary for the formation of metastases [5]. Beyond intrinsic cellular traits such as cytoskeletal changes, dynamic interactions between cancer cells and the foreign tissue environment may also play a crucial role [6, 7]. Molecular components secreted by the primary tumor can mobilize bone marrow-derived cells (BMDCs) into the healthy organ, such as the lung, creating a conducive microenvironment for cell colonization and proliferation [8, 9]. The recruited BMDCs, including tumor-associated macrophages and neutrophils, created a pre-metastatic niche (PMN) characterized by inflammation, immunosuppression, organotropism, and reprogramming [10]. Primary tumor-derived molecules such as cytokines, proteins, and extracellular vesicles [11], including C-X-C motif ligands (CXCLs) [12] and lysyl oxidase homolog 2 (LOXL2), are profoundly involved in the formation of the PMN [13]. Consequently, the inhibition of tumor biomolecule secretion may be a promising therapeutic avenue for impeding the formation of the PMN, thereby potentially suppressing breast cancer metastasis. However, the precise mechanisms by which cancer cells alter PMN formation to interfere with tumor metastasis remain largely unknown.

Collapsin response mediator protein 2 (CRMP2) was initially identified as a microtubule-associated protein for its role in regulating neuronal polarity and axon extension [14, 15]. Reduced CRMP2 expression is often associated with poor prognosis in a variety of cancers, such as colorectal cancer and acute myeloid leukemia [16, 17]. C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) is a member of the IFN-γ-inducible chemokine subfamily. As a ligand of C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3), CXCL10 recruits CXCR3+ immune cells or cancer cells to alter the host microenvironment and facilitate digital metastasis. CXCL10 induces immune suppression to drive brain metastasis [18], conditions the stroma in favor of colonization by breast cancer cells [19], and triggers dormant breast cancer cells to emerge from the metastatic niche in the liver [20]. However, the key role of CRMP2 in PMN generation to interfere with breast cancer metastasis remains largely elusive. Furthermore, whether CXCL10 is the downstream of CRMP2 to influence PMN formation, thereby affecting metastasis, remains to be determined.

Here, based on public datasets and clinical correlation analysis, we identified CRMP2 as a suppressor of breast cancer metastasis. Using a spontaneous metastasis model, we demonstrated that CRMP2 overexpression in breast tumor cells suppressed lung metastasis following primary tumor excision. CRMP2 overexpression inhibited lung PMN formation, reduced recruitment of immunosuppressive neutrophils and macrophages, and impeded lung colonization by breast tumor cells. Significantly, overexpression of CRMP2 in primary tumor cells impaired the expression of CXCL10. Neutralization of CXCL10 reduced lung metastasis of breast tumors, while recombinant CXCL10 (rCXCL10) abolished the suppressive effect from CRMP2-overexpression. Mechanistically, we found that interleukin enhancer-binding factor 3 (ILF3), an RNA-binding protein, interacts with CRMP2. Overexpression of CRMP2 promotes ILF3 degradation via Lys (K) 48-linked-polyubiquitination-mediated proteasomal degradation at Lys257, Lys332, and Lys413. Given that ILF3 functions as an RNA-binding protein (RBP), we further showed that it binds to CXCL10 mRNA and increases its abundance by enhancing its stability. Overexpression of CRMP2 attenuated the association between ILF3 and CXCL10. Indeed, ectopically expressed ILF3 abolished the reduction of CXCL10 by CRMP2 overexpression. Finally, we identified a natural compound, psoralen, which targets CRMP2 to inhibit breast cancer metastasis. Our study identified CRMP2 as a critical regulator of breast cancer metastasis and highlighted the modulation of the CRMP2-ILF3-CXCL10 axis as a potentially practical therapeutic approach for metastatic breast cancer.

Materials and methods

Cells, tissues and cell culture

MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. HEK-293T cell line was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. SUM159PT cell line was purchased from COBIOER Biosciences. MDA-MB-231 and HEK-293T cells were cultured in DMEM medium (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGM12800-500). SUM159PT and 4T1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (KeyGEN BioTECH, KGM31800-500). All cell culture medium was supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Vazyme, F101-01).

Breast tumor specimens and paired adjacent breast tissues were collected with the approval of the Institutional Ethics Review Boards of Jiangsu Province Hospital in accordance with the guidelines of the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects (CIOMS). Study approval was obtained from the ethical committees of Jiangsu Province Hospital (case number 2021-SR-308). Patients gave written informed consent. Commercial tissue microarray with follow-up data was from Biotechwell.

Plasmid, transfection and lentiviral infection

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates. After confluence reached 70%, plasmids were transfected by using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #11668030). Human CRMP2 overexpression and CRMP2 short hairpin RNA (shRNA) plasmids were generated by us as indicated before [21]. Mouse CRMP2 overexpression plasmid was purchased from GENEWIZ. CRMP2-D1, D-HYD, D2, ILF3-6×HIS, ILF3-dsRBM1, and ILF3-dsRBM2 were cloned by Corues Biotechnology. 3×HA tag ILF3, ILF3-ΔDZF, ΔdsRBM1, and ΔdsRBM2, 6×His tag ILF3-K100R, ILF3-K257R, ILF3-K332R, and ILF3-K413R were obtained from Public Protein/Plasmid Library (PPL). Ub-WT-HA, Ub-K48-HA, Ub-K63-HA, Ub-K48R-HA, and Ub-K63R-HA were obtained from GenScript. Lentiviral packaging kit (Yeasen, #41102ES10) was performed to transfect lentiviral plasmid into HEK-293T cells. The viral media was collected at 48 and 72 h after being transfected and concentrated by the Lv-up lentivirus concentrate kit (PPL, #P002Y). Briefly, mixed the virus-containing medium with the lentivirus concentration reagent at a 4:1 ratio and incubated the mixture at 4 °C overnight. Centrifuged the mixture at 3500 × g for 10 min and collected the supernatant as the concentrated viral preparation on the next day.

Animal studies

Female, 6-week-old BALB/c mice were purchased from SLAC Laboratory Animal Company. All the animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (202404A093) and complied with national and international guidelines.

Murine 4T1 (TNBC) cell lines were used to generate tumor models in BALB/c mice. This tumor-derived cell line is an animal model for stage Ⅳ. The tumor growth and metastatic spread of 4T1 cells in BALB/c mice very closely mimic human breast cancer. When injected into BALB/c mice, 4T1 spontaneously produces highly metastatic tumors that can metastasize to the lung, liver, lymph nodes and brain while the primary tumor is growing in situ. The primary tumor can readily be surgically removed, so that metastatic disease can be studied in an animal setting comparable to the clinical situation where the primary tumor is surgically removed, and metastatic foci remain intact [22, 23].

For spontaneous lung metastasis assays, 5 × 105 4T1 cells in 100 μL PBS were injected into the No.4 mammary fat pad of mice. 4T1 cells were previously transduced with overexpressing CRMP2 and luciferase (Luc), enabling bioluminescent imaging (BLI) of lung metastasis progression. Tumor volume was measured every other day. On day 10, the primary tumor was removed and sewed up the wound. Lung metastasis was detected by bioluminescence imaging (BLI) on day 21 after tumor removal.

For tumor conditioned medium (TCM) generation and in vivo PMN formation, the procedure was adapted from elsewhere [24], and the details were shown in the supplementary methods 3.

For lung colonization, in vivo antibody treatments and cytokine co-injection, the procedure is an adaptation from elsewhere [18], and the details are shown in Supplementary Methods 4.

For BLI, mice were injected intraperitoneally with D-Luciferin, Potassium salt (150 mg/kg, Yeasen, #40902ES03), anesthetized using isoflurane (RWD, R510-22-10) and imaged with IVIS Spectrum (PerkinElmer).

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) analysis

Cells were lysed in co-immunoprecipitation lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, #87787) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (NCM Biotech, #P002, 1:100) for 30 min on ice and centrifuged at 13000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatants. Meanwhile, protein A/G immunomagnetic beads (TargetMol, #C0104B) were incubated with antibodies by rotating at room temperature for 1 h and washed with PBST three times to remove the non-specific binding. The primary antibody-conjugated beads and cell lysates were mixed and rotated at 4 °C overnight. The following day, the beads were washed with PBST five times on ice, and the protein was eluted by 30 μL SDS loading buffer and boiled for 5 min. The eluted protein or protein complexes were detected by immunoblot.

Ubiquitination assay

The indicated plasmids were transfected into HEK-293T cells for 48 h. Cells were lysed with IP buffer (Thermo Scientific, #87787) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (NCM Biotech, #P002, 1:100) after 8 h of 20 μM MG132 (GLPBIO, #GC10383) treatment. The lysate was centrifuged for 15 min at 13000 rpm at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and incubated with His-conjugated beads (Beyotime, #P2135) while rotating at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the bound beads were washed five times with PBST and proteins were used for western blot analysis. The ubiquitinated form of ILF3 was measured by western blot using an anti-HA antibody.

mRNA stability analysis

MDA-MB-231 cells transfected with negative control or ILF3 were treated with actinomycin D (10 mg/mL) (Selleckchem, #S8964) for the indicated time period. Total RNA was extracted, followed by quantitative qRT-PCR analysis. The mRNA levels were calibrated and compared to the 0 h time point.

Microscale thermophoresis ligand-binding assay (MST)

HEK-293T cells with mCherry-ILF3 expression were lysed in RIPA buffer. Cell lysates were prepared by dilution in PBST, and mCherry protein fluorescence signals were detected using Monolith NT.115 (NanoTemper). CRMP2 ligand was then mixed and injected into NT.115 standard coated capillaries (NanoTemper). To determine the affinity between psoralen and CRMP2, psoralen was mixed with the cell lysate of GFP-CRMP2-expressing HEK-293T cells. Standard curve fitting was used to calculate KD.

Statistical analysis

Data presentation and statistical analyses were described in the Fig. legends. Three independent experiments were performed. Results were expressed as the mean ± SD. Student’s t test was used to compare two groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare more than two groups. The Mann–Whitney test was used when the dispersion of the data did not conform to the normal distribution. All the statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 for Windows.

Results

Decreased CRMP2 expression correlates with metastasis in breast cancer

To identify critical factors involved in breast cancer pathogenesis, we performed differential gene expression analysis using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Genotype-Tissue Expression and GEO databases. Integration of data from tumor (T) versus normal tissues (N) and tumors isolated from the distant metastatic site (Met) versus primary tumors in situ (Pri) revealed that 66 genes were downregulated (Fig. 1A). Among these altered genes, we found that the expression of the CRMP2 gene (DPYSL2) was reduced in breast tumor samples (Fig. 1B). In addition, CRMP2 mRNA levels were significantly decreased in tumors isolated from the distant metastatic site compared to primary tumor (Fig. 1C), suggesting its potential role in metastatic breast cancer. To explore the clinical relevance and significance of CRMP2, we first analyzed 10 cancer cohorts from TCGA for transcriptomic datasets of clinical samples, and the results indicated that the expression of CRMP2 had no differences between normal and tumor tissues, except in breast cancer. A significantly lower expression of CRMP2 was observed in breast tumor tissues compared to normal breast tissues (Fig. 1D). To further elucidate the expression of CRMP2 in different cells of breast cancer, we obtained 5 breast cancer samples from the GEO dataset GSE148673 and analyzed the scRNA-seq data. The results showed that CRMP2 expression was significantly higher in normal breast epithelial cells than in tumoral breast epithelial cells (Fig. 1E). Moreover, we analyzed the breast cancer cohort from the TCGA datasets and found that CRMP2 gene expression was higher in normal breast tissues compared to various breast cancer subtypes (Fig. 1F). Additionally, according to TCGA datasets, higher CRMP2 expression was linked to better overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) in patients with TNBC, a highly aggressive form of breast cancer (Figs. 1G and S1A). Elevated CRMP2 expression was associated with improved DSS in Luminal A and HER2-positive breast cancer subtypes, and with enhanced Relapse-Free Survival in the Luminal B subtype (Fig. S1A), but not with OS in any of these subtypes (Fig. S1B). Notably, CRMP2 expression was significantly reduced at both the mRNA and protein levels in tumor tissue from breast cancer patients compared to paired normal tissue (Fig. 1H, I). These findings were confirmed by IHC and IF assays (Fig. 1J, K). By examining the association of CRMP2 with metastasis of breast cancer patients, we observed that CRMP2 levels were significantly higher in tumors without metastasis compared to tumors with metastasis (Fig. 1L). Meanwhile, we investigated the tumors with lower CRMP2 expression levels that were co-expressed with higher expression of MMP2 and MMP9, indicating stronger aggressive (Fig. S1C). Collectively, our results demonstrate that decreased CRMP2 expression correlates with metastasis in breast cancer.

CRMP2 overexpression inhibits spontaneous lung metastases and suppresses PMN formation

To elucidate the relevance of CRMP2 in breast cancer progression, a spontaneous lung metastasis model was induced by the mouse TNBC cell line 4T1 (Figs. 2A and S2A). 4T1 cells with or without CRMP2 overexpression were injected into the No.4 mammary fat pad of mice. CRMP2 overexpression did not affect primary tumor volume up to day 10 before resection (Fig. S2B). Analysis of the proliferation marker Ki67 and the apoptosis marker cleaved-caspase 3 in tumor tissues by IHC revealed that CRMP2 overexpression did not significantly alter tumor proliferation or apoptosis (Fig. S2C). CRMP2 overexpression markedly reduced spontaneous lung metastasis in the 3 weeks post-resection (Fig. 2B, C). In the pre-metastatic lung, several factors, including S100A8, S100A9, and MMP9, are induced to promote metastasis. Furthermore, S100A8 and S100A9 directly stimulate tumor cell migration and recruit myeloid cells [25, 26], while MMP9 promotes the invasion and survival of tumor cells in the lung [27]. We observed a significant reduction of S100A8, S100A9, and MMP9 in spontaneously metastatic lungs after CRMP2 overexpression in primary tumor cells (Fig. S2D). The primary tumor primes the lung before circulating tumor cells arrive, preparing a favourable environment named PMN in which cancer cells can seed and grow. Next, we then explored the role of CRMP2 in PMN formation. Tumor-conditioned medium (TCM) was generated by culturing 4T1 primary breast tumors in DMEM without fetal bovine serum (FBS). Subsequently, BALB/c mice received intravenous injections of either TCM or control medium (CM) over a period of 3 weeks to establish an experimental PMN model and determine the characteristic marker expression (Fig. 2D) [24, 28]. The results showed that breast cancer cells induced the expression of S100A8, S100A9, and MMP9 in the lungs. However, CRMP2 overexpression in the tumor cells markedly suppressed the production of these factors (Figs. 2E, F, and S2E). Meanwhile, the levels of other tumor-derived secreted factors (TDSFs), including TNF-α and IL-6, were also elevated in the PMN. This induction was significantly attenuated by CRMP2 overexpression (Fig. S2E). The PMN, formed through interactions between cancer-secreted factors and bone marrow-derived cells (BMDCs), comprises distinct BMDC subpopulations that facilitate tumor cell invasion into the lung [29]. The pre-metastatic lung is characterized by an enrichment of neutrophils, which can inhibit T-cell proliferation, whereas macrophages facilitate the seeding and growth of metastatic cells [30–32]. We observed neutrophil accumulation in the pre-metastatic lung, which was significantly reduced by CRMP2 overexpression (Fig. 2G). Similarly, macrophage recruitment was observed, although in lower numbers than neutrophils (Fig. 2H). CD4 and CD8 T cells were reduced in the pre-metastatic lung, whereas CRMP2 overexpression restored their levels (Fig. S3A, B, D). Dendritic cells (DCs) showed no significant difference with CRMP2 variations (Fig. S3C, D). Immunofluorescence assays confirmed these observations (Figs. 2I, J and S3E, F). Collectively, these data suggest that CRMP2 overexpression at the primary site inhibits the PMN formation and prevents the spontaneous lung metastasis of breast cancer.

CRMP2 overexpression suppresses the lung metastasis by down-regulating CXCL10

C-X-C chemokines accumulate immune suppressive cells to facilitate tumor metastasis, especially contributing to the formation of PMN. As a consequence, we analyzed the change of C-X-C chemokine genes via databases. Screening of C-X-C chemokine genes revealed increased CXCL10 expression in tumor versus normal tissues. Furthermore, CXCL10 levels were higher in invasive tumors than in situ tumors, suggesting its potential involvement in tumor metastasis (Fig. 3A). To further detect the association between CRMP2 and CXCL10, we examined the changes in C-X-C chemokines mRNA after Flag-CRMP2 overexpression in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. S4A). A significant reduction in CXCL10 was observed (Fig. 3B). Subsequent analysis showed that the levels of both mRNA and secreted CXCL10 were markedly reduced in response to CRMP2 overexpression in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 3C, D). These findings were also confirmed in 4T1 cells (Fig. 3E, F). Next, we detected CXCL10 levels in lung tissue from mice with spontaneous lung metastases. Our data indicated that CRMP2 overexpression in primary tumors resulted in lower CXCL10 secretion in the metastatic lung compared to the empty vector group (Figs. 3G and S4B). The IHC results further confirmed that CXCL10 was elevated in the lung PMN and significantly decreased by CRMP2 overexpression (Figs. 3H and S4C). Given that the CXCL10 binds to CXCR3 and mediates cell migration, we observed a marked accumulation of CXCR3+ neutrophils in the pre-metastatic lung, which was notably suppressed by CRMP2 overexpression. (Fig. S4D). To determine whether CRMP2 inhibits PMN formation via CXCL10, we established a lung colonization model by injecting 4T1 cells into mice to assess the functional roles of CRMP2 and CXCL10 in tumor colonization. Overexpression of CRMP2 in primary tumor cells reduced their lung colonization ability (Fig. 3I, J). Additionally, we co-injected αCXCL10 antibody with 4T1 cells or co-injected recombinant CXCL10 (rCXCL10) with CRMP2-overexpressing 4T1 cells into lung tissue. The findings demonstrated that neutralization of CXCL10 impeded 4T1 cell colonization in the lung, while rCXCL10 counteracted the colonization-suppressing effect of CRMP2 (Fig. 3I, J). Neutralization of CXCL10 was observed to significantly reduce the expression of S100A8, S100A9, and MMP9. Conversely, the administration of rCXCL10 was found to increase the levels of these markers, which demonstrated a decrease in response to CRMP2 overexpression in primary breast cancer cells (Figs. 3K and S4E). Also, we investigated how CXCR3+ neutrophils and macrophages were altered by different CRMP2 and CXCL10 expression levels. Consistent with these findings, neutralization of CXCL10 with αCXCL10 antibody inhibited the accumulation of CXCR3+ neutrophils and macrophages, whereas rCXCL10 promoted their recruitment, which was prevented by CRMP2 overexpression (Fig. S4F, G). Using a spontaneous metastasis model, we also found that the anti-metastatic function of CRMP2 is mediated through CXCL10: an CXCL10 antibody mimicked the effect of CRMP2 by suppressing lung colonization, whereas rCXCL10 neutralized it (Fig. S4H). To further investigate CXCL10’s role in breast cancer, we analyzed its expression in breast tumor tissues and their paired adjacent normal tissues. The results indicated that the mRNA levels of CXCL10 were significantly higher in tumor tissues compared to the normal tissues (Fig. S5A). By analyzing the TCGA TNBC subtype BRCA cohort for the transcriptomic datasets of clinical samples, we also observed significantly higher CXCL10 expression in breast tumor tissues compared to normal breast tissues (Fig. S5B). Furthermore, there was an inverse correlation between CXCL10 levels and OS in breast cancer patients (Fig. S5C). These findings suggest that CRMP2 overexpression impedes PMN formation and metastases in the lung by suppressing the expression of tumor-secreted CXCL10.

CRMP2 directly interacts with ILF3

To investigate how CRMP2, a known protein-binding partner, regulates CXCL10 expression, we next to identify its specific interacting protein. We performed silver staining on the co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay samples and analyzed the differential bands using LC-MS/MS assays (Figs. 4A and S6A). The results identified ILF3, an RNA-binding protein, as an interactor with CRMP2. This interaction was further validated by endogenous and exogenous co-IP experiments (Fig. 4B–D) and confirmed by the colocalization of the two proteins, as shown by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 4E, F). Using the microscale thermophoresis (MST) method, the dissociation constant (KD) between CRMP2 and ILF3 was determined to be 0.13 ± 0.12 μM (Fig. 4G). To map the binding domains in CRMP2 and ILF3, we generated several CRMP2 fragments and ILF3-truncated mutants. The co-IP assays revealed that ILF3 binds to the D-hydantoinases (D-HYD) domain of CRMP2, while the absence of the second double-stranded RNA binding motif (dsRBM2) domains of ILF3 prominently impaired their interaction (Fig. 4H, I), suggesting that these domains are required for the interaction between CRMP2 and ILF3. To further analyze the interaction between these domains, we detected the interaction between full-length Flag-CRMP2 and HA-ILF3 when transfected together with the D-HYD fragment of CRMP2. The results revealed that supplementation with D-HYD fragment competitively decreased the interaction between full-length CRMP2 and ILF3 (Fig. 4J). Meanwhile, co-transfected with the dsRBM2 fragment of ILF3 diminished the interaction between CRMP2 and full-length ILF3 (Fig. 4K). IF analysis of HEK-293T cells co-transfected with the D-HYD and dsRBM2 fragments revealed robust colocalization of these domains (Fig. 4L). Given the distinct subcellular localizations of CRMP2 and ILF3, we further examined ILF3 distribution. We found that CRMP2 primarily interacts with ILF3 in the cytoplasm and CRMP2 overexpression reduces the nuclear accumulation of ILF3 (Fig. S6B). We next predicted the specific amino acid interaction sites via molecular docking. The analysis indicated that the binding between CRMP2 and ILF3 involves key residues CRMP2 Thr567 - ILF3 Lys591 and CRMP2 Glu230 - ILF3 Ser575 (Fig. 4M). Taken together, these results demonstrate that CRMP2 directly interacts with ILF3.

CRMP2 promotes degradation of ILF3 via Lys 48-dependent ubiquitination at Lys257, Lys332 and Lys413

To determine the relationship between CRMP2 and ILF3, we analyzed their expression in tumor tissues from breast cancer patients. An inverse correlation between CRMP2 and ILF3 was observed in 10 out of 13 tumor tissues (Fig. 5A). We overexpressed CRMP2 in both MDA-MB-231 and SUM159PT cells and found that it significantly reduced ILF3 protein levels. In parallel, we observed that overexpression of ILF3 did not affect CRMP2 levels in these cells (Fig. 5B, C). Additionally, CRMP2 knockdown in MDA-MB-231 cells significantly elevated the expression of ILF3 (Fig. 5D). Further analysis showed a dose-dependent decrease in ILF3 expression with different concentrations of ectopically expressed CRMP2 in MDA-MB-231, SUM159PT, and HEK-293T cells (Fig. 5E). After CRMP2 knockdown, rescue of CRMP2 expression also reduced ILF3 expression (Fig. 5F). To determine the regulatory relationship between CRMP2 and ILF3, we performed a cycloheximide (CHX) chase assay to monitor protein degradation. Overexpression of CRMP2 shortened the half-life of endogenous ILF3 protein in MDA-MB-231 and SUM159PT cells after application of CHX (Fig. 5G). Selective degradation of proteins occurs primarily via the ubiquitin-proteasome system and autophagy. Therefore, to investigate the mechanism by which CRMP2 regulates ILF3 degradation, we used the proteasomal inhibitor MG132 and the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ). Treatment with MG132, but not CQ, restored ILF3 expression (Fig. 5H), suggesting that CRMP2 promotes ILF3 degradation via the proteasome manner. Meanwhile, the suppressive effect of CRMP2 overexpression on nuclear ILF3 was restored by MG132 (Fig. S6B). Consistently, CRMP2 overexpression increased the ubiquitination of ILF3, while CRMP2 knockdown abolished this effect (Fig. 5I–K). To elucidate the CRMP2-mediated linking of ILF3 to the polyubiquitin chain, a number of mutated ubiquitin isoforms were used. K48- and K63-linked ubiquitin chains, which represent the canonical linkage types, are often investigated for their distinct roles in protein regulation. The results revealed that CRMP2 specifically enhanced the K48-linked polyubiquitination of ILF3 but had no effect on K63-linked polyubiquitination (Fig. 5L), and the ability of CRMP2 to ubiquitinate ILF3 was impaired by the K48R mutation (Fig. 5M), suggesting that CRMP2 reduces ILF3 protein stability via K48-linked polyubiquitination.

To identify ubiquitinated lysine residues in ILF3, four Lys-to-Arg mutants were constructed (K100R, K257R, K332R, and K413R) according to the prediction of the GPS-Uber database (http://gpsuber.biocuckoo.cn/). Co-IP results showed that the K257R, K332R, and K413R mutations largely disrupted CRMP2-mediated ubiquitination, especially K413R (Fig. 5N). Compared with wild-type (WT) ILF3, the K413R mutant conferred resistance to CRMP2-induced ILF3 degradation (Fig. 5O). The K257R and K332R mutants showed the same resistance (Fig. 5O). The substrate-E3 interface is critical for dictating substrate specificity. To determine which E3 ligase is involved in ILF3 ubiquitination and degradation, we searched the potential E3 ligases in the BioGRID database and found that the WW domain-containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2 (WWP2) and E3 ubiquitin-protein ligase parkin are the co-interactors of both CRMP2 and ILF3. Co-IP results showed that WWP2, but not parkin, interacted with ILF3 (Fig. 5P), and overexpressed CRMP2 promoted the interaction between WWP2 and ILF3 (Fig. 5Q). Knocking down WWP2 abolished the degradation and ubiquitination of ILF3 induced by CRMP2 overexpression (Fig. 5R, S). Taken together, these results suggest that CRMP2 reduces ILF3 protein levels by promoting its K48-linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation via the E3 ligase WWP2.

ILF3 interacts with and stabilizes CXCL10 mRNA

Given the regulatory role of CRMP2 on ILF3 and CXCL10, we next explored whether ILF3 mediates the interaction between CRMP2 and CXCL10. ILF3 is an RNA-binding protein that regulates gene expression and stabilizes mRNAs through its RNA-binding activity [33]. Based on the known function of ILF3, we hypothesized that it acts as an mRNA stabilizer for CXCL10. Using RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) followed by qPCR assay, we found that ILF3 interacts with the mRNA of CXCL10 (Fig. 6A). To identify the binding domain, we introduced HA-ILF3-full length (FL) and ILF3 dsRBM deleted mutants. The RIP assay results indicated that CXCL10 mRNA interacts with ILF3 at the dsRBM2 motif (Fig. 6B). Given the mRNA stabilization and translation regulatory function from ILF3 [34–36], we hypothesized that ILF3 regulates the mRNA level of CXCL10 through its interaction. Indeed, our data revealed that overexpression of ILF3 in MDA-MB-231 cells resulted in higher mRNA and secretion levels of CXCL10 compared to the empty vector group (Fig. 6C, D). Consistent with its role as an mRNA stabilizer, ILF3 overexpression decelerated the decay of CXCL10 mRNA after actinomycin D treatment, validating a specific stabilizing function without altering housekeeping gene stability (Figs. 6E and S6C). To determine if CRMP2 modulates the interplay between ILF3 and CXCL10, we monitored their molecular interaction following CRMP2 overexpression or knockdown. The results showed that CRMP2 overexpression impaired the ILF3-CXCL10 mRNA interaction, whereas CRMP2 knockdown conversely enhanced it (Fig. 6F, G). Given that CRMP2 downregulates CXCL10 expression, we next investigate whether this regulatory effect is mediated by ILF3. ILF3 overexpression counteracted CRMP2-mediated suppression of CXCL10 in MDA-MB-231 cells, confirming that CRMP2 acts through an ILF3-dependent mechanism (Fig. 6H). Through these findings, we establish a mechanistic model wherein CRMP2 suppresses CXCL10 by targeting the mRNA-stabilizing function of ILF3, which binds CXCL10 transcripts specifically through its dsRBM2 domain (Fig. 6I).

Psoralen targets CRMP2 to ameliorate metastatic breast cancer

Given the therapeutic potential of CRMP2 in breast cancer, we screened natural products with the potential to target CRMP2 using a surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay. From hundreds of compounds in a self-house natural product library, 13 compounds that interact with CRMP2 were identified based on their pre-screen response values (Fig. 7A). We observed that 4 compounds (resveratrol, saikosaponin A, psoralen, and fraxetin) increased the expression of CRMP2, and among which psoralen displayed the highest anti-invasion activity (Figs. 7B and S7A). Psoralen is a furocoumarin compound derived from Psoralea corylifolia Linn (Fig. S7B). The CCK-8 assay showed that psoralen did not significantly affect the viability of MDA-MB-231 or SUM159PT cells at concentrations up to 500 μM (Fig. S7C). Using non-cytotoxic concentrations (25, 50, and 100 μM for MDA-MB-231; 50, 100, and 150 μM for SUM159PT), we evaluated the anti-invasive effect of psoralen. The results showed that psoralen inhibited the invasion of both MDA-MB-231 and SUM159PT cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7C), but this effect was abolished after CRMP2 silencing (Fig. 7D). Additionally, psoralen could increase the thermal and enzymatic stability of CRMP2 (Fig. 7E–G). To further investigate the interaction between psoralen and CRMP2, cell lysates were incubated with a synthesized biotinylated psoralen (bio-psoralen) (Fig. S7D), which exhibited a similar effect as psoralen on invasion inhibition (Fig. S7E). The results indicated that CRMP2 was pulled down by bio-psoralen in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 7H), and the pull-down of CRMP2 by bio-psoralen could be competed off by free psoralen (Fig. 7I). Moreover, the affinity of psoralen for CRMP2 was measured using MST assay, which yielded a KD value of 53.88 μM (Fig. 7J). Molecular docking analysis predicted the psoralen’s binding site was CRMP2 Thr408 (Fig. 7K). Also, psoralen markedly increased CRMP2 levels in a concentration-dependent manner while significantly down-regulated ILF3 and CXCL10 expression (Fig. 7L–N). These results suggest that there is a direct and specific interaction between psoralen and CRMP2.

We next investigated whether psoralen could inhibit breast tumor development in vivo. To establish a xenograft model, 4T1 cells were injected into the fourth mammary fat pad of mice. When tumor volumes reached about 100 mm3, measurements were initiated to monitor growth. Psoralen did not affect the growth of breast tumors in 4T1 xenograft mouse model (Fig. S8A–C). Psoralen treatment did not significantly affect tumor growth, as evidenced by unaltered levels of the proliferation marker Ki67 and the apoptosis marker cleaved-caspase 3 (Fig. S8D). Concurrently, this treatment resulted in upregulated CRMP2 expression and downregulated ILF3 and CXCL10 (Fig. S8E). We established an experimental lung metastasis model by injecting 4T1 cells into mice via the tail vein. As expected, psoralen treatment significantly suppressed breast cancer lung metastasis in a dose-dependent manner (at 20, 40, and 80 mg/kg) (Fig. S8F). To further validate its efficacy, we tested psoralen in a spontaneous lung metastasis model, where it again demonstrated robust inhibition of metastasis in a dose-dependent manner (at 20, 40, and 80 mg/kg) (Figs. 7O and S8G). Given that psoralen inhibits metastasis by targeting CRMP2, we hypothesize that it acts by influencing PMN formation. The expression of PMN markers (S100A8, S100A9, and MMP9) and CXCL10 in spontaneously metastatic lungs were markedly downregulated after psoralen treatment (Fig. S8H), thus demonstrating that psoralen enhanced CRMP2 to suppress the breast cancer lung metastasis. We next employed a PMN mouse model to further validate the effect of psoralen in suppressing PMN formation. Similarly, enhancement of CRMP2 by psoralen reduced the levels of PMN makers and CXCL10 (Figs. 7P and S9A, B) and suppressed the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages (Fig. S9C, D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that psoralen targets and elevates CRMP2 levels, inhibits the formation of the PMN, and impedes breast cancer metastasis.

Discussion

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy worldwide and the second leading cause of death from cancer among women. Metastasis is the major cause of breast cancer-associated mortality. Therefore, it is important to identify a potential target for metastasis suppression in breast cancer therapy. In this study, we found that CRMP2 is a crucial regulator of breast cancer metastasis, which prevents the formation of the immunosuppressive PMN, thereby suppressing breast cancer lung metastasis. CRMP2 interacted with ILF3 and promoted its degradation, which affected the stability of CXCL10 mRNA, resulting in the downregulation of CXCL10. This process reduced the accumulation of neutrophils and macrophages and prevented the colonization of breast cancer cells in the lungs. Our results indicate that CRMP2 expression in primary tumors inhibits lung PMN formation, thereby suppressing breast cancer cell colonization and blocking metastasis.

Cancer metastasis is a dynamic and complex process, with up to 63% of cancers leading to metastasis spread [37]. Recent studies have classified the cascade of metastasis into two major processes: (1) the invasion of primary cancer cells into distant tissues and (2) colonization [5]. Each step is influenced by the PMN, a predetermined microenvironment in distant organs that is altered by the tumor cells to favor their survival and outgrowth prior to the arrival of tumor cells [9]. Tumor cells release cytokines that induce the recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells to the PMN, supporting tumor cell seeding, survival, and proliferation. Blocking the formation of the PMN can inhibit cancer cell invasion and prevent colonization and distant metastasis. In this study, we found that CRMP2 was down-regulated in metastatic breast tumor tissues, as evidenced by database analysis and clinical specimen examination. Higher expression of CRMP2 indicated a positive survival prognosis, less invasiveness, and metastases. Next, we revealed that high levels of CRMP2 expression in the primary breast tumor inhibited spontaneous lung metastasis, suppressed the formation of the lung PMN, and blocked the recruitment of immunosuppressive neutrophils and macrophages, validating the crucial role of CRMP2 in breast cancer metastasis.

CXCL10 has been implicated in various pathological processes, including inflammation [38], tumor progression [39], and COVID-19 [40], due to its function as a mediator of immune cell migration, differentiation, and activation. In light of the canonical role of CXCL10 in the recruitment of disease-modifying T cells [41], recent research has suggested that CXCL10 recruits suppressor cells derived from myeloid cells to promote tumor metastasis, including breast cancer [18, 42, 43]. In this study, we demonstrated that CRMP2 downregulated the levels of CXCL10, which induced the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages to form the lung PMN. Accordingly, interference with CXCL10 expression may suppress the inhibition of breast cancer lung metastasis by CRMP2. Blocking CXCL10 with a neutralizing antibody results in reducing colonization of breast cancer in the lung, decreased expression of PMN markers, and suppressed accumulation of neutrophils and macrophages. In contrast, supplementation of rCXCL10 abolished the effect of CRMP2 on suppressing PMN formation and breast cancer cells colonization, suggesting that CRMP2 suppresses PMN formation and colonization of breast cancer cells via downregulating CXCL10.

CRMP2 is a cytoplasmic and cytoskeletal protein, whereas CXCL10 is a secretory protein. Given the differences in their subcellular location, we speculated that there might be some intermediate proteins bridging CRMP2 and CXCL10. By co-IP and LC-MS/MS assay, we identified ILF3 as an interacting protein of CRMP2. ILF3 is a double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)-binding protein that can bind proteins or nucleic acids to regulate gene transcription, mRNA stability, and protein translation [44]. In this study, we showed that CRMP2 interacts with ILF3 and promotes ILF3 degradation by regulating the ubiquitin-proteasome system. The ubiquitin-proteasome system is mainly driven by Ub as a degradation marker, which is under the control of multi-level reversible enzyme reactions [45]. Target proteins are covalently linked to different types of ubiquitin chains, and K48-linked polyubiquitination is used to signal for proteasomal degradation, whereas K63-linked polyubiquitination is a non-proteolytic mode of modification that is used for cell signaling [46]. We further revealed that CRMP2 promotes the ILF3’s ubiquitination mediated by K48-linked polyubiquitination at Lys257, Lys332 and Lys413. E3 ligase substrate interactions determine the specificity of the ubiquitin-proteasome system. We next explored the E3 ligase for CRMP2-induced ILF3 degradation and found that WWP2, a mammalian E3 ubiquitin ligase, mediated CRMP2-promoted ILF3 ubiquitination. High expression of ILF3 resulted in the interaction with CXCL10 mRNA, increasing its stability and leading to increased protein expression levels, suggesting that the regulatory effect of CRMP2 on CXCL10 is mediated by ILF3.

Numerous targets with known functions in tumor metastasis have been reported, but the development of new drugs for treating tumor metastasis remains challenging. CRMP2 is considered a druggable target, and a CRMP2-binding compound has been reported to reverse brain damage [47]. Since natural products generally have fewer side effects [48], and many traditional Chinese medicines have shown excellent efficacy in breast cancer, we screened over 100 natural compounds and verified psoralen as a CRMP2-binding agent. Psoralen significantly up-regulated CRMP2 protein expression while downregulating the expression of ILF3 and CXCL10. Further experiments revealed that psoralen could inhibit the formation of the PMN and metastasis of breast cancer by enhancing the expression of CRMP2 in lung metastatic, spontaneous lung metastasis, and lung pre-metastatic models. Psoralen is a furocoumarin compound derived from Psoralea corylifolia Linn. Psoralens have been used to treat vitiligo and induce pigmentation for thousands of years, along with exposure to sunlight. Recent research has reported that psoralen has anti-inflammatory and antibacterial effects. In our study, we reveal the anti-breast cancer effect and the mechanism of psoralen, but the toxicity of psoralen has not been evaluated. According to previous studies, psoralen showed liver damage by inhibiting the activity of CYPs, which limited further clinical use [49]. Therefore, a combination with other drugs or structural modification of psoralen is necessary.

Beyond its established roles in neurological conditions (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, ALS, multiple sclerosis, and bipolar disorder [50]), the DPYSL2-encoded protein CRMP2 is now being increasingly investigated in cancer. The role of CRMP2 has been reported in several cancers, including ovarian, colorectal, and lung cancer [16, 51, 52]. While CRMP2 has a well-described role in tubulin polymerization, it also distinctly functions in the regulation of calcium and sodium channel activity [53, 54]. Additionally, research has increasingly focused on the phosphorylation of CRMP2. Cell invasion and migration are driven by the restructuring of the cytoskeleton, which includes actin, tubulin, and intermediate filaments. The remodeling of actin enables the formation of filopodia, lamellipodia, and invadopodia, which facilitate cell migration and invasion [55]. The potential role of CRMP2 in actin-based structures like lamellipodia within cancer biology is a significant knowledge gap. This is striking, given that in neuroscience, CRMP2 is already established as a key regulator of lamellipodial dynamics, with its dysfunction causing aberrant F-actin bundling and stabilization linked to schizophrenia [56]. We therefore propose the novel hypothesis that CRMP2-mediated regulation of lamellipodia could be a critical mechanism driving cancer cell migration. In our previous study, we found that CRMP2 inhibited breast cancer invasion by stabilizing RECK, and that this suppressive effect was independent of tubulin stability [21], indicating that CRMP2 demonstrated the ability to regulate cell motility. On the contrary, this study demonstrates that CRMP2 impedes breast cancer metastasis by regulating CXCL10 to inhibit PMN formation, revealing a novel mechanism of action for CRMP2. Also, unlike prior reports on CRMP2 phosphorylation promoting nuclear translocation and progression, our data reveal a cytoplasmic CRMP2 D-HYD: ILF3 dsRBM2 axis inhibiting PMN via RBP ILF3 degradation. Acting as an RBP, ILF3 has recently been shown to contribute to ferroptosis resistance through stabilizing specific ferroptosis-related mRNAs [57, 58]. Given the newly identified function of ILF3 in ferroptosis resistance, the CRMP2-regulated degradation of ILF3 likely modulates ferroptosis sensitivity. Consequently, combining CRMP2-targeted agents with ferroptosis inducers presents a compelling approach to treat resistant cancers, merits further exploration.

In summary, our studies identify CRMP2 as an anti-metastasis protein, which impedes the formation of lung PMN and inhibits the metastasis of breast cancer. CRMP2 interacts with ILF3 and promotes its degradation through K48-linked polyubiquitination at Lys257, Lys332, and Lys413, thereby affecting the stabilization of CXCL10. Finally, we are screening a CRMP2-targeting compound, psoralen, which showed a potential anti-breast cancer effect. This study proposes a potential therapeutic strategy targeting CRMP2 for the prevention and treatment of metastatic breast cancer. These findings suggest that CRMP2 may be a promising target for anti-metastatic therapy for breast cancer treatment.
