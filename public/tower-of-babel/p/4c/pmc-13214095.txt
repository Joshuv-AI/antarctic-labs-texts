Cerebrospinal fluid and frontal cortex TMPRSS2 and ACE2 protein levels differ in Down syndrome and Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Carlos Avilés-Granados, Adriana Gea-González, Jorge Sáez-Leyva, Sylvia E. Perez, Elliott J. Mufson, Ann-Charlotte Granholm, María Carmona-Iragui, Henrik Zetterberg, Kaj Blennow, Juan Fortea, María-Salud García-Ayllón, Javier Sáez-Valero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Individuals with Down syndrome (DS) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) are vulnerable to COVID-19, but whether alterations in ACE2, the viral receptor, and TMPRSS2, the spike-priming protease for SARS-CoV-2, differ between these disorders is unknown. We analyzed cleaved fragments and full-length species of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) from non-infected individuals with DS (n = 9) without memory decline (nDS), DS with dementia (dDS; n = 10) and aged matched controls (n = 10). CSF levels were compared to levels in frozen postmortem frontal cortex in nDS (n = 4), dDS (n = 8) and 11 controls using quantitative fluorescent western blotting. We also examined CSF (19 AD and 19 age-matched non-AD controls) and frontal cortex from 30 AD cases and 7 non-disease controls. CSF and frontal cortex TMPRSS2 full-length zymogen and active protease-domain fragment were significantly increased in nDS, but not in dDS despite elevated zymogen. In contrast, AD CSF and frontal cortex levels of the TMPRSS2 protease fragment and zymogen remained unchanged. Regarding the viral receptor, an ~ 80 kDa fragment of ACE2 was significantly reduced in nDS CSF compared to controls, whereas there was a significant decrease in an ACE2 fragment/full-length quotient in both nDS and dDS. In frontal cortex, full-length ACE2 levels were preserved in nDS compared to controls, but several ACE2 species (120 and 110 kDa full-length, and 80 kDa fragment) were significantly decreased in dDS compared to nDS and controls. In contrast, CSF ACE2 130 kDa full-length levels were decreased compared to an increase in the fragment/full-length ratio in AD compared to controls, opposite to DS. In AD, frontal cortex levels of the 150 kDa ACE2 full-length species was significantly reduced across all Braak stages. ACE2 immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence of frontal cortex sections revealed positive puncta in the neuropil, astrocytes and blood vessels in AD and DS with or without dementia. Overall, we found an increase in active TMPRSS2 and reduced soluble ACE2 fragments individuals with DS, particularly those without dementia, which differs from that observed in AD that may differentially affect susceptibility to COVID-19 viral infection in these conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02289-9.

Background

Down syndrome (DS) is the most common genetic cause of intellectual disability. The triplication of chromosome 21 leads to over-expression of several genes associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), substantially increasing the risk of early onset dementia in individuals with DS [5]. Indeed, it has been suggested that dementia on DS individuals is a genetic form of AD [20]. Additionally, individuals with DS and dementia display increased tau pathology [42]. Likewise, individuals with DS show increased vulnerability to the SARS-CoV-2 virus leading to an increase in both morbidity and mortality [14, 27]. This could be due to multiple cellular factors including over-expression of select genes located on Hsa21, such as the transmembrane protease serine 2 (TMPRSS2) gene, a cell surface protein involved in the entry and spread of SARS-CoV-2 (reviewed in [56]). Although studies have shown that AD dementia is a significant risk factor for COVID-19-related deaths in the general population [4, 61], less is known about individuals with DS [14] despite having a ten-fold increased of dying from SARS-CoV-2 infectivity. However, even though epidemiological evidence demonstrating an increased vulnerability to the virus in people with DS or AD, the factors that predispose these individuals to the COVID-19 virus remains unclear.

The proteins TMPRSS2 and ACE2 are involved in the entry and activation of SARS-CoV-2 into host cells in the brain. TMPRSS2, is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein synthesized as an inactive zymogen, which undergoes autoproteolytic cleavage to produce an active protease that primes the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein after cell entry. The cleaved protease fragment of TMPRSS2 either remains linked to the pro-domain via an interdomain disulfide bond or circulates freely [1, 12]. In human cells, the unprocessed form of the TMPRSS2 full-length zymogen [61], is abundant in both cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [32] and plasma [49].

SARS-CoV-2 enters human cells via the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) a cell surface functional receptor, which requires priming of its spike protein by TMPRSS2 (reviewed in [29]). Although routes for SARS-CoV-2 entrance into the central nervous system (CNS) are still debated (discussed in [37]), ACE2 is found in human brain cells [17, 46], CSF [32] and primary human cortical cultures are susceptible to ACE2 receptor-mediated infection by the virus [37]. In fact, it has been suggested that altered expression of ACE2 may partly explain the link between AD with COVID-19 infection, hospitalization and mortality (reviewed in [35]). ACE2 is a type I transmembrane protein that can be cleaved through constitutive and regulated shedding, and both ectodomain fragments and full-length species are enzymatically active in the renin-angiotensin system (RAS); but membrane-resident ACE2 also plays a role in amino acid transport across the cell membrane (reviewed in [59]). Membrane-bound ACE2 facilitates SARS-CoV-2 entry into cells, whereas its soluble forms bind to the viral spike protein, blocking viral access through cellular receptors (reviewed in [3]). In fact, recombinant soluble ACE2 has shown potential therapeutic promise by inhibiting viral infection [41, 63]. Soluble full-length ACE2 and cleaved fragments co-exist in CSF [32] and plasma [21, 49]. Soluble full-length forms reflect viral tissue expression, whereas circulating fragments arise from processing of the membrane-resident protein [33]. Consequently, ACE2 dynamics require separate analysis of its molecular species within the brain and CSF of individuals with DS without (non-demented DS; nDS), with dementia (dDS) compared to levels in patients with sporadic AD. Defining alterations in active protease fragment species of TMPRSS2 and ACE2 that could influence treatment paradigms for COVID-19 infections in these disorders remains a major knowledge gap.

In the present study, we examined whether the proteins ACE2 and TMPRSS2 that are involved in the entry and activation of SARS-CoV-2 into host cells, were altered in individuals with nDS, dDS, and sporadic AD. We quantified soluble full-length and cleaved fragments of both proteins in lumbar CSF samples and in frozen postmortem frontal cortex samples obtained from different cohorts. Electrophoresis followed by quantitative fluorescent western blotting was used to discriminate between specific protein species, fragments, and full-length forms. In addition, we also examined ACE2 immunoreactivity in postmortem frontal cortex tissue from controls, DS and AD cases. Findings are discussed in the context of susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 brain infection.

Methods

DS case demographics

CSF samples from individuals with DS were obtained from the Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau (Barcelona, Spain). In total, we examined 9 cases with nDS without dementia diagnosis (5 men and 4 women, 47.5 ± 0.9 years old), 10 participants with DS and Alzheimer’s type dementia (dDS; 5 men and 5 women, 50.3 ± 1.8 years old) and 10 age-matched disomic controls (5 men and 5 women, 49.5 ± 1.5 years old) (Supplementary Table 1A). Clinical differences between non-demented individuals with DS (nDS) and dDS was assessed using the modified Cued Recall Test and the CAMDEX-DS battery [6, 19]. The intellectual disability level (ID assessment) in individuals with DS was established following the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-V) criteria, that relies on the best level of functioning assessed through a clinical interview and a semi-structured questionnaire. Examination of medical records confirmed that disomic control participants had no history or symptoms of neurological or psychiatric disorders, or memory complaints.

Frozen frontal cortex samples and associated demographic data from subjects with DS and disomic controls were provided by the HCB-IDIBAPS Biobank (B.0000575), integrated in the Platform ISCIII Biobanks and Biomodels and National Network of Biobanks (Red Nacional de Biobancos, Spain), and processed following standard operating procedures with appropriate approval of the Ethics and Scientific Committees. Four DS (2 men and 2 women, 44.3 ± 9.7 years), 8 dDS (3 men and 5 women, 58.5 ± 1.1years), and 11 control cases with no known neurological conditions (3 men and 8 women, 64.2 ± 4.5 years) were obtained from the Down Syndrome Biobank Consortium (DSBC), a national and international multisite brain bank consortium [2].

Biobanks used harmonized brain bank collection and processing protocols. Tissue collection parameters between the different biobanks did not reveal inter-batch variability in the outcome measures used here. All samples were processed following standard operating procedures with the appropriate approval of the Ethics and Scientific Committees and were subjected to standard neuropathological staging [2]. The presence of an extra chromosome 21 was confirmed by FISH and/or karyotyping in the DS cases by respective biobanks.

AD case demographics

CSF samples were from patients who sought medical advice because of subjective cognitive impairments, were obtained from the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory, Sahlgrenska University Hospital (Mölndal, Sweden). All sample aliquots were derived from de-identified clinical cases following a procedure approved by the Ethics Committee at the University of Gothenburg, Sweden. Further clinical information was not accessible. Individuals were designated as AD or non-AD controls according to CSF biomarker levels based on low Aβ42 and elevated total tau for AD. Defined cutoffs are > 90% specific for AD [25], and consist on total-tau (T-tau) > 400 pg/mL and phosphorylated tau (P-tau181) > 60 pg/mL, together with low Aβ1–42 (Aβ42) < 550 pg/mL (Supplementary Table 1B). Pre-analytical handling of the samples followed recommended published protocols [24], including centrifugation at 2200 × g for 10 min at 20 °C, subsequent aliquoting, and storage at − 80 °C until use.

Frozen frontal cortex was provided by Biobanco de Navarrabiomed a component of the Red Nacional de Biobancos (Spain). In total, 30 clinically and neuropathologically confirmed sporadic AD (18 men and 12 women, 80.8 ± 1.7 years old) cases were selected based on a premortem clinical history of dementia, neuropathological CERAD diagnosis [40] and then categorized according to Braak neurofibrillary tangle staging I to VI [9]: 10 Braak I-II (4 men and 6 women, 77.7 ± 3.4 years old), 10 Braak III-IV (4 men and 6 women, 84.7 ± 2.7 years old) and 10 Braak V- VI (4 men and 6 women, 79.9 ± 2.6 years old). Age-matched non-AD controls (5 men and 2 women, 74.7 ± 5.7 years old) were obtained from individuals with no clinical dementia and no evidence of AD brain pathology. Mean post-mortem intervals for the tissue obtained from the DS and AD collections ranged between 1.5 and 6 h, with no significant differences between groups. Freeze-thaw cycles were avoided, and new aliquots were used for each independent analysis.

This study was not pre-registered. A generic consent was obtained from all subjects, approved by Universidad Miguel Hernández ethics committee (IN.JSV.04.20) and performed in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Brain homogenization and protein extraction

For protein solubilization, approximately 0.1 g of human frontal cortex (stored at − 80 °C) were thawed slowly at 4 °C and homogenized (10% w/v) in 50 mM Tris-HCl (Cat. No. T1503, Sigma-Aldrich Co.), pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl (Cat. No. S9888: Sigma-Aldrich Co.), 5 mM EDTA (Cat. No. ED-500: Sigma-Aldrich Co.), 1% (w/v) Nonidet P-40 (Cat. No I3021: Sigma-Aldrich Co.), 0.5% (w/v) Triton X-100 (Cat. No. T9284: Sigma-Aldrich Co.) and complemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail [50]. Homogenates were sonicated and centrifuged at 70,000 × g at 4 °C for 1 h and supernatants collected and frozen at − 80 °C until assayed. Total protein concentration of the supernatants was determined by the bicinchoninic acid method (BCA) with bovine serum albumin as standard using the Protein Assay Kit (Cat. No 23225, Thermo Fisher), according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitation of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 in CSF and frontal cortex by fluorescent western blotting

ACE2 and TMPRSS2 species were detected in CSF and frontal cortex extracts by fluorescent-based imaging after sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and western blotting. The former technique provides a wider linear dynamic range than chemiluminescence [39] including a greater upper linear detection range [22]. Stripping and reprobing was avoided during western bloting. Loading volume for CSF (20 µL loaded) and protein for cortical extract (35 µg loaded) samples were selected in preliminary experiments, where using serial dilutions of control samples we confirmed that they were measured within their linear range of detection. For electrophoresis preparation, samples were heated in reducing Laemmli SDS sample buffer (Thermo Scientific™) for 7 min at 70 °C for ACE2 and 5 min at 100 °C for TMPRSS2 and resolved on 7.5% for ACE2, and 12% for TMPRSS2 Tris Glycine gels and transferred to 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with Odyssey Blocking Buffer (PBS) and incubated for ACE2 immunodetection with antibodies raised against the ectodomain (immunogen: amino acid residues 18–740) AF933 (R&D Systems; polyclonal goat, 1:200) or the anti-C-terminus (immunogen: amino acid residues 788–805) antibody ab15348 (Abcam; rabbit polyclonal; 1:500). In previous studies, the protein levels detected for the immunoreactive band attributed to the full-length species correlated with the AF933 and the ab15348 (lineal regression analysis: R = 0.8–0.9 p < 0.001). Moreover, preliminary experiments designed for attributing immunoreactive bands to specific ACE2 species, alternative C-terminal antibodies OAAB16464 (Aviva Systems Biology; polyclonal rabbit) and ABIN2840584 (Antibodies online; polyclonal rabbit) were also assayed. Antibodies directed against TMPRSS2, a 492 amino acid protein, were the 14437-1-AP antibody raised against a recombinant protein containing the 108–492 amino acids (Proteintech; rabbit polyclonal; 1:1000), the anti-C-terminus antibody H00007113 raised against a recombinant protein containing the 383–492 amino acids (Abnova; mouse monoclonal; 1:500) or the anti-N-terminus antibody (OAAB04388) raised against a synthetic peptide between amino acids 1–30 (Aviva Systems Biology; rabbit polyclonal; 1:1000). Finally, blots were incubated with the appropriate conjugated secondary antibodies (IRDye 800CW donkey anti-goat, IRDye 800CW goat anti-mouse, IRDye 680RD goat anti-mouse and IRDye 680RD goat anti-rabbit, LI-COR Biosciences) and imaged on an Odyssey Clx Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences). Quantitative analysis of ACE2 blots were resolved with the AF933 antibody, which recognizes all species, while the ab15348 antibody labeled the truncated C-terminal fragments. A comparison of the immunoreactivity of ACE2 species bands was used to estimate the relative amount of fragment with respect to full-length forms (fragment/full length ratio) for each sample (see Results section). The 14437-1-AP antibody was used for the quantitative analysis of TMPRSS2. An aliquot of the same control CSF sample was resolved in all blots to normalize the immunoreactive signal between blots. For brain samples, immunoreactivity levels were normalized to vinculin levels resolved in the same blot (a commonly used loading control of ~ 130 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich; mouse monoclonal; 1:1000). All samples were analyzed at least in duplicate on independent blots. Band intensities were evaluated using LI-COR software (Image Studio Lite).

RNA isolation and analysis of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 transcripts by qRT-PCR

For analysis of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 transcripts, total RNA from human frontal cortex was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat. No. 74104 : Qiagen) or the PureLink® RNA Mini Kit (Cat. No. 12183018 A, Invitrogen) both according to manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNAs were synthesized by reverse transcription of 2 µg of total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat. No. 10186954, Applied Biosystems; Life Technologies Paisley), according to manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) amplification was performed using QuantStudio™ (Applied Biosystems) with TaqMan™ Universal PCR Master Mix (Cat. No. 4304437, Applied Biosystems) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Transcript levels were calculated by the comparative 2−ΔCt method with respect to GAPDH cDNA. We used the specific Thermo Fisher Scientific TaqMan probes for ACE2 (assay ID: Hs01085333_m1), TMPRSS2 (assay ID: Hs01122322_m1) and human GAPDH as a housekeeping gene (assay ID: Hs99999905_m1) to normalize expression levels of the target genes. mRNA samples with RNA Integrity Number (RIN) < 6 were excluded from the analysis.

Frontal cortex ACE2 and TMPRSS2 immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

ACE2 and TMPRSS2 immunoreactivity was performed using paraformaldehyde fixed frontal cortex sections from 3 nDS, 3 dDS, 3 AD and 2 controls cases (Supplementary Table 2) visualized by avidin-biotin and immunofluorescence. We tested ACE2 antibodies from Abcam (ab15348, see above) and Proteintech (Cat. No. 66699, mouse monoclonal). Only the Proteintech ACE2 antibody revealed positive cellular and vascular immunoreactivity. We next tested three TMPRSS2 antibodies (Invitrogen: Cat. No. PA5-14264 and PA5-96019; Proteintech: Cat. No. 14437) but none revealed TMPRSS2 immunoreactivity. Therefore, the Proteintech ACE2 antibody combined with avidin-biotin based immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence was used to visualize labeling in the frontal cortex.

Immunohistochemistry: Briefly, after deparaffinization, one 8 micron section per case was pretreated with a solution of citric acid (pH 6) for 10 minutes in a microwave and incubated with the Proteintech monoclonal mouse antibody against ACE2 (66699) at an optimal dilution of 1:500 in a Tris-buffered saline (TBS) Triton X-100/1% goat serum (GS) solution overnight at room temperature (RT). After three 1% GS TBS washes, sections were incubated with a secondary goat biotinylated anti-mouse antibody (1:200; 1 hr) (Vector Labs) followed by a 1 hour incubation using a Vectastain ABC kit. Subsequently, sections were developed in an acetate-imidazole buffer solution containing 0.05% 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) (ThermoFisher Scientific) and 0.005% H2O2. Slides were dehydrated in an ascending series of ethanol concentrations (50%, 70%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene and cover-slipped using DPX (Electron Microscopy Sciences). To control batch-to-batch variation in chemicals, sections from each case were processed simultaneously. Controls consisted of the omission of primary antibody resulting in an absence of immunoreactivity.

Immunofluorescence: Antibodies against the ACE2 receptor, glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and endothelial protein CD31 were used to visualize the relationship between astrocytes and microvasculature, respectively. One section per case was incubated overnight at RT with the Proteintech monoclonal ACE2 mouse (1:100) and polyclonal GFAP rabbit (1:500, Dako Cat. No. Z0334) or ACE2 mouse and polyclonal CD31 rabbit (1:50, Abcam Cat. No. 289364) after antigen retrieval consisting of a solution of citric acid (pH 6) for 10 min in a microwave. Secondary Cy5-conjugated donkey anti-mouse IgG and Cy2-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc) were applied together for 1 h at RT. Sections were counter stained with the nuclear fluorescence marker DAPI (1:2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at RT for 10 min. Auto-fluorescence was blocked with Auto-fluorescence Eliminator Reagent (Millipore) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were cover-slipped with an aqueous mounting media (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Immunofluorescence was visualized with the aid of a Revolve Fluorescent microscope (Echo laboratories, San Diego, CA) with excitation filters at wavelengths 350 nm, 395 nm and 650 nm for DAPI (emission blue), Cy2 [emission green (CD31); pseudo-color red (GFAP) and Cy5 [emission far-red; pseudo-color red (ACE2) and green (ACE2)], respectively, as described previously [43, 44].

Statistical analysis

All data was analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). To assess normality of the data, the Shapiro-Wilk and D’Agostino and Pearson tests were used to analyze the distribution of each variable. The statistical analyses performed for parametric data were the Student t-test (two-tailed), for comparisons between two groups when presenting the same standard deviation (SD) or the Welch’s correction for unequal SD or a one-way ANOVA, when comparing across groups (Brown-Forsythe and Welch ANOVA test when SDs were unequal). On the other hand, non-parametric data were compared using the Mann-Whitney test, for comparisons between two groups; and the Kruskal-Wallis test across groups. Matrix correlations were based on Pearson and Spearman tests. Results were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). P values < 0.05 were considered significant. Atypical data was removed from analysis using the ROUT method (Robust regression and Outlier removal), Q = 1%.

Results

TMPRSS2 fragments are increased in nDS, but not dDS or AD

We analyzed TMPRSS2 species in lumbar CSF from nDS and dDS and age-matched controls (see Supplementary Table 1A), using western blotting with the 14437-1-AP antibody, which targets the TMPRSS2 ectodomain, detects the 492-amino acid full-length zymogen, cleaved fragments that carry the protease domain, and fragments that include the membrane-tethered pro-domain [49]. In all CSF samples, immunoblotting revealed a ~ 55 kDa band attributed to the full-length zymogen, and a ~ 25 kDa, attributed to the active protease fragment [58] (Fig. 1A). The pro-domain fragment (expected 35 kDa), which has been reported in plasma [49] was not detected in the CSF samples.

CSF TMPRSS2 analysis in DS and AD

In CSF from non-demented DS individuals, both the full-length zymogen (85% increase, P = 0.045) and the 25 kDa peptidase fragment (215% increase, P = 0.025) were significantly increased compared to controls (Fig. 1B). By contrast, dDS showed a non-significant trend toward increased levels of the full-length and the peptidase fragment (Fig. 1B). Correlations between core biomarkers and CSF TMPRSS2 species showed a negative correlation between the protease fragment with Aβ42 levels in dDS cases (Spearman coefficient= − 0.685, P = 0.035). Correlations were not found with ID classification level (mild, moderate, severe).

We also analyzed CSF TMPRSS2 levels from individuals with AD and non-AD subjects with cognitive impairment but negative CSF biomarkers of AD (Supplementary Table 1B). In contrast to DS, AD CSF showed a non-significant trend towards decreased levels of both the protease fragment (34% decrease, P = 0.062) and zymogen (24% decrease, P = 0.065) compared to non-AD disomic controls (Fig. 2). Core CSF biomarkers and TMPRSS2 species negatively correlated, specifically with TMPRSS2 zymogen and T-tau levels in AD (Pearson coefficient = − 0.630, P = 0.003), while controls displayed a positive correlation with P-tau levels (Pearson coefficient t = 0.600, P = 0.007) and age (Pearson coefficient = 0.527, P = 0.020). There was a negative correlation between the protease fragment and T-tau levels in AD (Spearman coefficient = − 0.647, P = 0.003).

Frontal cortex TMPRSS2 analysis in DS and AD

In frontal cortex extracts, we detected the TMPRSS2 zymogen and the peptidase fragment, but also a ~ 35 kDa form attributed to the prodomain fragment [49] (Fig. 3A). Consistent with CSF findings, nDS brain extracts exhibited a significant increase in the peptidase fragment (143% increase, P = 0.041) and the zymogen (79% increase, P = 0.022) compared to controls, while the prodomain fragment remained unchanged (Fig. 3B). Notably, the peptidase fragment levels were significantly reduced in dDS compared to nDS (P = 0.020) and controls, while zymogen levels were still increased (57% increase, P = 0.003).

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed a non-significant increase on TMPRSS2 mRNA levels in nDS (98% increase, P = 0.369) and dDS (115% increase, P = 0.088) with respect to controls (Fig. 3C). It is important to consider that the limited number of DS samples (4 nDS and 8 dDS), likely effected the statistical outcome. However, TMPRSS2 expression was significantly elevated when nDS and dDS cases were pooled compared to controls (109% increase, P = 0.047).

None of the TMPRSS2 species analyzed in the frontal cortex exhibited significant alterations compared to controls across Braak tau stages (Fig. 4A, B). Frontal cortex TMPRSS2 mRNA expression was also unchanged between AD and controls (Fig. 4C).

CSF ACE2 analysis in DS and AD

CSF levels of the full-length ACE2 or the 85-kDa fragment were not significantly different in either DS group compared to age-matched controls (Fig. 5B). Although the 80-kDa fragment was significantly decreased in nDS compared to disomic controls (44% decrease, P = 0.033), a significant reduction was not found for dDS cases (24% decrease, P = 0.302). To better reflect the proteolytic processing of ACE2, we calculated the ratio between the levels of 80-kDa fragment and the 100-kDa full-length ACE2 species for each subject. This quotient confirmed a significant reduction of the cleaved fragment in relation to the unprocessed form in nDS (P = 0.008), which was not seen in dDS (P = 0.046) compared to disomic controls (Fig. 5C).

Correlations between core biomarkers and ACE2 species showed a positive relationship between the 100-kDa full-length species and T-tau levels in dDS (Pearson coefficient = 0.686, P = 0.041). For disomic controls, the 80 kDa species exhibited a strong positive correlation with Aβ42 (Pearson coefficient = 0.891, P = 0.003) and P-tau levels (Pearson coefficient = 0.835, P = 0.010), while the 80/100 kDa ratio correlated positively with age (Pearson coefficient = 0.709, P = 0.049).

In AD, only CSF levels of the 130-kDa full-length ACE2 specie was significantly reduced (21% decrease, P = 0.035) compared to non-AD controls (Fig. 6A, B) but other full-length and fragment species were unchanged. However, the ACE2 fragment/full-length ratio was significantly increased in AD (P = 0.025), opposite to that observed in DS (Fig. 6C). While DS is associated with reduced ACE2 fragment levels, we found a reduction in ACE2 full-length species in AD.

Discussion

Individuals with DS are more susceptible to SARS-CoV-2-induced infections and epidemiological evidence identified this population at a high-risk for developing severe COVID-19 outcomes and increased mortality [18, 45]. Postmortem analysis of brain tissue obtained from individuals with DS revealed a significant increase in neuroinflammation in response to the COVID-19 virus, suggesting an enhanced glial response compared to AD patients that suffered from COVID-19 [23]. Increased vulnerability in individuals with DS may, in part, be due to alterations to cellular components that enhance viral entrance into the brain, including an increase in TMPRSS2. Interestingly, we found that the TMPRSS2 mRNA expression was higher when DS subtypes were combined compared to disomic controls in agreement with a gene dosage effect due to the triplication of the TMPRSS2 gene on Hsa21. However, when nDS and dDS were considered separately statistical significance was not reached likely due to the small size of subgroups. Recently, higher TMPRSS2 mRNA expression was reported in dDS compared to disomic controls [37].

Since TMPRSS2 is synthetized as a zymogen species, defining alterations associated with an increase in susceptibility to brain infection depends on post-translational processing. In CSF and frontal cortex obtained from nDS cases, there was significant increase in both the full-length zymogen and the 25-kDa protease fragment active species compared to controls. In contrast, there was a non-significant trend toward an increase in CSF level for the full-length and protease fragment in dDS compared to nDS cases. Moreover, the protease fragment significantly decreased in the dDS frontal cortex compared to nDS, suggesting that other factors underlying the onset of dementia that attenuate TMPRSS2 in DS. Despite finding a trend toward a reduction in the active protease fragment in CSF from disomic AD subjects, decreases were not observed in frontal cortex extracts even across Braak stages, suggests that mechanisms that activate TMPRSS2 increasing vulnerability to SARS-CoV-2 infection in DS, are not linked to AD in the general population.

Taken together, the results indicate that triplication in DS leads to an increase in TMPRSS2 protein levels, potentially resulting in an increase in its catalytic activity enhancing SARS-CoV-2 susceptibility, which was not observed in disomic individuals with AD. Interestingly, the generation of the TMPRSS2 protease appears to blunted in dDS affecting viral entrance in the CNS. Perhaps n viral entry into brain during COVID-19 infection occurs via altered vasculature including a leaky blood-brain barrier [51] or that trisomy differentially affects the TMPRSS2 gene located on chromosome 21 in DS. These factors may underlie the increased susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection and a higher likelihood of experiencing severe clinical outcomes in DS and AD (reviewed in [10]). Interestingly, pharmacological inhibition of TMPRSS2 reduced viral replication in DS-derived organoids to levels observed in euploid controls [54]. Since the brain contains abundant amounts of TMPRSS2 in its inactive zymogen form, perhaps only the cleaved catalytically protease-domain fragment reflects increased vulnerability to viral entry. In sum, the present findings suggest that increased levels of the active TMPRSS2 protease constitute a potential risk factor for enhanced entrance of SARS-CoV-2 into the brain of individuals with DS.

On the other hand, ACE2 facilitates SARS-CoV-2 entry into cells, when the soluble forms bind to the viral spike protein and recombinant soluble ACE2 inhibits viral infection [41, 63]. The soluble full-length and cleaved fragments co-exist in CSF [32] and plasma [21, 49], suggesting that soluble full-length forms reflect viral tissue expression, whereas cleaved fragments arise from processing of the membrane-resident protein [33]. However, the role of ACE2 in SARS-CoV-2 vulnerability is not well defined. Elevated levels of cleaved ACE2 fragments have been interpreted as potentially protective, as soluble ACE2 may act as a decoy receptor that binds and neutralizes the virus [7]. Moreover, higher baseline ACE2 gene expression, including both a full-length, receptor encoding, ACE2 isoform, as well as a short ACE2 isoform, do not by themselves translate into a higher ACE2 receptor-mediated SARS-CoV-2 infectivity [37]. In fact, administration of soluble recombinant ACE2 was proposed as a strategy to inhibit SARS-CoV-2 dissemination, thereby preventing viral entry into host cells. Preliminary clinical evidence supports this concept, suggesting that recombinant ACE2 may reduce systemic viral spread [63].

CSF ACE2 likely represents a biomarker for assessing changes over the lifespan of DS cases. However, the co-existence in the CSF of full-length soluble ACE2 and its cleaved fragments complicates interpretation, particularly when these species are not evaluated independently. Therefore, a biochemical analysis of ACE2 species in CSF and brain appears necessary to define susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. Moreover, previous findings indicate the differential significance of ACE2 species in various clinical scenarios. First, in moderate COVID-19 patients, levels of ACE2 fragments increase at the expense of membrane-resident ACE2, and the ratio of fragment to soluble full-length ACE2 in plasma rises accordingly [21]. Second, the concentration of soluble full-length ACE2 increases in the CSF of patients with encephalitis [32], likely reflecting upregulated brain expression of ACE2 associated with neuroinflammation. This effect is even more pronounced in encephalitis associated with SARS-CoV-2 infection, where both levels of full-length ACE2 and its fragments are elevated [32]. Although an early increase in serum levels of cleaved ACE2 fragments was observed in K18-hACE2 transgenic mice that coincided with a downward trend in soluble full-length ACE2 following a lethal dose of SARS-CoV-2 [33], overtime there was an increase in both full-length ACE2 and its fragments, due to a systemic inflammatory response [32]. The distinct functional implications of ACE2 fragments and full-length species, the vulnerability to SARS-CoV-2 infection, particularly in brain, suggests that assessment should be based on the relative abundance of specific ACE2 species. Evaluating the ratio between cleaved fragments and full-length soluble ACE2 in CSF, likely will provides a more informative measure of susceptibility.

ACE2 immunoreactive astrocytes and blood vessels in the frontal cortex in both DS with and without dementia and AD compared to controls, suggests increased capacity of viral entry via a breakdown of the blood brain barrier. CSF samples from nDS exhibited a significant decrease in levels of cleaved ACE2 fragments, which was not seen in dDS. Calculation of fragment/full-length ACE2 ratio demonstrated a specific reduction in ACE2 fragment levels in DS, suggesting increased vulnerability to SARS-CoV-2 infection. By contrast, a similar alteration was not observed in frontal cortex extracts from non-demented individuals with DS. In frontal cortex extracts from dDS cases there was a decrease only for species attributable to full-length forms. Indeed, CSF 130-kDa full-length ACE2 species was significantly decreased in AD compared to non-AD controls, resulting in an increased ratio of ACE2 fragments to full-length, opposite to that seen in DS. In frontal cortex the 150-kDa full-length ACE2 species showed a significant decrease across Braak tau stages. Here we have shown a close relationship between ACE2 profiles, glia end-feet and the endothelial vascular cell maker CD31, suggesting possible alteration in the blood brain barrier in DS and AD that could influence viral entry.

Previous transcriptomic and proteomic studies reported increased ACE2 levels in different regions in the AD brain [16, 28, 36, 47, 62]. Conversely, others have reported no significant differences in ACE2 protein levels in AD brain [38, 55] or in AD CSF [48], while and others found reduced ACE2 activity in postmortem AD brain tissue compared to controls [15, 30]. Conflicting reports on ACE2 deregulation in AD may be associated with methodological variability, regional heterogeneity and comorbidities that could influence protein levels. Moreover, regarding the complex role of ACE2 in COVID-19 vulnerability, studies should separately address the analysis of membrane full-length and soluble species in AD. A recent study showed a non-significant trend to increase in the expression of the short ACE2 isoform, which is not a membrane-resident protein and does not function as a SARS-CoV-2 receptor [37]. Despite epidemiological studies showing that AD patients are more likely to suffer from COVID-19 and have a much higher risk of death than other chronic diseases (discussed in [34]), cellular models suggest that cognitively healthy individuals and subjects with AD do not differ in biological susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 [53].

There are limitations to the current study, particularly related to sample size, which may affect analysis. For example, the size of the brain tissue cohorts of nDS is small, as well as difference in the mean age of nDS and dDS cases and controls, issues related with the fact that in people with DS AD-like dementia is highly prevalent. The lack of longitudinal data limits our ability to confirm possible causal mechanisms related with differential affectation of dDS individuals. Moreover, the CSF and brain samples are not from the same subjects, which may underlie the differences found between the CSF and brain extracts. Despite biological diagnosis based on core AD biomarkers, diagnostic uncertainty and existence of comorbidities are also potential confounding factors. Finally, we acknowledge that it can be challenging to assign of specific protein species to an immunoreactive band in western blot analyses, particularly when evaluating blots from brain tissue. However, the accuracy of analysis of the bands seen in the western blots presented here is supported by robust extensive experimental design, evaluation and extensive previous methodological experience in the biochemical characterization of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 species across biological fluids and experimental models [21, 32, 33, 49]. Further investigations, consisting of clinically and neuropathologically well-characterized DS and age-matched controls are warranted. Due to these caveats, the present finding should be considered conservatively and replicated using a larger cohort of cases.

In summary, the present study provides novel evidence that ACE2 and TMPRSS2, which mediate SARS-CoV-2 cellular entry, are differentially altered in DS with and without dementia and AD highlight distinct roles in modulating viral disease susceptibility. Notably, only individuals with DS exhibited molecular changes consistent with increased vulnerability to SARS-CoV-2 infection in brain. This unique molecular signature is characterized by elevated levels of active TMPRSS2 species and a compromised generation of soluble ACE2 fragments, which may otherwise act as decoy receptors to neutralize the virus before its interaction with membrane resident ACE2. Importantly, these alterations appear to be specific to DS and are not associated with an increased predisposition to develop AD. Interestingly, SARS-CoV-2 nucleoprotein was detected in neurons and microglia in the frontal cortex and hippocampus of individuals with DS with confirmed viral infection, accompanied by marked glial pathology, compared to a lesser extent in COVID-19 patients with AD or non-DS controls [23].

Multiple studies have emphasized the importance of comprehensive healthcare for older individuals with DS, particularly those with multiple comorbidities, including dementia [26]. The present findings also highlight the need for monitoring and targeted interventions in individuals with DS, including young cases infected with the SARS-CoV-2 virus. This population may represent a particularly vulnerable group in which novel therapeutic strategies, aimed at blocking viral entry, should be explored. For example, treatment approaches could include enhancing soluble ACE2 levels to act as decoy receptors [13, 60] or inhibiting TMPRSS2 activity to prevent spike protein priming [8, 52]. In fact, a clinically approved TMPRSS2 inhibitor has been shown to block viral entry in vitro, suggesting a promising therapeutic option that could be evaluated in younger individuals with DS during SARS-CoV-2 infection [27]. Moreover, the current study underscores the complexity of ACE2 regulation in AD and DS highlighting the need to consider individual ACE2 species, full-length and fragments, as well soluble splicing isoforms, when evaluating its role in COVID-19 infectivity.

Although our data does not support a direct association between ACE2 or TMPRSS2 alterations and increased brain infectivity in AD, it is well established that individuals with AD display increased morbidity and mortality following COVID-19 infection [10], probably due to other biological factors. Interestingly, amyloid precursor protein (APP), the source of Aβ in the brain, interacts with the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, potentially facilitating viral entry [11]. Furthermore, epidemiological evidence suggests that the APOE-ε4 allele, the strongest genetic risk factor for sporadic AD, is also associated with increased severity and mortality from COVID-19 [31]. A greater research effort is essential to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying COVID-19 susceptibility in DS and other neurodegenerative diseases, which may ultimately inform targeted therapeutic interventions and improve clinical outcomes in these vulnerable populations.
