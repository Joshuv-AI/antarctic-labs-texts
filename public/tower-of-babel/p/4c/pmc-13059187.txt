The development of national learning collaborative for ePRO enabled RSM in oncology: insights for co-implementation
Authors: Nicole Lynn Henderson, Tanvi V. Padalkar, Jennifer Jansen, Philip Carr, Angela M. Stover, Courtney P. Williams, Stephanie B. Wheeler, Meghan C. O’Leary, Katie Boyke, Stephanie Crist, Brian Bourbeau, Stephen Grubbs, Ethan Basch, Gabrielle B. Rocque
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLearning collaboratives are a widely used implementation strategy for supporting the spread of complex innovations, but little is known about how learning collaboratives develop and sustain over time. The OncoPRO initiative, a PCORI-funded national learning collaborative focused on implementing remote symptom monitoring (RSM) using electronic patient-reported outcomes (ePROs) in oncology, provides a unique opportunity to explore this process. By examining how OncoPRO fosters collaboration, shares strategies, and adapts to diverse sites, this study offers critical insights into both the development of learning collaboratives and their ability to support the long-term success of complex healthcare initiatives.MethodsThis study employed a multi-methods implementation science approach to examine the development and first year of the OncoPRO initiative. From conception through year 1 (March 2023-December 2024), OncoPRO provided support to 12 independent health systems. We identified cross-organizational barriers encountered during the development of a national learning collaborative, and the implementation strategies employed to address them, using field notes generated during all OncoPRO-related meetings, site-level communications, and site presentations during meetings. We systematically identified and categorized barriers and implementation strategies using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) 2.0 and the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) frameworks. Strategies were then categorized into domains based on their alignment with each other and learning collaborative implementation components or processes.ResultsWe identified 29 overarching barriers (e.g., lack of best practices; clinician buy-in) that were addressed through 37 foundational implementation strategies relevant to developing and facilitating the learning collaborative. These implementation strategies were organized into six domains: building a multi-level foundation, engaging and onboarding implementation sites, building shared learning structures, supporting technical rollout, embedding feedback loops and quality monitoring, and stimulating demand for RSM and collaborative participation. Most barriers were addressed using multiple strategies, and individual strategies often targeted several barriers simultaneously. Broad strategies addressing multiple barriers (e.g. build a coalition; identify early adopters) were deployed early to develop a base for the collaborative. As the initiative matured, strategies targeting specific barriers (e.g. develop and implement quality monitoring systems) were added to support site-level operationalization and continuous improvement.ConclusionThis study describes our approach to building a national learning collaborative for ePRO-enabled RSM implementation in oncology, focused on the initial phase of implementation. It offers a case study and potential roadmap for others involved in the initial development of large-scale collaboratives for complex interventions. This descriptive process analysis lays the groundwork for future analyses of implementation variation and strategy effectiveness across participating health systems, and highlights how learning collaboratives can support the implementation of complex quality initiatives like RSM in oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-026-00889-z.

Background

Learning collaboratives are a widely used implementation strategy for supporting the spread of complex innovations, particularly in systems where variability, coordination challenges, and multi-level change are common [1–4]. Originating from the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s Breakthrough Series model [3], these collaboratives have evolved into structured communities of practice that facilitates shared learning, peer exchange, expert coaching, and collective problem-solving across organizational boundaries [5–8]. Through iterative, goal-directed collaboration, teams adapt evidence-based innovations to their local contexts, overcome implementation barriers, and build sustainable capacity for improvement. Increasingly, such collaboratives reflect a broader shift toward embedded, participatory implementation approaches such as co-implementation, networked improvement communities, and multi-level collaboratives that emphasize concurrent development and real-time adaptation of interventions in partnership with end users [9, 10]. These models have been applied to a range of initiatives, including chronic disease management, behavioral health integration, patient safety, and care delivery transformation, often embedded within broader quality improvement or policy-driven reform efforts​​ [11–17].

Despite its promise, remote symptom monitoring (RSM) for patients on active cancer care (e.g., chemotherapy) exemplifies how even well-evidenced innovations can face persistent challenges in translation to routine care [18–22]. RSM programs use electronic patient-reported outcomes (ePROs) to systematically track symptoms and trigger timely care management responses. Patient survey responses are transmitted directly to the patient’s care team, allowing for proactive symptom management that can prevent avoidable emergency department visits, hospitalizations, or treatment interruptions, while improving quality and even length of life [23–25]. Still, real-world implementation has revealed substantial barriers to broad adoption [18, 21, 22, 26–34]. Many sites struggle with inconsistent patient engagement, especially among individuals with limited digital access or lower health literacy. Additionally, variability in workflows, staffing, and local priorities can result in inconsistent use of RSM across settings. These barriers directly undermine the intended patient benefit and may exacerbate disparities if not addressed.

Thus, a learning collaborative model is well suited to RSM implementation because it provides a structured mechanism for sites to share strategies for improving patient engagement, adapting workflows, and aligning RSM use with clinical goals, ultimately supporting more consistent, equitable outcomes. In response to this need, the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) funded the development of OncoPRO, a national learning collaborative designed to support the implementation of RSM across oncology practice settings (DI-2023C1-31,283). OncoPRO brings together academic and community oncology sites, electronic health record (EHR) vendors, ePRO software companies, and national organizations such as the American Society for Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the American Cancer Society (ACS). Oncology practices participating in OncoPRO receive supportive materials and a shared learning environment for sustainably implementing RSM in cancer care.

Despite the widespread use of learning collaboratives to support implementation in complex healthcare settings, the foundational activities required to design, launch, and sustain these collaboratives remain under-described. Existing literature tends to focus on later-stage outcomes or broad lessons learned, offering limited insight into the early-stage decisions, strategy selection, and real-time adaptations that shape collaborative function. This manuscript addresses these gaps by documenting the development and first-year execution of OncoPRO, a national initiative to support implementation of remote symptom monitoring in oncology. By detailing the infrastructure, stakeholder coordination, and adaptive processes involved, this work contributes to a more nuanced understanding of initial design of collaborative implementation in complex, multi-level systems.

Methods

Study design

This study employs a multi-method implementation science approach to examine the development and first year (March 2023-December 2024) execution of a national learning collaborative for the implementation of ePRO-enabled RSM in oncology. We prospectively documented the processes through which the collaborative was constructed, coordinated, and adapted to support implementation of RSM across systems. A key focus of the study was the identification of cross-organizational barriers encountered during collaborative development and the implementation strategies employed to address them. This approach enabled us to analyze how barriers and strategies evolved over time, how they were shaped by multi-level actors, and how they informed the structure and function of the learning collaborative.

Learning collaborative participants and structure

While the evolution of the collaborative is detailed in results, the participants and structure outlined here reflects the configuration in place at the end of OncoPRO’s first year, providing context for the implementation activities described throughout (Fig. 1). Grant preparation began in March 2023, with the notice of award received in November 2023 and the project officially launching in March 2024. The OncoPRO core team consists of two medical oncologists (GR; EB), two project manager (JJ), and a project administrator (PC). An implementation scientist (AS), medical anthropologists (NH; TP), and health services researchers (SW; CW; MO) support the data collection and analysis associated with the project. Partners and coaches from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) (KB; SC; BB; SG) facilitate and moderate learning collaborative meetings and maintain participant access to shared learning materials.Fig. 1Implementation strategies utilized to target overarching barriers and build the OncoPRO Learning Collaborative in Year 1

Given the digital infrastructure of delivering RSM, EHR and ePRO vendors participated in monthly meetings alongside the oncology practices that used their respective platforms. Cerner-based practices used Carevive for ePRO collection, iKnowMed and Flatiron practices used Navigating Cancer, and Epic practices leveraged the built-in ePRO module, eSyM, or developed their own within Epic. The OncoPRO initiative remains agnostic to the specific technology vendors used; participating practices independently selected their EHR and ePRO platforms prior to and apart from their involvement in the learning collaborative. Further, practices utilizing any technology platforms to conduct ePRO-enabled RSM are also welcome to join, and OncoPRO would coordinate integration with those vendors as part of the collaborative structure. Domain I of the initiative included 12 oncology health systems across the country (Table 1). This included four experienced practices who had already implemented ePRO-enabled RSM at scale (University of Alabama at Birmingham [UAB], University of South Alabama [USA], Dana Farber Cancer Institute [DF], and Texas Oncology [TO]. Eight additional practices were initiating implementation for the first time: Park Nicollet, University of Colorado-Anschutz, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, Infirmary Cancer Care, Mitchell Cancer Institute, Indiana University, Blue Ridge Cancer Care, and Compass Oncology. Quarterly all-affinity meetings brought together all participating sites to share cross-platform strategies and lessons learned. The advisory board, which met twice yearly, included national experts, patients, and advocates who provided high-level guidance and feedback on implementation progress. Data collection and source documentation

The team used multiple data sources to document the development and execution of the OncoPRO initiative learning collaborative, with field notes serving as the primary source of information. Field notes were generated during weekly core team meetings, monthly affinity group meetings, quarterly all-partner meetings, and participating practice-level communications and presentations. To enhance the completeness and accuracy of field note documentation, all affinity group sessions and meetings onboardings practices to OncoPRO were recorded and transcribed to support detailed review by the research team (NH, TP, GR). Field notes, along with additional materials such as notices/memorandums delivered to sites via email, site visit observations, and PowerPoint presentations shared by sites, were imported into Dedoose for centralized organization. All materials were categorized by meeting type, date, and key participants to enable systematic identification of barriers and strategies across the collaborative. This study was approved by a central IRB housed at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC-# 467,503).

Data analysis

In conceptualizing the OncoPRO learning collaborative, a research plan was developed to systematically identify and iteratively analyze barriers and strategies involved throughout the duration of the project. This plan involved systematically identifying and categorizing barriers and implementation strategies using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) 2.0 [35] and the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [36] frameworks. Barriers were classified by CFIR domain and subdomain, with accompanying detail on their manifestation during collaborative development. Strategies were coded using ERIC, with structured documentation of the strategy category, name, description, primary actor, target, start date, and links to affiliated barriers. Published codebooks were used to operationalize definitions for both CFIR 2.0 and ERIC, and research team discussion settled disagreements [35, 36].

In the learning collaborative meetings, academic and community oncology sites, EHR vendors, ePRO software companies, ASCO and the core OncoPRO initiative team were given opportunities to openly discuss barriers or shortcomings each party faced, and strategies they deployed. In some cases, barriers were discussed prior to strategies being mentioned, which provided a sense of directionality for the barrier and strategy relationship. In others, particularly for more general and high-level barriers such as leadership and clinical team buy in that play a factor in most many multi-level health implementations, plans were devised and initiated from the beginning to address these barriers by the OncoPRO team or barrier-strategy connections were intuited post-hoc.

A medical anthropologist (NH) and master’s level anthropologist (TP) reviewed the field notes and then prospectively identified, synthesized and coded the barriers and strategies using constant comparison [37–39] and the framework method [40] to organize and condense the data. After barrier and strategy identification, the 2 coders then documented the barriers and strategies to CFIR 2.0 and ERIC, respectively. Thereafter, the research team consisting of the coders (NH, TP), two medical oncologists (GR, EB) and OncoPRO project managers (JJ, PC) iteratively reviewed the coded barriers and strategies at weekly meetings to conduct consensus discussion and to ensure accurate capture of barriers and strategies along with appropriate mapping to CFIR and ERIC constructs. The team then categorized the strategies into domains by grouping based on alignment with each other and a specific component or process of implementing the learning collaborative. Given the structure of the data and the emphasis on iterative, consensus-based coding, interrater reliability was not calculated, as our approach prioritized shared interpretation through team discussion over independent coder agreement. This process directly informed the structure and content of Fig. 1 in Canva, which presents the categorized strategies and their alignment to collaborative development domains.

This approach enabled us to trace how specific challenges influenced the structure and operations of the collaborative, and how implementation strategies were applied to support collaboration, engagement, and adoption across diverse oncology practices. The present manuscript focuses on barriers encountered and strategies employed during the development and first year of the collaborative, offering a detailed case study and insights into how to build and operationalize a national learning collaborative around a shared clinical innovation. Future work will examine site-level adaptations, idiosyncratic barriers, and the evolution of strategy use over time.

Results

Across the first year of the OncoPRO Initiative, we identified 29 overarching barriers (Table 2) that were addressed through 37 foundational implementation strategies (Table 3) as relevant to developing and facilitating the learning collaborative from March 2023 through December 2024 (Fig. 1). These strategies were grouped into six domains that reflect key areas of collaborative development and supports: building a multi-level foundation, stimulating demand for RSM, onboarding systems, building shared learning structures, supporting technical rollout, and quality monitoring. This progression illustrates how implementation was structured over time, beginning with foundational design and culminating in broader execution and external engagement. Domain I: building a multi-level foundation

The OncoPRO learning collaborative was launched using a suite of foundational strategies designed to initiate structure, build engagement, and coordinate multisector participation (Supp. Figure 1). First, Creating a Learning Collaborative provided the structural backbone for cross-site collaboration. This process was launched through a kickoff meeting with the full OncoPRO team, including investigators from the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB: GR, NH) and the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC: EB, JJ, PC), to align on shared goals, roles, and expectations for collaborative participation. To enhance expertise in implementation science, the team Used an Implementation Advisor(AS) to guide strategic development based on her national leadership in ePRO implementation [22, 41–43].

Next, the team Built a Coalition by recruiting and leveraging resources from national organizations critical to the success of RSM implementation, including ASCO, the Proteus Consortium, EHR vendors (Epic, Cerner, iKnowMed, Flatiron), and ePRO software companies (Epic eSyM, Carevive, Navigating Cancer) (Fig. 2). This engagement broadened the collaborative’s reach and fostered early alignment on implementation needs. To support sustained participation, the team Funded and Contracted for Clinical Innovation by issuing sub-contracts to key partners, ensuring dedicated time and resources to the initiative. Using Advisory Boards and Workgroups formalized governance and feedback loops, with thrice-yearly meetings involving OncoPRO members, ASCO, patient advocates, and national leaders. Additional strategies included Developing Academic Partnerships with researchers and methodologists, Accessing New Funding to support OncoPRO’s mission through grant submissions, and Changing Accreditation or Membership Requirements by aligning with evolving accreditation, data governance, and site-level implementation needs.Fig. 2Year 1 Structure of the OncoPRO Learning Collaborative

Year 1 Structure of the OncoPRO Learning Collaborative

Together, these strategies addressed foundational barriers related to the innovation itself, the outer setting, and the inner setting, including limited structure, expertise, incentives, clinical alignment, and analytic readiness. Importantly, many strategies were longitudinal, iterative, and adapted in response to emerging needs, reinforcing a strong and coordinated foundation for ongoing collaborative activity.

Domain II: stimulating demand for RSM

A key focus of early OncoPRO implementation was expanding awareness and buy-in among oncology stakeholders to foster adoption of remote symptom monitoring (RSM) (Supp. Figure 2). This domain included multiple Increase Demand strategies aimed at reducing skepticism, demonstrating value, and building momentum for collaborative participation. The OncoPRO team conducted direct outreach and engagement (1) with potential implementation sites through personalized emails, one-on-one conversations, and virtual information sessions. These interactions helped address early concerns about burden and clarified the collaborative’s structure, goals, and available support. Participation was further promoted by leveraging participating practice networks (2) and practices (3). Established relationships with national organizations such as US Oncology and OneOncology were used to encourage participation among affiliated sites, building trust and demonstrating alignment with ongoing innovation efforts. The team also disseminated RSM impact data through presentations at academic conferences (4). These events increased visibility, highlighted clinical and implementation benefits, and positioned OncoPRO as a leading collaborative in cancer care innovation. Engagement with CMS-participating practices in the Enhancing Oncology Model (EOM) created a strong policy alignment (5). OncoPRO offered these sites practical tools to meet EOM goals related to symptom monitoring, patient engagement, and quality improvement. Finally, the team leveraged coexisting and complementary research networks with shared goals to support the implementation of RSM across additional health systems (6). Together, these strategies addressed persistent challenges regarding identifying and onboarding new sites and potential lack of broader oncology community buy-in.

Phase III: onboarding systems

The OncoPRO team focused on onboarding clinical sites to support RSM adoption across diverse practice settings (Supp. Figure 3). This domain centered on a second set of five strategies aimed at fostering early implementation momentum, clarifying site-level roles, and ensuring institutional commitment. To begin, the team used three intertwined strategies, Identify Early Adopters, Assess for Readiness and Identify Barriers and Facilitators, and Develop Academic Partnerships, to ensure participating sites had the motivation and capacity to launch RSM workflows, while aligning with the broader goals of the learning collaborative. Participating sites differed in their prior experience with ePRO-enabled RSM. Some, such as UAB, Mitchell Cancer Institute (MCI) at the University of South Alabama, Texas Oncology, and Dana-Farber, had already integrated RSM into routine care. Others, like the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and Indiana University, had conducted smaller pilots or trials. Sites such as Park Nicollet, the University of Colorado-Anschutz, Blue Ridge Cancer Care, Compass Oncology, and Mary Bird Perkins Cancer Center had no prior experience with RSM but demonstrated sufficient infrastructure, staffing, and institutional support to implement at scale. Still, some sites expressed interest in OncoPRO but were encouraged to build these capacities for future engagement.

For sites selected to join, the next domain focused on establishing local leadership and accountability through the strategies Identify and Prepare Champions and Obtain Formal Commitment. Each participating site was asked to identify key champions across clinical (i.e. physician, nurse, pharmacist), IT, and value-based care (VBC) roles who would serve as internal leaders for RSM implementation. These individuals were responsible for coordinating efforts across departments, aligning workflows, and maintaining communication with the OncoPRO team. In tandem, sites were required to formalize their participation through signing data use agreements (DUAs) or providing other documentation that outlined responsibilities related to data sharing, collaborative engagement, and adherence to RSM implementation goals. These agreements solidified organizational buy-in and promoted accountability, while also addressing practical considerations such as legal compliance, data governance, and long-term commitment to the initiative.

Together, these strategies addressed critical barriers related to the innovation itself, the outer setting, the inner setting, and the individuals involved. These included data privacy and security concerns, limited support and structure for RSM implementation, lack of best practices, general technological issues, lack of leadership buy-in, variation in site readiness, resistance to change, role confusion, lack of clinician buy-in, limited awareness of site clinical norms and workflows, need for analytic support, challenges in identifying and onboarding new sites, and inconsistent engagement by sites.

Domain IV: building shared learning structures

To support collaboration across a growing and diverse network of implementation sites, the OncoPRO team developed a structured approach to shared learning and peer exchange (Supp. Figure 4). This domain of collaborative development was anchored by 8 strategies: Promote Network Weaving, Facilitation, Develop Educational Materials, Centralize Technical Assistance, Capture and Share Local Knowledge, and three instances of Distribute Educational Materials. Together, these strategies formed the backbone of the collaborative’s learning infrastructure, supporting consistent dialogue, inter and intra-site troubleshooting, and implementation refinement.

Promote Network Weaving was operationalized by the OncoPRO team through the creation of affinity groups, initially organized based on the EHR and ePRO software systems used by the practices, with the exception of one site who was the sole Domain I site utilizing Flatiron, so they were combined with the iKnowMed group who also utilized Navigating Cancer. These small-group communities met monthly to facilitate peer learning across sites with similar vendor systems and were designed to evolve over time to reflect emerging implementation themes and shared challenges. Additionally, all affinity groups met quarterly to share cross-group insights and maintain alignment across the broader collaborative. This structure addressed the need for a community of practices and helped mitigate wide variation in site readiness, infrastructure, and system compatibility. Facilitation occurred within these affinity groups through structured guidance and meeting moderation by ASCO coaches. OncoPRO leadership and technical partners (e.g. EHR and ePRO vendors) regularly participated in the sessions, providing expertise, responding to site-level concerns, and supporting group problem-solving. This facilitation reinforced guidance for sites needing more intensive support and allowed experienced sites to help guide peers.

Capture and Share Local Knowledge was implemented through structured health system-led presentations during affinity group meetings. Each month, a participating system was assigned to present in-depth on a predefined topic, such as alert management, documentation workflows, team roles, physician engagement, audit and feedback reporting, or sustainability strategies. Representatives from presenting sites responded to structured discussion guides that prompted detailed operational insights, including decisions on alert routing, documentation flow, and team coordination. These sessions served as a core mechanism for surfacing implementation variation, troubleshooting persistent barriers, and generating peer-driven solutions. Importantly, they emphasized sustainability by asking sites to describe how key processes had been maintained or adapted over time.

To reinforce shared learning, the team and partners Developed Educational Materials that included one-pagers, how-to guides, and implementation process overviews tailored to different vendor platforms. The American Cancer Society (ACS) also produced and shared a patient-facing educational video regarding RSM and CMS linked interested health systems and patients to RSM on their website. ASCO then launched MyConnection, a digital platform for Centralized Technical Assistance that was designed to host implementation tools, technical documentation, and collaborative materials. This platform addressed the need for a standardized information hub and gave site teams on-demand access to onboarding tools, process documentation, and peer-developed resources.

Together, these strategies operationalized the collaborative’s learning infrastructure and directly addressed barriers related to the limited support and structure for RSM implementation, the need for expert guidance, the lack of best practices, the lack of standardized resources, the lack of a centralized information source, the then for a community of practice, the lack of buy-in from the broader oncological community, and variation in readiness, clinical priorities and EHR/ePRO system use across sites.

Domain V: supporting technical rollout

With foundational structures and shared learning mechanisms in place, the OncoPRO collaborative shifted toward broader implementation of RSM workflows across participating practices (Supp. Figure 5). This domain involved five strategies aimed at supporting local rollout efforts, bolstering technical capacity, and ensuring access to assistance resources for sites at varying stages of implementation.

The domain began with Conducting Local Consensus Discussions to align site stakeholders on standardized data collection and sharing practices. These discussions addressed concerns about data privacy, integration complexity, and analytic readiness, while promoting shared workflows across participating systems. Sites then proceeded to Stage Implementation Scale-up, beginning with select clinics or patient populations to refine workflows before broader rollout. This allowed each practice to refine workflows, build internal confidence, and demonstrate early success prior to broader expansion.

To ensure that site teams had access to robust technical assistance, the OncoPRO initiative incorporated 2 complementary support mechanisms under Centralize Technical Assistance. First, many practices relied on local IT champions and on-site EHR support teams to manage integration, adapt system configurations, and troubleshoot issues during implementation. These internal resources served as essential, real-time problem solvers and helped ensure alignment between RSM workflows and existing clinical systems. Second, sites maintained connections with their ePRO vendors for guidance on configuring survey tools, customizing alerts, and aligning dashboards with site-specific needs. The OncoPRO team supported these vendor relationships to ensure that site feedback was heard and translated into technical solutions.

Finally, to help sites navigate financial barriers and sustain implementation, the team Made Billing Easier by sharing guidance on RSM-related billing codes and reimbursement strategies. These discussions helped practices identify feasible pathways for financial sustainability. Together, these three strategies addressed outer setting, inner setting, and implementation process barriers, including difficulty aligning goals, limited ability to bill for RSM services, data privacy and security concerns, inconsistent engagement by sites, a need for analytic support, a lack of clinician buy in, variation in clinical priorities and workflows across sites, general technical issues, IT integration challenges, and a need for audit and feedback mechanisms.

Domain VI: quality monitoring

As sites advanced into RSM implementation, the OncoPRO team employed a set of strategies to support continuous improvement, surface emerging priorities, monitor implementation progress and bolster sustainability (Supp. Figure 6). This domain emphasized the importance of iterative reflection and feedback loops across both centralized and site-level efforts.

The strategy Purposefully Reexamine the Implementation was operationalized through weekly project team meetings to evaluate collaborative functioning and assess progress across the network. These sessions enabled ongoing reassessment of strategy deployment, identification of cross-cutting challenges, and rapid-cycle adjustments to collaborative activities. By facilitating adaptive problem-solving, these meetings helped address foundational barriers such as limited implementation structure and the absence of established best practices.

To expand input across participating sites, the team Conducted Local Consensus discussions by polling members across affinity groups (AGs) to identify priority topics for collaborative discussion. This participatory approach ensured that group agendas reflected real-time implementation needs and encouraged deeper cross-site engagement.

To support tracking at the site level, ASCO led the strategy Develop and Implement Tools for Quality Monitoring through the creation and deployment of the Smartsheet, a structured tracking tool for onboarding status, training completion, and implementation milestones. This tool allowed the team to monitor variation in site progress, identify bottlenecks, and provide targeted support. It also reinforced standardization in data collection for reporting needs (e.g., PCORI) and strengthened the collaborative’s analytic infrastructure.

The team also Obtained and Used Feedback through semi-structured interviews with patients, caregivers, and providers. These interviews offered qualitative insights into user experience, barriers to engagement, and perceived value of RSM integration. To further support reflection and institutional learning, the team Changed Record Systems by using Zoom recordings and AI-enabled tools to capture, transcribe, and analyze collaborative meetings. This strategy facilitated documentation, ensured continuity, and enabled iterative review of collaborative evolution.

Together, these strategies embedded robust mechanisms for responsive monitoring and shared decision-making. By creating formal pathways to capture feedback, track progress, and evolve over time, the collaborative was able to maintain alignment, sustain engagement, and remain attentive to site-level needs. Specifically, these implementation strategies addressed the complexity of the intervention, the need for standardized reporting metrics, the need for analytic support, variation in clinical priorities across sites, a need for audit and feedback mechanisms, and a lack of site training tracking.

Discussion

The development and first-year execution of the OncoPRO Initiative offers critical insights into how national learning collaboratives can be constructed and adapted to support implementation of complex care innovations in real-world settings. While existing literature frequently emphasizes collaborative outcomes or high-level lessons learned [3, 4, 44–46], this study adds to a growing body of work calling for more detailed accounts of the formative and adaptive processes that shape collaborative structure and function over time [2, 47–49]. By tracing how specific strategies deployed in response to multi-level barriers, our findings advance empirical understanding of collaborative design, particularly in systems with diverse technologies, variable readiness, and shifting policy demands. In doing so, OncoPRO exemplifies operationalization of participatory approaches, such as co-implementation [9], networked improvement communities [50], and multi-level collaboratives [51, 52] for innovations such as RSM in oncology.

While consistent with core tenets of existing models like the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s Breakthrough Series [3] and AHRQ’s learning collaborative frameworks [4], OncoPRO extends these models in several important ways. It preserves the foundation of structured peer learning, cross-site problem-solving, and iterative implementation support, but incorporates novel components tailored to the unique demands of RSM. These include intentional integration across multiple EHR and ePRO vendors, alignment with policy and accreditation mechanisms (e.g., CMS EOM [53]), and a phased implementation approach designed to accommodate wide variation in site readiness. Additionally, OncoPRO’s structure, comprising vendor-specific affinity groups, centralized resource hubs, and continuous stakeholder feedback loops, mirrors and advances more recent models emphasizing embedded, multi-level collaboration [54, 55]. These design features respond to persistent implementation challenges and description here provides empirical specificity to concepts often cited but rarely operationalized in detail. The inclusion of multiple technology vendors is particularly important in the context where practices have variability in technology partners and technology is necessary for programmatic success. As such, OncoPRO both supports established collaborative principles and provides novel insights into how those principles can be tailored for emerging innovations in complex healthcare systems.

Further, as healthcare delivery becomes increasingly shaped by digital infrastructure [56, 57], implementation efforts must evolve to account for the dynamic interplay between clinical, organizational, and vendor-driven systems [58]. OncoPRO highlights the importance of integrating technology partners into collaborative design from the outset, enabling more responsive, co-developed solutions to emerging implementation challenges. Rather than implementing a static blueprint across sites, the collaborative structure and strategy use evolved in response to emerging challenges and contextual variation [59–61]. This iterative approach highlights the value of adaptability in supporting innovation uptake, particularly in technology-enabled care settings where needs and capacities differ across systems [62–64].

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our findings are based exclusively on data from the first year of the OncoPRO initiative and do not yet capture the full evolution of the learning collaborative over time, implementation outcomes, or the longer-term sustainment of implementation efforts. Second, to maintain participant comfort and reflect the operational nature of the meeting analyzed in this study, we did not use direct quotes in our analysis, which may limit the richness of participant voice typically available in qualitative research. Third, this analysis focused only on shared, cross-site barriers and strategies that were experienced to some extent by all participating sites; we did not include site-specific or subgroup challenges that may have required additional or different strategies. We plan to publish these data in future publications. Fourth, while our approach used rigorous data collection methods and systematic coding, much of the data came from field notes and meeting materials rather than direct observation of on-the-ground implementation across sites, limiting our insight into day-to-day operational challenges. In this way, many barriers and strategies were identified concurrently, limiting our ability to determine precise temporal ordering. To mitigate this, we documented the timing of strategy initiation and used team consensus discussions to assess the likely sequence and rationale where possible.

Fifth, the generalizability of our findings may be influenced by the RSM and oncology-specific context of OncoPRO, the funding support by PCORI, and the relatively high level of buy-in from participating partners and practices. While the structure and progression of strategies we outline may be replicable in other health care settings, adaptations may be needed to align with different clinical domains, resource environments, or organizational structures. This collaborative model was implemented in systems with moderate to high levels of infrastructure and staffing support; in resource-constrained settings, adaptations may be required to reduce participation burden, such as fewer meetings, lighter-touch facilitation, use of existing networks, or simplified tools for tracking and implementation. Future research should examine how core components of the model can be retained while modifying intensity and format to align with available resources. Sixth, the analysis is restricted to that of widely experienced and employed barriers and strategies within the first year of the learning collaborative, and there needs to be future assessment of facilitators and site-specific deviations involved.

Finally, the role of infrastructure and resources available to the team implementing the learning collaborative may directly influence the process. Although we used well-established frameworks (ERIC and CFIR 2.0) to guide data collection and analysis, interpretation of barriers and strategies may still be shaped by the perspectives of the core research team and participating stakeholders.

Conclusion and future directions

As health systems continue to navigate the challenges of integrating digital health innovations into routine cancer care, the OncoPRO initiative demonstrates how a deliberately structured, participatory learning collaborative can build momentum, address variability, and facilitate sustainable implementation at scale. By offering detailed insights into the strategy selection, adaptive processes, and infrastructure underpinning the first year of OncoPRO, this work helps fill a critical gap in the implementation literature and offers a replicable model for future collaboratives targeting similarly complex, cross-sector innovations. Continued evaluation of site-level adaptations and longer-term outcomes will be essential for understanding how such collaboratives can evolve to support not just initial uptake, but enduring impact on care quality, equity, and patient outcomes in oncology and beyond.

Future work will continue to prospectively track collaborative development and strategy deployment over time, with a focus on identifying how strategies evolve in response to new implementation needs. In parallel, we will analyze site-specific barriers and strategies to examine how patterns of experience vary across practices, and how implementation strategies may need to be tailored accordingly. We are also collecting implementation outcomes to evaluate how specific barriers and strategies influence progress toward RSM adoption, workflow integration, and sustainability. Finally, we are developing site-level process maps to better understand how RSM workflows are operationalized, how roles are distributed, and how costs, labor, and resource utilization differ across contexts. This occurs through interview-based methods and will also provide insight on the value placed on certain strategies by the practices, either through cost or time metrics. These analyses will deepen our understanding of how collaborative structures drive implementation success and inform scalable models for future innovation deployment.
