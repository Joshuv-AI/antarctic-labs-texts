Functional Dissection of a Wheat NLR Protein Reveals a Minimal Active Region and Key Regulatory Sites for Immune Signalling
Authors: Xiaoxu Zhu, Shuo Jiang, Jinchen Zhao, Fangfang Peng, Ran Zhao, Xinnuo Li, Yi Luo, Zhihao Xiong, Qi Li, Filip Rolland, Zhensheng Kang, Xiaojie Wang, Yunqi Liu, Li Huang, Xiaojing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNucleotide‐binding site (NBS) leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) receptors (NLRs) are crucial for plant immunity but often come with trade‐offs in plant growth. Understanding the mechanisms of their self‐regulating activity for controlled immune responses is essential for optimising crop resistance. In this study, we investigated the activity fine‐tuning of the wheat NLR protein NLRMoro, derived from the cultivars Moro and AvS + Yr10. Overexpression in Nicotiana benthamiana and wheat demonstrated that NLRMoro, or its N‐terminal coiled‐coil (CC) domain alone, auto‐activated cell death and that plasma membrane‐localised NLRMoro‐CC conferred stripe rust resistance through induction of hypersensitive response, reactive oxygen species accumulation and Ca2+ influx. Through truncation analysis, we identified the amino acids 62–116 region within the CC domain as the minimal active region, essential and sufficient for its activity. Further investigation revealed that the adjacent central NBS domain suppresses the signalling activity of CC, while the C‐terminal LRR domain alleviates this suppression, both exclusively via intramolecular interactions. Critical sites outside the CC domain, including the P‐loop, RNBS‐A and MHD motifs and the phosphorylation site S198, were found to significantly influence NLRMoro‐induced cell death, presenting potential targets for fine‐tuning NLR activity. This study advances our understanding of CC‐NLR protein regulation and provides a theoretical foundation for optimising NLR activity to balance growth–defence trade‐offs in plants.Keywords: CC domain, cell death, LRR domain, NBS domain, NLRs, plant immunity

Introduction

Plants resist pathogens mainly through a two‐layered defence system, including PAMP‐triggered immunity (PTI) and effector‐triggered immunity (ETI). In ETI, resistance (R) proteins, most of which are nucleotide‐binding site (NBS) and leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) receptors (NLRs), monitor the cellular integrity or directly recognise pathogen effectors to activate rapid defence responses. ETI triggers the production of salicylic acid, localised cell death, known as the hypersensitive response (HR), reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and activation of defence‐related genes, thereby effectively restricting pathogen growth (Jones and Dangl 2006; Ngou et al. 2022; Islam et al. 2024).

In addition to a central NBS domain and C‐terminal LRR domain, most NLRs carry either an N‐terminal coiled‐coil (CC) domain, designated CC‐NLR (CNL) class proteins, or a toll/interleukin 1‐like receptor (TIR) domain, designated TIR‐NLR (TNL) class proteins and these domains are generally involved in the downstream signal transduction and effects (Pan, Wendel, and Fluhr 2000; Martin et al. 2003; Jones and Takemoto 2004; Wang, Han, et al. 2021). While the LRR domain is largely responsible for pathogen specificity, the NBS domain, also known as NB‐ARC, is typical for STAND (signal transduction ATPases with numerous domains) proteins which act as molecular switches in a variety of cellular activities, such as immunity, apoptosis and transcriptional regulation (Wang, Huang, et al. 2021; Danot et al. 2009).

Plant NLR‐activated‐immune responses often involve HR cell death, which greatly affects plant growth. In order to prevent uncontrolled and ectopic immune responses, NLR proteins have a sophisticated self‐regulatory mechanism involving intramolecular interactions (Shepherd et al. 2023). Binding of adenosine diphosphate (ADP) maintains the protein in an inactive state. When the protein directly or indirectly recognises its corresponding pathogen effector, a conformational change causes release of ADP and binding of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), in turn transforming the NLR into its activated state (through altered intramolecular interactions) to initiate downstream signalling and corresponding immune responses (Bonardi et al. 2012). After activation, NLR proteins often form oligomeric complexes, called resistosomes (Huang et al. 2025; Madhuprakash et al. 2024; Selvaraj et al. 2024). In the case of the CNL protein ZAR1, for example, conformational changes in the NBS domain trigger ADP release and ATP binding after recognition of the Xanthomonas campestris pv. campestris effector AvrAC. ATP binding induces structural remodelling, resulting in the formation of a pentameric ZAR1 resistosome, which exposes the first helices of the CC domains and forms a calcium‐permeable plasma membrane (PM) channel to trigger cell death activation and disease resistance (Wang, Hu, et al. 2019; Wang, Wang, et al. 2019; Bi et al. 2021). A similar phenomenon was also found in the wheat CNL stem rust resistance protein Sr35 (Förderer et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2022). Recent studies have shown that the activity of NLRs can also be regulated through post‐translational modifications, such as phosphorylation (Yan et al. 2025; Zhong, Li, et al. 2025; Zhong, Chen, et al. 2025; Nounurai et al. 2022; Guo et al. 2020). For example, the cell surface pattern recognition receptor WAKL20 has been reported to directly phosphorylate the NB‐ARC domain of the CNL protein BSR1, causing intramolecular steric hindrance that suppresses the assembly of BSR1 resistosomes (Zhong, Li, et al. 2025).

The N‐terminal CC or TIR domains of NLR proteins often play a role in transmitting immune signals downstream (Wang, Han, et al. 2021; Maekawa et al. 2011; Swiderski et al. 2009; Rairdan et al. 2008). The CC domains of many CNL proteins, like ZAR1, MLA10, Sr33, Sr50, Rp1‐D21 and Pm21, can induce cell death by themselves (Baudin et al. 2017; Maekawa et al. 2011; Casey et al. 2016; Cesari et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2020). The conserved EDVID motif in the CC domain is believed to participate in intramolecular interactions between the CC and NBS or LRR domains in the CNL protein. Analysis of the Sr35 protein structure indicated that the acidic amino acid residues in the EDVID motif make contact with five arginines (R) in the LRR domain (Förderer et al. 2022). Compared with the clear cell death‐inducing activity of the CC domain, there are relatively few studies demonstrating that the CC domain alone can cause plant disease resistance. Hu et al. (2017), for example, showed that the CC domain of the CNL protein BPH14 alone could confer planthopper resistance in rice but was unable to induce cell death in Nicotiana benthamiana.

The central NB‐ARC domain (also known as NBS) comprises three subdomains: a core nucleotide‐binding (NB) domain, followed by a four‐helix bundle (ARC1/HD1) and a winged‐helix fold (ARC2/WHD) (Albrecht and Takken 2006; Madhuprakash et al. 2024). Mutations in the NB‐ARC domain often influence R protein function, highlighting the regulatory function of this domain (Dinesh‐Kumar et al. 2000; Tao et al. 2000; Tornero et al. 2002). The NBS domain typically contains several conserved small motifs, including the P‐loop (Walker A or kinase 1a), Walker B (kinase 2), resistance nucleotide binding site (RNBS)‐A, RNBS‐D and MHD (Met, His, Asp) motifs, to name just a few (Tameling et al. 2002, 2006; Walker et al. 1982; Saraste et al. 1990; Traut 1994; Muneyuki et al. 2000; van der Biezen and Jones 1998; Meyers et al. 1999, 2003; Pan, Liu, et al. 2000). The P‐loop motif with consensus sequence GXXXXGK[T/S], where X represents any amino acid residue, contains an invariant Lys (K) residue involved in orientation of the β‐ and γ‐phosphates of the bound nucleotide (NTP). Mutation in the corresponding Lys (K207) in the tomato CNL protein I‐2 to Arg (K207R), for example, greatly reduced the ATP binding ability of this protein (Tameling et al. 2002). Moreover, mutation of the P‐loop in many NLRs leads to loss of cell death‐inducing activity (Tameling et al. 2002, 2006; Williams et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2020; Bai et al. 2012; Bendahmane et al. 2002; Dinesh‐Kumar et al. 2000; Howles et al. 2005). In contrast, introducing a mutation into the RNBS‐A motif of I‐2 greatly increased its cell death‐inducing activity due to a diminished rate of ATP hydrolysis (Tameling et al. 2006). Mutation of the MHD motif also activated NLRs like Rp1‐D21, I‐2 and Pm21 (Wang et al. 2015; Tameling et al. 2006; Gao et al. 2020). For the M rust resistance protein in flax, ATP bound to an autoactive MHD motif mutant, whereas ADP bound to the wild‐type M protein (Williams et al. 2011).

The C‐terminal LRR domain of NLR proteins is typically less conserved and responsible for recognition of the pathogen effector proteins, leading to the conformational changes that activate the defence response (Jones and Jones 1997; Wang, Huang, et al. 2021; Yu and Banfield 2025). The cryo‐electron microscopical structure of Sr35, for example, revealed that the LRR domain directly recognises the effector protein AvrSr35 (Förderer et al. 2022). Furthermore, the LRR domain may play a role in self‐inhibition and regulation of NLR protein activity. For example, the LRR domain of the tomato CNL protein Sw‐5b interacts with the CC and SD domains to inhibit the cell death‐induction and this inhibition is relieved upon recognition of the avirulence gene NSm product (De Oliveira et al. 2016; Chen et al. 2016).

Yr10 CG , identified by its role in the important wheat–Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici (Pst) interaction, produces a functional CNL protein, though it was later shown that it is not the main causal coding sequence in the Yr10 stripe rust (or yellow rust) resistance locus (Liu et al. 2014; Wu et al. 2023; Yuan et al. 2018; Ni et al. 2023; Dibley et al. 2024). In the wheat AvS + Yr10 and Moro cultivars, a highly homologous sequence was found to be a splice variant of Yr10 CG . To avoid confusion, we henceforth rename this splice variant as NLR Moro . Recent advances in NLR engineering have highlighted the great potential of engineered NLRs for improving disease resistance in crops (Marchal et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2025; Yu and Banfield 2025). In this study we performed a detailed molecular and functional analysis of the NLRMoro protein because of its typical CC‐NBS‐LRR constructions, with a primary focus on the cell death‐inducing activity of its CC domain and the regulatory mechanisms mediated by other components. This work aimed to uncover the molecular basis of NLR self‐regulation, thereby advancing our understanding of NLR receptors and facilitating their optimisation and strategic application in developing resilient high‐yielding crops.

Results

The NLRMoro CC Domain Confers Resistance Through Cell Death and ROS Accumulation

The 2541‐bp NLR Moro coding sequence, amplified from AvS + Yr10 and Moro (Figure S1), differs from Yr10 CG by two single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and a short fragment at the end of the gene (Figure S2A), thereby encoding a CC‐NLR protein that is similar to Yr10CG (Figure S2B). When transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves, NLRMoro induced cell death (confirmed by trypan blue staining) at 5 days post‐infiltration (dpi), even without pathogen inoculation, suggesting that NLRMoro is auto‐activated (Figure 1A,B; Figure S3A), similar to the Bcl‐2‐associated X protein (BAX), included as a positive control. To determine which domain of NLRMoro is essential for induction of cell death, the full‐length protein and various domains were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana. Only the full‐length protein and the CC domain induced a visible HR (Figure 1A,B; Figure S3A), accumulation of ROS confirmed by 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine [DAB] staining (Figure 1C), and severe electrolyte leakage (Figure 1D), indicating cell death. However, when the CC domain was fused with its NBS domain (CN), no visible HR cell death, ROS or electrolyte leakage was detected (Figure 1B–D; Figure S3A). The NBS and LRR domains of the protein alone or fused (NL) did not induce a HR cell death either (Figure 1B–D; Figure S3A). The presence of each protein/peptide was confirmed by observing green fluorescent protein (GFP) fluorescence (not shown) and western blotting assays (Figure S3B). Interestingly, we found that the position of the GFP tag affected the activity of both NLRMoro and its CC domain. When GFP was fused to the N‐terminus of NLRMoro or the C‐terminus of CC, their ability to trigger cell death was abolished (Figure S3A). This loss of activity is probably due to structural interference caused by the GFP tag or the masking of critical regulatory sites essential for their function. Therefore, in subsequent N. benthamiana experiments, we fused the tag to the C‐terminus of NLRMoro and the N‐terminus of CC to preserve their native activity.

To further investigate if the CC domain‐induced cell death could defend against stripe rust, two stable NLRMoro CC domain overexpression (CC‐OE) lines were generated in wheat variety Fielder. Presence of the transgene, high‐level transcription and proteins of the CC domain sequence (driven by the maize ubiquitin promoter) were confirmed by PCR, reverse transcription‐quantitative real‐time PCR (RT‐qPCR) and western blotting assays (Figure S3C,D). Leaves of both CC‐OE lines infected with Pst race CYR32 at the two‐leaf stage showed evidence of HR cell death and fewer fungal uredinia than those of Fielder wild type (WT) plants at 14 days post‐inoculation (dpi) (Figure 1E). Biomass analysis demonstrated that the abundance of Pst DNA was significantly lower in the CC‐OE plants relative to Fielder (Figure 1F). Importantly, the thousand‐grain weight loss of CC‐OE plants was significantly lower than that of WT when cultivated in the stripe rust nursery field, while the thousand‐grain weights, plant height, ear length, tiller number, seed areas, seed lengths and seed widths were similar in control (without pathogens) field (Figure 1G; Figure S4). CC‐OE plants showed no visible spontaneous HR‐like lesions or growth penalties in the absence of pathogen infection (Figure S4), indicating the potential of this gene in disease resistance breeding.

In order to dig out how the CC domain confers resistance, the transcription levels of pathogenesis‐related (PR) genes TaPR1, TaPR2 and TaPR5 were tested and found significantly higher in the CC‐OE lines than in Fielder both at 0 and at 120 h post‐infiltration (hpi) (Figure 1H). The fungal growth of Pst was microscopically observed, which showed that Pst infection areas were significantly reduced in the CC‐OE plants (Figure 1I; Figure S5A). At 48 hpi the fungal hyphal length was slightly decreased in the CC‐OE plants whereas the number of hyphal branches, haustorial mother cells and haustoria were comparable to those in Fielder (Figure S5B–E). Microscopic examination of host cell defence responses by DAB staining for H2O2 accumulation and observation of infection site necrosis by autofluorescence (Figure S5F) revealed that the CC‐OE plants accumulated more H2O2 and had more dead cells than Fielder at 48 and 120 hpi (Figure 1J,K), suggesting that the CC domain activated an ETI‐like defence, including but not limited to HR cell death. However, when CC‐OE wheat lines were inoculated with Blumeria graminis f. sp. tritici isolate E09, there was no visible change in response (Figure S5G). In summary, overexpression of the NLRMoro CC domain conferred significant stripe rust resistance, presumably through induction of genes underlying PR, ROS production and HR cell death.

The NLRMoro CC Domain Oligomerises in the PM and Promotes Ca2+ Influx

To elucidate how NLRMoro and its CC domain work to regulate cell death, the localisation of NLRMoro and its domains was firstly investigated. Proteins/peptides fused with a GFP tag were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves and then the subcellular localisation was observed using confocal microscopy. The results indicated that the CC, CN and full‐length NLRMoro proteins were mainly localised in the PM while the NBS protein, similar to free GFP, was localised throughout the cytosol, PM and nucleus (Figure S6A). What is interesting is that the green fluorescent signals of LRR and NBS‐LRR (NL) showed a dotted distribution in the N. benthamiana cells (Figure S6A). To validate the PM localisation of the CC, CN and NLRMoro proteins, we co‐expressed them with a PM marker protein, TaWPI6 fused with an mCherry tag. Considerable overlap of the GFP and mCherry signals was observed and measured, affirming the PM localisation of the CC, CN and NLRMoro proteins (Figure 2A). In addition, plasmolysed N. benthamiana cells showed localisation of the proteins on Hechtian strands (see magnification in Figure S6B), connections between PM and cell wall, further indications for the PM localisation of these tested proteins. Moreover, PM localisation of the CC domain was also observed in the CC‐GFP transgenic wheat leaves (Figure S6C) and confirmed by western blot from the N. benthamiana membrane and cytosolic fraction proteins (Figure 2B). To test whether the PM localisation is important for its cell death‐inducing ability, we constrained the CC domain in the nucleus by fusing it to a nuclear localisation signal (NLS). Expression in N. benthamiana leaves revealed that this blocked cell death‐induction (Figure 2C; Figure S6D,E). To explore the relevance of the CC domain's PM localisation, the dynamics of Ca2+ flux in N. benthamiana leaves and mesophyll cells of CC‐OE wheat leaves in response to chitin were investigated using a non‐invasive micro‐test technology (NMT). After chitin stimulation, the mesophyll cells of both CC‐overexpressing N. benthamiana leaves and CC‐OE wheats exhibited a distinct Ca2+ influx, while little change was observed in the control plants (Figure 2D,E), suggesting that the CC domain is able to affect Ca2+ influx, most likely enabled by its PM localisation. The Ca2+ influx‐inducing ability of CN and NLRMoro were also observed in N. benthamiana leaves, although CN exhibited a markedly lower intensity (Figure 2D). These findings suggest that the PM localisation of the CC domain is critical for its cell death‐inducing activity, possibly by regulation of Ca2+ influx.

Because some NLR proteins undergo structural transformation from the resting state to an active oligomer, possible self‐association of the full length NLRMoro protein and the NLRMoro CC domain were monitored by split‐luciferase complementation assays (Split‐Luc) (Figure 2F,G). Obvious luminescent signals were detected when the full‐length protein nLuc‐NLRMoro and cLuc‐NLRMoro constructs were co‐expressed, whereas there was no signal in the control (Figure 2F), indicating that the NLRMoro protein indeed self‐associates. A similar observation could be seen with the CC domain, but not the NBS or LRR domains, suggesting that the NLRMoro‐CC domain also self‐associated by itself (Figure 2G; Figure S7A). This self‐association was also verified through bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BIFC) and co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) (Figure 2H,I). Clear fluorescence signals were observed in the PM of N. benthamiana cells at 48 h after co‐infiltration with nYFP‐CC and cYFP‐CC constructs, whereas no fluorescence was detected in the control groups (Figure 2H). For Co‐IP experiments, CC domain proteins fused with a triple HA (human influenza hemagglutinin) tag were co‐expressed with CC domain proteins fused with a GFP tag or with GFP only (as negative control). All proteins were successfully expressed at 48 hpi as confirmed by western blotting (input) and using GFP‐Trap agarose beads (ChromoTek) CC‐3HA was co‐immunoprecipitated by GFP‐CC protein, but not by GFP alone, which confirmed the self‐association of the CC domain (Figure 2I).

We tested possible NLRMoro intramolecular interactions and found that the CC domain also interacts with NBS or LRR alone (Figure S6B,C). We also observed the interaction between NBS and LRR (Figure S7D). Collectively, the results suggested that the NLRMoro CC domain self‐associates in the PM and interacts with the other NLRMoro domains, which may contribute to activity regulation of NLRMoro.

The NLRMoro CC Domain EDVID Motif Is Indispensable for Cell Death Induction

Several studies (Förderer et al. 2022; Bai et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2015) showed that a conserved EDVID motif in the CC domain is involved in intramolecular interactions, but is not essential for inducing cell death. In order to investigate the role of the EDVID motif in the CC domain of NLRMoro, which contains the EDGID amino acids, we constructed three proteins with a mutation of one of the three most conserved negatively charged residues (CCE78A, CCD79A and CCD82A) along with a triple mutant (CC3A) (Figure 3A) and expressed them in N. benthamiana leaves. The CCE78A, CCD79A and CCD82A mutant proteins did not show altered cell‐death induction capacity, whereas the triple mutant CC3A protein had considerably compromised ability to induce cell death (Figure 3B). Western blotting confirmed the expression of all tested proteins, although CCD79A protein levels were lower (Figure S8A). Split‐Luc assays showed that the triple CC3A mutant protein was not affected in its ability of self‐association or interactions with the NBS or LRR domains (Figure S3B–D). Confocal microscopy observation showed that the subcellular localisation of both GFP‐CC3A and the other three mutants was similar to that of the wild‐type GFP‐CC protein, predominantly on PM, suggesting the loss of function of CC3A is not due to mislocalisation (Figure S8E). To explore how the EDVID‐3A mutation affects CC domain function, we employed AlphaFold 3 (Abramson et al. 2024) to predict the pentameric CC domain, as well as full‐length NLRMoro monomer and pentamer, which positioned the EDVID motif in a relatively conformationally stable position (Figure 3C; Figure S8F). Notably, despite preserving an overall pentameric architecture, the CC3A mutant showed pronounced local conformational rearrangements. Specifically, both the N‐ and C‐terminal α‐helices collapse inward, resulting in the loss of the characteristic funnel‐shaped cavity at the C‐terminus (Figure 3C). These structural distortions provide a plausible mechanistic explanation for the selective loss of CC‐mediated cell death, independent of CC self‐association, interactions with the NBS or LRR domains, or plasma membrane localisation.

CC62 ‐116 Is the Minimal Active Region of NLRMoro for Cell Death and Defence Response

To further explore the core region of the NLRMoro CC domain for cell death induction, we truncated the N‐terminus and C‐terminus of the domain, which indicated that the N‐terminal amino acids 1–62 and the C‐terminus amino acids 116–193 are not necessary for the domain's cell death‐inducing activity (Figure 4A–C; Figure S9A,B). We then confirmed that the remaining amino acids 62–116 of the CC domain (CC62‐116) were sufficient to induce cell death, although the activity was weakened compared to the intact CC domain (Figure 4A,D,E; Figure S9C). We also found that removing amino acid 62 (CC63‐116) or 116 (CC62‐115) further reduced the activity (with more variable responses), but that removing both amino acids (CC63‐115) restored the cell death inducing activity (to that of CC62‐116) (Figure 4A,D,E; Figure S9C). Secondary structure prediction analysis of the CC domain indicated that the two coiled coils of the CC domain are produced by the aa22‐56 and aa125‐145 sequences (highlighted by the red colour in Figure 4A), just outside the core CC62‐116 region, suggesting that the coiled coils are not essential for CC domain activity, but have a significant regulatory function.

Next, the wheat stripe rust resistance activity of the truncated NLRMoro CC62‐116 domain was further tested. We transiently overexpressed CC62‐116 in Fielder wheat through the BSMV‐induced gene overexpression system (VOX). Both RT‐qPCR and western blotting experiments showed that CC62‐116 was normally expressed in wheat (Figure S9D,E). After inoculation with Pst CYR32, we found that wheat plants overexpressing CC62‐116 (VOX:CC62‐116) showed enhanced resistance. Compared with the control VOX:00, VOX:CC62‐116 wheat leaves had fewer Pst spores and showed a HR phenotype (Figure 4F). Biomass analysis of infected leaves showed a significant reduction of Pst biomass in VOX:CC62‐116 (Figure 4G). Moreover, further RT‐qPCR analysis showed that the TaPR1, TaPR2 and TaPR5 genes were all significantly increased in VOX:CC62‐116 leaves, especially at 120 hpi (Figure 4H–J). Next, microscopy revealed increased ROS production in VOX:CC62‐116 (Figure 4K; Figure S9F), with reduced infection area and Pst hyphal length (Figure 4L,M; Figure S9G). In summary, these results indicate that CC62‐116 has a similar effect as the intact NLRMoro CC domain in inducing wheat stripe rust resistance, further indicating that it is the minimal active region of the NLRMoro protein.

The Cell Death‐Inducing Ability of the NLRMoro CC Domain Is Tightly Regulated by the NBS Domain

The identification of key signalling regions within NLRs and especially their CC domain holds great promise for resistance breeding. However, other elements, like the NBS and LRR domains, may also be critical for the precise regulation of CC‐mediated signalling, warranting further investigation into their regulatory roles. The CC domain of NLRMoro was capable of initiating ROS production and cell death in N. benthamiana leaves, but this ability was compromised when fused with the NBS domain in the CN protein (Figures 1 and 5A–D; Figure S10A). These results led to the assumption that the NBS domain could repress CC function through direct domain interaction. We used Co‐IP, glutathione S‐transferase (GST) pull‐down and yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) assays to further confirm CC and NBS interaction. In the Co‐IP experiment, using N. benthamiana leaves after 48 h co‐expression of GFP‐NBS and GFP (as control) with mCherry‐CC and immunoprecipitation with anti‐GFP antibody, western blotting showed that the mCherry‐CC protein co‐immunoprecipitated with GFP‐NBS, but not with the GFP control, demonstrating in planta interaction (Figure 5E). A GST pull‐down assay with purified GST‐tagged NBS and SUMO‐His‐tagged CC showed that the NBS proteins directly bound to CC in vitro (Figure 5F). Finally, yeast co‐transformed with pGADT7 (AD)‐CC and pGBKT7 (BD)‐NBS but not control groups grew on strongly selective SD (synthetic dropout)− Trp−His−Leu−Ade medium and the blue colour after X‐α‐Gal staining confirmed interaction (Figure 5G). Combined with the result of Figure S7B, these experiments demonstrated that the CC domain directly interacts with the NBS domain, both in vivo and in vitro. To examine whether the interaction between the NLRMoro NBS domain and the CC domain is specific, we performed an additional Co‐IP assay using the CC domain derived from a CNL protein of Aegilops tauschii , which confers resistance to multiple Pst races (Zhang et al. 2019). Interestingly, the NLRMoro NBS domain was also able to interact with this heterologous YrAS2388R CC domain (Figure S10B), suggesting that NBS‐CC interactions may not be strictly limited to cognate NLR pairs, but instead reflect a conserved regulatory mechanism among CNL proteins.

To investigate how the interaction between CC and NBS affects CC conformation and activity, we examined whether NBS influences the subcellular localisation or self‐association capacity of CC. Transient separate expression and co‐expression of mCherry‐tagged CC and GFP‐tagged NBS in N. benthamiana indicated their co‐localisation and that NBS did not influence the subcellular localisation of CC (Figure S10C). Whereas co‐expressing NBS with nLuc‐CC and cLuc‐CC revealed that NBS partially attenuated CC self‐association (Figure 5H). Moreover, when NBS was fused with CC to form the CN protein, CN exhibited no detectable self‐association in either Split‐Luc or Y2H assays (Figure 5I,J). These findings indicate that the interaction between NBS and CC markedly suppresses the ability of CC to self‐associate and subsequently form higher‐order oligomers.

Interestingly, NBS still blocked cell death induction of CC62‐116 in a transiently expressed CC62‐116‐NBS fusion protein with the protein interaction shown by Split‐Luc (Figure S10D–F), indicating that the core CC62‐116 sequence is also regulated by the NBS domain.

Regulation of the CC Domains's Cell Death Inducing Ability by NBS and LRR Involves Intramolecular Interactions

As the full‐length NLRMoro and the NLRMoro CC domain (CC) but not the CC‐NBS fusion protein (CN) are capable of inducing cell death (Figures 1A,B,D and 5A,C), we assumed that the fused NBS blocks the CC domain's cell‐death induction ability and that the LRR domain in the full length NLRMoro protein relieves this inhibition, both through intramolecular interactions (within the same molecule). To further investigate this, we co‐expressed CC with the separate NBS and LRR domains and their fusion protein (NL) in N. benthamiana and found that these could not suppress the cell death induction by CC (Figure 6A,B,D; Figure S11). In addition, LRR co‐expression with the CC‐NBS fusion protein (CN) did not restore cell death induction (Figure 6A,C,D; Figure S11), indicating that regulation of the CC by the NBS and LRR domains requires intramolecular interactions and that the position of each domain is important for regulating the cell‐death induction activity.

To further elucidate how the LRR domain influences the regulatory relationship between NBS and CC, we co‐expressed LRR or a GFP control with nLuc‐NBS and cLuc‐CC in N. benthamiana. Compared with the control, LRR partially reduced the interaction between NBS and CC (Figure 6E). Given that NBS suppresses CC activity through intramolecular interaction and that all three domains exhibit mutual interactions (Figure 5; Figure S7), these findings suggest that LRR can competitively bind to NBS, thereby relieving the NBS‐mediated inhibition of CC and releasing CC activity. This regulatory mechanism may underlie the ability of full‐length NLRMoro to exhibit autoactive characteristics in N. benthamiana.

NLRMoro ‐Induced Cell Death Is Precisely Regulated by the P‐Loop, RNBS‐A and MHD Conserved Motifs Outside of CC Domain

Several conserved motifs in NLR proteins play a regulatory role on the protein activity, especially those in the NB‐ARC domain. To study the function of the P‐loop, RNBS‐A and MHD motifs of NLRMoro, we generated mutations in these motifs and transiently expressed the mutant proteins. Substitution of the conserved Lys (K207) in the P‐loop with Arg (NLRMoro K207R) abrogated the protein's ability of cell death induction (assessed by electrolyte leakage; Figure 7A; Figure S12A,C). However, transient expression of an RNBS‐A mutant (NLRMoro S231F) and MHD mutant (NLRMoro D502V) induced a faster and stronger HR cell death (Figure 7A; Figure S12A,C). Combinations of the P‐loop and RNBS‐A mutations (NLRMoro K207R+S231F) still resulted in deficient cell death induction, whereas the P‐loop and MHD double mutant protein (NLRMoro K207R+D502V) maintained cell death inducing ability, although the effect was delayed by about 24 h (Figure 7A; Figure S12A). Given that the CC domain plays a key role in inducing cell death in NLRMoro (Figure 1A,B,D), we hypothesised that the mutations in the P‐loop, RNBS‐A and MHD motifs influence the interaction between NBS and the CC or LRR domain, causing loss or enhancement of the cell death inducing ability. To test this hypothesis, we constructed mutant NBSK207R and CNK207R fusion proteins. Neither induced cell death in N. benthamiana leaves (Figure S13A–C). However, when the NBSK207R was co‐expressed with the CC domain (in two separate molecules), there was a surprising enhancement of cell death (Figure 7B,C; Figure S13C). This indicates that the K207R substitution may cause a substantial conformational change in the NBS domain, thereby altering its mode of regulation from intramolecular suppression to intermolecular enhancement. Moreover, split‐luciferase protein interaction assays revealed that the NBSK207R P‐loop mutation did not affect binding with CC (Figure 7D) but significantly weakened interaction with LRR (Figure 7E), suggesting that interaction between NBS and LRR is required for regulating cell death in NLRMoro. Such findings further underscore the pivotal role of the P‐loop motif in modulating NLR activity and highlight its context‐dependent regulatory capacity.

S198 Phosphorylation Blocks the Cell Death Inducing Ability of NLRMoro

To identify additional key sites involved in the regulation of NLR activity, particularly those related to post‐translational modifications, a mass spectrometry search for phosphorylation sites on NLRMoro was conducted. S198, adjacent to the P‐loop motif in the NBS domain, was repeatedly identified as a phosphorylated residue. Subsequently, two mutants were constructed in order to test the function of the S198 phosphorylation site in NLRMoro activity. One was a phosphorylation‐mimic mutant NLRMoro S198D, in which S198 was replaced with aspartic acid (D) to generate a negative charge mimicking the phosphate group, and the other was NLRMoro S198A in which the Ser residue site was substituted with a non‐phosphorylated alanine. When transiently expressed in N. benthamiana leaves, the NLRMoro S198A mutant showed similar cell death inducing ability as the wild‐type protein, whereas the cell death inducing ability was lost in the NLRMoro S198D mutant protein, with a phenotype similar to the GFP control (Figure 7F; Figure S12B,D).

To investigate whether the S198 phospho‐site and/or the P‐loop motif of the NBS domain are key physical sites to prevent auto‐activation of the CC domain, three mutant CN proteins were constructed (structures were shown in Figure 13D): a S198D‐K207R double mutant protein (CNS198D‐K207R), a mutant protein combining S198 deletion with the K207R mutation (CNcut200‐K207R), and S198 + P‐loop double deletion mutant (CNcut210). When expressed in N. benthamiana, none of them could trigger cell death (Figure S13D–F), suggesting that neither S198 nor the P‐loop motif are the physical sites required to block the function of CC by NBS.

Anyway, the results highlight the importance of the S198 phosphorylation site adjacent to the P‐loop in regulating NLRMoro activity, which may represent a mechanism by which plants prevent immune overactivation.

Discussion

Different domains of multidomain CNL proteins perform distinct roles to collectively regulate plant immunity (Takken and Goverse 2012). In this study, we demonstrated that both NLRMoro and its CC domain independently induced strong cell death in N. benthamiana, whereas other individual domains or combinations, such as NBS, LRR, CC‐NBS and NBS‐LRR, did not, highlighting the CC domain as the central component in NLRMoro‐mediated cell death signalling (Figure 1B). Truncation analysis further identified the CC62‐116 region, located between the two coiled‐coils of NLRMoro‐CC, as the minimal active unit sufficient to induce cell death (Figure 4E). The CC62‐116 signalling region is proposed to interact with yet‐to‐be‐identified downstream factors, possibly plasma membrane‐associated elements or specific signalling proteins, to activate cell death or disease resistance. Moreover, wheat plants overexpressing CC or CC62‐116 exhibited partial resistance to Pst, suggesting that these regions function as key signalling modules in activating HR cell death through ROS production, thereby restricting stripe rust pathogen growth and spread (Figures 1E and 4F). Together with the resistance conferred by CC domain from other NLRs like BPH14, this result indicates that some CC domains alone are sufficient to prime or initiate effective defence signalling, which could be valuable for breeding applications (Hu et al. 2017). Meanwhile, these findings align with observations in other CNLs such as MLA10, ZAR1 and RPM1, which also harbour defined active regions (Maekawa et al. 2011; Wang, Hu, et al. 2019; Wang, Wang, et al. 2019; Collier et al. 2011), highlighting the conserved role of CC cores in NLR‐mediated immunity and their potential as modular elements for engineering disease resistance.

Interestingly, although NLRMoro exhibits strong auto‐activity in N. benthamiana (Figure 1B; Figure S3A), it does not trigger self‐activation in wheat AvS + Yr10 or Moro, suggesting that specific effectors or physiological conditions may be required to induce HR cell death in wheat. This discrepancy could also be explained by the presence of a wheat‐specific guardee or regulatory factor that is absent in N. benthamiana, thereby suppressing autoactivation under basal conditions. Consistently, only C‐terminally GFP‐tagged NLRMoro (NLRMoro‐GFP) and N‐terminally GFP‐tagged CC (GFP‐CC) induced cell death in N. benthamiana (Figure S3A), whereas CC‐GFP still conferred partial resistance in wheat (Figure 1E), which supports species‐specific regulatory differences, and further implies that the full‐length NLRMoro and the CC truncation may operate through partially distinct activation or signalling mechanisms, which are differentially sensitive to tag placement and host cellular context. This phenomenon is not unprecedented (Bai et al. 2012; Wang et al. 2015; Hu et al. 2017). Notably, CC‐OE plants showed enhanced resistance upon Pst infection but did not exhibit obvious growth penalties under non‐infected conditions (Figure 1E,F; Figure S4), indicating that CC overexpression establishes a primed immune state that remains below the threshold required to trigger overt HR or developmental defects. Upon pathogen challenge, this CC‐mediated priming may be integrated with additional immune pathways through currently unknown factors, thereby enabling a sufficiently strong defence response (Islam et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2022; Hu et al. 2017). Nevertheless, extensive genetic validation and functional testing in wheat remain essential. Further genetic validation in wheat, such as testing GFP‐CC transgenic lines (though potentially limited by spontaneous cell death), would help verify this hypothesis. Together, beyond serving as a mechanistic tool, the intrinsic signalling capacity of the CC domain highlights its potential as a modular immune execution unit, offering opportunities for engineering controllable defence responses through inducible or pathogen‐responsive designs, complementing recent NLR engineering strategies.

The EDVID motif is conserved in many CNLs, including Rx, MLA10 and ZAR1 (Rairdan et al. 2008; Maekawa et al. 2011; Lewis et al. 2010). It has been implicated in mediating interactions between the CC and NBS or LRR domains but appears dispensable for the cell death–inducing activity of isolated CC domains. For example, mutations in the EDVID motif do not impair the ability of the CC or CN domains of MLA10 to trigger cell death, although the full‐length MLA10 protein requires an intact EDVID motif for this function (Förderer et al. 2022; Bai et al. 2012). In contrast, in the case of NLRMoro, mutation of three polar residues within the EDVID motif of the isolated CC domain significantly attenuated its cell death‐inducing activity, while leaving CC self‐association, interactions with the NBS or LRR domains, and PM localisation largely unaffected (Figure 3). These observations suggest that, rather than serving solely as an intramolecular interaction motif, the EDVID region in NLRMoro‐CC contributes to cell death signalling in a context‐dependent manner, potentially by maintaining a local structural or electrostatic configuration required for efficient downstream signalling (Figure 3C). This points to an additional layer of regulatory complexity for the EDVID motif among different CNLs.

NLR proteins exhibit diverse subcellular localisations, which often reflect distinct mechanisms of immune signalling (Shepherd et al. 2023; Sun et al. 2023; Cesari et al. 2016). We found that CC, CN and NLRMoro all target the PM (Figure 2A,B), suggesting that the NLRMoro‐CC may mediate interactions with PM‐associated components or influence PM structure and signalling. This is further supported by the enhanced Ca2+ influx observed upon CC overexpression (Figure 2D,E). Notably, nuclear sequestration of CC abolished its ability to induce cell death, highlighting the essential role of PM localisation in its function (Figure 2C). In addition, NLRMoro‐CC was observed to self‐associate at the PM in N. benthamiana leaves, indicating an intrinsic propensity of CC to engage in NLRMoro oligomerisation (Figure 2F–I). However, CC self‐association alone is not evidence for resistosome formation, as stable CC‐NLR oligomerisation requires cooperative CC–NB‐ARC interactions (Madhuprakash et al. 2024; Förderer et al. 2022; Wang, Hu, et al. 2019; Wang, Wang, et al. 2019). Although neither NBS nor LRR self‐associated in our Split‐Luc assay (Figure S7A), pairwise combinations of NLRMoro domains did interact (Figure S7B–D), indicating CC‐mediated interactions may represent an initiating or permissive step, while coordinated interactions between the CC and other domains remain indispensable.

While the CC domain serves as the principal signalling module driving immune activation in CC‐NLRs, its activity is subject to multilayered regulation by the NBS and LRR domains to maintain immune homeostasis, a paradigm well established across diverse plant NLRs (Wang, Hu, et al. 2019; Wang, Wang, et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2020; Guo et al. 2025; Gao et al. 2020; Moffett et al. 2002; Förderer et al. 2022; Madhuprakash et al. 2024). Consistent with this canonical model, we found that both the isolated CC domain and the full‐length NLRMoro (CC‐NBS‐LRR) protein triggered cell death, whereas the CC‐NBS construct did not (Figures 1B and 5A), indicating that both the NBS and LRR domains contribute to regulating NLRMoro‐CC activity. Specifically, the NBS domain suppressed the cell death–inducing activity of CC, whereas the LRR domain was able to relieve this suppression both exclusively through intramolecular interaction, but not through intermolecular association (Figures 1B, 5A and 6). We further found that the NBS domain—particularly when fused with CC as CN—impaired CC self‐association (Figure 5H–J), whereas the LRR domain disrupted the interaction between NBS and CC (Figure 6E), suggesting a potential self‐regulatory mechanism in which NBS suppresses CC activity through direct interaction, while LRR relieves this suppression by modulating NBS–CC interactions. These findings highlight a hierarchical and dynamic regulatory mechanism within NLRMoro, ensuring precise control of immune activation.

The NBS domain harbours several conserved motifs critical for NLR regulation. The P‐loop mediates ATP/ADP binding, and its mutation abolishes cell death activity in many NLRs (Tameling et al. 2002, 2006; Williams et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2020; Bai et al. 2012; Bendahmane et al. 2002; Dinesh‐Kumar et al. 2000; Howles et al. 2005). The RNBS‐A motif facilitates ATP hydrolysis, and its disruption leads to constitutive activation (Tameling et al. 2006). The MHD motif maintains the resting state, with mutations causing autoactivation (Tameling et al. 2006; Williams et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2015; Gao et al. 2020; Bai et al. 2012; Gao et al. 2011; Bendahmane et al. 2002; Howles et al. 2005). Mutation analysis of these conserved motifs in NLRMoro revealed their distinct roles in the activity regulation. Disruption of the P‐loop abolished its ability to induce cell death, whereas mutations in RNBS‐A or MHD resulted in accelerated and intensified cell death (Figure 7A), indicating that these motifs outside of the CC domain are critical. Double‐mutant analyses showed that the enhanced activity from the RNBS‐A mutation was suppressed by the P‐loop mutation, while the MHD mutation‐induced activation remained strong but slightly delayed (Figure 7A). These results suggest that RNBS‐A‐mediated activation depends on an intact P‐loop, whereas MHD‐triggered activation is largely P‐loop‐independent. Therefore, the RNBS‐A and MHD motifs function in maintaining the rest state of NLRMoro through different mechanisms. We propose that RNBS‐A mutations reduce ATP hydrolysis, promoting a persistently active state, but require ATP/ADP binding via the P‐loop. In contrast, MHD mutations probably disrupt the autoinhibitory conformation, enabling activation even in the absence of nucleotide binding. We further found that although the P‐loop mutation did not alter the activity of the NBS domain alone, it weakened the interaction between NBS and LRR (Figure S13B; Figure 7E). This may explain why full‐length CC‐NBS‐LRR with a mutated P‐loop lost the ability to induce cell death, as the inhibitory effect of LRR on NBS was compromised. Surprisingly, when the P‐loop‐mutated NBS was co‐expressed with CC intermolecularly, rather than intramolecularly, CC activity was markedly enhanced (Figure 7B,C)—an unexpected result that further highlights the functional differences between intra‐ and intermolecular interactions and underscores the profound impact of the P‐loop mutation on NBS's regulating function. Collectively, these findings highlight the context‐dependent regulatory capacity of the P‐loop motif and suggest that precise modulation of NLR regulatory elements may offer opportunities to fine‐tune immune activation while avoiding excessive growth penalties.

A few studies have shown that phosphorylation plays an important role in regulating NLR protein activities (Zhong, Li, et al. 2025; Zhong, Chen, et al. 2025; Nounurai et al. 2022; Guo et al. 2020; Li 2020; Nakagami et al. 2010; Dinesh‐Kumar et al. 2000). In this study, mass spectrometry identified a phosphorylation site at Ser198, adjacent to the P‐loop motif in NLRMoro‐NBS. Functional analysis using phospho‐null (S198A) and phospho‐mimic (S198D) mutants revealed that only the S198D mutation abolished cell death induction, suggesting that phosphorylation at this site maintains NLRMoro in an inactive state (Figure 7F). Notably, a global phosphoproteomic study in rice also detected high‐confidence phosphorylation at the corresponding residue in the CNL protein LOC_os09g14490.1, highlighting the potential conservation and functional relevance of this site (Nakagami et al. 2010). Given its proximity to the P‐loop, S198 phosphorylation may modulate NLR activity by influencing nucleotide binding or adjacent regulatory motifs, though combined mutations of S198 and the P‐loop did not give clear clues (Figure S13E). Together, these findings suggest that phosphorylation serves as a key post‐translational mechanism for controlling NLR activation, offering a promising target for precise immune regulation. As the upstream kinases and phosphatases responsible for S198 phosphorylation remain unknown, identification of these regulatory enzymes would be highly informative for understanding how phosphorylation dynamics fine‐tune NLRMoro activation and may provide new entry points for modulating NLR‐mediated immunity.

To summarise these findings, and building on previous genetic, biochemical and structural studies of CC‐NLRs (Madhuprakash et al. 2024; Förderer et al. 2022; Wang, Hu, et al. 2019; Wang, Wang, et al. 2019; Hu et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2015; Moffett et al. 2002), we propose a self‐regulatory model for NLRMoro function (Figure 7G). The CC62‐116 region acts as a core signalling module within the CC domain, which is capable of inducing cell death by disrupting the PM or transducing signals downstream. The NBS domain negatively regulates CC activity via intramolecular interactions, probably by modulating its conformation or restricting access to downstream signalling elements. The LRR domain may perceive pathogen effectors or physiological condition changes upon pathogen challenge, alter CC and NBS spatial conformation through protein interactions, thereby relieving the NBS‐mediated suppression of CC. Within this regulatory framework, specific NBS motifs—such as the P‐loop and phosphorylation sites—coordinate domain interplay by controlling ATP/ADP exchange or post‐translational modification, maintaining a dynamic balance between the resting and activated states. Disruption of this balance toward activation leads to HR and immunity. These findings support and reinforce a mechanistic basis for the rational engineering of NLRs, including pathogen‐responsive systems such as those described by Wang et al. (2025). Based on this model, two strategies for optimising NLR‐based disease resistance in plants can be envisioned. First, overexpressing the CC active region under the control of pathogen‐inducible promoters could enable precise and timely immune responses, particularly against biotrophic pathogens, while minimising growth–defence trade‐offs. Second, engineering regulatory motifs outside the CC domain, such as the P‐loop or phosphorylation sites, could fine‐tune NLR activation thresholds, allowing for more flexible immune modulation. These strategies offer promising directions for NLR engineering, though further investigation is needed to fully explore their potential and context‐specific outcomes.

Experimental Procedures

Materials

Seeds of wheat ( Triticum aestivum ) lines Fielder, AvS + Yr10 and Moro, as well as N. benthamiana seeds and the isolate of Pst race CYR32 were provided by the State Key Laboratory for Crop Stress Resistance and High‐Efficiency Production, NWAFU, Yangling. B. graminis f. sp. tritici isolate E09 was provided by Prof. Qiang Li, College of Plant Protection, NWAFU.

Plant Growth Conditions and Pathogen Inoculations

All plants were grown in a greenhouse with a 16/8 h light/darkness cycle. Wheat was grown at 16°C and N. benthamiana at 22°C. Pst inoculation was performed as described previously (Zhu et al. 2021). Briefly, Pst urediniospores were suspended in isohexadecane and sprayed evenly onto the first and second wheat seedling leaves with a small air pump. The inoculated plants were incubated at 13°C for 24 h in a dark chamber with 100% relative humidity before returning them to the greenhouse. B. graminis f. sp. tritici inoculation was performed by shaking conidiospores from infected plants' conidia onto the detached third seedling leaves maintained in water agar in a Petri dish.

Agrobacterium tumefaciens‐ Mediated Transient Expression in Nicotiana benthamiana

Candidate genes were subcloned separately into the pBIN vector to construct recombinant plasmids and then transformed into the A. tumefaciens EHA105 (pSoup). Bacteria were grown in Luria Bertani (LB) medium containing 50 mg/mL rifampicin and 25 mg/mL kanamycin at 28°C for 24 h and harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in AS buffer containing 10 mM MES (pH 5.6), 10 mM MgCl2 and 150 μM acetosyringone. A. tumefaciens was adjusted to OD600 = 0.6 with AS buffer and infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves with a 1 mL syringe without a needle. The infiltrated leaves were sampled and stained by DAB or trypan blue (Bai et al. 2012). H2O2 content in the agroinfiltrated leaves were measured using a Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2) Content Assay Kit (Solarbio). The severity of cell death was assayed by measuring electrolyte leakage following the method described by Mittler et al. (1999). The presence of each protein/peptide was confirmed by western blotting assay.

Development of Transgenic Wheat

The full‐length coding sequence of NLRMoro‐CC‐GFP was inserted into the pCub vector under control of the maize ubiquitin promoter. The recombinant vector was then separately transformed into A. tumefaciens EHA105 and transformed into immature embryos of bread wheat cv. Fielder as previously described (Ishida et al. 2015). Genomic DNA was extracted from regenerated plants, and positive transgenic lines were detected by PCR with the Ubi‐F and gene‐specific primers (All primers are shown in Table S1).

Transient Gene Overexpression in Wheat Seedlings

A barley stripe mosaic virus (BSMV)‐mediated gene overexpression (VOX) system was constructed as described previously for transient expression of NLRMoro‐CC62‐116 (Lee et al. 2012; Upadhyaya et al. 2021). The CDS of NLRMoro‐CC62‐116 with a P2A peptide and HA tag on the N‐terminal was cloned into the BSMV‐γ vector (VOX:CC62‐116). Then the BSMV‐γ‐P2A‐NLRMoro‐CC62‐116 vector was mixed with the BSMV‐α and BSMV‐β to inoculate Fielder wheat seedlings. BSMV‐γ (VOX:00) was used as a control. At 9 dpi, the three leaves of wheat seedlings were further challenged with Pst CYR32. RNA and protein samples were harvested to confirm the expression of target genes and the presence of HA‐tagged NLRMoro‐CC62‐116 protein at several time points.

Histological Studies of Fungal Growth and Host Response

Wheat leaves after Pst inoculation were fixed, bleached and stained with DAB as described by Wang et al. (2007). H2O2 accumulation areas stained by DAB and autofluorescence areas of necrotic cells were observed using an Olympus BX‐51 fluorescence microscope. The formation of a substomatal vesicle was defined as a penetration site. Leaf segments were stained with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) conjugated to Alexa Fluor‐488 and observed for fungal substomatal vesicles, hyphal length and hyphal infection area (Ayliffe et al. 2011). At least 30 infection sites in each group were counted from randomly selected leaf segments for each experiment.

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR Analysis

Leaf samples collected at different time points were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using a Quick RNA isolation kit (Huayueyang), and then reverse transcribed to cDNA using HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme) following the manufacturer's protocols. qPCR was performed using a Bio‐Rad CFX96 Real‐Time PCR detection system with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). TaActin (wheat actin gene, NCBI accession number: AQU14670), TaEF1a (wheat elongation factor 1α, NCBI accession number: Q03033) and PsEF1 (Pst elongation factor 1, NCBI accession number: KNE93481) genes were used as internal references. The data were analysed using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Statistical significance was evaluated by Student's t‐tests.

Measurements of Net Ca2+ Flux

Net Ca2+ flux was measured using non‐invasive micro‐test technology (Xuyue.net). Leaves from transient gene‐overexpressing N. benthamiana or 3‐week‐old CC‐OE and Fielder wheat were immobilised in measuring buffer (1 mM KCl, 0.2 mM CaCl2, 0.2 mM MES, pH 5.8) for 30 min to equilibrate. Steady‐state fluxes in mesophyll cells were recorded for 3 min prior to chitin treatment. Chitin was then slowly added to the buffer to a final concentration of 50 μM. Transient Ca2+ fluxes were subsequently recorded and analysed using imFluxesV3.0 software, with flow rates expressed in pmol·cm−2·s−1. Positive values indicate efflux, while negative values indicate influx. A total of 19 replicates were tested for CC‐OE and 14 for Fielder.

Protein Interaction Assays

For BiFC assay, the two candidate genes were cloned into pSPYNE and pSPYCE vectors to generate gene‐nYFP and gene‐cYFP constructs and co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. YFP fluorescence signals were observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope 2 days post‐agroinfiltration.

For Y2H analysis, two candidate genes were each inserted into one of the two plasmids of pGBKT7 (BD) and pGADT7 (AD), and co‐transferred into the yeast strain AH109. Transformants were incubated on SD medium lacking Trp and Leu (SD/−Leu−Trp) and cultured at 30°C for 24 h in SD/−Trp−Leu liquid medium. Yeast cells were collected and suspended in sterile water at OD600 = 1.0. The diluted yeast cells were incubated on SD/−Trp−His−Leu−Ade plates. Yeast colonies successfully grown on SD/−Trp−Leu−His−Ade medium were identified as positive colonies and X‐α‐Gal was used to further confirm the interactions by the development of blue colour.

GST pull‐down assays were performed using a GST Protein Interaction Pull‐Down Kit (ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer's instructions. CC and NBS were separately ligated into pET15b and pGEX‐4 T‐1 vectors and transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 for protein expression and purification. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated anti‐GST‐tag or His‐Ttag mouse monoclonal antibody (Beyotime) was used for western blotting.

For Split‐Luc assays, constructs nLuc‐gene and cLuc‐gene were co‐agroinfiltrated in N. benthamiana leaves. After 2 days, the infiltrated leaf areas were treated with 0.15 mg/mL D‐luciferin potassium salt (AbMole) and kept in darkness for 5 min. The Luc image was captured using the PlantView100 system (Biolight).

For Co‐IP analyses, two candidate genes each carrying a GFP or mCherry tag were co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves. 48 h later, the agroinfiltrated leaves were ground into fine powder in liquid nitrogen and dissolved in plant protein extraction buffer containing 1 mM PMSF and 1% (v/v) proteinase inhibitor (Solarbio). The extracts were centrifuged and then the GFP‐Trap Agarose (Proteintech) was added to the supernatant and incubated at 4°C for 2 h. The purified samples were then analysed by western blotting assay with appropriate antibodies.

Western Blotting Assay

For western blotting assay, the protein sample was mixed with SDS‐PAGE Sample Loading Buffer (Beyotime) and boiled for 5 min. Then, the mixture was separated by 10% or 12.5% SDS‐PAGE and transformed to PVDF blotting membrane (Beyotime). Finally, the protein carrying different tags was labelled by corresponding mouse monoclonal antibodies (Beyotime) separately and visualised by HRP activity (Beyotime).

Mass Spectrometry

To identify the putative phosphorylation site in the NBS domain mass spectrometry analysis was performed using GFP‐trap Agarose (Proteintech) to purify the NBS protein from infiltrated N. benthamiana 48 h post‐agroinfiltration. The protein fractions were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE. Specific protein bands detected by Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) staining were cut into small cubes of 1 mm3, and after decolouring with 50 mM NH4HCO3 were treated with 10 mM dithiothreitol, 60 mM iodoacetamide and then digested overnight with trypsin at 37°C. The phosphopeptides enriched using TiO2 Phospho‐peptide Enrichment Spin Tips (ThermoFisher) were analysed by LC–MS/MS (QExactive HF‐X, ThermoFisher) in the proteomics platform of the State Key Laboratory for Crop Stress Resistance and High‐Efficiency Production, NWAFU, Yangling. Analysis was performed using Thermo Scientific Proteome Discoverer 2.4 software. The same phosphorylation site found in two independent experiments was considered a potential phosphorylation site in the NBS and was mutated to verify its effect on the cell death induction.
