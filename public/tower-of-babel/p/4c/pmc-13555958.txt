Integrative analysis of SLFN11-related DEGs reveals novel biomarkers for cisplatin sensitivity and immune modulation in colorectal cancer
Authors: Wei Wang, Lijiang Zhou, Jing Wang, Tingting Li, Zheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCisplatin is a widely used chemotherapy drug, but resistance mechanisms, including the role of SLFN11, pose significant challenges in colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment, with only 15–20% of patients showing objective responses.MethodsWe conducted differential gene expression analysis using datasets GSE181094, GSE179896, and GSE231559, alongside TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ datasets. DEGs were identified using DESeq2, Limma, and edgeR, followed by protein-protein interaction network analysis with STRING, and functional enrichment analysis via KEGG, GO, and GSEA. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed using the TwoSampleMR package with robust instrumental variable validation, and single-cell RNA sequencing data was analyzed using Seurat and SingleR to examine cell-type specific expression patterns.ResultsOur analysis identified several DEGs, including FOXL1 and GPC4, associated with cisplatin sensitivity in SLFN11 knockout cell lines. MR analysis revealed SLC46A3 as a significant gene related to CRC, showing a causal relationship (β=-0.2935, p = 0.0308). Single-cell RNA sequencing demonstrated high expression of SLFN11 in granulocyte-monocyte progenitor and common myeloid progenitor cells in adjacent normal tissues, with reduced expression in cancerous tissues. These findings highlight the significant impact of SLFN11 on gene expression and its potential as a biomarker for cisplatin sensitivity.ConclusionSLFN11 plays a critical role in modulating cisplatin sensitivity in CRC, with implications for personalized cancer therapy. These findings establish SLFN11 as a potential biomarker for personalizing cisplatin-based therapies in CRC patients, potentially improving response rates and clinical outcomes.Keywords: SLFN11, Colorectal cancer, Cisplatin resistance, DEGs, Mendelian randomization, Single-cell RNA sequencing

Introduction

Cisplatin, a platinum-based chemotherapy drug, is commonly used to treat various cancers, including colorectal cancer (CRC). Despite its widespread use, objective response rates to cisplatin-based regimens in CRC remain limited to approximately 15–20%, with many patients developing resistance during treatment. This therapeutic challenge underscores the urgent need to identify predictive biomarkers and understand the molecular mechanisms of resistance [1].

Studies have shown that various molecular pathways are involved in mediating resistance to cisplatin in CRC. For instance, the overexpression of DNA repair genes and the activation of the PI3K/AKT and NF-κB pathways have been implicated in enhancing the survival and proliferation of cancer cells despite cisplatin treatment. Additionally, epigenetic modifications and the tumor microenvironment, including hypoxia and stromal interactions, also play crucial roles in modulating drug resistance. Addressing these resistance mechanisms through combination therapies or novel agents targeting these pathways holds promise for enhancing the effectiveness of cisplatin in treating cancer [2, 3].

SLFN11 has emerged as a critical player in the response of cancer cells to DNA damage and replication stress. It acts as a DNA/RNA helicase and has been implicated in enhancing the cytotoxicity of DNA-damaging agents across various cancer types. It’s demonstrated that the role of SLFN11 in multiple cancers, highlighting its ability to enhance the effectiveness of DNA-damaging therapies by promoting DNA damage responses. Recent studies have delved into the mechanistic understanding of SLFN11’s functions. Metzner et al., 2022 provided insights into the molecular mechanisms of SLFN11, elucidating its role in maintaining genomic stability by blocking stressed replication forks independently of ATR [4]. Furthermore, SLFN11 has been implicated in modulating protein homeostasis, adding another layer to its multifaceted role in cancer biology [5]. In breast cancer, Raynaud et al., 2023 showed that modulation of SLFN11 induces significant changes in the DNA damage response, further emphasizing its importance in cancer therapeutics [6].

Despite the substantial body of research on SLFN11 in various cancers, there has been a noticeable gap in studies focusing on its role in CRC. Existing studies predominantly concentrate on cancers such as glioblastoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, and breast cancer, with findings suggesting that SLFN11 is a key determinant of sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents [7]. Notably, SLFN11 expression has been associated with improved responses to platinum-based chemotherapy in advanced prostate cancer and has been proposed as a liquid biomarker in small cell lung cancer [7].

The lack of comprehensive studies on SLFN11 in CRC represents a significant knowledge gap, particularly given that CRC is the third most common cancer globally with over 1.9 million new cases annually [8]. Platinum-based therapies are frequently used in metastatic CRC, yet response rates remain suboptimal, highlighting the need for better predictive biomarkers. Additionally, while molecular subtypes and biomarkers like microsatellite instability (MSI) status have improved treatment stratification in CRC, there is still a lack of reliable biomarkers for predicting response to platinum-based chemotherapy specifically.

This study aims to fill this research gap by investigating the association between SLFN11 and CRC. Given the critical role of SLFN11 in modulating the response to replicative stress and its potential as a therapeutic target, understanding its function in CRC could provide new insights into treatment strategies. This study leverages gene expression profiling and functional analysis to elucidate the role of SLFN11 in CRC, potentially paving the way for novel therapeutic approaches and improving patient outcomes through personalized treatment selection.

Materials and methods

Resource and datasets

We carefully selected specific datasets to comprehensively examine SLFN11’s role in cancer, with a focus on its implications for CRC. While some datasets are not CRC-specific, they were chosen due to their unique SLFN11 knockout models, which provide valuable insights into SLFN11-dependent gene expression and cisplatin sensitivity applicable across cancer types.

The dataset GSE181094 focuses on the role of SLFN11 in pediatric sarcoma, exploring its potential as a biomarker for sensitivity to DNA-damaging agents (DDA). This dataset was selected because it offers one of the few available SLFN11 knockout models with comprehensive gene expression data following DNA damage, which is directly relevant to understanding cisplatin sensitivity mechanisms. The study found that SLFN11 expression correlated with responses to the PARP inhibitor talazoparib and the topoisomerase I inhibitor irinotecan in sarcoma cell lines. However, high SLFN11 expression did not correlate with favorable outcomes in children with sarcoma, indicating that its biomarker potential may be context-dependent. The dataset includes gene expression profiles from the ES8 Ewing’s sarcoma cell line and a CRISPR/Cas9 SLFN11 knockout derivative, both treated with irradiation [8].

The dataset GSE179896 examines the role of SLFN11 in high-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC), particularly its association with platinum sensitivity and immune responses. This dataset was chosen because ovarian cancer shares similar platinum-response mechanisms with CRC, and the SLFN11 knockout model provides critical insights into gene regulation following treatment with DNA-damaging agents. The study found that SLFN11 expression in both cancer and microenvironmental components correlated with favorable outcomes and immune activation in HGSOC. This suggests SLFN11 as a dual biomarker for predicting responses to DDA treatment. The dataset includes gene expression profiles from DU145 wild-type and SLFN11 knockout clones treated with gemcitabine [9].

The dataset GSE231559 is part of the MD Anderson Cancer Center’s CRC Moon Shot initiative. This CRC-specific dataset was selected to provide direct evidence of SLFN11 expression patterns in CRC tissues. It features single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data obtained from fresh colorectal cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues of patients with primary and liver/peritoneum metastases. The study aims to explore the cellular diversity within colorectal cancer, contributing valuable insights into the tumor microenvironment and the progression of CRC.

The TCGA-COAD (Colon Adenocarcinoma) and TCGA-READ (Rectal Adenocarcinoma) datasets are part of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project, which provides comprehensive molecular profiles of cancer types. These datasets were selected to validate our findings in a large cohort of CRC patients with clinical outcome data. They contain multi-omics data, including genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenomic information, allowing for in-depth analysis of colon and rectal cancers [10]. The TCGA-COAD dataset focuses on tumors originating in the colon, while the TCGA-READ dataset focuses on rectal tumors. Both datasets are valuable resources for understanding the molecular differences and similarities between these closely related cancer types. The data were downloaded from the UCSC Xena database [11], a platform that facilitates access to and visualization of TCGA data.

DEGs analysis in SLFN11 knockout

The analysis of the DEGs was performed using the R package DESeq2 [12], Limma [13], and edgeR [14]. We employed multiple methodologies to ensure robust identification of differentially expressed genes and minimize false positives. DESeq2 is a robust statistical tool for analyzing count data from high-throughput sequencing assays such as RNA-Seq. It uses a model based on the negative binomial distribution to determine the variance-mean dependence in the data and tests for differential expression. We also used the limma package to perform differential gene expression analysis, leveraging its robust linear modeling and empirical Bayes methods to identify DEGs between SLFN11 and SLFN11-KO cell lines. edgeR is also a powerful tool for analyzing count data from RNA-Seq experiments, utilizing empirical Bayes estimation and exact tests based on the negative binomial distribution.

Genes were considered significantly differentially expressed if they had an adjusted p-value (padj) or false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute log fold change (|logFC|) > 1 or < -1. We consistently applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate across all analyses. Visualization of the DEGs was achieved using the pheatmap tool (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pheatmap/), which is widely used for creating heatmaps in R. pheatmap provides various options for hierarchical clustering and allows the integration of annotation data, helping to visualize the overall patterns and relationships between samples and genes.

The STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/) was used to construct and visualize PPI networks. STRING is a comprehensive resource that integrates known and predicted protein-protein interactions from various sources, including experimental data, computational prediction methods, and public text collections. The PPI network helps in understanding the functional interactions between proteins encoded by DEGs.

Venn diagram and functional enrichment analysis

The Venn diagram analysis was conducted using an online tool available at http://www.cloud.biomicroclass.com/CloudPlatform/SoftPage/Venn. This tool allows for the comparison of gene lists to identify overlapping and unique genes among different datasets. In this study, Venn diagram analysis was used to compare DEGs from the SLFN11 knockout ES8-1F2 and DU145 cell lines, revealing distinct gene expression profiles with no overlapping DEGs.

For functional enrichment analysis, we employed multiple approaches to ensure comprehensive pathway identification. The KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analysis tool was utilized available at http://www.cloud.biomicroclass.com/CloudPlatform/SoftPage/KEGG, and the GO (Gene Ontology) enrichment analysis tool was utilized available at http://www.cloud.biomicroclass.com/CloudPlatform/SoftPage/GO. These tools provide pathway mapping and enrichment analysis based on DEGs. By setting different p-value thresholds, the tool identifies pathways that are significantly enriched in the dataset.

Additionally, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the fgsea R package to detect more subtle pathway changes. GSEA evaluates whether genes in pre-defined sets (such as pathways) show statistically significant differences between two biological states. This approach allows for the identification of pathways that might be missed by traditional over-representation analysis, especially when individual gene changes are modest. We used the MSigDB hallmark gene sets and C2 canonical pathways for our analysis, with 1000 permutations and a significance threshold of FDR < 0.25, as recommended by the GSEA developers.

Mendelian randomization analysis using GWAS data

Using the R package gwasrapidd [15], we extracted all DEGs-related SNPs from the SLFN11 Knockout ES8-1F2 and DU145 cell lines. These SNPs were then used as instrumental variables. To ensure robust causal inference, we assessed the strength of instrumental variables using F-statistics, with F > 10 considered sufficiently strong to minimize weak instrument bias. We also conducted sensitivity analyses to evaluate potential pleiotropy, including MR-Egger regression and heterogeneity tests.

Subsequently, we utilized the SMR (Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization) tool [16] to obtain the necessary data for Mendelian randomization analysis based on the extracted SNP list. The Mendelian randomization analysis was performed using the TwoSampleMR package [17], with exposure data sourced from the genome-wide association study (GWAS) database on CRC patients, specifically the datasets ieu-b-4965 available at https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/.

Tcga data conversion and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis

Using the data downloaded from Xenabrowser.net, the FPKM data for CRC was converted into TMP data utilizing tools such as tibble, dplyr, tidyr, and the Gencode annotation file. This transformation allowed for a more accurate analysis of gene expression levels. The resulting TMP data was then visualized through box plots, which highlighted the differential expression of genes in SLFN11 Knockout ES8-1F2 and DU145 cell lines.

In this study, tumor samples from patients were categorized using three different algorithms based on gene expression levels: (1) higher gene expression compared to adjacent normal tissue (Compared_with_Normal), (2) quartiles-based high and low expression groups, and (3) higher gene expression compared to the median value. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was employed to investigate the association between DEGs in SLFN11 knockout ES8-1F2 and DU145 cell lines with the survival of CRC patients.

Additionally, we analyzed the correlation between SLFN11 expression and cisplatin response using TCGA data. We extracted clinical data from CRC patients who received platinum-based therapies and stratified them based on SLFN11 expression levels to evaluate potential associations with treatment response and survival outcomes.

scRNA-seq analysis using seurat and singleR

We employed two primary bioinformatics tools, Seurat [18] and SingleR [19], to analyze single-cell RNA sequencing data from CRC patients. Seurat was utilized for data preprocessing, normalization, and clustering. Initially, raw sequencing data were imported into Seurat, where they underwent quality control to filter out low-quality cells and genes. The data were then normalized to account for technical variability. Following normalization, highly variable genes were identified, and dimensionality reduction was performed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The top principal components were selected for further analysis, and cells were clustered using a graph-based clustering approach. Finally, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used to visualize the clusters in a two-dimensional space.

SingleR was subsequently applied to annotate cell types based on reference transcriptomic datasets. SingleR compares the gene expression profile of each single cell to reference datasets and assigns the cell type label that most closely matches its expression profile. For our analysis, we used the Human Primary Cell Atlas (HPCA) as the reference dataset. The main cell types identified by SingleR were then mapped onto the UMAP plot generated by Seurat, providing a comprehensive view of the cellular composition within the CRC samples.

To link our scRNA-seq findings to cisplatin sensitivity, we performed additional analyses on DNA repair pathways in SLFN11-high versus SLFN11-low immune cell clusters. This included examining the expression of genes involved in nucleotide excision repair, mismatch repair, and homologous recombination pathways. We also employed cell-cell communication analysis using the CellChat package to identify potential SLFN11-dependent interactions in the tumor microenvironment, with a focus on signaling pathways related to immune surveillance and drug response.

Cisplatin sensitivity analysis within SLFN11-KO DEGs

In this study, we utilized bulk RNA sequencing data from the GSE217845 dataset to investigate differential gene expression between SLFN11 and SLFN11-KO cell lines. We performed expression profiling by high throughput sequencing, focusing on perturbation-informed signatures derived from the integration of transcriptome and chromatin accessibility data. The DEGs were identified using established bioinformatics tools, emphasizing the susceptibility of various candidate anticancer drugs. For validation, we specifically analyzed DEGs associated with cisplatin sensitivity, ensuring robust and reliable results through stringent statistical criteria (adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |logFC| > 1).

We further investigated the functional implications of these DEGs in cisplatin response by conducting pathway analysis focused on DNA damage response, apoptosis, and drug efflux mechanisms, which are known to contribute to cisplatin resistance in cancer cells.

To detect more subtle pathway changes, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). The limited number of significantly enriched pathways in our traditional analysis may reflect the subtle yet specific effects of SLFN11 on gene expression or the relatively small number of DEGs identified in our analysis. However, the GSEA results suggest that even subtle changes in gene expression can have meaningful impacts on key pathways related to cancer cell survival and drug response.

Mendelian randomization analysis of DEGs in SLFN11 knockout cell lines using GWAS data

For the dataset of CRC bbj-a-76, TwoSampleMR identified one gene, SLC46A3, that has a significant causal relationship with the outcome variable CRC. In contrast, MRMix found no genes that have a significant causal relationship with CRC in this dataset. The Mendelian randomization calculation results for SLC46A3 using TwoSampleMR showed a beta coefficient (β) of -0.2935, standard error (se) of 0.1359, and p-value of 0.0308, indicating a statistically significant association between the exposure and outcome variables. The OR was estimated to be 0.7457, with a lower CI of 0.5713 and upper CI of 0.9732 (Fig. 4).

Mendelian Randomization Analysis of SLC46A3. (A) Scatter Plot by TwoSampleMR; (B) Forest Plot by TwoSampleMR; (C) Funnel Plot by TwoSampleMR; (D) Forest Plot by forestploter with OR Values

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses to evaluate the validity of our instrumental variables. The F-statistic for the SLC46A3-associated SNPs was 21.4, well above the threshold of 10, indicating strong instruments that minimize the risk of weak instrument bias. The MR-Egger regression intercept was not significantly different from zero (p = 0.42), suggesting no directional pleiotropy. Additionally, the heterogeneity test (Cochran’s Q) showed no significant heterogeneity (p = 0.38), further supporting the validity of our MR analysis.

These results suggest that SLC46A3, a gene downregulated in SLFN11 knockout cells, may have a protective effect against CRC, with a 25.4% reduction in the odds of developing CRC per standard deviation increase in SLC46A3 expression. This finding provides valuable insights into the potential mechanistic links between SLFN11-regulated genes and CRC pathogenesis.

The role of SLFN11 in cisplatin sensitivity: insights from differential gene expression analysis

Our investigation focused on understanding the DEGs between SLFN11 and SLFN11-KO cell lines within the cisplatin sensitivity context. Using the bulk RNA sequencing data from the GSE217845 dataset, we analyzed perturbation-informed signatures derived from the crosswise integration of transcriptome and chromatin accessibility data. This analysis predicted the susceptibility of various candidate anticancer drugs, with a specific emphasis on validating the findings using cisplatin. In our study, we identified a distinct set of DEGs that were significantly associated with cisplatin sensitivity in SLFN11-KO cell lines. These DEGs include FOXL1, CA8, SLFN11, SLC15A3, PTPRG, PTN, GPC4, BCHE, and DHX58. These genes exhibited marked differential expression between the SLFN11 and SLFN11-KO groups, suggesting a potential mechanistic link to cisplatin sensitivity (Table 3).

The remarkably high AUC value for PTN (0.9922) in rectal adenocarcinoma suggests its exceptional potential as a diagnostic biomarker. PTN encodes pleiotrophin, a growth factor that is involved in cell growth, differentiation, and angiogenesis. Its significant differential expression and high diagnostic value highlight its potential importance in rectal cancer pathogenesis and as a therapeutic target. For instance, while PTN showed the highest diagnostic value in rectal adenocarcinoma (AUC = 0.9922), it had a lower diagnostic value in colon adenocarcinoma (AUC = 0.7635). These differences suggest that biomarker selection for CRC may need to account for tumor location and molecular subtype.

Correlation analysis of differentially expressed genes and survival period in colon adenocarcinoma

In our study, KM survival analysis as shown in Fig. 7 was employed to investigate the association between ten overlapping DEGs (including SLFN11, FOXL1, PTPRG, CA8, BCHE, SLC15A3, DHX58, GPC4, PTN, GPC6) and survival period in patients with colon adenocarcinoma. Specifically, PTPRG showed the following: its expression was relatively higher in cancerous tissue compared to adjacent normal tissue (p = 0.044, statistically significant). When expression was above the median, the result was also statistically significant (p = 0.034). No other DEGs showed significant correlations with prognosis.

The selective prognostic significance of PTPRG, despite the diagnostic relevance of multiple DEGs, highlights the complex relationship between gene expression and clinical outcomes in CRC. PTPRG (Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase Receptor Type G) is a member of the protein tyrosine phosphatase (PTP) family, which regulates a variety of cellular processes including cell growth, differentiation, and oncogenic transformation. The significant correlation between PTPRG expression and survival suggests its potential functional importance in CRC progression and response to therapy.

The lack of prognostic significance for other DEGs, including SLFN11 itself, may be due to various factors such as post-transcriptional regulation, protein-level interactions, functional redundancy, or context-dependent effects. For example, while SLFN11 is known to influence cisplatin sensitivity, its impact on overall survival may be masked by other prognostic factors or may be more evident in specific patient subgroups, such as those receiving platinum-based therapies. Additionally, tumor heterogeneity and the complex interplay between multiple genetic and environmental factors likely contribute to the observed discrepancy between diagnostic and prognostic relevance.

Further analysis of TCGA data showed that among CRC patients who received platinum-based therapies, those with high SLFN11 expression had a trend toward better response rates (42.3% vs. 27.8%, p = 0.08) and improved progression-free survival (median PFS: 8.4 vs. 5.7 months, p = 0.06) compared to those with low SLFN11 expression. While these differences did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the limited sample size of platinum-treated patients in the TCGA cohort, they suggest a potential association between SLFN11 expression and platinum response in CRC that warrants further investigation in larger, dedicated studies.

Further analysis of DNA repair pathway genes in SLFN11-high versus SLFN11-low immune cell clusters revealed significant differences in the expression of key genes involved in nucleotide excision repair (NER) and homologous recombination (HR). Specifically, SLFN11-high immune cells showed decreased expression of ERCC1, ERCC4, and RAD51, which are critical for repairing cisplatin-induced DNA damage. This suggests that SLFN11 may modulate cisplatin sensitivity in the tumor microenvironment by influencing DNA repair capacity in immune cells.

Additionally, CellChat analysis identified altered communication patterns between SLFN11-high immune cells and cancer cells, with significant changes in signaling pathways related to cellular stress response (p < 0.01) and apoptosis regulation (p < 0.01). These findings suggest that SLFN11 may influence cisplatin sensitivity not only through cancer cell-intrinsic mechanisms but also by modulating tumor-immune interactions in the microenvironment.

Discussion

Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of SLFN11 in various cancers, indicating its involvement in tumor suppression and modulation of cellular responses to DNA damage. Zhang et al., 2021 provided a comprehensive overview of SLFN11’s role across multiple cancers, demonstrating that SLFN11 can enhance the effectiveness of DNA-damaging agents by promoting DNA damage responses, thereby inhibiting tumor growth [20]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, Zhou et al., 2020 found that SLFN11 inhibits tumorigenesis and metastasis by targeting the RPS4X via the mTOR pathway, suggesting a tumor suppressor role for SLFN11 in liver cancer [21].

Our research expands on these findings by exploring the gene expression profiles of SLFN11 knockout cell lines, with a specific focus on the implications for CRC. While SLFN11 has been established as a tumor suppressor in liver cancer and a sensitizer to DNA-damaging agents in various cancer types, our study provides unique insights into its role in colorectal cancer specifically. In CRC, we observed that SLFN11 expression correlates with a trend toward improved response to platinum-based therapies.

In the ES8 Ewing’s sarcoma cell line and the DU145 prostate cancer cell line, SLFN11 knockout led to distinct gene expression changes, indicating the diverse impact of SLFN11 across different cancer types. The differential expression analysis revealed significant downregulation of several genes in both cell lines, underscoring SLFN11’s role in maintaining normal gene expression profiles and cellular functions. Furthermore, our study identified significant pathways influenced by SLFN11 knockout. For instance, the KEGG enrichment analysis pointed to pathways related to nitrogen metabolism and vitamin digestion, while GO enrichment analysis highlighted the “heparan sulfate proteoglycan binding” pathway. These findings suggest that SLFN11 may play a role in various metabolic and biosynthetic processes, potentially impacting cancer cell survival and proliferation.

When comparing our findings to existing literature on SLFN11 in other cancer types, we observe both similarities and differences. Similar to reports in hepatocellular carcinoma [21], our data suggests a potential tumor suppressor role for SLFN11 in CRC, with reduced expression in cancer tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues. However, unlike in small cell lung cancer where SLFN11 expression strongly predicts clinical outcomes [7], in our CRC cohort, SLFN11 itself did not show significant prognostic value. This discrepancy highlights the tissue-specific nature of SLFN11’s functions and underscores the importance of cancer type-specific studies.

In glioblastoma, Fischietti et al., 2022 reported that SLFN11 negatively regulates non-canonical NF-κB signaling, promoting tumor progression [22]. This contrasts with its tumor-suppressive role in hepatocellular carcinoma and its sensitizing effect to DNA-damaging agents in various cancers, including our observations in CRC. This context-dependent role further emphasizes the complexity of SLFN11’s functions and the need for cancer-specific investigations.

Our analysis of SLFN11 knockout in B-cells, particularly in germinal center B-cells during B-cell development, further supports the importance of SLFN11 in immune regulation and cancer biology. The high expression of SLFN11 in these cells, as discussed by Moribe et al., 2021, indicates its potential role in maintaining genomic stability and preventing malignancies within the immune system [23]. Overall, the cumulative evidence underscores SLFN11 as a critical player in tumor suppression and cellular response to DNA damage, warranting continued exploration of its mechanisms and therapeutic potential.

The knockdown of SLFN11 using CRISPR/Cas9 technology in ES8 and DU145 cell lines has elucidated a variety of DEGs, which have significant implications in cancer biology. In the SLFN11-KO ES8-1F2 cell line, five key DEGs were identified: SYCP1, PTPRG, CYTL1, SLC46A3, and MBD3L3. Notably, CYTL1, which is downregulated, has been previously reported to play a role in cancer growth and metastasis. Wen et al., 2012 demonstrated that CYTL1 is involved in the growth and metastasis of neuroblastoma cells, highlighting its importance in cancer cell proliferation and spread [24]. Furthermore, Schneller et al., 2019 identified CYTL1 as a proangiogenic factor, which is essential for tumor vascularization, thus facilitating tumor growth [25]. Wang et al., 2019 showed that CYTL1 can inhibit tumor metastasis by decreasing STAT3 phosphorylation, underlining its potential as a therapeutic target [2].

In the DU145 SLFN11-KO cell line, significant DEGs included BCHE, GPC4, CA8, RP11-800A18.4, RP11-120J1.1, PRSS45, MAGEE1, PRRT2, RP5-864K19.6, and FAM196B. Among these, GPC4 and SLC15A have documented roles in cancer. GPC4, which is downregulated, has been noted for its involvement in cellular growth and differentiation, with implications in various cancers. PTN, another DEG in the DU145 cell line, has been shown to stimulate fibroblasts, endothelial, and epithelial cells, and is expressed in human cancer, as reported by Fang et al., 1992 [26]. Additionally, Chauhan et al., 1993 demonstrated that overexpression of PTN leads to transformation of NIH 3T3 cells and tumor formation in nude mice, indicating its role in oncogenesis [27].

SLC15A4, identified as an upregulated gene, serves as a novel prognostic biomarker and target for lung adenocarcinoma, as highlighted by Huang et al., 2021 [28]. This gene is implicated in immune responses and cellular communication, particularly within the macrophage population in the tumor microenvironment. The involvement of these DEGs in various cancers underscores the multifaceted role of SLFN11 in cancer biology and its potential as a therapeutic target.

Recent advances in non-coding RNA research have revealed their important roles in cancer pathogenesis, progression, and response to therapy. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs) can influence gene expression through various mechanisms, affecting tumor growth and metastasis. As demonstrated by Ashrafizadeh et al., these non-coding RNAs can regulate critical tumor-suppressor pathways like PTEN, which inhibits cancer cell proliferation and invasion [29]. In particular, circRNAs have emerged as novel actors in cancer progression through their interaction with key signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, as highlighted in studies by Alimohammadi et al. and Mafi et al. [30, 31].

In the context of chemotherapy resistance, miRNAs have shown significant potential as therapeutic targets. Deldar Abad Paskeh et al. demonstrated that miRNA-489 functions as a tumor-suppressor by sensitizing cancer cells to chemotherapy through disruption of oncogenic pathways like PI3K/Akt and induction of apoptosis [32]. Similarly, Kirtonia et al. reported that lncRNAs contribute significantly to drug resistance mechanisms in hematological malignancies by regulating key cellular processes including proliferation, survival, and differentiation [33].

The involvement of circRNAs in regulating the PI3K signaling pathway, as described by Kahkesh et al. in non-small cell lung cancer, suggests similar mechanisms might be at play in modulating cisplatin sensitivity in CRC [34]. Furthermore, Mirzaei et al. demonstrated that lncRNAs can directly regulate the response of cancer cells to cisplatin and other chemotherapeutic agents by affecting various molecular pathways including apoptosis, glycolysis, and EMT [35]. The regulatory role of circRNAs in PI3K signaling described by Mafi et al. in glioma progression further suggests potential parallel mechanisms in CRC [36].

Given these findings, investigation of potential regulatory relationships between non-coding RNAs and SLFN11 in CRC could provide new insights into the mechanisms of cisplatin resistance and opportunities for therapeutic intervention. The ability of non-coding RNAs to modulate chemosensitivity across different cancer types suggests they may play a similar role in regulating SLFN11-dependent cisplatin responses in CRC, representing an important area for future research.

SLFN11 has emerged as a critical biomarker in the landscape of cancer therapeutics, demonstrating significant predictive value across various treatment modalities. Studies such as those by Gardner et al., 2017 [37] and Akashi et al., 2024 [38] have established SLFN11’s role in chemotherapy, particularly in predicting responses to platinum-based therapies. SLFN11 expression is correlated with increased sensitivity to cisplatin and oxaliplatin in high-grade serous ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, and gastric cancer, as shown by He et al., 2017 [39], Peng et al., 2019 [40], and Deng et al., 2015 [41]. This relationship underscores SLFN11’s potential as a biomarker for stratifying patients who are more likely to benefit from platinum-based chemotherapies.

Beyond chemotherapy, SLFN11 also plays a role in the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Zhou et al., 2023 highlighted how SLFN11 deficiency disrupts CCL2 signaling and macrophage M2 polarization, thereby potentiating the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in hepatocellular carcinoma [42]. This suggests that SLFN11 status could be a valuable biomarker for predicting responses to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and improving patient outcomes in immunotherapy.

SLFN11’s predictive power extends to novel therapeutics as well. Murai et al., 2021 demonstrated that SLFN11 inactivation sensitizes cancer cells to the ubiquitin-activating enzyme inhibitor TAK-243, indicating that SLFN11 could guide the application of targeted therapies [43]. Furthermore, studies by Winkler et al., 2021 [9] and Zhao et al., 2023 [44] have shown that epigenetic modulation of SLFN11 can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of treatments like sacitizumab govitecan, an antibody-drug conjugate. Additionally, the role of SLFN11 in overcoming resistance to PARP inhibitors and in enhancing responses to combination therapies involving ATR inhibitors has been highlighted by Murai et al., 2016 [45] and Kundu et al., 2021 [46]. This positions SLFN11 not only as a biomarker for response but also as a strategic target to overcome drug resistance.

Our investigation focused on understanding the DEGs between SLFN11 and SLFN11-KO cell lines within the context of cisplatin sensitivity. Using the bulk RNA sequencing data from the GSE217845 dataset, we analyzed perturbation-informed signatures derived from the crosswise integration of transcriptome and chromatin accessibility data. This analysis predicted the susceptibility of various candidate anticancer drugs, with a specific emphasis on validating the findings using cisplatin. In our study, we identified a distinct set of DEGs significantly associated with cisplatin sensitivity in SLFN11-KO cell lines. These DEGs include FOXL1, CA8, SLFN11, SLC15A3, PTPRG, PTN, GPC4, BCHE, DHX58, and GPC6. These genes exhibited marked differential expression between the SLFN11 and SLFN11-KO groups, suggesting a potential mechanistic link to cisplatin sensitivity.

The data reveal that FOXL1, which showed a significant logFCs of -8.805, and CA8, with a logFC of 7.289, were among the most highly dysregulated genes. Other notable genes, such as SLC15A3 and GPC4, demonstrated logFCs of -5.473 and − 5.150, respectively, further underscoring their potential roles in mediating the response to cisplatin treatment. This extensive differential expression highlights the critical involvement of these genes in the cellular response to cisplatin, paving the way for further functional studies to elucidate their precise roles and therapeutic potential.

Based on our findings, several therapeutic strategies could be developed to leverage SLFN11-related pathways in CRC treatment:

Patient Stratification: SLFN11 expression levels could be used to identify CRC patients who are more likely to benefit from platinum-based chemotherapies. Our data suggests patients with higher SLFN11 expression may show better responses to cisplatin, potentially improving treatment outcomes.

Combination Therapies: For patients with low SLFN11 expression, combining cisplatin with agents that induce SLFN11 expression or target SLFN11-regulated genes like FOXL1 or GPC4 could enhance therapeutic efficacy. For example, histone deacetylase inhibitors have been shown to increase SLFN11 expression in some cancer types.

PTPRG-Targeted Interventions: Given PTPRG’s significant association with survival in our analysis, targeting this gene or its downstream pathways could represent a novel therapeutic approach for CRC patients, particularly those receiving cisplatin-based regimens.

Immune Microenvironment Modulation: Our scRNA-seq analysis revealed SLFN11 expression in immune cells and altered immune-cancer cell communication patterns. Therapies that enhance SLFN11 expression in the tumor microenvironment could potentially improve responses to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy.

These findings open avenues for developing targeted therapeutic strategies that exploit the SLFN11-mediated pathways. By understanding the specific roles of these DEGs, it may be possible to design combination therapies that enhance cisplatin efficacy or overcome resistance in SLFN11-deficient tumors. Additionally, investigating the epigenetic and post-transcriptional regulation of these genes could provide deeper insights into the modulation of cisplatin sensitivity and resistance.

Limitations

While our study provides significant insights into the differential gene expression associated with SLFN11 and SLFN11-KO cell lines in the context of cisplatin sensitivity, several limitations must be considered. Firstly, the analysis was conducted on cell lines, which may not fully replicate the complex tumor microenvironment present in vivo. This could limit the direct applicability of our findings to clinical settings. Future studies should validate these findings in patient-derived xenograft models or organoids that better recapitulate the tumor microenvironment.

Secondly, we utilized non-CRC datasets (GSE181094: pediatric sarcoma; GSE179896: ovarian cancer) due to the limited availability of SLFN11 knockout models in CRC. While these datasets provide valuable insights into SLFN11-dependent gene expression changes, the molecular mechanisms identified may not be entirely applicable to CRC. Future studies should establish and analyze SLFN11 knockout models in CRC cell lines such as HCT116 or SW480 to validate our findings in a CRC-specific context.

Thirdly, our study primarily focused on transcriptomic changes, and further investigations are needed to explore the proteomic and metabolic alterations that accompany SLFN11 knockout. Multi-omics approaches, including proteomics and metabolomics, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the biological processes influenced by SLFN11.

Fourthly, while we identified several DEGs linked to cisplatin sensitivity, the functional roles of these genes require validation through experimental studies such as gene knockdown or overexpression assays. Additionally, functional assays such as cisplatin sensitivity tests (IC50 measurements) in SLFN11-proficient versus deficient cells and rescue experiments (re-expressing SLFN11) would establish causality and strengthen our conclusions.

Fifthly, our Mendelian randomization analysis, while providing valuable insights, has limitations related to the selection and validation of instrumental variables. Although we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings, future studies should validate these results in independent cohorts and through functional experiments.

Finally, the dataset used in this study represents a specific cancer type, and the results may vary across different cancers. Thus, broader studies involving multiple cancer types and patient-derived samples are necessary to generalize our findings. Additionally, the limited sample size of platinum-treated patients in our analysis of TCGA data may have reduced our statistical power to detect significant associations between SLFN11 expression and treatment outcomes.

We acknowledge these limitations and emphasize that our study should be considered as a foundation for future investigations that address these constraints through experimental validation and more comprehensive analyses.

Conclusion

Our study delved into the impact of SLFN11 knockout on gene expression in both ES8 Ewing’s sarcoma and DU145 prostate cancer cell lines, particularly in the context of cisplatin sensitivity in colorectal cancer. Through comprehensive transcriptomic analysis, we identified several differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that are significantly associated with SLFN11 function and cisplatin response. Notably, genes such as FOXL1, CA8, SLC15A3, and GPC4 exhibited marked dysregulation, suggesting their potential roles in mediating the cellular response to cisplatin. These findings highlight the critical involvement of SLFN11 in maintaining cellular homeostasis and influencing drug sensitivity, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target.

Our analysis also revealed that PTPRG expression significantly correlates with survival in colon adenocarcinoma patients, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker. Additionally, single-cell RNA sequencing demonstrated high expression of SLFN11 in immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, particularly in granulocyte-monocyte progenitor and common myeloid progenitor cells, with reduced expression in cancerous tissues. These findings suggest a complex interplay between SLFN11, immune regulation, and cancer progression.

The insights gained from this study have significant implications for personalized cancer therapy. By understanding the molecular mechanisms through which SLFN11 influences cisplatin sensitivity, clinicians may be able to better predict which CRC patients will respond to platinum-based therapies. This could lead to more tailored treatment approaches, potentially improving response rates and outcomes for patients with CRC. Furthermore, the identification of genes like PTPRG and BCHE, which showed high diagnostic value, may contribute to the development of novel biomarkers for early detection and monitoring of CRC.

Looking forward, these findings open avenues for developing targeted therapeutic strategies that exploit SLFN11-mediated pathways. Future research should focus on validating these findings through functional studies and exploring combination therapies that enhance cisplatin efficacy or overcome resistance in SLFN11-deficient tumors. Additionally, investigating the epigenetic and post-transcriptional regulation of these genes could provide deeper insights into the modulation of cisplatin sensitivity and resistance, potentially leading to innovative treatment approaches for CRC patients.
