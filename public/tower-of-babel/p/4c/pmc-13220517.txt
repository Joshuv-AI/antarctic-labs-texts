Morphological brain changes in high-altitude careers and spaceflights: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mohamed I Mohamed, Yomna E Dean, Shree Rath, Atta Ur Rehman, Muhammad Ali Aleem, Adham Hamza, Nasser A Abdelall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimOccupational exposure to high-altitude environments, such as those experienced by pilots and astronauts, has been associated with unique challenges, including neurocognitive implications. White matter hyperintensities (WMH) detected through neuroimaging have emerged as potential markers of brain pathology in individuals with high-altitude careers. This meta-analysis aimed to explore the association between occupational high-altitude exposure and WMH burden.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted following PRISMA guidelines in databases such as PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane. Studies reporting WMH in pilots were included without language or date restrictions. Data extraction and quality assessment were performed independently by two reviewers. Pooled analyses were conducted using R software, and the risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale.ResultsA total of 1,036 articles were screened, with five studies meeting the inclusion criteria. These studies included 441 individuals, comprising 264 pilots and 177 controls. Pilots exhibited a significantly higher mean number (MD: 3.57, 95% CI: 1.71 — 5.43, p-value = 0.0002) and volume (MD: 0.09, 95% CI: 0.04 — 0.14, p-value = 0.0001) of WMH compared to non-flying personnel. No heterogeneity was observed in the analyses.ConclusionOccupational exposure to high altitudes is associated with a higher prevalence and volume of WMH, highlighting the possible need for regular screening and monitoring of individuals in high-altitude careers to mitigate the risk of cerebral diseases, and their implications. This meta-analysis underscores the importance of addressing the neurocognitive consequences of high-altitude professions in aviation and space exploration industries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04272-8.Keywords: Occupational high-altitude exposure, White matter hyperintensities, Pilots

Introduction

High-altitude exposure triggers numerous adaptational changes in the cells of the human body. The brain undergoes unique and extensive dynamic changes during hypoxic high-altitude exposure [1]. Commercialization of the aviation and space exploration sector has ensured the accessibility of people to the idea of ‘vacation in space’ rather than the conventional explorations taken to conduct scientific research. This commercial sector is projected to grow nearly 41% over the next five years [2]. With the booming aviation and space exploration industries, more individuals are being exposed to environments where hypoxic conditions, hypobaric stress, or microgravity may occur — although the physiological exposures and risks differ between aviation and spaceflight settings. One of the most common challenges that manned space flight missions face today is the safety of aircraft pilots and astronauts. This is elucidated by a study by Clement GR et al., 2020 [3], which reviews the various neurological changes documented in humans and test animals and hypothesizes regarding the challenges that lay ahead in ensuring the safety of the astronauts for future planetary exploration. There is a significant and potentially irreversible impact on these professionals’ physical and mental well-being. Cognitive dysfunction, abnormal sleep-wake cycle, psychological implications, and morphological brain changes (such as decrease in thalamic size, pituitary gland deformation, or thinning of the occipital lobe) are a few of the many associated risks of space exploration [4].

The primary driver triggering different morphological brain changes in high-altitude exposure is hypoxia [5]. This can cause significant neuropsychological impairment, as hypoxia has been implicated in causing cognitive dysfunctions, such as memory, mood, and reasoning [6]. Studies have analyzed modifications in the brains of residents of high-altitude locations that show decreased gray matter volume in some regions of the brain, accompanied by changes in white matter volume as well [7, 8].

White matter hyperintensities (WMH) are abnormal brain lesions detected by neuroimaging modalities. The presence of these lesions is known to be associated with an increased risk of neurodegenerative disorders and mood disorders [9]. Common factors involved in pathogenesis include hypoxic-ischemic injury, vascular abnormalities, and inflammation-associated tissue damage. All these factors lead to axonal damage, hence forming hyper-intense lesions in white matter [10]. The appearance of WMH is often indicative of aging and chronic ischemia. However, aging-independent formations of WMH are also being found, pointing to the involvement of complex etiopathogenesis. A study conducted on pilots of similar age groups found an increased number of lesions corresponding to the time of flight [11]. Previous studies have also noted an increase in WMH on exposure to microgravity in astronauts [12].

High-altitude flights are typically defined as flights reaching altitudes above 10,000 feet, where supplemental oxygen or cabin pressurization becomes necessary to prevent hypoxic injury [13]. In contrast, spaceflights can be suborbital (brief exposures with pressurized cabins), orbital (e.g., International Space Station missions with stable cabin pressure near 1 atmosphere), or deep-space missions (still largely speculative) [14]. Notably, astronauts may experience hypobaric hypoxia during specific extra-vehicular activities despite overall cabin pressurization [15].

While hypoxia is a central concern in high-altitude physiology, it is generally considered an undesired and carefully mitigated condition during aviation and space operations [16]. Pilots, especially in military and high-altitude reconnaissance flights, may experience hypoxic stress during unpressurized or partially pressurized flights, or in cases of cabin pressure failure [17]. Astronauts typically operate in well-pressurized environments; however, during extra-vehicular activities, hypobaric conditions may temporarily expose them to reduced oxygen partial pressures [18].

It is important to distinguish among the different environments discussed in this review. High-altitude terrestrial exposure refers to conditions encountered by individuals such as mountaineers, where low atmospheric pressure leads to hypoxia without environmental controls. High-altitude terrestrial exposure goes beyond the scope of this review. Aviation exposure involves pilots and crew who, despite generally flying in pressurized cabins, may still face hypoxic stress, particularly in military or high-altitude operations. Spaceflight exposure subjects astronauts to combined effects of hypobaria, hypoxia, microgravity, and fluid shifts; presenting unique challenges distinct from terrestrial or aviation exposures. While hypoxia is a common factor across these environments, the physiological stressors and mechanisms of injury differ considerably.

Given the distinct environmental exposures across aviation and spaceflight [14], understanding the associated neurological impacts - particularly the development of WMHs- is critical. These lesions are known markers of hypoxic-ischemic injury, vascular compromise, and inflammatory damage [19], all of which may be exacerbated under conditions of hypoxia, hypobaria, or microgravity [20].

The existing literature on WMH in high-altitude professions is very limited, with a lack of proper associative risk factors of these morphological brain changes in pilots and astronauts. This meta-analysis aimed to explore the association between occupational high-altitude exposure and WMH burden.

Methods

This paper is registered on Prospero (CRD42023487668) and follows the regulations of PRISMA [21].

Search strategy

A literature search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane was conducted on the 29th of November, 2023, using keywords such as “Hyperintense white matter” and “pilot” to identify the studies of interest. (View supplementary material for the full search strategy)

Study selection

Each study was screened according to the following criteria.

Inclusion criteria

Case-control and longitudinal cohorts of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based studies reporting on white matter hyperintensities in pilots, astronauts, and altitude chamber instructors with or without a control group comprised of healthy non-flying personnel. No language or date restrictions were applied. All those with high-altitude careers with at least one exposure to altitudes greater than 3048 m (10,000 feet) as part of their occupation were included in the analysis.

Exclusion criteria

Editorials, reviews, commentaries, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, case series, and animal studies. Studies reporting on individuals with permanent residence at high-altitude mountainous regions were excluded.

At least two independent reviewers screened the studies according to the criteria listed above. In case of conflict, a third independent reviewer was consulted to resolve the conflict.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Each study was extracted by two reviewers independently. The data was then compared to confirm accuracy. If a consensus was not reached, a third independent reviewer was consulted to resolve the conflict.

For the baseline and summary, the following data were extracted from the eligible studies: the last name of the first author, the year of publication, study design, country, sample size, age, type of imaging utilized, flight hours, altitude, prevalence of smoking, and the conclusion.

For the outcomes, we extracted the following: the number and volume of WMH in pilots and non-flying personnel.

The risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) which has a total score of nine points to evaluate the quality of the studies. We defined the studies with a score of seven points or more as a high-quality study and those with a score of less than seven points as low quality [22].

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using R software. Sensitivity analyses were conducted in the form of leave-one-out test and subgroup analyses. If no heterogeneity was observed, results were presented in a fixed effect model and a random effect model if significant heterogeneity was observed. A mean difference (MD) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was to represent continuous data. Results were considered significant if the p-value was less than 0.05.

Results

A search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane yielded 1,565 articles, which later became 1,035 after removing duplicates. 1,035 studies were eligible for the title and abstract screening; 1,009 were deemed irrelevant, and 26 articles underwent full-text screening. Lastly, five studies [11, 23–26] were included in the analysis as shown in the PRISMA diagram in Fig. 1.

Outcomes

Number of white matter hyperintensities

According to our analysis, pilots and astronauts had a mean number of 6.97 WMH (Fig. 2). We further compared the mean number of WMH in pilots and astronauts to non-flying personnel; pilots had a significantly higher number of WMH (MD: 3.57, 95% CI: 1.71 — 5.43, p-value = 0.0002). No heterogeneity was observed in the analysis. (p-value = 0.66, I2 = 0%) Fig. 3.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, we presented herein the first pooled analysis correlating occupations characterized by repetitive high-altitude exposure with significantly greater numbers and volumes of WMHs.

Literature reveals that occupation-related dysbarism may predispose individuals to WMHs. Connolly’s 2015 meta-analysis [27] reported that divers with repeated hyperbaric exposure (mean years of diving ranged between 4.3 years and 28 years, mean time of diving ranged between 857 h and 9700 h, mean diving depth ranged between 13 and 78 m) are almost two times more likely to exhibit WMHs when compared to control subjects (95% CI 1.088 to 3.405). McGuire et al. [28] concluded that occupational non-hypoxic hypobaric exposures may induce WMHs in seemingly healthy subjects without apparent clinical manifestations. Interestingly, they observed no statistically significant relationship between the WMH burden and the duration of hypobaric exposure [28]. This prompts the need for further exploration of the causative factors of increased WMHs post-hypobaric exposure. Shu et al. [29] reported an increased WMH severity (odds ratio for severe WMHs: 4.083; 95% CI, 2.496–6.680) in patients with cerebral small-vessel disease (CSVD) residing at higher altitudes (Tibet Autonomous Region, altitudes 3000–5000 m) as compared to those at lower altitudes (Beijing, altitudes 0–50 m). This suggests prolonged exposure to hypobaric environments with no accompanying occupational exposure may also increase WMH counts. As interesting as this finding may seem, one must not extrapolate the significantly increased WMHs in CSVD patients residing permanently in high altitudes to astronauts and pilots who are subjected to brief – albeit recurrent - high-altitude environments. It is worth noting that exposure to hypoxia in normobaric environments might also heighten the number and volume of WMHs among mountaineers, as revealed by Sönksen et al. [30]

The pathophysiological process linking higher altitudes with increased WMHs is not well-established. One hypothesis states that intracellular ischemia may activate a neuroinflammatory response which - over repetitive exposures - may upregulate the innate immune system resulting in fluid shifts appearing as WMHs [28]. Another hypothesis suggests that hypobaric exposure may produce microemboli in the blood that get distributed in the cerebral arteries and manifest as WMHs [11, 28]. According to the “bubbling theory”, hypobaria leads to the formation of tiny gas microbubbles (less than 30 μm in diameter), disrupting blood-gas exchange at the alveolar level and triggering subsequent metabolic consequences. This theory suggests that these microbubbles are the primary, if not sole, cause of arteriolar damage following high-altitude exposure [31]. In the first analysis of large mammalian brains (swine brains), Iacono et al. illustrated how exposure to non-hypoxic hypobaric environments is associated with significantly greater ptau accumulation, neuroinflammatory reactions mediated by astrocytes and microglial cells, and reduced myelination levels in subcortical areas. This neuropathologic evidence confirmed how hypobaria without hypoxia can trigger neurodegenerative process, albeit without testing for possible cognitive-behavioural disturbances triggered by those brain morphological changes [32].

A high WMH burden can be associated with reduced cognitive functions and dementia [33]. McGuire et al. [34] demonstrated that U-2 pilots with repetitive hypobaric exposure and a high WMH burden had lower scores in reasoning/calculation, memory, information processing accuracy, general cognitive proficiency, and general cognitive processing on the MicroCog assessment compared to U2 pilots with a lower WMH burden. Importantly, despite these statistical differences, U2 pilots overall still performed within the expected range for their age and peer group, suggesting that the observed cognitive differences are not immediately clinically significant. However, when comparing U2 pilots to other USAF pilots flying different aircraft, U2 pilots showed lower MicroCog scores in reasoning/calculation, memory, information processing accuracy, and overall cognitive function. Clancy et al. [35] - in their 2024 meta-analysis - revealed a 49% prevalence of CSVD in individuals with high-altitude careers. WMHs are an established cardinal feature in patients with CSVD [36]. Whether or not occupational subjection to hypobaria and the subsequent WMH burden is associated with the development of CSVD has not been established yet. Further research may have to be conducted to assess this relationship. Lastly, Debette and Markus - in their 2010 meta-analysis - noted the increased risk of strokes, dementia, and mortality among those with greater WMH volumes [37]. Their meta-analysis, however, did not include a separate analysis on those with occupational high-altitude exposure. Whether or not these clinical correlations with greater WMHs burden are generalizable to astronauts and pilots remain poorly explored.

As delineated in Arone et al.’s 2021 narrative review [4], astronauts undergo a set of brain morphological changes not limited to WMHs. These include increased brain and CSF volumes, expansion of the lateral ventricle, structural and functional changes in the vestibular system, among others. Although astronauts represented a minority (3.5%) of our pooled cohort, the neurophysiological stressors they encounter - including exposure to hypobaria during extravehicular activities, chronic fluid shifts due to microgravity, and potential oxidative stress - may independently or synergistically contribute to WMH formation. Previous studies, such as those by Roberts et al. [38], identified an upward shift of the brain, narrowing of the central sulcus, and narrowing of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) spaces at the vertex in astronauts following long-duration spaceflights, suggesting that spaceflight alone (even without traditional hypobaric exposure) can impact brain morphology. However, dedicated analyses focusing exclusively on spaceflight-associated WMH burden remain limited, warranting further investigation.

Despite differences in flight dynamics, both high-altitude aviators and astronauts experience environmental conditions characterized by repetitive exposure to hypobaria, intermittent hypoxia, oxidative stress, and cerebrovascular fluid shifts [38–40]. These shared physiological stressors justify evaluating both populations together when assessing WMH burden, particularly since the underlying pathophysiology - neuroinflammation, vascular injury, and demyelination [32, 41] - may be common across these environments.

This meta-analysis provides evidence supporting a correlation between occupational high-altitude exposure and increased WMH burden. Hypobaric environments may contribute to the pathogenesis of WMH through mechanisms such as hypoxia-induced neuroinflammation or microemboli formation. The implications of elevated WMH burden include cognitive decline and dementia. Further research is needed to delineate the causal pathways/pathogenesis and potential preventive and therapy measures. Additionally, whether or not these WMHs may resolve spontaneously on the long-term following non-exposure to high-altitude remains underexplored. Thus, we recommend conducting MRI-based longitudinal studies periodically examining the evolution of WMHs in pilots and astronauts with repetitive exposures to high-altitude and following retirement to understand the course of WMHs over the years.

Strength and limitations

This is the first meta-analysis conducted on this topic, which we believe is crucial due to the impact of WMH on the brain and the increased popularity of spaceflights. Moreover, we strengthened our analysis by including a control group for comparison, and no heterogeneity was observed in any of our analyses. Due to the lack of data and published studies, we faced some limitations; first, the total sample size was limited to 441 individuals. Second, we could not stratify and analyze the effect of different high altitudes and durations on the development of WMH. More studies are warranted to explore the impact of these confounding factors.

Conclusion

Our meta-analytic findings contribute to the growing body of evidence suggesting that occupational exposure to hypobaric environments, such as in high-altitude pilots, is associated with an increased burden of WMHs. Although cognitive or functional outcomes were not assessed in this analysis, WMHs have been linked to cognitive impairment, stroke, and mortality in broader populations [33, 37]. Therefore, individuals with repeated high-altitude exposure may be at increased risk for related morbidities. However, the direct clinical implications for these populations remain uncertain.

While this study focused on high-altitude exposure and did not directly assess spaceflight professionals, similar physiological stressors — including hypoxia, microgravity-induced fluid shifts, and other environmental factors — suggest that WMH development may also be relevant to astronauts. However, this remains speculative and highlights the need for targeted research evaluating brain health in spaceflight populations – especially given how astronauts constituted only 3.5% of our sample size.

Future prospective and longitudinal studies are warranted to better characterize the cognitive and cerebrovascular risks associated with increased WMH burden in both high-altitude and spaceflight-exposed professionals. Additionally, future longitudinal studies in astronauts and aviators should specifically incorporate WMH assessments to determine whether these microstructural changes precede, accompany, or follow the larger macroscopic brain alterations observed after repetitive high-altitude or spaceflight exposure.
