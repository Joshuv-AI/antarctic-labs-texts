From knowledge to action: Why midwifery-led counseling is the missing link in antenatal exercise adherence
Authors: Susilawati
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The article by Alhajiri et al. (2025), which examines the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of antenatal exercise among pregnant women in KSA, provides a significant empirical contribution to the development of modern obstetrics. The finding that only 15.5 percent of respondents adhered to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommendations confirms a significant gap between maternal health knowledge, attitudes, and practices, even though the majority of respondents demonstrated relatively good knowledge and attitudes toward physical exercise during pregnancy.1 From a midwifery perspective, these results highlight a fundamental issue in antenatal care practice: that knowledge transfer alone is insufficient to drive behavior change. The logistic regression analysis, which showed attitude as the most consistent predictor of adherence, conveys an important message: the role of midwives needs to go beyond informative education to a transformative counseling approach oriented toward changing the meaning of health for pregnant women. Global literature consistently demonstrates that midwives, through ongoing therapeutic relationships, are strategically positioned to influence women's attitudes, motivation, and self-confidence during pregnancy.2,3

Furthermore, the finding that most women obtain antenatal exercise information from social media, while the proportion of counseling from health professionals is relatively low, has serious implications for the midwifery care system. This situation indicates that evidence-based health messages are potentially overshadowed by information that is not scientifically curated. In this context, midwives need to be positioned as primary reference sources who not only convey ACOG recommendations but also help mothers interpret, personalize, and integrate antenatal exercise into their daily lives. Evidence from international studies indicates that physical exercise during pregnancy contributes to a reduced risk of gestational diabetes, operative delivery, and faster postpartum recovery.4 However, as Alhajiri et al. (2025) point out, these benefits will not be achieved if adherence remains low. Therefore, strengthening midwives' competencies in behavioral counseling, empathetic communication, and shared decision-making is a crucial agenda in contemporary midwifery education and practice.

Furthermore, the context of the transforming health system in KSA highlighted in this article reinforces the relevance of promotive and preventive approaches in maternity care. A culturally sensitive, community-based midwifery model emphasizing continuity of care has the potential to bridge the gap between positive attitudes and actual practice, particularly in communities with strong family values and social norms.5,6 In closing, the article by Alhajiri et al. (2025) not only presents an empirical overview of antenatal exercise but also serves as a reflective call for the midwifery profession to reposition physical exercise counseling as a core competency in antenatal care. Further research with longitudinal and interventional designs is urgently needed to test the effectiveness of attitude-based midwifery counseling models in improving adherence and maternal health outcomes sustainably.
