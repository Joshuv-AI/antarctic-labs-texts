A Case of Meige Syndrome in a Pediatric Patient With Schizophrenia
Authors: Kosuke Mizobuchi, Hisashi Tanii, Koji Kanai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLong‐term treatment with multiple antipsychotic medications for schizophrenia may increase the risk of developing tardive dystonia, including Meige syndrome. Although clozapine has been suggested as a way of improving dystonia symptoms, there is no established consensus regarding its use in pediatric patients with treatment‐resistant schizophrenia, and careful consideration is required. A 12‐year‐old boy with schizophrenia experienced blepharospasm and oromandibular twitching while undergoing treatment with multiple antipsychotic medications. We stopped giving him the same medications that had been used during his previous hospitalizations (risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole, lurasidone, and paliperidone, paliperidone palmitate (a long‐acting injectable), asenapine, and blonanserin). We then started giving him clozapine, gradually increasing the dose to 125 mg. Following this treatment intervention, the dystonic symptoms resolved completely, and the psychiatric symptoms stabilized.Keywords: clozapine, Meige syndrome, pediatric patient, schizophrenia, tardive dystonia

Introduction

The pathogenesis of tardive dystonia is unclear, but long‐term treatment with antipsychotic medication is known to induce movement disorders, including tardive dystonia. One such disorder is Meige syndrome. In cases of childhood‐onset schizophrenia, the severity of psychiatric symptoms and treatment resistance may necessitate the use of multiple antipsychotic medications over extended periods. This makes selecting an appropriate treatment challenging when movement disorders are also present. We encountered a patient with childhood‐onset schizophrenia who had been treated with multiple antipsychotic medications long term. The patient's response to these medications was inadequate, and long‐term use of drugs with dopamine receptor‐blocking effects led to blepharospasm and oromandibular twitching, resulting in Meige syndrome. It was difficult to adjust the treatment as reducing the patient's antipsychotic doses led to a relapse of psychiatric symptoms. The coexistence of psychiatric symptoms and movement disorders resulted in significant functional impairment. Treatment with clozapine was initiated with careful dose titration. This led to an improvement in the patient's psychiatric symptoms and the remission of Meige syndrome. This report highlights the efficacy of clozapine treatment for childhood‐onset schizophrenia complicated by Meige syndrome induced by antipsychotics.

Case History

The patient was a 12‐year‐old boy who had shown limited interest in others and had had restricted interests since infancy. Around the time he started junior high school, he began laughing emptily and experiencing bizarre delusions of persecution, such as believing that his mother had poisoned his food and that someone had replaced his brain. He was taken to Hospital A, where doctors suspected schizophrenia. Antipsychotic medication was prescribed, but he refused to take it. His symptoms worsened, resulting in his first admission to our hospital in August 2022 (Day 1). At his initial consultation at our hospital, he was 12 years old.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

The patient's symptoms stabilized temporarily following the introduction of risperidone and olanzapine during his initial hospitalization. However, after discharge, poor treatment adherence resulted in the persistence of auditory hallucinations, persecutory delusions, and impulsivity. These symptoms resulted in risky behaviors and elopement while the patient was out with family members, leading to readmission in May 2023 (Day 267). During this second admission, the patient was administered a regimen of multiple antipsychotics, including aripiprazole, lurasidone, olanzapine, and paliperidone, supplemented by paliperidone palmitate (a long‐acting injectable). Despite these efforts, the patient exhibited prominent impulsivity and delusions of persecution during outpatient follow‐up, necessitating a third admission in December 2023 (Day 476). Further adjustments involved switching to or combining asenapine and the blonanserin transdermal patch. Nevertheless, the patient's persecutory delusions, impulsivity, and externalization of blame persisted, resulting in a treatment‐resistant clinical course.

Around 2024, the patient began experiencing increased blinking, persistent blepharospasm, and oromandibular twitching, all of which gradually worsened. Suspecting that the symptoms were medication‐induced side effects, we attempted to reduce the dosage of antipsychotics. However, this proved difficult because it triggered a relapse of impulsivity. In March 2025 (Day 924), the patient underwent a neurological evaluation at another hospital. An MRI of the head and an EEG showed no organic abnormalities. Due to the patient's history of long‐term polypharmacy, we suspected drug‐induced Meige syndrome. Because the patient had treatment‐resistant schizophrenia and antipsychotic‐induced Meige syndrome, we proposed introducing clozapine. The patient was readmitted in May 2025 (Day 994). Upon admission, the patient exhibited prominent auditory hallucinations, delusions of being observed, and impulsivity, along with frequent empty laughter. Dystonic symptoms, particularly severe blinking, were also present. The patient's initial medication regimen included 100 mg of paliperidone palmitate (a long‐acting injectable), 40 mg of blonanserin transdermal patch, and 20 mg of olanzapine.

Laboratory tests upon admission revealed no abnormalities. Paliperidone palmitate was discontinued immediately after the patient's admission, and medication adjustments were made to reduce the dosage of other antipsychotics in preparation for introducing clozapine. On the ninth day of hospitalization, clozapine was initiated at 12.5 mg, and the blonanserin transdermal patch was discontinued. By the 36th day, olanzapine was gradually tapered and discontinued, resulting in progressive improvement and eventual resolution of blepharospasm and oromandibular twitching. Though the patient experienced some behavioral issues due to impulsivity and auditory hallucinations during this medication transition, their symptoms gradually stabilized after clozapine was initiated. Conclusion

This case suggests that the reduction of antipsychotic medications and the introduction of clozapine may contribute to the improvement of treatment‐resistant schizophrenia complicated by Meige syndrome.

Discussion

We present the case of a child with Meige syndrome suspected to be induced by long‐term antipsychotic treatment. The patient's psychiatric symptoms and dystonia improved with clozapine treatment initiation. Before the patient began treatment with clozapine, his severe auditory hallucinations, persecutory delusions, and high impulsivity required the use of multiple antipsychotics. The patient had developed blepharospasm and oromandibular twitching. Treatment with multiple antipsychotic medications provided little improvement for these symptoms. Reducing the dose was difficult because it led to the recurrence of psychiatric symptoms. These symptoms significantly impaired the child's quality of life. Following the introduction of clozapine, the blepharospasm and oromandibular twitching gradually improved, and the psychiatric symptoms stabilized. This eventually allowed the patient to be discharged home.

Meige syndrome is a form of cranial dystonia involving blepharospasm and oromandibular dystonia. It can also involve complex movements of the lower facial, oral, mandibular, lingual, pharyngeal, and/or cervical muscles [1]. The pathogenesis of tardive dystonia remains largely elusive; however, it has been suggested that dopaminergic and acetylcholinergic mechanisms, as well as the regulation of cholinergic pathways by serotonin and norepinephrine, may be involved [2]. Our patient experienced discomfort around the mouth and blepharospasm, which later manifested as facial twitching around the mouth, following long‐term administration of antipsychotics with potent dopamine receptor‐blocking properties. These antipsychotics included paliperidone palmitate (a long‐acting injectable), olanzapine, and the blonanserin transdermal patch. We believe that this prolonged dopamine receptor blockade induced the tardive dystonia observed in this patient.

Wong et al. [3] discussed the efficacy of clozapine in treating tardive dyskinesia and tardive dystonia. Van Putten et al. [4] also reported its effectiveness in cases of tardive dystonia complicating chronic schizophrenia. Compared to other antipsychotics, clozapine has a lower binding affinity for dopamine D2 receptors, and this lower affinity is considered a key factor in improving tardive dystonia. Pattnaik et al. [5] noted that individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) can exhibit idiosyncratic thought processes similar to those observed in individuals with schizophrenia, which highlights the diagnostic challenges in such cases.

Our patient developed pronounced delusions of persecution when he entered junior high school. This led to a psychiatric consultation and a diagnosis of schizophrenia. Despite multiple hospitalizations and treatment with antipsychotic medications, there was minimal improvement in the patient's pathological experiences, and he subsequently developed Meige syndrome. Given the patient's combination of treatment‐resistant psychiatric symptoms and a drug‐induced movement disorder that significantly impaired his quality of life, introducing clozapine was highly significant, as it improved both the psychiatric symptoms and the movement disorder.

Among 9635 patients prescribed clozapine in Japan from July 2009 to January 2020, neutropenia occurred in 5.0% (n = 411). The median time to neutropenia onset was 14.3 (7.3–45.6) weeks, with most cases occurring at a dose of 200 mg (100–350 mg) [6]. Japanese guidelines recommend starting with a clozapine dose of 12.5 mg and gradually increasing by 25–50 mg/day, depending on tolerability, to reach 300–450 mg/day in approximately 2 weeks. This titration schedule quickly reaches the 200 mg (100–350 mg) threshold associated with neutropenia. Since the patient was a child, caution was required regarding the rate of clozapine dose escalation and the oral dosage. The dosage was increased by 12.5 mg at intervals of at least one week, and blood tests were performed weekly. The patient underwent frequent medical examinations to monitor for side effects. The parents were informed about the use of clozapine and gave their consent.

Since the improvement in Meige syndrome occurred after discontinuing previous medications and introducing clozapine, it is difficult to determine the effects of each drug. However, it is noteworthy that reducing the patient's previous antipsychotics decreased dopamine receptor blockade. Meanwhile, clozapine, with its low D2 receptor affinity, allowed for psychiatric stabilization without recurrence of Meige syndrome. Clozapine is less likely to induce extrapyramidal symptoms due to its low D2 receptor occupancy and its unique pharmacological properties, which include anti‐dystonic effects. These characteristics potentially alleviate dystonia induced or exacerbated by other antipsychotics. Thus, we suspect that the improvement in our patient's case was likely due to a synergistic effect between withdrawing from the dose‐dependent side effects of his previous medications and clozapine's specific pharmacological profile.
