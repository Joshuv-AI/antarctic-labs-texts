Thymic Stromal Lymphopoietin–Driven B-Cell Hyperactivation Impairs Intestinal Epithelial Homeostasis Predisposing to Enterocolitis in Hirschsprung Disease
Authors: Shiwei He, Ziyi Zheng, Lingdu Meng, Lin Lin, Zhe Wang, Jie Zhou, Jiaye Lin, Ran Yang, Yang Lin, Yanlei Huang, Zhihong Huang, Yanbing Huang, Yingjian Chen, Lizhi Li, Feng Chen, Rui Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsHirschsprung-associated enterocolitis, a lethal complication of Hirschsprung’s disease—a prevalent congenital disorder with high morbidity—has a poorly understood pathogenesis. We investigated how aberrant immune–stromal crosstalk drives mucosal injury in Hirschsprung’s disease.MethodsIntegrated single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, bulk RNA sequencing, and proteomics were performed using human Hirschsprung’s disease tissues. Validation was conducted using murine Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis (Ednrb−/−) models, flow cytometry, multiplex immunohistochemistry, in vitro B-cell stimulation, epithelial coculture, rescue experiments with the thymic stromal lymphopoietin inhibitor baicalein, and ex vivo intestinal organoid assays.ResultsWe identified significant expansion and hyperactivation of B cells that colocalized with epithelial barrier defects in Hirschsprung’s disease segments. Multiomics analysis revealed an activated phenotype in these naive and memory B cells, characterized by the upregulation of B lymphocyte kinase, B cell linker, and proliferating cell nuclear antigen, which may contribute to impaired epithelial homeostasis. Crucially, we identified profibrotic thymic stromal lymphopoietin+ fibroblasts enriched in Hirschsprung’s disease. Spatial mapping and in vitro assays demonstrated that thymic stromal lymphopoietin potently activated B cells, synergizing with classical stimuli to enhance proliferation. Activated B cells, in turn, secreted proinflammatory factors, such as, ICAM1 and CCL5, which induced epithelial apoptosis, suppressed proliferation, and disrupted tight junctions—effects that were amplified by thymic stromal lymphopoietin costimulation. Activated B cells and epithelial dysfunction were conserved in Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis (Ednrb−/−) mouse models. Coculture and ex vivo organoid experiments confirmed that thymic stromal lymphopoietin–activated B cells directly destabilized epithelial homeostasis; crucially, baicalein treatment rescued epithelial proliferation, attenuated apoptosis, and reduced inflammation-associated gene expression.ConclusionsOur study reveals a novel pathogenic mechanism whereby thymic stromal lymphopoietin instigates pathogenic B-cell hyperactivation, thereby disrupting epithelial integrity and increasing susceptibility to Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis. Inhibition of thymic stromal lymphopoietin signaling with baicalein ameliorates epithelial injury, highlighting this axis as a promising therapeutic target for Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis prevention in patients with Hirschsprung’s disease.

SummaryThis study reveals that thymic stromal lymphopoietin from profibrotic fibroblasts drives B-cell hyperactivation, disrupting epithelial integrity and promoting Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis. Inhibiting this pathway with baicalein rescues epithelial damage, highlighting a promising therapeutic strategy for this life-threatening complication. What You Need to Know

Background

Hirschsprung’s disease, a prevalent congenital aganglionosis affecting 1 in 5000 births, frequently leads to lethal Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis. Pathogenesis remains poorly understood, particularly how aberrant fibroblast/B cell/epithelial crosstalk drives mucosal injury in Hirschsprung’s disease segments.

Impact

Multiomics and functional studies reveal a critical pathogenic axis in which thymic stromal lymphopoietin–secreting fibroblasts hyperactivated B cells, disrupting epithelial homeostasis through proinflammatory mediators. Crucially, the inhibitor baicalein ameliorates this damage, identifying a novel therapeutic target for Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis.

Future Directions

Targeting the thymic stromal lymphopoietin–B-cell axis with baicalein or biologics offers a promising preventive strategy for Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis in patients with Hirschsprung’s disease. Future studies will dissect B-cell subsets, incorporate preoperative risk stratification via mucosal biopsy analysis, and advance clinical trials of thymic stromal lymphopoietin inhibition to improve outcomes.

Hirschsprung’s disease (HSCR) is a common congenital disorder of the intestinal tract affecting approximately 1 in 5000 births and is characterized by the absence of enteric ganglia (aganglionosis).1 HSCR often leads to severe complications, particularly Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis (HAEC), which has preoperative incidence rates ranging from 5.7% to 50% and postoperative incidence rates of 2%–25%.2 HAEC is a major contributor to mortality among infants diagnosed with HSCR.3 Despite this, the pathogenesis of HSCR complicated by HAEC is poorly understood.4

Characteristic disruptions of intestinal epithelium have been reported in the HSCR intestine, including abnormal cellular states in enterocytes,5,6 goblet cells,7, 8, 9 and enteroendocrine cells.10,11 Recently, the role of an abnormal immune microenvironment in disrupting intestinal epithelial homeostasis and increasing vulnerability to HAEC in HSCR has garnered increasing attention.12 The balanced and stable activity of various immune cells, particularly B cells, is crucial for maintaining the stability of the intestinal environment.13 B cells protect the intestines from infection by secreting antibodies to neutralize pathogens and by marking pathogenic microorganisms in the intestinal lumen for elimination.14

On the one hand, abnormal B-cell activation, including abnormal B-cell proliferation, has been observed; however, the underlying mechanism has not been thoroughly validated in various enterocolitides, primarily in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)15, 16, 17 and necrotizing enterocolitis.18 Previous studies on HSCR have roughly estimated the increase in the number of B cells19 and plasma cells20,21 through immunohistochemical (IHC) staining and manual counting. In an HSCR animal model,22 bulk RNA sequencing followed by deconvolution analysis inferred an upregulation of memory B-cell proportions. Nevertheless, there is a need for a clearer definition of the characteristics of B-cell alterations in the HSCR intestine and a need to be defined more clearly at the single-cell level.

On the other hand, although activated B cells interfere with fibroblast–epithelial cell interactions and thereby accelerate the IBD progression, it remains unclear whether activated fibroblasts in intestinal fibrosis affect B cells and epithelial cells. This gap is particularly significant in the context of HSCR and other enterocolitides. However, no relevant studies have yet examined their role in HSCR. B cells can regulate intestinal epithelial cell homeostasis through paracrine mechanisms, thereby interfering with the progression of intestinal inflammation.14,23 Furthermore, Frede et al15 demonstrated that B-cell accumulation impairs repair by hindering stromal–epithelial interactions, and they suggested that paracrine B-cell signaling might directly affect epithelial cells. Consequently, the activation status of B cells within the HSCR intestine directly or indirectly modulates intestinal epithelial cells, necessitating a thorough exploration of the underlying cellular interaction mechanisms.

In our prior research,24 we identified fibrosis within HSCR segments, and hypothesized that the upregulation of proinflammatory factors in activated fibroblasts disrupts the immune microenvironment, ultimately fostering intestinal inflammation. Notably, previous studies25, 26, 27 have shown that activated fibroblasts can influence the immune microenvironment, including B-cell populations. Fibroblast involvement in B-cell development and activation has been observed in IBD,25,28 rheumatoid arthritis,29 and systemic sclerosis.30

In this study, we comprehensively investigated the effects of activated B cells on intestinal epithelial cell homeostasis in HSCR at the single-cell resolution, offering novel insights into the intestinal microenvironment of HSCR and suggesting innovative strategies for prevention and treatment of HAEC. First, we analyzed the characteristics of B-cell activation and epithelial dysregulation in 13 HSCR segments in the pre-enterocolitis state, defined as a subclinical inflammatory phase characterized by localized immune dysregulation without the overt clinical hallmarks of acute HAEC; these samples comprised 5 ganglionic and 8 aganglionic segments and were compared with 29 healthy segments from a public database that were explicitly diagnosed as healthy using the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). A ganglionic HSCR intestine with elevated intraepithelial lymphocytes was compared with a healthy intestine (explicitly diagnosed as healthy) from a public database, and these were analyzed using spatiotemporal transcriptomics, which enabled spatial mapping of cell distribution and gene expression characteristics across tissue sections. Second, bulk RNA sequencing (bulk RNA-seq) data and proteomics data from 15 aganglionic HSCR segments and 15 healthy segments (explicitly diagnosed as healthy) from a public database were utilized to verify the observed alterations; proteomics of 3 HAEC animal model (Ednrb−/− mice, a well-established congenital aganglionosis model that phenocopies HAEC) and 3 wild-type (WT) mice were incorporated to increase the generalizability of our findings. Third, the phenotypes of B-cell activation and epithelial dysregulation were confirmed through reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), flow cytometry, and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC). Finally, in vitro stimulation showed that thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) significantly activated primary B cells and promoted their proliferation, whereas coculture and rescue experiments demonstrated that TSLP-activated B cells markedly disrupted intestinal epithelial homeostasis by suppressing proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and increasing permeability. Baicalein, a naturally occurring flavonoid derived from the root of Scutellaria baicalensis, is a known inhibitor of TSLP signaling; therefore, we selected this compound to investigate whether blocking the TSLP pathway could ameliorate the epithelial damage induced by TSLP-activated B cells. These deleterious effects were substantially attenuated by baicalein treatment and further validated in ex vivo intestinal organoid models, in which baicalein rescued epithelial integrity and reduced inflammation-associated transcriptional dysregulation.

Results

Pathogenic B-Cell Expansion With Enhanced Proliferation Colocalizes to Mucosal Barrier Defects in the Hirschsprung’s Disease Intestine

To systematically investigate disturbances in B-cell homeostasis and the accompanying phenotypic alterations in HSCR, scRNA-seq, spatiotemporal transcriptomics, bulk RNA-seq, and proteomics were conducted in this study. Subsequently, to confirm these phenotypic changes, validation experiments were performed in the HAEC phenotype mice (Ednrb−/− mice, a well-established congenital aganglionosis model that phenocopies HAEC). We conducted scRNA-seq on a total of 13 pre-enterocolitis (subclinical inflammatory phase, characterized by localized immune dysregulation without the overt clinical hallmarks of acute HAEC) intestinal tissue samples from HSCR, comprising 5 ganglionic intestines adjacent to normal intestines and 8 aganglionic intestines, resulting in the acquisition of 92,540 high-quality cells for analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, we incorporated scRNA-seq data (consisting of 72,795 cells) from 29 intestinal tissues of 7 healthy donors who were explicitly diagnosed as healthy and sourced from the Gut Cell Atlas into our analysis. These tissues encompassed 6 cecum samples, 4 ascending colon samples, 6 transverse colon samples, 3 descending colon samples, 7 sigmoid colon samples, and 3 rectum samples (Figure 1A; Supplementary Table 1).

Canonical genes were used to delineate the 15 distinct cell subpopulations, including B cells (n = 24,798 cells), plasma cells (n = 10,354 cells), fibroblasts (n = 15,610 cells), epithelial cells (n = 75,875 cells), and other cells. This classification was pivotal for exploring the characteristics and mechanisms underlying the aberrant alterations observed in B cells (Figure 1B and C). It is noteworthy that the proportion of B cells was significantly elevated within HSCR tissues, particularly in aganglionic segments (Figure 1D and E). This increase was not observed in healthy controls (Figure 1D and E). Analysis of gene set score (focused on cell proliferation gene sets) from scRNA-seq data revealed a marked increase in the proliferative activity of B cells and epithelial cells (Figure 1F).

A panel of classical cell proliferation genes (PCNA, TYMS, FEN1, TOP2A, CKS1B, MKI67, etc) was selected, and their expression levels across different groups were visualized using a heatmap. Notably, the expression of these genes was significantly upregulated in B cells and epithelial cells, with the most prominent increase observed in aganglionic segments (Figure 1G). Furthermore, the relative expression alteration of cell proliferation and activation marker proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) in aganglionic segments is agreement with the findings obtained from the scRNA-seq analysis (Figure 1H).

Spatial transcriptomic analysis of a ganglionic HSCR intestine with intraepithelial lymphocyte elevation, which typically indicates ongoing inflammation within the mucosa,31, 32, 33 revealed significant B-cell infiltration within the mucosal layer, suggesting the disruption of epithelial barrier integrity (Figure 2A and B). Spatial cell-density mapping demonstrated colocalized cellular enrichment at B-cell infiltration sites, indicative of an active proliferative status (Figure 2C). Spatial colocalization analysis via FeaturePlot demonstrated B-cell enrichment adjacent to the epithelial compartment, with coexpression of the proliferation marker PCNA and the B cell marker MS4A1 (Figure 2D). B cells were sparse in healthy control intestinal tissues and rarely colocalized with PCNA (Figure 2E and F). mIHC demonstrated that PCNA is predominantly expressed in the B cells within gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) of the intestinal mucosa and the epithelium within intestinal crypts in the ganglionic and aganglionic segment of HSCR (Figure 2G and H). Notably, the sites of proliferating B cells coincided with abnormalities in the intestinal epithelial mucosal barrier. These results were consistent with previous findings obtained through IHC studies, indicating increased B-cell infiltration in HSCR intestines.19, 20, 21

A previous study15 has established that abnormal proliferation of B cells can hinder intestinal mucosa repair in IBD, suggesting that aberrant proliferation of B cells in HSCR may contribute to the pathogenesis of HAEC. Consequently, our subsequent objective was to further elucidate the alterations in B cells in HSCR intestines and their underlying mechanisms in depth.

Multiomics Integration Reveals B-Cell Hyperactivation and Fibrotic Remodeling in Hirschsprung’s Disease and Hirschsprung-Associated Enterocolitis Mice

To delve deeper into the disruptions in B-cell homeostasis, we integrated data from scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics (ST), bulk RNA-seq, and proteomics to elucidate the status of B cells.

By intersecting the results of differential expression analysis within B-cell subsets, we pinpointed 443 upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Figure 3A). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated DEGs were enriched in Gene Ontology (GO) terms pertinent to B-cell activation and proliferation (Figure 3B and C). A heatmap was employed to visualize the expression levels of classical B-cell activation markers, such as B lymphocyte kinase (BLK) and B cell linker (BLNK), across different groups. Notably, within HSCR tissues, the expression of B-cell activation genes was significantly upregulated in both B cells and plasma cells, with a particularly striking increase observed in aganglionic segments (Figure 3E). Additionally, the changes in the relative expression of B-cell activation markers BLK and BLNK in aganglionic segments were in line with the scRNA-seq findings (Figure 3Fi and ii). Spatial colocalization analysis via FeaturePlot demonstrated B cells with coexpression of the B-cell activation markers BLK (Figure 3Gi), BLNK (Figure 3Gii), and the B cell marker MS4A1. Expression of BLK and BLNK was sparse in healthy control intestinal tissues (Figure 3H). Differential analysis (DA) of extracted B cells revealed significant upregulation of BLK and BLNK in HSCR intestinal tissues compared with healthy controls (Figure 3I). These findings suggest that B cells were activated in inflamed HSCR intestines.

To extrapolate whether B-cell activation signatures are conserved in HAEC, we performed proteomic profiling of the Ednrb−/− HAEC mouse model (Ednrb−/− mice, a well-established congenital aganglionosis model that phenocopies HAEC). When Ednrb−/− mice showed signs of HAEC (abdominal distension, inability to expel feces, lethargy, and weight loss) at postnatal 3 to 4 weeks, inflamed intestinal tissues from homozygous mice were harvested. Analysis of inflamed intestinal segments revealed significant upregulation of B-cell markers with WT controls (Figure 4H). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated pronounced enrichment of B-cell activation pathways (Figure 4I). Additionally, mIHC confirmed proliferative B-cell activity within the mucosal layer of HAEC colonic tissue (Figure 4J). These findings substantiate that B-cell activation may mechanistically contribute to HAEC pathogenesis.

Previous studies35 have established that abnormalities in intestinal epithelial cells in HSCR may be linked to alterations in the immune microenvironment, including the dysfunction of B cells,15 which predisposes individuals to HAEC. Moreover, our team has also previously demonstrated the occurrence of intestinal fibrosis in segments affected by HSCR, highlighting that fibroblast activation can disrupt the intestinal microenvironment.24,36

The multiomics results presented here confirm the presence of an activated B-cell phenotype in intestinal tissues from patients with HSCR, suggesting a potential role of intestinal fibrosis in this process.

Based on the expression profiles of cellular markers, we annotated 17 cell subsets, categorized into 4 types (Figure 5B). Compared with healthy intestinal tissues, HSCR intestinal tissues showed an increased proportion of B cells (Figure 5C). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed the elevated proportion of B cells in the aganglionic segments of HSCR intestines (Figure 5Di and ii).

We further dissected the propensity for aberrant activation and proliferative states across distinct B-cell subpopulations. Gene set scoring for B-cell activation and proliferation signatures revealed the highest scores in naive and memory B-cell subsets (Figure 5E). Compared with healthy controls, these scores were further elevated in B-cell subpopulations from HSCR intestinal tissues (Figure 5F). We applied various cellular composition analysis methods to investigate changes in subpopulation dynamics. Compared with healthy controls, HSCR intestinal tissues exhibited an expansion of naive and memory B cells, alongside a reduction in plasma cell subsets (Figure 5G–I). These findings suggest that B cells in HSCR intestinal tissues are predominantly arrested in a proliferative and activated state during development, with impaired differentiation into plasma cells that execute specific immune functions. This indicates a pathologic proliferation of B cells coupled with defective maturation.

To investigate the development patterns of activated B cells across various segments, we isolated B cell and plasma cell subsets and employed pseudotime cell ordering inference for analysis (Figure 5Ji–iv). Naive B cells can differentiate along 2 routes. On the one hand, upon encountering an antigen, naive B cells are activated and undergo proliferation, leading to their differentiation into memory B cells.37 On the other hand, another portion of these B cells may choose to directly differentiate into plasma cells rather than becoming memory B cells. Once memory B cells encounter the same antigen again, they can rapidly initiate a germinal center reaction, proliferate extensively, and differentiate into antibody-producing plasma cells.38 Compared with healthy segments, more naive B cells differentiate into memory B cells, and memory B cells were more likely to undergo differentiation into plasma cells in HSCR segments (Figure 5Ki–iv). These findings suggested that in the HSCR segments, more naive B cells and memory B cells differentiated into plasma cells at an earlier stage.

Previous studies39,40 have shown that abnormal proliferation and differentiation of B cells can interfere with intestinal mucosal repair and promote the progression of enteritis. After verifying the phenotype of B-cell activation in the HSCR intestine, we aimed to investigate whether fibroblasts within the intestinal fibrosis are involved in regulating B-cell behavior.

Through systematic literature screening, we identified the TSLP gene as a classic regulatory factor promoting the proliferation and activation of B cells. Previous studies41,42 have already established the role of TSLP in activating B cells. Visual analysis of scRNA-seq data suggests that TSLP is primarily expressed in stromal cell subpopulations, such as fibroblasts and myofibroblasts (Figure 6C). The cell subpopulations with high TSLP expression were identified and named as TSLP+ fibroblasts. An analysis of the upregulated DEGs unveiled a notable enrichment of proinflammatory factors, encompassing TGFBI, CXCL14, and MMP3, etc, within TSLP-positive fibroblasts (Figure 6D). After stratifying fibroblasts into TSLP-high and TSLP-low subpopulations according to their TSLP expression levels, we detected a higher expression of proinflammatory factors in the TSLP-high subset than in the TSLP-low subset (Figure 6E and F). Subsequent cell type–specific DA was conducted, and highly expressed, top upregulated DEGs with high expression were extracted for functional enrichment analysis. The results indicated that TSLP+ fibroblasts regulate B-cell development by modulating inflammatory responses and secreting proinflammatory cytokines (Figure 6G).

Spatial colocalization analysis via FeaturePlot demonstrated spatial proximity expression between TSLP expression and B-cell marker MS4A1 (Figure 6H), whereas the TSLP expression was minimal in the healthy control group (Figure 6I). mIHC showed that TSLP is mainly expressed in the fibroblast surrounding GALT of the intestinal mucosa. TSLP+ fibroblasts encapsulated B cells, and the secreted TSLP infiltrated the B-cell compartment (Figure 6J). In accordance with the findings from the scRNA-seq analysis, the alteration in the relative expression of TSLP within the aganglionic segments of HSCR was validated (Figure 6K). TSLP expression was significantly elevated in both aganglionic and ganglionic HSCR segments compared with healthy controls (explicitly diagnosed as healthy) at the single-cell level (Figure 6L). This trend was also validated at the bulk transcriptomic level in aganglionic HSCR segments compared with matched controls (Figure 6M).

Although TSLP+ fibroblasts may contribute to B-cell activation, their specific functions require further elucidation through targeted in vitro experiments.

The viability of B cells under distinct stimulation conditions was assessed via CCK-8 assay following 72 hours of culture (Figure 7B). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interleukin (IL)4 are classical B-cell activators that play an important role in the normal growth of primary B cells. Compared with the negative control (neg; n = 5), TSLP-only intervention (TSLP; n = 5) induced a modest but nonsignificant increase in B-cell proliferation (P > .05). In contrast, classical B-cell activation (activate; n = 5) alone substantially enhanced proliferation relative to the control (P < .001). Notably, costimulation with TSLP and the classical activator (activate + TSLP; n = 5) demonstrated a synergistic effect, resulting in a statistically significant further elevation of proliferation compared with the classical activator group (P < .01). The impact of TSLP stimulation on B-cell activation marker PCNA was also evaluated via Western blot (Figure 7C). Costimulation with TSLP and the classical activator synergistically elevated PCNA expression beyond levels observed in the classical activator group (P < .0001). These findings suggest that TSLP amplifies B-cell responses when combined with conventional activation signals.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis of costimulation with TSLP and the classical activator B cells (n = 3) vs B cells treated with the classical activator alone (n = 3) revealed significant transcriptional reprogramming, as illustrated by the enriched GO terms in upregulated DEGs (Figure 7D). The upregulated DEGs were linked to B-cell activation and epithelial cell dysfunction. The GSEA plot demonstrated significant enrichment of the B-cell activation gene set in the TSLP costimulation group, indicating that TSLP amplifies transcriptional programs associated with effector differentiation (Figure 7E). A heatmap visualizing expression patterns of 20 curated B-cell activation markers across experimental groups (activate, n = 3; activate + TSLP, n = 3) revealed distinct clustering patterns (Figure 7F). Although classical activation alone induced moderate expression changes, costimulation with TSLP elicited a marked upregulation of genes critical for B-cell activation. Bulk RNA-seq analysis suggested that TSLP signaling simultaneously promotes immunostimulatory programs while perturbing epithelial homeostasis-related processes in B cells.

Notably, a positive correlation was observed between the expression levels of fibroblast activation markers and TSLP in fibroblasts and the expression of B-cell activation markers in B cells (Figure 7G). We also conducted in vitro experiments where primary B cells were stimulated with TSLP and found that TSLP significantly activated B cells and promoted their proliferation (Figure 7Hi). Further cell–cell interaction analysis revealed that TSLP can function as a ligand to regulate the expression of target genes in downstream B cells, including proliferation markers such as PCNA and MKI67, as well as B-cell activation markers like BLK and BLNK (Figure 7Hii).

After exploring the upstream cellular subpopulations that were potentially involved in regulation, we also focused on abnormal changes in epithelial cells and the role that B cells play in this context.

We delved into the upregulated DEGs within the epithelium, encompassing both absorptive and secretory cells, in the aganglionic intestines of HSCR. Moreover, the integrity of the mucosal barrier is pivotal for the normal functioning of the intestinal wall and for safeguarding against the infiltration of intestinal contents.45,46 The observed decrease in its expression suggests impairment of the intestinal mucosal barrier function.

After identifying the dysregulation of the intestinal epithelium, we endeavored to explore the mechanisms through which B cells impact the intestinal epithelium and to identify the regulatory factors involved.

Inhibition of TSLP by Baicalein Rescues B-Cell–Activated Epithelial Dysfunction

To investigate whether blocking the TSLP signaling pathway could ameliorate the epithelial damage caused by TSLP-activated B cells, we introduced baicalein, a known TSLP inhibitor, into our coculture system (Figure 11A). We sought to confirm the direct interaction between baicalein and the TSLP signaling complex. Molecular docking simulations predicted a potential binding interaction between baicalein and the TSLP-receptor complex, suggesting a mechanistic basis for its inhibitory effect (Figure 11B).

To establish the optimal therapeutic window, we first assessed baicalein cytotoxicity using CCK-8 assays. B cells stimulated with the classical activator and TSLP were treated with gradient concentrations of baicalein (10 μM, 20 μM, 30 μM, and 40 μM) for 72 hours. Notably, baicalein at 20 μM exhibited minimal cytotoxicity across all tested concentrations, with cell viability comparable to untreated controls (Figure 11C) (n = 5; P < .001), indicating good biocompatibility for subsequent functional studies.

We next evaluated whether baicalein could rescue epithelial damage induced by TSLP-activated B cells using our established coculture system (Figure 11A). Bright-field microscopy revealed that epithelial cells cocultured with TSLP-activated B cells (activate + TSLP) displayed morphologic disruption, characterized by reduced cell density, and irregular cell boundaries compared with non-cocultured controls or classical activation alone (Figure 11D).

Functional validation using CCK-8 proliferation assays confirmed these morphologic observations. Although TSLP-activated B cells significantly suppressed epithelial proliferation, consistent with Figure 10B, baicalein treatment reversed this effect, with the 20-μM concentration restoring proliferation to levels comparable to those in the classical activation group (Figure 11E) (n = 5; P < .001). The increased epithelial barrier permeability induced by B-cell stimulation, as measured by the FITC-dextran assay, was not significantly reversed upon baicalein treatment (Figure 11F) (n = 3; P > .05).

TUNEL immunofluorescence staining and quantification revealed that TSLP-activated B cells significantly promoted epithelial apoptosis (Figure 11G and H (activate + TSLP vs Neg, n = 3; P < .001), consistent with the BAX upregulation observed in Figure 10C and D. Baicalein treatment markedly reduced the number of TUNEL-positive cells, with the 20-μM concentration achieving statistical significance compared with the TSLP costimulation group (Figure 11G and H) (n = 3; P < .001), indicating potent antiapoptotic effects.

To validate these findings in a more physiologically relevant context, we employed intestinal organoid models (Figure 11I). Bright-field imaging of organoids cocultured with TSLP-activated B cells revealed progressive structural deterioration over 72 hours, including core darkening, and structural collapse—hallmarks of epithelial stress and apoptosis (Figure 11J). RT-qPCR analysis of epithelial disorder markers showed that TSLP-activated B cells significantly upregulated genes associated with intestinal inflammation and epithelial disorders, whereas baicalein treatment attenuated these transcriptional changes (Figure 11K) (n = 3; P < .05). The quantitative morphologic assessment revealed that the trend in mean diameter aligned with the impact of B cells on the epithelium, yet no statistically significant difference was observed (Figure 11L) (P > .05). Temporal Sankey analysis tracking organoid state transitions from 0 hours to 72 hours illustrated that TSLP-activated B cells drove progression toward apoptotic and disintegrated states, whereas baicalein intervention shifted the trajectory toward preserved or recovering morphologies (Figure 11M).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that baicalein effectively attenuates TSLP-activated B-cell–induced epithelial damage through a functional rescue of epithelial proliferation and reduction of apoptosis. This positions baicalein as a potential lead compound for therapeutic intervention in HSCR-associated mucosal injury, targeting the fibroblast–TSLP–B cell–epithelial pathogenic axis. These findings raise an intriguing hypothesis that B-cell activation may not follow a simple linear deleterious trajectory during acute epithelial injury. Instead, there may exist a critical dose–response relationship wherein the “right amount" of B-cell activation is required to trigger a protective, rather than a destructive, compensatory response within the immune–stromal crosstalk. This potential biphasic mechanism suggests that targeted pharmacologic therapies might function not through a blunt, total suppression of B cells, but potentially by titrating their activation kinetics back into a homeostatic window to preserve barrier components.

This study provides systematic evidence for understanding the predisposition of patients with HSCR to developing HAEC both pre- and postoperatively due to abnormal B-cell activity. Consequently, it provides new theoretical support for the future prevention and treatment of HAEC and other enterocolitides.

Discussion

The activated B cells interfere with fibroblast-epithelial cell interactions, thereby accelerating the enterocolitis in the inflamed intestine of inflammatory bowel disease15; yet, it remains unclear whether activated fibroblasts in intestinal fibrosis affect B cells and epithelial cells. Patients with HSCR are predisposed to developing enterocolitis both before and after surgery, which significantly affects prognosis and quality of life. To date, the precise etiology and pathogenesis of this condition remain elusive.

Our findings reveal a novel pathogenic axis in which fibroblast-derived TSLP drives aberrant B-cell activation, ultimately leading to epithelial barrier dysfunction and predisposition of HAEC. By integrating multiomics profiling (scRNA-seq, ST, and proteomics) with functional validation across human tissues and murine models, we provide mechanistic insights into HAEC pathogenesis that may bridge gaps in understanding stromal–immune–epithelial crosstalk in HSCR.

Although previous histopathologic studies have identified B-cell enrichment in HSCR,19, 20, 21 our spatial and single-cell profiling provides a mechanistic bridge, linking these immune alterations to the underlying stromal fibrosis. We demonstrate that the B-cell expansion in the HSCR segment is not merely a numerical increase but rather a functional reprogramming characterized by a shift toward a proinflammatory state (upregulation of B-cell activation markers, including BLK49,50 and BLNK51, 52, 53). Notably, B-cell proliferation is also evident in the inflamed intestines of patients with IBD15, 16, 17,54 and patients with necrotizing enterocolitis,18,55 where it interferes with intestinal mucosa healing and thereby promotes inflammation. Crucially, the spatial colocalization of TSLP+ fibroblasts with these activated B cells within the GALT of HSCR intestines suggests that the fibrotic microenvironment, potentially triggered by the mechanical strain of chronic intestinal stasis, acts as a localized signaling hub. Furthermore, TSLP, a well-established regulatory factor for B-cell activation, directly promotes B-cell proliferation and activation. Vosshenrich et al validated the ability of TSLP to support the proliferation of fetal or adult precursor B cells in mice.42 Lu et al found that elevated TSLP promoted B-cell proliferation in IgG4-related disease.56 Domeier found that TSLP signaling regulates the germinal center (including B cells) and the magnitude of antigen-specific antibody responses.41 This TSLP-driven “niche” likely provides the necessary costimulatory signals to sustain B cell activation, paralleling mechanisms observed in other chronic inflammatory states such as systemic sclerosis,57 asthma,58,59 and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis,60 yet remaining distinct in its stromal-driven origin within the HSCR context.

The transition of B cells into potent paracrine effectors marks a critical step in the pathogenesis of HAEC. Our findings—particularly the correlation between B-cell–derived mediators and markers of epithelial apoptosis—support a model in which immune–epithelial crosstalk directly compromises the mucosal barrier. A range of proinflammatory factors, including ICAM1,61,62 CCL5,63 and CXCL5,64 which interfere with epithelial cells, were highly expressed in B cells and plasma cells. Cell–cell communication analysis and differential gene expression analysis identified upregulated genes, including classical enteritis markers, such as, TGFB1, TNF, and IL1B in epithelial cells of HSCR. By suppressing epithelial proliferation and inducing cell death and inflammation, this TSLP–B-cell axis effectively lowers the threshold for inflammatory escalation. We propose that this persistent barrier vulnerability, sustained by activated stromal–immune niches, provides a permissive environment for the explosive inflammation seen in HAEC upon any subsequent physiological triggers.

Beyond the descriptive cellular census, our study identifies a sophisticated immune dysregulation pathway mediated by the activated stromal microenvironment in the HSCR intestine. We propose that the transition of fibroblasts toward a “proinflammatory TSLP-secreting” phenotype is not an isolated event but rather a reactive adaptation to the unique pathologic niche of the HSCR segment. In HSCR, the failure of coordinated peristalsis leads to chronic intestinal stasis, which imposes persistent mechanical wall tension and alters the local chemical gradients, such as by inducing localized hypoxia.65,66 These biophysical stressors are known to be potent activators of stromal cells; specifically, mechanical strain and hypoxic signaling can trigger fibroblasts to secrete TSLP as an alarmin,67, 68, 69, 70 thereby initiating a cascade of B-cell–mediated epithelial dysfunction. Although this fibroblast–TSLP–B-cell axis represents a significant component of HAEC pathogenesis, it likely functions within a broader, multifactorial framework. It acts in concert with other elements—such as the “neuro-immune void” created by the absence of enteric ganglia and pre-existing barrier defects—to collectively lower the threshold for enterocolitis. Recognizing this axis as a key yet nonexclusive contributor provides a more nuanced understanding of how structural abnormalities in HSCR are translated into acute inflammatory episodes.

The integration of ST allows us to move beyond cellular enumeration toward defining the proinflammatory “niche” within the HSCR mucosal landscape. Our data reveal that B cells in the HSCR segment undergo a fundamental functional shift, transitioning from passive antibody producers to active sources of proinflammatory mediators. This phenotypic “re-arming” is topographically linked to their proximity to activated fibroblasts. We identified localized clusters in which TSLP+ fibroblasts and activated B cells are intricately interspersed, forming a specialized inflammatory unit or niche. Within these spatial domains, the sustained paracrine delivery of TSLP provides a localized high-concentration stimulus that may bypass systemic regulatory checks, driving B cells to upregulate genes such as BLK and BLNK and secrete cytokines like CCL563,71 and ICAM1.61,62 This spatial architecture suggests that the HAEC-prone environment is not a diffuse inflammatory state but rather is driven by these discrete stromal–immune hubs. In these niches, the physical contact and short-range signaling between fibroblasts and B cells essentially “educate” the immune cells to adopt a pathogenic profile, thereby creating a localized reservoir of inflammation that directly borders and compromises the intestinal epithelium.

Our findings identify the TSLP signaling pathway as a critical node in B-cell–mediated epithelial damage, and we demonstrate that baicalein, a natural flavonoid with potent anti-inflammatory properties,72,73 effectively interrupts this pathogenic axis. Molecular docking simulations provided a structural basis for the interaction between baicalein and the TSLP receptor complex, suggesting its role as a competitive or allosteric inhibitor. In our coculture and organoid models, baicalein treatment significantly reversed the morphologic disruption and apoptotic surge induced by TSLP-activated B cells. Although baicalein showed limited efficacy in fully restoring barrier permeability, its robust protection of epithelial proliferation and organoid structural integrity highlights its potential to prevent the “apoptotic shift” in the mucosal microenvironment. These results suggest that targeting the fibroblast-derived TSLP with baicalein could be a viable pharmacologic strategy to mitigate the mucosal predisposition to HAEC in patients with HSCR.

Given the established role of B cells in disrupting intestinal epithelial cell homeostasis, we propose exploring multiple avenues to restore B-cell function, thereby repairing intestinal mucosa and inhibiting intestinal inflammation. Strategies to prevent and treat intestinal fibrosis and to control the inflammatory factor secretion by fibroblasts may contribute to the regulation of the immune microenvironment. Current research highlights that anti-fibrotic drugs, such as AMA0825, are significant in inhibiting fibroblast activation and restoring the intestinal microenvironment.74 Modulating B-cell responses represents a potential alternative therapy for the prevention and treatment of HAEC. Notably, rituximab has been found to maintain normal B-cell function and inhibit intestinal inflammation.75 Importantly, our study introduces a more targeted approach: the use of baicalein to inhibit the TSLP signaling complex, thereby preventing B-cell hyperactivation. Furthermore, in future cell therapy strategies76 for HSCR, gene expression in transplanted neural stem cells or intestinal organoids could be modified to inhibit lymphocyte-activating molecules like TSLP, aiming to repair the enteric nervous system while preserving the normal function of the intestinal wall microenvironment.

An intriguing spatial feature observed in our study is the dense infiltration of B cells specifically clustered within and adjacent to epithelial breaks. This localized accumulation does not alter our core conclusion regarding TSLP-driven B-cell activation; rather, it underscores a highly coordinated spatial–immune response to mucosal barrier disruption. Epithelial breaks serve as entry portals for luminal antigens and microbial pathogen-associated molecular patterns. The recruitment of B cells to these specific injury sites likely represents a compensatory defense mechanism aimed at accelerating secretory IgA production and neutralizing invading pathogens at the breach site. Furthermore, damaged intestinal epithelial cells flanking these breaks are known to markedly upregulate TSLP in response to mechanical stress and inflammatory cues. This localized surge in TSLP creates a potent chemotactic or survival microenvironment, directly trapping and activating B cells at the front lines of epithelial damage. This spatial correlation highlights a critical feed-forward loop where mechanical epithelial failure drives localized stromal–immune crosstalk, leading to B-cell–mediated mucosal inflammation.

A potential concern in this study is that the strongest B-cell expansion and activation were observed in the aganglionic segment, which is routinely resected in patients with HSCR, whereas postoperative HAEC predominantly affects the retained proximal ganglionated bowel. Although B-cell signatures were also detectable in the ganglionic segment, their magnitude was substantially lower. This discrepancy does not invalidate our findings but necessitates careful interpretation. One possibility is that the aganglionic segment serves as a driver of initial B-cell priming, and upon resection, the immune system may retain a memory response or ongoing low-grade activation that predisposes the remaining bowel to HAEC. Moreover, the lower level of B-cell activation in the ganglionic segment might still be pathologically relevant, especially when combined with other factors such as dysmotility, fecal stasis, or microbial dysbiosis. We therefore suggest that, although the aganglionic segment shows the highest B-cell activity, the same pathways could contribute, albeit at a reduced scale, to HAEC in the postoperative ganglionic bowel. Prospective studies examining B-cell phenotypes in postresection HAEC tissues are warranted to directly test this hypothesis.

Despite the promising findings regarding the inhibitory effect of flavonoid on TSLP-mediated inflammation in this study, several inherent limitations must be acknowledged before considering their clinical translation. First, flavonoids are well-known pleiotropic compounds that exert anti-inflammatory effects through multiple broad pathways, including antioxidative, antiproliferative, and antiplatelet mechanisms. Therefore, the therapeutic outcomes observed here may not be exclusively driven by TSLP inhibition alone. Second, the in vivo efficacy of flavonoids as a viable therapy is heavily constrained by their complex metabolic profile. Flavonoids undergo extensive biotransformation involving β-glucuronidases, a process critically dependent on liver function and the intestinal microbiota. Crucially, patients with HSCR frequently exhibit profound gut dysbiosis, which could lead to significant interindividual variability in β-glucuronidase activity and flavonoid bioavailability. Consequently, the actual therapeutic efficacy of these compounds in a clinical setting warrants cautious interpretation, and future studies must investigate how variations in the host microbiome and metabolic landscape influence their translational potential.

A particularly compelling conceptual direction for future study is the potentially nonlinear dynamic of immune-mediated barrier regulation under chronic HSCR bowel wall inflammation. Our observation that TSLP stimulation triggers a paradoxical synchronization of ZO-1 upregulation and heightened epithelial leakage suggests that the baseline immune response may initially attempt a protective, adaptive feedback loop. Biologically, this implies that there is likely a delicate dose–response threshold defining the “right amount” of B-cell activation necessary to be protective within the immune–stromal crosstalk. Under physiological or mildly stressed states, a controlled degree of B-cell engagement might serve as an indispensable homeostatic signal that instructs neighboring stromal elements to fortify the mucosal barrier.

Some additional limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, owing to the constraints of scRNA-seq technology in this study, the complex structure and large volume of enteric nerves prevented them from passing through the intestinal tissue dissociation and library construction, resulting in the failure to detect intestinal neurons. Enteric neurons are important cell subsets that regulate intestinal epithelial cells and immune cells. In the future, the effects of neurons on immune cells and epithelial cells could be analyzed through single-nucleus RNA sequencing Second, B cells differentiate into various memory B cells and plasma cells, but the roles and mechanisms of these B-cell subsets in regulating intestinal epithelial homeostasis have yet to be formally explored. Based on this study, our research group plans to apply for funding to investigate the functions of B-cell subsets in cellular and animal experiments using techniques such as flow cytometry sorting or induced differentiation. Third, the chronic intestinal stasis characteristic of HSCR creates a unique intraluminal environment marked by increased mechanical wall tension and altered chemical gradients (such as localized hypoxia or metabolic byproducts). Future studies should explore whether these biophysical stressors directly program fibroblasts toward a proinflammatory TSLP+ phenotype, thereby initiating the immune cascade. Moreover, in future studies, we will attempt to incorporate whole-genome sequencing data and sort intestinal tissue B cells to expand the scope of our research, while further controlling for potential confounding factors. Furthermore, the TSLP–B-cell axis was not validated in HSCR mouse models. Comparing TSLP responsiveness between ganglionic and aganglionic segments would better elucidate how enteric nervous system deficiency reshapes the mucosal immune microenvironment. The neurologic deficit in aganglionic segments may alter mechanical stress or stromal cell behavior, aggravating localized TSLP secretion and B-cell infiltration. Future studies using Ednrb−/− mice are needed to dissect this neuroimmune crosstalk and evaluate the therapeutic potential of targeting TSLP.

Our findings highlight the TSLP–B-cell axis as a therapeutic target for HAEC prevention. Neutralizing TSLP with baicalein or other medicines (eg, with monoclonal antibodies such as tezepelumab) or inhibiting B-cell effector functions could mitigate epithelial damage. The spatial colocalization of TSLP+ fibroblasts and B cells within mucosal niches suggests that localized drug delivery (eg, topical biologics) might be effective. Furthermore, detecting activated B cells or TSLP in mucosal biopsies could stratify HAEC risk preoperatively.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrate that HSCR intestines exhibit pathogenic B-cell expansion, hyperproliferation, and hyperactivation, accompanied by mucosal barrier defects and epithelial dysregulation. Multiomics integration (scRNA-seq, ST, bulk RNA-seq, and proteomics) revealed TSLP-secreting profibrotic fibroblasts as key upstream regulators that encapsulate B cells and synergistically amplify their activation and effector functions. Activated B cells reciprocally impair epithelial homeostasis by secreting proinflammatory mediators, suppressing proliferation, inducing apoptosis, disrupting tight junctions, and increasing permeability, thereby predisposing patients to HAEC. These features were conserved in Ednrb−/− HAEC mice. Critically, baicalein, a TSLP inhibitor, rescued epithelial damage in coculture and organoid models by restoring proliferation and attenuating apoptosis. These findings elucidate a fibroblast–TSLP–B cell–epithelial pathogenic axis in HSCR mucosal injury and position baicalein as a promising therapeutic candidate for HAEC prevention.

Materials and Methods

HSCR Samples

The study enrolled a cohort of 34 patients with sporadic HSCR of Chinese ethnicity from the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University. Their biopsy samples and surgical specimens were meticulously evaluated by at least 2 experienced pathologists. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of both the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University and Fujian Medical University and adhered strictly to governmental guidelines and the ethical principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

To be eligible for inclusion, participants were required to have no other congenital malformations. Surgical specimens were collected from proximal ganglionic segments (exhibiting ganglion cells adjacent to normal), proximal ganglionic segments (proximal dilated portion), and aganglionic segments (lacking ganglion cells). Detailed information regarding each participant in this study is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

The pre-enterocolitis HSCR ganglionic intestine for ST is in a subclinical inflammatory phase, characterized by localized immune dysregulation without the overt clinical hallmarks of acute HAEC. We define this as a state of subclinical immune activation, marked by an increased density of infiltrating lymphocytes (such as B cells and plasma cells), in the absence of macroscopic ulceration, neutrophil infiltration, or crypt abscesses. The intestinal samples in scRNA-seq discernible immune cell infiltration in both histologically ganglionic and aganglionic segments of patients with HSCR, indicating systemic enterocolitis predisposition. According to current clinical guidelines, HAEC is a contraindication for surgery.77,78 Consequently, obtaining intestinal tissue from children with HAEC is restricted by ethical and legal regulations. In this study, we utilized histopathologic examinations of surgically resected specimens to identify intestinal segments characterized by elevated intraepithelial lymphocytes, which served as a model to explore how abnormal B-cell proliferation interferes with intestinal epithelial cells. Hence, the intestinal tissue of ST profiling is from a patient with HSCR, where pathologically confirmed immune cell infiltration and epithelial disruption align with characteristics of intraepithelial lymphocyte elevation, typically indicating ongoing inflammation within the mucosa.31, 32, 33

Furthermore, the histologic classification, age, and gender of the subjects were taken into consideration. HAEC is a complication with no sex-based incidence difference; therefore, sex was not considered a biological variable in this study. Detailed information regarding each participant in this study is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Hirschsprung-Associated Enterocolitis Mice

The Ednrb−/− mice serves as a congenital aganglionosis model (HAEC analog). Postnatally, homozygous pups develop spontaneous enteritis. At 3 to 4 weeks, when Ednrb−/− mice showed signs of HAEC (abdominal distension, inability to expel feces, lethargy, weight loss), inflamed intestinal tissues from homozygous mice were harvested, including aganglionic and ganglionic segments. Tissues underwent phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washing followed by snap-freezing or 4% paraformaldehyde fixation, with age-matched WT mice intestines serving as healthy controls. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University.

Primary B Cell Culture and Activation

Total splenocytes were isolated from 10-week-old C57BL/J mice, which were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University. The total B cells were sorted from total splenocyte suspensions using a B-cell isolation kit (Miltenyi; cat #: 130-090-862) and validating purity by CoraLite Plus 488 Anti-Mouse CD19 (Proteintech; cat #: CL488-65290) with flow cytometry (Agilent; NovoCyte 2060R).

B cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium (Pricella; PM150110A) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Meisen; cat #: CTCC-002-071) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (Beyotime; cat #: C0222). The B cells were stimulated with LPS (10 μg/mL; Sigma; cat #: O111:B4), IL4 (8 ng/mL; PeproTech; cat #: 214-14), with or without TSLP (100 ng/mL; MCE; cat #: HY-P70626) for 72 hours. LPS and IL4 are classical B-cell activators that play an important role in normal growth of primary B cells. We also established the PBS control group.

Mouse Epithelial Cells and the Coculture System

Mouse colonic mucosal epithelial cells (Pricella; CP-M159) were inoculated into 6-well plates at an appropriate density (1 × 105 cells/well) and cultured for 24 hours to allow cell attachment. The culture medium used was mouse colonic mucosal epithelial cell complete medium (Pricella; CM-M159). For B-cell coculture, a Transwell chamber was placed into the 6-well plate, and 1 × 105 B cells were added to the upper chamber for a 72-hour coculture period.

The experimental groups were as follows: (1) Neg group: intestinal epithelial cells in normal culture; (2) Coculture group 1: epithelium coculture with the classical B-cell activator intervention B cells; (3) Coculture group 2: epithelium coculture with the costimulation of B cells with both classical B-cell activator and TSLP. Following coculture, CCK-8 assays, TUNEL assays, FITC-dextran permeability assays, Western blot, and bulk RNA-seq were performed.

Mouse Organoid Culture

The crypts for organoids were isolated from colon of 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/J mice, which were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University. All tissues were processed on ice: surrounding connective tissue was removed, the intestinal segment was opened longitudinally, and luminal contents were washed away with cold Dulbecco’s PBS (DPBS). The tissue was minced and rinsed with cold DPBS containing 1% double antibiotics, then digested with 0.02% EDTA on ice for 15 minutes. After removing EDTA, fragments were resuspended in precooled DPBS and filtered through a 70-μm strainer to isolate crypts. Following centrifugation at 300 g for 5 minutes, crypts were embedded in Matrigel (Mogengel; cat #: 082755) and seeded into 24-well plates. Each well was supplemented with 500 to 1000 μL of organoid culture medium (RISEZI; cat #: RSQ-OCM1102).

Coculture of B Cells and Mouse Intestinal Organoids

To evaluate the immunomodulatory effects on epithelial homeostasis, mouse intestinal organoids were cocultured with activated B-cell conditioned media. Unlike animal models, organoids allow for real-time, high-resolution visualization of the inflammatory cascade within a simplified yet highly controlled 3D microenvironment.79 The activated B-cell experimental groups were defined as follows: (1) Control group (organoids only); (2) Activation group (B cells stimulated with LPS + IL4); (3) TSLP-Activation group (B cells stimulated with LPS + IL4 + TSLP); and (4) Baicalein interference group (B cells stimulated with LPS + IL4 + TSLP + 20 μM baicalein). For conditioned media experiments, B cells were activated for 72 hours, after which the supernatant was collected. Newly passaged organoids were cultured in 12-well plates containing 1 mL of mixed medium per well, consisting of 25% B-cell supernatant and 75% standard organoid culture medium. Following 72 hours of incubation, the morphologic changes of the organoids were monitored and imaged using an inverted microscope (Nexcope; cat #: NIB620-FL). All organoids were harvested after the 72-hour coculture period for subsequent molecular assays.

TSLP Inhibition and Epithelial Rescue Assay

To investigate the rescue effects of TSLP signaling inhibition on intestinal epithelial damage, B cells were subjected to specific activation before coculture with mouse colonic mucosal epithelial cells. B cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 complete medium and assigned to 4 experimental groups: (1) Control group (mouse colonic mucosal epithelial cells only); (2) Activation group (B cells stimulated with LPS + IL4); (3) TSLP-Activation group (B cells stimulated with LPS + IL4 + TSLP); and (4) Baicalein interference group (B cells stimulated with LPS + IL4 + TSLP + 20 μM baicalein). All B-cell groups were stimulated for 72 hours to achieve the desired polarized phenotype. Subsequently, the mouse colonic mucosal epithelial cells were exposed to these conditioned B cells to evaluate the extent of inflammatory injury. After a 72-hour coculture period, cells from all groups were harvested for subsequent molecular assays.

CCK-8

B cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well (100 μL of cell suspension per well), with 5 replicate wells per group. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. After incubation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime; cat #: C0009S) was added to each well, and the plates were returned to the incubator for continued culture. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices; CMax Plus) after 3 hours of incubation with CCK-8.

Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling In Situ Apoptosis Assay

Apoptotic cells were identified using the One-step TUNEL In Situ Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience; cat #: E-CK-A322) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, colonic epithelial cells cultured on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes at room temperature, followed by 3 washes with PBS. The cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 at 37 °C for 10 minutes. After washing, each sample was incubated with 100 μL of TdT Equilibration Buffer at 37 °C for 15 minutes. Subsequently, the buffer was replaced with 50 μL of TUNEL labeling working solution and incubated in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 60 minutes in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 1× 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 minutes. Following the final PBS washes, the coverslips were mounted with antifluorescence quenching mounting medium and visualized using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nexcopel cat #: NIB620-FL).

Fluorescein Isothiocyanate–Dextran Permeability Assay

To assess intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, mouse colonic mucosal epithelial cells were seeded into the apical chambers of Transwell inserts at a density of 1 × 105 cells/cm2. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, with the culture medium replaced every 3 days to facilitate monolayer formation and differentiation. Following the establishment of the monolayer, cells were cocultured with B cells or specific reagents according to the experimental groups. For the permeability flux measurement, the culture medium was aspirated, and the monolayers were gently rinsed twice with prewarmed Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). The apical chamber was then loaded with 1 mg/mL FITC-dextran (Yeasen; cat #: 61220ES50) in HBSS, while the basolateral chamber received an equal volume of probe-free HBSS. After 4 hours of incubation at 37 °C, 200-μL aliquots were collected from the basolateral chamber and immediately replaced with fresh HBSS. The fluorescence intensity of the samples was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (Agilent).

Western Blot

Cells were washed thrice with PBS, lysed in 100 to 300 μL enhanced RIPA buffer at 4 °C for 20 minutes, and centrifuged (12,000 rpm, 10 minutes). Protein concentrations were quantified using a bicinchoninic acid assay (Beyotime). Denatured samples (20–60 μg) were resolved on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels (Servicebio; 80 V for 30 minutes, then 120 V for 80 minutes) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (BOSTER) using transfer system (400 mA, 90 minutes, 4 °C). Membranes were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin/tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 for 1 hour at room temperature, incubated with PCNA (Abcam; cat #: ab29; 1:1000), BAX (Proteintech; cat #: 50599-2-Ig; 1:2000), ZO-1 (Proteintech; cat #: 21773-1-AP; 1:5000), Claudin 1 (Proteintech; cat #: 13050-1-AP; 1:2000), and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Proteintech; cat #: 10494-1-AP; 1:5000) at 4 °C overnight, followed by secondary antibodies (Proteintech; cat #: SA00001-1 & SA00001-2; 1:10,000, 1 hour). Chemiluminescence was detected using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Yeasen) after tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 washes. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (version 1.5.3), with target protein bands normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase.

Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Tissue samples were collected from both normal and aganglionic segments of patients with HSCR. These samples were subjected to extract RNA using Trizol. Subsequently, the RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Hifair Ⅲ First Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR kit (Yeasen). cDNA amplification was performed on a Celemetor Real-Time Fluorogenic Quantitative PCR Analysis System (Yeasen) by using the Hieff UNICON Advanced qPCR SYBR Master Mix (Yeasen). The RT-qPCR procedure was conducted as outlined below: an initial denaturation step was executed at 95 °C for a duration of 2 minutes, subsequently followed by 40 cycles of RT-qPCR. Each cycle encompassed a denaturation phase at 95 °C for 5 seconds and a primer annealing phase at 60 °C for 30 seconds. Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining

Histologic sections were processed for morphologic analysis using standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Briefly, paraffin-embedded sections were baked at 60 °C for 1 to 2 hours, followed by deparaffinization in xylene and rehydration through a graded series of ethanol to distilled water. Sections were first stained with hematoxylin (Sigma; cat #: H3136) for 10 minutes to label the nuclei, followed by rinsing under running tap water. Differentiation was performed using 0.7% acid alcohol for several seconds, and bluing was achieved by washing in running water for 15 minutes. After brief dehydration in 95% ethanol (30 seconds), sections were counterstained with alcoholic Eosin (Sigma; cat #: E4009) for 30 seconds to visualize the cytoplasm and extracellular matrix. Following staining, slides were dehydrated through graded ethanol (95% and 100%), cleared in phenol-xylene and pure xylene, and finally mounted with neutral balsam. Microscopic observation and imaging were performed using a KF-PRO-120 digital scanner.

Multiplex Immunohistochemistry

After sectioning the paraffin-embedded tissue samples from both normal and aganglionic segments, dewaxing was performed using xylene (3 times; 10 minutes each), followed by gradient ethanol hydration (100%, 95%, 85%, 75%; 5 minutes each). After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp) for 30 minutes, antigen retrieval was performed using Tris-EDTA (Solarbio) at 95 °C for 20 minutes. The primary antibodies included PCNA (Abcam; cat #: ab29; 1:500), decorin (Abcam; cat #: ab137508; 1:200), TSLP (Proteintech; cat #: 13778-1-AP; 1:500), and CD20 (Abcam; cat #: ab64088; 1:500). The following day, fluorescent staining was conducted using a multiplex immunofluorescence kit (Panovue). The stained slides were mounted with an antifade mounting medium and visualized under a laser confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8) for image capture.

Briefly, single-cell suspension was washed by stain buffer, and stained with antibodies for 30 minutes at 4 °C in the dark. Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Data of Healthy Controls

To account for potential confounding biases related to intestinal location, we incorporated scRNA-seq data from healthy controls sourced from the Gut Cell Atlas (https://www.gutcellatlas.org/).81 Specifically, we utilized scRNA-seq data from 29 colon tissues of 7 healthy individuals that are explicitly diagnosed as healthy donors, spanning various anatomic locations. These data were subsequently employed for cell type annotation and comprehensive further analyses.

First, we conducted a comprehensive literature review to identify pertinent studies focusing on the intestine and scRNA-seq. Notably, our search revealed a lack of research utilizing scRNA-seq on the colon of healthy pediatric populations. Second, obtaining fresh colon tissue from various anatomic positions in healthy children poses significant ethical and practical challenges. Third, prior studies82, 83, 84 typically use adult scRNA-seq data as a control group, which poses challenges in obtaining healthy controls, especially for studies involving children with diseases. Our research endeavor is centered on elucidating the expression characteristics at the single-cell level, while disregarding age-related variations in cellular proportions. Ultimately, to account for sample variability, we utilized Harmony and SCTransform for corrective measures, effectively diminishing the impact of age to a certain degree.

Drawing upon the anatomic positions of aganglionic and normal intestines of HSCR, we isolated scRNA-seq data from the sigmoid colon and rectum, designating them as distal healthy controls. Analogously, scRNA-seq data were extracted from the transverse colon and descending colon, which were categorized as proximal healthy controls.

Spatial Transcriptomics Data of Healthy Controls

To investigate the spatial expression patterns of target genes within healthy intestinal architecture, public ST data from a representative healthy control that was explicitly diagnosed as a healthy donor (sample B10) were obtained from the CPI Colitis Data Portal (https://simmonslab.shinyapps.io/CPI_COLITIS_DATA_PORTAL/).34 The original data were generated using the 10x Genomics Visium Spatial Gene Expression protocol. For the current study, processed data objects and spot metadata for sample B10 were utilized to map the spatial distribution of candidate genes. Expression levels were projected onto the matched H&E histologic images to correlate molecular signatures with specific anatomic regions of the healthy gut. This spatial mapping facilitates a high-resolution understanding of gene expression within the native tissue context, providing a baseline for comparison with pathologic states.

Bulk RNA Sequencing Data of Patients With Hirschsprung’s Disease and Matched Controls

RNA-seq profiles pertaining to aganglionic segments of patients with HSCR, along with those from controls (matched colon tissues unrelated to HSCR), were sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (GSE96854). A total of 30 colon intestines, earmarked for bulk RNA-seq analysis, were randomly combined to formulate 3 pooled samples for each group: the healthy group and the aganglionic group. Further details regarding these samples can be accessed in the original study.85

Proteomics Data of Patients With Hirschsprung’s Disease and Matched Controls

The differentially expressed proteins between aganglionic segments of patients with HSCR and controls (matched colon tissues unrelated to HSCR) were directly obtained from a previous study.85 Analogously, for proteomics analysis, 30 colon samples were randomly combined, yielding 3 pooled samples for each of the healthy and aganglionic groups. The comprehensive analysis process is documented in the original study.85

Proteomics of Hirschsprung-Associated Enterocolitis Mice Intestines

Protein extraction involved grinding samples in liquid nitrogen. The resulting powder was lysed in urea buffer (8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor; supplemented with tryptic soy agar with N-acetylmuramic acid for acetyl- or phosphatase inhibitors for phospho-proteomics) on ice, sonicated (3 minutes), and clarified by centrifugation (12,000 g, 10 minutes, 4 °C). Supernatant protein concentration was quantified by bicinchoninic acid assay. Trypsin digestion precipitated proteins using trichloroacetic acid (20% final concentration), washed the pellet with cold acetone, and dried it. The protein was redissolved in 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate, ultrasonicated, and digested overnight with trypsin (1:50 w/w ratio). Peptides were reduced (5 mM dithiothreitol, 56 °C, 30 minutes), alkylated (11 mM iodoacetic acid, room temperature, 15 minutes, in the dark), and desalted using Strata X SPE columns. Tandem mass tag labeling and high-performance liquid chromatography (LC) fractionation were not performed. For LC–tandem mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, tryptic peptides in Solvent A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile) were separated on a custom C18 column via a NanoElute ultra-high performance LC system using a gradient against Solvent B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) at 500 nL/min (6%–24% B: 0–14 minutes, 24%–35% B: 14–16 minutes, 35%–80% B: 16–18 minutes, 80% B: 18–20 minutes).

Eluting peptides were ionized (1.75 kV) and analyzed on a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer operated in dia-PASEF mode. MS scans spanned 300-1500 m/z; MS/MS scans (20 per cycle) covered 400-8500 m/z with 7 m/z isolation windows. Database searching utilized DIA-NN (v1.8) against a Mus musculus database (UniProt; 17,191 entries) plus decoys. Search parameters included: trypsin/P cleavage (max 1 missed cleavage), N-terminal Met excision, carbamidomethyl (C) as fixed modification, and false discovery rate <1%. The proteomics data were then used to perform bioinformatic analysis.

Bulk RNA Sequencing of B Cells and Cocultured Mouse Colonic Epithelial Cells

Total RNA was extracted from activated B cells and cocultured mouse colonic epithelial cells using TRIzol. The RNA concentration was detected by a UV spectrophotometer, and the RNA samples were analyzed by Qsep1 for quality assessment and precise concentration quantification to ensure qualified RNA quality for library construction. The constructed libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform. To ensure data quality, raw data were filtered under the following conditions: removing paired reads containing adapters; removing paired reads with >3 'N' bases; removing low-quality reads (where the number of bases with quality value Q ≤20 accounted for >50% of the entire read) to reduce analytical interference from invalid data.

Molecular Docking Simulation

To investigate the potential binding mode and affinity between baicalein and the TSLP complex, molecular docking simulations were performed using the CB-Dock2 server (http://cao.labshare.cn:11080/cb-dock2/). The 3D crystal structure of the TSLP signaling complex was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Prior to docking, the protein structure was preprocessed by removing water molecules, ions, and original ligands while retaining the essential TSLP protein chain. The chemical structure of baicalein was obtained from the PubChem database in SDF format and utilized as the ligand. The docking process was executed through the “Blind Docking” module of CB-Dock2. Baicalein was docked into the predicted cavities using the integrated AutoDock Vina engine. The docking results were evaluated based on the Vina score (kcal/mol), representing the binding affinity. Finally, the visual analysis of the baicalein-TSLP complex was conducted to elucidate the mechanistic basis for their interaction.

Gene Sets

We collected the gene sets of cell proliferation from Human Molecular Signatures Database86 (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/genesets.jsp), such as KEGG_CELL_CYCLE.v2023.2.Hs.gmt and CELL_CYCLE_PHASE.v2023.2.Hs.gmt. We collected the gene set of B-cell activation (GOBP_B_CELL_ACTIVATION), B-cell proliferation (GOBP_B_CELL_PROLIFERATION), and cell apoptosis (HALLMARK_APOPTOSIS) from MSigDB86 (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/genesets.jsp).

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing and the Upstream Process

HSCR tissue dissociation was conducted according to the previous study.80 The single-cell suspension derived from HSCR tissues was processed utilizing a 10x Chromium (10x Genomics). Subsequently, the cDNA libraries were tested on an Illumina sequencing platform (NovaSeq 6000). The raw sequencing data underwent processing through the Cell Ranger (version 3.1.0). The resultant gene expression matrices were further processed and analyzed using Seurat R package (version 5.1.0) and SCTransform R package (version 0.4.1). The specific sample information and analytical details have been reported in our previous research.24

Differential Gene Expression Analysis and Enrichment Analysis

We employed the Seurat package to identify DEGs between different segments among different cell types. Given the heightened expression of genes associated with B-cell activation in HSCR segments, our primary focus was on upregulated DEGs. The HSCR data was stratified by patient, then corresponding subsets were extracted accordingly. For the patients with HSCR, the normal intestine from each subject acted as an internal control for the aganglionic segment, thereby enabling us to detect significant segment-specific effects even with a limited count of patients. DEGs that were present in 3 or more patients were deemed as target DEGs. UpSetR (version 1.4.0), pheatmap (version 1.0.12), ggpubr (version 0.4.0), and ggplot2 (version 3.3.5) were used to visualize the gene expression levels between segments. For bulk RNA-seq analysis, we utilized the limma (version 3.54.2) and edgeR (version 3.40.2) package to analyze the DEGs. Specifically, we included genes with P value less than .05 to validate the results obtained from the scRNA-seq analysis.

GO enrichment analyses were conducted using the clusterProfiler R package and simplifyEnrichment R package (version 1.7.1). The GO terms were significantly enriched and associated with HAEC phenotypes, such as B-cell activation, intestinal fibrosis, epithelial dysfunction, and regulation of cell death. GSEA analyses were also conducted using the clusterProfiler and ggplot2 R package. We focus on the gene sets from Molecular Signatures Database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) related to B-cell activation, epithelial dysfunction, and regulation of cell death.

Gene Set Score Analysis

To investigate the genes encoding B-cell proliferation or activation in diverse intestinal cell types, a gene set score was employed as a metric to assess the state of B cells. In the combined dataset, cells harboring active gene sets indicative of B-cell activation were pinpointed using the AUCell R package (version 1.12.0). Subsequently, FeaturePlot visualizations were crafted with the ggplot2 R package (version 3.3.5) to visually depict the B-cell activation gene expression. Additionally, heatmaps were generated using the R package pheatmap (version 1.0.12) to provide a comprehensive view of the scaled expression levels of target genes across various cell subsets. These visualizations aid in understanding the differential expression patterns and activation states of B cells within the intestine.

Differential Abundance Analysis

To assess B-cell subpopulation composition differences across HSCR and healthy control segments, 2 complementary analytical approaches were applied. First, a ratio of observed to expected analysis was conducted for each B-cell subset, with a clustered heatmap to visualize abundance discrepancies between HSCR segments and healthy controls. Subsequently, MiloR DA analysis88 was performed to identify significantly altered B-cell subsets using R package miloR (version 1.6.0), comparing HSCR segments with proximal and distal healthy controls separately. Results were visualized via cumulative density bar plots and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection FeaturePlots to display subset-specific abundance changes.

Cell Development Trajectories Construction

To investigate B-cell activation across various segments, we constructed single-cell trajectories among these segments, enabling us to delineate the temporal changes in B-cell activation states. The monocle R package (version 2.18.0) and the ggplot2 R package (version 3.3.5) were used to analyze and visualize the single-cell trajectory.

Naive B cells can differentiate along 2 routines. On the one hand, upon naive B cells encountering an antigen which are activated and undergo proliferation, leading to the differentiation into memory B cells. On the other hand, another portion of these B cells choose to directly differentiate into plasma cells rather than becoming memory B cells. Once memory B cells encounter the same antigen again, they can rapidly initiate a germinal center reaction, proliferate extensively, and differentiate into antibody-producing plasma cells.

All B cells and plasma cells were chosen and categorized to distinct intestinal groups. For each group, the cells were utilized to construct single-cell trajectories, with the naive B cells serving as the starting point of the trajectory, based on the well-established biological process of B-cell differentiation.

Cell–Cell Communication

NicheNet (version 4.3.2) was employed to predict potential ligand–receptor interactions that could underlie the gene expression changes observed between B cells and intestinal epithelial cells. Both absorptive and secretory cells were abnormal proliferation in HSCR; thus, we focused on these 2 types of cells for cellular communication analysis. The markers (cell proliferation, mucosal barrier, goblet function, HAEC-related genes, and inflammasomes) were chosen as target genes to infer the impact of B cells and plasma cells on absorptive cells.

Spatiotemporal Transcriptomics

Stereo sequencing spatiotemporal transcriptomics utilizes DNBSEQ-based capture chips with spatial barcoding probes to achieve high-resolution in situ whole-transcriptome analysis across inflamed intestinal tissue areas. Tissue sections embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound are cryosectioned, slide-mounted, and fixed in methanol for morphologic assessment via H&E staining and microscopy. Permeabilization time is empirically optimized by quantifying fluorescence intensity of cDNA synthesized in situ after timed application of permeabilization reagents. During formal experiments, optimal cutting temperature–embedded sections undergo methanol fixation followed by permeabilization at the predetermined optimal duration to release messenger RNA, which is captured by spatially barcoded probes on the chip. Reverse transcription generates spatially resolved cDNA on-chip, after which residual tissue is removed. The cDNA is collected, purified, PCR-amplified, and repurified. Library construction involves cDNA fragmentation, PCR amplification with size selection, and quality control. Sequencing employs rolling circle amplification to generate DNA nanoballs using DNBSEQ-T7, which are loaded into nanoarrays via high-intensity patterning and sequenced using combinatorial Probe-Anchor Synthesis technology, enabling spatially resolved transcriptome reconstruction.

The resultant gene expression matrices were further processed and transferred to rds. file using Stereopy Python code (version 1.6.0, https://stereopy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html). Then the Seurat R package (version 5.1.0) was used to analyze and visualize. Employing scRNA-seq data from HSCR specimens, cellular deconvolution was performed using the RCTD algorithm within the spacexr (version 3.21, https://github.com/dmcable/spacexr) package to annotate cell types. Subsequent integration with the Seurat package enabled spatial mapping of cell distribution and gene expression characteristics across tissue section.

Statistical Analysis

For scRNA-seq analysis, the Seurat package was used to identify DEGs between different segments among different cell types. DEGs that were present in 3 or more patients were deemed as target DEGs. For bulk RNA-seq and proteomics analysis, we utilized the limma (version 3.54.2) and edgeR (version 3.40.2) to analyze the DEGs. Specifically, we included genes with a P value less than .05 to validate the results obtained from the scRNA-seq, proteomics, and bulk RNA-seq analyses. Statistical differences between numerical data from in vitro experiment were calculated by the Student t test or ordinary 1-way analysis of variance test using GraphPad (version 9.4.0). All experiments were performed independently at least 3 times.
