Advances in Gene and Cellular Therapy in Friedreich Ataxia
Authors: Michael P. Lazaropoulos, David R. Lynch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Friedreich ataxia is a rare, autosomal recessive neurogenerative disorder caused by mutations to the frataxin (FXN) gene resulting in loss of functional FXN protein. Applications of cutting-edge genetic and cellular therapies expand therapeutic options for patients with rare, genetic diseases including Friedreich ataxia. Multiple investigational techniques and strategies seek to permanently alter the disease course in patients with Friedreich ataxia, although no product has established definitive benefit. This review catalogs both the history and ongoing efforts of genetic and cellular therapies applied to Friedreich ataxia and its disease models, including therapeutic efficacy and adverse effects. We list the key limitations and cautions of such therapies, chiefly those of potential FXN overexpression toxicity, critical therapeutic windows, and adverse effects of these therapies applicable to any disease target. As gene and cellular therapy continue to diversify in design and strategy, Friedreich ataxia patients will likely have multiple therapeutic options in the future from both investigational therapies described here and future ones yet to be optimized.

Key Points

Introduction

Friedreich ataxia (FRDA) is an autosomal recessive ataxia that typically manifests in childhood with limb ataxia, gait instability, and areflexia. Patients progressively decline in motor function and develop sensory deficits, cardiomyopathy, scoliosis, and variably diabetes, optic neuropathy, and auditory dysfunction. It is caused by mutations in the frataxin (FXN) gene, predominantly by GAA triplet-repeat expansions in intron 1 that profoundly limit the amount of functional FXN protein by suppressing transcription [1]. About 4% of FRDA patients are compound heterozygous for a GAA expanded allele and one non-traditional disease-causing FXN allele, consisting of point mutations or indels in FXN. About 1:50,000 individuals are diagnosed with FRDA in the USA and Europe, with a carrier frequency of about 1 in 100 [2]. Mechanistically, FXN facilitates iron-sulfur cluster synthesis, providing this vital prosthetic group for the Electron Transport Chain (ETC), mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, and many other enzymes in the cell. Without FXN, these bioenergetic processes are impaired, and iron can accumulate in mitochondria, potentially leading to increased oxidative stress [3]. FXN deficiency impacts highly metabolic tissues such as the heart and nervous system, the latter of which demonstrates marked degeneration of the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), cerebellar dentate nucleus (DN), corticospinal tracts, and various other regions of the brain [4]. Lack of FXN also results in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the sequelae of which cause premature death in FRDA patients. While FRDA now has a US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved therapy, omaveloxolone [5], it acts downstream of FXN deficiency and does not fully prevent progression; therapies designed to increase FXN expression may stabilize or reverse FRDA disease processes.

Various investigational molecular therapies have reached clinical trials to correct frataxin deficiency from FRDA-causing mutations. Such therapies aim to increase FXN protein expression through gene addition/replacement, DNA editing, epigenetic reversal, or peptide replacement. Some rely on viral vectors to deliver genetic material for FXN replacement or editing; presently, they investigate multiple administration strategies to efficiently target affected tissues in FRDA. Cellular therapies seek to provide donor or autologous cells that secrete protective factors in degenerating tissues or perhaps replace resident FXN-deficient cells in such tissues. Such cutting-edge therapies have gained federal approval for other rare and common diseases, and at present in FRDA they have advanced to clinical trials. This review catalogs the gene and cellular therapies being pursued to treat FRDA, from active clinical trials to mechanistic pre-clinical studies.

Gene Therapy

Gene Addition

Gene-targeted therapies comprise multiple interventional strategies aimed at correcting pathologic DNA sequence variants, gene expression, or splicing. The gene addition strategy delivers extrachromosomal DNA encoding the wild-type sequence of a deficient or dysfunctional gene. Currently initiated genetic therapies for FRDA in clinical trials aim to deliver a copy of the FXN gene to affected tissues (Fig. 1). Delivery of the human FXN gene (hFXN) to FXN-deficient hearts in FRDA mouse models rescues both the molecular phenotype (mitochondrial and sarcomeric deficiencies) and improves cardiac function and long-term survival [6, 7]. Encouragingly, functional improvement in these hearts can occur when only 50% of cardiomyocytes re-express FXN [7], suggesting that less than 100% transduction efficiency might achieve clinical benefit (Fig. 2). Moreover, intraperitoneal injection of viral vectors containing hFXN improved function in FXN-conditional knockout in cardiac muscle and neural tissue, respectively, and improved survival [8]. Viral delivery of hFXN can be achieved in non-human primates (NHPs), even at the low viral infusion titers [9]. While delivering copies of genes is routine in cell culture and in mice, the difficulty of delivering genetic material to human cells complicates such approaches, particularly due to the low transduction rate. Hence, great emphasis is placed on optimizing the vector, the vehicle that delivers the gene, and particularly its capsid, the outer covering. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is the most used vector for clinical applications because of its relative safety and in vivo efficiency. Astellas Gene Therapies using single IV administration of the AAV8 serotype to deliver FXN to cardiac and skeletal muscle FXN-conditional knockout mouse (MCK-Cre) demonstrates improved survival and preserved ejection fraction (Table 1) [10]. The Phase I clinical trial (NCT06483802) of this product was planned but ultimately withdrawn. Artificially modifying natural AAV serotypes can optimize tissue specificity and delivery efficiency, as seen with AAVrh.10hFXN, also called LX2006, developed by Lexeo Therapeutics. This technology comprises a recombinant AAV optimized to deliver hFXN to the heart via a single infusion. Interim updates on two ongoing clinical trials of LX2006 (NCT05445323, NCT05302271) find that administration reduces left ventricular mass index (LVMI) and LV wall thickness on echocardiographs, reduces troponin I after at least 6 months, and increases FXN protein in cardiac biopsies [11]. Such results demonstrate drug activity and downstream effects, but the direct relationship of hypertrophy to eventual systolic dysfunction and death is not linear; only longer observation times can truly demonstrate clinical benefit [12]. Still, it represents a significant result in this moderate-duration study. While LX2006 aims to manage FRDA cardiomyopathy, Solid Bioscience has developed SGT-212, an AAV approach that expresses FXN in the heart and CNS via dual routes of administration: MRI-guided intradentate nucleus (IDN) infusion and systemic IV delivery. Single administration of SGT-212 in parvalbumin-Cre mice driving conditional Fxn knockout in DRG and cerebellar neurons or striated muscle (MCK-Cre) improves neurobehavioral and cardiac function, respectively, and increases survival in both models [13]. Testing on NHPs shows their dual-route approach successfully expresses hFXN protein in the DN and heart [14]. MRI-guided neurosurgical placement of cannulas in the DN of cadavers demonstrates the technical feasibility of such an approach in humans [15]. In 2026, the FALCON trial (NCT07180355) administered SGT-212 to its first patient [16]. However, this approach has study-specific limitations. Peripherally, the exact tropism of SGT212 for skeletal muscle dysfunction, an under-appreciated feature of FRDA, is unknown. In addition, IDN injection would not provide replacement to the neurons of the corticospinal tract, which causes substantial dysfunction in middle- and late-stage FRDA patients [17]. IDN injection could also directly damage that nucleus; while this seems undesirable, dentate lesions could ameliorate some of the spasticity of late-stage disease [18]. Thus, surgery itself could theoretically provide benefit.

In contrast, Capsida Biotherapeutics similarly seeks to deliver wildtype FXN to all affected tissues but with a single IV administration of their modified vector. Their product, CAP-004, is a modified viral capsid capable of penetrating the blood brain barrier (BBB) while minimizing non-target transduction. CAP-004 increases hFXN expression in multiple neural tissues and left ventricle in non-human primates, with less transduction in non-target tissues compared to AAV9 [19]. It remains to be seen if CAP-004 can improve symptomatic features of FRDA in animal models, but this preliminary data bodes well for efficacy in FXN re-expression and its safety profile. However, the Phase I/IIa trial on their related product CAP-002 aiming to treat STXBP1-related Developmental and Epileptic Encephalopathy has been suspended due to the unfortunate death of a participant, so the future of CAP-004 is uncertain [20]. Separately, a collaboration between Neurocrine Biosciences and Voyager Therapeutics aims to develop the latter’s TRACER AAV capsid for IV delivery of a FXN replacement gene to the brain. The product, NBIB-′223, has no publicly available data of its preclinical studies at the time of writing, but are presumptively in final studies for IND status. StrideBio has also been developing a modified capsid designed for efficient BBB crossing to treat FRDA, but after their acquisition by Ginkgo Bioworks, the future of this product is unknown. These gene-addition trials have the potential to prevent further clinical decline in FRDA if their translational studies yield consistent, long-term benefits for participants.

There are limitations and cautions with these strategies that must be considered when predicting prognosis and potential adverse effects of gene addition. They must be carefully monitored to avoid increasing FXN protein expression to toxic levels. hFXN overexpression in cardiac tissue in mice is safe up to ninefold, but toxic at 20-fold [21]. Special consideration of the delivered construct and vector must ensure efficient transduction and that FXN does not reach such toxic levels by optimizing the promoter and other such elements. However, it remains unknown how stable the delivered gene is once it enters target cells. Current trials have not continued long enough to assess long-term responses, and most animal studies do not assess transgene degradation after delivery. If such degradation occurs, it may affect the prognosis of those treated with these strategies and necessitate repeat administrations. However, this raises another limitation of viral vectors on whether subsequent administrations of AAV beyond the planned one-time administration are efficacious or safe. Immunogenicity of AAVs is a common concern in gene therapy, and patients may develop acquired immunity to the vector after the first administration. Those with pre-existing antibodies are commonly ineligible to receive this form of treatment. Hence, neutralizing antibodies may block the second administration of the same AAV or the first administration of similar AAVs targeting different tissues. The immune response to the vector could be managed with short-term immune suppression at the time the AAV is delivered, but this strategy poses inherent risks. Of greatest concern are the acute and delayed toxicities of AAV gene delivery, as the infusion of these viral particles leads to substantially higher circulating virus than typically seen during systemic viral transduction [22]. Excessive activation of complement and cytotoxic T-cells will damage tissues that can be compounded by antibodies against capsid proteins leading to thrombotic microangiopathy [23, 24]. In addition, various degrees of hepatoxicity commonly manifest in primate and human studies of AAV delivery, ranging from transiently elevated liver enzymes to acute liver failure [25, 26]. Dorsal root ganglion toxicity also occurs, but this may be moot in FRDA as such cells are likely absent in FRDA patients at presentation [17]. These broad risks necessitate multidisciplinary monitoring by various consulting services in patients receiving viral vector-delivered gene therapy to ensure safety. Nonetheless, FXN gene delivery is in some ways the most developed genetic therapy approach available for FRDA patients, and its efficacy and safety will be closely monitored to determine its readiness for Phase 3 trials.

Gene Editing

With the advent of CRISPR technology, the prior conceptualization of genetic editing to correct pathological mutations in patients has become a reality. In vivo gene editing delivers the molecular machinery necessary to edit the chromosomal DNA of target tissues within the patient. In vivo CRISPR editing of chromosomal DNA in humans has been pursued clinically in hereditary angioedema [27] and CPS1 deficiency [28]. Strategies of in vivo gene editing extend beyond correcting mutations to wild-type sequences and include inactivating or dampening gene expression that contributes to pathology despite the related sequence being otherwise wild-type. Preclinical work in FRDA-derived patient cells demonstrates that it is possible to remove the entire GAA-repeat expansion using zinc-finger nucleases (ZFN) or CRISPR in vitro. Removal of the GAA repeats from the FXN gene of patient-derived induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) increases FXN protein expression and restores dysregulated gene expression associated with the FRDA cellular phenotype [29, 30]. Similarly, removal of GAA repeats in patient iPSCs, followed by differentiation into neurons, increases FXN, ameliorates the mitochondrial phenotype, mitigates neuronal death, and improves neurite outgrowth [31, 32]. Additional removal of the differentially methylated region in intron 1 and the GAA repeats further improves FXN expression and restores the neuronal phenotype. These studies collectively provide proof of principle for removing the GAA repeats and other repressive elements in the FXN gene as a preventive therapy for GAA repeat-associated FRDA. However, these studies remove the GAA repeats from iPSCs before differentiating them into neurons or cardiomyocytes, distinct from in vivo gene editing of target tissues that have already developed in utero in the context of FXN deficiency. AAV-delivered CRISPR-Cas9 with guide RNAs (gRNA) directing removal of GAA repeats in YG8 and YG8sR mice demonstrates sufficient GAA removal in heart, liver, and skeletal muscle, but only increases FXN in the liver [33]. While removal of the expanded GAA repeats is possible in vivo, further optimization at the preclinical level is required to demonstrate reversal of both molecular and functional phenotypes in FRDA animal models. Such approaches would have many of the same adverse issues as in gene addition, but require delivery of a larger amount of material, thus complicating transduction of large numbers of cells. However, gene-editing approaches would return FXN regulation toward endogenous mechanisms rather than introducing an uncontrollable FXN gene, a strategy that is less likely to restore normal physiology.

Alternatively, antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) could provide a gene-editing approach to benefit FRDA in both GAA-repeat-associated alleles and select non-GAA mutations. ASO therapy delivers a short nucleotide sequence that binds to pathologically associated DNA elements or RNA molecules, degrading targeted RNAs, or promoting a specific transcript derived from alternative splicing. In FRDA, ASOs have been applied to both GAA-repeat-associated pathology and point mutations. Modified ASOs that target the GAA repeat increase FXN protein more than threefold in patient cell lines [34, 35]. Similarly, the c.165+5G>C splice-site mutation is amendable to ASO therapy, as ASO targeting this mutation corrects aberrant splicing and produces transcript synthesizing functional FXN [36]. These studies provide rationale for ASO therapy in clinical trials as a more accessible option for restoring FXN expression.

The most notable limitation for gene editing and targeting patient chromatin is the potential for off-target editing, which could adversely alter gene expression in patient tissues. This off-target editing is a concern of any CRISPR-based approach, as the non-specificity of gRNAs may direct Cas9 to damage multiple chromatin loci, which may be deleterious irrespective of whether it affects a non-coding gene. In FRDA, such a risk is theoretically doubled as removing the GAA repeat expansion requires two unique gRNAs. Off-target effects are also concerning for ASO therapy. Furthermore, successful in vivo editing in animals has not consistently restored FXN expression, a minimum requirement for investigating such strategies clinically. One possible solution to this issue may be to remove both the GAA expansion and the methylated region upstream of it [31]. At present, gene editing of the FXN gene by removing the GAA expansion requires further preclinical work to demonstrate consistent benefit in animal models and in terminally differentiated affected cell types.

Epigenetic Therapy

Unlike genetic therapies, epigenetic therapies target chromatin modifications and impact gene expression without modifying the DNA sequence of affected genes. These therapies often use more traditional small-molecule pharmaceuticals to inhibit specific classes of enzymes that modify chromatin organization. Classical FRDA caused by GAA expansions involves repressive chromatin modifications at the FXN gene and its regulatory elements. These include increased DNA methylation at CpG islands in the FXN gene and decreased occupancy by acetylated histones permissive to expression, with a reciprocal increase in repressive histone marks (e.g., H3K9me3 and H3K27me3). Initial attention has been on histone deacetylase inhibitors (HDACi) as a means increase FXN mRNA transcription. The most notable HDACi in FRDA was RG2833, a brain-penetrant molecule that increases FXN protein expression in multiple cellular and rodent models [37, 38]. Patients treated with RG2833 show increased levels of FXN mRNA and increased histone acetylation at the gene locus [39]. Ultimately, RG2833 failed to demonstrate sufficient benefit to FXN expression, and its use is confounded by potentially problematic metabolites. The effects of RG2833 on epigenetic marks of the FXN gene show that DNA methylation persists with inhibitor treatment [40]. These data suggest that inhibiting HDACs alone may not be sufficient to reverse the repressive epigenetic changes at the FXN gene.

Current clinical trials utilize an epigenetic approach in FRDA with Design Therapeutics product DT-216P2. This investigational drug leverages a technology dubbed GeneTAC that promotes transcription elongation of nascent FXN mRNA through the GAA-repeat expansion. This results in increased full-length FXN mRNA and protein expression [36]. A prior formulation of DT-216 increased FXN mRNA expression in skeletal muscle biopsies of FRDA participants [41]. This formulation was largely considered safe with the notable exception of injection site thrombophlebitis that has been addressed with DT-216P2 in the RESTORE-FA Phase I/II trial (NCT06874010) [42]. This application of GeneTAC in FRDA offers a key advantage over previous epigenetic therapeutics. DT-216P2 shows specificity for the GAA-repeat expansion, likely having fewer global effects on chromatin while also targeting the genetic pathophysiology of FRDA. However, the clinical efficacy of this new technology in FRDA remains to be seen.

Epigenetic therapies avoid the risks of genetic therapies, which can cause irreversible DNA damage, mutations, or errant gene expression. The adverse effects of epigenetic therapies are hopefully reversible with medication withdrawal. However, there are multiple concerns regarding both the safety and efficacy of these medications. Drugs that target entire classes of epigenetic enzymes may have global consequences. For instance, global HDAC inhibition may de-repress inappropriate gene programs aside from FXN. DT216P2 addresses this limitation somewhat by binding to repeat sequences, but the off-target effects of this class of novel therapeutics on microsatellite repeats are not well understood. Nonetheless, targeting epigenetic enzymes or processes through pharmacologic approaches may still represent a niche of potential therapies in FRDA, as to date most attention has been given to HDACs and histone acetylation. However, there has been little clinical exploration of DNA or histone methylation, alone or in conjunction with previous therapies, as a means of FXN gene activation.

Peptide Replacement

A final approach to reversing the genetic defect is to supply FXN itself as a protein product. Such approaches will have some of the limitations of gene therapy (distribution to relevant tissues, potential immunogenicity) and issues of limited stability. Proteins are intrinsically less stable than DNA, and such agents will require more frequent administration (daily in some cases). Still, they are simpler to develop in many situations. The first such form, TAT-MTS-hFXN (nomlabofusp) has entered clinical trials after preclinical development [43]. In the Fxn-MCK mouse, a model with severe cardiomyopathy, it extends life expectancy and corrects many of the structural and biochemical features of FRDA cardiomyopathy [44]. Similarly, it decreases pathological features of neurons derived from FRDA mice. However, its CNS penetration in mice is unclear, but it is still a potential cardiac therapy. In human trials, it restores FXN levels to almost carrier levels in skin (a proxy for peripheral tissues) with daily injection, showing that, based on correlations with rodents, it likely reaches all peripheral tissues including affected heart and skeletal muscle. However, no published evidence shows BBB penetration at present, and no placebo-controlled data is available on CNS benefit [44]. Furthermore, re-administration gives rise to a high rate of anaphylaxis, presumptively reflecting the TAT motif, confounding easy management in clinical situations. Still, it provides a paradigm through which more advanced approaches can overcome the issues of limited CNS penetration and immune response through alternative cell penetrant approaches.

Cellular Therapy

Cellular therapies provide an evolving field of investigational interventions aimed at ameliorating disease by introducing donor or autologous edited cells that replace diseased cells or secrete protective factors into affected tissues. Recent investigations are examining the role of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) transplants in ameliorating various diseases, from heart failure to neurodegeneration [45]. Transplantation of these cells from healthy donors or from edited autologous cells may exert protective effects by secreting soluble factors or by replacing select cell types, such as resident macrophages or microglia. In FRDA mouse models, peripheral delivery of wild-type HSPC in YG8R mice improves muscle weakness, DRG degeneration, and neural bioenergetics, mechanistically believed to be due to donor HSPC engraftment in the brain and differentiation into microglia that transfer wild-type FXN to affected neurons [46–48]. Similar phenotypic improvement is seen with wild-type MSC intrathecal delivery in FRDA YG8 mice, which demonstrate improved motor function and DRG survival, from MSC migration to DRGs and secretion of neurotrophic factors [49–51]. Implantation of donor cells into affected brains may provide neuroprotective benefits in FRDA, as implantation of FRDA-iPSC-derived microglial progenitors into the cerebellum of non-FRDA mice recapitulates the neurodegeneration observed in FRDA, suggesting that replacement of such cells with FXN wildtype progenitor cells may be a therapeutic approach [52]. Interestingly, the benefit to these YG8 FRDA mice is present with MSCs derived from both wild-type and YG8 mice themselves, suggesting that secretion of these neurotrophic factors does not depend on FXN expression; thus, clinical application of this strategy may not require healthy donor or even edited autologous MSCs [49]. Altogether, these studies suggest that MSC/HSPC transplantation represents a potentially effective means to ameliorate neurological decline in FRDA patients. Still, this approach may require autologous bone marrow transplantation, a procedure with significant and tangible complications [48]. There are no cellular therapy trials for FRDA active or recruiting presently, but the preclinical studies described here provide proof of concept for designing future trials. These studies are in fact the basis for Papillon Therapeutics’ PPL-001, a strategy that seeks to use CRISPR-edited removal of GAA repeats from autologous HSPCs to restore FXN expression. This product has received an Orphan Drug Designation from the FDA, so the first cellular therapy for FRDA entering clinical trials is on the horizon.

A limitation of cellular therapy is immune-mediated rejection of donor cells. Autologous cell donation could circumvent immune rejection, although genetic editing of autologous cells may inadvertently contribute to immunogenicity. Furthermore, if HSPC transplants from healthy donors require bone marrow depletion in FRDA recipients, then implementation of such a protocol as a clinical trial would impose a risk of bone marrow failure. If the mechanisms of the described cell therapies occur in patients, then the benefit of microglial replacement in nervous tissue is still questionable, as any protective effect these therapies may exert, whether by secretion of neurotropic factors or transfer of FXN protein, may not result in functional recovery if neurodegeneration is too advanced by the time of diagnosis and management.

Overall Limitations

Three issues confound all attempts to replace FXN in animals. First, while immunoassays of mass spectrometry-based approaches readily quantify FXN levels in solubilized tissue, details of quantification in other tissues are less well defined, and the relevant tissue for FXN assessment is commonly inaccessible [53, 54]. Muscle and cardiac tissue can be tested for FXN through biopsies (with different levels of invasiveness), but FXN cannot be assessed in living brain, and FXN is not a normal component of CSF. Thus, detection of increases in FXN in CNS must rely on downstream biomarkers in affected tissue for quantification of FXN replacement. Among soluble serum-based biomarkers, circulating neurofilament light (NFL) is elevated in younger FRDA patients, but is confounded by the age dependence of NFL; it is also higher in normal children for reasons that are unclear [55–58]. As a cytoskeletal protein, its levels conceivably could rise during normal axonal growth and repair, making interpretation of incomplete responses difficult [59]. Similar issues confound use of troponin as a marker of cardiac disease [60–62]. It is high consistently in FRDA in a manner that is minimally predictive of eventual outcome. Other blood biomarkers of disease status in FRDA are presently incompletely explored [63–65].

Imaging biomarkers using brain MRI may provide alternatives to serum biomarkers, as volume loss of brainstem regions, cerebellar DN, inferior and superior cerebellar peduncles are established disease features appreciable on MRI [66–68]. Additionally, cervical spine cross-sectional area decreases with disease progression, providing a simple and accessible means of examining therapeutic response at a broad range of institutions [69]. Volume loss of these regions correlates with disease onset, duration, and severity [70], thus these imaging modalities may provide a meaningful neurological readout if the therapeutic interventions discussed here are administered early in the disease course. However, the change over time of most of these is slow, and in unselected cohorts may not be sufficient to detect change over short time periods. Thus, they may be most useful for cohort selection to enhance the sensitivity of response.

Second, what level of FXN replacement is needed for clinical meaning? In unaffected tissue, the most severely affected patients have FXN levels around 2–5% of normal with the least affected patients having up to 30–40% of control [71, 72]. In affected tissues the level appears much lower, but the exact level defining clinical disease is unclear. The inactivation of cellular alleles through epigenetic silencing suggests that the correct measure is not the level of frataxin, but the number of cells that have been restored to a minimal sufficient level. The regulation of FXN levels differs somewhat between tissues, so extrapolating from blood levels to affected tissue does not clearly have meaning. In mice, animals can have as low as 2% of normal FXN levels without developing a phenotype, and cell culture models suggest that normal cellular functions can occur with levels less than 10% of normal [73–75]. This suggests that restoration of FXN to carrier levels is unnecessary, but the level that is clinically meaningful in specific tissues is not yet well-defined.

Third, all FXN restoration methods must address the issue of timing the therapy to disease course. While FRDA is a progressive disease, many of the neurological changes appear before presentation. This includes loss of large DRG cells before presentation, and early MRI changes happening by 5 years of age (Fig. 3) [68, 76]. Restoration of FXN after such times may provide limited benefit, though other affected tissues such as cardiac and skeletal muscle may have fewer developmental issues. During typical clinical progression, the major loss of neurons and axons occurs in the DN and in the corticospinal tract, but other areas such as the Purkinje cells may be involved in FRDA pathophysiology [77–80]. In later stages of disease (and potentially earlier), the retina becomes a clear target [81–83]. Unfortunately, preclinical experiments in animals with gene replacement and gene editing have generally not addressed these issues; most of the experiments have been performed in models in which FXN is knocked down after birth, thus, they model subacute FXN deficiency, not FRDA. Results comparing animal and cellular models can provide discrepant results. Conclusion

Advances in gene therapies have been rapid in the last decade and have considerable momentum to address genetic disorders such as FRDA. Gene addition strategies are the furthest along in clinical trials and may represent the first gene therapy for FRDA, while in cellular therapies, the indication of MSC transplantation for neuroprotection in general may allow for their application in FRDA indirectly. The goal of any of these strategies is to prevent or reverse neurological dysfunction by restoring FXN expression or the degenerative consequences of its deficiency. Special considerations that gene therapy must bear in mind for FRDA are: (1) FXN overexpression toxicity, (2) transduction efficiency in affected tissues, and (3) timing of the intervention during the neurodegenerative process to demonstrate meaningful clinical improvements. These issues and others are actively discussed among FRDA experts and guide trial design and implementation. The current genetic therapies approach FRDA from diverse angles. Epigenetic modification and ASO increase endogenous FXN gene expression, which avoids FXN protein overexpression and chromatin modification, but these techniques lack tissue specificity and may affect expression of off-target genes. Gene addition or editing require specific or modified vectors that facilitate tissue specificity but then pose the risk of toxic protein overexpression, off-target chromatin modification, or immunogenicity of the host to said vector. Furthermore, these vectors are typically designed to transduce only a single affected tissue, and if the immune response to one AAV-associated vector generalized to others, then a patient receiving AAV-delivered gene therapy targeting cardiomyocytes may be ineligible to receive a similar AAV-delivered therapy targeting neurons. This possibility has left many patients to reconsider participating in current AAV-associated gene therapies if only one affected tissue can be restored. Peptide replacement benefits by supplying only the target protein without any direct effects on host DNA, but the requirement of FXN protein to be fused to potentially immunogenic peptides required to facilitate membrane permeability introduces the risk of anaphylaxis. Cellular therapies via autologous HSPC/MSC transplantation may avoid the issues with gene/peptides replacement or gene editing altogether by providing direct neuroprotective effects or even leveraging mitochondrial transfer to restore FXN expression in affected tissues. However, these mechanisms demand further investigation and ultimately validation in human participants. These various strategies have demonstrated their efficacy in animal models, but at present it is too early to say whether there is clear benefit for FRDA patients. The possibility that FRDA may have a therapy that can prevent, or even reverse, disease progression is becoming a more concrete ideal with these advances in gene and cellular therapy.
