Rate of Participation in a Second Randomized Clinical Trial and Factors Associated with the Likelihood of Participation among IBD Patients who already Experienced a Randomized Clinical Trial
Authors: Roberto Gabbiadini, Demis Pitoni, Peter Bertoli, Giuseppe Privitera, Arianna Dal Buono, Patrizia Danieli, Alessandro Repici, Laura Loy, Cristina Bezzio, Alessandro Armuzzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the rate of participation in a second trial among inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients and to identify factors influencing their likelihood of re-engagement.MethodsRetrospective, single-center study. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with re-participation.ResultsA total of 29.0% participated in a second trial. In ulcerative colitis, distance >100 km from the clinic and steroid use before the first trial were associated with reduced re-participation. In Crohn’s disease (CD), long-standing disease and reimbursement during the first trial were associated with increased re-participation; previous CD-related surgery was associated with reduced re-participation.ConclusionsUnderstanding factors affecting re-participation in IBD trials is crucial to improving patient involvement in research.Keywords: inflammatory bowel disease, Crohn’s disease, ulcerative colitis, drug development, patients recruitment

Introduction

The development of new therapies for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) has become increasingly difficult, partly due to challenges in recruiting patients for clinical trials.1 Over the last 20 years, the average recruitment rate for IBD trials has decreased to approximately 0.1 patients per site per month.2 Prior experience with clinical trials may motivate patients to re-engage, but barriers such as logistical, clinical, and economic factors remain poorly understood.3,4 This study aimed to evaluate the rate of participation in a second randomized clinical trial (RCT) among IBD patients and to identify factors influencing their likelihood of re-engagement.

Methods

This retrospective, single-center study included IBD patients treated between 2015 and 2024. Patients who participated in at least one for-profit RCT were included. Data on demographics, IBD type, and clinical characteristics were collected. Kaplan-Meier analysis evaluated the time to participation in a second trial, stratified by disease type. Multivariate logistic regression with stepwise backward selection was performed to identify independent factors associated with re-participation in a second RCT. All candidate variables were initially included, and covariates were sequentially removed using backward conditional elimination based on the likelihood ratio test (P < .10 was used to retain variables). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P-values were reported for all variables retained in the final model. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Results

A total of 293 patients were included: 99 with Crohn’s disease (CD), 186 with ulcerative colitis (UC), and 8 with pouchitis. Baseline data are shown in Table 1. The median follow-up period was 30 months (range 1-169). During follow-up, 85 patients (29.0%) participated in a second clinical trial, after a median time of 80.7 months (59.2-102.3). Survival analysis (Figure 1) revealed no significant differences in participation rates between patients with CD, UC, and pouchitis (log-rank test; P > .05 for all comparisons). We conducted multivariate regression analyses separately for patients with UC and CD, but did not perform regression on the pouchitis cohort due to the exiguous sample size. In the UC cohort, distance >100 km from the clinic (OR, 0.484; 95% CI 0.238-0.984; P = .044) and steroid use before the first trial (OR, 0.083; 95% CI, 0.010-0.719; P = 0.024) were significantly associated with reduced re-participation. In the CD cohort, long-standing disease (OR, 3.279, 95% CI, 1.130-9.515, P = .029) and reimbursement during the first trial (OR, 3.047, 95% CI, 1.048-8.861, P = .041) were associated with increased re-participation; conversely, previous CD-related surgery was associated with reduced re-participation (OR, 0.363, 95% CI, 0.132-0.998, P = .050). Other factors, including the possibility of receiving placebo, the patient’s education level, the average number of visits per month, and the number of endoscopies performed during the first trial, were not significantly associated with re-participation in either cohort. Discussion

Recruitment in RCTs is essential for advancing medical knowledge and improving therapeutic options in IBD. Although in several surveys, most patients reported that their experience in an RCT was better than expected,3 with a high rate of willingness to participate in an RCT (61%, increasing to 78% among those with prior trial experience),5 our real-life study highlights important barriers to re-engagement in RCTs, with only 29% participating in a second trial. This probably reflects differences between the “hypothetical situation” of a survey and the reality of clinical practice.

We found no significant differences in participation rates between CD and UC, suggesting that disease type alone is not a primary determinant of trial engagement; conversely, logistical and clinical factors play a more significant role.

Regarding logistical factors, geographical distance emerged as a significant barrier in UC, with patients residing farther from the clinic demonstrating reduced re-participation rates. Decentralized trial models, including telemedicine and local trial sites, might enhance participation rates.3 Furthermore, the association between reimbursement and participation in CD suggests that compensation is important,4 although other studies have found no connection on this topic.5 In addition, there are concerns about patients ignoring trial risks when offered money.5,6

Unexpectedly, the risk of placebo exposure did not significantly influence re-engagement. Patients often worry that their condition may worsen if they receive a placebo, and they do not appear to understand the rationale behind the study design.7 Our finding may reflect effective communication during the first trial, which helped patients understand the scientific rationale and potential benefits of participation, even without active treatment.

Similarly, the number of endoscopies performed in the first trial did not reduce re-participation. Despite being poorly tolerated, patients with chronic conditions like IBD value these procedures for their benefit,5 especially when provided with supportive care during the trial.

Regarding clinical factors, steroid use before the trial reduced re-participation in UC patients, possibly reflecting the difficulties of managing patients with higher disease burden during the wash-out period and steroid tapering. This is further supported by the fact that steroid use was not relevant in CD, which is frequently characterized by a less symptomatic disease course. On the other hand, long-standing disease was associated with increased re-participation in individuals with CD, probably due to the fact that they may have already been exposed to multiple lines of biological therapies.

Conversely, previous CD-related surgery was associated with reduced re-participation. The need for surgery represents one of the most common fears among IBD patients.8 It is understandable that patients who failed the first RCT and had already undergone surgery before the first trial may prefer effective therapy over the risk of placebo. Another possible explanation is the difficulty of recruiting CD-operated patients due to the well-known poor agreement between clinical scores used in the trials (ie, Crohn’s Disease Activity Index) and the severity of mucosal inflammation.9,10

In conclusion, approximately one-third of patients re-participated in a second clinical trial. Long distance from the IBD clinic, steroid use before the first trial, and previous CD-related surgery could be barriers to re-participation in RCTs.

A limitation of our study is its single-center design, as it could limit generalizability across diverse geographic, socioeconomic, and institutional settings, all of which influence trial recruitment. Furthermore, although it is our standard clinical practice to discuss the various therapeutic options with patients after the first RCT, including the possibility of participating in a second RCT, the retrospective nature of the study prevents us from determining the reasons for non-participation. Nevertheless, it is plausible to speculate that patient refusal may have been the most likely reason. In addition, another limitation of the study is the lack of analysis of relationships with trial staff. However, a key strength is that it is the first real-life analysis of re-participation in RCTs, highlighting aspects that can help physicians attend to patients’ concerns and overcome obstacles.

Addressing these challenges through decentralized trial models and innovative study designs (eg, shorter wash-out periods) could improve participation and accelerate advancements in IBD research.
