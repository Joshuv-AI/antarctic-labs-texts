Newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia in Fit patients: 2026 treatment algorithms
Authors: Naseema Gangat, Farhad Ravandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe introduction of venetoclax (a BCL2 inhibitor) and targeted therapies, including inhibitors of CD33, FLT3, IDH1, IDH2, and menin, has expanded treatment options for newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia (AML). In younger, fit patients, the primary goal remains long-term survival, which in most cases is secured through allogeneic stem cell transplant. Transplant in first complete remission is recommended for FLT-ITD, TP53 mutated, KMT2A rearranged, AML with other adverse genetic abnormalities, and is considered in most intermediate-risk patients. It is also recommended in relapsed/refractory disease or persistent measurable residual disease (MRD). The role of intensive chemotherapy, such as cytarabine (7) plus anthracycline (3), is limited to patients with core-binding factor AML, NPM1 mutation, CEBPA bZIP mutation and those with intermediate-risk disease. Intensive regimens such as FLAG-IDA and CLIA plus venetoclax have shown impressive long-term outcomes, but their use is not widespread. In FLT3 mutated AML, 7 + 3 plus an FLT3 inhibitor (midostaurin or quizartinib) remains a standard, with venetoclax-hypomethylating agent-FLT3 inhibitor triplets emerging as an alternative. Similarly, in IDH1/2 mutated AML, venetoclax-hypomethylating agent with or without IDH1/2 inhibitor combinations challenge intensive chemotherapy approaches. Patients with TP53 mutations or other adverse-risk features, where intensive chemotherapy is known to be less effective, should be referred for clinical trials. There remains ongoing debate regarding optimal management of fit patients with newly diagnosed AML without targetable mutations, as emerging data suggest that venetoclax- hypomethylating agents may be comparable to intensive chemotherapy in selected patients proceeding to transplant. Accordingly, treatment decisions should be individualized to maximize remission while minimizing toxicity. Subject terms: Acute myeloid leukaemia, Acute myeloid leukaemia

Introduction

Over the past decade, treatments for newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia (AML) have expanded beyond conventional cytarabine and anthracycline-based chemotherapy with the introduction of venetoclax (a BCL2 inhibitor) [1] as well as molecularly targeted therapies including inhibitors of CD33, FLT3 [2, 3], IDH1 [4, 5], IDH2 [6], and menin [7, 8]. Despite these advances, allogeneic stem cell transplant (ASCT) remains the cornerstone of curative therapy for the majority of younger, fit patients with AML [9]. The incremental value of these newer therapies on long-term survival remains to be fully defined and will be critical for determining their optimal sequencing within treatment strategies. This is particularly relevant for younger patients who are candidates for ASCT. In this setting, the primary rationale for incorporating novel therapies is to secure deep and durable remissions, ideally with undetectable measurable residual disease (MRD) while minimizing treatment-associated toxicity. In this review, we outline our management approach for fit patients with newly diagnosed AML, acknowledging that these strategies will evolve as clinical trial data mature and highly sensitive MRD detection techniques become more widely implemented.

Treatment selection (intensive vs less-intensive therapies)

Patient-related factors, including fitness [10], physiological age, and medical or social comorbidities together with disease-related features, particularly genetic profile, are the primary determinants guiding treatment selection in AML. Please refer to the companion paper on newly diagnosed AML in unfit patients for details on measures of patient fitness [11].

Genetic risk stratification for patients receiving intensive therapy is traditionally based on the ELN-2022 genetic risk model [12] which considers three risk categories: favorable (core-binding factor, RUNX1-RUNX1T1 or CBFB-MYH11, NPM1 MUT without FLT3-ITD or adverse karyotype, and CEBPA bZIP MUT), intermediate (karyotype not classified as adverse or favorable, NPM1 MUT with FLT3-ITD, wild type NPM1 with FLT3-ITD without adverse risk genetic lesions, t(9;11)(p21.3;q23.3)-MLLT3-KMT2A) and adverse (adverse karyotype, BCR/ABL1, TP53 MUT, ASXL1MUT, BCOR MUT, EZH2 MUT, RUNX1 MUT, SF3B1 MUT, SRSF2 MUT, STAG2 MUT, U2AF1 MUT, and/or ZRSR2 MUT). Among these categories, favorable-risk AML is most effectively managed with intensive chemotherapy–based approaches, which remain the standard therapeutic strategy in this population.

In recent years, favorable outcomes with venetoclax (Ven) combined with hypomethylating agents (HMA) in elderly or unfit patients with AML [1, 13, 14], have led to its use in selected younger and fit patients (Table 1). Emerging data indicate that oral decitabine–cedazuridine plus Ven may achieve outcomes similar to Ven-azacitidine or intravenous decitabine–Ven, while offering the convenience of an all-oral treatment approach [15]. In this context, the PARADIGM study compared intensive induction therapy (7 + 3 or CPX-351) with Ven plus azacitidine in younger patients with AML (median age 65 years), excluding those with CBF, FLT3 MUT or NPM1 MUT [16], In this study, the rate of complete remission (CR) with or without count recovery (CRi) was higher in the Ven-azacitidine arm than with intensive therapy (81% vs 55%) and a greater proportion of patients proceeded to ASCT (61% vs 40%) [16]. Event-free survival (EFS) was significantly superior in the Ven-azacitidine arm (53% vs 39%; HR 0.61). Ven-azacitidine was also associated with lower toxicity and shorter inpatient stays, without early mortality [16]. Similar findings were reported in another phase II randomized study comparing 7 + 3 with Ven plus decitabine, in which the composite complete remission (CRc) rate was higher with the Ven-based regimen (89% vs 79%) along with fewer infectious complications (63% vs 90%) [17]. A Mayo Clinic study of 600 newly diagnosed AML patients treated with CPX-351 (n = 112) or Ven-HMA (n = 488) demonstrated similar CR/CRi rates (55% vs 60%) and overall survival (10 months vs 13 months), including in AML with myelodysplasia-related gene (MRG) mutations, with fewer infections in patients receiving Ven-HMA (62% vs 83%) [18]. Taken together, these findings have generated clinical equipoise regarding the optimal use of intensive chemotherapy versus Ven-HMA combination therapy in fit patients with AML, particularly in those destined for ASCT.

Additionally, there is increasing interest in incorporating Ven with intensive chemotherapy backbones, including 7 + 3 [19, 20], FLAG-IDA (fludarabine, cytarabine, granulocyte colony stimulating factor and idarubicin) [21, 22], and CLIA regimen (cladribine, high-dose cytarabine, idarubicin) [23, 24] (Table 2). In a single-arm study of FLAG-IDA plus Ven in newly-diagnosed younger/fit patients (median age, 45 years; 48% ELN adverse risk) conducted at MD Anderson cancer center, long-term follow-up (median, 32 months) demonstrated a CRc rate of 95%, with 90% achieving MRD negativity by flow cytometry and the 3-year overall survival (OS) and EFS rates were 66% and 64%, respectively [22].

Similarly, the combination of CLIA plus Ven in newly diagnosed younger/fit patients (median age, 49 years) resulted in a CRc rate of 93%, with 71% achieving MRD negativity [23, 24]. Comparable efficacy signals have also been reported with the 7 + 3 plus Ven regimen [19, 20].

The phase II MyeloMATCH trials (NCT05554406, NCT05554393) are evaluating frontline strategies in younger patients (18–59 years) with newly-diagnosed AML stratified by ELN 2017 risk. In high-risk AML, approaches include CPX-351 (liposomal formulation of daunorubicin and cytarabine)–based regimens and Ven combinations (CPX-351-Ven, 7 + 3-Ven, Ven-azacitidine), while in intermediate-risk, 7 + 3 is being compared with Ven-based combinations (7 + 3-Ven, and Ven-azacitidine). These studies aim to clarify the role of Ven combinations in frontline therapy for younger patients with higher-risk AML.

Overall, the management approach to fit patients with AML has become increasingly nuanced with the approval of Ven and targeted therapies, including FLT3, IDH1, IDH2, and menin inhibitors (Figs. 1–3). In the sections that follow, we outline practical treatment strategies for AML subtypes based on their molecular profile.

AML with nucleophosmin-1 mutations (NPM1MUT)

NPM1 MUT occur in approximately 30% of adults with AML [25, 26]. Clinically, both the International Consensus Classification (ICC) [27] and the World Health Organization (WHO) [28] recognize NPM1 MUT AML as a distinct entity. Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-PCR) is the recommended method for detecting NPM1 mutations because of its high sensitivity and specificity [29]. To date, over 50 different mutations located within exon 12 of the NPM1 gene have been identified. Three mutation types (A, B, and D) represent about 90% of NPM1 MUT: type A (c.860_863dupTCTG) accounts for 70–80% of cases while type B and D (c.863_864insCATG and c.863_864insCCTG, respectively) together account for 15–20% [29]. The Mayo Clinic laboratory offers NPM1 MUT testing as two complementary assays. The first is a highly sensitive quantitative RT-PCR that measures NPM1 transcript levels (types A, B, and D) with an analytical sensitivity of ~0.01% (mutant/ABL1 transcript copies) [30]. The second is a qualitative DNA-based exon 12 fragment analysis, which detects all NPM1 MUT types, including rare non-A/B/D variants, but with lower sensitivity ( ~ 5% mutant/wild-type DNA). NGS based NPM1 MRD assay is available through MD Anderson and Invivoscribe which can identify NPM1 MUT variants ‘A,’ ‘B,’ ‘D,’ and ‘others’ at a sensitivity level of 0.001%.

Prognosis

Within the ELN 2022 prognostic model, NPM1 MUT AML without FLT3-ITD or adverse karyotype is classified as favorable [12]. However, co-mutations are frequent and may significantly modify prognosis. In the HARMONY study, the combination of FLT-ITD and DNMT3A MUT identified a particularly adverse subset, whereas FLT3-ITD and DNMT3AWT (without IDH1/2 MUT mutations) and DNMT3A MUT /IDH1/2 MUT cases without FLT3-ITD were intermediate risk; TET2MUT also shifted DNMT3AWT toward intermediate risk. [31]. Overall, patients with NPM1 MUT AML were stratified into favorable (51.8%), intermediate (24.8%), and adverse (23.4%) risk groups, with median overall survival of 14.4, 2.2, and 0.9 years, respectively [31]. Additionally, the prognostic impact of MDS-associated mutations (ASXL1, BCOR, EZH2, RUNX1, SF3B1, SRSF2, STAG2, U2AF1 or ZRSR2) mutations remains unclear [32, 33]. The above illustrates the heterogeneity within NPM1 MUT AML and supports incorporation of newer therapies such as Ven and menin inhibitors within intensive chemotherapy–based approaches and consideration of ASCT in select patients, particularly those with persistent MRD. Notably, in a UK National Cancer Research Institute AML17 study, pre-transplant NPM1 MRD positivity was strongly associated with post-transplant relapse and inferior survival [34].

Management recommendations

We recommend induction treatment, usually a combination of cytarabine for 7 days with an anthracycline (either daunorubicin 60 mg/m2 or idarubicin 12 mg/m2) for 3 days (7 + 3 regimen) followed by consolidation with 3 to 4 cycles of high dose cytarabine (1.5–3 g/m2), In this subset of patients, ASCT in first CR is not recommended and reserved for relapsed/refractory disease, or MRD+. NPM1 MUT AML with FLT3 MUT should also receive a FLT3 inhibitor, midostaurin (50 mg twice daily) [2] or quizartinib (40 mg daily) [3] during days 8–21 of induction and in all cycles of consolidation and eligible patients, particularly those with co-mutations in DNMT3A and FLT3-ITD considered for ASCT (see section on AML with FLT3MUT). The intensive FLAG-IDA plus Ven and CLIA plus Ven regimens from the MD Anderson Cancer Center have been reported to have impressive MRD-negative response and survival outcomes and should be considered in appropriate patients when feasible (Fig. 2).

MRD monitoring

MRD assessment should be performed with NPM1 transcripts (PCR) in the bone marrow or peripheral blood after two cycles of intensive chemotherapy, at the end of consolidation, and every 3 months for 24 months after treatment completion [35]. MRD positivity is defined as ≥0.1% or failure to achieve a ≥3–4 log reduction at completion of consolidation. Low-level detectable MRD (<0.1%) is considered MRD-negative, as its presence at the end of consolidation is associated with a very low risk of relapse [35].

Investigational approaches

With respect to investigational approaches, the Phase 1b Chemotherapy And Venetoclax in Elderly Acute Myeloid Leukemia Trial (CAVEAT) consisted of induction therapy with cytarabine for 5 days and idarubicin for 2 days (5 + 2) plus Ven. [36]. CR and 2-year OS in this older but fit cohort were highest for patients with NPM1MUT (81% and 69%), and median OS was 43.9 vs 15.4 months in NPM1 WT [36]. Similarly, in other studies incorporating Ven with intensive chemotherapy such as FLAG-Ida-Ven (n = 77) [22] and CLIA-Ven (n = 95) [24] in newly diagnosed AML, 12% and 31% of patients harbored NPM1 MUT, and yielded CRc rates of over 90% (Table 2).

Biologically, NPM1MUT AML overexpress the HOX gene (HOXA9) and activates the HOXA9/MEIS/CCAAT enhancer-binding protein alpha (CEBPA)/lysine methyltransferase 2 (KMT2A) axis [37–39]. These findings form the therapeutic basis for inhibition of menin-KMT2A in NPM1 MUT AML [40, 41]. Currently, two menin inhibitors, revumenib and ziftomenib, are FDA-approved in the relapsed setting and investigations with multiple menin inhibitors in frontline combinations are ongoing for newly diagnosed NPM1 MUT AML. Preliminary phase 1 results from frontline 7 + 3 plus ziftomenib (600 mg daily on days 8-28) in 35 patients with NPM1 MUT showed a CR/CRi rate of 94% [42] (Table 3).

AML with FMS-like tyrosine kinase mutations (FLT3MUT)

Mutations in FLT3 are found in 30% of newly diagnosed AML patients [26]; two separate mutations, i) internal tandem duplications (FLT3-ITD), occur in 25% [43], and point mutations in the tyrosine kinase domain, primarily in the activation loop in codon D835 (FLT3-TKD), in the remainder ~7% of cases [44].

Clinically available assays for detection of FLT3 include PCR and next-generation sequencing (NGS) with variable sensitivities: 5% for qualitative PCR and 0.001% for NGS. With regard to disease prognostication and monitoring, it is important to conduct a quantitative analysis of FLT3-ITD; however, there is a need for caution in the interpretation of the FLT3-ITD mutant burden based on the variant allele fraction (VAF) and allelic ratio (AR). AR determined by PCR is calculated as the ratio of the area under the curve of the mutant allele to the wild-type allele. In contrast, NGS quantifies FLT3-ITD as VAF, which is calculated as the fraction of mutant alleles relative to all FLT3 alleles (wild-type+mutant).

Prognosis

Historically, high FLT3-ITD AR (>0.5) and VAF (>30%) were associated with higher relapse rates and poorer survival [45–47]. However, outcomes of FLT3MUT patients have improved with the introduction of FLT3 inhibitors, and ELN 2022 has placed patients with FLT3-ITD in the intermediate-risk group [48]. In contrast, FLT-TKD are associated with more favorable outcomes, particularly in the context of NPM1 MUT AML [49].

FLT3 inhibitors

There are two types of FLT3 inhibitors: Type 1 inhibitors, such as midostaurin and gilteritinib, are less selective tyrosine kinase inhibitors, whereas the type 2 FLT3 inhibitor quizartinib is highly selective for FLT3-ITD [50]. Midostaurin was the first FLT3 inhibitor to receive regulatory approval for newly diagnosed FLT3MUT AML in combination with 7 + 3 chemotherapy, based on its activity against both FLT3-ITD and TKD mutations [2]. In the phase 3 placebo-controlled RATIFY trial, midostaurin 50 mg twice daily (days 8-21) was added to induction with cytarabine 200 mg/m2 days 1–7 and daunorubicin 60 mg/m2 days 1–3, in patients up to 60 years of age [2]. While CR rates were similar between the two arms (59% vs 54%), midostaurin significantly improved survival outcomes, with longer median OS (74.7 vs 35.6 months) and EFS (8.2 vs 3.0 months) [2] (Table 4).

Gilteritinib, another FLT3 inhibitor with activity against both ITD and TKD mutations, is currently approved for relapsed/refractory disease based on ADMIRAL trial results and is under investigation in newly diagnosed AML [51]. In a phase II randomized study of patients up to 70 years old with newly diagnosed FLT3MUT AML, gilteritinib was compared with midostaurin in combination with intensive induction chemotherapy (daunorubicin 90 mg/m² for 3 days plus cytarabine 100 mg/m² for 7 days) [52]. Baseline genomic features, including the frequency of co-occurring NPM1MUT and DNMT3A MUT, were balanced between the arms [52].CRc rates were higher with gilteritinib (86% vs 72%), including among patients harboring FLT3-ITD with co-mutations in NPM1 and DNMT3A (96% vs 72%) [52]. MRD negativity rates were similar; however, a greater proportion of patients in the gilteritinib arm proceeded to ASCT (66% vs 46%) [52]. Whether these differences translate into an overall survival benefit remains to be determined. Results from the Phase 3 HOVON 156/AMLSG28-18/PASHA trial comparing gilteritinib vs midostaurin in newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD AML were recently unveiled; a total of 768 patients were randomized to receive gilteritinib 120 mg daily or midostaurin 50 mg twice daily along with daunorubicin and cytarabine in cycle 2, following cycle 1 7 + 3 induction therapy [53]. Although median OS was comparable, median EFS was superior for gilteritinib vs midostaurin (51.1 vs 19.9 months; p = 0.05), and relapse rates after CR were lower with gilteritinib vs midostaurin (21% vs 36%; p = 0.0003) [53].

Quizartinib, in contrast, is a more selective FLT3 inhibitor with activity primarily against FLT3-ITD. In a phase 3, placebo-controlled QuANTUM-First study enrolling patients up to 75 years of age, the addition of quizartinib to intensive chemotherapy resulted in similar CR rates in both arms (55% each), but significantly improved survival outcomes, including OS (31.9 vs 15.1 months) and EFS (39.3 vs 13.6 months) [3]. OS rates at 24 months were 54.7% and 44.7% in the quizartinib and placebo arms, respectively; however, in transplant-censored analyses, the respective median OS were 20.8 months in the quizartinib arm and 12.9 months in the placebo arm, with the survival benefit limited to younger patients [3] (Table 4).

Notably, no randomized trials have directly compared quizartinib with midostaurin in the frontline setting. Data from the QuANTUM-First and RATIFY studies showed that quizartinib yielded a risk reduction in mortality of roughly 47% in patients with a higher FLT3 VAF (>50%) compared to midostaurin with a risk reduction of 20%; however, cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously [54, 55]. Furthermore, patients with a high FLT3-ITD AR treated with midostaurin in the AMLSG 16-10 trial did not show a significant improvement in OS compared to patients in the historical cohort treated with chemotherapy alone [56].

Management recommendations

Currently, for patients with FLT3-TKD, we recommend induction with 7 + 3 plus midostaurin followed by consolidation with 3-4 cycles of high-dose cytarabine (1.5–3 g/m2) in combination with midostaurin. For patients with FLT3-ITD AML, induction with 7 + 3 in combination with either quizartinib (40 mg daily) or midostaurin (50 mg twice daily, days 8–21) is appropriate, followed by continuation of the FLT3 inhibitor during consolidation. Although both agents remain reasonable options for newly diagnosed FLT3-ITD AML, most clinicians favor quizartinib in the absence of significant cardiac comorbidity. However, its use requires careful monitoring due to the risk of QTc prolongation and associated torsades de pointes or other serious cardiac arrhythmias. The MD Anderson group prefers high-dose cytarabine-based regimens with the addition of either gilteritinib or quizartinib, with reported favorable tolerability and encouraging long-term outcomes (Figs. 2 and 3). It is to be noted that gilteritinib is not FDA-approved as frontline therapy.

ASCT should be considered for all patients with FLT3-ITD AML, particularly given the limited availability of standardized NGS-based MRD assessment. In MRD-positive cases, Ven–HMA plus a FLT3 inhibitor (gilteritinib) may be used to achieve MRD negativity prior to transplant. Post-transplant FLT3 inhibitor maintenance is recommended, particularly in patients with a peri-transplant positive MRD test. In a randomized study of post-transplant maintenance, gilteritinib improved relapse-free survival (RFS) but did not meet statistical significance overall; however, a clear benefit was observed in MRD-positive patients, with no benefit in MRD-negative cases [57]. On the other hand, in the QuANTUM-First study, quizartinib maintenance did not improve OS post-transplant (except for those who were MRD positive), but conferred a significant OS benefit in patients who did not undergo transplant (MRD negative; 3-year OS 89%) [58]. These findings support a risk-adapted approach; prioritizing transplant and post-transplant FLT3 inhibitor maintenance, while recognizing that select MRD-negative patients on maintenance FLT3 inhibitor may achieve durable survival without transplant.

Investigational approaches

There is an emerging role for FLT3 inhibitor-based triplet therapy (FLT3i-Ven-HMA) in FLT3MUT AML [59–63] In a single-center study, the combination of Ven, azacitidine, and FLT3 inhibitor achieved a CR rate of 93% with FLT3 MRD negativity rates of 60% after cycle 2 and 90% after cycle 4; 3-year OS rate was 45% for FLT3-ITD and 76% for FLT3-TKD [59–61, 64]. The ongoing multi-center VICEROY study is evaluating the efficacy and optimal dosing of Ven, azacitidine, and gilteritinib in patients with newly diagnosed FLT3MUT AML who are ineligible for intensive induction [65]. The regimen includes Ven (200 mg or 400 mg daily), azacitidine (75 mg/m², days 1–7), and gilteritinib (80 mg daily), with day 14 bone marrow assessment guiding treatment duration. Among 44 patients, CR rates were 70% with Ven 200 mg and 65% with Ven 400 mg, while CRc rates were 90% and 91%, respectively; 12-month OS was 64% and 77% for the 200 mg and 400 mg cohorts, respectively [65].

Core-binding factor AML

Core-binding factor (CBF-AML) is defined by t(8;21)(q22;q22) or inv(16)(p13q22)/t(16;16), resulting in RUNX1/RUNX1T1 (AML1/ETO) and CBFB/MYH11 fusions, respectively [66, 67]. It is classified by the ICC as AML with recurrent genetic abnormalities and accounts for approximately 15% of all AML [27]. CBF fusions are detected using RT-PCR, which is the preferred method for MRD monitoring.

Prognosis

CBF-AML is generally associated with increased sensitivity to cytarabine-based therapy [68]. Although both t(8;21) and inv16 AML are considered favorable, relapses are more frequent in patients with t(8;21) [69]. Studies with long-term follow-up have demonstrated a median OS of <5 years, with a 30%–50% relapse rate [70]. Additional cytogenetic abnormalities occur in over 60% of t(8;21) AML patients and in 35%–40% of those with inv(16) [71]; in a multicenter retrospective study of 537 patients with CBF AML, the presence of a complex karyotype did not confer a worse prognosis [72]. On the other hand, KIT mutations, which occur in 20%–30% of cases, may be associated with an increased risk of relapse [73].

Management recommendations

Treatment in young, fit patients with CBF-AML typically consists of a 7 + 3 regimen, which achieves CR rates of approximately 90% in both subtypes. The addition of gemtuzumab ozogamicin (GO), a CD33-directed monoclonal antibody–drug conjugate, has been shown in a meta-analysis of five randomized trials to reduce the risk of relapse and improve OS, although dosing schedules have varied across studies [74]. The ALFA-0701 trial utilized GO at a dose of 3 mg/m² on days 1, 4, and 7 during induction, with additional doses administered on day 1 of two consolidation cycles and showed that GO improved EFS (median; 19.6 vs. 11.9 months) and OS (34 vs. 19.2 months), however, the difference was no longer statistically significant in the final analysis [75]. Beyond 7 + 3, FLAG-GO has also been explored as frontline therapy in CBF-AML, achieving a 95% CRc rate with 5% induction mortality in a cohort of 45 patients [76]. At 3 years, OS and RFS were 78% and 85%, respectively, supporting its efficacy in this population [76].

In a Mayo Clinic study of 70 patients with CBF-AML treated with 7 + 3, CR/CRi rates approached 100%, confirming the highly favorable response of this AML subtype to intensive therapy [77]. However, relapse was documented in 45% of patients, and whether or not the addition of GO to 7 + 3 is effective in reducing relapse rates in CBF-AML requires examination in a controlled setting [77]. GO is associated with toxicities including sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (necessitating ursodiol prophylaxis) [78], as well as myelosuppression and thrombocytopenia; these risks should be carefully considered. The addition of gemtuzumab is favored by F.R, whereas NG reserves its use for select cases without significant hepatic dysfunction, uncontrolled infections, or critical thrombocytopenia.

MRD monitoring

For disease monitoring, bone marrow MRD assessment by PCR is recommended every 3–6 months for at least 2 years, with consideration of ASCT in patients with persistent MRD or relapsed disease. After induction chemotherapy, a ≥3-log reduction is considered optimal, and at the end of consolidation and during follow-up, MRD < 0.001% or undetectable is optimal, while persistent low-level disease warrants caution and rising or recurrent MRD (≥0.1%) defines relapse [35]. Notably, low-level detection of CBF transcripts at the end of treatment may reflect expression from non-leukemic cells, such as mast cells [35].

AML with isocitrate dehydrogenase mutations (IDH1/2MUT)

IDH1/2MUT are present in ~20% of patients with AML [79] and typically involve three mutational hotspots, resulting in single amino acid substitutions at codon R132 of IDH1 (IDH1R132) and codons R140 and R172 of IDH2 (IDH2R140 and IDH2R172) [80].

Prognosis

Prognostic impact of IDH MUT AML is context-dependent, influenced by the presence of NPM1 MUT and FLT3-ITD [81]. In a French cohort of 206 intensively treated patients with IDH1/2MUT AML, 90% achieved CR, with NPM1 MUT (seen in IDH1R132 and IDH2R140) associated with improved OS (61% vs 38%), while DNMT3A MUT predicted inferior outcomes (5-year OS 37% vs 61%) [82]. Combined NPM1/DNMT3A status further stratified prognosis, identifying favorable (NPM1 MUT /DNMT3A wt, 5-year OS 74%) and unfavorable (NPM1wt/DNMT3A MUT, 5-year OS 29%) groups [82].

IDH inhibitors and venetoclax

Given the availability of targeted IDH1 (ivosidenib, olutasidenib) [4, 5, 83, 84] and IDH2 inhibitors (enasidenib) [84], including triplet combination therapies, the optimal induction regimen for fit patients with IDH1/2MUT AML remains unclear (Table 5). Both ivosidenib and enasidenib are under evaluation in combination with 7 + 3 chemotherapy [85]. In a multicenter, phase 1 study in newly diagnosed IDH1/2MUT AML (median age: 63 years), patients received induction therapy with cytarabine 200 mg/m2/d for 7 days and either daunorubicin 60 mg/m2 or idarubicin 12 mg/m2/ for 3 days and ivosidenib 500 mg once daily. Overall, CR was 70%, OS rate at 3/5 years was 67/61%, and 48% underwent ASCT in CR1 [86]. The regimen was as well tolerated as induction/consolidation chemotherapy alone, with no new safety signals compared with ivosidenib monotherapy [86]. The value of enasidenib and ivosidenib to intensive induction chemotherapy in newly diagnosed IDHMUT AML is currently being investigated in a phase 3, multicenter, double-blind randomized clinical trial (NCT03839771).

IDH inhibitors show synergy with Ven, and triplet regimens with Ven-HMA are under active study. In newly diagnosed IDH1MUT AML, ivosidenib–Ven achieved CR/CRi rates of 83%, increasing to 90% with azacitidine, with deeper responses (MRD-negative 75% vs 50%) and improved 24-month OS (75% vs 58%) [83]. In a pooled analysis of 60 unfit patients with newly diagnosed IDH1/2MUT AML treated with IDH inhibitor–based triplets (ivosidenib–Ven–azacitidine or IDH inhibitor–oral decitabine–Ven), CR/CRi was achieved in 92%, with a 2-year OS of 69% and a cumulative incidence of relapse of 24% [84].

Similarly, in a multicenter phase Ib/II study of 40 patients with IDH1MUT newly diagnosed AML, the triplet regimen of azacitidine, Ven, and ivosidenib produced a CRc rate of 93%, and 92% MRD negativity among responders [87]. With a median follow-up of 35 months, median OS was not reached, and the estimated 3-year overall OS was 79%, with durable remissions and a manageable safety profile [87]. A phase 3 randomized trial (EVOLVE-1) is ongoing to confirm the benefit of frontline triplet therapy in IDH1MUT mutated AML.

With regard to the role of Ven in combination with intensive therapy, a propensity matched cohort study which compared Ven plus intensive therapy (n = 85), vs intensive therapy alone (n = 194), included 20% of patients with IDH1/2MUT; 86% of patients in the Ven plus intensive chemotherapy cohort had an MRD-negative CRc rate compared with 61% in the intensive chemotherapy cohort and a higher proportion were transitioned to ASCT (79% vs 57%), however, OS was no different [88]. Also, in a single-centre retrospective analysis of 124 patients (99 IDHwt, 10 IDH1MUT, and 15 IDH2MUT). Treated with FLAG-Ida-Ven or CLIA-Ven as part of clinical trials, CR/CRi was achieved in 92% of IDHwt, 80% of IDH1MUT, (including 7 of 8 MRD negative), and 100% of IDH2MUT (12 of 15 MRD negative). Furthermore, OS at 3 years for IDHwt, IDH1MUT, and IDH2MUT patients was 76%, 60%, and 61%, respectively [89].

Management recommendations

We encourage enrollment in clinical trials incorporating IDH1/2 inhibitors in combination with intensive chemotherapy (7 + 3) or Ven-HMA. If trial is not a possibility, 7 + 3 is a reasonable standard option, while FLAG-IDA plus Ven and CLIA plus Ven regimens represent promising alternative regimens. For patients with concomitant NPM1 MUT or FLT3 MUT (see respective sections).

MRD monitoring

IDH1/2 MUT MRD testing has primarily been investigated in the context of intensive therapy. In an analysis of 163 patients with newly diagnosed IDH1/2 MUT AML enrolled in HOVON/SAKK trials, persistence of IDH1 MUT in CR (40%) was not associated with increased relapse risk or inferior OS [90]. In contrast, persistent IDH2 MUT (47%) was associated with a higher risk of relapse, although without a clear impact on survival, particularly in the absence of co-occurring NPM1 MUT or FLT3-ITD [90]. Importantly, IDH1/2 MUT may persist in remission as part of clonal hematopoiesis rather than active leukemia. Although digital droplet PCR enables sensitive detection, mutation persistence alone should not be considered definitive evidence of residual disease; interpretation must account for clonal hierarchy, co-mutations, and blast percentage.

AML with TP53 mutations (TP53MUT)

TP53 mutations occur in 10-15% of patients with AML and are frequently observed in the therapy-related setting [91, 92]. There is no clear consensus on how best to define allelic status based on VAF thresholds in TP53MUT AML. TP53 inactivation generally follows a multihit model requiring biallelic alterations, which are historically associated with complex/monosomal karyotype, poor response to intensive therapy, and inferior survival [93, 94]. As a result, TP53MUT AML is often approached as a high-risk entity regardless of VAF or allelic burden as illustrated by a recent hierarchical prognostic model for TP53MUT myeloid neoplasms, which confirmed dismal outcomes (median OS 4 months) for AML patients with TP53MUT and VAF ≥ 2% [95].

Prognosis

To date, none of the approved therapies has shown durable efficacy in TP53MUT AML. In regard to intensive chemotherapy, CPX-351 yields suboptimal responses in TP53MUT AML compared with TP53 wild-type disease [96]. Real-world data evaluating TP53MUT AML treated with Ven-HMA vs. HMA alone demonstrated higher CR/CRi rates and a greater proportion of patients proceeding to ASCT; however, no OS benefit was observed with the addition of Ven [97]. While incorporation of Ven into FLAG-IDA showed CRc rate of 95% and a 3-year OS of 66%, only a small proportion of patients (8%) with TP53MUT were included in the study [22]. All of the TP53MUT cases achieved CRc; however, responses were short-lived, with a median OS of 13 months [22].

Management recommendations

Based on the limitations of existing therapies, we recommend prioritizing enrollment in clinical trials for patients with TP53MUT AML whenever feasible, followed by ASCT. In patients not eligible for clinical trials, Ven-HMA combination remains a reasonable off-trial option, although responses are often short-lived, while cladribine plus low-dose cytarabine and Ven as utilized at MD Anderson represents another approach. ASCT remains the only approach within the TP53MUT AML that offers a chance for improved long-term survival [98]; in a multicenter study conducted by the COMMAND consortium, 68 of 370 (18%) patients with TP53MUT proceeded to ASCT; the median EFS and OS were 14.6 and 30.5 months, respectively, among patients who underwent transplant after first induction [99]. In another retrospective analysis of 240 patients with TP53MUT MDS and AML undergoing ASCT, those with VAF < 50% and without complex or 5q/7q cytogenetic abnormalities had a 2-year progression-free survival (PFS) of 60% vs 22% in patients with TP53 VAF < 50% but with complex and/or 5q/7q abnormalities, underlining the adverse prognostic impact of complex/monosomal karyotype in TP53MUT MDS and AML [100].

Investigational approaches

In recent years, several immunotherapy-based strategies have been investigated in TP53MUT AML. Notably, the TP53 reactivator eprenetapopt (APR-246) [101] and the anti-CD47 antibody magrolimab [102] had shown initial promise. Magrolimab with azacitidine showed promising efficacy in newly diagnosed AML, including 83% of patients with TP53MUT [103]. This led to randomized trails evaluating magrolimab+azacitidine vs. investigator choice in TP53MUT AML ineligible for intensive chemotherapy (ENHANCE-2) [104] and Ven-azacitidine plus magrolimab vs. Ven-azacitidine (ENHANCE-3) [102]. Both studies were discontinued due to futility. Among ongoing newer therapies, rezetapopt, a Y220C variant–selective TP53 reactivator, has demonstrated promising preclinical activity in AML, with evidence that combination strategies targeting MDM2, XPO1, or BCL-2 may be required for optimal apoptosis induction [105]. A clinical trial evaluating rezetapopt in TP53MUT MDS and AML (NCT06616636) is currently ongoing.

AML with KMT2A rearrangement (KMT2Ar)

Balanced rearrangements involving the lysine methyltransferase 2a gene (KMT2A; 11q23.3), occur in 3–6% of adults with de novo AML and are typically associated with therapy-related disease following exposure to topoisomerase II inhibitors [106]. Over 130 fusion partners have been identified, most commonly the MLLT3 gene (t (9;11), accounting for 30% of cases.

Prognosis

ELN 2022 classifies KMT2A::MLLT3 as intermediate risk, and other KMT2Ar (ELL, AFDN, MLLT10)as adverse risk [46], although prognosis remains inconsistent across studies. In the HARMONY cohort (n = 205), OS did not differ by fusion partner; instead, adverse factors included age >60, secondary AML, and DNMT3AMUT or KRASMUT, while in younger de novo AML, KRASMUT or TP53MUT predicted lower CR rates (50% vs 86%) and shorter OS (7 vs 30 months) [107]. In an MD Anderson series (i = 69), intensive chemotherapy achieved CR/CRi rates of 81–100% (higher with Ven), compared with 33% with lower-intensity therapy, improving to 61% with Ven. 5-year OS was higher with intensive approaches (66% vs 7%), N/KRASMUT predicted inferior outcomes with lower-intensity regimens [106]. Similarly, in a Chinese cohort (n = 292) treated intensively, CR was achieved in 87% (76% underwent ASCT); KRASMUT mutations were adverse, but transplantation mitigated this risk [108]. Among transplanted patients, survival was comparable between KMT2A::MLLT3 and other KMT2Ar (3-year OS ~ 74–78%) [108]. Of interest, patients with KMT2Ar treated with FLAG-IDA-Ven (n = 9) had favorable outcomes, with all achieving CRc and undetectable MRD; median OS and EFS were not reached, and 7 proceeded to allo-HCT in CR1 [22]. Similarly, KMT2Ar treated with CLIA-Ven (n = 16) yielded a CR/CRi rate of 100%; all responders achieved MRD negativity by flow cytometry, and 15/16 (94%) subsequently underwent transplant [109].

Management recommendations

Menin inhibitors have shown single-agent activity in relapsed/refractory KMT2Ar AML and are currently under evaluation in combination with intensive chemotherapy and Ven-HMA in the frontline setting. Enrollment in clinical trials incorporating menin inhibitors (revumenib, ziftomenib, bleximenib or enzomenib with 7+) or Ven-HMA, followed by ASCT in first CR, is encouraged; outside of a trial, 7 + 3 or FLAG-IDA plus Ven and CLIA plus Ven remain a reasonable option.

MRD monitoring

KMT2Ar MRD is best assessed by quantitative RT-PCR, with positivity defined as ≥0.001% KMT2Ar transcripts relative to ABL1. In a cohort of 64 patients (median age 43 years), detectable MRD prior to transplant was associated with significantly inferior outcomes, including lower 2-year relapse-free survival (17% vs 59%) and higher 2-year cumulative incidence of relapse (75% vs 25%) [110]. Similarly, in another study of 217 patients (median age 41 years) treated with intensive chemotherapy, CR/CRi was achieved in 82% and MRD negativity in 65%. MRD positivity after cycle 2 strongly predicted relapse (3-year cumulative incidence 75% vs 21%) and inferior survival (3-year OS 19% vs 71%) [111].

Investigational approaches

Early-phase studies in newly diagnosed, fit adults show high response rates with menin inhibitor plus 7 + 3 combinations: revumenib (days 2–28) plus 7 + 3 achieved CR in 89% (33% transplanted; n = 12) [112], ziftomenib (600 mg, days 8–28) + 7 + 3 achieved CR in 83% (n = 16) [42], and bleximenib (days 4–28) + 7 + 3 achieved CR in 88% (17% transplanted) [113]. In older patients, revumenib combined with venetoclax–azacitidine yielded a CR rate of 78%, with 33% proceeding to transplant, and median OS was 18 months; differentiation syndrome occurred in ~5% [114] (Table 3). We eagerly await results of the ongoing phase 3 EVOLVE-2/HO177/AMLSG35-24/ACT-HOV-AML-002 trial evaluating revumenib in combination with Ven-azacitidine in adults with newly diagnosed NPM1 MUT or KMT2Ar AML who are ineligible for intensive chemotherapy [115].

Allogeneic transplantation

Despite therapeutic advances in AML, one-third of patients are refractory to induction therapy, and about half relapse. ASCT remains a potentially curative treatment option, not only in the relapsed/refractory setting but also for high-risk patients in first CR and those with persistent MRD. In intermediate-risk AML, a randomized trial of 143 adults ≤60 years with an available donor showed that ASCT in first CR improved disease-free survival (69% vs 40%) but not OS compared with consolidation chemotherapy, with transplant reserved for relapse [116], suggesting deferral of transplant may be reasonable in selected patients. However, MRD is increasingly used to guide transplant decisions and also predict post-transplant outcomes; in a large retrospective study of 1,075 patients (including 822 with FLT3-ITD and/or NPM1 MUT) in first remission prior to ASCT, persistence of these mutations at an allele fraction ≥0.01% was associated with higher relapse risk and inferior survival [117].

Overall, ASCT has been facilitated by expanded donor availability. According to the 2023 Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research (CIBMTR) report [118], matched unrelated donors (MUD) are the most common donor source (45%), followed by haploidentical donors (21%), matched related donors (MRD;18%), mismatched unrelated donors (MMUD; 12%), and cord blood (3%) with MMUD use increasing since 2020 due to PTCy (post-transplant cyclophosphamide)-based graft versus host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis [118]. Outcomes have improved across donor types: among 3,468 adults with AML undergoing MRD transplantation (2017-2022), 3-year OS by ELN risk was 71% (favorable), 65% (intermediate), and 50% (adverse), compared with 68%, 62%, and 48%, respectively, for 8,022 MUD recipients [118]. Relapse remains the leading cause of post-transplant mortality, underscoring the need for MRD-guided monitoring and post-transplant maintenance therapies.

Table 6 summarizes the topline results of recently reported randomized studies. PARADIGM was a phase 2 study that compared intensive induction chemotherapy (7 + 3 or CPX-351) with Ven-azacitdine in newly diagnosed AML patients fit for intensive therapy (median age 65 years), excluding those with CBF-AML, FLT3-ITD AML, or NPM1 MUT AML [16].Ven-azacitidine resulted in higher CR/CRi rates (81% vs. 55%), increased rates of ASCT (61% vs. 40%), superior EFS (53% vs. 39%; HR 0.61), lower toxicity and shorter hospitalizations compared with intensive chemotherapy [16]. Notably, only 68% of patients in the 28-day arm received the full planned Ven duration during cycle 1 compared with 89% in the 14-day arm, primarily because of infection-related adverse events [119]. With regard to FLT3MUT AML, the phase 3 HOVON 156/AMLSG28-18/PASHA trial randomized 768 patients with newly diagnosed FLT3MUT AML to receive either gilteritinib or midostaurin in combination with intensive chemotherapy [53]. While OS was similar between treatment arms, gilteritinib significantly improved EFS (51.1 vs. 19.9 months) [53]. In addition, relapse rates after achieving CR were substantially lower with gilteritinib than with midostaurin (21% vs. 36%) [53]. These findings suggest that gilteritinib may provide more durable disease control than midostaurin in the frontline treatment of FLT3MUT AML.

Concluding remarks

AML outcomes have improved gradually over 50 years with evolving chemotherapy, transplant, and supportive care. Despite ~10 newly approved drugs in the past decade, survival gains in fit AML patients remain modest and may partly reflect improvements in transplant and supportive care rather than drug effects alone. We therefore recommend an individualized, molecularly guided approach for younger, fit patients with newly diagnosed AML. While intensive chemotherapy remains the standard, Ven-HMA is emerging as a frontline option in selected patients, with growing interest in triplet regimens incorporating FLT3, IDH, or menin inhibitors alongside Ven-HMA or intensive therapy. Several randomized studies, including the MyeloMATCH precision medicine trials, are ongoing and are anticipated to further refine treatment selection. Until such data mature, treatment decisions should balance promise with prudence when choosing between intensive conventional chemotherapy and molecularly targeted approaches. The longstanding experience and curative potential of intensive regimens should not be prematurely supplanted by newer targeted strategies, particularly in the absence of definitive comparative data. Future trial designs in AML should incorporate biologic information beyond bulk genomics, including multi-omics and single-cell sequencing, to facilitate precision therapeutics.
