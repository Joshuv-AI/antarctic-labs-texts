Mortality, Health Care Use, and Spending Patterns During South Korea’s Trainee Physicians’ Walkout
Authors: David D. Kim, Ji-sook Choi, Hee Sun Jung, Soontack Kwon, Won Mo Jang, Hayoung Jang, Hwan Bin Choi, Sungchul Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWas the 18-month nationwide walkout by South Korean trainee physicians in 2024 and 2025 associated with changes in mortality, health care use, and spending patterns at teaching hospitals?FindingsIn this cohort study of 200 teaching hospitals in South Korea, the 2024 to 2025 trainee physician walkout was associated with marginal mortality increases, substantial declines in utilization, and higher spending per encounter in 2024, largely driven by patients admitted with general conditions. Mortality, utilization, and spending for patients admitted with complex conditions did not change, and little evidence indicated care shifted to nonteaching hospitals.MeaningThese findings suggest that workforce disruptions may necessitate the prioritization of high-acuity care, potentially constraining general inpatient services, while simultaneously demonstrating both the resilience and vulnerabilities of South Korea’s health system.

Introduction

South Korea operates a universal, single-payer health care system administered by the National Health Insurance Service, providing comprehensive coverage at relatively low cost.1,2,3,4 Nonetheless, South Korea’s health system faces persistent challenges, including constrained workforce capacity and uneven access to essential services. To address these issues, the government announced a health care reform on February 1, 2024,5,6 including 4 initiatives: expanding medical school enrollment by 2000, strengthening local and essential care services, establishing a medical malpractice safety net, and reforming compensation for essential medical services.7 On February 6, the government confirmed plans to increase medical school enrollment from 3058 to 5058.

The proposed medical school expansion triggered strong opposition from professional medical associations.8 On February 19, 2024, trainee physicians—who constitute approximately one-third of the physician workforce in major teaching hospitals—began submitting resignations and initiated a walkout in protest of the reforms. Their actions reflected concerns about poor working conditions and fears that rapid expansion of medical school quotas would compromise training quality.9,10 The initial resignations involved 1091 residents and interns across 10 hospitals. By February 20 to 23, resignations had surged to 10 034 (80.5%) across 100 hospitals, ultimately involving more than 90% of trainee physicians and disrupting services at teaching hospitals nationwide.11

The government responded by issuing back-to-work orders and warning of disciplinary and legal actions, including license suspensions and potential criminal charges, and mobilizing military physicians and organizing emergency surgical teams to mitigate staffing shortages.12 Although the proposed increase was temporarily reduced from 2000 to 1500 students, negotiations remained stalled. The policy direction shifted amid political turnover, as the prior administration left office. In March 2025, the new administration announced that the existing 3058-student quota would be maintained, with the 2000-student increase applying only to the 2025 intake. After an 18-month dispute, trainee physicians returned to work in August 2025.13

Evidence from other countries suggests that physician strikes rarely increase short-term mortality but substantially reduce health care use, especially elective and nonurgent services, while emergency and critical services are typically maintained, and most strikes are brief.14,15,16,17,18,19,20 However, the effects vary by scale, duration, and context. The South Korean trainee physicians’ walkout is especially informative because it was unprecedented in both scale and duration, lasting more than a year and involving over 90% of trainee physicians. Existing studies have assessed its consequences, but the available evidence is limited to population-level mortality, pediatric emergency care use, or single-hospital settings,21,22,23,24 leaving broader health system consequences largely unknown.

To address this gap, this study examined the following questions: (1) Did patient composition change before and after the walkout? (2) Was South Korea’s trainee physicians’ walkout associated with changes in mortality, health care use, and spending patterns? (3) Was there evidence of potential spillover through shifts in care from teaching to nonteaching hospitals?

Methods

Data

This cohort study used data obtained from 2 sources: (1) National Health Insurance (NHI) claims maintained by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA), which capture all reimbursed inpatient, outpatient, and prescription drug services for nearly the entire South Korean population; and (2) mortality data from the Ministry of the Interior and Safety, which record all deaths registered in South Korea regardless of the place of death. This study was reviewed and approved by HIRA’s institutional review board , with informed consent waived because deidentified administrative claims data were used. The study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using a historical-control event-study design to estimate the association between the trainee physicians’ walkout and hospital-level mortality, health care use, and spending patterns. This approach allowed us to estimate changes across multiple postwalkout periods relative to corresponding prior-year seasonal patterns.25,26 The analytic unit was the hospital-week, with weeks defined by week-of-year, and the study period spanned October 2022 through September 2024. The walkout began on February 19, 2024 (week 8 of 2024), and this onset was applied uniformly across all 200 teaching hospitals because the walkout was a nationwide event with a common start date.

The exposed-year observations consisted of 200 teaching hospitals from October 2023 to September 2024, with prewalkout and postwalkout periods defined relative to week 8 of 2024. The historical control consisted of the same hospitals observed during the corresponding prior-year period (October 2022 to September 2023), allowing each hospital to serve as its own counterfactual and accounting for seasonal variation in hospital activity. This approach is consistent with prior studies using historical controls to evaluate physician labor disruptions.15,27 Nonteaching hospitals were not used as a contemporaneous control group because nonteaching hospitals may also have been affected by systemwide spillovers from the walkout. Moreover, differences in size, patient acuity, and scope of services also raised concern that nonteaching hospitals would not provide a credible counterfactual trend for teaching hospitals.

The key identifying assumption was that, absent the walkout, outcomes among teaching hospitals during the exposed-year period would have followed the corresponding prior-year seasonal patterns, conditional on measured hospital-level covariates. To reduce potential bias from COVID-19 recovery, we restricted the historical-control period to observations after October 2022. Because the government announced the policy on February 1, 2024, and the walkout began on February 19, 2024, outcomes during weeks 6 and 7 of 2024 may have already begun to deviate from prior-year patterns due to anticipatory changes in care-seeking or hospital behavior. We, therefore, designated weeks 6 and 7 of 2024 as a 2-week anticipation period and excluded these weeks from the prewalkout comparison baseline.

Sample

The study sample included 200 teaching hospitals with consistent institutional classifications throughout the study period. Forty-seven hospitals were classified as tertiary teaching hospitals (ie, advanced referral centers providing highly specialized, intensive services for patients with complex or high-acuity conditions), and 153 were classified as nontertiary teaching hospitals (ie, secondary hospitals affiliated with medical schools that provide trainee education while delivering general inpatient and outpatient care). Primary analyses pooled all teaching hospitals, and heterogeneity by hospital type was assessed in subgroup analyses.

Outcomes

We examined 3 outcome domains, measured weekly at the hospital level: mortality, health care use, and spending patterns. Mortality outcomes included weekly mortality among hospital users and 30-day and 90-day mortality after admission. Weekly mortality was defined as the percentage of patients with any inpatient and/or outpatient encounter in a hospital-week who died that week, with deaths attributed to the hospital of the most recent encounter. Postadmission mortality was defined as the proportion of patients whose hospitalization began in a given week who died within 30 or 90 days of the admission date. Health care use outcomes included the weekly number of inpatient admissions and outpatient visits, standardized per 10 million NHI beneficiaries using the annual national NHI population as the denominator, and mean length of stay per inpatient admission. Spending outcomes included mean spending per inpatient admission and per outpatient visit, adjusted for inflation to 2025 Korean won (KRW) using the consumer price index and converted to US dollars (1 KRW = $0.000678). To reduce short-term fluctuations, hospital-level outcomes were smoothed using 4-week rolling averages.

Statistical Analysis

We estimated the association between the trainee physicians’ walkout and hospital-level outcomes using the group-time average treatment effect framework developed by Callaway and Sant’Anna,25 implemented with the DiD package in R version 4.5.3 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Because all 200 teaching hospitals were exposed to the walkout in the same week (calendar week 8 of 2024), this framework was applied to estimate dynamic postwalkout average treatment effects relative to the historical-control period rather than to address staggered adoption, with separate estimates at each event-time week to allow the magnitude of the association to evolve over the postwalkout period.

Statistical models included hospital and week-of-year fixed effects to account for time-invariant hospital characteristics and recurrent seasonal patterns, and additionally adjusted for baseline hospital-level covariates measured during the prewalkout period, including age distribution, sex distribution, insurance type, residence, and the prevalence of 11 common chronic conditions. We used the doubly robust estimator, which combines outcome regression and inverse probability weighting, and aggregated group-time average treatment effects into event-time estimates to characterize the dynamic changes in average treatment effects. SEs were clustered at the hospital level to account for within-hospital serial correlation and estimated using the multiplier bootstrap. Statistical significance was defined as a 95% CI that did not include the null value of zero.

Subgroup Analysis

To assess heterogeneity across hospital types, we conducted analyses separately for tertiary and nontertiary teaching hospitals, and also examined outcomes among nonteaching hospitals to assess potential systemwide spillovers (ie, shifts in care away from teaching hospitals) during the walkout. For inpatient outcomes, analyses were further stratified by disease severity using South Korea’s tertiary hospital designation system into 3 categories: complex conditions (rare, highly complex, or high-fatality conditions, such as organ transplantation, complex cerebrovascular disease, and brain tumors), general conditions (common and typically nonfatal conditions treatable in most hospitals, such as neonatal cases and cerebrovascular or neurologic diseases of moderate complexity), and simple conditions (conditions manageable with basic care, such as routine neurosurgical and orthopedic procedures, neurologic functional impairments, and mild injuries). Additional details are provided in eAppendixes 1 and 2 in Supplement 1.

Sensitivity Analysis

We conducted multiple sensitivity analyses for 30-day posthospitalization mortality, comparing (1) the primary 2-week anticipation specification with a no-anticipation specification; (2) 4-week rolling average outcomes with raw weekly outcomes; (3) alternative covariate specifications, ranging from fixed effects only to models additionally adjusting for baseline characteristics, time-varying demographics, and time-varying demographics with case mix; and (4) the primary Callaway-Sant’Anna estimator with alternative estimators, including static 2-way fixed-effects, one published by Sun et al28 and another by Borusyak et al.29 To assess whether shifts in patient composition not captured by the included characteristics may have contributed to the observed associations, we conducted an additional analysis comparing the distribution of diagnosis related group (DRG) codes between the prewalkout and postwalkout periods.

Results

Hospital-Level Patient Characteristics

The analysis included 7 719 828 patients with inpatient admissions (3 520 242 [45.6%] aged ≥65 years; 3 883 073 [50.3%] female) and 41 891 392 with outpatient visits (17 552 493 [41.9%] aged ≥65 years; 21 992 981 [52.5%] female) across 200 teaching hospitals, divided between an exposed cohort (3 727 632 inpatient; 20 400 814 outpatient) and a historical control cohort drawn from the same calendar window 1 year earlier (3 992 196 inpatient; 21 490 578 outpatient). Patient characteristics were broadly balanced between the exposed and historical-control cohorts in both prewalkout and postwalkout periods. In the exposed-year prewalkout period, the mean (SD) hospital-level inpatient population was 53.7% (14.2%) younger than 65 years, 49.5% (8.8%) male, 90.6% (7.2%) covered by NHI, and 47.1% (34.2%) resided in metropolitan areas (Table 1); the corresponding outpatient population was a mean (SD) of 57.7% (11.5%) younger than 65 years, 47.7% (7.3%) male, 92.0% (4.9%) covered by NHI, and 48.8% (36.0%) resided in metropolitan areas (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). The most prevalent chronic conditions were diabetes, hip or pelvic fracture, and hyperlipidemia among inpatients, and hip or pelvic fracture, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes among outpatients. Unadjusted weekly trends for the exposed and control groups are presented in eFigures 1 to 5 in Supplement 1.

Changes in Patient Composition

We found no evidence of substantial changes in patient composition after the walkout. In the inpatient sample, composition remained stable across most characteristics, with small changes in the proportion of adults aged 65 years or older (0.9 percentage points [pp]; 95% CI, 0.1 to 1.8 pp), those living in metropolitan areas (−0.9 pp; 95% CI, −1.5 to −0.3 pp), those living in rural areas (0.6 pp; 95% CI, 0.1 to 1.1 pp), and those with acquired hypothyroidism (1.0 pp; 95% CI, 0.3 to 1.6 pp). Similar results were observed in the outpatient sample: statistically significant changes were limited to age, insurance type, and chronic conditions, and the magnitudes were modest (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).

Mortality

The trainee physicians’ walkout was associated with a 0.008–percentage point increase in weekly mortality among hospital users (95% CI, 0.000 to 0.015 pp) and a 0.39–percentage point increase in 30-day mortality after hospitalization (95% CI, 0.24 to 0.54 pp) at teaching hospitals (Table 2). The increase in 30-day mortality was driven primarily by patients admitted for general conditions (0.60 pp; 95% CI, 0.38 to 0.81 percenage points); estimates for admissions for simple and complex conditions were not statistically significant. Notably, associations were largest immediately after the walkout and attenuated over time (Figure 1; eFigure 6 in Supplement 1). In stratified analyses, associations were more pronounced in nontertiary teaching hospitals than in tertiary teaching hospitals (eTables 3-4 and eFigures 7-8 in Supplement 1). Similar patterns were observed for 90-day mortality after hospital admission. There was no significant mortality change among nonteaching hospitals (eTable 5 and eFigure 9 in Supplement 1).

Health Care Use and Spending Patterns

The trainee physicians’ walkout was associated with lower health care utilization and higher spending per encounter at teaching hospitals. These patterns were observed in both inpatient and outpatient settings, with a larger relative reduction in inpatient care than outpatient care. Across all teaching hospitals, weekly hospitalizations decreased by 19.2 per 10 million people (95% CI, −24.1 to −14.3 hospitalizations per 10 million people), a 17.3% relative reduction from baseline, while mean spending increased by US $301 per hospitalization (95% CI, $202 to $399), an 11.6% relative increase from baseline (Table 3 and Figure 2; eFigure 10 in Supplement 1). Mean length of stay did not change significantly. These changes varied by disease severity: hospitalizations fell most for general conditions (−23.5%; −14.1 hospitalizations per 10 million people; 95% CI, −16.9 to −11.3 hospitalizations per 10 million people), followed by simple conditions (−17.4%; −2.6 hospitalizations per 10 million people; 95% CI, −3.2 to −1.9 hospitalizations per 10 million people), with minimal change for complex conditions (−7.4%; −2.6 hospitalizations per 10 million people; 95% CI, −4.6 to −0.6 hospitalizations per 10 million people) (Figure 2; eTable 6 in the Supplement). Notably, hospitalizations for complex conditions dropped immediately after the walkout but rebounded over time, whereas admissions for simple and general conditions remained persistently low. Mean spending per hospitalization for general conditions increased by $242 (95% CI, $108 to $375; 11.6%), with no significant change for simple or complex conditions (eTable 6 in Supplement 1).

These associations varied by hospital type and were larger in tertiary teaching hospitals than nontertiary teaching hospitals (Table 3; eTables 7-8, and eFigures 11-12 in Supplement 1). In tertiary hospitals, weekly hospitalizations decreased by 63.0 per 10 million people (95% CI, −76.3 to −49.6 per 10 million people; −25.7%), mean length of stay increased by 0.17 days (95% CI, 0.03 to 0.31 days; 2.9%), and mean spending per hospitalization increased by $350 (95% CI, $261 to $438; 11.2%). In nontertiary teaching hospitals, weekly hospitalizations decreased by 6.1 per 10 million people (95% CI, −8.7 to −3.6 per 10 million people; −8.5%), while mean spending increased by $278 per hospitalization (95% CI, $153 to $404; 11.4%). Mean length of stay did not change significantly.

Across all teaching hospitals, outpatient visits also declined after the walkout by 158 weekly visits per 10 million people (95% CI, −198 to −118 visits per 10 million people; −8.6%) (Table 3; eFigure 14 in Supplement 1). Although outpatient visits declined more than hospitalizations in absolute terms (158 vs 19 visits per 10 million beneficiaries), the relative decrease from baseline was smaller for outpatient visits than for hospitalizations (−8.6% vs −17.3%). Mean spending per outpatient visit increased by $3.1 (95% CI, $2.6 to $3.6; 4.3%). As observed for hospitalizations, the reductions in outpatient visits were larger in tertiary teaching hospitals than in nontertiary teaching hospitals (eFigures 15-16 in Supplement 1).

Spillover Effects

We found limited evidence of care shifting from teaching to nonteaching hospitals. Weekly hospitalizations at nonteaching hospitals increased slightly by 0.33 admissions per 10 million people (95% CI, 0.21 to 0.45 admissions per 10 million people; 4.0%) (Table 3; eTable 9, and eFigure 13 in Supplement 1). In contrast, outpatient visits at nonteaching hospitals decreased by 5.2 weekly visits per 10 million people (95% CI, −5.4 to −4.9 visits per 10 million people; −5.9%), while mean spending increased modestly by $1.2 per outpatient visit (95% CI, $1.1 to $1.3; 3.8%) (Table 3; eTable 9, and eFigure 17 in Supplement 1).

Sensitivity Analyses

Our sensitivity analyses showed that the findings were most sensitive to the 2-week anticipation specification, compared with the no-anticipation specification (eFigure 18 in Supplement 1). Under the no-anticipation specification, the overall association between the walkout and 30-day mortality across all hospitalizations shifted toward a positive but statistically nonsignificant estimate, although the association among patients admitted for general medical conditions remained statistically significant. Results were robust to alternative outcome definitions, covariate specifications, and difference-in-difference estimators. We found limited evidence of substantial changes in patient composition, as the distribution of DRG codes did not meaningfully differ between the prewalkout and postwalkout periods.

Discussion

This cohort study assessed the systemwide consequences of South Korea’s 18-month nationwide trainee physicians’ walkout during 2024 to 2025, which involved more than 90% of trainee physicians. Whereas prior studies examined narrower impacts on population-level all-cause mortality, pediatric emergency care use, or single-hospital settings,21,22,23,24 we used nationwide claims to identify 4 main findings. First, patient composition at teaching hospitals remained stable before and after the walkout. Second, the walkout was associated with marginally higher mortality, substantially lower utilization, and higher spending per encounter. Third, these associations varied by disease severity and hospital type, most pronounced among patients admitted for general conditions while outcomes for those with complex conditions changed little. Finally, we found limited evidence of care shifting from teaching to nonteaching hospitals.

The walkout was associated with a small but statistically significant increase in mortality, contrasting with prior studies reporting no mortality effects during physician strikes elsewhere,14,15,16,17,18,19,20 including South Korea.21,23 With little change in patient composition, the mortality increase likely reflects care disruptions under staffing constraints (eg, delays in admission, evaluation, monitoring, or escalation) rather than admission of sicker patients. Mortality increases were greater in nontertiary teaching hospitals than in tertiary teaching hospitals, reflecting greater reliance on trainee physicians and fewer alternative staffing or surge resources.24 Despite statistical significance, the increase was modest, concentrated in the early phase of the walkout, and attenuated over time, possibly indicating effective mitigation by government and hospital actions to sustain emergent and high-acuity services, including mobilization of public and military hospitals, deployment of emergency surgical teams, and care adaptations.12

At the same time, care for patients admitted for complex conditions appeared largely preserved as mortality, utilization, and spending outcomes changed little. The increase in 30-day mortality was not statistically significant among these patients, and hospitalizations for complex conditions dropped immediately after the walkout but quickly rebounded over time, whereas hospitalizations for simple and general conditions remained reduced throughout. These findings suggest that high-acuity services were protected by prioritizing time-sensitive intensive care and emergency surgery through clinician redeployment, elective care deferral, and resource concentration in tertiary centers,30 highlighting health system resilience during a major workforce disruption.

However, prioritizing high-acuity care amid limited resources may have constrained capacity for intermediate-acuity care. Patients admitted for general conditions represent an intermediate-risk population for whom timely inpatient evaluation, monitoring, and escalation are integral to preventing further deterioration. As shown in literature, understaffing and potential burnout were associated with delays in diagnostic testing or specialist consultation, reduced monitoring and reassessment, slower recognition of deterioration, and bottlenecks in transfer to higher levels of care,31,32 possibly leading to increased short-term in-hospital mortality.

The increase in spending per hospitalization, particularly among patients with general conditions, is also notable and may reflect several mechanisms. First, during the walkout the government introduced supplemental payments, including emergency-care funds for hospitalized patients, to support hospital operations, which could have increased spending per admission.33 Second, spending may have increased in part because a relatively higher share of patients died, and end-of-life care is typically more resource intensive.34,35 Third, hospitals facing sharp declines in patient volume and revenue may have increased resource use per admission through greater service intensity, changes in care processes, or shifts in practice patterns, consistent with patterns reported during the COVID-19 pandemic.36,37,38

Finally, we found limited evidence of care shifting from teaching to nonteaching hospitals. Despite South Korea’s open-access system, which allows patients to seek care at any facility without referral (eg, approximately 15% of outpatient visits among patients eligible for primary care occurred in hospitals),39 reductions in hospital admissions and outpatient visits at teaching hospitals were not fully absorbed by nonteaching hospitals. The modest increase in hospitalizations at nonteaching hospitals was insufficient to offset declines observed at teaching hospitals. In a health care system heavily reliant on tertiary hospitals and lacking established primary care coordination,40,41 these patterns suggest that some forgone care may have been potentially avoidable, discretionary, and high-cost. However, the small increase in mortality observed during the early walkout phase raises concerns about disruptions in care transitions for some patients. Thus, the longer-term consequences of delayed or forgone care during the walkout period warrant further investigation.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not evaluate broader or longer-term consequences of the walkout, such as complications, disease progression, or health-related quality of life, and delayed, forgone, or shifted care occurring after the observation window was not captured; our estimates may, therefore, understate the full extent of changes linked to the walkout. Second, claims data exclude utilization and spending for services not covered by NHI, which account for approximately 16% of total health care expenditures in South Korea, potentially underestimating overall impacts if patients substituted forgone care with noncovered or out-of-pocket services. Third, available workforce data lacked the precision and granularity needed to quantify the magnitude of the workforce disruption or to link staffing-level changes to outcomes. Fourth, although DRG and case-mix distributions remained largely stable across the prewalkout and postwalkout periods, we cannot fully rule out unmeasured shifts in patient composition. Subtle differences in illness severity or care-seeking behavior within DRG categories may not be fully captured by claims-based measures. Fifth, despite adjustment for time-invariant hospital characteristics and common temporal shocks, residual confounding from unmeasured time-varying factors (eg, physician burnout or changes in care-seeking behavior) remains possible.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study, the trainee physicians’ walkout in South Korea was associated with marginal, short-term increases in mortality, decreases in utilization, and higher spending per encounter, concentrated among patients with general conditions, while outcomes for those with complex conditions were largely unchanged. Rapid hospital and governmental responses to maintain critical services may have helped limit further harms and supported the allocation of constrained resources, though prioritization of high-acuity care may also have reduced capacity for routine inpatient services during staffing shortages. Together, these findings highlight both the resilience and the vulnerabilities of South Korea’s health system in the face of large-scale workforce disruptions.
