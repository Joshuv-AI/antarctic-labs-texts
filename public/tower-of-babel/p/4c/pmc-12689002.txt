Application of Mendelian randomization to explore metabolic pathways in colorectal cancer
Authors: Tung Hoang, Van Mai Truong, Tho Thi Anh Tran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:We conducted a Mendelian randomization (MR) study to investigate the relationship between genetically predicted metabolites and the risk of colorectal cancer (CRC) and to explore the underlying pathways.Methods:Genetic instruments for metabolite levels were selected based on data from 64 genome-wide association studies involving a total of 362 750 individuals. Using a two-sample MR approach, we assessed associations with CRC utilizing summary statistics from a meta-analysis of the UK Biobank and FinnGen. The primary analysis was conducted using the inverse-variance weighted method, with additional sensitivity analyses employing the median-weighted and MR-Egger methods to account for potential pleiotropy. The identified significant metabolites were further analyzed through enrichment analysis to explore the metabolic and lipid pathways involved.Results:Across the three MR methods, we identified 67 metabolites that were positively associated and 92 metabolites that were inversely associated with CRC risk. Among these, 7-methylguanine and creatinine are the most strongly connected metabolites. Enrichment analysis revealed that positively associated metabolites were significantly linked to pathways such as small nuclear ribonucleoprotein assembly, non-coding RNA metabolism, and gut-liver indole metabolism. In contrast, inversely associated metabolites were enriched in pathways related to the urea cycle, amino group metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism disorders, and pyrimidine catabolism.Conclusion:This study highlights genetically predicted metabolites associated with CRC risk, suggesting that metabolite panels and lipid-based biomarkers have potential for CRC risk assessment and early detection. However, further standardization and extensive validation are necessary before its clinical application.

Introduction

Cancer cells undergo metabolic reprogramming to support their increased energy and biosynthesis needs while managing oxidative stress for survival and growth[1]. This process is driven by cancer mutations, nutrient availability, and abnormal accumulation of metabolites, which can further promote tumor development[1]. Cancer metabolism research dates back to the early 20th century when Otto Warburg discovered that cancer cells preferentially rely on glycolysis for energy production, even in the presence of oxygen[4,5]. This phenomenon, known as the Warburg effect, challenges the prevailing notion that oxidative phosphorylation is the primary energy source for all cells, including cancerous cells[4,5]. Beyond glycolysis, the key energy pathways in cancer cells include oxidative phosphorylation, glutaminolysis, and lipid metabolism[4]. Oxidative phosphorylation is a critical process for ATP generation through the mitochondrial electron transport chain[4,6]. Glutaminolysis catabolizes glutamine into glutamate, which is further metabolized into α-ketoglutarate and fed into the tricarboxylic acid cycle to generate energy and support biosynthesis[4,7]. Lipid metabolism, essential for cellular function, is often reprogrammed to sustain heightened energy demands and rapid proliferation of malignant cells[4,8]. Metabolomics, which is a comprehensive assessment of metabolites in biological specimens, offers a powerful approach to identify biochemical signatures associated with cancer.

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide, with an estimate of almost 2 million incident cases by 2022[9]. Currently, colonoscopy remains the most widely used screening method in the United States and is a key component of national cancer screening programs in many countries[10,11]. Among noninvasive options, the fecal immunochemical test offers good sensitivity, high specificity, and affordability[11]. Additionally, at least five blood tests have been proposed as potential alternatives to colonoscopy and fecal immunochemical tests[11]. Novel imaging techniques, including the colon capsule, magnetic resonance imaging, and computed tomography capsule, are being actively explored for their diagnostic potential[11]. Despite the wide range of screening options currently available for CRC, there is a strong need for further development of noninvasive techniques that offer improved acceptability, accessibility, and effectiveness[12].

In recent years, several metabolomic studies have analyzed various biological samples, including blood, urine, fecal water, and tissues[13,14]. Utilizing advanced analytical techniques, such as gas and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry and nuclear magnetic resonance, these studies highlight metabolites as promising biomarkers for the early detection of CRC[13]. Using a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach, this study aimed to identify metabolites that differentiate CRC patients from healthy controls, serve as potential biomarkers for CRC screening, and reveal the underlying metabolic pathways.

Materials and methods

This work has been reported in line with the REMARK criteria[15,16].

Two-sample MR design

In this study, we applied a two-sample MR design to assess causality and provided that certain assumptions were met[17]. First, genetic variants that serve as instrumental variables are strongly associated with metabolites[17]. Second, instrumental variables should be independent of potential confounders, including those influencing the relationship between metabolites and CRC. Third, instrumental variables should affect CRC solely through their association with metabolites[17].

Exposure data

Genetic instruments were selected using metabolite quantitative trait loci from previously published genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on metabolites and accessed via the MetaboAnalyst database. Specifically, we obtained data from 362 750 individuals for 337 881 genetic variants significantly associated with over 4000 metabolites from 64 GWAS, excluding data from the UK Biobank[18–81]. The sample size and significance threshold used in each GWAS are available at MetaboAnalyst portal (https://www.mgwas.ca/mGWAS/upload/MGBrowseView.xhtml). While the standard genome-wide significance threshold of P< 5e-8 was commonly used, some GWAS used more stringent or lenient significance thresholds, ranging from P< 4.5e-12 to P< 1e-5, depending on study design and statistical power. In this study, we used the original significance thresholds reported in each GWAS to ensure consistency with the genetic associations specific to each metabolite.

To ensure the data quality, we applied several filtering steps. First, we removed variants with missing allele information, thereby reducing the dataset to 263 736 variants. Subsequently, variants with zero standard errors were excluded, resulting in a final set of 191 696 variants. A meta-analysis was subsequently performed to estimate the combined effect of each variant on metabolite levels, ultimately yielding summary statistics for 186 348 significant variants across 3280 metabolites (Fig. 1).Figure 1.Flowchart for identification of instrumental variables. Outcome data and harmonization

For outcome summary statistics, we obtained meta-analysis findings from FinnGen (11 790 cases and 378 749 controls) and the UK Biobank (8635 cases and 327 520 controls), comprising a total of 32 344 798 variants (https://finngen.gitbook.io/documentation/data-download)[82,83]. To facilitate MR analysis, we harmonized metabolite summary statistics with CRC summary statistics, identifying 165 947 variants as instrumental variables for assessing the MR associations between 3280 metabolites and CRC (Fig. 1).

MR analysis

For the primary analysis, we used the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method with the assumption of balanced pleiotropy[84]. This method involved calculating single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-specific Wald estimates by dividing the SNP-outcome association by the SNP-exposure association and then combining these estimates using a random-effects model to account for potential heterogeneity. To verify the robustness of our results, we performed additional sensitivity analyses using the weighted median method[85] and the MR-Egger regression[86]. While the IVW approach yields the most precise estimates when all instrumental variables are valid, the weighted median method provides a balance between efficiency and robustness by tolerating invalid instruments. Moreover, MR-Egger regression was employed to identify and adjust for directional pleiotropy, despite it being less efficient than IVW[84–86]. All MR analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.2.0)[87].

Enrichment analysis

To gain deeper insights into the mechanisms connecting metabolites with CRC, we performed enrichment analyses of metabolic pathways associated with CRC using a comprehensive web-based platform MetaboAnalyst (version 6.0). This analysis utilized the RaMP-DB (Relational Database of Metabolic Pathways), which integrates metabolite and lipid annotations from multiple sources, covering 3694 metabolites across pathways. Specifically, the analysis employed over-representation analysis based on the hypergeometric test to evaluate whether specific metabolic pathways were statistically enriched with significant metabolites more than expected by chance. Pathway definitions were derived from the Human Metabolome Database and the Small Molecule Pathway Database. Significantly associated metabolites were matched to reference metabolite sets, and pathway enrichment was assessed using over-representation analysis with false discovery rate correction to identify pathways potentially involved in CRC[88].

Metabolites negatively associated with CRC risk and underlying pathways

In the sensitivity analyses using both the median-weighted and MR-Egger methods, 92 metabolites remained significant (Table 2). Discussion

Observational studies have suggested that metabolites may serve as potential biomarkers for the early detection of CRC and for identifying high-risk populations. However, these studies are often subject to bias, limiting their ability to establish causal relationships. MR provides a robust approach to overcome these limitations by leveraging genetic variants as instrumental variables. In our MR analysis, we identified 67 metabolites that were positively associated with CRC risk and 92 metabolites that were negatively associated with it. Further enrichment analysis of these metabolites revealed key metabolic and lipid pathways that are potentially involved in CRC pathogenesis, offering insights into the underlying biological mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

Observational studies have suggested that metabolites play a potential role as biomarkers for CRC. Validation of a nine-metabolite panel has demonstrated an area under the curve ranging from 0.69 to 0.95 for CRC precursors and from 0.72 to 1.00 for CRC[12]. Another systematic review, which included evidence from at least five individual observational studies, identified upregulated metabolites such as glutamic acid, glycine, histidine, isoleucine, and palmitic acid, as well as downregulated metabolites, including alanine, asparagine, and tyrosine[89]. Consistent with these findings, our study also observed positive associations between histidine (OR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.15–1.21) and isoleucine-butyrylcarnitine (OR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.25–1.50) with CRC. Additionally, we identified negative associations for alanine derivatives, including glycylphenylalanine (OR = 0.97, 95% CI = 0.96–0.98) and leucylphenylalanine/isoleucylphenylalanine (OR = 0.98, 95% CI = 0.97–0.99).

Findings from previous MR studies on the genetic predisposition to metabolites and CRC remain inconclusive. A previous MR study that examined 137 blood metabolites found no significant association with CRC[90]. Yun et al investigated the relationship between 486 blood metabolites and CRC risk and identified six significant metabolites, including a positive association with gamma-glutamylthreonine and inverse associations with pyruvate, 1,6-anhydroglucose, nonadecanoate, 1-linoleoylglycerophosphoethanolamine, and 2-hydroxystearate[91]. More recently, a MR study that analyzed a larger dataset, including 651 plasma and 208 urinary metabolites, detected 10 significant metabolites, suggesting potential therapeutic targets through metabolic mediation effects[92]. Our study builds on and extends previous MR research by assessing a broader range of metabolites. Instead of relying on a single GWAS, we utilized summary statistics from 64 GWAS and performed a meta-analysis to obtain more robust effect estimates for genetic variants influencing metabolites. This approach allowed us to identify a greater number of significant metabolites, thereby providing stronger evidence for metabolic contributions to CRC risk and identifying potential targets for future research.

In this study, metabolites positively associated with CRC were enriched in the gut-liver indole and tryptophan catabolism pathways. Indoles, which are produced by gut bacteria through tryptophan breakdown, play a crucial role in maintaining intestinal health[93]. They contribute to strengthening the intestinal barrier, modulating the host immune response, and enhancing the antioxidant activity[93]. These findings suggest that alterations in indole metabolism may influence CRC development, potentially through the disruption of gut microbiota interactions and immune regulation. Furthermore, our enrichment analysis showed that metabolites negatively associated with CRC were enriched in pyrimidine-related metabolism[94]. This metabolic pathway plays a critical role in cancer progression, and several anticancer drugs that target pyrimidine metabolism have been approved for various cancer types[95]. Research has also demonstrated that inhibiting key rate-limiting enzymes within this pathway can directly affect tumor growth, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for CRC.

Despite the strengths of our MR approach, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the possibility of false negatives cannot be ruled out, particularly for metabolites with low genetic explanatory power. When genetic instruments explain only a small proportion of variance metabolite levels, statistical power is limited, especially for detecting weak causal effect. This constraint may have led to an underestimation of true associations, resulting in missed causal signals. Therefore, caution is warranted in interpreting null findings, especially for metabolites with few or weakly associated variants. Second, our use of summary-level data constrained our ability to investigate nonlinear or interaction effects between metabolites and CRC risk. This approach assumes a linear relationship between exposures and outcomes, which may overlook threshold effects or U-shaped associations that may exist in complex metabolic pathways. Future MR analyses using individual-level data are warranted to more thoroughly explore these dynamics. Third, our study population was predominantly of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups, such as Asian and African populations. A recent systematic review of 26 epidemiological studies has revealed significant differences in metabolic profiles across populations from China, United States, Canada, and various European countries[12]. These differences may be attributed to heterogeneity in biospecimen types, metabolomic platforms, and importantly, genetic ancestry. Notably, prior multiethnic studies have identified gene–metabolite interactions in the one carbon metabolism pathway contributing to CRC risk[96], underscoring the need to validate the association between metabolites and CRC risk in ancestrally diverse populations. Given the known differences in genetic architecture and metabolic profiles across populations, replication in diverse cohorts is essential to validate the identified associations and ensure the relevance of these findings across global populations. Fourth, several metabolites in this study were derived from blood-based GWAS; they may only indirectly reflect metabolic changes in the colorectal tumor microenvironment. While systemic metabolism can influence the tumor microenvironment, particularly through metabolic reprogramming that alters the availability of energy and nutrients[97], these effects are often broad and nonspecific. In contrast, intratumoral metabolic reprogramming more directly shapes the local energy landscape and immune interactions[98]. Observational studies showed that plasma metabolites may serve as proxies for broader metabolic alterations[99,100]; however, it may not capture tissue-specific dynamics due to spatial heterogeneity. Fifth, although instrumental variables were selected from metabolite GWAS and filtered to exclude SNPs associated with CRC to minimize reverse causation, potential bias cannot be entirely excluded if the original GWAS datasets included individuals with undiagnosed or prevalent CRC. Such inclusion could influence metabolite levels and confound the observed associations, thereby violating key MR assumptions and biasing causal estimates. Last, while MR helps address confounding and reverse causation, it cannot completely eliminate all sources of bias, such as horizontal pleiotropy, which may still influence the results despite sensitivity analyses. The inclusion of CRC patients in GWAS data of metabolites that might not have excluded CRC patients may introduce bias and confound the estimates of metabolite-CRC associations. Future research should incorporate more refined genetic instruments and explore integrative multiomics approaches to strengthen causal inference and biological interpretation.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings highlighted the potential role of metabolites as biomarkers for CRC risk assessment and early detection. By applying MR, we mitigated the biases common in observational studies and identified the key metabolites associated with CRC risk. Enrichment analysis further uncovered metabolic and lipid pathways that may contribute to CRC development. These insights provide a foundation for future research aimed at understanding the metabolic underpinnings of CRC and exploring novel strategies for its prevention and early intervention.
