Determinants of food retail outlet choice in an urban food environment: a qualitative study in Indonesia
Authors: David Colozza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrbanisation and food system transformations in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are reshaping local food retail environments, influencing diets and nutrition outcomes. The expansion of ultra-processed products (UPPs) and changing food outlet landscapes are key drivers of the triple burden of malnutrition across LMICs, including rising rates of obesity and related non-communicable disease (NCD). Yet, qualitative insights into how local communities interact with evolving food retail environments remains limited.MethodsA qualitative study in three urban communities of Yogyakarta, Indonesia, between September 2018 and April 2019. A total of 45 semi-structured interviews lasting between 35 min and 1.5 h were conducted with individuals primarily responsible for household food purchasing and preparation, supported by transect walks to map food outlet types. Data were analysed iteratively, combining emic perspectives with pre-determined themes based on established urban food environments and food systems frameworks.ResultsParticipants accessed a wide range of formal and informal food retail outlets. Food acquisition strategies were diverse and dynamic, with respondents often relying on multiple outlets for different product types. Five main themes emerged as key drivers of food retail outlet choice: economic reasons, convenience, produce quality, product variety, and personal or social relationships with vendors. Supermarkets were visited infrequently and mainly used for bulk or non-perishable purchases, while markets and other traditional outlets were preferred for daily needs due to perceived freshness, affordability, and trust in sellers.ConclusionFindings highlight the complexity of urban food acquisition in LMIC settings, where residents navigate diverse food retail options shaped by economic, social, and contextual factors. Informal vendors remain central to everyday food access across socio-economic groups, pointing to the need for context-specific public health policies and programmes that do not assume a linear transition toward formalisation, but instead work with existing informal structures. Interventions should aim to regulate the availability and marketing of UPPs, support healthier retail environments across all outlet types, and leverage trust, familiarity, and local networks in shaping dietary behaviours through bottom-up approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23574-7.

Background

Malnutrition remains a critical public health challenge worldwide, and unhealthy diets are a critical driver of all its forms: undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, obesity, and related non-communicable diseases (NCDs) [1]. The dramatic increase in the availability of cheap ultra-processed products (UPPs) high in unhealthy fats, sugar, and salt in the global food system is a key contributor to all forms of malnutrition [2]. UPPs, designed for hyper palatability and convenience, have a strong demonstrated link with overweight, obesity and NCDs [1, 3]. Importantly, by displacing healthier foods in the diet, UPPs can also lead to an increased risk of undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies, particularly in vulnerable populations, including children [4].

The rapid expansion of UPPs in the global food supply has coincided with significant transformations in retail food environments—the settings where people purchase food, including both formal outlets (e.g., supermarkets), informal vendors (e.g., street food sellers), and digital food retail platforms [5]. Recent evidence indicates that, over the past decade, the global expansion of retail chain outlets and their increased sales of unhealthy UPPs have coincided with a decline in traditional local food retailers—and these shifts are associated with rising obesity rates [6]. Urban areas have experienced the fastest, most profound shifts in food retail landscapes, raising concerns about their impact on dietary choices and, consequently, health outcomes [7]. However, evidence linking specific characteristics of urban retail food environments, such food outlets density and type, to dietary behaviours and nutrition outcomes remains mixed [8, 9] and disproportionately focused on high-income countries (HICs), with research from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including in Asia, still emerging [10].

Understanding the complex interactions between individuals, communities, and their retail food environments in LMICs is crucial, given the persistent issues with malnutrition and prominent burden from NCD that many face. However, as LMICs often present more complex and diverse retail food environments compared to HICs [10], studying these settings using simplified proxies common in research from HICs, such as food outlet density and availability, fails to capture the nuances required for effective public health interventions [10, 11]. As in many other areas of health research, qualitative methods can offer critical insights into the nuanced perceptions and motivations guiding individual behaviours, including dietary choices within food retail environments, which in turn can help the design of more targeted and effective public health interventions [12, 13].

This article contributes to the growing literature on urban food environments in LMICs through an in-depth qualitative study from Indonesia—the most populous country in Southeast Asia and one facing a pronounced triple burden of malnutrition. Stunting and wasting remain high at 19.8% and 7.4%, respectively [14], while overweight and obesity rates are rising, affecting 19.7% of children aged 5–12, 14% of adolescents aged 13–18, and 37.8% of adults [15]. Poor diets are also widespread, posing a significant barrier to reducing malnutrition. For example, an estimated 9 in 10 children and adults do not meet recommended fruit and vegetable intake, and 1 in 2 consume at least one sugary drink per day [16].

Like many LMICs, Indonesia has undergone rapid urbanisation and socio-economic transition, resulting in major shifts in food retail environments that have influenced diets and lifestyles through, among others, increased availability, access, and marketing of UPPs [9, 17, 18]. While some research has examined urban food environments in Indonesia [13–16], there is limited evidence on the specific role of the retail food environment and how local communities engage with it. In this context, the specific aims of this study were (1) to map the types of food outlets available to residents of three urban communities, and (2) to explore residents’ motivations behind choice of food retail outlet. Findings add to the relatively limited body of qualitative research on food environments in Southeast Asia. A recent review of LMICs found that only one-third of 70 studies across 22 countries employed qualitative or mixed methods, and just one from Indonesia [12].

A deeper understanding of these dynamics can help inform the development and strengthening of policies aimed at improving food environments at the local level. This, in turn, can support healthier diets and better nutrition and health outcomes. Findings from this study will be particularly relevant for shaping regulations on the marketing and sale of UPPs in local communities, including school settings, for addressing the role of informal food vendors in shaping dietary habits, and for regulating digital food retailers [5]. Findings also offer broader relevance for LMICs undergoing similar transformations, where early policy action targeting food retail environments can help address emerging nutrition challenges [5].

Methods

Data collection took place between September 2018 and April 2019 in Yogyakarta city, Indonesia, as part of a larger research study looking at the impact of food systems change on diets and nutritional health in the country. The selection of Yogyakarta as a study area enabled an assessment of changing food environments in an urban context that may better reflect the reality of many Indonesian cities. Compared to Jakarta—Indonesia’s megacity capital, where food systems may have undergone more advanced transformations [9, 17]—Yogyakarta, with an approximate population of 467,000 [19], still maintains strong linkages with surrounding peri-urban and rural areas [20]. This makes it a compelling case study for examining shifts in food systems and the retail environment. Within Yogyakarta, three study sites were selected to reflect different socio-economic profiles, based on conversations with local leaders and confirmed through first-hand observations by the study team. The three sites—named after their respective administrative districts: Sagan, Cokrodiningratan, and Terban—were heterogeneous in socio-economic composition, but overall represented high, middle, and lower socio-economic groups, respectively.

Data was collected by the principal researcher and two research assistants, each knowledgeable of one of the three research areas. Interviewers spoke the same language as participants, had experience in urban studies research, and received training on food environments and dietary behaviours prior to data collection. Regular debriefings following each data collection session enabled the team to collaboratively reflect on potential influences on data collection and interpretation. In-depth semi-structured interviews (N = 45), lasting between 35 min and 1.5 h, were carried out in Indonesian, recorded, transcribed verbatim and then translated to English for analysis. Interviews included a 24-hour dietary recall to capture broad food consumption and sourcing patterns, along with open-ended questions to explore participants’ views and perceptions on dietary behaviours, health understandings, and food systems change.

Transect walks with the team, local community leaders, and residents were conducted in the study areas prior to the start of the interviews. These walks helped prepare food outlets maps, which were used to elicit further responses on residents’ food sources, confirm study characteristics (e.g., socioeconomic makeup), introduce the team to the communities, and discuss respondent selection. Interviews sought to target the person responsible for food shopping and preparation in the household. This led to most respondents (93%) being the female head of household, as in Indonesia women are still largely responsible for these tasks. Respondent selection was first guided by conversations with community leaders, and later followed a snowballing sampling approach, where respondents themselves suggested further possible interviewees in the area. Food outlet maps (Figure S1) and interview guides (Table S1) are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Data was analysed using an iterative analysis strategy [21], which combined and alternated emic interpretations of the information collected—focused on understanding participants’ own beliefs and triangulating them with considerations for the local context—with etic use of existing models and theories, particularly those looking at urban food environments [5, 22–24] and, more broadly, food systems and dietary change [2, 25, 26]. Broad brush coding was first employed to identify initial descriptive themes, which were then further refined through line-by-line coding. The data analysis process was conducted in parallel with data collection, which enabled the research team to identify the point of data saturation—when no new themes or insights were emerging across the areas explored in the study. While dual coding was not employed (due to resource constraints), to mitigate potential bias, the primary coder cross-checked coding decisions and emerging themes with field notes and drew on prolonged engagement in the study areas (approximately eight months between 2018 and 2019) to validate interpretations through regular discussions with the research team.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from Research Ethics Office, King’s College London (Research Ethics Number: MR/17/18–244), and the Ministry of Technology, Research and Higher Education of the Republic of Indonesia (Approval Number: 2948/FRP/E5/Dit.KI/IX/2018). Informed consent was obtained in writing from all participants prior to data collection. NVivo v.15 (Lumivero, 2024) was used to facilitate data entry, cleaning, and analysis.

Food sources

A wide variety of food retail types was available, at short distance, to residents of all three areas (Fig. 1). Among informal retailers were warung (open-air or indoor small shops offering raw ingredients and/or cooked foods); traditional wet markets, selling a variety of perishable foods including fresh produce, meats, and cooking ingredients; mobile food vendors and anggkringan (semi-mobile carts typical of the region, serving beverages and small snacks). Formal retailers included instead supermarkets and convenience stores; international and local fast-food chains; restaurants and cafes.

Food shopping frequency by outlet type was highly heterogeneous. While visits to warung and market varied between daily to infrequent (2–3 times per month), all respondents reported infrequent supermarket patronage (1–2 times per month). This was largely due to supermarkets being viewed as places to purchase bulk or non-perishable items, rather than for daily food needs. Products such as toiletries, soap, instant noodles, cooking oil, flour, and sugar were typically bought during these occasional visits. In contrast, for everyday food needs, respondents preferred to frequent warung, wet markets, or rely on peddlers:‘Supermarket? I only go there once a month, mostly for toiletries or dry goods. My children go after they get their salary, but also mostly for monthly needs, like toiletries, or to buy products in bulk like instant noodles, or baby food.’ (Female, 33, high-income (5), Cokrodiningratan).‘I only go to [local supermarket chain] once a month, just to get things like soap or toothpaste. For vegetables and fruit? I rarely buy those there.’ Female, 43, low-income (2), Sagan)‘I don’t go [to supermarkets]. I shop at Kranggan [local wet market], and if I need snacks or something is missing, I’ll go to the warung nearby. My daughter-in-law goes to the supermarket to buy things for the baby, like milk, after work.’ (Female, 56, high-income (4), Cokrodiningratan).

Interestingly, UPPs such as instant noodles and packaged snacks were not confined to supermarkets but, conversely, were frequently purchased from traditional outlets. These included small shops, peddlers, and wet markets—highlighting the ubiquity of UPPs across all types of food retail outlets, including ‘traditional’ and less formal ones. The 24-hour dietary recall conducted as part of the study confirmed that most participants who reported purchasing UPPs or local snacks high in fat, sugar, and salt had obtained them from outlets other than supermarkets, including warung, peddlers, and wet markets. Similarly, when eating out or consuming pre-made meals, participants often frequented informal or traditional food vendors rather than ‘modern’ retail outlets.

Besides food acquisitions based on monetary transactions, many respondents reported frequent non-monetary strategies to acquire food. Some respondents relied on relatives or neighbours daily to receive groceries and/or cooked foods. Such non-market-based food acquisition options were reported as essential by some older respondents who, due to health conditions, could not visit in person nearby food outlets:‘Yes, I do [go to the market]. But sometimes I ask someone [to go] instead. I often get dizzy nowadays. (…) I have been sick for three months now’. (Female, 75, low-income (1), Terban)

Motivations behind food outlet choice

The wide range of food outlets available in the study areas reflected in residents’ food acquisition strategies. These were highly varied and generally characterised by the reliance on multiple outlets, each chosen for several different reasons. Five main drivers of food outlet choice were identified during thematic analysis: economic reasons, convenience, produce quality, product variety, social and personal connections.

Economic reasons included considerations for price of groceries at different outlets. Most respondents perceived supermarket and traditional wet markets as the most and least expensive options respectively, however, they would shop at these outlets interchangeably depending on prices and offers.‘Yes, sometimes I buy food from the supermarket too when there are special offers. If the discount price in the supermarket is cheaper than the market, then I go there.’ (Female, 45, high-income (5), Terban).‘Yes, we buy things like soap at the supermarket (…). Food, never, the market is closer, and cheaper.’ (Female, 39, high-income (5), Cokrodiningratan).

Attention to quality referred to a varied range of produce characteristics, including considerations on taste and freshness, but also localness, and food safety. For example, one respondent reported preference for shopping at the traditional market because she felt that produce there is more ‘natural’ compared to that in supermarkets:‘[The supermarket] is not good for vegetables… It is better to buy vegetables in the traditional market, it is more natural. [Vegetables are] also good in the supermarket, but they contain a lot of pesticides.’ (Female, 36, middle-income (3), Terban).

But I prefer the warung here. The produce is fresh from local farmers. [The seller] gets it directly from the farmers.’ (Female, 66, high-income (4), Cokrodiningratan).

A further driver of food outlet choice reported by respondents was the variety of products available at different outlet types. This resulted in virtually all respondents visiting daily several different outlets, to acquire specific products at each. Most respondents reported the traditional market and the supermarket as the most and least preferred outlet respectively for everyday shopping needs. Visits to the supermarket were universally reported as infrequent and driven by the need to purchase non-food items such as soap and toiletries; specific non-local products; specific products that cannot be found elsewhere; and food items usually bought in bulk, e.g. cooking oils and packaged products such as instant noodles:‘[I go to the supermarket] to buy aloe from Pontianak and other products made from it, which are not available elsewhere in the city.’ (Female, 50, low-income (2), Terban).‘I make pizza sometimes and must use dried oregano for the topping; but that’s hard to find at the market, that’s why I go to the supermarket.’ (Female, 35, high-income (4), Sagan).

A last driver of outlet choice was social and personal connections. Several respondents reported preference for certain outlets over others because of long-standing relationships with specific sellers, which in turn reflected in more flexible and convenient food acquisition options. One respondent reported getting food products that she regularly buys in bulk delivered to her house from a specific market stall, thanks to a long-standing patronage and trust-based relationship with the seller. Discussion

This article has presented a qualitative exploration of the food retail environment in three urban communities in Indonesia, focusing on mapping the variety of retail outlets available to local residents, and the drivers behind their food outlet choice. Overall, the food retail environment in the three communities was highly heterogeneous, characterised by the coexistence of formal retailers (e.g., supermarkets and convenience stores) with traditional and informal outlets (e.g., wet markets, mobile food sellers, and street food stalls). This diversity of food outlets is common in Indonesia [27] and other LMICs [28–30] and contrasts markedly with the typical dominance of formal retailers as the primary food source in urban areas of HICs, where the food retail transition occurred earlier, and may have already reached an end [6]. Unlike what has been observed in some HICs [31], this study found no clear link between the socioeconomic makeup of the areas and the availability of food outlets, nor between income and food outlet choices. Regardless of location, respondents had access to a diverse range of food outlets, including both formal stores and informal retailers. While higher-income respondents frequented supermarkets more often, all participants, regardless of income, reported shopping at a mix of outlets, such as warung, wet markets, and street vendors. Similarly, when eating out or consuming ready-made meals, participants often chose informal and traditional vendors over ‘modern’ food outlets, as described in detail in a previous study [32].

Regarding food outlet choice, the findings indicate five main drivers: economic reasons, convenience, produce quality, product variety, and social/personal connections. Some of these motivations align with observations from HICs [23], such as the importance of grocery prices, the proximity and overall shopping experience of outlets, and product range. In HICs, produce quality is often judged by nutritional value—with a preference for fruits, vegetables, meats, and organic or locally grown items [33] —or by perceived freshness [34]. Similarly, study respondents evaluated produce quality based on factors including taste, freshness, local sourcing, and food safety. These diverse assessments—reflecting varied understandings of health and nutrition [20], alongside personal preferences and practical considerations—influenced their retail choices, with some favouring informal retailers like wet markets or warung for their “natural” or “local” appeal, while others viewed supermarkets as offering superior quality due to better freshness, cleanliness, and storage.

Despite common drivers influencing food retail outlet choice, most respondents reported sourcing food from a diverse mix of retail types. This indicates more complex and heterogeneous food acquisition strategies compared to those observed in HICs. While the formal retail sector in Indonesia has expanded significantly in recent decades—both in outlet numbers and sales [16, 35]—the informal sector continues to play a key role in local food environments. This suggests that the so-called ‘supermarket revolution’ seen in many LMICs has yet to fully transform everyday shopping behaviours. Indonesia was among the first countries in the region to experience supermarket penetration [36], and although the number of supermarkets has grown considerably [37, 38], findings suggest that they have not yet taken the primary role in the local food retail environment. This is testified by the low frequency of visits to supermarkets by respondents compared to traditional or informal food outlets, and consistent with findings from other LMICs [28, 30, 39, 40].

These findings carry several important implications for policies and programmes aimed at improving food retail environments in LMIC settings. First, they suggest that regulatory frameworks and nutrition-related interventions cannot focus exclusively on the formal food retail sector—such as supermarkets, hypermarkets, and convenience stores—but must also account for the role of traditional and informal outlets. These retailers remain central to everyday food acquisition strategies for urban residents across socio-economic groups and thus represent an important point of leverage for interventions aiming to improve access to healthier foods [41]. Importantly, despite the common association in global nutrition transition narratives of ‘modern’ retailers like supermarkets with UPPs, and of ‘traditional’ outlets with healthier or more minimally processed foods [7, 38], the findings challenge this binary. Many participants, regardless of income level, reported more frequently sourcing UPPs and other foods high in fat, salt, and sugar from semi-formal or informal outlets—including wet markets, peddlers, and warung. This further highlights the blurred boundaries between modern and traditional food environments when it comes to the availability and accessibility of unhealthy food options. Taken together, these findings suggest that measures such as nutrition labelling regulations, vendor training and standards, incentives to stock healthier foods, and efforts to improve food safety and hygiene could—and should—be extended to include informal retailers in a way that is feasible and locally appropriate [41–43].

The last driver—social and personal connections—also offers important, policy-relevant insights for improving food retail environments in LMICs. While previous research in HICs has noted the influence of familiarity and trust in food retail interactions [31], the relationships described by respondents in this study often extended beyond transactional exchanges, embedding food acquisition within broader social networks. Such dynamics are less common in more formalised urban food systems and highlight the deeply social nature of food environments in LMICs. These findings suggest that urban food systems may not be following a linear path toward formalisation, even amid increasing penetration of formal retailers. Instead, they remain shaped by local relationships and informal practices—pointing to the need for context-sensitive policies that engage with the social as well as structural dimensions of food access. Interventions that strengthen trust and social capital—such as through community-based nutrition education or partnerships with trusted local vendors—may be more effective than those relying solely on top-down regulation. This further challenges the assumption that urban food systems are inevitably moving toward a more formalised model, and instead suggests a need to work with, rather than against, existing informal structures [41].

Three key limitations of the study should be noted. First, the purposive sampling and focus on three specific urban food retail environments, while appropriate for generating in-depth qualitative insights, limit the generalisability of findings beyond the study areas and participants. Second, the sample primarily comprised adult Javanese women. As dietary behaviours are shaped by age, gender, and socio-cultural background, additional research should focus on gathering insights from younger and older individuals, men, and participants from other regions of the country. Third, the data were collected in 2018–2019, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. While some contextual changes—such as shifts in food retail and access—are not captured, the structural dynamics identified, including the coexistence of formal and informal retail and diverse shopping patterns across income groups, are likely to remain relevant. Further research could explore how these dynamics have evolved in recent years—for example, in relation to the growing role of online retailers and food delivery platforms in driving dietary choices.

Conclusions

This study mapped the urban food retail environment in three Indonesian communities and investigated the factors shaping residents’ food outlet choices. Findings reveal a highly heterogeneous landscape, characterised by the coexistence of formal and informal food retailers. Regardless of income, respondents reported shopping across a variety of outlets, with purchasing decisions influenced by economic considerations, convenience, produce quality, product variety, and social or personal relationships. These findings highlight the continued relevance of informal retail outlets in shaping food access and choices in LMICs—including in relation to the purchase of UPPs and foods high in fat, sugar, and salt—even in urban contexts experiencing rapid formal retail expansion. Policies and interventions aiming to improve urban food environments in LMICs should therefore extend beyond the formal retail sector to engage with the informal systems and social dynamics underlying food acquisition. Context-sensitive approaches that acknowledge the social embeddedness of food environments may offer more sustainable pathways to supporting healthier diets.
