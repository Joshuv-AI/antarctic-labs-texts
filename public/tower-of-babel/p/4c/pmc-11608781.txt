Anti‐Shortening Screw for the Prevention of Postoperative Shortening in Displaced Femoral Neck Fractures: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Dongze Lin, Weipeng Gong, Chaohui Lin, Jiajie Liu, Ke Zheng, Peisheng Chen, Fengfei Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly femoral neck shortening after femoral neck system (FNS) fixation for displaced femoral neck fractures can occur in 22.3%–39.1% of cases, leading to decreased hip joint function. This study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of using an anti‐shortening screw (ASS) in FNS fixation to prevent postoperative femoral neck shortening in displaced femoral neck fractures.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 106 cases of displaced femoral neck fractures treated with FNS at the Hospital from April 2020 to April 2023. Patients were divided into two groups based on the use of an ASS: the traditional group and the ASS group, each consisting of 53 cases. The ASS group was age‐matched with the traditional group treated during the same period without an ASS. The study variables included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking and drinking history, injury mechanism, side of injury, fracture type, surgical time, intraoperative blood loss, Harris Hip Score (HHS) at the final follow‐up, radiographic assessment (femoral neck shortening), and complications (infection, femoral head necrosis, nonunion, and secondary surgery). Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, with continuous and categorical variables analyzed using appropriate parametric (t‐test) and nonparametric (Mann–Whitney U test) tests, and chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests, respectively. A p‐value <0.05 was considered significant.ResultsThere were no significant differences in background characteristics between the traditional and ASS groups. The shortening distance at postoperative day 1 did not differ significantly between the two groups (0 vs. 0 mm, p = 0.120). However, at 1, 3 months, and 1 year postoperatively, the femoral neck shortening in the ASS group was significantly less than that in the traditional group (1 month: 2.3 vs. 3.1 mm, p = 0.007; 3 months: 2.6 vs. 3.5 mm, p = 0.005; 1 year: 2.6 vs. 3.5 mm, p = 0.002). The ASS group also had a significantly lower shortening distance during the fracture healing process (0.9 vs. 2.7 mm, p = 0.005). The incidence of moderate to severe shortening (≥5 mm) at 1 year postoperatively was lower in the ASS group compared with the traditional group (15.1% vs. 37.7%, p = 0.001). The ASS group had a longer surgical time (63.0 ± 13.4 vs. 73.0 ± 23.2 min, p = 0.008) and a higher HHS (90.7 vs. 94.8, p = 0.008). There was no significant difference in fracture healing time or postoperative complications between the two groups. The traditional group had 3.8% cutouts, 7.5% nonunions, 5.7% avascular necrosis, and 7.5% secondary hip replacements. The ASS group saw 0% cutouts, 1.9% nonunions, 3.8% avascular necrosis, and 3.8% hip replacements. No significant differences in complication rates (p > 0.05).ConclusionThe use of an ASS in FNS fixation for displaced femoral neck fractures can reduce the degree of postoperative shortening and improve hip joint function.Keywords: Anti‐Shortening Screw, Femoral Neck Fracture, Femoral Neck Shortening, Femoral Neck System

Introduction

Femoral neck fractures are among the most common hip fractures, accounting for over 50% of such injuries.1, 2, 3 In non‐elderly patients, complications such as avascular necrosis of the femoral head, nonunion, and shortening of the femoral neck are prevalent following internal fixation of femoral neck fractures.4, 5, 6, 7 While there is considerable research on late‐stage avascular necrosis and nonunion, early post‐fracture shortening of the femoral neck has received less attention.8, 9 Numerous studies have shown that postoperative shortening is not only widespread but also significantly impacts patients' physical function and quality of life. Zlowodzki et al.10, 11 discovered in a multicenter study that femoral neck shortening can decrease SF‐36 scores, particularly when the shortening exceeds 10 mm. Additionally, Zielinski et al. 12 found that femoral neck shortening leads to a reduction in gait speed and an alteration in gait symmetry. Stockton et al. 13 reported that over half of the patients experienced at least 5mm of femoral neck shortening, with 32% showing shortening greater than 10 mm, and severe shortening (>10 mm) was associated with a marked decrease in hip function scores.

Stoffel et al. 14 conducted a biomechanical analysis revealing that the femoral neck system (FNS) offers better resistance to varus and rotation compared with three cannulated screws and sliding hip screws, but it has a weaker ability to prevent shortening. Previous literature has reported an approximate 39.1% incidence of moderate to severe shortening following FNS fixation. 15 Risk factors contributing to shortening include displaced fractures (Garden III and IV types) and comminution of the fracture ends. Femoral neck shortening can lead to decreased hip function and abnormal gait, with some patients experiencing complaints related to shortening and requiring the use of height‐increasing insoles in their daily lives.

The sliding mechanism of FNS applies compression to the fracture ends to promote healing, but within a sliding distance of 20 mm, FNS lacks an anti‐shortening mechanism. The force against shortening comes from the reaction force generated by the contact surface at both ends of the fracture. Therefore, when displaced fractures are combined with comminution of the fracture ends and poor reduction, the reaction force is reduced, leading to an increased incidence of moderate to severe shortening. To reduce the incidence of shortening, we have employed an anti‐shortening screw (ASS) to enhance the anti‐shortening mechanism (Figure 1).

Illustration of anti‐shortening screw (AS screw) in the management of femoral neck fractures, the application of the Femoral Neck System (FNS) augmented with an Anti‐Shortening Screw (ASS), the screw is a double‐threaded screw. The ASS is positioned above the FNS.

Starting in April 2020, our center began using FNS for the fixation of displaced femoral neck fractures. To clarify the clinical efficacy of the ASS in preventing shortening, we conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of patients treated with FNS for displaced femoral neck fractures at our center (a Level I trauma center) from April 2020 to April 2023. The patients were divided into a traditional group and an ASS group based on the use of the ASS. We hypothesized that the ASS could reduce the incidence of shortening and improve hip function after its use.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This study was reported in accordance with the STORBE guidelines 16 for clinical observational reports and was conducted following the approval of the Ethics Committee of the Hospital, as per the revised Declaration of Helsinki in 2013 17 and received approval from the Hospital's Ethics Committee (No. 2,021,185). Owing to the anonymized processing of data and the absence of harm to participants, the requirement for informed consent was waived. To elucidate the clinical efficacy of ASS in the treatment of displaced femoral neck fractures using the FNS. The primary objective was to assess the rate of severe shortening and the degree of shortening within the first year postoperatively. Secondary objectives included evaluating bone healing as determined by the treating physician and documenting patients' hip function and complications. Through this study, we aim to provide further evidence supporting the use of ASS in the clinical practice of FNS treatment for displaced femoral neck fractures.

Inclusion Exclusion Criteria

Patients admitted to the Level I trauma center between April 2020 and April 2023, with a diagnosis of displaced femoral neck fractures (Garden Types III and IV) confirmed by x‐ray, aged 18 to 65 years, and treated with the FNS (FNS, Johnson & Johnson, USA), were included in this study. Participants were categorized into a traditional group and an ASS group based on the utilization of a screw (Johnson & Johnson, USA) as the ASS. Exclusion criteria comprised pathological fractures, fractures older than 2 weeks, poor fracture reduction (Garden Index III/IV), multiple fractures, incomplete follow‐up data, post‐polio syndrome, and conditions such as preoperative osteoarthritis of the hip affecting hip joint function assessment (Figure 2).

Patient inclusion flowchart.

Procedure and Follow‐Up

In this surgical procedure, the patient is initially positioned in a supine position with the healthy side flexed at the hip and knee for elevation. After anesthesia and sterilization, closed reduction is performed, and intraoperative fluoroscopy is used to confirm that the reduction meets the required standards. Two 2.5‐mm Kirschner wires (K‐wires) are then temporarily inserted for fixation. Following the guidelines for FNS, a series of steps are carried out, including the insertion of guide pins, depth measurement, drilling, and the installation of the FNS device.

For the ASS group, an additional guide pin is inserted above the FNS following the standard procedure, with depth measurement and drilling conducted accordingly. An ASS, which can be a cannulated screw or a double‐threaded screw, is then implanted. After verification with fluoroscopy to ensure proper placement, the wound is irrigated and sutured closed.

In our postoperative protocol for FNS, patients receive first‐generation cephalosporins as a prophylactic measure against infection and low‐molecular‐weight heparin for thromboprophylaxis. Partial weight‐bearing is cautiously initiated at 6–8 weeks, contingent on radiographic findings. Subsequent follow‐up appointments are scheduled at 1, 3, 6 months, and 1 year postoperatively, involving radiographs to assess healing and functional scoring to evaluate clinical outcomes.

Data Collection and Outcome Measures

The initial information collected encompassed demographic and injury‐related details such as sex, age, Body Mass Index (BMI), the cause of the injury, and the categorization of fractures based on the Garden 18 and Pauwels 19 systems.

Primary Outcome Measures

Radiographic Assessment: Femoral Neck Shortening Measurement Method (Zlowodzki et al., 2007) 20 : This technique requires obtaining an AP radiograph of both hips, mirroring the healthy side to the affected side, and then measuring the horizontal (X‐axis) and vertical (Y‐axis) shortening of the femoral head. The axial shortening of the femoral neck (Z‐axis) is calculated using the formula Z = Ysin(θ) + Xcos(θ), where θ is the angle between the Y‐axis and the femoral neck axis. Shortening is categorized into mild (<5 mm), moderate (5‐10 mm), and severe (>10 mm) based on the Z value.

The assessment of femoral neck shortening at different time points postoperatively was conducted to monitor the progression and severity of shortening. This evaluation is crucial for understanding the long‐term outcomes of the surgical intervention.

Secondary Outcomes Measures

Secondary outcomes included surgical time, intraoperative blood loss, the Harris Hip Score (HHS) at final follow‐up, and the occurrence of complications. Surgical time was defined as the duration from the initial incision to the completion of the surgical wound closure. The criteria for determining fracture healing 21 include the absence of significant pain reported by the patient in the hip and lower extremity upon percussion testing postoperatively. Confirmation through x‐ray examination or computed tomography (CT) imaging reveals that the original fracture line is now indistinct, and continuous bridging of trabecular bone can be observed at the site of the old fracture.

HHS 22 : The HHS was utilized to assess hip function at the final follow‐up visit. This score evaluates pain, function, range of motion, and deformity, with a maximum score of 100. Scores are graded as excellent (90–100), good (80–89), fair (70–79), and poor (<70).

For the evaluation of the quality of reduction in femoral neck fractures, we employed both the Garden Index18, 23, 24 system and the Gotfried system. 25 The Gotfried system delineates three categories: anatomic reduction, positive support, and negative support, with anatomic reduction being termed Neutral Support. Specifically, positive support is characterized by the medial cortex of the femoral neck head–neck fragment being positioned laterally relative to the medial cortex of the distal femoral shaft. Conversely, in negative support, the medial cortex of the femoral neck head–neck fragment is situated medially in relation to the medial cortex of the distal femoral shaft. Negative support is recognized as the least stable configuration.

Tip Apex Distance (TAD): In our study, we utilized the method described by Lin 26 for measuring the TAD for FNS, which involves calculating the distance between the tip of the screw (Tip) and the apex of the junction of the medial axis of the femoral head and neck with the articular surface of the femoral head (Xap), as well as the lateral position distance (Xlat). The magnification factor of the x‐ray is corrected using the actual width of the screw (Dtrue) and the measured widths of the anterior–posterior and lateral position screws (Dap, Dlat). The TAD value is then computed as TAD = Xap × (Dtrue/Dap) + Xlat × (Dtrue/Dlat). A TAD value less than 25 mm is generally considered optimal because it is associated with a lower risk of complications and better clinical outcomes. This method provides an accurate assessment of screw positioning postoperatively.

Postoperative complication assessment criteria, 27 evaluated by a team of two specialized physicians, include the following aspects: (1) deep incisional infection; (2) implant penetration through the femoral head into the joint cavity; (3) implant displacement relative to the bone, without accompanying penetration of the femoral head; (4) implant failure, such as fracture or deformation; (5) delayed healing or nonunion; (6) avascular necrosis in the femoral head region; (7) necessity for secondary total hip arthroplasty.

Statistical Methods

For the analysis in this study, we utilized SPSS software, version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), to manage and interpret the data. Participant demographics and clinical data were characterized through descriptive statistics, with continuous variables such as age, BMI, time to surgery, TAD, and postoperative shortening distances reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data, and as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data like surgical time. Categorical variables including sex, smoking and drinking habits, injury side, reduction quality, and classifications (Garden and Pauwels), were presented in terms of frequencies and percentages.

The comparison of continuous variables between groups was performed using the independent samples t‐test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U test for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test where appropriate. A two‐tailed p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was applied to assess the inter‐rater reliability, with higher values indicating better agreement. Post hoc power analysis was conducted to confirm that the sample size was adequate for the observed effects.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

All patients were followed for a mean duration of (19.33 ± 4.595 months) (range: 12–28 months). In the Traditional group, 53 patients were enrolled, comprising 36 men and 17 women with a median age of 50 (37, 58) years. The left side was affected in 23 cases and the right side in 30 cases, with a mean BMI of 23.4 (21.6, 24.6) kg/m2. The ASS group included 53 patients, there were 49 cases utilizing cannulated screws and 4 cases with double‐threaded screws, with 34 men and 19 women, and a median age of 53 (40, 57) years. The fractures occurred on the left side in 29 cases and on the right side in 24 cases, accompanied by a mean BMI of 23.7 (22.3, 24.8) kg/m2.

There were no significant differences in background characteristics between the traditional and ASS groups (age, sex, BMI, smoking and drinking history, injury mechanism, side of injury, fracture type, and cortical comminution, all p > 0.05). (Table 1).

Primary Outcome Measures

Femoral Neck Shortening

The traditional group exhibited a M (Q1, Q3) shortening distance of 3.1 mm (1.9, 6.7) at 1 month, 3.5 mm (2.4, 7.5) at 3 months, and 3.5 mm (2.4, 7.6) at 1 year postoperatively. In contrast, the ASS group demonstrated significantly less shortening, with median values of 2.3 mm (0, 3.8), 2.6 mm (0, 4.2), and 2.6 mm (0, 4.2) at the respective time points (p = 0.007, p = 0.005, and p = 0.002, respectively). The shortening during healing also favored the ASS group, with a median of 0.9 mm (0, 3.9) compared with 2.7 mm (1.2, 5.6) in the traditional group (p = 0.005). Furthermore, the proportion of patients with no shortening was significantly higher in the ASS group (47.2%) than in the traditional group (17.0%), and the incidence of moderate to severe shortening (≥5 mm), the traditional group showed a higher rate of moderate to severe shortening, affecting 37.7% of patients, compared with just 15.1% in the ASS group (p = 0.001) (Table 2). Each case is depicted with three figures: A (preoperative), B (1 day postoperative), and C (after fracture healing), highlighting the effectiveness of the ASS in reducing postoperative femoral neck shortening. The use of cannulated screws in cases 1–3 and double‐threaded screws in cases 4–5 is evident in the figure. It is noteworthy that non‐severe shortening (less than 5 mm) was not observed during the healing period for cases 1, 4, and 5. However, persistent shortening exceeding 10 mm was recorded in cases 2 and 3, emphasizing the importance of the ASS in managing femoral neck fractures.

Secondary Outcome Measures

Quality of Reduction and Surgical Outcomes

The quality of reduction, as assessed by the Garden index, was comparable between the two groups, with a majority of patients in both groups achieving Grade I or II reduction (p = 0.834). Reduction quality assessed by Gotfried system, the ASS group: 69.8% (37 cases) had Neutral Support, 22.6% (12 cases) had Positive Support, and 7.6% (4 cases) had Negative Support, and the traditional group: 66.0% (35 cases) Neutral, 28.3% (15 cases) Positive, and 5.7% (3 cases) Negative, with no significant differences between groups (p = 0.767), with no significant TAD differences (12.9 [11.8, 17.5] mm vs. 12.3 [11.3, 14.2] mm, p = 0.141). However, the ASS group had a longer surgical time (73.0 ± 23.2 min) compared with the traditional group (63.0 ± 13.4 min), indicating a more complex procedure (p = 0.008). Despite this, there was no significant difference in intraoperative blood loss between the groups (p = 0.742).

Harris Hip Score

At the final follow‐up, the ASS group had a higher HHS (M [Q1, Q3] of 94.8 [89.9, 96.7]) compared with the traditional group (90.7 [80.3, 96.1]), suggesting better functional outcomes in the ASS group (p = 0.008).

Complications

In a comparative analysis of traditional and ASS groups, postoperative complications, including cutout, nonunion, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, fracture deformity healing, and secondary total hip arthroplasty, were assessed. The traditional group reported 3.8% cutouts, 7.5% nonunions, and 5.7% avascular necrosis of the femoral head, with 7.5% requiring secondary total hip arthroplasty. The ASS group had 0% cutouts, 1.9% nonunions, 3.8% avascular necrosis, and 3.8% undergoing secondary total hip arthroplasty. No significant differences were observed in the rates of these complications (p > 0.05 for all). Joint replacement was primarily attributed to avascular necrosis and cutout issues (Table 3).

Overall, the ASS group exhibited a marked decrease in femoral neck shortening and enhanced functional results, as reflected by the HHS. Although the surgical procedure was longer, the quality of reduction and the rate of complications were on par with the traditional group. This indicates that the ASS could provide notable benefits in treating femoral neck fractures.

Discussion

This study compared the efficacy of the ASS versus traditional methods in the treatment of femoral neck fractures. The results indicated that the ASS group experienced a significant reduction in postoperative femoral neck shortening and better functional recovery, as evidenced by higher HHS. Although the ASS group had longer operative times, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of fracture healing or complications.

Shortening as a Necessary Condition or Complication for Fracture Healing?

For patients with femoral neck fractures who have undergone fixation with the FNS, the issue of femoral neck shortening persists, posing challenges to recovery and functional restoration. Stockton et al. 13 reported a shortening incidence rate of 54% (≥5 mm) after cannulated screw fixation in young patients with femoral neck fractures. Although some studies have shown that the FNS, a novel internal fixation device, has a lower incidence of shortening compared with cannulated screws,28, 29 it still ranges as high as 23.3%–39.1%.15, 30 Currently, moderate to severe shortening (≥5 mm) in femoral neck fractures is generally considered a complication because fracture healing requires stress stimulation rather than shortening. Particularly, moderate to severe shortening is associated with poor functional outcomes. Zlowodzki et al. 20 found that common issues following femoral neck shortening include gait changes, limited hip joint mobility, and pain, which can significantly affect patients' daily life and mental health. This may lead to a shortened stride while walking and increased reliance on walking aids or other assistive devices. 31 Knobe et al. 32 suggested that changes in the hip joint's mechanical environment due to femoral neck shortening could accelerate the degeneration of articular cartilage and increase the risk of osteoarthritis. Slobogean et al. 33 reported decreased hip joint function and increased pain. For patients who have already experienced femoral neck shortening, more aggressive treatment measures may be necessary, such as structural bone grafting or hip replacement surgery, to restore lower limb length and improve hip joint function. Zhu et al. 34 noted that postoperative shortening is multifactorial, including the type of fracture, the quality of surgical reduction, and the type of internal fixation.15, 35 To reduce femoral neck shortening after FNS, strategies such as reducing sliding distance and using ASS have been employed. 15 Our analysis of patients who underwent internal fixation with ASS revealed a significantly reduced rate of femoral neck shortening. The ASS group had a severe shortening incidence rate of 15.1% within 1 year postoperatively, lower than the traditional group's 37.7%, and also reduced the distance of shortening during the healing process. The ASS screw, by integrating with the bone to form a nail‐bone complex, increases the internal fixation's anti‐shortening force through friction, reducing the occurrence of shortening and thereby improving hip joint function.

Analysis of Shortening Causes

Postoperative femoral neck shortening in patients who have undergone FNS fixation for femoral neck fractures is a complex phenomenon that can be attributed to multiple factors. Firstly, it is associated with the degree of cortical comminution and the extent of fracture displacement. 30 Rajnish et al. 29 mentioned in their study that comminution of the cortical bone at the fracture ends leads to insufficient stability of internal fixation, increasing the risk of femoral neck shortening. Wang et al. 15 found in their research that cortical comminution near the fracture line could lead to fixation failure and femoral neck shortening. High‐grade displacement fractures are more likely to result in femoral neck shortening because displacement increases the difficulty of reduction and maintaining length. 36 Secondly, surgical technique is also a contributing factor. The positioning of FNS fixation and the allowed sliding distance are crucial in preventing shortening, and Yan et al. 37 emphasized the importance of controlling femoral neck shortening in surgical treatment. To reduce the occurrence of postoperative shortening, a comprehensive consideration of these factors is necessary. In addition to assessing the fracture pattern and improving the quality of reduction, appropriate strategies can be taken to prevent shortening. We found that the surgical strategy of adding an ASS can improve the degree and incidence of shortening, but it requires additional surgical time. Therefore, standardized procedures and assessments of the indications for the use of FNS and ASS screws are necessary.

Indications for the Use of ASS

The ASS are particularly indicated for the treatment of displaced femoral neck fractures (Garden Types III or IV), which often accompany cortical comminution. For stable fractures (Garden Types I or II) of the femoral neck, anatomical reduction of the cortex is achieved after closed reduction, and a stable structure can be formed with internal fixation using the FNS, which is less likely to result in postoperative shortening, eliminating the need for additional screws. In contrast, displaced femoral neck fractures (Garden Types III or IV) often involve cortical comminution at the fracture ends, with an incidence rate of 50%–96%, and 82% of the cortical comminutions are located in the posteromedial aspect of the femoral neck.38, 39, 40 Post‐reduction, the comminuted cortical areas cannot provide sufficient mechanisms or forces to prevent shortening. The FNS, which is designed to promote compression at the fracture ends and facilitate healing, includes a 20 mm sliding distance 14 and does not have an anti‐shortening mechanism, making it less effective for displaced femoral neck fractures with cortical comminution. Therefore, the use of ASS is indicated for such cases. Our findings indicate that the use of ASS screws in displaced fractures has reduced the incidence of shortening.

Summary

In summary, the ASS has demonstrated advantages in the treatment of femoral neck fractures by reducing femoral neck shortening and improving functional outcomes. Despite longer operative times, there was no increase in complications or the risk of secondary surgeries. These findings suggest that the ASS may be an effective means of treating femoral neck fractures, contributing to better patient outcomes. Future research could further explore the application of ASS in various types of femoral neck fractures and how to optimize surgical techniques and postoperative management to enhance treatment efficacy.

Strengths and Limitations

The study's primary strength lies in its demonstration of the ASS as an effective intervention for reducing postoperative femoral neck shortening in patients with displaced femoral neck fractures.

The limitations of this study include a limited sample size, which may restrict our ability to detect small effect size differences, and it is not feasible to analyze the differences among various types of anti‐migration screws or the differences in screw placement at different locations. Future studies should consider increasing the sample size to enhance statistical power and validate our findings. The lack of a multicenter or randomized design may introduce selection and implementation biases, affecting the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the relatively short follow‐up period and the absence of long‐term data limit our assessment of the long‐term effects and potential distant complications of ASS such as avascular necrosis of the femoral head. Although this study provides preliminary evidence for the use of ASS in the treatment of femoral neck fractures, more rigorously designed studies are needed to overcome these limitations and yield comprehensive and reliable conclusions.
