Early PET response predicts the risk of relapse after 2L axi-cel in large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Andrea Kuhnl, Amy A Kirkwood, Michael Northend, Rajesh Alajangi, Ben Uttenthal, Jane Norman, Hwai Hiew, Frances Seymour, Bernard D Maybury, Wendy Osborne, Francesca Sillito, Ahmed Abdulgawad, Ceri Jones, Alison Delaney, William Townsend, Aikaterini Panopoulou, John G Gribben, Edward Bataillard, Amrith Mathew, Nicolas Martinez-Calle, Lavanya Gajendran, Andrew J Davies, Nikesh Chavda, Emil Kumar, Robin Sanderson, Sachin Kamat, George Petrides, Claire Roddie, Tobias Menne, Sridhar Chaganti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•One-month PET response predicts the risk of relapse afte 2L axi-cel.•Clinical risk model using PET response and pre-treatment LDH level may be used to guide post-CAR-T management.

Introduction

Despite the success of CD19-targeted chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR T) therapies in relapsed or refractory (R/R) large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL), ∼50% to 60% of the patients will ultimately progress. Outcome after CAR T failure is generally poor, with a median overall survival (OS) of ∼5 months after third-line (3L) CAR T.1,2 However, patients who receive effective post-CAR T treatments, including CD20 × CD3–bispecific antibodies or radiotherapy (RT) for limited-stage relapse, may achieve prolonged remissions.1

With an increasing number of new treatment options available in the post-CAR T setting, early identification of CAR T refractoriness is important before patients become unwell with high-burden disease, which might compromise not only their fitness for further therapies but also the efficacy of treatments. Patients with LBCL and high tumor burden had inferior response rates and long-term survival after bispecific antibodies3; hence, introducing these agents early might increase the chance of treatment success. At the same time, most treatments considered in patients R/R to CAR T are associated with significant toxicities, most importantly the risk of prolonged cytopenias and severe infections, which is of particular concern in a patient population heavily immunocompromised as a consequence of CAR T cell therapy. Therefore, unnecessary treatments and additional toxicities should be avoided in patients likely to be cured by CAR T.

Most post-CAR T progression events occur early, within 6 months of infusion, often after a transient response to treatment. We have previously demonstrated that the depth of early fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG PET) response predicted the risk of CAR T failure in the 3L setting: responding patients with deep response (Deauville score [DS] 1-2) had significantly lower risk of progression and better long-term survival than those with response DS ≥3.4 However, patients who had received RT bridging therapy with DS4 activity confined to the radiation field had similar outcomes to patients with DS1-2 response at 1 month (M1).4 Other retrospective studies have confirmed the prognostic significance of early DS response in the context of 3L CAR T.5,6 These findings have led to the development of clinical trials investigating post-CAR T consolidation approaches in patients with “insufficient response” using CAR T-cell engagers, antibody-drug conjugates, or bispecific antibodies (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT06045910, NCT05464719, and NCT06071871).

No data on the use of PET metrics in patients with early response to second-line (2L) CAR T are available yet to guide risk stratification and post-CAR T management. Results from the 3L CAR T setting cannot necessarily be extrapolated to a distinct cohort of 2L CAR T patients with primary refractory disease, which might significantly differ in terms of T-cell fitness, tumor burden, dynamics of response, and inflammatory state.

In this multicenter, national cohort of 302 patients with LBCL treated with 2L axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel), we investigated the prognostic significance of early PET response in the 2L setting.

Methods

Patients

Consecutive patients with R/R LBCL approved by the UK National CAR T Clinical panel for 2L axi-cel between May 2023 and November 2024 and treated across 18 commissioned CAR T centers were included. During the time of this analysis, lisocabtagene maraleucel (liso-cel) was not yet available in the United Kingdom and therefore not included. National CAR T Clinical panel eligibility criteria, data recording, lymphodepletion (LD) chemotherapy, and toxicity management were described previously.7 Cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome grading was performed as per Autologous Stem Cell Transplant consensus guidelines.8 FDG PET scans were planned at M1, 3 months, and 6 months after axi-cel infusion; however, some sites may perform the 6-month assessment by contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan or clinical examination if a complete metabolic response (CMR) was reported at month 3. Response was assessed locally according to the 5-point DS system.9 PET scans were analyzed using non–point spread function reconstructions. Patients with localized DS4 in-field uptake after RT bridging were classified as having DS4RT as described previously.4

Statistical considerations

Pretreatment factors and toxicity were compared using Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney/Kruskal-Wallis (continuous variables) or χ2/Fisher exact tests (discrete variables). Progression-free survival (PFS; events: progression and death) and OS (event: death) were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox regression. Times were measured from the date of infusion to the date of the first event, with patients who did not experience an event censored at the date last seen. Time to progression was analyzed using competing-risk survival analysis by the method of Fine and Gray with death in remission as a competing event. A sensitivity analysis for PFS was performed censoring at the time of new treatment in PR (supplemental Data 1; supplemental Figure 3). Multivariable models used stepwise selection techniques, and sensitivity and specificity were calculated using survival receiver operating characteristic analysis. All analyses were performed in STATA version 19.5 (StataCorp, TX).

Data were collected retrospectively from electronic records as part of a national service evaluation/with ethical approval (Research Ethics Committee reference: 24/LO/0527, Integrated Research Application System project ID: 331212). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Patient characteristics

Of the 302 patients treated with 2L axi-cel, 297 were evaluable for response at M1, of which 245 responded and had available PET data (analysis set; Figure 1). Of the 245 patients included, 150 had CMR (DS1-3), 70 had a partial response (PR) DS4, and 25 had PR DS5. Among patients with DS4 response, 16 were grouped as DS4RT (focal DS4 activity confined to the bridging RT field). In line with our previous report,4 outcomes of DS4RT patients were similar to patients in CMR and were therefore grouped together (supplemental Figure 1). For 59 patients with M1 CMR, DS3 vs DS1-2 data were available, revealing no significant difference in PFS between the 2 groups (hazard ratio [HR], 1.26 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.47-3.39]; P = .65); hence, patients were analyzed as a combined group DS1-3 (supplemental Figure 2).

Patients with high International Prognostic Index at approval, high lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at approval or before LD, primary refractory disease, or high CAR-HEMATOTOX score were less likely to achieve deep response at M1 (DS1-3/DS4RT vs DS4 vs DS5). In addition, patients with a response to bridging therapy had a significantly higher chance of having CMR/DS4RT at M1 (77.3% vs 51.7% for patients with no response to bridging; P < .0001). There were no significant differences in cytokine release syndrome, immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome, or M1 cytopenia rates between DS categories (data not provided).

With a median follow-up of 13.9 months from infusion (interquartile range, 9.5-18.3), PFS and OS were significantly worse for patients with DS4 or DS5 response. The 12-month PFS was 50.1% (DS4) and 27% (DS5) vs 71.2% for DS1-3/DS4RT (DS4: HR, 2.54 [95% CI: 1.56-4.12] and DS5: HR, 4.60 [95% CI: 2.68-7.90]; P < .0001; Figure 2A). Similar results were found when censoring at the time of starting new antilymphoma treatment (supplemental Figure 3). The 12-month OS was 60.6% (DS4) and 66% (DS5) vs 87.2% for DS1-3/DS4RT (DS4: HR, 3.31 [95% CI: 1.80-6.07] and DS5: HR, 4.54 [95% CI: 2.32-8.88]; P < .0001; Figure 2B).

The M1 DS was significantly associated with the durability of response. Patients with DS4 and DS5 response had a 50% and 73% risk of progression by month 6, respectively, compared with only 25% for DS1-3/DS4RT (DS4: HR, 3.07 [95% CI: 1.85-5.07] and DS5: HR, 5.20 [95% CI: 2.95-9.16]; P < .0001; Figure 2C). Within the DS4/5 group, 39 of 74 (53%) progressed between 1 and 3 months.

In multivariable analyses (MVA) for time to progression, DS categories at M1, C-reactive protein, and LDH levels before LD were independently predictive of outcome (Table 2). Response to bridging was no longer significant in MVA; however, 21 of the 22 patients in CMR after bridging remained in CMR at M1.

With the DS1-3/DS4RT cohort having favorable outcomes supporting observation and the DS5 groups having sufficiently poor outcomes to justify escalation, we primarily focused on the DS4 group to further refine risk stratification. Adding pre-LD LDH as risk factor to our model (significant in MVA and strongest effect within DS4 group), we were able to separate 2 groups of DS4 responders with distinct outcome (high-risk vs low-risk DS4: 83.3% risk of progressive disease by month 6 vs 35.3%; HR, 4.39 [95% CI: 1.96-9.85]; P < .001; Figure 2D; supplemental Data 2).

Sensitivity and specificity of our model were 31.1% and 96.3%, respectively (supplemental Table 1), identifying a high-risk group (DS5 or high-risk DS4) and low-risk group (DS1-3/DS4RT or low-risk DS4) with a 6-month progressive disease risk of 70.3% (95% CI: 55.4-83.9) vs 17.7% (95% CI: 13.1-23.6), respectively (HR, 5.21 [95% CI: 3.27-8.29]; P < .001; Figure 2E).

Discussion

Most patients with R/R LBCL will progress after CAR T, and effective treatments are now available for these patients. We demonstrate that early FDG PET response at M1 after infusion can identify patients at high risk of CAR T refractoriness who might benefit from timely intervention.

In our cohort of patients responding to 2L axi-cel, those with DS5 response or DS4 response with high pretreatment LDH had a 70.3% risk of disease progression by month 6. These results validate findings by Crombie et al,5 who have demonstrated significant differences in outcome after 3L CAR T according to DS4 and 5 response, with the additional prognostic impact of preinfusion LDH levels. Given the high risk of relapse in this group, we believe that patients should be consulted at this early time point, offered participation in relevant clinical trials, and allowed to weigh the risks and benefits of observation vs active treatment if available. Particularly, treatments not interfering with potentially ongoing CAR T effector function, such as focal irradiation, should be strongly considered. Retrospective and prospective clinical data suggest that early consolidation RT to residual FDG activity after CAR T may alter the pattern of relapse and improve long-term outcome.10,11

Patients with deep early response DS1-3/DS4RT have an excellent prognosis, irrespective of pretreatment risk factors such as tumor burden, LDH, or response to bridging therapy. These patients are likely cured with CAR T, and overtreatment and additional toxicities should be avoided. As shown previously, patients with focal in-field DS4 uptake after bridging RT (DS4RT) have similar outcomes to CMR patients and should not be regarded as “insufficient responders.” Histiocyte-rich inflammatory changes in irradiated lesions leading to increased uptake after CAR T have been described,12 highlighting the importance of differentiating inflammatory changes from tumor activity by an experienced multidisciplinary team to avoid overcalling of active lesions. In contrast to our previous analysis, there was no significant difference in outcomes between DS1-2 and DS3 responses, consistent with findings from US and French cohorts.5,6

Assessment of minimal residual disease via circulating tumor (ct) DNA levels early after CAR T is a promising new biomarker of response, complementary to standard imaging. Patients treated with 3L axi-cel who had residual PET activity on the M1 scan but undetectable ctDNA levels had significantly longer PFS and OS.13,14 Results from patients treated with liso-cel on the TRANSCEND trial demonstrated that ctDNA levels were of prognostic significance even within the cohort of patients achieving early CMR.15 Despite the great potential for using ctDNA to refine risk stratification after CAR T, these markers are unlikely to become widely available to CAR T patients in the near future, and rapid turnaround for timely decision-making will be challenging. Our model uses the Deauville 5-point scale and LDH levels as universally available clinical variables. Simple clinical risk models can be promptly implemented in routine practice and clinical trials, allowing equity of access to stratification tools and matching of large international cohorts. HRs for PFS in DS4 and DS5 responders were similar to the analysis of Crombie et al5, despite differences in 2L vs 3L CAR T cohorts with respect to total metabolic tumor volume (TMTV), bridging practice, and inflammatory response, underscoring the robustness of early Deauville response as a prognostic marker across different settings and CAR T products. However, our model should be validated in independent 2L CAR T cohorts, with inclusion of liso-cel–treated patients.

Although the Deauville scale is widely available and easy to assess, it does not reflect the volume, heterogeneity, or distribution of metabolically active disease. Other semiquantitative PET metrics such as TMTV, total lesion glycolysis, or Standardized Uptake Valuemax/mean may improve prognostication after CAR T cell therapy16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 but were not available for this cohort. Routine implementation of volumetric PET data is currently limited by the lack of standardization and time requirements, but this might become broadly available through artificial intelligence–based tools in the future.

Preinfusion TMTV has been associated with CAR T outcomes across many data sets22, 23, 24, 25, 26 and can, if available, help to guide toxicity management and predict the risk of primary progression and post-CAR T outcomes. However, in most analyses, preinfusion and postinfusion metrics were not fully integrated, and the weight of preinfusion risk factors in the context of treatment response is less clear. In the study by Galtier et al,6 TMTV was powerful in predicting primary progression, but its significance was mitigated in patients with deep response at M1. Conversely, patients with low preinfusion TMTV and suboptimal M1 response had poor outcomes. Therefore, post-CAR T treatment decisions should include response-based risk stratification.

Clinical responses to CAR T are fast, particularly for axi-cel, with the vast majority of responses evident on the M1 scan. However, progression events occur early, mostly within 2 months of initial response, resulting in a short window when consolidation approaches to deepen and prolong transient CAR T responses can be considered. If the clinical decision on post-CAR T intervention in partial responders is delayed to the 3-month time point, in our cohort 53% of these patients would have already overtly progressed, highlighting the difficulty in navigating between high accuracy of risk prediction vs the optimal window for treatment success.

Limitations of our study include the retrospective design, the lack of availability of other PET metrics such as Standardized Uptake Valuemax or TMTV, and the lack of centralized imaging review. The strength of our analysis lies in the consecutive national cohort of patients uniformly selected and treated with axi-cel.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated the use of early PET Deauville response as a tool to risk stratify patients undergoing 2L axi-cel treatment and identify those at very high risk of CAR T failure. Our risk model uses universally available clinical markers that can guide CAR T consolidation decisions in daily practice and inform CAR T combination and consolidation trial designs.
