CircCdh7 induces astrogliosis and neuroinflammation to trigger hypertensive effects in the rostral ventrolateral medulla
Authors: Shuai Zhang, Tengteng Dai, Bingjie Zhao, Xueping Wang, Linping Wang, Lei Tong, Qiangcheng Zeng, Qing He, Haili Zhang, Dongshu Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM) is a critical vasomotor center that plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of hypertension. However, the involvement of circular RNAs (circRNAs) in this nucleus in blood pressure (BP) regulation remains incompletely understood.MethodsIn this study, we investigated functional circRNAs in the RVLM associated with hypertension and elucidated their underlying mechanisms using multiple experimental approaches, including RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), primary cell culture, and intra-RVLM microinjection.ResultsOur results revealed a significantly elevated level of the highly conserved circCdh7 in the RVLM of spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs). Downregulation of circCdh7 in the RVLM reduced neuronal excitability, sympathetic outflow, and BP in SHRs. Mechanistically, circCdh7 functioned as a sponge for miR-346. miR-346 knockdown largely abolished the inhibitory effects of circCdh7 suppression on RVLM astrogliosis and neuroinflammation. Furthermore, miR-346 was found to target Osmr. Overexpression of miR-346 attenuated RVLM astrogliosis and neuroinflammation, but these beneficial effects were abolished by Osmr overexpression.ConclusionsCollectively, our findings demonstrate that elevated circCdh7 expression in the RVLM drives hypertension progression, with the circCdh7/miR-346/Osmr axis serving as a key regulatory mechanism. Targeting circCdh7 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for hypertension.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01480-0.

Introduction

Hypertension, a chronic condition defined by sustained blood pressure (BP) ≥ 140/90 mmHg, increases the risk of complications such as kidney disease and retinopathy [1, 2]. Heightened sympathetic nerve activity (SNA) is a critical contributor to the pathogenesis and persistence of hypertension [3, 4]. The rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM), a major brainstem pressor region, exerts fundamental control over both SNA and BP. Previous studies have demonstrated that β-arrestin 1 overexpression in the RVLM reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) and attenuates hypertension [5]. The significantly higher levels of YTHDF3 in the RVLM inhibited the repair of neuronal DNA oxidative damage, promoted neuronal apoptosis, and ultimately led to the development of hypertension [6]. Additionally, enhanced cannabinoid type 1 receptor expression in the RVLM contributes to obesity-related hypertension [7]. Collectively, these findings indicate that abnormal gene expression in the RVLM promotes sympathetic overactivation and hypertension pathogenesis. Therefore, elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of gene expression in the RVLM is essential for preventing disease progression.

Noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), a significant component of the transcriptome, lack the capacity to encode proteins [8]. However, these molecules exert profound influence over gene expression through intricate mechanisms operating at both transcriptional and posttranscriptional levels [9]. Circular RNAs (circRNAs), a subclass of ncRNAs, are distinguished by the absence of a 5ʹ cap and 3ʹ tail [10, 11] and play crucial roles in a wide range of pathological processes [12–14]. Several circRNAs, such as hsa_circ_0037897, hsa_circ_0105015, and circHIPK2, have been linked to hypertension [15–17]. However, research into circRNAs in the cardiovascular center and their role in SNA and BP remains in its infancy. Our previous study found that circRNA Galntl6 was significantly downregulated in the RVLM of stress-induced hypertensive rats, promoting neuronal oxidative stress and hypertension development [18]. Further research in this area is urgently needed.

This study involved the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of RVLM tissues taken from spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs) to explore alterations in the circRNA transcriptome. The expression levels of circCdh7 in the RVLM of SHRs were further determined by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). The impact of reduction in circCdh7 on RVLM astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, neuronal excitability, sympathetic tone, and BP were revealed. Whether circCdh7 functions as a sponge of miR-346 targeting oncostatin M receptor (Osmr) was verified. Furthermore, the interactions among circCdh7, miR-346, and Osmr in relation to astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, and hypertension development were demonstrated. This study identified RVLM circCdh7, which could potentially be associated with high BP, offering a valuable asset for exploring its functional implications in addressing hypertension.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male SHRs and Wistar Kyoto (WKY) rats at the age of 8 weeks were sourced from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd, and allowed to freely consume chow and water. The rats were guaranteed to be pathogen- and virus-free and were housed in an environment with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. The room temperature was controlled at 23 °C with a variance of ± 1 °C, and the humidity level was monitored within 50–60%. They were randomly assigned to diverse experimental groups. No criteria were predetermined for determining inclusion and exclusion. Sample sizes comparable to those in previous studies were utilized in this study [19–21]. WKY rats less than 24 h of age were procured from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. for primary astrocyte culture. The rats were euthanized by CO2 inhalation at 30% volume displacement rate. The animal ethics committee at Shanghai University follows the rules of Basel Declaration.

Primary astrocyte culture

Primary astrocytes were extracted from the RVLM tissues of WKY rats less than 24 h of age. RVLM tissues were collected through a punch-out method by following the criteria outlined in the rat brain atlas [22]. The tissues were dissected and digested with 0.125% trypsin (including 100 U DNase I) at room temperature for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min, after which the supernatant was meticulously aspirated. The obtained pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium formulated with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine. Then, the cells were transferred to an incubator set at 37 °C with a 5% CO₂ atmosphere and cultured for 14 days. Afterward, the cells were placed on a continuous-temperature shaker working at 37 ℃ and agitated at 200 rpm for 24 h for purification.

Mean arterial pressure (MAP) recording

The MAP in conscious rats was monitored in real time by employing a radiotelemetry system manufactured by Kaha Sciences (AD Instruments, Australia). Briefly, as the rats were anesthetized through isoflurane inhalation (oxygen flowmeter turned to 0.8–1.5 L/min; isoflurane vaporizer turned to 3–5%), the abdominal aorta was surgically implanted with a telemetry catheter, which was securely attached with tissue adhesive and a mesh for stability. The telemeter module was firmly fastened to the peritoneal area. The rats were given a week-long recovery phase after the surgical operation before recording was started. The Kaha Sciences SmartPad system (AD Instruments, Australia) was utilized for acquiring MAP signals. Data were systematically analyzed each day with a PowerLab system (AD Instruments, Australia).

Recording of renal sympathetic nerve activity (RSNA)

The left renal sympathetic nerve was surgically exposed and isolated through incision procedure under inhalational anesthesia with isoflurane (oxygen flowmeter turned to 0.8–1.5 L/min; isoflurane vaporizer turned to 3–5%). The nerve was situated onto a pair of platinum–iridium electrodes. Kwik-Sil gel (World Precision Instruments, the USA) was then applied to shield the nerve-electrode combination. Nerve activity was amplified (1000×) and filtered (bandpass 100–3000 Hz) by utilizing a grass P55C preamplifier. Nerve signals were integrated through the application of a PowerLab system (AD Instruments, Australia). Background noise was evaluated at the end of the recording following intravenous injection of hexamethonium bromide (30 mg/kg), a ganglionic blocking agent. The integrated values of nerve activity were adjusted by subtracting the noise level.

Plasma norepinephrine (NE) measurement

Blood samples were collected from the rats via cardiac puncture under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia (oxygen flowmeter turned to 0.8–1.5 L/min; isoflurane vaporizer turned to 3–5%). The blood samples were transferred to collection tubes preloaded with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid. They were then immediately centrifuged at 1000×g for 15 min at 4 °C to acquire the supernatant for analysis. An enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (FineTest, China) was employed to determine plasma NE levels in accordance with the manufacturer’ s protocols.

Immunofluorescence

The rats were anesthetized by administering pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) via intraperitoneal injection and subjected to transcardial perfusion with 200 mL of heparinized saline then to infusion with 200 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde through the ascending aorta. Brains were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 12 h, followed by dehydration in 20% sucrose for 24 h at 4°C, and then further dehydrated in 30% sucrose for another 24 h at the same temperature. Slices measuring 30 μm in thickness were taken from the RVLM by utilizing a cryostat apparatus (Thermo Scientific, the USA). Upon being cultured on glass coverslips, primary astrocytes were fixed through treatment with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. The slices or coverslips were permeabilized for 15 min by using 0.3% Triton X-100 and blocked with QuickBlock buffer (Beyotime, China) for 30 min. After being blocked, the samples underwent an extended overnight incubation at 4 °C in the presence of the following specified primary antibodies: rabbit anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, ab7260, 1:600, Abcam, the USA), rabbit anti-c-Fos (ab190289, 1:400, Abcam, the USA), and mouse anti-NeuN (66836-1-lg, 1:400, Proteintech, the USA). On the next day, they were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with the following secondary antibodies: goat antirabbit IgG (111-585-003, 1:400, Jackson ImmunoResearch, the USA) and goat antimouse IgG (115-095-003, 1:200, Jackson ImmunoResearch, the USA). Fluorescent signals were visualized by employing a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

Library preparation and sequencing

cDNA libraries were constructed in accordance with previously outlined methods [18]. Briefly, total RNA was isolated from RVLM tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, the USA) according to the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. A NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, the USA) and 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent, the USA) were employed for the evaluation of RNA purity and integrity, respectively. Eight cDNA libraries, identified as SHR (SHR_1, SHR_2, SHR_3, and SHR_4) and WKY (WKY_1, WKY_2, WKY_3, and WKY_4), were assembled by employing a TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Human/Mouse/Rat kit (Illumina, the USA) after the eradication of ribosomal RNA by using Ribo-Zero rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina, the USA). The quality of the libraries was assessed by using a 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent, the USA). An Illumina HiSeq 4000 sequencer at Lianchuan Bio (China) was used to sequence the libraries, resulting in 150 bp paired-end reads.

Evaluation of differentially expressed circRNAs

Clean reads were generated by eliminating reads with adaptors, low quality, and undetermined bases from the raw data by utilizing Cutadapt [23]. Afterward, the clean reads were referenced to the rat genome (ftp://ftp.ensembl.org/pub/release-109/fasta/rattus_norvegicus/) by using Bowtie [24] and Hisat [25] for alignment. The alignment of unmapped reads to the rat genome was extended by using Tophat-fusion [26]. Back-spliced reads within these unmapped reads were identified by using CIRI and CIRCExplorer [27, 28]. Each sample produced circRNAs. EdgeR [29] was applied to analyze the differential expression of circRNAs between the two groups.

RT-qPCR

The TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was used for total RNA extraction following the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription of miR-346 was carried out with a stem-loop primer (5ʹ GTCGT ATCCA GTGTG CAGGG TCCGA GGTAT TCGCA CTGGA TACGA CAGAG GC 3ʹ). RNA was converted into cDNA by utilizing Hifair V reverse transcriptase (Yeasen, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. A LightCycler 480 system (Roche, the USA) was utilized for RT-qPCR with Hieff SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China). The list of primer pairs was presented in Supplemental Table 1. Data were normalized on the basis of U6 and GAPDH by using the 2−∆∆Ct method.

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated and converted into cDNA as described above. RT-PCR was conducted by using 2 × Hieff PCR Master Mix (Yeasen, China) on the MyCycler Thermal Cycler system (Bio-Rad, the USA). The divergent primer for circCdh7, sanger sequencing primer for circCdh7, convergent primer for Cdh7, and convergent primer for GAPDH employed in RT-PCR are detailed in Supplemental Table 2. PCR products were determined through electrophoresis on 2% agarose gel or sanger sequencing.

RNase R treatment

Total RNAs were isolated in accordance with the above-mentioned steps and then treated with RNase R (Yeasen, China) at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction mixture consisted of 1 μg of RNA, 0.2 μL of RNase R, and 2 μL of 10× RNase R reaction buffer added with PCR-grade H2O to a final volume of 20 μL. After RNase R treatment, the RNAs were purified by using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) as outlined in the provided assay procedure. The purified RNAs were converted into cDNA and analyzed through RT-qPCR.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fraction separation assay

RNAs were separated and purified from the nucleus and cytoplasm using the cytoplasmic & nuclear RNA purification kit (Norgen Biotek, the USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quantity of circCdh7 in the cytoplasmic or nuclear fraction was determined by using RT-qPCR.

Intra-RVLM microinjection

HBLV-r-circCdh7 shRNA-PURO (circCdh7 shRNA, lentivirus containing shRNA targeting circCdh7, > 109 TU/mL, Supplemental Table 3) and HBLV-PURO negative control (shRNA NC, > 109 TU/mL, Supplemental Table 3) were synthesized by Hanbio (China). Agomir and antagomir specific for miR-346 and the NCs were synthesized by Ribobio (China, Supplemental Table 3) and diluted to 10 nmol/μL per the manufacturer’s recommended guidelines. pLV-EF1a > Osmr (pLV-Osmr, lentiviral vector-mediated Osmr overexpression, > 109 TU/mL) was constructed by GenePharma (China). The rats were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation (oxygen flowmeter turned to 0.8–1.5 L/min; isoflurane vaporizer turned to 3–5%) and then positioned in the prone posture with their heads secured in a stereotaxic device (RWD Life Science, China). The skull was exposed by a midline incision, with attention given to aligning the bregma and lambda points on a level horizontal plane. In accordance with the rat brain atlas [22], injections were administered bilaterally into the RVLM (Situated 3.7–4.0 mm distally to the lambdoid suture, 2 mm off the midline, and 8.0 mm beneath the dura surface) at a volume of 0.05 μL per side by using a glass micropipette. The injection site was marked by injecting 0.05 μL of 2% pontamine sky blue into the RVLM, as illustrated in Supplemental Fig. 1. After the injection, the surgical incision was closed by using sutures.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The pmiRGLO dual-luciferase miRNA target expression vector was modified through the addition of the wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) binding sites of miR-346 sourced from the circCdh7 sequence or Osmr 3ʹ UTR, resulting in the formation of circCdh7-WT/MUT or Osmr-WT-3ʹ UTR/MUT-3ʹ UTR constructs. The recombinant reporter plasmids and miR-346 agomir or agomir NCs were introduced simultaneously into primary astrocytes via cotransfection. Relative luciferase activity was assessed by using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Yeasen, China) after 48 h in accordance with the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

RNA pull-down assay

Primary astrocytes were subjected to transfection with LV5-EF-1a-Puro > circCdh7 (pLV-circCdh7, lentiviral vector-mediated circCdh7 overexpression, > 109 TU/mL, GenePharma, China). Then, primary astrocytes with circCdh7 overexpression were transfected with biotinylated miR-346 agomir or biotinylated agomir negative control (NC). Streptavidin magnetic beads (Thermo Scientific, the USA) were used to capture the biotin-coupled RNA complex. CircCdh7 levels in the bound fraction were determined via RT-qPCR.

Western blot

Protein samples were extracted with RIPA buffer containing 1% PMSF (Beyotime, China) as per the manufacturer’s instructions, separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel, and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with QuickBlock buffer (Beyotime, China) for 1 h under room-temperature conditions and blotted overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, ER1919-22, 1:2000, Huabio, China), mouse anti-interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β, HA601036, 1:1000, Huabio, China), mouse anti-Osmr (sc-271695, 1:100, Santa Cruz, the USA), and mouse anti-GAPDH (HRP-60004, 1:5000, Proteintech, the USA) primary antibodies. On the following day, the membranes were exposed to goat antirabbit IgG-HRP (7074, 1:3000, Cell Signaling, the USA) and horse antimouse IgG-HRP (7076, 1:3000, Cell Signaling, the USA) secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized through the application of a super ECL detection reagent (Yeasen, China), with GAPDH functioning as the internal control for the experiment.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, China) was employed to assess cell proliferation ability. By following the guidelines proposed by Lee et al. and Yang et al. [30, 31], LPS (1 μg/mL) was used to establish the astrogliosis and astrocyte inflammation model successfully in vitro. Meanwhile, the cells were subjected to transfection with circCdh7 shRNA, shRNA NC, miR-346 agomir, and agomir NC; cotransfection with circCdh7 shRNA and miR-346 antagomir; or cotransfection with miR-346 agomir and pLV-Osmr for 48 h. Subsequently, CCK-8 working solution was added to each well for coincubation for 1 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a LabServ K3 microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, the USA).

5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

The transfection protocol applied to LPS-treated primary astrocytes included the delivery of circCdh7 shRNA, shRNA NC, miR-346 agomir, and agomir NC; cotransfection with circCdh7 shRNA and miR-346 antagomir; or cotransfection with miR-346 agomir and pLV-Osmr for 48 h. After treatment, BeyoClick™ EdU cell proliferation kit (Beyotime, China) was employed for EdU labeling and staining in cells on the basis of the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were captured by utilizing a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

ELISA assay of TNF-α and IL-1β

The LPS-treated primary astrocytes were subjected to transfection with circCdh7 shRNA, shRNA NC, miR-346 agomir, and agomir NC; cotransfection with circCdh7 shRNA and miR-346 antagomir; or cotransfection with miR-346 agomir and pLV-Osmr for 48 h. Culture media were collected, and the quantities of TNF-α and IL-1β were assessed by using ELISA kit (Multiscience, China) by following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. The absorbance values at 450 nm were analyzed by utilizing a LabServ K3 microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, the USA).

Statistical analysis

The investigators were kept blinded during data collection to ensure neutrality. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism software (version 9.1), and the results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For small sample sizes (n < 6), nonparametric tests were employed, as normality tests lack sufficient power in such cases. For larger samples (n ≥ 6), the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. Data were analyzed using parametric tests if normally distributed; otherwise, nonparametric alternatives were applied. Two-group differences were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test for parametric data or Mann–Whitney U test for nonparametric data. For comparisons across more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction was applied to parametric data, while the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test was used for nonparametric data. The threshold for statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

CircCdh7 as a potential regulator in the RVLM of SHRs

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was first utilized to analyze and compare circRNA expression profiles in the RVLM of SHRs and WKY rats, resulting in a total of 690,950,420 raw reads–comprising 353,162,464 reads for SHRs and 337,787,956 reads for WKY rats. Following quality filtering, 608,887,188 clean reads remained, comprising 315,124,966 reads for SHRs and 293,762,222 reads for WKY rats. Analysis of 1,747 identified circRNA transcripts revealed 24 differentially expressed circRNAs (p < 0.01) between the two groups, as visualized in a heatmap displaying the top five differentially expressed circRNAs (Fig. 1A). RT-qPCR analysis corroborated the RNA-seq findings for four circRNAs: circCdh7, circAtrnl1, circDnajc1, and circRit2 (Fig. 1B and Supplemental Fig. 2). However, circKdm6a expression appeared unchanged by RT-qPCR, contradicting the RNA-seq data (Supplemental Fig. 2). This discrepancy reflected biological variations between samples. According to the circAtlas annotation [32], circCdh7 is derived from exons 3, 4, 5, and 6 of the Cdh7 gene and has a length of 771 bp. Sequence alignment between hsa_circ_0108807 (derived from human Cdh7 mRNA and 771 bp in length) and circCdh7 revealed that circCdh7 exhibits evolutionary conservation from rat to human (Supplemental Fig. 3). CircRNA Atrnl1, circRNA Dnajc1, and circRNA Rit2 are non-conserved across species. Therefore, further investigation focused on circCdh7’s role in hypertension progression. RT-qPCR demonstrated high circCdh7 expression specifically in the RVLM tissue compared with the other 10 tissues (Fig. 1C). Divergent primer design ensured exclusive amplification of circCdh7 from cDNA, with no amplification observed in genomic DNA (gDNA) (Fig. 1D). RNase R treatment validated circCdh7’s resistance to degradation compared to the susceptibility of linear Cdh7 mRNA (Fig. 1E). Sanger sequencing confirmed the head-to-tail splicing pattern characteristic of circCdh7 within the RT-PCR product (Fig. 1F). Downregulation of circCdh7 in RVLM attenuates hypertension development

To elucidate the functional role of circCdh7 in hypertension, a loss-of-function system was employed using bilateral injections of circCdh7 shRNA into the RVLM of 10-week-old SHRs. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed a significant reduction in circCdh7 expression within the RVLM following shRNA administration (Fig. 2A). Radiotelemetry recordings revealed a substantial decrease in MAP levels in SHRs with circCdh7 knockdown (Fig. 2B). Sympathetic activity was assessed through RSNA recordings and plasma NE ELISA, demonstrating significant reductions in both RSNA and plasma NE levels following circCdh7 downregulation (Fig. 2C and D). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a marked decrease in the percentage of c-Fos-positive neurons within the RVLM of SHRs with inhibited circCdh7 expression (Fig. 2E). CircCdh7 promotes astrogliosis and neuroinflammation in the RVLM to drive hypertension development

Previous studies established a link between astrogliosis, increased RVLM neuronal activity, heightened sympathetic outflow, and elevated BP [34, 35]. Furthermore, astrogliosis is implicated in promoting neuroinflammation [36, 37], and evidence suggests that RVLM neuroinflammation can activate neurons, escalate sympathetic tone, and contribute to hypertension progression [38, 39]. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying circCdh7’s role in neurogenic BP regulation, we investigated its potential influence on astrogliosis and neuroinflammation within the RVLM. Following microinjection of circCdh7 shRNA into the RVLM of SHRs, we assessed GFAP, a biomarker of neuroinflammation and astrocyte activation [40], using immunofluorescence. Knockdown of circCdh7 resulted in a significant reduction in the number of GFAP-immunoreactive (GFAP-IR) astrocytes (Fig. 3A). Additionally, suppression of circCdh7 in the RVLM led to a notable decrease in the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-1β (Fig. 3B). To further investigate this mechanism, we established an LPS (1 μg/mL)-induced astrogliosis and inflammation model in vitro. LPS-treated primary astrocytes were transfected with circCdh7 shRNA, which successfully reduced circCdh7 expression (Fig. 3C). Downregulation of circCdh7 significantly decreased the proliferation ability of LPS-treated primary astrocytes, as demonstrated by both CCK-8 and EdU assays (Fig. 3D and E). Immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked reduction in GFAP-IR astrocytes in the LPS plus circCdh7 shRNA group compared to the LPS group (Fig. 3F). Western blot analysis and ELISA further confirmed that downregulation of circCdh7 in LPS-treated primary astrocytes significantly decreased TNF-α and IL-1β levels (Fig. 3G and H). Discussion

CircRNAs are covalently closed RNA molecules that lack 5’ and 3’ ends and were initially identified in plant pathogenic viroids [47]. Later investigations progressively discovered the production and presence of circRNAs in humans, animals, and plants. The extensive prevalence of circRNAs has led to their thorough assessment, which revealed their aberrant behavior in pathological states [12–14, 48]. Nevertheless, only a few studies have tied circRNAs to the neurogenic etiology of hypertension. The RVLM is a distinct and crucial brain region with a vital role in regulating sympathetic tone and BP [5–7, 49]. In this study, a pioneering genomic repository of RVLM circRNAs in SHRs and WKY rats was meticulously constructed. The findings of this study elucidate the crucial roles of these circRNAs in hypertension development and suggest their potential as valuable therapeutic targets.

Generally, changes in expression can provide insight into the discrepancies in biological processes between states of well-being and pathology. The computational analysis of circRNAs in the RVLM revealed the presence of 24 dysregulated circRNAs between SHRs and WKY rats. Notably, circCdh7, which is highly expressed in the RVLM, exhibited increased expression in SHRs, a finding that was further validated by RT-qPCR. CircCdh7, a conserved exonic circRNA found in human and rat species, was derived from exons 3, 4, 5, and 6 of the cadherin 7 (Cdh7) gene and subsequently back-spliced to generate a circular form. The Latin phrase “in vivo” signifying “within the living” describes studies conducted on living organisms. In vivo studies provide precise target validation and are crucial for the development of innovative therapies. Knockdown of circCdh7 in the RVLM of SHRs using circCdh7 shRNA significantly reduced MAP, RSNA, plasma NE levels, and the percentage of c-Fos-positive RVLM neurons. As evident from the data, circCdh7 acts as a key element in increasing BP, sympathetic discharge, and RVLM neuronal excitability, thus contributing to the development of hypertension.

Research findings have shown that astrogliosis triggers neuronal activation in the RVLM, leading to elevated sympathetic tone and BP [34, 35]. The involvement of astrogliosis in promoting neuroinflammation is evident [36, 37]. A growing body of evidence suggests that RVLM neuroinflammation is a key factor in activating neurons and enhancing SNA, which is associated with the development of hypertension [38, 39]. Using in vivo and in vitro approaches, the present study revealed that circCdh7 enhances astrocyte proliferation and increases levels of the proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β, suggesting that circCdh7 promotes RVLM astrogliosis and neuroinflammation, thereby contributing to hypertension development. Previous studies have demonstrated that the functional roles of circRNAs are closely linked to their cellular localization [50]. Specifically, cytoplasmic circRNAs can regulate mRNA stability by functioning as miRNA decoys [33, 51]. In this study, nuclear and cytoplasmic RT-qPCR analysis showed that circCdh7 is primarily localized in the cytoplasm, suggesting that it may interact with miRNAs to influence RVLM astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, and hypertension progression.

miR-346, an evolutionarily conserved miRNA, is engaged in the regulation of various diseases. Uno et al. reported that miR-346 could serve as a potential biomarker to enhance tuberculosis activity [52]. By targeting Bax, miR-346 exerted an inhibitory effect on apoptosis in myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury [53]. Evidence indicates that miR-346 regulates pulmonary microvascular endothelial cell function, thereby controlling the development of acute respiratory distress syndrome [54]. Additionally, miR-346 has been linked to numerous malignancies [55–57]. Crucially, our earlier research demonstrated that elevated miR-346 levels in the RVLM markedly decreased neuronal excitability and sympathetic output, resulting in a significant improvement in hypertension [41]. Herein, we observed a significant decrease in miR-346 levels in the RVLM of SHRs compared to WKY rats. The application of RNA pull-down, dual-luciferase reporter, gain-of-function, and loss-of-function assays confirmed that circCdh7 sponged miR-346. Herein, we observed a significant decrease in miR-346 levels in the RVLM of SHRs compared to WKY rats. Furthermore, in vivo and in vitro experiments showed that miR-346 reversed the enhancement of astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, and BP induced by circCdh7. The results of these experiments offered proof that circCdh7 stimulated RVLM astrogliosis and neuroinflammation by sequestering miR-346, which contributed to the pressor response.

Fully developed miRNAs attach to argonaute to build the RNA-induced silencing complex, which participates in gene silencing processes [58]. Based on this mechanism, we further explored the downstream genes regulated by miR-346. The present study revealed that Osmr is upregulated in the RVLM of SHRs. The abundant expression of Osmr in astrocytes results in the upregulation of GFAP, triggering astrogliosis [45, 46]. Through the utilization of bioinformatics prediction, dual-luciferase reporter assay, as well as gain-of-function and loss-of-function experiments, miR-346 was found to target Osmr. Moreover, Osmr counteracted the suppressive effects of miR-346 on astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, and blood pressure, as observed in both in vivo and in vitro experiments. The data demonstrated that miR-346 suppressed RVLM astrogliosis and neuroinflammation through targeting Osmr, leading to a decrease in BP.

Additionally, in vivo and in vitro investigations were conducted to explore the relationship between circCdh7 and Osmr. Overexpression or knockdown of circCdh7 increased or decreased Osmr expression, respectively, and these effects were counteracted by miR-346 agomir or antagomir. These observations supported the notion that circCdh7 functioned as a sponge of miR-346, thereby modulating Osmr expression. CircCdh7 drove the development of hypertension by triggering RVLM astrogliosis and neuroinflammation, with the key molecular mechanism involving the upregulation of Osmr expression through the targeting of miR-346.

A limitation of this study is the absence of female rats; however, previous research has highlighted the impact of sex differences on BP regulation [59].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals a previously unrecognized circCdh7-dependent regulatory pathway that orchestrates hypertension pathogenesis through coordinated induction of RVLM astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, neuronal hyperexcitability, and subsequent sympathetic hyperactivity. Molecular characterization identifies the circCdh7/miR-346/Osmr axis as a mechanistic driver of this pathological cascade. These findings nominate circCdh7 as a molecular target for innovative antihypertensive therapeutic development.
