Genome-wide analysis of the Alba gene family members in soybean (Glycine max L.): characterization and stress-responsive expression patterns
Authors: Yongsheng Bai, Xin Huang, Xinxin Wu, Huanmei Liang, Meiting Liu, Shahid Ali, Yuling Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Alba (Acetylation lowers binding affinity) protein family consists of small, conserved nucleic acid-binding proteins involved in regulating gene expression and stress responses. Although studied in several plants, their roles in legumes remain poorly understood. Characterizing Alba genes in soybean (Glycine max L.), a crop highly affected by climate stress, is essential for improving its stress resilience.ResultsIn this study, a total of 15 Glycine max Alba (GmAlba) genes were identified, unevenly distributed across 14 chromosomes with varied exon-intron structures and conserved hydrophilic domains. Phylogenetic analysis grouped them into five clades: Groups 1–2 (Rpp25-like) and Groups 3–5 (Rpp20-like), with Group 4 only containing soybean-specific members, indicating functional divergence. Promoter analysis revealed multiple cis-elements related to phytohormone and stress responses. Transcriptomic data showed broad expression across tissues, with GmAlba5/6/10 highly expressed, especially in reproductive organs. RT-qPCR confirmed differential expression under salt, heat, cold, and ABA treatments, suggesting roles in stress adaptation. Structural modelling revealed protein diversity, while subcellular localization showed nuclear, cytoplasmic, and dual localization patterns, supporting DNA/RNA-related functions. Co-expression network analysis indicated interactions with diverse proteins, highlighting involvement in multiple biological processes.ConclusionsThis study provides the first comprehensive analysis of the GmAlba gene family in soybeans, detailing their phylogeny, structure, and spatiotemporal expression across tissues and stress conditions. These findings lay the groundwork for future research to enhance stress resilience in soybeans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07453-2.

Introduction

The Alba (Acetylation lowers binding affinity) protein family comprises a class of highly conserved, small DNA/RNA-binding proteins that were first discovered in archaea and are now known to be widely present in eukaryotes [1, 2]. These proteins share a conserved Alba domain with an IF3-C fold and typically function as dimers or heterodimers, playing crucial roles in regulating gene expression [1, 3–6]. Alba protein may function similarly to histone-like transcriptional regulators. It undergoes acetylation by protein acetyltransferase (PAT) and deacetylation by Sir2, a NAD⁺-dependent histone deacetylase (HDAC) [7–11]. In eukaryotes, Alba proteins are classified into two subfamilies: a smaller group with a single Alba domain (Rpp20-like) and a larger group that contains RGG/RG repeat motifs or additional domains (Rpp25-like or Mdp2-like) [1, 2, 12, 13]. RGG/RG motifs are known to mediate interactions between nucleic acids and proteins, and the proteins containing RGG/RG motifs are implicated in numerous physiological processes, including transcription, pre-mRNA splicing, DNA damage signaling, mRNA translation, and apoptosis regulation [2, 12].

Beyond the RGG/RG repeat motif, the Alba protein often contains additional domains that diversify their functions across species. For example, unique domains such as ATP synthase subunit H domain (Thileria), CLIP1 zinc finger domain (Pristionchus), FAD-binding domain and F-box domains (Taphrina), NT5C domain (Pythium), dynactin p22 subunit, and sugar transporter domains (Branchiostoma floridae) have been found in combination with the Alba domain [2]. In plants, Alba proteins bind to specialized domains such as the proteasome regulatory subunit, xylanase inhibitor N-terminal (Taxi_N), thioredoxin-4 (Trx-4), and DEAD/DEAH box helicase domains [1]. The presence of these additional domains in Alba has a great impact on the evolution and classification of its structure and function. These domain fusions have a significant influence on the structural and functional diversity of the Alba proteins.

Although Alba was initially thought to function primarily in maintaining chromatin structure and transcriptional repression, numerous studies have demonstrated its functional diversity, encompassing roles in genome packaging and organization, transcription and translation regulation, RNA metabolism, and developmental and differentiation processes [2, 14–17]. In archaea, Alba proteins have been identified as binding to and affecting DNA stability [5, 18]. In Trypanosoma brucei, four Alba proteins are RNA-binding proteins that localize to the cytoplasm as parts of stress granules (SG) upon exposure to nutrient stress [19]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, six AtAlba proteins, AtAlba1 to AtAlba6, were identified; among them, the AtAlba1 and AtAlba2 complex was proved to be crucial for the genome stability of the genic R-loop reader [13, 20].

Abiotic stressors such as heat, drought, and salinity disrupt plant growth, causing significant yield losses [21]. Among stress response regulators, the ALBA protein family plays conserved roles across various stress pathways. For example, AtAlba3 is highly expressed in the cytoplasm of both vegetative and sperm cells and plays a protective role in pollen during heat stress [22]. In Arabidopsis, ALBA4/5/6 form cytoplasmic aggregates via liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), which specifically bind and stabilize heat shock factor (HSF) mRNAs (such as HSFA2 and HSFB2A) under heat stress, thereby enhancing heat tolerance. Deletion of these proteins accelerates degradation of HSF mRNAs by XRN4 exonuclease, resulting in a heat-sensitive [23]. In rice, nine Alba proteins have been identified with tissue-specific expression patterns. OsAlba1 is implicated in adaptation to stress conditions [24, 25]. In cotton, silencing of GhAlba4 and GhAlba5 impairs root growth and increases sensitivity to drought and salinity [26].

Despite the growing understanding of the Alba proteins in several plant species [25, 27] and many gene families have been reported in soybean [28–30], there has been no comprehensive analysis of the Alba gene family in legumes. Soybean (Glycine max L.) is a globally important legume crop and a major source of plant protein and edible oil [31]. Increased climate variability and extreme environmental conditions have severely impacted soybean productivity. Therefore, understanding gene families that contribute to stress adaptation and development is critical.

In this study, we conducted a genome-wide analysis of the Alba genes in soybeans to examine their gene structures, chromosomal locations, and expression profiles in various tissues, as well as their response to different abiotic stresses. We also analyzed the protein phylogenetic relationships, conserved motifs, and co-expression networks. And analyzed the cellular localization of representative members from each phylogenetic Group. This work lays the groundwork for a deeper understanding of Alba genes and their roles in soybean development and stress tolerance.

Materials and methods

Identification and sequence analysis of Alba genes

To identify Alba gene family members, we retrieved protein sequences from soybean (Glycine max), Arabidopsis thaliana, rice (Oryza sativa), and maize (Zea Mays) using the Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) [32]. The keyword “Alba” was used in a search restricted to the following genome versions: G.max Wm82.a4.v1, A. thaliana TAIR10, O. sativa v7.0, and Z. mays PHB47 v1.2. To confirm the presence of the Alba domain, all retrieved sequences were analyzed using the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org/) [33] with default parameters. Sequences lacking the Alba domain were excluded from further analysis. Based on this approach, we identified 15 Alba gene family members in soybeans.

To characterize the physicochemical properties of the soybean Alba proteins (GmAlbas), we used the ExPASy ProtParam tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) [34]. This analysis provides information on protein length (number of amino acids), molecular weight (MW, Da), isoelectric points (pI), and the grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) index.

Phylogenetic analysis

To investigate the evolutionary relationships among Alba proteins from various plant species, including soybean, Arabidopsis, rice, and maize, all predicted full-length Alba protein sequences were analyzed. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbour-joining (NJ) method in MEGA 11 software [35], with 1000 bootstrap replicates to assess the robustness of the tree topology. The resulting phylogenetic tree was graphically edited by iTOL (Interactive Tree of Life, https://itol.embl.de/) [36]. The gene names and corresponding accession numbers used in the phylogenetic analysis are listed in Table S1.

Conserved motifs and gene structural analysis of GmAlba

The MEME suite 5.5.5 (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) [37] was used to search for the conserved motifs in GmAlba protein sequences. Default parameters were applied except for the number of motifs, which was set to 10. Gene structure visualization was performed by uploading the GmAlba gene sequences to Gene Structure Display Server 2.0 (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) [38].

Chromosomal locations

Soybean genomic data (assembly Gmax_508_Wm82.a4.v1) and Alba sequences were obtained from the Phytozome database. Alba genes were mapped to chromosomes using TBtools, and Photoshop was used to create the final visualization.

Prediction of cis‑acting elements and miRNA target sites in GmAlba

Cis-acting elements were analyzed by uploading the putative promoters (1,500 bp upstream of the initiation codon) of the GmAlba gene to PlantCARE (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [39] using default settings. The results were visualized with TBtools [40]. A heatmap depicting the distribution of various cis-acting elements across the GmAlba promoters was generated using Excel. Potential miRNA binding sites targeting Alba genes were predicted using PsRNATarget (https://www.zhaolab.org/psRNATarget/analysis) [41].

Molecular modelling of soybean Alba proteins

Soybean Alba protein sequences were submitted to SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/) [42] for molecular modelling. The recommended model was selected based on a GMQE (global model quality estimate) value close to 1, and the template was derived from the soybean. The final model (cartoon representation) and PDB file were saved. Template detail and GMQE values are recorded in Table S2.

Expression pattern analysis

To analyze the expression patterns of the Alba gene family members during soybean growth and development, transcriptomic data across various developmental stages and tissues were retrieved from the SoyOmics database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/soyomics/index) [43]. The data were to be log2-transformed and visualized as a heatmap using TBtools.

Protein-protein interaction network analysis

Intra- and interspecific collinearity analyses (soybean, Arabidopsis, rice) were performed using the One Step MCScanX-Super Fast plugin in TBtools, with Blast P validation (e-value ≤ 1e-10). Results were visualized using the Advanced Circos plugin and annotated using Adobe Photoshop.

For protein-protein interactions, soybean Alba protein sequences were submitted to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/; confidence score > 0.4). Interaction networks were constructed using identified partners.

Plant materials and stress treatments

Soybean seeds (Glycine max cv. Williams 82) were sown in nutrient-rich soil and grown in a greenhouse under long-day conditions (16 h day/8 h dark) at 22 ℃. Four-day-old seedlings were transferred to ½ MS liquid medium for hydroponic cultivation (10 days). Stress treatment was applied to the 14-day-old seedlings as follows. Salt stress: 200 mM NaCl in ½ MS liquid medium. Heat stress: incubation at 37 ℃. Cold stress: incubation at 4 ℃. ABA treatment: foliar spraying with 100 mM ABA. Samples (third true leaves and roots) were collected at 2-hour intervals post-treatment.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from ground plant tissue using the Trizol method. cDNA was synthesized with PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara). Quantitative PCR was performed using PerfectStartTM Green qPCR SuperMix (SYBR Green-based detection) with CYP2 as the reference gene. All the primer information is shown in Table S3.

Subcellular localization

For subcellular localization, full-length Alba cDNA sequences were synthesized and cloned into the pCAMBIA1300 vector (containing 35 S promoter, synthetic GFP coding region, and E9 terminator) (SYNBIO, Suzhou, China). The nuclear marker plasmid pCAMBIA2300-NLS-Aquamarine [44] was co-transformed with the Alba-GFP constructs into Agrobacterium GV3101 (WEIDI, Shanghai, China). Transformed Agrobacterium was infiltrated into tobacco leaves, incubated in darkness for 16 h, and then grown under normal conditions for 48 h. Fluorescence signal (green: GFP, excitation wavelength 488 nm; blue: Aquamarine, excitation wavelength 405 nm) and bright-field images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscopy (LSM880; Zeiss, Germany) with a 20x objective lens.

Statistical analysis

All data comprised at least three biological replicates. Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism 8.0 using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Significance thresholds were set as: * P ≤ 0.05, ** P ≤ 0.01, *** P ≤ 0.001.

Results

Identification and characterization of the GmAlba genes and their proteins

Fifteen soybean Alba genes were identified from Phytozome v.13 and validated using Pfam in soybean (Glycine max). These genes were designated GmAlba1 to GmAlba15 based on their chromosomal order. It was found that the length of the coding sequence (CDS) ranges from 297 bp to 966 bp (average: 525 bp). The shortest CDS is GmAlba12 (297 bp), and the longest CDS is GmAlba13 (966 bp). The CDSs’ length of GmAlba1, GmAlba8, GmAlba15 are 399 bp, GmAlba1, GmAlba8, GmAlba15 are 399 bp, GmAlba5 and GmAlba6 are 435 bp. Alba protein amino acid (aa) number from 98 to 321, with an average of 173. The predicted size of protein is from 34156.3 Da to 11232.9 Da, with an average of 19159.48 Da, and the range of isoelectric point (pI) from 5.09 to 10.33. Although the CDSs of GmAlba1, GmAlba8, and GmAlba15 are 399 bp in length, their predicted molecular weights and isoelectric point (pI) of their proteins are slightly different, and they are all basic proteins with pI greater than 7.00. All GmAlba proteins display a negative GRAVY (Grand Average of Hydropathy) score, confirming their hydrophilic nature (Table 1).

Chromosomal distribution and structure of GmAlba genes

Chromosomal localization analysis of soybean GmAlba genes was performed by TBtools. The 15 GmAlba genes were distributed on 14 of the 20 soybean chromosomes (Fig. 1); among them, two (GmAlba6 and GmAlba7) were on Chr8. Most GmAlba genes were located near chromosomal termini, except GmAlba4, GmAlba6, GmAlba8, and GmAlba13.

Gene structure analysis (Fig. 2) showed variation in the exon-intron organization: GmAlba3/4/10/13 contained 8, GmAlba7 had 7, GmAlba12 had 3, and others contained 5 exons. Intron length varied significantly, with GmAlba13 possessing the longest intron (~ 3 Kb). GmAlba1 had the 5’ UTR and 3’ UTR, and all other GmAlba genes only had the 5’ UTR at variable lengths. The differences in gene structures reflect evolutionary divergence and suggest functional diversity during soybean growth and development.

Phylogenetic analysis of GmAlba proteins

To analyze the evolutionary relationships among Alba protein family members in soybean, MEGA11 software was used to construct phylogenetic trees of Alba proteins across four plant species, including soybean and Arabidopsis from dicot plants and rice and maize from monocot plants. Based on the topological structure of the Alba phylogenetic tree, these Albas proteins were divided into five Groups, designated Group 1 to Group 5 (Fig. 3). Except for Group 4 and Group 5, the other three Groups contained Alba proteins from soybean, Arabidopsis, rice and maize, indicating that these soybean Alba proteins are evolutionarily conserved across species and may share similar functions. Group 4 exclusively contained a soybean Alba protein, suggesting that this GmAlba may have acquired lineage-specific functional divergence. In contrast, Group 5 lacked soybean GmAlba members and included rice Alba, two Arabidopsis Alba, and three maize Alba proteins. Additionally, three of the five Groups exhibited high bootstrap values, while the remaining two showed low bootstrap values.

Conserved motifs in GmAlba proteins

In general, conserved regions of protein sequences form motifs that determine structural and functional properties [45]. To explore conserved motifs in the 15 GmAlba proteins, sequences were analyzed using the MEME tool, and their phylogeny was reconstructed with MEGA11 (Fig. 4A-C). Ten conserved Motifs were identified, with Motif 2 present in all proteins. This indicates that Motif 2 is the core Motif of the Alba protein. Based on Motif composition and phylogenetic clustering, GmAlba proteins were divided into two subfamilies according to previously defined structural characteristics [1, 2, 12, 13]: the Rpp20-like subfamily (contain Motif 1: a conserved NRIQVS-hexapeptide) and the Rpp25-like subfamily (or Mdp2-like; contain Motif 3, a conserved NEIRIT-hexapeptide) (Fig. 4C-D). Rpp20-like subfamily comprises GmAlba1, GmAlba2, GmAlba5, GmAlba6, GmAlba8, GmAlba9, GmAlba11, GmAlba14, and GmAlba15 (containing conserverd motifs 1, 2). Rpp25-like subfamily comprises GmAlba3, GmAlba4, GmAlba7, GmAlba10, GmAlba12, and GmAlba13 (containing conserverd motifs 2, 3). GmAlba12 harbors only 2 Motifs, while GmAlba3 and GmAlba4 harbor Motifs. GmAlba proteins with shared Motifs clustered together phylogenetically (e.g., GmAlba5/GmAlba6/GmAlba7/GmAlba13, GmAlba3/GmAlba4), suggesting functional similarity. GmAlba12 and GmAlba10 clustered in the same branch despite GmAlba12 lacking two motifs present in GmAlba10, possibly reflecting functional divergences during evolution.

The results showed that the types, content, and distribution of cis-acting elements on the promoter of each GmAlba gene varied significantly (Fig. 5A-B). GmAlba8 contained the highest number of cis-acting elements (15), while GmAlba10 had the fewest (2). However, cis-elements associated with stress-responsive (ABARE, MYB, MYB-DRE, MYB-FBRE, MYB-LRE, StressRE, LTRE) are commonly present in the promoters of all GmAlba genes, suggesting conserved roles in stress adaptation. Notably, GmAlba8 contained the highest number of ABARE elements (8), while GmAlba3 and GmAlba4 completely lacked ABARE but instead possessed MYB-DRE and StressRE. All GmAlba genes except GmAlba3 contained 1–4 types of hormone-responsive cis-elements (including AuxinRE, GARE, MeJARE, SARE, and ZeinRE), indicating their potential involvement in hormonal regulation. Except for GmAlba3, GmAlba7, GmAlba10, and GmAlba15, which contained only 2–3 types of cis-acting elements, the other GmAlbas harbored diverse cis-acting elements, implying regulation by multiple transcription factors during soybean growth, development, and responses to environmental stress.

miRNA target prediction: Potential miRNA binding sites were predicted by submitting GmAlba CDS sequences to the psRNATarget database (default parameters). Several miRNAs (e.g., miR168, miR408, miR1513, miR4382), known to regulate plant growth and development, were identified as putative regulators of GmAlba expression (Table S4), indicating post-transcriptional control of GmAlba genes.

Expression pattern analysis of GmAlba genes based on transcriptomic data

Transcriptional data for GmAlba genes across 18 different tissues (including cotyledon, stem, leaf, root, pod, and others) were obtained from the SoyOmics database to investigate their expression patterns (Fig. 6). Among these, GmAlba5 and GmAlba6 showed relatively high expression across multiple tissues, while GmAlba1, GmAlba2, GmAlba8, and GmAlba15 exhibited lower expression levels. All GmAlba genes were expressed in flower buds (flo-1) and buds before flowering (flo-2) tissues, and GmAlba5, GmAlba6, and GmAlba10 displayed relatively high expression. Additionally, these three genes were also highly expressed in the leaf bud, at a different date (flo-5), and in the pod and seed.

Discussion

The Alba gene family encodes a group of evolutionarily conserved nucleic acid-binding proteins found across eukaryotes and prokaryotes [1, 2]. While the Alba genes have been previously characterized in species such as rice and tomato [25, 27], their roles in soybean, a key legume crop, remain unexplored. In this study, 15 GmAlba genes were identified and named based on their chromosomal locations (Table 1). Compared to 6 in Arabidopsis, 9 in rice, and 8 in tomato [20, 25, 27], soybean contains a relatively large number of Alba genes, suggesting an expanded and potentially diversified functional role in this species.

The coding sequence of the GmAlba gene ranges from 297 bp (GmAlba12) to 966 bp (GmAlba13), with GmAlba12 being the smallest and potentially representing a foundational form. All GmAlba proteins exhibited negative hydrophilicity values (Table 1), indicating their hydrophilic nature, consistent with roles in water-soluble processes such as RNA metabolism, transcription regulation, or signal transduction.

Chromosomal distribution analysis revealed that 15 GmAlba genes were unevenly distributed on 14 chromosomes, mostly near the terminal region (Fig. 1). This pattern, also seen in other plants like tomato [27], suggests gene expression through tandem or transposon-mediated duplication. Structural differences in exons, introns, and UTRs among genes indicate functional diversification (Fig. 2). Genes located away from the chromosome ends may be ancestral or core members, while terminally located genes linked to the ends evolve into specialized paralogs.

Phylogenetic analysis grouped Alba proteins from soybean and three plant species into five clades (Fig. 3). Notably, one clade (Group 4) contained only soybean Alba proteins, suggesting soybean-specific evolution linked to legume-specific physiological needs. Meanwhile, Group 5 lacked soybean members, while Groups 1–3 showed conserved clustering with Alba proteins from multiple species.

Conserved Motif analysis further divided GmAlba proteins into two subfamilies according to previously defined structural characteristics (Fig. 4) [1, 2, 12, 13]: the Rpp20-like subfamily (contain Motif 1: a conserved NRIQVS-hexapeptide) contained GmAlba1, GmAlba2, GmAlba5, etc.; the Rpp25-like subfamily (or Mdp2-like; contain Motif 3, a conserved NEIRIT-hexapeptide) contained GmAlba3, GmAlba4, etc. Motif 2, the amino acid sequence is highly similar to the IF3C-fold sequence, is conserved in all GmAlba proteins, which may be the core site of the Alba domain for DNA/RNA binding or involved in intricate regulatory roles such as RNA binding or translational control [19, 25, 46]. In addition, compared with the RPP20-like members, Rpp25-like GmAlba proteins exhibited longer sequences and more complex Motif patterns, which may be more likely to participate in more complex biological reactions, such as binding or translational regulation of specific RNAs [2, 47]. However, the exact function of Motifs remains to be experimentally validated.

Expression profiling revealed that GmAlba genes exhibit tissue-specific and stage-specific expression patterns during development. GmAlba5 showed the highest expression overall, particularly in new leaves, flowers, and seeds, while expression was lower in stems and mature leaves. Early developmental stages exhibited higher GmAlba expression, supporting their involvement in organ development and growth at an early stage (Fig. 6) [27]. GmAlba5 and GmAlba6, from the same phylogenetic Group, showed consistently high expression across organs, indicating roles in fundamental growth and development. Their stress-related promoter elements suggest that they link core function with environmental stress responses.

Cis-acting elements analysis revealed diverse regulatory elements in the promoter region of GmAlba genes, varying in type, number, and distribution. GmAlba8 contained the highest number of elements, while GmAlba10 had the fewest. Notably, all GmAlba genes (except GmAlba3 and GmAlba4) contained ABA-responsive elements (ABARE) (Fig. 5), suggesting their expression is most probably regulated by abscisic acid (ABA) when plants experience abiotic stress or during developmental processes such as seed dormancy. GmAlba8, with 8 ABAREs, appears especially responsive to stress. Additionally, elements related to auxin, gibberellin, salicylic acid, and jasmonic acid are relatively commonly present in GmAlba genes’ promoters, implying potential cross-talk between hormones in regulatory growth and stress responses [25].

The GmAlba gene exhibited differential expression across soybean tissues under various abiotic stresses (Fig. 7). GmAlba1 and GmAlba2 were significantly up-regulated in leaves under salt stress, indicating their potential involvement in salt tolerance. Some other Alba genes were also identified to respond to salt stress, such as tomato (SlAlba6) [27] and cotton GhAlba4, GhAlba5 [26]. Under heat treatment, GmAlba2 and GmAlba14 were highly expressed in leaves, while GmAlba1, GmAlba3, GmAlba8, GmAba14 and GmAlba15 were up-regulated in roots. This is consistent with the heat-induced expression of Alba genes reported in Arabidopsis, rice, and tomato [20, 25, 27]. Low-temperature stress induced the expression of GmAlba1, GmAlba2, GmAlba5, GmAlba8, GmAlba11, and GmAlba14 in leaves and GmAlba7 and GmAlba8 in roots, again supporting their role in cold stress response. The expression of Alba genes is also associated with ABA signaling, a key regulator of stress responses. Most tomato Alba genes are up-regulated by ABA, supporting the involvement of GmAlba genes in ABA-mediated stress response [48, 49]. Furthermore, several stress- and developmental-related miRNAs (e.g., miR168, miR408, miR1513, miR4382) were predicted to target GmAlba genes in post-transcriptional regulation under stress conditions and contribute to a better understanding of stress adaptation mechanisms in soybean [50] (Table S4).

The 3D models of GmAlba proteins revealed diverse structures with varying α-helices, β-strands, and coils (Fig. 8). Similar patterns in some proteins suggest related functions, while higher coil content in others may indicate flexible or regulatory roles. These structural differences likely support the functional diversity of GmAlba proteins in soybeans.

The functionality of proteins depends on their precise transport to specific subcellular compartments. In this study, the subcellular localization analysis of soybean Alba fusion protein revealed that GmAlba2 was detected in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, but predominantly localized in the cytoplasm. Similarly, GmAlba5, GmAlba9, and GmAlba12 showed mainly cytoplasmic localization with some nuclear presence. In contrast, GmAlba4 showed strong nuclear localization (Fig. 9). These findings are consistent with previous reports of dual localization for rice (OsAlba1) and tomato Alba proteins [24, 27], suggesting compartment-specific functions: nuclear localization may indicate roles in gene regulation, while cytoplasmic localization suggests involvement in post-transcriptional regulation or signaling pathways.

The analysis of co-expression networks and microsynteny relationships reveals the evolutionary dynamics and functional organization of the GmAlba gene family in soybeans. The microsynteny analysis (Fig. 10) reveals both conserved orthologous relationships (e.g., AtAlba5 with five GmAlba genes) and lineage-specific patterns, indicating that differential evolutionary pressures have acted on GmAlba gene copies following soybean's paleopolyploid history. Co-expression network analysis identifies GmAlba6 and GmAlba8 as central hubs interacting with RNA-binding proteins, translation factors, and stress-responsive components (Fig. 11), consistent with their broad expression patterns (Fig. 6) and stress responsiveness (Fig. 7). These highly connected nodes likely coordinate multiple cellular processes, while soybean-specific Group 4 members (e.g., GmAlba12) show unique evolutionary trajectories potentially related to legume-specific adaptations. The integration of microsynteny and co-expression data reveals how gene duplication and subsequent functional specialization have shaped the current GmAlba family organization, with conserved members maintaining essential functions and soybean-specific copies potentially acquiring novel roles. These findings provide a framework for understanding Alba protein functions in soybean development and stress responses while highlighting promising targets for future functional characterization and crop improvement strategies.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically analyzed the GmAlba gene family in soybeans. A total of 15 GmAlba genes were identified, and their sequence structures, phylogenetic relationships, cis-acting elements, and expression patterns across various tissues and under abiotic stress conditions were examined. Phylogenetic analysis classified the Alba family into five distinct groups. Groups 1 and 2 corresponded to the Rpp25-like (MDP2-like) subfamily, while Groups 3, 4, and 5 belonged to the Rpp20-like subfamily. Subcellular localization analysis indicated that these genes are expressed in both the cytoplasm and the nucleus. Expression profiling revealed that GmAlba5 and GmAlba6 were highly expressed during different stages of tissue development, suggesting key roles throughout tissue growth. The expression of several GmAlba genes was significantly induced under abiotic stress, implying their involvement in stress responses. Co-expression analysis further suggested that these genes may interact with multiple partners during soybean growth, development, and stress adaptation.

Future studies should focus on experimentally validating the functions of GmAlba genes through genetic and molecular approaches such as gene knockout, knockdown, and overexpression analyses. Elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms by which GmAlba proteins regulate stress responses and development will facilitate the identification of promising targets for breeding soybean varieties with enhanced climate resilience and yield. Additionally, investigating the interaction networks between GmAlba proteins and other regulatory factors will deepen insight into their roles within complex signaling pathways. This knowledge could enable precise genetic manipulation to improve soybean performance under diverse environmental conditions.
