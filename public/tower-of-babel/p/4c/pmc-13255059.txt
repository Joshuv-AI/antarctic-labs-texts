Ancestral roles of the classical complement pathway uncovered through functional analysis of factor D-depleted carp serum
Authors: Akhil Kizhakkumpat, Michiyo Kimura, Takahiro Nagasawa, Tomonori Somamoto, Miki Nakao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Functional analysis of Df-depleted serum reveals the dependence of teleost classical pathwas (CP) on the alternative pathway (AP) to complete entire complement cascade.•CP-derived C3b is insufficient for C5 activation without AP amplification.•Findings support an ancestral role of CP as an initiator of AP-driven complement activation.Keywords: Complement, The classical pathway, The alternative pathway, Hemolytic reaction, Carp, Cyprinus carpio

Introduction

The complement system plays a pivotal role in the elimination of invading pathogens as a major effector of innate immunity. Jawed vertebrates, including fish and tetrapods, possess a well-developed complement system equipped with three activation pathways: the classical pathway (CP), primarily triggered by immune complexes; the lectin pathway (LP), activated by microbial polysaccharides; and the alternative pathway (AP), initiated by certain activators such as bacterial lipopolysaccharides and β-1,3-glucans [1,2].

In the CP, C1—a Ca²⁺-dependent complex consisting of C1q, C1r, and C1s—proteolytically activates C4 and C2 to form the Mg²⁺-dependent protease complex C4bC2b, which functions as the C3 convertase of the CP [1]. Similarly, activation of the LP begins with recognition of microbial surfaces by mannose-binding lectin (MBL) in complex with MBL-associated serine proteases (MASPs), ultimately leading to the formation of the same C3 convertase, C4bC2b [3]. In contrast, the AP is initiated by activated forms of C3, such as C3(H₂O) and C3b, together with factor B (Bf) and factor D (Df), resulting in the formation of Mg²⁺-dependent enzyme complexes, C3(H₂O)Bb and C3bBb, which serve as the AP C3 convertases [1].

These C3 convertases cleave C3 into the active fragments C3a and C3b. C3a, a small (∼9 kDa) peptide, promotes inflammation by stimulating leukocytes and vascular endothelial cells, whereas C3b (∼180 kDa) covalently binds to target surfaces via its thioester site. Surface-bound C3b enhances phagocytosis (opsonization) and participates in the formation of C5-convertases—C4bC2bC3b in the CP/LP and C3b₂Bb in the AP [4,5]. These enzymes cleave C5 into C5a and C5b; the latter serves as a scaffold for assembly of the membrane attack complex (MAC; C5b-9) in the terminal pathway (TP) comprising of C5, C6, C7, C8 and C9, ultimately leading to disruption of the target cell membrane [6].

The evolutionary origin of the complement system can be traced to both protostome and deuterostome invertebrates, as evidenced by the identification of key AP and LP components such as C3, Bf, and MASP [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. These findings suggest that an opsonic system based on the AP and/or LP represents the ancestral architecture of the complement system.

Within the vertebrate lineage, jawless fish such as lampreys lack immunoglobulins and the CP, and appear to rely primarily on an opsonic complement system consisting of the AP and LP [15]. However, recent studies suggest a functional linkage between the lamprey complement system and variable lymphocyte receptors, the antigen receptors of agnathan adaptive immunity [16]. Advances in whole-genome sequencing of lower vertebrates have facilitated the identification of immune-related genes, including those encoding complement components. These analyses indicate that the CP and TP first emerged in cartilaginous and bony fish [17,2]. Accordingly, these pathways are considered relatively modern innovations that likely co-evolved with adaptive immunity.

In this context, the CP and TP in extant fish are expected to retain ancestral characteristics of their mammalian counterparts. Nevertheless, their functional properties and activation mechanisms remain poorly understood, particularly at the protein level, as most studies have been limited to analyses of primary structures and gene expression patterns. Functional investigations at the protein and cellular levels are therefore urgently needed. Progress in this area, however, has been hampered by the technical difficulty of purifying native complement components from fish and producing recombinant proteins that retain their native activities. Thus, functional characterization of complement pathways in bony fish is essential for understanding the evolution of the complement system. In particular, the reaction mechanisms and physiological significance of the CP in teleosts remain largely unresolved, with only a limited number of studies addressing these issues [[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]].

Complement factor D (Df), a serine protease essential for AP activation [23], is recognized as a rate-limiting component of the mammalian AP. In teleosts, the roles of Df have been characterized in complement activation and immune responses in several species [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], [30]].

In the present study, we investigated the functional maturation of the teleost CP using the common carp (Cyprinus carpio) as a model species. We examined complement-mediated hemolytic activity in normal carp serum following specific depletion of Df. Unexpectedly, depletion of Df abolished not only AP- but also CP-mediated hemolytic activity. Further analyses revealed reduced C3 deposition on target cells and an inability of the CP alone to generate functional C5 convertase complexes in the absence of Df. These findings indicate that the CP is functionally dependent on AP amplification in teleosts and highlight an essential role of the AP in the overall complement cascade. Our results provide new insights into the evolutionary significance of CP emergence alongside the AP.

Materials and methods

Materials

Reagents were obtained from the following sources: protein A-Sepharose, Cytiva Japan (Tokyo, Japan); sheep and rabbit blood in Alsever’s solution, Nippon Bio-Supply Center (Tokyo, Japan); hen egg lysozyme and Freund's complete adjuvant, 2,2′-azinobis (3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS), Wako Pure Chemical (Osaka, Japan); anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with peroxidase, Cappel. Carp serum was obtained from fish cultured in a commercial fish farm as described elsewhere [24]. The fish was anesthetized with 100 mg/L MS-222 in freshwater and bled from caudal vessels. The fish experiment procedures used in this study were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of Kyushu University, Japan.

Carp complement protein and antibodies

Carp Df and C3-H1 was purified to homogeneity from serum as described [24,31]. Anti-carp Df and anti-carp C3 were raised in rabbits and purified to IgG using protein A-Sepharose, following manufacturer’s instructions. Mono-specificities of the antibodies were confirmed by western blotting using whole carp serum proteins as antigens. Rabbit antiserum directed to carp C5 (anti-recombinant C5 NTR domain of carp C5-I isotype) was prepared as reported [32]. Carp antisera directed to sheep erythrocytes and that to rabbit erythrocytes were prepared as reported previously [33]. Anti-hen egg lysozyme IgM was raised in a carp (body weight of about 150 g) by injecting intraperitoneally four times with 0.1 mg of lysozyme emulsified in Freund's complete adjuvant at weekly intervals. The antiserum was fractionated by gel-filtration through a column of Superdex 200pg (1.6 × 60 cm) equilibrated with 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer containing 150 mM NaCl (pH 7.4). The first peak rich in IgM protein was collected and stored at -80°C.

Buffers for hemolytic assay

The following buffers were used in hemolytic assays: GVB2+, 5 mM veronal-buffered isotonic saline containing 0.15 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2 and 0.1% gelatin, pH 7.5 (This buffer allows any complement activation pathway.); Mg-EGTA-GVB, 5 mM veronal-buffered isotonic saline containing 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM EGTA and 0.1% gelatin, pH 7.5 (This allows only AP by blocking Ca2+-dependent CP and LP); EDTA-GVB, 5 mM veronal-buffered isotonic saline containing 10 mM EDTA, pH 7.5 (This blocks all the cascades.); DGVB2+, 2.5 mM veronal-buffered saline containing 2.5% dextrose, 0.15 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, and 0.1% gelatin, pH 7.5 (This is for preservation of EsA and ErA.).

Hemolytic assays

Sheep erythrocytes sensitized with carp antibody (EsA) and rabbit erythrocytes (Er) suspensions were prepared as described elsewhere [33]. Briefly, sheep erythrocyte (Es) suspension (1 × 109/mL in EDTA-GVB) was incubated with an equal volume of anti-Es carp serum diluted 1/40–1/100 (at dilution factor determined empirically to give a minimum concentration at which hemolytic activity reaches a plateau) at 25°C for 30 min, and resuspended in DGVB2+ at 2 × 108/mL. Rabbit erythrocytes (Er) were suspended in Mg-EGTA-GVB at 2 × 108/mL. Antibody-sensitized Er (ErA) was also prepared in a same manner as described for EsA.

The hemolytic activity through CP was assayed in a reaction mixture (300 µL) containing 2 × 107 cells of EsA or ErA, and serum diluted in GVB2+. After incubation at 25°C, optimum temperature for carp CP [34], for 60 min, the reaction was halted by addition of 1.2 ml of 0.9% NaCl containing 10 mM EDTA (pH 7.5), followed by centrifugation at 1600 x g for 5 min. The degree of hemolysis was determined by measuring the A414 of the supernatant. The hemolytic activity via AP was assayed in the same manner except that Mg-EGTA-GVB was used in place of GVB2+ to block the Ca2+-dependent CP and LP and that the AP reaction mixtures were incubated at 20°C, because carp AP activity has been shown to be significantly lower at 25°C [35,36].

Immunochemical depletion of Df from carp serum

An immobilized anti-carp Df column was prepared by passing 1 mL of anti-carp Df rabbit IgG (2 mg/mL) through a protein A-Sepharose column (0.7 × 1 cm). After thorough washing of the column with GVB2+, 1 ml of carp serum was applied to the column and eluted with GVB2+ at 4°C. The pass-through fraction (3 ml) was collected and stored on ice for at longest 3 h until use.

Indirexct competitive ELISA for quantitative measurement of C3-deposition

Levels of C3 deposition on complement-activating targets were quantified by an indirect competitive ELISA, schematically illustrated in Fig. S1a. Wells of a 96-well ELISA plate were coated with 5 µg/mL hen egg lysozyme (HEL) in 100 mM sodium carbonate buffer (pH 9.5) and blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.1% gelatin (blocking solution). Anti-HEL carp IgM (50 µg/mL, 100 µL/well) was added and incubated at 25°C for 30 min to form immune complexes on the plate surface. After washing with 0.05% Tween 20-PBS (TPBS), 100 µL of serially diluted normal carp serum or Df-depleted carp serum in GVB2+ (1/50–1/200) was added and incubated at 25°C for 30 min to allow complement activation and C3 deposition, followed by washing with TPBS.

For preparation of a standard curve, purified carp C3-H1 [31] at known concentrations (2–2000 ng/mL) was added to separate wells in parallel with the serum-treated wells. Rabbit anti-carp C3 IgG (1 µg/mL, 100 µL) was then added to all wells and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. In the serum-treated wells, the antibody reacted with deposited C3b, whereas in the standard wells it reacted with fluid phase C3-H1.

The reaction mixtures were subsequently transferred to another ELISA plate pre-coated with purified carp C3-H1 (5 µg/mL) and blocked as described above. After incubation at room temperature for 1 h, the plate was washed with TPBS and incubated with peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1/2000 dilution), followed by color development using ABTS substrate and measurement of A405.

Because anti-carp C3 IgG was consumed by binding to deposited C3b or soluble C3-H1 during the first reaction step, the residual free antibody measured in the second ELISA plate was inversely proportional to the amount of C3 bound in the first step. C3-deposition levels in serum-treated wells were calculated from a standard curve generated using purified carp C3-H1. The assay was performed in triplicate.

Flow cytometric analyses of C3-and C5-deposition

Sheep erythrocytes (Es) were fixed with 0.02% glutaraldehyde in PBS for room temperature for 30 min, blocked with 1% gelatin-PBS, and resuspended in GVB2+ at a concentration of 1 × 108/mL. The fixed Es (Esf) were sensitized with anti-Es carp antibody (1/80 diluted in GVB2+) to generate EsfA, and stored at 4°C until use.

EsfA (1 × 108/mL) were incubated with Df-depleted carp serum (1/20 diluted in GVB2+), normal carp serum (1/20 diluted in GVB2+), or normal carp serum (1/20 diluted in Mg-EGTA-GVB) at 25°C for 30 min and washed with PBS. The suspensions were incubated with 1/1000 diluted anti-carp C3 or anti-carp rC5-NTR polyclonal antibody, and then FITC-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (goat), followed by flow-cytometry on Attune NxT Acoustic Focusing Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analyses

Data from hemolytic assay and ELISA were subjected to One-way ANOVA and Tukey-Kramer test for multiple comparison, using R-software packages, at a significance level of P<0.05.

Results

Hemolytic activity of D-depleted carp serum

The effect of Df-depletion on the hemolytic activities of carp serum through the classical and alternative pathways were assayed, using EsA and Er as target cells, respectively. Unexpectedly, Df-depleted serum lysed neither EsA nor Er (Fig. 1). The lost hemolytic activities against both target cells were fully recovered by supplementing the Df-depleted serum with purified carp Df at a concentration equivalent to the previously estimated normal level (∼6 µg/ml). These results suggest that, in contrast to mammalian complement, the hemolytic reactions initiated by both pathways in carp contain an activation step that involves Df. We thus hypothesized that the factor D-depleted serum reacted with EsA to yield an intermediate hemolytic complex, tentatively designated EsAC(–D).

Loss of the hemolytic activities by depletion of Df from serum. Carp serum was depleted of Df using anti-Df column and supplemented with purified Df, equivalent to 1 to 20 µg/mL-original serum. The Df-free and reconstructed sera were examined for the hemolytic activity against EsA in GVB2+ (representing the classical pathway activity, shown by open squares) and Er in Mg-EGTA-GVB (representing the alternative pathway activity, shown by open circle). Solid plots represent activity in the presence of normal serum concentration (6 µg/mL) of Df.

C3-deposition on EA treated with factor D-depleted carp serum

In the previous study, we have observed that Df-depletion diminished deposition of two major carp C3 isotypes, C3-H1 and C3-S, by activation through CP and LP, by an ELISA using plates coated with lysozyme-anti-lysozyme immune complex and yeast mannan (as shown in Fig. 5 of our previous report [37]). In the present study, we further evaluate effect of Df-depletion on degree of C3-deposition on complement-activating target surface through CP in two distinct approaches, a quantitative ELISA and flow cytometry, using polyclonal anti-carp C3, which recognizes both C3-H1 and C3-S equally [31].

Fig. S1b shows the results of the quantitative ELISA, where normal carp serum diluted in EDTA-GVB and Mg-EGTA-GVB gave no detectable C3-deposition on the well surface coated with lysozyme-anti-lysozyme immune complex, confirming that the immune complex activates only CP, which is Ca2+-dependent. Whereas normal carp serum in GVB2+ yielded ∼8 ng/well of C3-deposition, Df-depleted serum gave C3-deposition at only 1/10 (∼0.8 ng/well) of that caused from the normal serum. Interestingly, the immune complex-coated wells that were pretreated by Df-depleted serum in GVB2+ led to C3-deposition level comparable to that was given by the normal serum in GVB2+, after subsequent incubation with normal serum in Mg-EGTA-GVB, which allowed AP activation. This result suggests that once small amounts of C3 are deposited by CP, the C3b-tagged target surface can efficiently activate AP.

In the flow cytometry (Fig. 2a), Df-depleted serum in GVB2+ yielded much attenuated C3-deposition level compared with normal serum in GVB2+, even lower than the level caused by normal serum in Mg-EGTA-GVB, which allowed a low level activation of AP on EsfA.

Flow-cytometric analysis of depositions of C3b (a) and C5b (b) on antibody-sensitized sheep erythrocytes fixed with glutaraldehyde (EfA). EfA were incubated with normal carp sera in the presence/absence of EDTA and EGTA or Df-depleted serum (–Df serum), then deposition of C3b and C3b were detected using polyclonal anti-carp C3 and anti-rC5-NTR domain, respectively.

Loss of C5-deposition by Df-depletion from serum

C5-deposition on EsfA target cell was assayed by flow cytometry employing anti-carp rC5-I-NTR. As shown in Fig. 3b, Df-depleted carp serum totally lost C5-deposition on the target cell, suggesting that C5 convertase was not formed in the absence of Df.

Schematic diagram representing the mechanism of the classical pathway-initiated hemolytic reaction by carp complement. Activation of the classical pathway, consisting of C1, C4, and C2, may lead to low level C3-deposition on the target cells (Ab-sensitized erythrocytes, EA). The EA baring low level C3b are unable to form C5 convertase to lead terminal pathway activation but can trigger efficiently the amplification loop of alternative pathway activation to form C5-convertases and complete hemolysis.

Hemolytic characteristics of EA treated with D-depleted serum

To assess the role of the smaller amount of C3 (probably as a C3b form) bound on the EsA and EsA treated with Df-depleted serum, the hemolytic intermediates, EsAC(–D) and ErAC(–D), were examined for their ability to proceed activation of AP and TP by hemolytic assay. As shown in Table 1, both EsAC(-D) and ErAC(-D) were strongly lysed by normal serum in Mg-EGTA-GVB, showing comparable levels of hemolysis caused by normal serum in GVB2+. On the other hand, neither EsAC(-D) nor ErAC(-D) was lysed by normal serum in EDTA-GVB. The results indicate that C3b deposited at low level by Df-depleted serum can efficiently activate AP, leading to further activation of TP to achieve hemolysis.

Discussion

The present results indicate that the teleost CP alone can activate C3 and deposit a small amount of C3b on target surfaces, but is unable to form a functional C5 convertase and thus cannot drive the terminal pathway (TP) independently, as summarized in Fig. 3. Under physiological conditions, in which Df is functional, the deposited C3b would efficiently triggers the AP, leading to C5 convertasee formation and subsequent TP activation. The AP is widely recognized as an amplification loop that enhances C3 activation by generating additional C3b, which in turn forms more C3 convertase complexes with Bf and Df. Therefore, the teleost complement system appears to require the AP for completion of the entire complement cascade.

This feature contrasts with the CP of mammals, such as humans and guinea pigs, in which a functionally active C5 convertase complex, C4bC2bC3b, can be formed [38]. Indeed, CP-mediated hemolytic activity remains at near-normal levels in Df-deficient human patients, despite markedly reduced AP activity [23]. However, impaired C5 convertase formation via the CP has been reported in mice, where C4 lacks C5 convertase subunit activity due to an amino acid substitution at the C5-interacting site [39]. In addition, a human C4 allotype, C4A6, exhibits reduced affinity for C5 within the C4bC2bC3b complex [40]. It remains to be determined whether the defective C5 convertase activity observed in carp CP is attributable to properties of C4, as in mice. Interestingly, the human LP has also been reported to be incapable of forming a C5 convertase independently [41].

Sunyer et al. [42] reported that both AP- and CP-mediated hemolytic activities were abolished in factor B-depleted rainbow trout serum, and that supplementation with a purified Bf isoform (Bf2) restored both activities. They interpreted these findings as evidence that Bf participates in both pathways. Our present results provide an alternative explanation. Namely, as suggested for carp, the trout CP alone may be incapable of generating a functional C5 convertase without amplification of C3 activation through the AP. Therefore, the apparent involvement of Bf in both pathways observed in rainbow trout complement likely reflects the indispensable contribution of the AP amplification loop to CP-mediated hemolysis in teleosts, consistent with the mechanism inferred from Df-deficient carp serum in the present study. However, to verify this hypothesis, it will be necessary to confirm in rainbow trout or carp that Df deficiency and Bf deficiency produce the same phenotype, namely defective formation of the C5 convertase.

Because bony fish are among the most primitive vertebrate groups possessing the CP, the present findings would provide insight into the evolutionary origin of this pathway. The CP may have emerged in parallel with immunoglobulins as a mechanism linking antigen recognition to the pre-existing AP. The ancestral form of the CP likely lacked the ability to directly activate C5 and instead functioned primarily to initiate amplification of C3 activation through the AP. The carp CP characterized in this study appears to retain such ancestral features. At the same time, our results emphasize the critical importance of the AP as a central effector mechanism of innate immunity in early vertebrates.

The functional sophistication of the complement system associated with the emergence of the CP in jawed vertebrates remains an important subject for future investigation for better understanding of evolution of the complement system. In this context, elucidation of the protein-level mechanisms underlying CP- and AP-mediated activation in cartilaginous fish, another primitive vertebrate lineage alongside bony fish, will be an important direction for future research.
