Extranodal Marginal Zone Lymphoma of the Breast in a Patient with Chronic Hepatitis C Infection: A Case Report
Authors: Ravneet K Dhanoa, Bipasha Goyal, Prateek Jain, Marilyn Baird–Howell, Mounika Gangireddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary breast lymphomas account for less than 1% of all non-Hodgkin lymphomas and 0.04–0.5% of all malignant breast neoplasms. We report a case of breast extranodal marginal zone lymphoma (EMZL) in a 60-year-old woman with chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection. After comprehensive evaluation excluded other known etiologies, chronic HCV infection emerged as the most plausible contributing factor. Epidemiologic and biological evidence supports an association between HCV and B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas, particularly marginal zone lymphomas. Our case adds to the limited literature suggesting that HCV should be considered as a potential etiological factor in breast EMZL. We discuss the emerging role of direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy in HCV-associated indolent lymphomas and its implications for management.Keywords: Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma, Hepatitis C virus, Primary breast lymphoma Di, Direct acting anti-virals

Introduction

Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma (EMZL) is a low-grade B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) that most commonly affects the gastrointestinal mucosa but can involve any mucosal tissue, including the breast.1 Marginal zone lymphomas (MZLs) are the third most common type of B-cell NHL, with increasing incidence in the United States.1

Established risk factors for MZL include family history of NHL, genetic loci in the HLA region, and Helicobacter pylori infection (associated with gastric MALT lymphoma).1 Autoimmune diseases such as Sjögren syndrome, systemic lupus erythematosus, and Hashimoto's thyroiditis are also risk factors. Strong evidence links Chlamydia psittaci (ocular adnexal MALT lymphoma), Borrelia burgdorferi (cutaneous MZL), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) to MZL.1

Primary breast lymphoma (PBL) accounts for approximately 0.4–0.5% of all breast malignancies and less than 1% of all NHLs. 1,3 EMZL represents about 3% of all extranodal marginal zone lymphomas at this site.1 The etiology of PBL is not well understood, and no infectious trigger has been definitively established for breast EMZL.4

Chronic HCV infection is associated with a two-to four-fold increase in the risk of developing B-cell NHL, with HCV-associated lymphomas being more likely to be marginal zone lymphomas and diffuse large B-cell lymphomas, frequently found at extranodal sites.2,5 HCV-associated B-cell NHL usually occurs after long-term infection, with a median of approximately 25 years.5 EMZL, mainly the nongastric type, is among the B-cell NHLs most frequently linked with HCV.1,3

Very few case reports have described HCV as a potential contributing factor for breast EMZL. We report this case to raise awareness that HCV may represent an underrecognized etiological factor for EMZL in breast tissue. Testing for HCV antibodies and RNA levels is recommended as part of the standard workup for EMZL per current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines. 1 We discuss the implications of direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapy, which has shown efficacy in inducing regression of HCV-associated indolent lymphomas and is now recommended as first-line therapy in this setting.1,6,7

Case presentation

An asymptomatic 60-year-old hypertensive female with a smoking history of 92 pack-years was incidentally found to have a right lower lobe lung nodule on routine low-dose computed tomography screening. Subsequent PET/CT revealed a nodular density in the upper-inner quadrant of the left breast with mild to moderate FDG uptake. The patient denied B symptoms and had no history of oral contraceptive pills or hormone replacement therapy use.

Physical examination was unremarkable for any palpable breast lump, skin changes, nipple discharge, or lymphadenopathy. Family history was negative for ovarian, colon, prostate, pancreatic, or gastric cancer. She underwent an excisional biopsy that confirmed the diagnosis of EMZL with negative margins. Histopathologic examination demonstrated a dense lymphoid infiltrate effacing the normal breast parenchyma (Fig. 1). Higher magnification revealed small to medium-sized lymphocytes with monocytoid cytoplasm surrounding and infiltrating residual breast ductal epithelium, forming characteristic lymphoepithelial lesions (Fig. 2). The neoplastic cells were positive for CD20 (Fig. 3), PAX-5, and BCL-2 rearrangement, consistent with the typical immunophenotype of EMZL.1

The patient contracted HCV 18 years prior to her EMZL diagnosis after receiving a platelet transfusion for chronic thrombocytopenia. She received interferon-based therapy, which was the standard of care at the time. As per documentation, she had post-treatment HCV RNA testing that confirmed persistent viremia, indicating treatment failure. She did not receive subsequent antiviral therapy due to insurance-related barriers and was therefore never treated with modern direct-acting antiviral agents. HCV RNA was not retested at the time of EMZL diagnosis.

She was determined to have stage IE EMZL given the absence of distant metastatic disease. Per NCCN guidelines, she was placed on active surveillance with clinical examinations every 6 months for 5 years. However, after one surveillance visit, she was lost to follow-up.

Discussion

Chronic HCV infection is associated with several malignancies beyond hepatocellular carcinoma.9,10 A large SEER-Medicare registry-based study by Mahale et al. confirmed that HCV was positively associated with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, intrahepatic bile duct cancer, and myelodysplastic syndrome, among others.11 The NCI-SEER Multi-Center Case-Control Study demonstrated a positive association between HCV and NHL (OR 1.96; 95% CI 1.07–4.03), with particularly elevated risk for marginal zone lymphoma (OR 3.99).12 A large NIH-funded US Veterans Affairs cohort study (n = 718,687) found that HCV infection confers a 20–30% increased risk of NHL overall (adjusted HR 1.28; 95% CI 1.12–1.45) and a 3-fold higher risk of Waldenström macroglobulinemia.13 The NIH/CDC Chronic Hepatitis Cohort Study similarly demonstrated significantly increased NHL incidence among HCV-infected patients (SRR 1.6; 95% CI 1.2–2.1).14 A pooled analysis from the International Lymphoma Epidemiology Consortium (InterLymph) of 4784 NHL cases and 6269 controls confirmed HCV association with marginal zone lymphoma and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.15 A comprehensive metaanalysis of epidemiologic studies reported a pooled relative risk of 2.5 (95% CI 2.1–3.0) for NHL among HCV-positive individuals.16

EMZL invariably develops from chronic inflammatory disorders caused by microbial infection and/or autoimmunity.1 Recognized infectious etiologies include H. pylori (gastric), C. psittaci (ocular adnexal), B. burgdorferi (cutaneous), C. jejuni (intestinal), and HCV (nodal, splenic, and various extranodal sites).1,5 Autoimmune conditions associated with EMZL include Sjögren syndrome and Hashimoto's thyroiditis.1

In our patient, autoimmune diseases were systematically excluded: ANA was negative, complement C4 levels were normal, rheumatoid factor was negative, and there were no symptoms of sicca syndrome. TSH was normal (2.5 μIU/mL), excluding Hashimoto's thyroiditis. HIV and EBV were both negative. Our patient likely has chronic hepatitis C infection of approximately 18 years' duration. A repeat positive HCV titer obtained 5–6 years after initial treatment suggests prior treatment failure and supports the likelihood of persistent infection. In the absence of alternative etiologies for EMZL, there is reasonable suspicion that HCV infection may have contributed to the development of EMZL in the breast.

The biological plausibility of the association between HCV and EMZL is supported by substantial evidence. HCV can infect mononuclear lymphocytes, including cells that express CD81 receptors, which play a crucial role early in the HCV lifecycle.5,17 Chronic antigenic stimulation of B lymphocytes by HCV has a central role in lymphomagenesis. 3 This chronic stimulation can result in B-cell lymphoproliferative disorder, initially manifesting as mixed cryoglobulinemia and potentially evolving into overt NHL.5,18 The pathogenesis parallels that seen in the development of gastric MALT lymphoma due to H. pylori infection, where chronic antigenic stimulation drives clonal B-cell expansion.1,2 HCV-associated B-cell NHL usually occurs after long-term infection, with a median of approximately 25 years—consistent with our patient's 18-year history of chronic HCV infection. 4 The strongest evidence supporting a causal relationship between HCV and lymphoma comes from the observation that HCV-associated lymphomas can regress following successful antiviral therapy.5 Hosry, Torres et al. from MD Anderson Cancer Center demonstrated that antiviral therapy significantly improved 5-year overall survival in HCV-infected patients with DLBCL, further supporting a potential association between HCV and B-cell lymphomagenesis.18,21

The prospective BArT study demonstrated that DAAs achieved a 100% sustained virologic response in 40 patients with HCV-associated indolent lymphomas, with an overall lymphoma response rate of 45%.19 Long-term follow-up of this cohort confirmed durable responses, with no relapses among patients achieving a complete response.20 Current NCCN guidelines recommend that DAA therapy should be considered in asymptomatic patients with HCV-positive low-grade B-cell NHL.8 The updated ECIL-9 recommendations similarly endorse DAAs as first-line treatment for HCV-associated marginal zone lymphoma.6 Torres et al. from MD Anderson Cancer Center further demonstrated that sofosbuvir-based DAA therapy in HCV-infected cancer patients was effective and safe, with two of six patients with indolent NHL achieving complete remission with antiviral therapy alone.21

In our patient, excisional biopsy achieved complete resection with negative margins, which is an appropriate management strategy for stage IE nongastric EMZL per NCCN guidelines.1 However, the patient was subsequently lost to follow-up, and further evaluation, including HCV RNA testing and consideration of direct-acting antiviral therapy, could not be completed.

Conclusion

For HCV-positive patients with indolent lymphomas, DAA therapy represents a paradigm shift in management. The evidence supporting DAAs as first-line therapy in HCV-associated indolent lymphomas is robust, with multiple studies demonstrating that viral eradication can lead to lymphoma regression.5,19,20 Future cases of breast EMZL in HCV-positive patients should incorporate DAA therapy into the treatment algorithm, as recommended by current guidelines.6 More cases of breast EMZL occurring in the setting of chronic HCV infection should be identified and reported to better characterize this association and optimize management strategies.
