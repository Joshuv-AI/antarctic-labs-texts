Quantifying DNA strand breaks from targeted alpha emitters 225Ac and 227Th via Geant4-DNA: implications for RBE and cell survival
Authors: Samaneh Zolghadri, Payman Rafiepour, Hassan Yousefnia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTargeted alpha therapy (TAT) utilizing high-LET alpha particles from radionuclides like 225Ac and 227Th shows promise in cancer treatment due to their ability to induce dense, localized DNA damage. This study uses the Geant4-DNA Monte Carlo toolkit to simulate DNA damage and evaluate the radiobiological effects of 225Ac and 227Th.ResultsThe simulations revealed that both 225Ac and 227Th induce significant DNA damage, particularly complex double-strand breaks (DSBs), with 227Th producing slightly more clustered damage due to its longer decay chain and higher alpha energy. Background

Targeted α-therapy (TAT) is emerging as one of the most promising targeted cancer treatments due to the high Linear Energy Transfer (LET) and short range of alpha particles [1, 2]. While the limited range results in selective treatment of cancer cells and reduced side effects compared to the other modalities, the high linear energy transfer leads to effective cell killing via DNA double-strand breaks [1, 3]. This makes them far superior to beta particles, which mainly result in single-strand DNA breaks and have a longer tissue path length, leading to increased toxicity [4]. The clinical relevance of this approach is especially evident in its potential to overcome challenges faced by traditional therapies, such as radiation resistance and treatment-related toxicity.

DNA damage including single-strand breaks (SSB), double-strand breaks (DSB), and base modifications is a critical concern in the study of radiobiology. The research on how alpha particles impact DNA structure and function has significant implications for advancing radiation protection strategies and therapeutic methods in cancer treatment, particularly in the use of alpha particles in targeted radionuclide therapy.

Calculating the extent of DNA breakage caused by alpha particles involves understanding the physical and biological processes that take place as these particles travel through the cells. Measuring this damage necessitates an understanding of factors like the linear energy transfer (LET) of alpha particles, the interaction cross-sections with DNA molecules, and the impact of the cellular environment on repair mechanisms.

Today, different Monte Carlo (MC) codes including MCNP [5], FLUKA [6], GATE [7], GEANT4 [8], are used to study the DNA breakage. Geant4 is a versatile simulation toolkit with C + + class libraries. An advanced application of Geant4 is in the field of radiobiology, where it is utilized to simulate the interactions between radiation and biological tissues. Geant4-DNA is an extension of the Geant4 toolkit, tailored to simulate the interactions of radiation with biological systems at the DNA level. It models the physical, chemical, and biological phases of radiation interaction.

Among the various radionuclides used for TAT, 225Ac and 227Th have emerged as highly promising radionuclides. 225Ac offers distinct advantages due to its effective delivery of multiple high-energy alpha particles (with an energy range of 5.8–8.4 MeV) which can lead to enhanced therapeutic efficacy, an optimal half-life for therapeutic applications (approximately 10 days), and increasing availability. The 225Ac decay chain is illustrated in Fig. 1. Besides, 225Ac can be attached to various targeting molecules that permits its application in treating a wide range of cancers. Recently, different radiolabeled compounds of 225Ac including 225Ac-PSAM-617 and 225Ac-DOTATATE have been introduced, demonstrating significant effectiveness in clinical trials [9–11].

Thorium-227 is an alpha-emitting radionuclide gaining attention for TAT due to its favorable 18.7day half-life, which allows effective tumor targeting with limited long-term radiation. It decays by emitting high-energy alpha particles (with an energy range of 5.7–7.5 MeV) that cause severe DNA damage, particularly in cancer cells. The 227Th decay chain is illustrated in Fig. 1227Th can be stably attached to monoclonal antibodies using specialized chelators, enabling selective delivery to tumors while minimizing healthy tissue exposure. Its longer half-life makes it suitable for treating solid tumors that require prolonged circulation. Several 227Th-labeled therapies, like BAY 2287411 and BAY 2701439 are showing promise in early clinical trials.

To improve the clinical application of 225Ac and 227Th, understanding the underlying mechanisms of DNA damage and repair induced by these radionuclides is critical. This study employs the Geant4-DNA Monte Carlo toolkit to simulate DNA damage induced by 225Ac and 227Th, focusing on the characterization of double-strand breaks (DSBs) and their repair kinetics. These insights are essential for optimizing TAT protocols, as they provide a clearer understanding of how different types of DNA damage, especially complex DSBs, affect tumor cell viability and survival.

Given that 225Ac and 227Th induce high RBE (Relative Biological Effectiveness) and complex DNA damage, their potential to enhance therapeutic outcomes in clinical settings is substantial. By providing data on the RBE and cell survival rates, this research directly supports the development of more precise and effective clinical protocols for TAT. Furthermore, understanding how these radionuclides interact with tumor DNA could help tailor treatments based on tumor-specific characteristics, ultimately improving the efficacy and safety of 225Ac and 227Th-based therapies in clinical oncology.

Methods

To simulate radiation-induced DNA damage, we utilized the Geant4-DNA code [12] (version 11.1 patch 3), an extension of the Geant4 MC toolkit [13] which provides a detailed, track-structure approach. This allowed us to simulate the physical, chemical, and biological stages of radiation damage to DNA, enabling a comprehensive understanding of the effects of ionizing radiation at the molecular level. Recently, A new tool called “dsbandrepair” has been developed for simulating DNA damage and repair using Geant4-DNA. This tool uses DNA geometries generated by the DNAFabric computational tool to simulate primary single- and double-strand breaks [14]. Geant-based Monte Carlo techniques and modeling with TOPAS and TOPAS-nBio were also used to simulate absorbed doses and DNA damage within a simplified cell model for some alpha-emitting radionuclides [15].

Mathematical modeling of DNA repair mechanisms is crucial for understanding how cells respond to radiation-induced damage. One important model used in this context is the Two-Lesion Kinetic (TLK) model, which is employed to describe the repair of double-strand breaks (DSBs) in DNA. This model is particularly useful for analyzing both complex and simple damage caused by radiation. The TLK model integrates various repair mechanisms, such as Homologous Recombination (HR) and Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ), and describes how these pathways contribute to cell survival after radiation exposure. In this regard, complex DSBs, which are harder to repair, tend to have a more significant impact on cell viability than simple ones. By incorporating both simple (repairable) and complex (non-repairable) lesions, the TLK model offers insights into how different types of DNA damage affect cell survival. Specifically, simple lesions are typically repaired more easily, while complex lesions, caused by misrepair of DNA ends, pose a greater challenge for repair. This distinction is vital for understanding how radiation-induced DNA damage influences cellular outcomes post-irradiation.

Geant4-DNA toolkit

The Geant4-DNA toolkit is an advanced simulation tool used for analyzing DNA damage induced by ionizing radiation at the molecular level. This tool employs track-structure simulations, which offer much higher spatial resolution compared to traditional condensed-history methods. In these simulations, instead of using an artificial step to simulate interactions, each interaction is modeled individually, providing more accurate results, especially at the nanoscale. This is crucial for simulating complex processes such as DNA ionization and molecular excitation.

Geant4-DNA is capable of modeling the various stages of radiation damage to DNA, including physical (where radiation energy is deposited in DNA or its surrounding medium), chemical (where radicals are generated and interact with DNA), and biological stages (where these radicals cause damage to DNA). Specifically, in this study, the IRT-sync model is used to simulate the chemical stage and radical formation. This model, an upgraded version of the Independent Reaction Time (IRT) model, allows for more precise simulations of the radical diffusion process and its interactions with DNA [16].

Geant4-DNA simulations combine physical and chemical models to simulate DNA damage under realistic cellular conditions. For instance, simulations performed using DNA geometries such as cylinders and a simplified human cell model have successfully predicted radiation-induced DNA damage. These simulations were validated by comparing the results with existing literature, showing less than a 5% discrepancy in all cases. By simulating radiation damage in a more biologically relevant context, Geant4-DNA provides valuable insights into the impact of different radiation types and energies on DNA at the molecular level [17].

Cell nucleus geometry

The “molecularDNA” example of the Geant4 toolkit was used for the construction of the fibroblast cell nucleus. In this example, an advanced fractal model of a fibroblast cell nucleus built with Hilbert curves [18] is applied. The fractal model for fibroblast nuclei was chosen to accurately represent the three-dimensional and self-similar arrangement of DNA in the nucleus. This model, based on Hilbert curves, allows for more biologically relevant simulations of radiation damage interactions at the molecular level. In fractal geometry, a small piece of DNA is continuously repeated in three dimensions and in a special arrangement. The full DNA chain consists of straight, turned, and turned with 90° twist sections, including histones [19]. The continuous DNA chain has a length of nearly 6.4 giga base pairs (Gbp). An ellipsoid mask was created to limit the DNA chain inside an ellipsoid volume of 14.2 μm × 5 μm × 14.2 μm dimensions. The effective nucleus density is approximately 0.015 bp per nm3. For more details, refer to references [8]. The constructed geometry is shown in Fig. 2a. Figure 2b displays eight small cubes with a side of 75 nm with straight, turned, and 90-degree twisted turned sections. A magnified view of a 5 nm length of the base pairs and sugar-phosphate parts is also illustrated in Fig. 2c.

Radiation source

Ac-225 and Th-227 is uniformly distributed in a fibroblast cell nucleus. Therefore, the radiation source was defined as a volumetric source with dimensions exactly equal to the cell nucleus. The angular distribution of the source is isotropic. The full decay chain of Ac-225 (up to Bi-209 stable isotope) and Th-227 (up to Pb-207 stable isotope) was considered for the calculation of early DNA damage. A total of 1000 decays were simulated to ensure statistical reliability with a relative standard deviation of less than 0.01%, providing a robust dataset for the DNA damage calculations.

Physics and chemistry constructors

Based on the decay scheme of Ac-225, Th-227 and their subsequent chain, we are dealing with alpha and beta particles as well as gamma radiation. The “G4EmDNAPhysics_option4” constructor was utilized for modeling physical interactions such as ionization, scattering, and excitation of DNA. This choice ensures the accurate simulation of alpha particle interactions with DNA at the nanometer scale. The corresponding physics are G4DNAIonElasticModel (100 eV–1 MeV) for elastic scattering, G4DNAMillerGreenExcitationModel (1 keV–400 MeV) for electronic excitation, and G4DNARuddIonisationModel (100 eV–400 MeV) for electronic ionization, and G4DNADingfelderChargeDecreaseModel (1 keV–400 MeV) for electron capture.

The physical interactions for electrons are elastic scattering, ionization, and electronic excitation. The corresponding physics are G4DNAUeharaScreenedRutherfordElasticModel (10 eV–1 MeV) for elastic scattering, G4DNAEmfietzoglouExcitationModel (8 eV–10 keV) and G4DNABornExcitationModel (10 keV–1 MeV) for electronic excitation, and G4DNAEmfietzoglouIonisationModel (11 eV–10 keV) and G4DNABornIonisationModel (10 keV–1 MeV) for electronic ionization [20, 21]. The cut-off energy for secondary electrons is 10 eV, i.e., the secondary electrons up to this kinetic energy will not be tracked. The interactions of photons are based on the Geant4 Livermore/EADL97 models [22]. To consider the physics of decay, the G4RadioactiveDecayPhysics and G4DecayPhysics constructors were registered in the physics list class of the Geant4-DNA simulation [23]. The physical interactions of primary and secondary particles occur in the physical phase of the simulation.

After the physical stage, the physico-chemical (or pre-chemical) and chemical stages initiates. The chemical species (i.e., the free radicals) are generated in the physico-chemical stage, and their diffusion and mutual chemical reactions occur in the chemical stage [24, 25]. The simulation time for the chemical stage was 1 ns [26]. For simulating the chemical stage, the “G4EmDNAChemistry_option3” chemistry constructor was used. To simulate the chemical effects of free radicals produced during radiation interactions, the Independent Reaction Time (IRT) approach [27] was employed, which allows for faster and more efficient simulations of radical diffusion and chemical reactions compared to traditional methods. The implemented chemical reactions and the related reaction rates in Geant4-DNA are provided in references [28, 29].

DNA damage calculation

The DNA damage was determined based on the complexity and source. DNA damage was categorized as direct, indirect, or mixed based on its origin. Direct damage results from physical interactions between radiation and the DNA molecule, while indirect damage arises from chemical reactions between DNA and reactive radicals. Mixed damage involves two simultaneous lesions, one caused by a direct interaction and the other by an indirect mechanism [30]. Similar to previous simulations, only hydroxyl radical (OH•) was considered for calculating indirect DNA damages since they are widely recognized as the predominant reactive species responsible for indirect DNA damage. Other reactive species such as hydrogen atoms (H•) and solvated electrons (e⁻aq) were not included in order to simplify the chemical modeling and to reduce computational complexity [31]. Note that not all OH• induce an indirect DNA damage. Thus, a probability of 40% was considered for the interaction of an OH• with a target volume to lead to an indirect damage. Moreover, all free radicals that travel more than 4 nm were killed [32].

In terms of damage complexity, DNA strand breaks are categorized not only into single-strand breaks (SSBs) and double-strand breaks (DSBs), but also into more intricate forms originally proposed by Nikjoo et al. [27]. Figure 3 shows the types of these DNA damages. A SSB can occur when an energy greater than 5 eV is deposited in the target volume (sugar-phosphate parts) [29]. This is the minimum probability of SSB, and as the energy deposited increases, the probability of a SSB occurrence increases linearly. The probability of a SSB occurrence with the deposited energy of 37.5 eV (or more) is 100%.

Classification scheme of DNA damage types, including single-strand breaks (SSBs), double-strand breaks (DSBs), and their subcategories based on the distance between breaks (e.g., < 10 bp or > 10 bp) and the origin of damage (direct, indirect, or mixed)

Two SSBs on the opposite DNA strands with a distance of less than 10 bp (roughly 3.4 nm) would turn to a DSB. A radial distance of 0.5 nm beyond each target volume was taken into account as hydration shells to include the quasi-direct effect. The quasi-direct effect refers to the damage caused by charge transfers after ionization of the hydration shell around the DNA [33]. SSBd, SSBi, and SSBm refers to direct, indirect, and mix SSBs, respectively. DSBd, DSBi, and DSBm refers to direct, indirect, and mix DSBs, respectively.

Cell survival calculation (SF)

As irradiation proceeds, the frequency of radiation-induced DNA damage increases, though a portion of this damage is gradually repaired over time through the cell’s DNA repair mechanisms. The Python code featured in the “molecularDNA” example was employed to simulate these cellular repair processes and to generate cell survival curves. This mathematical model for repair was developed by the research conducted by Belov et al. [35] and is accessible through the Python interface in the “molecularDNA” example [14].

Discussion

In this study, the Geant4-DNA code (version 11.1 patch 3) was used to calculate the DNA damage caused by the particles emitted in 225Ac and 227Th decay chain and to compare their biological effectiveness relative to a reference gamma emitter, 60Co. The Geant4-DNA code belongs to the family of track-structure codes, which takes into account all the details of the interaction at micro and nanometer scales [43, 44]. Geant4-DNA offers significant advantages over other simulation codes, including precise modeling of physical and chemical processes at the nanoscale, the ability to define realistic geometries for DNA structures, high spatial and energy resolution in simulating radiation interactions, its open-source nature, and the capability for continuous updates and development based on the latest scientific findings.

The spatial distribution of energy deposition and, consequently, the complexity of induced DNA damage is significantly affected by the organization and topology of DNA [28]. Utilizing an advanced fractal model of the fibroblast nucleus (based on Hilbert curves) closely resembles the physical packing of DNA in vivo. The geometry used in this work represents a more biologically realistic and sophisticated model compared to traditional geometrical assumptions, leading to more reliable estimates for SSB, DSB, and DNA fragment length distributions after alpha irradiation.

To validate the simulation, the energy spectrum of alpha particles and photons emitted from the Ac-225 full decay chain was obtained by MC simulation and compared with the data available in the literature. The energy spectrum of alpha particles matches well with the data presented by Suliman and his colleagues [45] who measured the energy spectrum of alpha particles experimentally. The energy spectrum of the emitted photons is also consistent with Tichacek et al.’s study [46], which calculates the spectrum by MCNP6 MC code.

The results showed a distinct DSBs formation by 227Th and 225Ac, consistent with the known high linear energy transfer (LET) properties of alpha radiation. For both radioisotopes, the number of SSBs and DSBs caused by indirect effects is lower than those caused by direct effects. In high-LET radiation, the local energy density is so high that most damage is already caused directly before reactive oxygen species (ROS) can even form or act. Even so, they can also generate a certain amount of indirect DNA damage by producing free radicals [47]. For an indirect effect to cause a DSB, two ROS must independently cause two SSBs very close together on opposite DNA strands—which is statistically much less likely than direct ionization causing a DSB immediately.

Although significant progress has been made in the development of alpha-emitting radiopharmaceuticals, quantitative assessments of DNA damage remain limited. Nevertheless, in this work, an attempt is made to compare the obtained values with those of several therapeutic radioisotopes. In 2021, Seifi Moradi and Bidabadi have calculated the SSBs and DSBs for several different diagnostic and therapeutic radioisotopes including 131I and 211At [45]. The calculated values for 131I and 211At as well as the obtained amounts for 225Ac and 227Th (from this study) are presented in Table 1 for better comparison. As expected, the SSBs and DSBs are considerably higher for 225Ac and 227Th even compared with 211At due to their decay characteristics.

The analysis of DNA fragment length frequency distributions provides key insights into the complexity and extent of radiation-induced DNA damage at the nanometer scale. The fragment length distributions exhibit a high frequency of short DNA fragments, especially those below 100 base pairs. Short fragments suggest higher lethality and mis-repair risk, supporting their use in cancer therapy. In comparison with proton or beta irradiation, the DNA fragment length spectrum produced by 225Ac and 227Th shows a shift toward smaller fragments, higher frequency of complex breakage sites and also significantly steeper drop-off in fragment length frequency, which implies more clustered damage [48, 49].This finding is consistent with the high LET characteristics of alpha particles and aligns with prior research, including Belli et al. and Campa et al. [50, 51], which have shown that high-LET radiation preferentially induces short DNA fragments as a result of spatially correlated strand breaks.

The RBE quantifies the biological potency of a radiation type relative to a reference radiation, typically γ-rays from 60Co. In this study, RBE was calculated by comparing the dose required to induce a single strand break or double strand break by 225Ac and 227Th relative to 60Co. The results demonstrate that both 225Ac and 227Th exhibit significantly higher RBE values compared to low-LET radiation like 60Co.

The SF quantifies the percentage of cells that maintain their ability to reproduce after exposure to radiation. In TAT, SF serves as a key indicator linking DNA-level damage to broader biological outcomes, such as tumor suppression or tissue damage. In this study, survival curves were generated for fibroblast cell nuclei subjected to the full decay chains 225Ac and 227Th using the Two-Lesion Kinetic (TLK) model embedded within the Geant4-DNA simulation framework.

This model accounts for both rapidly repairable (simple) and slow or unrepaired (complex) DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), and simulates the dynamics of various DNA repair mechanisms, including homologous recombination (HR), non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), and alternative end joining (A-EJ). The TLK-based modeling approach enhances the clinical applicability of the results, providing valuable insights for refining dosing strategies in TAT. The results demonstrate a steep decline in cell survival with increasing radiation dose from both 225Ac and 227Th, consistent with the high-LET characteristics of alpha particles. These particles creating clustered DNA damage that overwhelms the cell’s repair capacity, particularly when DSBs are spatially correlated or chemically complex.

In this study, higher SSBs and DSBs were observed for 227Th decay chain compared to 225Ac. Furthermore, 227Th generally results in a higher yield of short DNA fragments than 225Ac. While both radionuclides show elevated RBE values and significant reduction in cell survival, the study indicates that 227Th tends to have slightly higher RBE and slightly more lethal at equivalent absorbed doses compared with 225Ac. This difference may stem from the distinct energy spectra and number of alpha particles emitted in their respective decay chains. This pattern suggests that while 225Ac is already highly cytotoxic and therapeutically potent, 227Th could induce even greater biological disruption under similar dose conditions.

The results of this study can provide crucial insights into optimizing treatment and significantly improve the precision and efficacy for therapies involving 225Ac and 227Th plans. The Geant4-DNA’s modeling of radiation quality, combined with RBE values for 225Ac and 227Th, can help personalize radiotherapy by adjusting doses according to the tumor type and its repair capability. This would lead to better-targeted treatments that minimize damage to surrounding healthy tissue while maximizing therapeutic effects.

Moreover, the simulation data on DNA fragment length distributions and damage types will help predict how different types of radiation, including alpha particles, contribute to complex tumor responses. This data can be instrumental in designing optimized protocols for both initial radiation dose delivery and follow-up treatments [42]. A deeper understanding of how 225Ac and 227Th impact DNA at the molecular level will allow clinicians to predict which tumors are more likely to respond to these treatments, based on their DNA repair profiles. Understanding the three-dimensional genome architecture and enhancer rewiring in cancer is crucial for optimizing targeted alpha therapies (TαT). Genomic alterations like translocations and amplifications can influence DNA repair and therapeutic outcomes, highlighting the importance of considering enhancer dynamics in cancer treatment [52].

Finally, the inclusion of a chemical stage in these simulations, where radicals produced during radiation interact with DNA, is essential for enhancing the clinical application of these radionuclides. By understanding the radical species diffusion and the subsequent DNA damage, researchers can refine the selection of radiolabeled compounds for targeted therapy, ensuring that radionuclides like 225Ac and 227Th are used in conjunction with the right drugs to maximize tumor cell damage while minimizing collateral damage. These insights are crucial for the clinical translation of 225Ac and 227Th therapies, allowing for better targeting of alpha particles to tumors, more efficient planning of radiation doses, and improved patient outcomes [15].

In addition to radiobiological effects, pharmacokinetic properties are an important consideration in therapeutic planning. While the longer half-life of 227Th (18.7 d) may enable extended circulation and tumor uptake when conjugated to antibodies, 225Ac (10 d), with its shorter half-life show faster pharmacokinetics. These differences influence not only tumor uptake but also normal tissue exposure, and should therefore be carefully considered when selecting radionuclides for targeted alpha therapy.

While the Geant4-DNA simulations provide valuable insights into DNA damage induced by alpha emitters, several assumptions and uncertainties must be considered when interpreting the results. One key limitation of the model is the idealized geometry of the DNA, specifically the use of a fractal model for the fibroblast nucleus. While this model offers a more biologically realistic representation of DNA organization compared to simpler models, it still remains an approximation of the actual DNA structure in vivo. This could introduce uncertainties, particularly in simulations that involve the spatial arrangement of DNA and its interaction with radiation.

Furthermore, the simulations assume uniform distribution of the radioactive decay products within the cell nucleus, which may not reflect the actual distribution in biological systems where factors such as molecular binding affinity and compartmentalization could influence the distribution of radiation damage. Additionally, the chemical stage in the simulation, where free radicals interact with DNA, relies on the IRT approach, which simplifies the modeling of radical diffusion and chemical reactions. While this approach offers computational efficiency, it may not capture all the complexities of radical interactions in vivo, potentially affecting the accuracy of the calculated damage, particularly in highly heterogeneous cellular environments. For example, a study in 2025 demonstrates that the oxygen enhancement ratio (OER) in high-LET radiation correlates with clustered DSBs in the three-dimensional genome [53].

Finally, while the study presents a robust dataset with a low relative standard deviation, uncertainties inherent in the physical interactions and biological processes modeled in Geant4-DNA remain. Variations in key parameters, such as the energy deposition of alpha particles and the exact nature of DNA repair pathways, could influence the outcome of the simulations. Despite these limitations, the study provides a valuable foundation for further exploration of the clinical potential of 225Ac and 227Th in targeted alpha therapy.

Conclusion

This study successfully quantified the DNA damage induced by alpha particles emitted from the 225Ac and 227Th decay chains using Geant4-DNA Monte Carlo simulations. By employing an advanced fractal model of the fibroblast nucleus, the simulations provided detailed insights into the spatial distribution and complexity of radiation-induced DNA damage. The findings underscore the superior cytotoxic potential of 225Ac and 227Th in targeted alpha therapy (TAT), particularly due to the high density of double-strand breaks (DSBs) induced by these radionuclides. This high-density DNA damage highlights the importance of precise microdosimetric modeling to optimize radiopharmaceutical treatments and improve therapeutic outcomes.

Moving forward, this study paves the way for further investigation into the clinical applications of 225Ac and 227Th-based therapies. The results suggest that both radionuclides could be highly effective in treating cancers resistant to conventional therapies, with their elevated RBE making them ideal candidates for combination therapies with chemotherapy or immunotherapy. Additionally, the findings from this research can help refine dosing strategies in TAT, enhancing the precision of treatments tailored to tumor-specific characteristics and DNA repair profiles. Future studies should focus on integrating biological repair mechanisms, chromatin compaction dynamics, and progeny migration effects into the Geant4-DNA framework. Such integrations will enable a more comprehensive understanding of radiation damage repair and the long-term effects of alpha radiation, thereby improving the clinical translation of 225Ac and 227Th therapies.

In summary, this work contributes valuable insights into the potential clinical applications of 225Ac and 227Th in TAT. The findings offer critical data that can be used to optimize treatment protocols, maximize therapeutic efficacy, and minimize collateral damage to healthy tissues. This study serves as a foundation for advancing the clinical use of these radionuclides, providing a step closer to personalized radiotherapy strategies and improved patient outcomes.
