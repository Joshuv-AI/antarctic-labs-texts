Introduction

Hepatic subcapsular hematoma (HSH) is a rare but potentially life-threatening condition characterized by bleeding between the liver parenchyma and Glisson’s capsule [1]. Although most commonly reported in association with pregnancy-related disorders, trauma, anticoagulation, or underlying hepatic pathology, HSH may also occur as an uncommon postoperative complication following abdominal surgery, including cholecystectomy and common bile duct (CBD) exploration [2, 3]. When it arises in the postoperative setting, its abrupt presentation and rapid progression pose significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

The pathophysiology of hepatic subcapsular hematomas is multifactorial and incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms encompass the rupture of delicate intrahepatic capillaries, capsular microtears resulting from surgical manipulation, venous congestion, compromised hemostasis, or pharmacological influences such as anticoagulants and extended non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) usage [4, 5]. Importantly, several reports describe spontaneous subcapsular hematomas occurring in patients without identifiable trauma, hepatic disease, or coagulation abnormalities, emphasizing the unpredictable nature of this entity [6, 7]. Postoperative HSH following cholecystectomy is exceptionally rare, particularly when involving both hepatic lobes. Bilateral subcapsular involvement substantially increases the risk of capsular rupture, massive hemorrhage, and hemodynamic collapse [1, 8]. The case of a 34-year-old woman who developed a spontaneous bilateral subcapsular hepatic hematoma shortly after open cholecystectomy and CBD exploration highlights the severity of this complication and underscores the need for heightened clinical vigilance. This report adds to the limited body of literature on postoperative spontaneous hepatic subcapsular hematomas by illustrating their clinical presentation, diagnostic challenges, and surgical management. Given the high mortality associated with delayed recognition, an improved understanding of risk factors, imaging findings, and treatment strategies is essential to optimize outcomes.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old woman presented with a six-month history of progressively worsening right upper-quadrant abdominal pain. Initially, the pain was intermittent and colicky, exacerbated by fatty or heavy meals, and partially relieved by antacids and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which she had been using chronically. One week prior to admission, the pain became constant and non-relieving, accompanied by nausea, anorexia, generalized malaise, and progressive jaundice. She denied vomiting, fever, gastrointestinal bleeding, or weight loss. There was no history of previous abdominal surgery, trauma, bleeding disorders, or chronic medical illness, and she was not using anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications.

On examination, the patient appeared ill and markedly jaundiced but was hemodynamically stable. Abdominal examination revealed right upper-quadrant tenderness without guarding or rigidity. No abdominal mass was palpable, bowel sounds were present, and there were no peripheral stigmata of chronic liver disease. The remainder of the systemic examination was unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations obtained at an outside facility demonstrated a cholestatic and inflammatory pattern, including markedly elevated gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT 400 U/L), aspartate aminotransferase (AST 170 U/L), alanine aminotransferase (ALT 140 U/L), leukocytosis (WBC 16,000/µL), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP 200 mg/L), anemia with a baseline hemoglobin of 8.7 g/dL, total bilirubin of 8 mg/dL, and direct bilirubin of 6 mg/dL. Preoperative coagulation parameters were within normal limits, with a prothrombin time (PT) of 13.0 s, international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.05, and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) of 35.0 s.

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed multiple gallbladder calculi with dilation of the common bile duct. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography confirmed the presence of three common bile duct stones in addition to multiple gallbladder stones.

In our setting, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is not routinely available due to resource limitations, including the absence of necessary endoscopic equipment and specialized expertise. Therefore, endoscopic stone extraction was not a feasible option.

Given the confirmed diagnosis of choledocholithiasis with obstructive symptoms, a single-stage open cholecystectomy with common bile duct exploration was selected as the definitive management approach.

The patient was admitted for preoperative optimization and commenced on broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics (meropenem 1 g and metronidazole 500 mg every 8 h). She was kept nil per os except for clear fluids and transfused with two units of packed red blood cells to correct anemia. Following clinical stabilization and informed consent, she underwent open cholecystectomy with common bile duct exploration under general anesthesia.

A right subcostal incision was performed, and a fundus-first cholecystectomy was carried out. The cystic duct and cystic artery were individually ligated. Choledochotomy was performed, and three common bile duct stones were extracted. Intraoperative choledochoscopy confirmed complete clearance of the proximal and distal biliary tree. Primary repair of the common bile duct was completed, with no evidence of bile leakage or active bleeding at the conclusion of the procedure (Fig. 1). Hemostasis was secured, and two drains were placed: one Jackson–Pratt active drain and one passive drain. The abdomen was closed in layers, and the patient was transferred to the surgical ward in stable condition.

Intraoperative view of the subhepatic bed following cholecystectomy and choledochotomy with primary common bile duct repair, showing no evidence of bile leak, bleeding, or disruption

On postoperative day one, the patient remained clinically stable, with approximately 100 mL of serosanguinous output from the active drain. Given her overweight status, recent prolonged immobilization for more than one week prior to surgery, and the thrombotic risk associated with major open hepatobiliary procedures, low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin 0.4 mL once daily) was initiated for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis in accordance with institutional protocol.

On postoperative day two, the patient developed sudden massive hemorrhagic output, with approximately 500 mL of fresh blood from the Jackson–Pratt drain and 300 mL from the passive drain. She became tachypneic, and laboratory evaluation revealed an acute drop in hemoglobin to 6.5 g/dL. At the time of hemorrhagic deterioration, platelet count was 148 × 10⁹/L, prothrombin time (PT) was 14.8 s, international normalized ratio (INR) was 1.45, and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) was markedly prolonged at 135.6 s.

Immediate resuscitation was initiated with three units of packed red blood cells, two units of fresh frozen plasma, tranexamic acid (1 g), and vitamin K. Following transfusion, hemoglobin increased from 6.5 g/dL to 11.6 g/dL. However, despite transient hemodynamic stabilization, drain output remained persistently high. Repeat laboratory evaluation on postoperative day three demonstrated a further decline in hemoglobin to 8.5 g/dL, indicating ongoing bleeding. In addition, total drain output over the preceding 24 h was approximately 700 mL of fresh blood from both drains combined. Given the continued high-volume hemorrhagic output and progressive anemia despite transfusion, urgent surgical re-exploration was deemed necessary.

Urgent abdominal ultrasonography revealed a large right hepatic subcapsular hematoma measuring approximately 15 × 10 cm.

On postoperative day three, the patient underwent urgent surgical re-exploration through the previous incision. Intraoperatively, a large bilateral hepatic subcapsular hematoma involving both the right and left lobes was identified. The liver surface appeared tense and bluish, with marked capsular distention, consistent with acute subcapsular bleeding (Fig. 2). Extensive capsular shearing and diffuse venous oozing from the hepatic surface were observed, without an identifiable arterial bleeding source or bile leak (Fig. 3). Damage-control hemostasis was achieved by circumferential packing of the liver with multiple surgical compresses (Fig. 4). Temporary abdominal closure was performed at the skin level only, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for close monitoring.

Over the subsequent 48 h, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, with hemoglobin levels maintained at approximately 8 g/dL following transfusion. Drain output decreased significantly, and there were no clinical or laboratory signs of ongoing hemorrhage. Planned re-exploration and pack removal were subsequently performed. Upon de-packing, only minimal venous oozing was observed. Definitive hemostasis was achieved using high-voltage diathermy, Surgicel application, and plasma sealant. The greater omentum was mobilized and placed over the hepatic surface to reinforce hemostasis. The abdomen was then closed in layers.

Postoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen demonstrated a right hepatic pericapsular hematoma with postoperative changes, including surgical clips in the gallbladder fossa, subhepatic drainage tubes, and pericapsular omentoplasty (Fig. 5A, B and C, and 5D).

The patient demonstrated steady clinical improvement. Drain output progressively diminished and ceased. Liver function tests and bilirubin levels gradually normalized. She resumed oral intake without difficulty, drains were removed, and she was discharged home in stable condition with instructions for close outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

The occurrence of a spontaneous bilateral hepatic subcapsular hematoma following open cholecystectomy and CBD exploration, as observed in this case, represents an exceedingly rare postoperative complication. While hepatic injury during biliary surgery has been described, most reported cases involve focal or unilateral hematomas rather than extensive bilateral involvement [1, 4, 5]. The absence of pre-existing liver disease, trauma, or therapeutic anticoagulation in this patient suggests that although no capsular disruption or iatrogenic hepatic injury was identified during the index procedure, delayed bleeding from microscopic or subclinical vascular injury cannot be entirely excluded. This observation is consistent with reports documenting subcapsular hematomas occurring without clear precipitating factors [8, 9].

Intraoperative hemostasis is critical during hepatobiliary surgery; however, this case demonstrates that meticulous technique does not fully eliminate the risk of delayed hemorrhagic complications. The sudden onset of massive hemorrhagic drain output and precipitous hemoglobin decline on postoperative day two highlights the unpredictable nature of postoperative HSH. Similar reports emphasize that delayed bleeding may result from diffuse venous oozing rather than discrete arterial injury, particularly when no active arterial source is identified during re-exploration [10, 11]. The initiation of prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin may have facilitated expansion of an occult capsular bleed, although prophylactic dosing alone is rarely sufficient to cause such catastrophic hemorrhage.

The temporal relationship between initiation of prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) on postoperative day one and the onset of hemorrhage on day two warrants careful consideration. Although the patient was young, she had additional venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk factors, including overweight status, prolonged immobilization exceeding one week, and major open hepatobiliary surgery. Established guidelines support early postoperative thromboprophylaxis in moderate-risk surgical patients to reduce VTE incidence [12 ,13].

However, hepatobiliary procedures involving hepatic manipulation carry an inherent bleeding risk, requiring careful risk–benefit assessment. Anti-Xa levels were not measured, as routine monitoring is not recommended for prophylactic dosing [14]. Although LMWH may theoretically facilitate expansion of an occult bleed, prophylactic dosing alone is unlikely to cause catastrophic hemorrhage in the absence of significant coagulopathy. This case underscores the importance of individualized anticoagulation decisions following major hepatobiliary surgery.

Imaging plays a pivotal role in diagnosis. Ultrasonography provides a rapid bedside assessment, while contrast-enhanced computed tomography remains the gold standard for defining the extent of the hematoma, identifying postoperative changes, and guiding management decisions [15]. In this case, CT imaging was essential in confirming the diagnosis and excluding alternative sources of bleeding.

Management strategies depend largely on hemodynamic stability, hematoma size, and evidence of ongoing bleeding. Conservative treatment may be appropriate in stable patients; however, hemodynamic instability mandates immediate surgical intervention. The staged surgical approach employed in this case reflects the principles of damage control surgery, prioritizing rapid hemorrhage control and physiological stabilization before definitive repair [9, 16]. Perihepatic packing remains a cornerstone technique for controlling diffuse hepatic bleeding when no focal source is identified, allowing a tamponade of venous oozing and preventing capsular rupture [17].

Definitive management following stabilization involved meticulous hemostasis using electrocautery, topical hemostatic agents, plasma sealants, and omentoplasty. These adjunctive measures have been increasingly supported in the literature for reinforcing hepatic surface hemostasis and reducing rebleeding risk [5, 7]. The successful outcome in this patient underscores the effectiveness of a structured, stepwise surgical strategy in managing severe postoperative hepatic hemorrhage.

This report has several limitations. As a single-case report, its findings cannot be generalized or used to establish causality. Although no intracapsular injury was identified during the index operation, the exact mechanism of hematoma formation remains unclear. Advanced coagulation testing and anti-Xa level monitoring were not available in our resource-limited setting, which limits detailed assessment of hemostatic status. Additionally, long-term radiological follow-up was not performed to document complete resolution. Furthermore, the unavailability of advanced minimally invasive surgical options, including laparoscopic cholecystectomy with laparoscopic common bile duct exploration, reflects resource limitations in our setting and may limit the generalizability of this management approach to higher-resource centers. These limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the findings.

Bilateral hepatic subcapsular hematomas represent a formidable surgical challenge due to their high risk of rupture and mortality. Early recognition, aggressive resuscitation, timely imaging, and decisive surgical intervention are essential for survival. This case reinforces the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for HSH in patients who develop sudden postoperative bleeding after biliary surgery and highlights the need for continued research into preventive strategies and optimal management protocols.

Conclusion

Spontaneous bilateral hepatic subcapsular hematoma following open cholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration is an exceptionally rare but potentially fatal postoperative complication. Its abrupt onset, absence of a discrete bleeding source, and rapid progression underscore the unpredictable nature of this entity. This case highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients who develop sudden hemorrhagic deterioration after biliary surgery, even in the absence of predisposing risk factors. Prompt recognition, aggressive resuscitation, early imaging, and timely surgical intervention are crucial to prevent catastrophic outcomes. A staged damage-control surgical approach, including perihepatic packing followed by definitive hemostasis and omentoplasty, can be life-saving when diffuse venous oozing is encountered. Increased awareness of this rare complication may facilitate earlier diagnosis and improve postoperative outcomes in hepatobiliary surgery.
