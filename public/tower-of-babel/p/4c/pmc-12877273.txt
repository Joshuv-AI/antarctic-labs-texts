Comfort feeding in hospitalised people with dementia: a retrospective study of survival following comfort feeding recommendations
Authors: Christine Davis, Nasime Mokari-Manshadi, Viviane De Menezes Caceres, Patrick Russell, Toby Gilbert, Stephen Hedger, Udul Hewage, Dirk Hoffman, Yogesh Sharma, Alice O’Connell, Amrit Sandhu, Jeff Faunt, Campbell Thompson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPersistent and significant swallowing impairment can occur in individuals with dementia. Determining prognosis and establishing realistic goals of care in this population is complex and comfort feeding may be recommended. This study aimed to establish evidence relating to patient outcomes following recommendation of comfort feeding to aid informed decision making.DesignA multi-centre, retrospective audit was conducted for a two-year period to establish the survival and readmission rates for hospitalised people with dementia, following recommendation of a comfort feeding plan.SettingThe study was conducted at three acute care hospitals in Adelaide, South Australia.ParticipantsA total of 163 participants were included, 90 male and 73 female, with a median age of 88 years.MeasurementsMortality within 30 and 90 days of admission and readmission rates within 30 days of discharge were calculated.ResultsForty-two percent of participants died during the admission during which a comfort feeding plan was recommended. Overall median survival time and one month survival was 13 days and 25%, respectively. Readmission rates were low (7.4% of those discharged). Comfort feeding recommendations aligned with dysphagia severity and those for whom Nil By Mouth (NBM) or ice chips only were recommended were at highest risk of dying in hospital, those recommended thickened fluids +/− ice chips were most likely to be alive 30 days after their original admission date.ConclusionDementia and comfort feeding were associated with high mortality rates, high rates of discharge to a supportive care facility and low readmission rates. Dysphagia severity associated with the consistency of fluids recommended.Keywords: Comfort feeding, Dementia, Dysphagia, Mortality, Survival

Introduction

Dementia is a neurodegenerative syndrome, characterised by cognitive, psychological, and functional decline and associated with multiple complications [1,2]. Determining prognosis in dementia is challenging and the prognosis is influenced by the complexity of each individual’s comorbidities and associated complications [[2], [3], [4]]. Nevertheless, understanding the clinical trajectory for people with dementia can improve the quality of their care and reduce burdensome interventions, such as hospitalisation and tube feeding, towards the end of life [5]. Oropharyngeal dysphagia is a highly prevalent symptom of dementia [1,6,7] and is common in the terminal phase of dementia, when distressing symptoms including aspiration, dyspnoea, pain, and pressure ulcers increase [5].

In the acute hospital setting, speech-language pathologists (SLP) and medical officers form part of a multi-disciplinary team responsible for facilitating patient-centred, informed decision-making regarding the management of oropharyngeal dysphagia for people with dementia. In Australia, the SLP profession distinguishes between comfort feeding and eating and drinking with acknowledged risk. In the Australian context comfort feeding refers to continuation of eating and drinking with acknowledged risk of aspiration, when an individual’s health care goals are focussed on quality of life, not active treatment with curative intent, towards the end of life. In contrast, eating and drinking with acknowledged risk refers to a situation whereby an individual makes an informed decision to consume oral food and/or fluids, with known risk of aspiration or choking, in the context of ongoing active treatment goals of care [8].

Long term tube feeding is not recommended for people with dementia because there is no improvement in survival or quality of life associated with tube feeding in this population [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Instead, oropharyngeal dysphagia associated with dementia is often managed with a modification to diet texture and/or fluid thickness and compensatory and safe swallowing strategies to reduce aspiration and/or choking risk [13]. Comfort feeding may be recommended as an alternative to tube feeding for individuals with dementia complicated by persistent and significant swallowing impairment [10,14,15].

Known complications of oropharyngeal dysphagia associated with dementia include malnutrition, respiratory infections, and death [6]. Aspiration pneumonia is associated with hospitalisation, morbidity, and mortality in the geriatric population [16]. However, aspiration risk does not necessarily lead to an aspiration pneumonia. Various other factors have been demonstrated to contribute to pneumonia in patients with aspiration risk, including medical, functional, reflux and oral health status and the route of nutritional intake [17]. Determining prognosis and establishing realistic goals of care is complicated by the paucity of evidence relating to patient outcomes following recommendation of comfort feeding.

A person does not have to have a diagnosis of severe oropharyngeal dysphagia to be considered a candidate for comfort feeding. The option of comfort feeding is about aligning with a patient’s goals of care towards the end of life, allowing autonomy, choice and dignity. Also, there are various types of fluid thickness that can be recommended for comfort feeding. These recommendations arise on the basis of swallowing assessment by the SLP but also are based upon other factors including patient preferences and perceived prognosis. The characteristics of patients recommended for each of the different fluid consistencies are unknown as are the outcomes for these different categories of patients all of whom have been recommended for comfort feeding.

The aims of this study were several. First, we sought a snapshot of clinical practice in operation on the hospital wards. We sought to detail the characteristics and outcomes of a cohort of patients with dementia who received SLP assessment to establish a comfort feeding plan. Second, we sought to determine whether any of the characteristics and outcomes of this cohort are distinguishable on the basis of the recommended fluid thickness. We hypothesised that fluid consistency was related not only to characteristics of this inpatient group but also to the subsequent clinical course of the patient – specifically their discharge destination, the duration of their survival and their risk of readmission. As a result of this work, more informed research studies can be designed and better advice given to SLPs, medical staff, patients, and families on the prognosis and outcomes for this frail and vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and setting

A multi-centre, retrospective audit was conducted at three hospitals, the Royal Adelaide Hospital (RAH), The Queen Elizabeth Hospital (TQEH), and Flinders Medical Centre (FMC) in Adelaide, South Australia. Ethics approval, including a waiver of consent, was granted by the Central Adelaide Local Health Network and Southern Adelaide Local Health Network Human Research Ethics Committees, Reference Number 15560.

Participants

Included participants were admitted to RAH, TQEH or FMC between January 2020 and December 2021, under a general physician or geriatrician. No patient included in the study had a primary or secondary diagnosis of COVID-19 infection. Participants were aged 50 years or over, with a diagnosis of dementia documented in their medical records, and a recommended comfort feeding plan following assessment by a SLP. Participants with a cognitive impairment unrelated to dementia (e.g., congenital intellectual disability, brain injury, Huntington’s disease, and multiple sclerosis) were excluded. A search of electronic medical records (EMR) for documents containing the term “comfort feeding” was performed in addition to extraction of patients with International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes for dementia combined with dysphagia. The resulting EMR and hard copy medical records were screened to identify study participants.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes for the study were mortality within 30 and 90 days of admission and readmission rates within 30 days of discharge. Hospital readmission was defined as any subsequent admission, under a general physician or geriatrician to FMC, RAH or TQEH.

Data sources, clinical conditions, and covariates

Data were collected from participant medical records including medical, SLP and nursing documentation. Clinical data collected included age, gender and severity of dysphagia. Severity of dysphagia was determined by a SLP clinical swallow examination. For analytical purposes, subjects were divided into groups on the basis of the SLP’s recommended fluid consistency. Additional outcomes of interest included length of hospital stay (LOS) and discharge destination.

Statistical methods

Descriptive summaries, including baseline demographics and clinical outcomes, were presented according to fluid consistency recommended for comfort feeding. Categorical data were reported as frequency and percentage, with univariate P-values from chi-square tests, or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. Continuous data were presented as median and inter-quartile range with P-values from ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test as a non-parametric alternative. Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The number of patients screened for inclusion was 955 of whom 792 were excluded after application of the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The number of participants included from each site were as follows: 85 (RAH), 44 (TQEH) and 34 (FMC).

Just under half of the studied cohort (42%) died in hospital. Only 25% remained alive at 30 days following their original admission date and 15% remained alive at 90 days.

Ninety-five (58% of the original cohort) participants survived to discharge and most of these participants were discharged to a supportive care facility such as a residential care facility or a hospice with only 13% (12/95) of those discharged alive being discharged home. Very few of those discharged were readmitted (7/95 = 7.4%) within 30 days of the initial admission.

Recommended fluid consistency relation to characteristics and outcomes

There was a significant association between dysphagia severity and the consistency of fluids recommended by the SLP (p = 0.001).

Those who were recommended a combination of thickened and thin fluids or NBM+/− ice chips, had the shortest survival times (p = 0.02 for overall association between survival time and fluid consistency) and were the groups least likely to be alive at 30 (p = 0.0005 for overall association between 30 day mortality and fluid consistency) and at 90 (p = 0.001 for overall association between 90 day mortality and fluid consistency) days after their original admission date.

Of those who were discharged alive from hospital two observations could be made: (i) a recommendation of a combination of thickened and thin fluids was associated with the highest readmission rate within 30 days of discharge (14%) and (ii) no particular comfort feeding plan was associated with a longer survival time than any other.

Discussion

The recommendation of a comfort feeding plan for someone with dementia who is hospitalised is not common. These recommendations were made for 4% of patients referred to speech language pathology from the general medicine and geriatrics services in a 12-month period at the RAH, the largest site in this study (C. Davis, personal communication, 1st April 2024). Findings from this study demonstrate that mortality following SLP assessment and recommendation of a comfort feeding plan for hospitalised people with dementia is high. We found a one-month median survival of 25% from the point of hospital admission. Existing evidence focuses on survival associated with tube feeding in people with dementia [9,11,12] and comparisons of survival for people with dementia who receive tube feeding versus oral intake of food and fluids [18,19]. Current expert opinions discourage the insertion of percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy for feeding in the setting of dementia in favour of comfort feeding [20]. But there is limited evidence describing survival following implementation of comfort feeding for this cohort, with acknowledged risk of aspiration on oral intake. In a study of 555 hospitalised participants who made a decision to eat and drink with acknowledged risk (EDAR), three-month survival was established to be 44% [21]. Twenty-one percent of these participants, who did not necessarily have a diagnosis of dementia, were receiving end-of-life care at the time of their EDAR decision and were therefore receiving comfort feeding.

In our study, 8% of the 95 participants who survived their first admission, were readmitted to hospital within 30 days of the original admission. The low readmission rate in this study may be influenced by the overall high mortality rate and higher proportion of participants discharged to residential care facilities (45%) compared with discharge home (7%). Oropharyngeal dysphagia in the context of dementia has previously been demonstrated to be closely associated with mortality and respiratory infection, with higher hospital readmission rates in participants without dysphagia compared to those with oropharyngeal dysphagia [6]. It has been hypothesised that lower hospital readmission rates for those with oropharyngeal dysphagia is due to higher rates of discharge to nursing homes and long-term care facilities and associated with their models of care and access to nursing and medical support [6].

There was a range of fluid consistencies recommended by the SLPs in this study: thin fluids, thickened fluids, ice chips and nil by mouth. These differing recommendations were distinguishable on the basis of patient characteristics and outcomes before and after the patient was discharged from hospital. This study was not able to determine whether the prognosis influenced the fluid consistency recommendation or vice versa although the severity of dysphagia did associate with the recommended fluid consistency and severity of dysphagia is a recognised prognostic indicator in patients with dementia [1,6,22].

Implications for practice and future research

Complex discussions about goals of care often arise when people living with dementia develop dysphagia. Part of the challenge of these conversations arises from the lack of evidence relating to outcomes specific to comfort feeding for this vulnerable population. Evidence from this study can support clinicians in the provision of realistic expectations regarding survival times and hospital readmissions and assist people with dementia and their families in making informed decisions about comfort feeding. Further research is required to understand the influence of comfort feeding compared to that of comorbidities on survival.

Limitations

Retrospective data collection influenced quality of the data. Dysphagia severity ratings were attributed based on results of usual SLP practice and documentation. There was no standardised tool or scale used for assessment of dysphagia or allocation of severity rating. Thirty-five participants had no dysphagia severity rating documented. Data were not collected on use of instrumental assessment of swallow (videofluoroscopic swallow study or fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallow). However, it is unlikely participants underwent instrumental assessment of swallow given the overall clinical scenario of consideration of comfort feeding in the context of dementia. Lastly, the degree of dementia, the quality of life and adherence to SLP diet and fluid recommendations after discharge from hospital were not assessed.

Conclusion

Dementia and comfort feeding were associated with high mortality rates, high rate of discharge to a supportive care facility, and low readmission rates. There was a significant association between dysphagia severity and the consistency of fluids recommended by the SLP. Of those who were discharged alive from hospital, a recommendation of a combination of thickened and thin fluids was associated with the highest readmission rate within 30 days of discharge and no particular comfort feeding plan was associated with a longer survival time than any other. These findings can inform clinical decision making and goals of care discussions in the acute care setting. Future work on comfort feeding must include the assessment of quality as well as quantity of life.
