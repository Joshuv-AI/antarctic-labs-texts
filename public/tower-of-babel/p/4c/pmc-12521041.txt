Methodological and Contextual Considerations in Assessing Internet Addiction and Eating Disorder Risk Among Bangladeshi Adolescents
Authors: Bilal Akbar, Rida Eman, Syed Ibad Ali, Maryum Maryum, Muhammad Umar, Fatima Laique
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundInternet addiction and eating disorders are emerging public health concerns among adolescents, particularly in South Asia. A recent study by Siddik et al. explored these issues among Bangladeshi adolescents but contained several methodological and theoretical limitations.ObjectiveTo critically evaluate Siddik et al.'s study on internet addiction and eating disorders among Bangladeshi adolescents, identifying methodological oversights and providing recommendations for future research.MethodsA critical appraisal approach was applied to review the study's design, data interpretation, and theoretical grounding. Specific focus areas included BMI classification, gender imbalance, psychosocial variables, and adherence to established behavioral models.ResultsThe critique revealed methodological shortcomings such as inappropriate BMI classification, lack of gender balance, omission of key psychosocial determinants, and absence of a guiding theoretical framework. These limitations may reduce the study's interpretive validity and cultural relevance.ConclusionFuture research should strengthen methodological rigor, ensure inclusion of relevant psychosocial factors, and incorporate robust theoretical models to enhance understanding of adolescent digital behavior and its mental health implications.

To the Editor,

I read with great interest the article by Siddik et al. [1], titled “Unraveling Internet Addiction Among Adolescents in Bangladesh and Its Association With the Risk of Eating Disorders, Body Mass Index, and Other Factors: A Cross‐Sectional Study.” The authors should be commended for their work on a nationwide survey that inquired about a major health issue among Bangladeshi teens. Nonetheless, I would like to highlight a few critical points that require further attention and discussion.

Foremost, the authors claim that a substantial favorable relation exists among internet addiction (IA), eating disorder (ED) risk, and body mass index (BMI). Although, the study does not investigate potential confounding variables such as emotional regulation, impulsivity, or body image dissatisfaction—factors that are well‐established in literature to influence these relationships [2, 3]. Hence, without considering these, the interpretation of observed associations remains limited.

Secondly, the research utilizes CDC BMI percentile classifications created for American youth and teenagers. Due to variations in body composition and growth patterns among different ethnicities, especially within South Asian groups, applying these cut‐offs could lead to misclassification [4]. Employing locally validated or WHO growth references would improve the cultural and clinical significance of BMI‐related outcomes.

Additionally, the study indicates a markedly gender‐skewed sample, with more than 70% of participants being female. Although logistic regression was used to account for gender, the dominance of one sex could skew the overall prevalence estimates of IA and ED risk. Presenting stratified results by gender would facilitate a more detailed analysis, especially given that gender is known to influence both IA and disordered eating patterns.

Furthermore, the evaluation of IA centers solely on how often and why the internet is used, neglecting qualitative aspects like screen content exposure, parental oversight, or sleep disruption—factors shown to impact IA severity and its psychological effects. Their exclusion restricts the contextual richness of the analysis.

Finally, the manuscript would gain from enhanced theoretical integration. Models like the Interaction of Person‐Affect‐Cognition‐Execution (I‐PACE) for IA, or the dual‐pathway model of EDs, may offer interpretive support for the detected relationships, enhancing the findings' explanatory strength and practical significance.

In conclusion, although the research offers significant insights into adolescent health in South Asia, subsequent studies should focus on more equitable sampling, culturally relevant metrics, and theoretical cohesion to enhance targeted interventions and policy development.
