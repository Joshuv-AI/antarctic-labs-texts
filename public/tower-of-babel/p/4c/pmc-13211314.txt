Low-value glucose monitoring in noncritically ill hospitalized patients
Authors: Niloofar Latifi, Nestoras Mathioudakis, Mohammed S. Abusamaan, Megan M. Tschudy, Jianqiao Ma, Sarah Olson, Terra M. M. Jouaneh, Angie Yang, Daniel J. Brotman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Inpatient routine point-of-care glucose (POC-G) monitoring is common given association of persistent hyperglycemia with increase in morbidity and mortality. However, some patients receive frequent fingerstick testing without needing insulin to reach glycemic targets.Objectives:Determine the prevalence and characteristics of noncritically ill inpatients undergoing POC-G monitoring who do not meet guideline criteria for hyperglycemia requiring insulin initiation.Methods:We performed a secondary analysis of noncritically ill adult inpatients (≥18 years) with at least four glucose measurements, discharged from five hospitals over 4.4 years. Exclusions included type 1 diabetes, admission glucose >500 mg/dL, long/intermediate-acting insulin use, stays ≤24 h, ICU admissions, or inpatient use of noninsulin diabetes drugs. The primary outcome was the maximum POC-G value during hospitalization.Results:Among 23,134 patients undergoing POC-G monitoring on correctional insulin monotherapy, 20.9% had all POC-G ≤ 140 mg/dL and averaged 9.5 POC-G tests per hospitalization. Patients with POC-G levels of 141–179 mg/dL underwent an average of 15.8 tests per hospitalization, with 75% receiving no insulin or only a one-time insulin administration. Overall, 149,479 (39%) of POC-G tests were conducted in patients with maximum glucose <180 mg/dL who did not meet guideline criteria for insulin initiation. The estimated labor and supply costs ranged from $241,483 to $826,868 during the study period—driven primarily by 0.8 to 1.4 FTEs annually dedicated to low-value POC-G testing.Conclusion:Low-value POC-G testing is common among non-critically ill inpatients on correctional insulin monotherapy. Reducing unnecessary monitoring can decrease patient discomfort and preserve healthcare resources.

INTRODUCTION

Inpatient hyperglycemia may result from pre-existing or undiagnosed diabetes, stress-induced hyperglycemia, or steroid use in diabetic and nondiabetic individuals. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) and Endocrine Society define hyperglycemia in hospitalized patients as glucose >140 mg/dL and recommend obtaining hemoglobin A1C (HgbA1C) in patients with diabetes or hyperglycemia if not done in the prior 3 months. Insulin therapy initiation is recommended for persistent hyperglycemia, defined as ≥2 glucose values ≥ 180 mg/dL within 24 h. After starting insulin, a target glucose range of 140–180 mg/dL is recommended for most noncritically ill patients, with a lower target of 100–180 mg/dL considered if achievable without hypoglycemia. Recommendations established for glycemic targets in patients with diabetes apply to those with stress- or steroid-induced hyperglycemia.1–4

Hospitals are encouraged to use standardized order sets that include point-of-care glucose (POC-G) monitoring before meals and at bedtime, or every 4–6 h for patients not eating or on continuous enteral or parenteral nutrition to avoid hyperglycemia.1–7 Once initiated, order sets are commonly continued even without hyperglycemia requiring insulin. This results in a subset of patients undergoing unnecessary and uncomfortable fingerstick POC-G monitoring throughout their hospitalization.8

Despite the widespread use of POC-G monitoring in hospitalized patients, limited evidence exists to inform patient selection and guide safe de-escalation strategies for those not meeting insulin initiation criteria. Recent studies suggesting the effectiveness of correctional insulin monotherapy (without basal or prandial insulin) in a subset of patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) provide insight into possible characteristics of this patient population. A multicenter retrospective study of noncritically ill hospitalized patients with T2D found that among those on correctional insulin monotherapy, 96% with admission glucose <140 mg/dL and 87% with 140–180 mg/dL achieved a mean glucose <180 mg/dL during admission.9 Additionally, 94% of patients with HgbA1C < 7% on admission receiving correctional insulin monotherapy achieved mean glucose <180 mg/dL.9 This study concluded that correctional insulin monotherapy is suitable for most non-critically ill inpatients with admission glucose levels <180 mg/dL. However, whether insulin treatment and frequent POC-G monitoring were required to achieve the target glucose range remains uncertain.

Our study aimed to determine the prevalence and characteristics of noncritically ill inpatients undergoing POC-G monitoring who do not meet guideline criteria for hyperglycemia requiring insulin administration during hospitalization. We also aimed to calculate the health system’s costs associated with low-value POC-G testing.

METHODS

Data source

We performed a secondary analysis of a retrospective cohort dataset derived from five academic and community hospitals in the Johns Hopkins Health System.10 The initial dataset was limited to noncritically ill inpatients ≥18 years old discharged between January 1, 2015, and May 31, 2019 with at least four glucose values. As we aimed to describe the prevalence of unnecessary POC-G monitoring, we excluded several patient groups from our analysis: patients with type 1 diabetes, admission glucose >500 mg/dL, individuals taking long or intermediate-acting insulin at home or in the hospital, and patients receiving non-insulin diabetes medications during hospitalization. Consequently, only patients not receiving diabetes treatment or those on correctional insulin monotherapy were included in the study. We also excluded serum glucose values obtained through routine phlebotomy and patients with admission duration ≤24 h or ICU stay. For patients with multiple hospitalizations, only the first admission was included (Supporting Information S1: eFigure 1).

Patient data were analyzed per calendar day for glucose values and insulin administration. Each correctional insulin dose was linked to the highest POC-G test result within 2 h before insulin administration. The correctional insulin scales were uniform across institutions, recommending administration starting at a glucose level of 141 mg/dL at mealtime and 251 mg/dL at bedtime, based on low, medium, and high-intensity scales (Supporting Information S1: eFigure 2). To account for variation in recommended insulin dose by scale intensity, we recorded frequency of insulin administration rather than dose.

The diagnosis of diabetes was captured from ICD-10 code entries in the electronic health record (EHR) problem lists or as a documented hospital diagnosis at the time of discharge (Supporting Information S1: eTable 1). HgbA1C values collected either during hospitalization or within the 90 days prior to admission were included.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the maximum POC-G value during hospitalization, used to divide patients into three groups. POC-G testing in Groups 1 and 2 was considered “low value” for this study.

Group 1: Patients without hyperglycemia (max POC-G ≤ 140 mg/dL) during hospitalization. These individuals never reached the institutional or guideline threshold for correctional insulin administration.

Group 1: Patients without hyperglycemia (max POC-G ≤ 140 mg/dL) during hospitalization. These individuals never reached the institutional or guideline threshold for correctional insulin administration.

Group 2: Patients with hyperglycemia (POC-G > 140 mg/dL) who remained at the target inpatient glucose range (max POC-G 141–179 mg/dL) during hospitalization. These individuals reached the institutional threshold for correctional insulin administration without meeting the guideline criteria for insulin initiation.

Group 2: Patients with hyperglycemia (POC-G > 140 mg/dL) who remained at the target inpatient glucose range (max POC-G 141–179 mg/dL) during hospitalization. These individuals reached the institutional threshold for correctional insulin administration without meeting the guideline criteria for insulin initiation.

Group 3: Patients with any POC-G value ≥ 180 mg/dL during hospitalization. Patients with at least one value ≥ 180 mg/dL were classified as meeting minimal guideline criteria for insulin initiation though the ADA recommends therapy for ≥2 values ≥ 180 mg/dL in 24 h.

Group 3: Patients with any POC-G value ≥ 180 mg/dL during hospitalization. Patients with at least one value ≥ 180 mg/dL were classified as meeting minimal guideline criteria for insulin initiation though the ADA recommends therapy for ≥2 values ≥ 180 mg/dL in 24 h.

Secondary outcome

The secondary outcome was cost from the health system perspective attributable to low-value POC-G testing. Total low-value tests were calculated by multiplying the number of patients in Groups 1 and 2 by the mean tests per hospitalization for each group.

Depending on the hospital, Certified Nurse Assistants (CNAs) or Registered Nurses (RNs) often perform POC-G testing. We used data from the 2023 US Bureau of Labor Statistics to obtain mean hourly salaries of RNs and CNAs working on medical and surgical hospital floors and updated salaries with the US Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI inflation calculator for January 2025.11–13 Prior studies estimated that performing a POC-G test requires 3–5 min of staff time.14,15 The hourly wages multiplied by the time required for POC-G tests determined the minimum labor cost (CNA testing in 3 min) and the maximum labor cost (RN testing in 5 min).

Consumable costs of lancets and test strips vary based on an individual institution’s confidential negotiated contracts with their suppliers. We used publicly available list prices from three top suppliers of hospital-grade lancets and test strips (Novabiomedical, Abbott, and Roche Diagnostics) and prior studies with estimated costs for these items to estimate a range of $0.59–$1.48 for consumable supplies per POC-G test.16–19

Statistical analysis

Patient-level and hospital-stay characteristics were compared between groups, with a subgroup analysis conducted for patients with an ICD-10 code-based diabetes diagnosis (Supporting Information S1: eTable 1). Data were summarized as means or medians for continuous variables and as proportions for categorical variables. Continuous data were analyzed using a one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal–Wallis test for variables with unequal variance or skewed distributions. Categorical data were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s Exact test. Statistical significance was defined as p < .05. Analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software: Release 18: Stata Corp, LLC.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

Among 23,134 noncritically ill inpatients receiving POC-G testing on correctional insulin monotherapy, 4836 (20.9%) had all POC-G ≤ 140 mg/dL (Group 1), 6553 (28.3%) patients had a maximum POC-G > 140 and <180 mg/dL (Group 2), and 11,745 (50.8%) patients had at least one POC-G ≥ 180 mg/dL (Group 3) during hospitalization.

The patient characteristics of each group are shown in Table 1. The mean age of the patient population was 63.9 years, with 52.4% female and 32.3% Black patients. There were significant differences in age and BMI by outcome group (p < .001) with younger patients with lower BMI in Group 1. The median length of stay in the hospital was 4.2 days overall without significant difference by outcome group (p = .07).

Diabetes diagnosis, obtained from EHR ICD-10 codes, was seen in 25.6% of the total cohort—group 1 had the lowest percentage at 10.6% compared to Group 3 at 35.3%. The cohort’s mean HgbA1C was 6.5%. HgbA1C was obtained during hospitalization or within 90 days of admission in 5631 (31%) of patients with at least one POC-G > 140 mg/dL. There was a statistically significant difference in the mean admission POC-G value among groups: 100.4 (SD 20.2) in Group 1, 118.5 (SD 29.2) in Group 2, and 155.4 (SD 60.4) in Group 3 (p < .001). The mean difference in the first POC-G obtained in the combined Group 1 and Group 2 (low-value testing) compared to Group 3 patients was 44.6 mg/dL (95% CI 43.4–45.8).

Glucose monitoring and insulin administration

During hospitalization, patients in Group 1 had a median of 6 (IQR 3–11) and a mean of 9.5 (SD 10.7) POC-G tests despite having all glucose values ≤140 mg/dL. Group 2 patients with maximum glucose values of 141–179 mg/dL underwent a median of 11 (IQR 7–19) and a mean of 15.8 (SD 16.3) tests. In contrast, Group 3, which had at least one glucose value ≥180 mg/dL, underwent a median of 14 (IQR 9–24) tests, with a mean of 20.3 (SD 22.6) (Figure 1). The difference in the number of POC-G tests across the three groups was statistically significant (p < .001).

The number of times insulin was administered during hospitalization differed significantly across the three groups (p < .001). Group 1 had a median of 0 administrations (IQR 0–0), Group 2 had a median of 0 (IQR 0–1), and Group 3 had a median of 3 (IQR 0–7). These values represent the total hospitalization median frequency of insulin administrations, not the total units administered, as patients could have been on correctional insulin scales of variable intensity (Supporting Information S1: eFigure 2).

Hypoglycemia (POC-G <70 mg/dL) was observed in 1261 patients (5.5%) within 24 h of admission, with 138 (10.9%) receiving at least one unit of insulin and 1123 (89.1%) not receiving insulin (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis

Among 5914 patients with an ICD-10 code-based diagnosis of diabetes (excluding type 1 diabetes), 8.6% (513) were in Group 1, 21.2% (1258) in Group 2, and 70.1% (4143) in Group 3 (Table 2). Mean HgbA1C increased from 5.7% in Group 1 to 6.8% in Group 3, with a higher proportion of patients in each group having a HgbA1C measured during hospitalization or within 90 days compared to the total cohort (30%, 35%, and 40% in Groups 1–3).

Patients in Group 1 underwent a mean of 10.1 (SD 8.4) POC-G tests and received a median of 0 correctional insulin doses. Group 2 patients had a mean of 14.6 (SD 12.8) POC-G tests and a median of 0 (IQR 0–1) insulin administrations. Group 3 patients underwent the most intensive monitoring, with a mean of 19.8 (SD 19.1) POC-G tests and a median of 4 (IQR 2–8) correctional insulin doses per admission.

Hypoglycemia (POC-G <70 mg/dL) occured in 330 patients (5.6%) within 24 h of admission, with 46 (13.9%) receiving at least one unit of insulin and 284 (86.1%) not receiving insulin (Table 2).

Cost analysis

During the study period, 149,479 POC-G tests were performed in Group 1 and 2 patients (Table 3). Based on an estimated 3–5 min of staff time per test, over the 4.4-year study period, approximately 7474–12,457 staff hours (0.8–1.4 full-time equivalent annually) were dedicated to low-value POC-G testing. The estimated labor and supply costs of testing in Groups 1 and 2 during the study period were $241,483–$826,868 or $21–$73 per patient.

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter study of noncritically ill inpatients on correctional insulin monotherapy undergoing POC-G testing, 11,389 patients (49%) had no POC-G values ≥ 180 mg/dL requiring insulin initiation per guidelines during admission. Despite this, patients underwent a substantial number of low-value fingersticks, with 45,942 POC-G tests conducted on patients whose glucose values were always ≤140 mg/dL, and 103,537 tests on patients with maximum glucose values between 141 and 179 mg/dL during hospitalization. Although the latter group had at least one glucose value meeting the institutional threshold for initiation of correctional insulin (glucose >140 mg/dL), 75% of these patients either never received insulin or only received insulin once during hospitalization. In other words, no patients in Group 1 benefited from the average of 9.5 fingersticks per hospitalization, and most of the patients in Group 2 would have met target inpatient glucose goals without requiring an average of 15.8 fingersticks per hospitalization. A similar burden of low-value POC-G testing was identified in the subgroup analysis of patients with a documented diagnosis of diabetes.

The guideline-indicated test for patients with diabetes or those with a glucose value > 140 mg/dL (Groups 2–3) is a HgbA1C.1–3 However, only 38% of patients with diabetes and 31% of all patients with a POC-G >140 mg/dL had a HgbA1C test within 3 months of admission or during hospitalization. This finding is concordant with prior studies indicating poor adherence to obtaining an up-to-date HgbA1C, which ranges from 18% to 58%.20–22 HgbA1C values reflecting pre-existing diabetes are valuable for inpatient glycemic management and postdischarge care, including consideration of diabetes therapy intensification.1,4 Among patients without a prior diabetes diagnosis who develop stress- or steroid-induced hyperglycemia during hospitalization, a normal HgbA1C suggests the hyperglycemia is transient. In these cases, HgbA1C can be used to tailor inpatient glucose monitoring, especially with steroid initiation.23,24 For example, the Joint British Diabetes Society (JBDS) recommends that in patients without pre-existing diabetes who are initiated on steroids, once-daily glucose monitoring—preferably prior to lunch or dinner to capture prandial hyperglycemia—is appropriate. Only if glucose exceeds 12 mmol/L (216 mg/dL) should testing be increased to four times daily and insulin therapy considered; otherwise, once-daily monitoring can be maintained.23

A small subset of patients in this study had a glucose value <70 mg/dL on admission or within 24 h of hospitalization, potentially prompting additional POC-G monitoring for the indication of hypoglycemia. In the 5.5% of all patients who initially presented with hypoglycemia, spontaneous episodes occurring without insulin administration were noted in 96% of Group 1 and 91% of Group 2 patients. Among all patients who experienced any hypoglycemia during their admission, spontaneous cases predominated, comprising 98% in Group 1 and 84% in Group 2. While previous studies have shown that both patients with and without diabetes with hypoglycemia experience higher in-hospital mortality, further adjustment for demographics and comorbidities revealed no direct association between spontaneous hypoglycemia and mortality.25,26 This suggests that spontaneous hypoglycemia is a marker of acute illness severity rather than a direct contributor to mortality. Accordingly, close monitoring and treatment of spontaneous hypoglycemia may not confer clinical benefit beyond management of underlying conditions contributing to risk of spontaneous hypoglycemia.25–27 Current guidelines focus on management of hypoglycemia by reducing iatrogenic etiology without specific recommendations for tailored monitoring and management of spontaneous hypoglycemia.1–3

Although the higher prevalence of hypoglycemia on admission in Group 1 (6.9%) and Group 2 (4.9%) contributes to lower mean admission glucose in these groups, admission glucose values remain valuable for further exploration in assessing the need for correctional insulin therapy and frequent glucose monitoring during hospitalization. Prior randomized trials supporting the use of basal-bolus regimen over correctional insulin therapy have been limited to patients with a mean admission blood glucose >180 mg/dL in the correctional insulin monotherapy arm.28–31 However, in a more recent study of patients with T2D, admission blood glucose values < 180 mg/dL were found to be associated with successful glycemic management with correctional insulin monotherapy.9 In our study, patients with and without diabetes in Groups 1 and 2 (max glucose <180 mg/dL), had mean admission glucose values ≤ 119 mg/dL and a mean difference in admission glucose of 45 mg/dL compared to Group 3 patients. Lower glucose variability in patients with early-stage T2D managed by diet or non-insulin regimens likely explains the correlation between admission blood glucose and subsequent hospital measurements.32

Prospective identification of patients who do not clinically benefit from frequent POC-G testing to de-escalate monitoring may confer multiple benefits. In our study, 0.8–1.4 FTE of nursing time per year was spent on POC-G testing with minimal to no value added to patients, diverting resources from potentially higher-value patient care activities. The labor and supply cost per patient receiving low-value monitoring is estimated at $21–$73 per hospitalization. Notable unaccounted health systems costs in this study include the expense of utilizing single-patient insulin pens for those who required insulin only once during admission, followed by the disposal of the pen at discharge. Additionally, though challenging to quantify, workflow interruptions and patient discomfort from frequent fingerstick tests are essential factors to consider in the value equation of glucose monitoring.

Our study’s strengths include a multicenter analysis with a diverse patient population, including 30% black patients, providing a large representative cohort sufficient to explore group differences. Its limitations are its retrospective nature, reliance on EHR data not primarily collected for research, and identification of T2D through ICD-10 codes with potential to underestimate the prevalence of T2D in the study population.10 Additionally, testing in Group 2 was considered low-value, even though patients had at least one hyperglycemic episode that met the threshold of inpatient hyperglycemia (glucose >140 mg/dL). Although de-escalation of testing in this group could miss episodic hyperglycemia, due to the uncertain clinical significance of intermittent hyperglycemia below 180 mg/dL in inpatient settings, further research is needed to determine whether implementing safeguards would impact patient-centered outcomes. Lastly, we acknowledge that a small subset of patients may not have had correctional insulin therapy orders, as their monitoring may have been primarily for hypoglycemia. However, given that over 90% of patients in both Groups 1 and 2 with initial hypoglycemia exhibited spontaneous episodes, the overall utility of POC-G monitoring in the absence of modifiable iatrogenic causes remains limited. Our future research aims to create prediction models for prospective identification of patients for whom reduced POC-G monitoring is safe.

CONCLUSION

In this multicenter study of noncritically ill inpatients receiving POC-G monitoring on correctional insulin monotherapy, we observed that 49% of patients received low-value POC-G tests with 45,942 POC-G tests on patients with all glucose values ≤ 140 mg/dL and 103,537 tests on patients with maximum glucose values of 141–179 mg/dL during hospitalization. The prevalence of low-value glucose monitoring warrants further studies to identify an appropriate patient population for de-escalating testing.
