Clinical benefits of right-lumen fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided double-lumen tube placement in thoracic surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Qinyu Bao, Zhenghuan Song, Mengyue Zhu, Shiyi Huang, Jiaqin Cai, Yihu Zhou, Lijun Wang, Jing Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSingle-lung ventilation is frequently required in thoracic surgery, with double-lumen tubes (DLTs) serving as the standard device for lung isolation. However, DLT placement and accurate positioning remain technically challenging, and may cause airway trauma and postoperative discomfort that affect patient satisfaction. This study aims to compare the clinical efficacy of a novel fiberoptic bronchoscope (FOB)-guided intubation technique via the right lumen of left-sided DLTs with conventional blind intubation and left-lumen FOB-guided placement.MethodsThis prospective study enrolled 120 patients [aged 18–79 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status II–III] undergoing elective thoracic surgery requiring right one-lung ventilation. Participants were randomized into Group C (n=40) undergoing conventional blind intubation, Group R (n=40) receiving FOB-guided placement through the right lumen of left-sided DLTs (L-DLTs) and Group L (n=40) receiving FOB-guided placement via the left lumen of DLTs. The primary outcome of this study was the total DLT placement time (T-total), defined as the interval from insertion of the DLT through the lips to confirmation of correct anatomical positioning by fiberoptic bronchoscopy. Secondary efficacy outcomes included the final positioning time (T3), the other procedural time components (T1 and T2), and the first-attempt success rate of DLT placement. Safety outcomes comprised peri-intubation hemodynamic changes and airway mucosal injury assessed bronchoscopically, while patient-reported outcomes included postoperative throat symptoms (hoarseness, postoperative pharyngalgia, and odynophagia) and patient satisfaction.ResultsThe primary outcome, total DLT placement time (T-total), was significantly shorter in Group R than in Groups L and C (P<0.05). Group R also showed a significantly reduced T3 compared with the other two groups (P<0.05). No significant differences were observed among the three groups for T1 and T2. The tracheal injury score and the severity of airway mucosal injury in Group R were markedly lower than those in Group C (P<0.05). On postoperative day 1, both the incidence and severity of postoperative pharyngalgia in Group R were significantly reduced compared to Group C (P<0.05). Additionally, Group R exhibited a statistically significant decrease in the incidence and severity of postoperative pharyngalgia compared with Group C, and patients in Group R also reported the highest satisfaction scores among the three groups (P<0.01).ConclusionsCompared to conventional blind intubation and FOB-guided placement through the left lumen of L-DLTs, the right-lumen FOB-guided DLT positioning technique not only simplified the intubation process and significantly reduced the total DLT placement time, but also decreased patient-reported postoperative discomfort (postoperative pharyngalgia and hoarseness), minimized airway mucosal injury, and enhanced overall recovery experience. These findings suggest that the right-lumen technique may be considered a feasible alternative in selected clinical settings when performed by experienced anesthesiologists. However, further multicenter studies are required to validate these results and to determine their generalizability to broader patient populations and routine clinical practice.Trial RegistrationThe study was registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (www.chictr.org.cn; 21/06/2021; ChiCTR2100047678).

Introduction

Double-lumen tubes (DLTs) are considered the default choice for lung isolation in most adults, as they have an excellent quality of lung collapse and good airway suction ability (1). The left main bronchi (LMB; average length: 5.3 cm) is longer than the right main bronchi (RMB; average length: 2.7 cm) (2). Given the wide safety margin of left-sided DLTs (L-DLTs), the strategy of placing DLTs within the LMB and it is generally adopted in most surgery with one lung ventilation (OLV). However, the placement and positioning of DLTs can be challenging. The external diameter of DLTs is larger and stiffer. During passage through the glottis, the orifice of DLT might imping on the arytenoids and the posterior larynx. Moreover, the average inclination angle of the LMB is 108.4° (3). Improper rotation can lead to bending and deformation of the DLTs. These factors may inhibit advancement during placement of the DLTs and can often lead to damage to the trachea and mucosa (4). Therefore, many experts and scholars advocate that fiberoptic bronchoscopy (FOB) can be used to guide the placement of the DLTs during intubation (5,6).

Consequently, our study aims to compare the three methods of placement of a L-DLT via FOB-guided in tracheal and bronchial lumen as well as conventional intubation in the differences of intubation time, intubation success rate and postoperative pharyngeal complications, in order to provide more clinical evidence for a safer, more effective and less invasive intubation method. We present this article in accordance with the CONSORT reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2350/rc) (7).

Methods

This single-center, prospective randomized controlled trial was conducted at a major tertiary cancer center in China between September 2021 and September 2023. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Cancer Hospital on April 29, 2021 (No. 2021 ke-002) and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants. The study was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100047678). Participants were recruited through hospital advertisements. After providing written informed consent, eligible patients were randomly assigned to the Group C (blind intubation, n=40), Group R (FOB-guided intubation through the right lumen of the left DLT, n=40) or Group L (FOB-guided intubation through the left lumen of the left DLT, n=40) using computer-generated codes in sealed opaque envelopes. This study included patients who scheduled for elective thoracoscopic surgery using L-DLT intubation for right lung isolation. The inclusion criteria were: (I) age ≥18 years; (II) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade II–III. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) severe mental illness or communication difficulties; (II) difficult airway assessed by anesthesiologists, including the following situations (body mass index >30 kg/m2, mouth opening <3 cm, limited movement of the neck, loosening of incisor or when the head extended, the pleural distance was less than 6 or 12.5 cm); (III) history of gastroesophageal reflux; (IV) history of pulmonary surgery; (V) duration of tracheal intubation exceeding 6 hours or less than 1 hour; (VI) admission to ICU after operation.

Randomization and allocation concealment

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random allocation sequence created with STATA software. The random sequences were concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes prepared by an independent researcher who was not involved in patient recruitment, intervention, or outcome assessment. After obtaining written informed consent, an investigator who was not involved in sequence generation opened the next envelope in numerical order and assigned the patient to the corresponding group. Simple randomization without block randomization or stratification was used. Due to the nature of the intervention, the anesthesiologists performing the intubation procedures were aware of group allocation; however, data collectors and outcome assessors were blinded to treatment assignment.

Blinding and bias mitigation

Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of the anesthesiologists performing the intubation procedures was not feasible. To mitigate potential performance and detection bias, several measures were implemented. All intubations were performed by the same team of experienced attending anesthesiologists following a strictly standardized anesthesia and airway management protocol, thereby minimizing inter-operator variability. Allocation concealment was maintained until immediately before anesthesia induction using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, reducing the risk of selection bias and pre-procedural behavioral modification. Objective procedural time points were recorded by an independent observer who was not involved in the intervention. Airway mucosal injury was assessed bronchoscopically, and postoperative throat-related symptoms were evaluated by outcome assessors blinded to group allocation.

Anesthetic management

Preoperative DLT size selection was based on left main bronchus diameter (d) measured from axial computed tomography (CT) images by a single attending radiologist: Fr32 for d <11 mm, Fr35 for 11 mm ≤ d <13 mm, and Fr37 for d ≥13 mm (8). All DLT cuffs were lubricated preoperatively using sterile medical-grade lubricant.

All L-DLTs were inserted under videolaryngoscopic guidance by attending anesthesiologists with extensive experience (>300 procedures). The initial insertion was performed under continuous videolaryngoscopy, with the introducer stylet removed immediately after the bronchial cuff passed the glottis. Subsequent positioning strategies diverged based on group allocation.

In Group R, the DLT was advanced until the main tracheal cuff traversed the glottis, after which a FOB was inserted through the right lumen to visualize the tracheal carina (Figure 2A). Under direct bronchoscopic visualization, the tube tip was precisely guided into the left main bronchus while simultaneously confirming optimal depth, thereby achieving single-step anatomical positioning (9) (Figure 2B).

Group R intubation procedures. (A) Group R, step 1. (B) Group R, step 2. Group R: fiberoptic bronchoscope-guidedplacement through the right lumen of left-sided double-lumen tubes.

In Group L, the L-DLT was advanced until the main cuff passed through the glottis. A FOB was then introduced through the left lumen of the DLT and guided into the left main bronchus (Figure 3A). Under FOB visualization, the DLT tip was advanced into the left main bronchus (Figure 3B). Subsequently, the FOB was reinserted through the right lumen to visualize the tracheal carina. Final adjustments to the DLT depth were performed under direct FOB observation to ensure precise positioning (Figure 3C) (10).

Group L intubation procedures. (A) Group L, step 1. (B) Group L, step 2. (C) Group L, step 3. Group L: fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided placement via the left lumen of double-lumen.

For Group C, blind rotational maneuvers were implemented: a 180° counterclockwise rotation facilitated tracheal descent, where as a subsequent 90° clockwise rotation oriented the tube toward the left bronchus. Final positioning was confirmed via right-lumen FOB verification. In cases where right bronchial misplacement occurred, the protocol mandated partial withdrawal to supraglottic position, combined with 30° right head rotation and reattempted positioning; persistent malposition triggered protocol escalation to the Group L methodology (11).

Correct L-DLT placement was confirmed when fiberoptic bronchoscopy through the right lumen revealed: (I) the superior margin of the optimally inflated blue bronchial cuff precisely aligned with the tracheal carina; (II) complete sealing of the left main bronchial orifice without carinal or right bronchial obstruction; and (III) unobstructed visualization of both the carinal bifurcation and right main bronchial opening (12).

To prevent tube malposition, fiberoptic bronchoscopic verification was performed after lateral decubitus positioning to reconfirm proper DLT placement. All intubations were executed by a single attending anesthesiologist credentialed in advanced airway management (>300 videolaryngoscopic intubations).

After the surgery, the tracheal tube was removed after the patient’s consciousness was clear, breathing and circulation were stable, muscle strength and various protective reflexes were restored. Intubating data were recorded by an independent observer.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the total DLT placement time (T-total), defined as the interval from GlideScope® blade-mediated pre-curved left DLT passage through the lips to confirmed anatomical positioning. This duration was subdivided into three phases: T1: initiation of tube insertion to epiglottic visualization via videolaryngoscopy; T2: intubation commencement to glottic passage of the main tracheal cuff; T3: subsequent advancement to final positioning.

The secondary outcome was postpositioning airway mucosal injury, assessed by a blinded otolaryngologist using a validated 5-tier bronchoscopic grading scale: grade 0 (intact mucosa), 1 (scattered petechiae), 2 (confluent petechiae), 3 (erosions), and 4 (severe structural damage).

Safety outcomes included the first-attempt success rate and hemodynamic parameters recorded at baseline (T0), 1 min (T1), 3 min (T2), and 5 min (T3) after intubation and DLT-related complications. Postoperatively, an independent anesthesiologist blinded to group allocation documented voice hoarseness, odynophagia, and dysphagia severity for four consecutive days. Throat discomfort was quantified via visual analogue scale (VAS) (0= no pain; mild= 1–3; moderate= 4–6; severe= 7–10). Hoarseness was classified as absent [0], subjective [1], observed by the anesthesiologist [2] or aphonic [3]. Patient satisfaction was assessed on postoperative day 4 using a 10-cm vertical VAS, labelled at the bottom as “not satisfied at all” [0] and at the top as “very satisfied” [10].

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation

Our preliminary pilot data revealed mean total DLT placement times of 74.4±32.25 s in Group R, 80.6±26.75 s in Group L, and 61.8±24.63 s in Group C. An a priori power analysis was conducted based on the largest observed difference, namely between Group R and Group C, for the predefined primary outcome of total DLT placement time. This conservative approach was adopted to ensure sufficient power to detect clinically meaningful differences among groups.

Using Cohen’s d estimated from the pilot mean values, a minimum sample size of 34 patients per group was calculated to detect a 20% difference with a power of 80% and a two-sided α level of 0.05. Although nonparametric tests were planned for the final analysis due to non-normal data distribution, Cohen’s d was applied at the study design stage as an approximation based on pilot data, which is a common practice in clinical research. Allowing for an anticipated 20% attrition rate, the final target sample size was increased to 43 patients per group.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All datasets were confirmed to be independent. Normality of continuous variables was assessed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared among the three groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with subsequent pairwise comparisons performed by the Mann-Whitney U test applying Bonferroni adjustment to control for multiple testing. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition to P values, effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported for primary and key secondary comparisons. For continuous, non-normally distributed variables (e.g., time outcomes, injury scores), the Hodges-Lehmann median difference with its 95% CI was calculated. For binary outcomes (e.g., first-attempt success, injury presence), the risk difference with its 95% CI is presented.

Results

A total of 129 patients scheduled for thoracic surgery were initially screened for eligibility. Seven patients were excluded for failing to meet the predefined inclusion criteria. Subsequently, 122 patients were enrolled; however, two were excluded from the analysis—one due to an intraoperative duration exceeding 6 hours, and the other due to postoperative transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU). Ultimately, 120 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1).

Baseline and intraoperative characteristics

No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in clinical and demographic characteristics, upper airway anatomical features, or intraoperative parameters, including tracheal cuff pressure (white cuff), bronchial cuff pressure (blue cuff), surgical duration, anesthesia duration, or tube indwelling time (P>0.05) (Table 1). No statistically significant differences in systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and heart rate (HR) among the three patient groups at the four time points (P>0.05) (Figure 4).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial comprehensively compared FOB-guided intubation via different lumens of the DLT with conventional blind intubation. Our results demonstrate that the right-lumen approach (Group R) provided clear advantages in intubation efficiency, first-attempt success, airway protection, and patient satisfaction compared with both the left-lumen approach (Group L) and blind insertion (Group C).

Currently, two main approaches are used for FOB-guided DLT placement, differing primarily in whether the bronchoscope is advanced through the bronchial or tracheal lumen. These approaches vary in visualization quality, procedural complexity, and efficiency, which may influence intubation time, success rate, and airway safety.

In our study, the double-lumen intubation (DLT) guided by the right bronchial lumen (Group R) using FOB showed a significant advantage in intubation efficiency: the median total intubation time was only 54 s (IQR, 43.3–63 s), which is 31% shorter than when FOB was placed to guide the left bronchial lumen (Group L, 78 s, IQR, 58–94 s; P<0.001). Compared to traditional blind intubation (Group C, 66 s, IQR, 52–97 s; P=0.008), it was 18% shorter. This difference mainly stems from the time difference in the final positioning phase (T3). Group R achieved single-step precise positioning through continuous direct observation of the tracheal carina and bilateral bronchial openings via the right bronchus (T3: 28.5 s). In Group L, although FOB guidance improved the accuracy of DLT positioning, there were some reasons for longer duration, such as when the angle between the left main bronchus and the trachea is too large, the flexible FOB can enter the left bronchus but cannot effectively guide the rigid catheter tip through anatomical turns, leading to the catheter getting stuck above the cricoid cartilage (13). Limited visualization forced the operator to repeatedly withdraw the FOB and switch to the right lumen to confirm position, significantly increasing the risk of surgical time and mucosal injury (14). Correcting the maneuver requires alternating between bilateral bronchial lumens to confirm position, which increases operational complexity and significantly prolongs the operation time (T3: 50 s). Of particular note, while blind insertion (Group C) avoids the time-consuming issues associated with FOB, its positioning method can easily lead to misalignment of the right main bronchus, necessitating a switch to Group L for correction. In contrast, the right lobe view throughout the procedure uses the cricoid cartilage as a central landmark, eliminating the need for interlobar visual switching and simultaneously monitoring the cricoid cartilage and bilateral bronchial openings. Additionally, the single-lumen continuous visualization technique in Group R simplifies the surgical process, forming an “insertion-positioning” single workflow that overcomes the efficiency limitations of traditional guidance methods.

This study demonstrates that the success rate of initial attempts using a right-side guided FOB for DLT insertion (Group R) is significantly higher than that of traditional blind insertion (Group C), with success rates of 85% and 62.5%, respectively (P=0.02). However, no statistically significant difference was observed when compared to left-side guidance (Group L), which had a success rate of 80% (P=0.55). Previous research has indicated that the failure rate of blind insertion is higher for left-side DLTs, a phenomenon attributed to the anatomical complexity of adult airways (15). The anatomical features of adult airways, including the length, width, angle, and morphology of tracheal bifurcations, can influence the ease of DLT insertion (16,17). Furthermore, variables such as the patient’s body type, airway flexibility, and potential anatomical variations increase the likelihood of catheter deviation from the intended path during blind insertion, resulting in intubation failure or necessitating multiple attempts (18). Even when conducted by experienced thoracic anesthesiologists, the success rate of blind insertion remains limited to 63% (19), similar to the 62.5% success rate observed in Group C. In contrast, FOB guidance significantly improves localization accuracy through real-time visualization of the airway anatomy, effectively preventing common right bronchial misinsertion in blind insertion techniques (20).

In this study, airway mucosal injury associated with three types of double-lumen bronchial tube insertion techniques were also evaluated. The results showed that the right-lumen fiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided technique (Group R) performed best in tracheal protection, with a median tracheal injury score of 0 (IQR, 0–1), significantly better than the traditional blind insertion group (Group C) with a score of 1 (IQR, 0–2) (P=0.006). No severe injuries at grade 3/4 were observed, and the rate of grade 2 injuries was only 7.5% (3/40), far lower than Group C 27.5% (11/40). Notably, although the median score of the left-lumen guided group (Group L) was the same as Group R [0 (IQR, 0–1), P=0.53], its 22.5% rate of grade 2 injuries suggested that limited visualization might lead to more local friction. Further bronchial injury assessments confirmed the advantages of Group R: the left main bronchus had a 17.5% injury rate, significantly lower than Group C 40% (P=0.02) and Group L 42.5% (P=0.01); the right main bronchus achieved zero injuries, outperforming Group C 15% (P=0.003) and Group L 7.5% (P=0.03); the injury rate of the hilum was also significantly lower than Group L 12.5%. Postoperative complication analysis indicated that the 85% success rate on the first attempt in Group R (better than the 35–48% readjustment rate for blind insertion) was directly related to its lower airway injury. Research has confirmed that vocal cord injury, laryngeal edema, improper catheter size, excessive cuff pressure, and prolonged placement time are the primary causes of postoperative pharyngalgia and hoarseness (21,22). We used image-guided selection of standardized catheters and maintained comparable cuff pressure/sustained duration across groups, which suggests that differences in the incidence of hoarseness may be due to multiple attempts at insertion and adjustment frequency (23). The technical advantage of Group R lies in precise direct visualization, reducing mechanical friction and avoiding airway mucosal injury caused by repeated adjustments. Importantly, this reduction in airway mucosal injury translated into improved postoperative recovery. Group R patients reported fewer throat symptoms, including postoperative pharyngalgia and hoarseness, compared with Group C (P<0.05). Consistently, patient satisfaction scores were highest in Group R (6.86±0.94), significantly greater than those in Group L (6.39±0.92) and Group C (5.57±1.04) (P<0.001). These findings indicate that minimizing airway mucosal injury, not only improves procedural safety but also enhances patient comfort and satisfaction, underscoring the broader clinical value of the right-lumen FOB-guided technique.

Limitations

There are several limitations in this study. First, it was a single-center investigation, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, all intubations were performed by highly experienced anesthesiologists; while this minimized operator-related variability in this technically demanding procedure, it restricts applicability to less experienced practitioners. Third, patients with anticipated difficult airways were excluded to standardize the procedural environment and reduce anatomical confounding, limiting extrapolation to complex airway scenarios. Additionally, operator blinding was not feasible, though standardized protocols, objective timing endpoints, and blinded outcome assessment were used to mitigate potential bias. Finally, the sample size may have been insufficient to detect rare adverse events, and long-term airway outcomes were not evaluated. Future multicenter studies including a broader range of operators and patient populations are warranted to confirm generalizability.

Conclusions

Compared with conventional blind insertion and left-lumen FOB-guided DLT placement, the right-lumen FOB-guided approach was associated with a higher first-attempt success rate, shorter procedural time, more accurate positioning, and a lower incidence of airway mucosal injury in this single-center randomized study. These findings suggest that the right-lumen technique may be considered a feasible alternative in selected clinical settings when performed by experienced anesthesiologists. However, further multicenter studies are required to validate these results and to determine their generalizability to broader patient populations and routine clinical practice.
