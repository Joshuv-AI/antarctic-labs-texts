Machine learning-accelerated screening of hydroquinone analogs for proton-coupled electron transfer
Authors: Rajdeep Sarma, Yiwen Wang, David D. Hebert, Ethan Tran, Chenrui Shao, Sijie Fu, Ilkwon Cho, Olexandr Isayev, Isaac Garcia-Bosch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) mediated by hydroquinone and related molecules is key to natural and artificial energy conversion. The reactivity of these molecules depends on their bond dissociation free energy (BDFE), but studying the relationship between structure and thermochemistry across this chemical space has been limited by challenging experimental setup and high computational expense. Here, we present the first use of the AIMNet2 neural network potential to calculate average BDFE (BDFEavg) values for the 2H+/2e− dehydrogenation of about 200 000 hydroquinone-like compounds, including vicinal diamines, diols, and dithiols. Benchmarking against DFT calculations for 168 substituted ortho-phenylenediamines (opda) shows good agreement (R2 ∼ 0.84). Our analysis finds that the BDFEavg of diamines ranges from 50 to 80 kcal mol−1 and can be systematically tuned by modifying the backbone and N-substitution: electron-withdrawing groups raise BDFEavg by up to 15 kcal mol−1, while lower aromaticity in furan and thiophene backbones decreases BDFEavg by approximately 10 kcal mol−1 compared to the phenyl systems (∼65 kcal mol−1). Validation through cyclic voltammetry and reactivity studies with quinone oxidants for selected compounds supports the computational results. This extensive thermochemical database and a web-based prediction tool developed as a result of this work will offer valuable resources for designing PCET reagents for catalysis, energy storage, and biomedical uses.

Introduction

Proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) reactions are chemical transformations that involve the transfer of protons and electrons.1,2 These fundamental reactions are involved in many natural and artificial processes, including the reduction and oxidation of small molecules (O2 and N2 reduction, water oxidation, etc.) and chemical synthesis.3,4 The simplest PCET transformation is one that involves the transfer of one proton and one electron from a donor, X–H, to an acceptor, Y˙ (reaction between X–H and Y˙ to form X˙ and Y–H). The thermochemistry (Gibbs free energy, ΔG°) and kinetics (Gibbs free energy of activation, ΔG‡) of these 1H+/1e− PCET transformations are usually, but not always, determined by the difference between the BDFE (bond dissociation free energy) of the X–H bond broken and the BDFE of the Y–H bond formed (Fig. 1A).5 The bigger is the difference between the BDFE of the bond broken (BDFEXH) and the BDFE of the bond formed (BDFEYH), the higher is the thermodynamic tendency for that PCET reaction to occur (ΔG° = BDFEXH − BDFEYH), which usually leads to faster reaction rates (ΔG‡ is usually proportional to ΔG°).6 The BDFE of PCET reagents (e.g., X–H) can be determined experimentally by measuring the reduction potential (E1/2) and the pKa of the corresponding protonated/deprotonated species (for X–H, X–H+ and/or X−) using the so-called Bordwell equation (Fig. 1B, left). The mechanism of 1H+/1e− PCET transformations can be stepwise (i.e., with an initial proton transfer followed by electron transfer (PT-ET) or vice versa, (ET-PT)), but these reactions usually occurs in a concerted fashion (coupled-proton electron transfer, CPET) because the transfer of a proton and an electron in a single reaction step is energetically favored (Fig. 1B, right).7,8 Determining the BDFE by using the Bordwell equation is sometimes challenging since it requires for the reversible formation of the high-energy protonated/deprotonated and reduced/oxidized intermediates (for XH, X− and/or XH+). Very recently, Mayer and coworkers have shown that the BDFE of PCET reagents can be determined using open-circuit potential measurements (OCPs).9 This approach relies on the stability of the PCET reagent (X–H) and the corresponding reduced-deprotonated species (X˙). OCPs are electrochemical measurements carried out at different relative concentrations of the protonated/reduced form of the PCET reagent (X–H) and deprotonated/oxidized form (X˙), leading to linear plots that allow for determining the BDFE of the PCET couples (intercept: potential (E°, V vs. H2) when [X–H] = [X˙], BDFE = 23.06E° + ΔG(H˙)) and the number of protons and electrons involved in the transformation (slope = −0.0592 V/n). Hence, OCPs can also be used to study the thermochemistry of PCET transformations involving more than one proton and one electron (multi-proton multi-electron PCET).

Determination of the BDFE of X–H bonds using the Bordwell equation and potential energy surface of the removal of 1H+ and 1e− (in a concerted or stepwise fashion) from X–H bonds (B).

Hydroquinone and hydroquinone-like molecules (H2Qs) undergo 2H+/2e− transformations in a reversible fashion (Fig. 2A). These molecules are used by nature to accept, store, and deliver the protons and electrons in a reversible fashion. For example, the four protons and four electrons derived from water oxidation in photosystem II are transported to photosystem I by a pool of plastoquinone molecules, separating (in time and space) the generation of O2 with the biosynthesis of NADPH and ATP (Fig. 2B).10 Inspired by nature, H2Qs have been used in catalysis as redox-mediators and as electrochemical devices for energy-related applications (e.g., organic redox-flow batteries).11–15 For example, Stahl and coworkers have used H2Qs as redox mediators in the catalytic dehydrogenation of organic molecules using O2 as oxidant, sometimes in combination with metal-based catalysts (see Fig. 2C).16 Our research lab is interested in using transition metals and H2Qs to develop metal complexes capable of performing multi-electron multi-proton transformations (Fig. 2D). We have recently combined CuI and ortho-phenylenediamines to produce cuprous complexes able to promote reversible 4H+/4e− reactions.17,18

Reversible 2H+/2e− dehydrogenation of hydroquinone and hydroquinone-like species to produce the corresponding quinone and quinone-like products (A). Hydroquinones used as H-atom carriers during photosynthesis (B), as redox mediators in catalysis (C) and as redox-active ligands for metal complexes that carry out multi-proton multi-electron transformations (D).

The 2H+/2e− oxidative-deprotonation of H2Qs can be divided in two independent 1H+/1e− events that entail the formation of semiquinone-like and quinone-like species (Fig. 3A). Each of these 1H+/1e− PCET reactions has its corresponding BDFE value (BDFE1 and BDFE2 in Fig. 3A), which usually differ from each other. This effect, also known as redox-unlevelling, is proposed to be caused by the high instability of the open-shell semiquinone-like intermediate (Fig. 3B).19 For example, the BDFE for the 1H+/1e− interconversion between 1,2-hydroquinone and 1,2-semiquinone is substantially higher than the BDFE for the 1H+/1e− interconversion between 1,2-semiquinone and 1,2-quinone (BDFE1 = 82.8 kcal mol−1, BDFE2 = 60.6 kcal mol−1, BDFEavg = 71.7 kcal mol−1, in H2O).9 Given that the hydroquinone-like form and the quinone-like form of the H2Qs are usually both stable, the average of the two BDFE values (BDFEavg) can be determined experimentally by electrochemical methods (e.g., OCP). Several research articles have also computed these thermochemical parameters, but most of these studies have mainly focused on substituted 1,4-hydroquinones.20,21

Thermochemistry of the stepwise dehydrogenation of hydroquinones (H2Q) to produce the semiquinone (HQ˙) and quinone-like products (A) that involves the cleavage of two X–H bonds with different BDFEs, also known as redox-unlevelling (B).

Our interest in using substituted ortho-diamines as redox-active ligands that promote multi-electron multi-proton transformation triggered the work described herein in which we determined, computationally and experimentally, the BDFEavg of a family of H2Qs (Fig. 4). To do so, we used AIMNet2 (ref. 22) (an atoms-in-molecules neural network potential for modeling non-metallic compounds) to measure the difference in free energy between H2Q and the corresponding 2H+/2e− dehydrogenation product, Q. As shown in Fig. 3B, this energy difference is related to the BDFEavg of the 2H+/2e− oxidative-deprotonation of the H2Q (2BDFEavg = ΔG° = G°(X) − G°(XH2) + 2G°(H˙)), and it is independent of the free energy of the semiquinone intermediate. The validity of AIMNet2 for BDFEavg evaluation was initially confirmed through comparison with DFT calculations using a structurally diverse vicinal diamine dataset (see Fig. 4B). This methodology was subsequently expanded to a series of 70 000 vicinal diamines with adjustable R and R′ substituents employing AIMNet2 as the neural potential. Furthermore, vicinal diols and dithiols (up to 130 000) were investigated and compared with the corresponding diamines. It is worth mentioning that this is the first time where AIMNet2 has been used to calculate BDFEs.

Methodology followed in this project to determine the BDFEavg of H2Qs (A). This project has analyzed the BDFEavg for the 2H+/2e− dehydrogenation of the vicinal diamines, diols and dithiols, including N-substituted diamines and backbone-substituted systems (B).

We would like to emphasize that this article is focused on studying the BDFEavg of H2Q/Q couples, but the reactivity of hydroquinones and quinones is not only dictated by the thermochemistry of their 2H+/2e− (de)hydrogenation. The kinetics for transformations involving PCET reagents will also be highly influenced by the stepwise BDFEs (i.e., BDFE of the H2Q/HQ˙ and HQ˙/Q couples), and the activation energy of each PCET step. It is known that the rates of PCET can vary substantially due to proton-electron coupling strength, hydrogen-bonding interactions, solvent reorganization, nonadiabatic effects, as well as quantum tunneling.23–26

Methods

Data curation

SMARTS27 patterns were used to filter the desired diamine, diols, and dithiol from the PubChem Compound database (see Fig. 4 and 5).28N-substituted diamines were computationally generated using the corresponding unsubstituted diamine with the desired sidechains (e.g., 4,5-dichloro-1,2-dimethyl-opda was generated using 4,5-dichloro-1,2-opda and methylamine). The initial dataset consists of diverse families of aromatic redox-active molecules, as illustrated in Fig. 4B, including either reduced or oxidized forms sourced from the PubChem Compound database.28 Additional curation steps were required to make the calculated BDFEavg values chemically interpretable across this large and heterogeneous chemical space. Stereochemistry is not fully specified for any of the entries in this database, and therefore stereoisomer generation was essential to ensure that the molecular pairs corresponding to the reaction scheme shown in Fig. 4B used for BDFE calculation exhibit consistent and explicit stereochemistry. For molecules containing more than one vicinal redox-active site, all possible hydrogenated/dehydrogenated product pairings were considered, and the lowest-free-energy pairing was used for the reported value. For systems where keto–enol tautomerization is chemically relevant, particularly some furan-derived diols, alternative tautomeric forms were evaluated because they can substantially affect the computed BDFEavg. The reported set of approximately 200 000 compounds should therefore be interpreted as a curated collection of stereochemically specified redox pairs and chemically relevant forms derived from PubChem structures and generated analogs, instead of an unprocessed list of unique PubChem entries (see additional details on the SI).

Approach used for data curation from the PubChem dataset (A) and workflows followed for the curation and generation of vicinal diamines (B), and diols and dithiols (C).

Investigation of N-substitution was conducted by systematically sampling primary amines from five distinct structural families (see Fig. 4B) from the PubChem Compound database, to effectively capture a representative distribution of chemical fragments adjacent to nitrogen atoms in naturally occurring and synthetic compounds. The selected side chains were attached to opda to generate a library of vicinal diamines featuring only side chain substitutions. All resulting candidates underwent stereoisomer enumeration, and the corresponding vicinal diimines were then generated following the same protocol as described previously. Further details regarding this side chain dataset are provided in the SI.

BDFEavg evaluation using AIMNet2

Conformer generation, geometry optimization, and free energy calculations were performed using a computational workflow based on Auto3D,29 with AIMNet2 (ref. 22) serving as the machine-learned interatomic potential. The number of conformers generated for each molecule was determined following the guidelines proposed by Chan et al.,30 and the conformers were generated using the experimental-torsion-knowledge distance geometry (ETKDG) algorithm31 available through RDKit (see example in the SI). To maximize efficiency, molecules with the same number of atoms were batched together. Through this approach, padding for input was rendered unnecessary, and redundant matrix multiplications during optimization were eliminated. The conformers were subsequently optimized using the fast inertial relaxation engine (FIRE) optimizer32 and ranked based on their electronic energies. For each molecule, the Gibbs free energy of the lowest-energy conformer was computed using an approximate Hessian provided by the AIMNet2 model, via the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) package.33Fig. 4A provides a visual representation of the workflow. The corresponding code is available on GitHub (see SI). Once free energies were obtained, BDFEavg values were calculated using the equation: 2BDFEavg = ΔG° = G°(X) − G°(XH2) + 2G°(H˙). The G°(H˙) values were calculated by DFT (see SI), and slight differences between the values obtained in gas phase and in different solvents were observed (within 2 kcal mol−1, see SI). It is worth mentioning that the thermochemical information obtained with AIMNet2 is in the gas phase and that solvent effect is only included in the G°(H˙) term (i.e., it is assumed that the effect of the solvent in the thermochemistry of G°(X) and G°(XH2) is similar). For the vicinal diamines, diols and dithiols, we calculated the BDFEavg with the G°(H˙) in CH3CN because this is the solvent used for experimental validation. The BDFEavg values for the 1,4-hydroquinones systems were calculated using the G°(H˙) in water because the experimental BDFEavg values were measured in aqueous solutions.

DFT benchmarking and BDFEavg calculations

To validate AIMNet2-derived average bond dissociation free energies (BDFEavg) for vicinal aromatic diamines, we carried out solution-phase DFT calculations in acetonitrile using ORCA (v6.1.0). For each diamine/diimine couple, candidate conformers were generated upstream using the ETKDG method implemented in the Auto3D pipeline (see SI) and the selected starting geometries were fully reoptimized with the ωB97M-D3BJ functional and the def2-TZVPP basis set using CPCM (acetonitrile), TightSCF convergence, and tightened geometry convergence criteria; harmonic vibrational frequency calculations (analytic frequencies) were then performed at the same level of theory to obtain zero-point and thermal corrections. All reported free energies correspond to T = 298.15 K and P = 1 atm and include electronic energies plus zero-point, enthalpic, entropic, and solvation contributions from the CPCM model. The BDFEavg values were obtained as (G°(X) + 2 G°(H˙) − G°(XH2))/2. Additionally, we repeated the key calculations (geometry optimization and analytic frequency calculation) with B3LYP (def2-TZVPP basis set, D3BJ dispersion correction and CPCM solvation model) and M06-2X (def2-TZVPP basis set, D3ZERO dispersion correction and CPCM solvation model) to quantify functional sensitivity and compare BDFEavg trends across the full 168-diamine set as well as targeted subsets (12 diamines matched to the 12 diols and 12 dithiols, see SI). Our decision of using the ωB97M-D3BJ functional and def2-TZVPP basis set as our primary configuration for our benchmarking was based on its superior performance in thermochemical studies,34,35 and the fact that AIMNet2 was trained with it. We also studied the stepwise BDFE comparisons among the 12 diamines/diols/dithiols (see SI for details). Finally, to assess model transferability beyond the diamine space, ωB97M-D3BJ DFT free energies for a panel of 1,4-hydroquinones in acetonitrile were also computed and compared against AIMNet2 predictions using the same thermochemical protocol to calculate BDFEavg (see SI).

Offset model method

The BDFEavg of vicinal diamines can be decomposed into contributions from two components, the aromatic backbone and the sidechain substitution. To evaluate the interdependence of these contributions, an additive model denoted as the offset model was developed (see example in SI, Scheme S6). This simplistic additive model offers an alternative for discovery because the chemical spaces of backbone and sidechain substitutions can be treated (and explored) separately.

Materials

All reagents were purchased from commercial suppliers and used as received except as noted. All solvents were purchased at the highest level of purity and further purified and dried by passing through an activated alumina solvent purification system (MB SPS-7, M. BRAUN INERTGAS-SYSTEME, Garching, Germany). Deuterated solvents were purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories (Tewksbury, MA, USA) and used as received. The diamines, hydroquinones and quinones used in this article were obtained by commercial suppliers or were synthesized using methods previously reported.18

Physical methods

Air-free handling of the hydroquinones and quinones was performed inside a MBRAUN UNIlab Pro SP glovebox system with N2 working gas. Electrochemical measurements were performed using a CH Instruments 620E Electrochemical Workstation (CH Instruments, Austin, TX, USA). NMR spectra were on a 500 MHz NMR spectrometer (NEO 500 or Avance III, Bruker Corp., Billerica, MA) to acquire spectra with 16 cumulative scans.

Cyclic voltammetry of hydroquinones and quinones

3 mL of a CH3CN solution of the desired hydroquinone or quinone (1 mM) containing 0.1 M of [NBu4]PF6 was prepared in the glovebox and were transferred to an electrochemical cell outside the glovebox, which was purged with Ar for 5 minutes (note: a conventional three-electrode cell was used with a glassy carbon working electrode, an Ag/AgNO3 (0.01 M) and platinum wire as the counter electrode). The potentials were measured with respect to the Ag/AgNO3 reference electrode and converted to Fc0/+ (Fc0/+ potential measured under the same experimental conditions). Cyclic voltammograms were obtained at 100 mV s−1 scan rate. All electrochemical measurements were carried out under Ar atmosphere. See SI for further details.

Experimental determination of the BDFEavg of diamines

In the glovebox, stock solutions of appropriate concentrations of the desired opda system, 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene (Internal Standard, Int. Std.) and the desired diamine were prepared in CD3CN. In a 7 inch, 5 mm o.d. NMR tube, a 0.9 mL CD3CN solution containing desired concentrations of the diamine (10 mM, unless otherwise noted) and Int. Std. (10 mM, unless otherwise noted) was recorded as the blank solution for each reactivity study. Following the blank spectrum, 0.1 mL (unless otherwise noted) of the substituted 1,4-quinone reagent of desired concentration was added (10 mM, unless otherwise noted). Subsequently, the NMR tube was capped and sealed with Teflon tape to inhibit any unanticipated oxidation by O2 during reaction time scale. Multiple NMR spectra over time revealed the reactivity of the systems under study. The internal standard peak at 6.12 ppm (Ph–H, 3H) was used as reference for quantification of all components of the reactions. See further details in the SI.

Results and discussion

Validation of the BDFEavg values obtained with AIMNet2 by DFT

Computational evaluation of several substituted ortho-phenylenediamines was performed, using both AIMNet2 (ref. 22) and density functional theory (DFT), and the resulting bond dissociation free energies (BDFEs) were compared (see Fig. 6 and also SI). The approaches employed include AIMNet2 using the Auto3D29 pipeline, and DFT at the ωB97M-D3BJ/Def2-TZVPP level of theory36,37 with the conductor-like polarizable continuum model (CPCM)38 as the solvation model; acetonitrile was used as the solvent for the implicit solvation models. As previously discussed, the possibility of substituting the nitrogen atoms in vicinal diamines, as opposed to diols and dithiols, introduces increased variability. To comprehensively explore the range of BDFEs for vicinal aromatic diamines with varying backbones and N-substituents, an initial set comprising 12 aromatic backbones and 14 substituents was selected, yielding 168 diamines (see Fig. 6). Conformers of these diamines (H2Qs) and their corresponding diimines (Qs) were generated and optimized using AIMNet2 and Auto3D, and their Gibbs free energies were subsequently computed. A detailed description of this workflow is provided in the Methods section.

(Left) Heatmap of the DFT-computed BDFEavg for a family of 168 vicinal diamines. (Right) Parity plot of BDFEavg computed using DFT and AIMNet2 for the same 168 diamines.

The DFT-evaluated BDFEavg values for the 168 diamines are visualized in a two-dimensional heatmap (see Fig. 6), with backbone substitutions along the x axis and N-substitutions along the y axis. A similar heat map was also built using AIMNet2 (See SI). The BDFEavg values obtained using AIMNet2 are in close agreement with the ones computed by DFT (see Fig. 6, bottom right). The calculations using AIMNet2 were significantly faster (minutes) than the ones using DFT (days).

To reveal structure–property relationships, the data points were sorted along both axes so that diamines with lower BDFEavg values cluster in the bottom left corner, while those with higher values appear in the top right corner. This systematic pattern indicates that BDFEavg increases progressively both from left to right and from bottom to top. The regular distribution after sorting suggests that the BDFEavg of vicinal diamines can be decomposed into independent contributions from the backbone and N-substitutions. Based on this observation, an offset model that treats these effects as additive contributions to the overall BDFEavg was tested. The model's robust performance validates the hypothesis and offers a significant computational advantage: rather than calculating BDFEavg values for every possible combination of substitutions, backbone and N-substitutions can be explored independently (see example in Scheme S1 in the SI).

Molecules analyzed

As we have shown in Fig. 5, a family of vicinal diamines (e.g., opda) was extracted from PubChem. The N-substituted diamines were generated by combining computationally the desired vicinal diamine with the desired amine (e.g., N,N′-dimethyl-1,2-phenylenediamine was obtained by combining opda and 2 molecules of methylamine). Vicinal diols and dithiols were either extracted from Pubchem or built by substituting the N atom for O or S for a subset of vicinal diamines (Fig. 5C). For all diamines, diols and dithiols (∼200 000 molecules), the corresponding quinone-like dehydrogenation products were generated, and we calculated the BDFEavg of the H2Q/Q couples.

Structure–property relationships

The analysis of the effect on the BDFEavg upon opda modification has been evaluated using the data obtained from 200 000 molecules. This analysis includes the effect upon substituting the opda backbone and/or changing the NH2 groups for NHR, OH and SH (see details below).

Opda backbone substitution

To systematically analyze the change in BDFEavg upon backbone substitution, we selected opda's containing H, Me, MeO, F, Cl, NO2, CF3 and CN substituents (∼20 000 diamines), and these were divided in two groups, diamines with “near” substitution (3 and/or 6-substituted) and diamines with “far” substitution (4 and/or 5-substituted, see Fig. 7A). For example, 1096 opda's contained a methyl substituent in at least one of the “near” positions (3 and/or 6), with the other positions (4, 5, and 3 or 6) containing any other possible substituent. The distribution of the BDFEavg of these substituted opda's is then compared with the BDFEavg of unsubstituted opda (AIMNet2 value of BDFEavg = 68.4 kcal mol−1, depicted with a dotted vertical line in Fig. 7B). This categorization allowed us to analyze the electronic effects of the substitutions on the BDFEavg in which the “near” substitutions induce a “meta effect” to one of the N atoms that will undergo N–H cleavage, while “far” substitutions induce a “meta effect” to one of the N atoms undergoing N–H cleavage and a “para effect” to the other N atom undergoing N–H cleavage (see Fig. 7C). Hence, Hammett analysis for the “near” substitutions was performed by plotting the σmvs. the average BDFEavg of each substituent (e.g., average BDFEavg of the 1096 systems containing a Me substituent in the “near” position) and “far” substitutions can be analyzed by plotting (σm + σp)/2 vs. the average BDFEavg of each substituent (e.g., average BDFEavg of the 680 systems containing a Me substituent in the “far” position).

Analysis of the effect of “near” and “far” substitutions on opda's BDFEavg (A). The effect was analyzed by plotting the BDFEavg distributions for the different substituents in the “near” and “far” positions (B) and using Hammett parameterization (C). Note: the vertical line indicates the BDFEavg of opda (AIMNet2 value of BDFEavg = 68.4 kcal mol−1).

As shown in Fig. 7C, our data suggest that the use of electron-withdrawing (EW) groups (e.g., the NO2 substituent is EW both in meta and para) induce an increase in the BDFEavg of the opda systems. Conversely, the use of electron-donating (ED) groups (e.g., the Me substituent is ED both in meta and para) decreases the BDFEavg. In the case of the MeO substituent, we observed that “near” substitutions caused a decrease in the BDFEavg, associated with the EW nature of the MeO system in meta (σm = 0.10), and a slight decrease in the BDFEavg for the “far” substitutions, derived from the EW nature of the MeO in meta but ED in para (σm = 0.10, σp = −0.27).

These differences are also observed in 4-substituted anilines, in which the BDFE of the N–H increases with EW groups and decreases with ED groups. This effect, also known as thermochemical decompensation, has also been observed in 4-substituted phenols, but not in 4-substituted toluenes.5 For example, the E1/2 for the reduction of 4-MeO–aniline in water is 0.79 V (vs. Fc0/+) and the pKa of the resulting 4-MeO–PhNH2˙+ is 9.6, which leads to a BDFE of the 4-MeO–PhNH2/4-MeO–PhNH˙ couple of 84.1 kcal mol−1. For aniline, the corresponding E1/2, pKa and BDFE are 1.02 V, 7.05 and 85.9 kcal mol−1. Hence, the Gibbs free energy variation associated to the change in E1/2 upon substitution is not fully compensated by the free Gibbs energy variation associated by a change in pKa leading to a lower BDFE for 4-MeO–PhNH2 It is proposed that the thermochemical decompensation observed in 4-substituted anilines (and phenols) is because the deprotonation occurs locally at the N–H bond, while the oxidation involves the removal of an electron from a delocalized molecular orbital. In anilines and phenols, 4-substituents on the aromatic ring interact with the redox-active π system but are far from the acidic N–H bond.5

Opda N-substitution

The BDFEavg of a family of N-substituted opda systems was also determined (Fig. 8). As we stated, these N-substituted systems were computationally generated from opda backbones and amines. The substituents analyzed (common N-substituents in diamines and anilines) were classified in 6 families, including ureanyl (used in our lab to synthesize metal complexes bound by redox-active ligands), acyl, sulfonyl, phenyl, and alkyl groups. Our analysis indicated that diamines substituted by electron-donating substituents (e.g., NH–tBu substitution) had significant lower BDFEs than the diamines substituted by electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., NH–Ac). These observations seem contradictory since electron-withdrawing substituents induce a decrease on the pKa of amines (e.g., the pKa for the deprotonation of PhNH2 in DMSO is 30.6, while the pKa for the deprotonation of PhCONH2 in DMSO is 23.3), which should lead to lower BDFEs (easier to remove proton during dehydrogenation). However, the thermochemical decompensation depicted by aromatic amines suggests that the introduction of EW groups as N-substituents induces only small changes in the pKa of the N–H bond but has a bigger effect on the reduction potentials.

Analysis of the effect of N-substitutions on opda's BDFEavg (A). The effect was analyzed by plotting the BDFEavg distributions for the different substituents (B). Note: the vertical dotted line indicates the BDFEavg of opda (AIMNet2 value of BDFEavg = 68.4 kcal mol−1).

Diamines with different backbone scaffolds

Like in the DFT results presented above, substitution of the phenyl ring of opda's for other aromatic systems such as 2,3-pyridine, 3,4-pyridine, furan and thiophene caused dramatic variations on the BDFEavg (see Fig. 9). As we have discussed, the thermodynamics of dehydrogenation of these aromatic diamines seem to be highly affected by changes in the reduction potential of the Ar–NH2/Ar–NH2˙+ couple. So, breaking the aromaticity of the aromatic diamines (i.e., removing an electron from Ar–NH2 to produce Ar–NH2˙+) might be one of the major thermodynamic contributors to the overall BDFEavg of the H2Q/Q couple.39 Viskolcz and coworkers have quantified the degree of aromaticity and antiaromaticity of organic molecules, with benzene having the highest aromaticity (y = 100%) and cyclobutadiene having the highest antiaromaticity (y = −100%).40 The degree of aromaticity of benzene (backbone of opda, y = 100%) is similar to the one determined for pyridine (y = 98.12%), in agreement with the similarity of the BDFEavg values computed for the phenyl, 3,4-pyridine and 2,3-pyridine vicinal diamines (see Fig. 9B). Conversely, furan and thiophene have a substantial lower degree of aromaticity (y = 43.71% and 57.95%, respectively). These lower degree of aromaticity (which is quantified as ∼15 kcal mol−1), is aligned with the decrease of BDFEavg of the vicinal diamines containing these heterocycles (∼10 kcal mol−1 lower than opda systems). Very recently, Liu and coworkers have reported a method to quantify aromaticity (Harmonic Oscillator Model of Aromaticity, HOMA) and Hammett effects on heterocycles (σHet), which provides further evidence that the dramatic changes in the BDFEavg of the furan and thiophene systems is likely due to their reduced aromaticity (see SI for further details).41

Diamines vs. diols vs. dithiols

Our analysis also included the difference in BDFEavg between vicinal diamines, diols and dithiols with different backbones (see Fig. 9). Similar trends were observed for all the backbones analyzed, with the diols depicting slightly lower BDFEavg values than the diamines, and the dithiols slightly higher. We were somewhat surprised by these results, since the BDFE for the 1H+/1e− dehydrogenation of aniline (85.9 kcal mol−1 in H2O) is similar to the one observed for phenol (84.7 kcal mol−1 in H2O) but substantially higher than thiophenol (77.7 kcal mol−1 in H2O). However, it should be considered that the computed BDFEavg's entail two 1H+/1e− dehydrogenation steps, BDFE1 (corresponding to the H2Q/HQ˙ couple) and BDFE2 (corresponding to the HQ˙/Q couple) and the difference between these two values might be higher for some systems. We hypothesize that the BDFE1 for dithiols might be lower than diamines (like the difference between aniline and thiophenol) but that the BDFE2 for dithiols might be substantially higher, leading to an overall higher BDFEavg. It should also be considered that the 2H+/2e− dehydrogenation of diamines and diols entails the formation of CN and CO bonds, which are stronger than the CS bonds formed in the dehydrogenation of dithiols.42 To corroborate our hypothesis, we computed the stepwise BDFE of 12 diamines, 12 diols and 12 dithiols with the same backbone substituents using DFT, and we found substantial redox unlevelling in 1,2-diamines and 1,2-diols (i.e., the BDFE of the H2Q/HQ˙ couple was substantially higher than the BDFE of the HQ˙/Q couple) while 1,2-dithiols were more levelled (the BDFE of the H2Q/HQ˙ couple was similar or even lower than the BDFE of the HQ˙/Q couple, see SI).

Experimental validation

Computed vs. experimental BDFEavg of substituted 1,4-hydroquinones

In a recent report, Stahl and Hammes-Schiffer reported the experimental BDFEavg values for the 2H+/2e− dehydrogenation of 25 substituted 1,4-hydroquinones.21 The authors also included DFT computations on the BDFEavg values, reduction potentials and pKa's of a family of 134 scaffolds. The authors carried out isodesmic computations, which determined the BDFEavg of the substituted hydroquinone/quinone couples using the experimental BDFEavg of the unsubstituted 1,4-hydroquinone as reference. Based on this report, we utilized AIMNet2 to compute the BDFEavg of the 24 substituted 1,4-hydroquinones that were experimentally studied and compared our results with the DFT computations included in Stahl's and Hammes-Schiffer's report (Fig. 10A, see list of hydroquinones in the SI).21 AIMNet2 computed the BDFEavg of substituted 1,4-hydroquinones with a slight, but consistent, deviation (∼4.8 kcal mol−1 lower than experimental value). The same deviation was also observed when we computed the BDFEavg using DFT (∼4.2 kcal mol−1 lower than experimental value, see SI). It should be noted that the experimental BDFEavg values for the 1,4-hydroquinones included in the abovementioned study were measured in water, and that the BDFE values for O–H bonds are usually higher in water when compared to the values obtained in gas phase or organic solvents due to intermolecular H-bonding interactions.43 A recent study by Gerken and Stahl has shown that the experimental BDFE of the O–H bond in N-hydroxyphthalimide (NHPI) was significantly lower in CH3CN than in water (80.0 vs. 84.4 kcal mol−1).44 DFT calculations could only reproduce the experimental BDFE results when explicit molecules of solvent were included in the computations (gas phase: 78.6 kcal mol−1; implicit CH3CN: 80.0 kcal mol−1; implicit and explicit CH3CN: 81.8 kcal mol−1; implicit H2O: 80.0 kcal mol−1; implicit and explicit water: 83.9 kcal mol−1). Applying an isodesmic correction to the BDFEavg values for the 1,4-hydroquinones obtained with AIMNet2 led to a better correlation to the one found previously by DFT in the study by Stahl and Hammes-Schiffer (see Fig. 10A, right).

Experimental reduction potentials vs. computed BDFEavg

In the abovementioned report, Stahl and Hammes-Schiffer measured the 1e− reduction potential of 24 quinones and compared them with the experimental BDFEavg of the H2Q/Q couples.21 The authors observed that generally, the reduction potentials and the BDFEavg were correlated, with the quinones with higher E1/2 (stronger 1e− oxidants) also having higher BDFEavg. For this article, we measured the 1e− reduction potential of some 1,2-quinones and we also observed that higher potentials align with higher BDFEavg (Fig. 10B).

Our analysis of the BDFEavg of vicinal diamines, diols and dithiols suggested that one of the main factors influencing the BDFEavg might be the removal of the first electron (1e− oxidation potential of H2Q), in which the aromaticity of the systems is broken. For some of the vicinal diamines, we measured the 1e− oxidation potential by cyclic voltammetry using CH3CN as solvent (irreversible oxidations, see data in the SI). As observed in Fig. 10B, we found a good correlation between the 1e− oxidation potentials and the computed BDFEavg (i.e., higher potentials lead to higher BDFE values). We also measured the 1e− oxidation potential of 1,4-hydroquinones with known BDFEavg, and we observed a correlation between the oxidation potential and the BDFEavg values.

Reaction between opdas vs. 1,4-quinone oxidants of known BDFEavg

To provide additional evidence on the validity of the computed BDFEavg, we carried out the dehydrogenation of some vicinal diamines (opda's) using 1,4-quinones with known BDFEavg (see Fig. 11). The reactions were followed by NMR in which stoichiometric amounts of the oxidant were added to the diamine, all in CD3CN. In these experiments, three different outcomes are observed. In the first situation, the BDFEavg of the diamine analyzed is substantially higher than the BDFEavg of the 1,4-quinone oxidant utilized, no reaction is observed. In the second situation, the BDFEavg of the diamine analyzed is substantially lower than the BDFEavg of the 1,4-quinone oxidant utilized, and full oxidation of the diamine and full reduction of the 1,4-quinones is observed. In the third situation, the addition of 1,4-quinone to the diamine leads to partial dehydrogenation of the diamine and partial reduction of the quinone, indicative that both diamine and hydroquinone have similar BDFEavg.

Experimental determination of the BDFEavg of opda's using substituted 1,4-quinones as oxidants. (see SI for additional details).

For example, 4,5-dichloro-1,2-opda did not react with 2,6-dimethoxy-1,4-quinone (BDFEavg = 63.7 kcal mol−1 in CH3CN) or with 2,6-dimethyl-1,4-quinone (BDFEavg = 64.6 kcal mol−1 in CH3CN), suggesting that the BDFEavg of 4,5-dichloro-1,2-opda in CH3CN is higher than 65 kcal mol−1. In fact, 4,5-dichloro-1,2-opda partially reacted with 1,4-quinone (BDFEavg = 67.3 kcal mol−1 in CH3CN) and reacted stoichiometrically with 2,6-dichloro-1,4-quinone (the BDFEavg in CH3CN is unknown but it has been measured in H2O, 69.4 kcal mol−1, and in DMF, 68.9 kcal mol−1) and with 2,3-dichloro-5,6-dicyano-1,4-quinone (DDQ, the BDFEavg in CH3CN is unknown but it has been measured in H2O, 73.3 kcal mol−1). Hence, we estimated that the BDFEavg of 4,5-dichloro-1,2-opda in CH3CN should be approximately 67 kcal mol−1, a value that is in close agreement with the computed BDFEavg using AIMNet2 (69.7 kcal mol−1). It should be noted that the experimental BDFEavg values of 1,4-hydroquinones vary slightly in different solvents (∼1 kcal mol−1), and that the measurements of these values have some error (∼1 kcal mol−1).

Our experiments also confirmed the trends observed computationally, in which the BDFEavg of opda (BDFEavg ∼67/69 kcal mol−1 in CH3CN) could be decreased by using ED groups in the backbone (e.g., for 4,5-dimethoxy-1,2-opda, BDFEavg ∼ 65 kcal mol−1) or increase by using EW groups (e.g., for 4,5-dicyano-1,2-opda, BDFEavg ∼ 73 kcal mol−1). We also determined that N-substitution using EW groups such as the ureanyl moieties also caused an increase in the BDFEavg (e.g., addition of tert-butyl-ureanyl to 4,5-dimethoxy-1,2-opda lead to an increase of ∼5 kcal mol−1), an effect that we have also observed in our research on Cu-based ECPB systems.18

Further implementation

To facilitate community use of our thermochemical data, we developed three complementary computational resources with progressively increasing speed and accessibility. The complete computational workflow is available as an open-source Python package on GitHub (https://github.com/EQUUS-ORG). This workflow enables researchers to compute BDFEavg values for arbitrary hydroquinone-like structures within minutes per molecule on standard CPU hardware.

We trained a graph neural network (GNN) predictor on the complete 200 000-molecule dataset to enable sub-second BDFEavg predictions. Prediction time is <0.1 seconds per molecule, enabling real-time screening during molecular design. Hence, we have substantially shortened the timescale of computation, from days with DFT, to minutes with AIMNet2, to seconds with the BDFEavg predictor.

A user-friendly web application (https://cavall.chem.cmu.edu/equus) allows non-computational chemists to access both the database and predictor. Users can: (i) draw structures using a molecular editor or input SMILES strings; (ii) retrieve pre-computed BDFEavg values for molecules in the database with associated conformers and energetics; (iii) generate predictions for novel structures with confidence intervals; (iv) perform batch calculations on structure libraries (up to 1000 molecules); (v) visualize structure–property relationships through interactive heatmaps and filtering; and (vi) download results in CSV and SDF formats. The interface includes chemical similarity search to identify related molecules and substructure filtering for focused exploration.

Details on the website and the BDFEavg predictor can be found on the SI. We have also included some computations on the BDFEavg of natural molecules that undergo 2H+/2e− such as ascorbic acid,45 alcohols,46 flavins,45 and phenazines.47 We believe that our approach can be not only be used to determine the thermochemistry of these natural PCET reagents, which can be used in organic synthesis and catalysis, but also designing novel scaffolds for biomedical and biotechnological applications (e.g., targeting the metabolism of highly antibiotic tolerant bacterial populations using substituted phenazines48).

Conclusions and future perspectives

This work maps out a detailed thermochemical landscape for hydroquinone-like PCET reagents through high-throughput computational screening of approximately 200 000 molecules using the AIMNet 2 neural network potential. We show that BDFEavg values can be systematically adjusted over a 30 kcal mol−1 range (50–80 kcal mol−1) via structural modifications, with aromaticity as the main factor and Hammett substituent effects providing precise control. The publicly available database and machine learning predictor cut down BDFEavg calculation times from days (using DFT) to seconds, enabling quick in silico screening for target applications.

Immediate uses of this database include: (1) designing quinone-based electrolytes for organic redox-flow batteries with specific voltage windows; (2) choosing PCET mediators for metal-free oxidation catalysis with appropriate thermodynamic driving forces; (3) developing redox-active ligands for multi-electron metalloenzyme mimics; and (4) discovering phenazine derivatives to target antibiotic-resistant bacterial metabolism. We also plan to combine this database with generative AI models to explore uncharted chemical space and find unconventional molecular structures with desired PCET properties.

Several important limitations and opportunities remain. First, our method calculates only average BDFEavg values; determining the individual stepwise BDFEs for hydroquinone/semiquinone and semiquinone/quinone couples, as well as the stepwise pKa's and redox potentials is crucial for understanding the complete thermochemical landscape. Second, our method was successfully benchmarked against DFT computations for a series of vicinal diamines, diols, dithiols and 1,4-hydroquinones but experimental validation was limited to around 10 compounds with indirect measurements (i.e., reactivity of vicinal diamines with 1,4-quinones). Third, solvent effects are still underexplored; extending calculations and experimental validation to diverse solvents (water, DMF, ionic liquids) would increase the method's applicability.

In the future, automated high-throughput experimental setups combining robotic synthesis, electrochemical testing, and machine learning feedback could validate and improve the computational predictions at scale. Expanding the computational framework to predict the full thermodynamic “square scheme”, including pKa, E1/2, and stepwise BDFE values, would give a complete picture of PCET thermochemistry. Ultimately, these tools could shorten the discovery-to-application process for functional PCET systems from years to months, transforming how we design molecular redox catalysts and energy storage materials.
