Intraosseous Vancomycin in Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Troy B Puga, Vincent Dieu, Kyle P O’Connor, Logan Tackett, McKenna W Box, John T Riehl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProsthetic joint infections (PJIs) are a major concern in total knee arthroplasty (TKA). One recent measure of interest has been the use of intraosseous vancomycin (IOV). This systematic review aims to evaluate the efficacy of IOV to prevent infection in TKA.MethodsA search was conducted across MEDLINE/Pubmed, Wiley Central, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Embase databases. Demographics and results of the studies were extracted from the articles that met inclusion criteria. Meta-analysis was conducted on primary TKA studies.ResultsEight studies were included in this systematic review. IOV correlated with decreased odds of infection in primary TKA (odds ratio: 0.31, P = .02) when compared with controls of intravenous antibiotic and no regional IOV. IOV showed no increase in AKI or creatinine concentration in primary TKA when compared with controls. IOV and increased concentration of vancomycin in fat and subcutaneous tissues had a strong correlation (Cohen’s d = 0.9, P = .01). IOV and increased concentration of vancomycin in bone had a moderate correlation (Cohen’s d = 0.59, P = .01). No patients where IOV was used across all studies developed Red Man Syndrome.ConclusionsIOV is an effective adjunctive treatment for preventing infection in primary TKA, with this study showing a 69% decreased odds of infection when compared with controls of intravenous antibiotic and no regional IOV. IOV also appears to be a safe treatment, and with limited data there seems to not be an increase in AKI or creatinine concentration.Level of EvidenceLevel IV; Therapeutic.Keywords: Intraosseous vancomycin, Total knee arthroplasty, Intraosseous antibiotics, Infection

Introduction

Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a significant complication that can arise following primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [[1], [2], [3]]. PJI can have devastating consequences, including removal of components, bacteremia, and destruction of the joint structures [[1], [2], [3]]. PJI also leads to complex revision arthroplasty surgeries after the infection is eradicated, which can be a serious challenge for patients and surgeons [[4], [5], [6]]. In addition to the consequences to the patient, PJI has been shown to lead to extreme annual costs to the local and national health-care systems in the United States [7,8]. Prevention of PJI remains a major challenge in primary TKA, and surgeons routinely take numerous preventative measures.

A recent preventative measure of interest in primary TKA has been intraosseous vancomycin (IOV). Several studies and techniques have described the use of this adjunct in primary TKA [9,10]. Intrawound antibiotics, such as powdered vancomycin have generally been controversial, and considered ineffective at preventing PJI, with a recent clinical showing that powder vancomycin was not effective at preventing PJI [11,12]. However, this is spread within the wound and it is unclear what the absorption is within the bone. It is currently unclear what the efficacy of IOV is in preventing infection in primary TKA. Nonetheless, IOV appears to be a straightforward technique that can be performed quickly at the beginning of a TKA procedure.

Much information remains unknown regarding the benefits (if any) of IOV in preventing infection in TKA. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the efficacy of IOV in preventing infection in TKA. We hypothesize that IOV will be an effective therapeutic intervention for preventing infection in primary TKA when compared with controls of intravenous antibiotic and no regional IOV.

Material and methods

This study is a level IV therapeutic systematic review and meta-analysis. The study was exempt from institutional review board approval. This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis for Scoping Reviews statement standards [13]. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) evaluated use of IOV in primary TKA and (2) were completed in English. Reviews, commentaries, and editorials were excluded.

PubMed/MEDLINE, Wiley Central, Google Scholar, Web of Science and Embase databases were systematically searched for publications from database inception to January 20, 2025. Comprehensive search strategies were developed using keywords, Medical Subject Heading terms, and synonymous terms. The PubMed/Medline search was adapted to the other databases. See supplementary Table 1 for the complete database search.

One author (T.B.P.) performed the search. Two authors (V.D. and L.T.) excluded irrelevant articles and duplicates based on title and abstract. The remaining articles underwent an independent full-text review by 2 authors (V.D. and L.T.) and were assessed for eligibility based on established criteria. Any conflicts were resolved by discussion with a third author (T.B.P.)

Study demographic information was collected including the following: lead author and country of origin, study design and level of evidence, number of patients, database used, and study follow-up length.

The Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 for randomized control trials was used to evaluate clinical trial studies [14]. This risk of bias tool is composed of 5 domains which evaluates bias within randomized clinical trials. The bias is classified into 3 categories: high, some concern, and low. An overall bias assessment is produced upon the combination of the 5 domains.

The methodological quality of the nonrandomized studies included in the review was evaluated using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) Criteria for comparative studies [15]. The MINORS Criteria for comparative studies uses a validated 12-item checklist for comparative studies to ensure a critical appraisal of the study design through a standardized assessment. A maximum of 24 points is possible for comparative studies, and studies with scores less than 14 were considered poor quality.

Meta-analysis was performed using IMB SPSS Statistics Version 30 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). Meta-analysis was conducted on primary total knee joint data and evaluated comparison of an intervention group using regional IOV to controls of an intravenous antibiotic (ie,; vancomycin, clindamycin, or cephalosporin) and no use of a regional IOV. The primary intervention in all studies was the use of regional IOV. Random effects modeling, as described by DerSimonian and Laird was performed. Results were reported using Cohen’s d (continuous data) and odds radios (OR, binary data). Strength of Cohen’s d values were rated as small (≥0.2), medium (≥0.5), or large (≥0.8) [16]. Ranges of outcomes were reported at 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and significance was reported using P values. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A preliminary search of 7 databases provided 823 studies; 51 duplicates were removed; 772 abstracts and titles were screened; and 752 were removed for lack of relevance. Twenty reports were sought for retrieval. Eight studies met the final inclusion criteria and were included in this study. Eight studies were included for meta-analysis. See Figure 1.

Eight studies were included in this systematic review [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Four were Level 1 Randomized Control Trials [18,[22], [23], [24]]. Eight studies were included in the meta-analysis. Primary TKA comparison of IOV vs no IOV revealed a decreased odds of infection by 69% (OR: 0.31 [95% CI 0.11-0.83] P = .02). See Figure 2. Vancomycin concentrations were measured in tissues at variable times from study to study, ranging from the start of the procedure to the morning of postoperative Day 1 of the procedure. IOV did not reveal any statistical difference in synovial fluid concentration of vancomycin in primary TKA (P = .27). See Figure 3. IOV did reveal a strong correlation in local fat concentration of vancomycin in primary TKA (Cohen’s d = 0.9, P = .01). See Figure 4. IOV did reveal a medium correlation in bone concentration of vancomycin in primary TKA (Cohen’s d = 0.59, P = .01). See Figure 5. Primary TKA comparison of IOV vs no IOV revealed no increase in the odds of acute kidney injury (AKI) (OR: 0.56 [95% CI 0.27-1.17] P = .13). See Figure 6. Comparison of IOV vs no IOV revealed a small effect in the difference between groups, and demonstrated that IOV had a lower change in creatinine compared to IV (Cohen’s d: 0.31, P < .01). See Figure 7. Based on the studies included in this review, the number needed to treat with IOV to prevent one infection was 97.

Discussion

This study saw the use of IOV in primary TKA. Meta-analysis revealed a statistically significant decrease in the odds of infection when IOV was used in primary TKA when compared with controls of intravenous antibiotic and no regional IOV. There was also no statistically significant difference in AKI or creatinine concentration in primary TKA when IOV was used. No IOV patients developed Red Man Syndrome in primary TKA.

PJI remains one of the largest concerns of any TKA procedure. PJI is costly to both the local and national health-care systems and creates a large burden on patients and health-care providers [7,8]. The results of this study show that IOV is an effective adjunctive treatment to prevent infection in primary TKA. It is believed that mechanism of IOV is effective in preventing PJI is by providing significantly increased local concentrations of antibiotic in bone, soft tissues, and fat [22]. While there is some concern that tourniquet use may interfere with local tissue spread, Spangehl et al., demonstrated that local concentrations in the bone, fat, and soft tissues were significantly higher despite 10 minutes of tourniquet use [22]. This study also demonstrated that there was increased concentrations in the femur, away from the tibial injection site [22], but the full mechanisms of local spread and infiltration are not fully understood. Future research can evaluate at what level, if any, tourniquet use effects local concentration changes, and further evaluate the mechanisms of IOV. It also remains unclear if there is a net overall positive effect of using IOV in revision TKA and primary total hip arthroplasty (THA), with the current literature remaining limited or conflicting [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. Future clinical trials and comparative studies can evaluate if there is a positive benefit of using IOV in revision TKA and primary THA.

IOV was shown to be a safe treatment in primary TKA. Anytime medication is used, there is always a risk for side effects or complications. In primary TKA, there was no difference in AKI or creatinine concentration with IOV. There were also no cases of Red Man Syndrome with the use of IOV. Intravenous vancomycin has been shown to be associated with these complications [30,31], and therefore, there could be a potential risk of these complications with the use of IOV. While these risks did not appear in the studies evaluated, it could be possible that these complications could be present with larger population use. Surgeons should still consider the possibility of these risks and additional complications, such as anaphylaxis [32], when deciding whether to use IOV in TKA.

Overall, IOV is a safe and effective treatment in primary TKA to prevent infection. Surgeons may consider using IOV to reduce the odds of infection in their primary TKA procedures, as the meta-analysis showed that it reduced infections by 69%. Future large-scale studies should be completed to evaluate the overall cost and feasibility of IOV treatment. If IOV continues to show a strong decrease in infection and a beneficial cost profile, use of IOV may become a routine measure in TKA. Future studies can also evaluate if there are benefits of decreased infection outside of primary TKA. These future studies can evaluate efficacy in revision TKA, both primary and revision THA, and both primary and revision total shoulder arthroplasty.

Limitations

The results of this study should be evaluated within the context of the study's limitations. The first limitation is the potential for missed literature. Our search was comprehensive, and 6 major databases were evaluated; however, there is always the possibility that literature exists outside these databases or in another language. A second limitation is that the studies in this meta-analysis have heterogeneity in the comparator. Some studies compare the IOV to additional intravenous vancomycin, while some studies add IOV as an adjunct to the surgeons' standard procedure with the use of preoperative ancef or clindamycin, which could be a potential confounding factor. However, in all the studies evaluated, the major difference is the use intervention groups use regional IOV, and the control group does not use regional IOV. The third limitation is that this study only included primary TKA, and did not include additional forms of arthroplasty due to the limited available literature. Future studies can further evaluate these effects in additional arthroplasty surgeries. The fourth limitation is the many potential confounding factors that can influence infection such as surgical preparation methods, surgeon technique, patient comorbidities, and perioperative antibiotics. Additional confounding factors may include the heterogeneity of populations with different comorbidities, surgery types and techniques, operating time, and setting such as academic or private practice setting. These factors ultimately play a role in infection rates and cannot be excluded as potential confounders within each of these studies, or within the larger meta-analysis. Vancomycin may not also stop all infections, and future analysis and studies can evaluate if the effect of vancomycin is limited to gram-positive and overall infections, and if there is any gram-negative infection persistence. Future studies can aim to control these variables to see if these effects persist in an isolated controlled fashion. The fifth limitation is that the mechanism of local tissue infiltration and spread is not completely understood, despite evidence suggesting there is local spread [22]. It also remains unclear what role and effect tourniquets may play on local spread and infiltration, and future studies can aim to evaluate this. The final limitation is a lack of longitudinal studies evaluating long-term outcomes. Future studies can evaluate these longitudinal outcomes.

Conclusions

IOV is an effective adjunctive treatment for preventing infection in primary TKA, with this study showing a 69% decreased odds of infection when compared with controls of intravenous antibiotic and no regional IOV. It also appears to be a safe treatment, and with limited data there seems to not be an increase in AKI or creatinine concentration. Future studies can be done to confirm the findings here, and to evaluate if IOV is effective for the prevention of infection in additional arthroplasty surgeries.
