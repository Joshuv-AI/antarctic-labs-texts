Asiatic acid disrupts biofilm formation and shows additive effect with antibiotic against Acinetobacter baumannii
Authors: Anh Ngoc Xuan Nguyen, Made Rai Dwitya Wiradiputra, Krit Thirapanmethee, Teerawit Audshasai, Pimonrat Ketsawatsomkron, Mullika Traidej Chomnawang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Asiatic acid, a bioactive pentacyclic triterpenoid derived from Centella asiatica, has been widely used against infectious wounds and inflammation. Recently, this natural compound has also drawn attention to interfering with bacterial biofilms, a major phenotypic strategy that enables bacteria to survive under stress or antibiotic pressure. Acinetobacter baumannii, a nosocomial pathogen, is well known for its remarkable ability to form biofilms that promote chronic infections and contribute to conventional antibiotic resistance. The present study investigated the inhibitory effect of asiatic acid during the early stages of A. baumannii biofilm development. Despite exhibiting indirect antimicrobial activity, asiatic acid significantly reduced biofilm production in both drug-resistant clinical isolates and A. baumannii ATCC 19606 reference strain. Confocal microscopic evaluations supported the visual attenuation of biofilm structure following treatment. Molecular analyses revealed that asiatic acid downregulated the expression of critical biofilm-associated adhesion genes, csuE and bap. By suppressing these genes, this natural compound may prevent bacteria from surface attachment and interfere with the subsequent maturation of biofilms. Furthermore, asiatic acid additively contributed to antimicrobial activity of standard antibiotics against A. baumannii. These findings support the potential of asiatic acid as an adjunctive agent for the control of biofilm-associated infections caused by A. baumannii, particularly in the context of rising multidrug resistance. Continued investigation into its mechanisms and synergistic combinations may inform the development of novel anti-biofilm therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Pentacyclic triterpenes are a class of naturally occurring compounds abundantly present in various plants and foods, which are classified into ursane, oleanane, and lupane group (Furtado et al. 2017). They possess a 30-carbon chemical structure derived from isoprene units and comprise functional hydroxyl group, which has a high affinity for associating with their specific targets, such as bacterial cell receptors and microbial enzymes, and producing hydrogen bonds to inactivate them (Juven et al. 1994). Asiatic acid, a hydride derivative of ursane, is frequently included in the ingredient formulations of cosmetic products for aiding in wound healing, scar reduction, anti- aging, and anti-oxidant properties (Diniz et al. 2023). Moreover, recent studies showed the neuroprotective potential of asiatic acid by counteracting age-related cognitive deterioration and neurodegenerative conditions (Ding et al. 2023). This herbaceous compound is derived from Centella asiatica, which grows widely in tropical and subtropical regions of Asia, Africa, and Oceania. In addition to various pharmacological properties, asiatic acid exhibits remarkable antimicrobial effects against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. It shows great antimicrobial potential against Salmonella Typhimurium, Escherichia coli O157:H7, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, Bacillus cereus, Clostridium difficile. This activity is mediated by disruption of cell membrane integrity, resulting in the leakage of nucleotides and increase intracellular potassium ions concentration (Liu et al. 2015; Harnvoravongchai et al. 2018). Another study by Sycz Z et al., further revealed that asiatic acid suppressed the of bacterial metabolic activity within mono-, and multi-species biofilm consortia formed by uropathogenic E. coli, Enterobacter cloacae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Sycz et al. 2021). Additionally, when combined with ciprofloxacin, asiatic acid exhibited the greater inhibitory effect on E. coli biofilm formation, as well as promoted the disruption of biofilm biomass (Wojnicz et al. 2015). Moreover, this pure compound has been demonstrated to reduce the tolerance of P. aeruginosa biofilm cells to aminoglycosides, thereby enhance the antibiotic efficacy (Garo et al. 2007).

A. baumannii has been included as one of the most significant and common drug-resistant pathogens in the “ESKAPE” group, which comprises Enterococcus faecium, S. aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and Enterobacter spp. (Howard et al. 2012). This pathogen is responsible for nosocomial infections and may pose a significant threat in immunocompromised individuals, especially those with extended hospital stays in intensive care units (ICUs). Besides, A. baumannii also possesses biofilm-forming capacity, highly complex and active microbial communities embedded within extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), which may further exacerbate bacterial infection potential (Reysenbach and Cady 2001). Specifically, this phenotype enables bacterial adhesion to human tissue or hospital surfaces under antiseptic condition, rendering such medical devices a key vector for chronic infection and dissemination (Greene et al. 2016). As a bacterial survival strategy, biofilm development is primarily governed by various environmental factors, including temperature, pH, nutrient availability, surface properties, and exposure to antimicrobial agents (Lichtenberg et al. 2023). Researchers have been intensively investigating biofilm growth based on bacterial genetic regulatory elements that orchestrate the physiological traits throughout the biofilm life cycle, in addition to extrinsic determinants. The biofilm life cycle encompasses the sequential stages through which microorganisms aggregate, accumulate and eventually disperse from biofilm communities (Penesyan et al. 2021). In A. baumannii, the chaperone-usher (Csu) pili assembly system plays a critical role in mediating the initial adhesion to both abiotic and biotic surfaces (Tomaras et al. 2003). Additionally, bap gene encodes biofilm-associated protein (Bap), which not only involves in colonization of human epithelial cells by targeting carbohydrates in host cells but also helps bacterial cells to survive on medically abiotic surfaces by forming and maintaining biofilms (Loehfelm et al. 2008). Following the adhesion and the establishment of bacterial communities, cells within developing biofilms engage in intercellular communication via quorum sensing to coordinate and sustain biofilm activity during the maturation stage. The quorum sensing system is regulated by small signaling molecules, called autoinducers, whose synthesis is governed by the abaI gene. This coordinated regulation enhances and optimizes the survival of the microbial community under hostile conditions (More et al. 1996; Dou et al. 2017). Since the biofilm growth involves a wide range of intrinsic regulatory factors, targeting specific stages of the biofilm life cycle have been proposed as promising strategies for effective control (De Melo et al. 2022). Consequently, our study aimed to explore the inhibitory effect of asiatic acid on biofilms generated by A. baumannii, including reference and clinically drug-resistant strains, while elucidating the molecular mechanisms through transcriptional analysis of csuE, bap, and abaI. Besides, we also assessed the combination therapy between asiatic acid and conventional antibiotics against drug-resistant pathogens. These findings might offer new insight into anti-biofilm strategy and overcoming antimicrobial resistance.

Materials and methods

Bacteria strains and growth media

A. baumannii ATCC 19606 strain and clinically isolated strains collected from hospitals across Thailand were used for this study. Their phenotypes were identified by microbiological and biochemical tests, while their genotypes were then confirmed by the existence of the blaOXA−51−like gene. Additionally, multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was performed to characterize the strains by housekeeping genes. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was employed according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) to access their antibiotic resistance profile (CLSI 2024).

All strains were preserved in Luria-Bertani broth (LB broth) with 15% (v/v) glycerol at -40 °C to makethe working stock cultures. After that, bacteria strains were cultured in Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA, HiMedia) and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h for resuscitation. Subsequently, A. baumannii strains were subculture in MHB broth (HiMedia) at 37 °C for 18–24 h under continuous shaking in preparation for further experiments.

Chemical preparations

The pure asiatic acid compound (Wuhan ChemFaces Biochemical Co., Ltd., China) was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and vortexed vigorously to ensure complete dissolution. Sterile ultrafiltration water was subsequently added to gain the stock solution at the concentration of 2 mg/mL. To prepare working solutions for experimental use, appropriate volumes of the stock solution were further diluted with sterile ultrafiltration water and mixed thoroughly by vortexing. The final DMSO percentages in all working solutions did not exceed 4% served as to avoid solvent-related effects.

Biofilm-forming capacity

Bacterial biofilm formation was evaluated using crystal violet assay with a minor adjustment (Stepanovic et al. 2007). Initially, mid-log phase culture of A. baumannii was adjusted to a final concentration of 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL in MHB using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher™) at an optical density of 600 nm. Biofilms were grown in 96-well flat-bottom tissue culture microtiter plates, with each strain inoculated into five replicate wells to minimize variability and enhance data reliability. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C under static conditions, non-adherent cells were discarded. Sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used to gently remove residual planktonic bacteria. Before the heat-fixation of biofilm, all wells were drained by flipping the microplate. Staining was performed with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet for 15 min. Excess stain was removed, and wells were washed again with PBS to eliminate unbound dye. Ethanol was added and gently shaken for solubilization prior to measuring the biofilm formation capacity of A. baumannii strains at OD570nm by a microplate reader (SPECTROstarNano, BMG LABTECH). Each experiment was performed in three biological triplicates. While A. baumannii ATCC 19606 served as a reference strain, MHB media was a negative control. Antimicrobial activity and sub-MICs determination

The antimicrobial activities of asiatic acid were determined by using the broth microdilution method (CLSI 2024). The overnight bacterial culture was adjusted by a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at an OD of 600 nm to obtain approximately 108 CFU/mL and further diluted to reach the final concentration of 5 × 105 CFU/mL. Two-fold serial dilutions of each compound were prepared in 96-well microtiter plates prior to the incubation at 37 ℃ for 20–24 h. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were recorded at the lowest compound concentration inhibiting the growth of bacteria. The standard antibiotic colistin served as a positive control, while DMSO at the corresponding final dilution was included as a vehicle control. This experiment was replicated thrice to confirm the results.

The sub-MICs of the natural compound was determined using the drop plate method (Herigstad et al. 2001). Bacterial cultures were collected from the supernatant of the biofilm formation inhibition assay after 24 h of incubation. Bacterial suspensions were then serially diluted and plated for enumeration. Bacterial growth was quantified by calculating the logarithm of colony-forming units per milliliter (log CFU/mL) to assess bacterial survival in response to sub-MIC exposure of the natural compound.

Biofilm formation inhibition assay

The anti-biofilm activity of asiatic acid against A. baumannii was assessed using the crystal violet assay (Herigstad et al. 2001). Bacterial cultures were prepared as previously described, with the addition of natural pure compounds at their sub-MICs ranging from 25 to 200 µg/mL. Following 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-adherent cells were eliminated by washing with PBS, and the remaining biofilms were stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet for 15 min, as previously described. The absorbance OD570nm was used to measure the remaining attached biofilms. Bacterial suspension without treatment was served as the nontreated control group, while MHB was used as a blank to normalize the data and estimate the ODcut value. Each experiment was performed in three biological triplicates.

Confocal laser scanning microscopic analysis

The impact of asiatic acid against A. baumannii biofilm structure was assessed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), which also enables real-time visualization of live and dead cells (Schlafer and Meyer 2017). Selected strains were cultured with the compound (0–100 µg/mL) in 8-well chamber slides (Nunc Lab-Tek, USA) for 24 h at 37 °C. Untreated biofilms served as negative controls. The remaining biofilms were then stained using the FilmTracer™ LIVE/DEAD® biofilm viability kit (Invitrogen, USA), which allows viability assessment without the need for cell permeabilization or fixation. The staining solution was applied to the biofilms and incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. This kit utilizes SYTO 9, which labels live cells, and propidium iodide, which penetrates only cells with compromised membranes (dead or damaged cells). Subsequently, excess dye was removed with PBS washes, and the biofilm samples were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany) equipped with a 63×oil immersion objective lens. Each experiment was performed in thrice. Quantitative analysis of biofilm architecture was performed using the COMSTAT 2.1 software, which assessed three structural parameters: mean thickness and total biomass (Heydorn et al. 2000). Differences in biofilm morphology and viability between treated and untreated samples were evaluated to determine the anti-biofilm activity of compound. Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

A. baumannii strains with and without treatment with asiatic acid (100–200 µg/mL) were prepared in the microplate at 37 °C for 24 h, as previously described. Following incubation, the bacterial cultures were harvested and transferred into eppendorf tubes for RNA extraction using the Tissue Total RNA Purification Mini Kit (FavorPrep™, Taiwan). The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were measured using a Nanodrop Microspectrophotometer (Hercuvan, UK). RT-qPCR was performed to evaluate the expression of biofilm-associated genes, including bap, csuE, and abaI, in response to compound treatment by using KAPA SYBR® FAST One-Step kit (Roche, Switzerland). The genes bap and csuE are indicative of the initial stages of biofilm formation, while abaI, a quorum sensing (QS) regulatory gene, reflects changes during mature biofilm development. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized by adding reverse transcription enzyme. The specific forward and reverse primers of target genes, which were listed in the Table 1, were then added into the proper RNA concentration of each sample for the preparation. SYBR Green served as the fluorescent reporter, binding to double-stranded DNA and enabling real-time detection of amplification, while ROX was used as a reference dye. Amplification was conducted using a real-time PCR machine (Agilent Mx3000P and Mx3005P) under optimized cycling conditions. Gene expression levels were normalized to the housekeeping gene rpoB, and relative expression changes were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. All gene expression measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and accuracy.

Antimicrobial synergy assay

The potential synergistic antimicrobial property of antibiotic in combination with asiatic acid was examined by microdilution checkerboard assay (Rand et al. 1993). Overnight cultures of A. baumannii ATCC 19606 were adjusted to yield the final concentration of 5 × 105 CFU/mL in fresh MHB. A range of antibiotics such as cefepime, ceftazidime, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, imipenem, meropenem, tobramycin, amikacin, and colistin were used at serially diluted concentrations (0.0625xMIC to 2xMIC). Then, pure asiatic acid was added at sub-inhibitory concentrations ranging from 0 to 400 µg/mL, followed by the addition of bacterial inoculum. An equivalent concentration of solvent was used as a vehicle control. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, 0.01% resazurin solution was used to assess the bacterial cell viability. This redox indicator enabled rapid and accurate assessment of bacterial viability, as metabolically active cells reduced non-fluorescent blue resazurin to fluorescent pink resorufin. While a persistent blue color indicated inhibited growth or bacterial death. Following visual interpretation, the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was calculated to assess the interaction between asiatic acid and each antibiotic via the following formula. Cytotoxicity assay

Vero cell line (CCL-81) derived from the African green monkey and human hepatocellular carcinoma epithelial-like HepG2 cell line (HB-8065) were obtained from ATCC. Vero cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. HepG2 was grown in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium including 10% FBS, 1% nonessential amino acids, 1% L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Both cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator. When cells reached 80–90% confluence, they were subcultured using trypsin-EDTA and seeded in 96-well microplates at a density of 2 × 10⁴ cells per well to assess cell viability.

After overnight incubation, cells were treated with increasing two-fold concentrations of asiatic acid (0–500 µg/mL) for 24 h. Then, the culture medium containing the compound was removed, and 5 mg/mL of 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2, 5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) solution was added into each well, followed by incubation for 3 h in the dark. The yellow, water-soluble MTT was reduced by metabolically active, viable cells to insoluble purple formazan crystals, which were subsequently dissolved by 100 µL of DMSO. The intensity of the solubilized formazan was quantified by measuring absorbance at OD540nm using a microplate reader (SPECTROstarNano, BMG LABTECH) to calculate percentage of cell viability. The cells cultured in medium alone served as a negative control. Experiments were performed in triplicate to determine the half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC₅₀) using nonlinear regression analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel (Version 16.96.1) and GraphPad Prism (Version 10.4.1). The data obtained from experiment was analyzed using One-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test to assess the effect of the compounds at different concentration. The P-value < 0.05 between the nontreated and treated groups was considered statistically significant.

Results

Asiatic acid possessed limited impact on A. baumannii growth

To investigate the inhibitory effect of asiatic acid on A. baumannii, both drug-resistant clinical isolates and the reference strain ATCC 19606 were selected based on their biofilm-forming capacities. Among these strains, ATCC 19606 and MTC 45026 displayed strong biofilm-forming ability, while MTC 45043 and MTC 45030 were categorized as moderate- biofilm producers. These isolates also represented distinct antimicrobial resistance phenotypes, providing a suitable model for evaluating the compound’s activity against both susceptible and resistant A. baumannii populations by using the broth microdilution method. In parallel, the antibacterial potential of DMSO was also evaluated, since it was used as the solvent for dissolving asiatic acid. The results showed that DMSO concentrations above 8% exhibited detectable antibacterial activity.

The detailed antimicrobial susceptibility profiles of all isolates are presented in Table 2. Antimicrobial assays revealed that asiatic acid exhibited MIC values exceeding 400 µg/mL against all strains, indicating very weak bactericidal effects under the tested conditions. At higher concentrations of asiatic acid, dissolution requires more than 4% DMSO. The potential antibacterial effect of solvent may occur and confound the experimental results.

These findings indicate that asiatic acid possesses negligible direct antibacterial activity toward A. baumannii. The relatively poor antimicrobial activity of pentacyclic triterpenoid is often constrained by low aqueous solubility and limited cell membrane permeability.

Inhibitory effect of asiatic acid on A. baumannii biofilm formation

Previous studies have demonstrated that certain natural compounds can interfere with A. baumannii biofilm development independently of their antimicrobial activity (Runci et al. 2017; Guo et al. 2024). In the present study, the anti-biofilm potential of asiatic acid was evaluated using sub-inhibitory concentrations to ensure bacterial viability during the assay. As shown in Fig. 1, a consistent inhibitory trend was observed across all A. baumannii strains, with biofilm formation decreasing beginning at 25 µg/mL to the most potent inhibition at 200 µg/mL after 24 h of exposure. Notably, asiatic acid reduced biofilm formation by more than 50% at concentrations of 100 µg/mL and above in all tested isolates. Importantly, bacterial growth was not significantly affected at these concentrations over the 24-hour incubation period, indicating that the observed inhibition of biofilm formation was not attributable to bactericidal effects. These findings suggest that asiatic acid specifically impairs the biofilm developmental process of A. baumannii, rather than exerting direct antibacterial activity.

Confocal microscopy analysis of asiatic acid effects on biofilm structure

To explicitly validate the impact of asiatic acid on A. baumannii biofilm development, three-dimensional biofilms were captured by CLSM. After 24 h of incubation, cells in the untreated control formed compact, multilayered biofilm structures characterized by dense aggregation and strong green fluorescence, indicative of viable cells within mature and intact biofilms (Fig. 2). For quantitative assessment, at least five randomly selected regions from each condition were analyzed using COMSTAT 2.1 software. As a result, a remarkable reduction in spatial biofilm parameters, including thickness and biomass, was observed. Additionally, the proportion of viable cells, shown by green fluorescence, did not significantly decrease following treatment, indicating limited bactericidal activity of asiatic acid on A. baumannii. These microscopic observations confirmed that asiatic acid attenuated biofilm formation at concentrations between 25 and 100 µg/mL, rather than direct antibacterial activity. A corresponding reduction in biofilm biomass was also detected by the earlier crystal violet assay. Collectively, these findings supported that the major mode of action of asiatic acid involving interference with biofilm development.

Transcriptional modulation of biofilm-forming genes by asiatic acid in A. baumannii

Although previous studies have demonstrated the anti-biofilm potential of asiatic acid, the molecular mechanisms underlying its inhibitory effects on A. baumannii biofilm formation have not been fully elucidated. In this study, we sought to infer possible mechanisms by integrating our phenotypic observations with the molecular expression profiles of genes associated with biofilm development and quorum sensing. Gene expression analyses were performed to assess the transcriptional responses of A. baumannii following exposure to sub-inhibitory concentrations of asiatic acid (100–200 µg/mL). focusing on key determinants involved in biofilm initiation and maturation As illustrated in Fig. 3, asiatic acid treatment resulted in a significant downregulation of csuE and bap transcripts in all tested strains. The csuE gene encodes a component of the Csu chaperone–usher pili assembly system, which facilitates surface attachment and the establishment of initial biofilm layers. Similarly, the bap gene encodes the biofilm-associated protein (Bap), a high-molecular-weight surface adhesin essential for intercellular aggregation and biofilm stability (De Gregorio et al. 2015). Suppression of these genes suggests that asiatic acid primarily interferes with the bacterial surface-adhesion and microcolony formation stages of the biofilm life cycle, thereby preventing proper biofilm maturation (Tomaras et al. 2003). Regarding QS regulation, the expression of abaI, a key autoinducer synthase gene involved in acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL) production, exhibited variable and statistically insignificant changes following treatment. This observation implies that the anti-biofilm activity of asiatic acid may not primarily involve interference with the QS signaling pathway. Instead, its principal mode of action likely focuses on the repression of structural and adhesion-related genes critical for surface colonization.

Asiatic acid exhibits an additive effect with certain antibiotics

Despite its minimal bactericidal activity, asiatic acid markedly suppressed biofilm formation in A. baumannii, thereby targeting a key virulence mechanism responsible for enhanced resistance and chronic infection. Therefore, we examined further whether asiatic acid could increase the bacterial susceptibility with conventional antibiotics to offer a new adjunctive agent in antimicrobial therapy. The results demonstrated that asiatic acid exerted an additive effect on the activity of various antibiotics, including cefepime, ceftazidime, imipenem, meropenem, tobramycin, and ciprofloxacin, against A. baumannii ATCC 19606. The MIC values of these antibiotics in the combination decreased 2-fold compared to when used alone (Table 3). This observed additive effect may be partly attributed to its anti-biofilm activity, either it is also possible that other mechanisms contribute to this interaction. It was noteworthy that combinations of asiatic acid with amikacin, levofloxacin, or colistin displayed no notable change in antimicrobial activity, suggesting an indifferent interaction. Although no synergistic effects were investigated, the absence of antagonistic interation allowed these combinations to be considered for further investigation without any concern for potential interference between agents.

To validate the safety of asiatic acid for potential anti-biofilm applications, we assessed its cytotoxicity effects at the concentrations of 0–500 µg/mL in two different cell lines, including the animal-derived Vero cells and the human HepG2 cells. The MTT assay was conducted to determine cell viability levels. As a result, the CC50 of asiatic acid in Vero cell line and HepG2 cell line showed 137.6 µg/mL and 142.9 µg/mL, respectively (Fig. 4). Accordingly, asiatic acid concentrations of 50–100 µg/mL exerted less toxicity in mammalian cell lines while simultaneously demonstrating remarkable anti-biofilm activity, implying suitable concentration range for further drug discovery applications. However, progressively cytotoxicity effects were observed at higher concentrations of asiatic acid, reaching up to 500 µg/mL.

Discussion

Bacterial cells embedded within biofilms enhance tolerance to antibiotic treatments, often leading to recurrent infections and therapeutic failures which pose a major challenge in current biofilm control strategies (Hoffman et al. 2005; Aka and Haji 2015). Hence, the effective management of biofilm-associated infections frequently necessitates alternative approaches aimed at preventing biofilm formation, disrupting maturation, inducing dispersal, or the integration of multiple therapeutic modalities (Kim et al. 2009; Lijuan et al. 2017).

Pentacyclic triterpenes possess a range of potent pharmacological properties, among which their antibacterial activity has been extensively investigated due to their ability to inhibit the growth of diverse pathogens (Jesus et al. 2023; Wojnicz et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2021). Previous studies have demonstrated that asiatic acid disrupted K+ homeostasis, a process maintained by membrane transporters. By enhancing the efflux of K+ from the cytoplasm, asiatic acid induced excessive ion loss, which could culminate in bacterial cell dysfunction and death (Liu et al. 2015). In addition, asiatic acid increased the release of DNA and RNA nucleotides, resulting in compromising bacterial integrity (Liu et al. 2015; Harnvoravongchai et al. 2018). Accordingly, asiatic acid showed antibacterial activity against numerous species, including E. coli, P. aeruginosa, Bacillus subtilis, B. cereus, L. monocytogenes, C. difficile, Enterococcus faecalis and S. aureus (Liu et al. 2015; Wojnicz et al. 2017; Harnvoravongchai et al. 2018). However, this compound expressed a negligible antimicrobial activity against A. baumannii strains in our study. These differences may be attributed to diverse sources of microbial strains, including phenotypic and genotypic traits of each species. Besides, the outer membrane present in gram-negative bacteria usually acts as a permeability barrier, which may limit acid penetration (Sycz et al. 2022). However, the permeability barrier evolved in a non-uniform manner, with significant diversification observed at the outer membrane across gram-negative species (Powers and Trent 2018; Geisinger et al. 2019). In A. baumannii, the outer membrane is composed of lipid A and core oligosaccharides but lacks the O‑antigen polysaccharide, resulting in lipooligosaccharide instead of the typical lipopolysaccharide found in other gram-negatives (Kenyon et al. 2014). The negatively charged residues within the core oligosaccharides reinforce ionic interactions, thereby enhancing outer membrane integrity and reducing permeability (VanOtterloo et al. 2024). Besides, A. baumannii constitutively produces a hepta‑acylated lipid A, in contrast to most gram‑negative bacteria, which typically possess six acyl chains and add a seventh only under stress conditions (Geisinger et al. 2019). Although lipooligosaccharide composition contributes a fundamental barrier in outer membrane, A. baumannii exhibits an outer membrane permeability about 100‑fold lower than enteric bacteria due to the absence of large-channel non-specific porins (Maher and Hassan 2023).

Interestingly, the anti-biofilm activities of asiatic acid have been demonstrated at the sub-MIC levels (Wojnicz et al. 2015; Sycz et al. 2021). Previous studies have shown that asiatic acid suppressed biofilm formation in E. coli, E. cloacae, and P. aeruginosa under both single- and mixed-species conditions (Sycz et al. 2021). Moreover, when used in combination with conventional antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin, asiatic acid effectively disrupted established E. coli biofilms (Wojnicz et al. 2015). In line with this finding, Garo et al. (2007) demonstrated that the compound enhanced antibiotic efficacy by reducing biofilm-associated tolerance of P. aeruginosa cells to tobramycin (Garo et al. 2007). These observations collectively suggest that asiatic acid acts as a biofilm-modulating agent with the potential to potentiate antibiotic activity against recalcitrant bacterial infections. Consistent with these earlier reports, the present study confirmed that asiatic acid reduced biofilm formation in both drug-resistant clinical isolates and A. baumannii ATCC 19606 reference strain at concentrations ranging from 25 to 200 µg/mL following 24 h of exposure. Microscopic visualization using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), combined with quantitative COMSTAT analysis, revealed significant alterations in biofilm parameters, including decreased biomass density and mean thickness in treated samples compared with the untreated control. Importantly, these structural changes occurred without notable bactericidal effects, reinforcing that the compound specifically targets biofilm development processes rather than bacterial viability.

To explore the underlying mechanism, we assessed the effect of asiatic acid on distinct stages of the biofilm life cycle. This development process typically involved distinct biofilm-associated genes and pathways depending on the species and environmental conditions (Penesyan et al. 2021). Transcriptional analysis technique was used to evaluate the expression of csuE and bap, and a quorum sensing signaling gene, abaI. Normalization of RT-qPCR data was performed using the reference housekeeping gene rpoB, which was considered adequate to ensure stable normalization in A. baumannii (de Oliveira et al. 2024; Lutfi et al. 2024). However, no statistical algorithms were employed to formally assess the stability of the reference gene across nontreated and treated samples, representing a methodological limitation of the study.

Among numerous important virulence factors, pili are crucial for the initial adhesion of A. baumannii biofilms to surfaces. Particularly, csuE is a part of pili assembly, playing a key role in surface recognition and tip adhesion (Tomaras et al. 2003). Bap, encoded by bap gene, is a large protein secreted via a type I system and contains repetitive immunoglobulin-like domains. This protein contributes to the first bacterial colonization, thereby promoting intercellular adhesion within the biofilm, which is essential for biofilm maintenance (Loehfelm et al. 2008). Over time, the accumulation of the attached bacterial population becomes increasing, which triggers the quorum sensing (QS) system to respond to environmental changes (Novick and Geisinger 2008). In A. baumannii, the QS system is primarily mediated by N-acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL), encoded by abaI gene (More et al. 1996; Dou et al. 2017). By facilitating communication between bacterial cells in biofilms, these signaling molecules are able to regulate the expression or suppression of virulence genes (Novick and Geisinger 2008). Therefore, microorganisms can optimize resource nutrition and enhance the overall resilience of biofilm communities through these coordinated activities. Our results showed that the expression levels of csuE and bap were significantly downregulated upon treatment with 100–200 µg/mL asiatic acid. Regarding quorum sensing genes, asiatic acid caused a slight reduction in the expression of abaI. These results indicated that asiatic acid inhibited biofilm formation in A. baumannii, including clinical XDR strains, by disrupting initial attachment and colonization on abiotic surfaces during the early stages. However, asiatic acid exerted a minimal impact on bacterial communication.

The early prevention of biofilm establishment may reduce bacterial persistence against treatment and chronic infections. This property reinforces potential applicability of asiatic acid in attenuation of bacterial virulence factor, thereby highlighting treatment regimens aimed at overcoming the clinical challenges posed by persistent biofilm-related infections. Particularly, combination therapy involving two antimicrobial agents has become a common strategy in clinical practice to improve treatment efficacy and potentially slowing the emergence of resistance (Woods and Read 2023). In this study, we assessed the combinatory effect of asiatic acid with different groups of antibiotics, including cephalosporins, carbapenems, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, and polymyxins, which perform distinct mechanisms of action. Potentially additive effects of asiatic acid when combined with cefepime, tobramycin, or ciprofloxacin against A. baumannii ATCC 19606 growth were reported, which may be partly attributed to its anti-biofilm activity, either it was also possible that other mechanisms contributed to this interaction. Previously, asiatic acid has been proposed to exert the antibacterial effect through disruption of bacterial cell membranes in gram-negative species such as E. coli O157:H7, Salmonella Typhimurium, and P. aeruginosa (Liu et al. 2015). Thus, asiatic acid could additively contribute to the antimicrobial activity of conventional antibiotics against A. baumannii. However, precise mechanisms and potential effects with conventional antibiotics remain insufficiently investigated. Another study has reported that asiatic acid can enhance the susceptibility of P. aeruginosa biofilms to ciprofloxacin and tobramycin, indicating a potential synergistic interaction (Garo et al. 2007). Regarding gram-positive bacteria, asiatic acid has previously shown no synergistic effect with vancomycin or metronidazole against C. difficile (Harnvoravongchai et al. 2018). Further studies, such as transcriptomic profiling, membrane integrity assays, or enzyme activity measurements, are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved. Additionally, extensive investigation of the combinatorial effects of asiatic acid with antibiotics across multiple clinical isolates would substantially enhance its translational relevance and potential clinical applicability.

Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of asiatic acid was evaluated at concentrations up to 500 µg/mL. Though the progressive cytotoxic effects observed at higher concentrations, asiatic acid 50–100 µg/mL were associated with limited toxicity in mammalian cell lines while simultaneously exhibiting marked anti-biofilm activity against A. baumannii. Further in vivo investigations are warranted to validate these observations, assess pharmacokinetic properties, and determine the clinical applicability of asiatic acid.

Conclusion and perspectives

In summary, although asiatic acid exhibited minimal direct antimicrobial activity against A. baumannii, it effectively reduced biofilm formation. This anti-biofilm effect was mediated in part by downregulation of the csuE and bap genes, a critical pili assembly and bacterial surface-adhesion during the early stages of biofilm development. Importantly, the combination of asiatic acid with conventional antibiotics, including cefepime, tobramycin, and ciprofloxacin, resulted in additive effects, highlighting its potential as an adjunctive therapeutic agent against biofilm-forming and drug-resistant A. baumannii. These findings suggest that natural triterpenoids such as asiatic acid may offer a dual advantage: directly attenuating virulence-related biofilm formation while simultaneously supporting the effectiveness of standard antimicrobial treatments. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate the precise molecular pathways by which asiatic acid modulates biofilm formation, including potential effects on quorum-sensing networks, surface adhesins, and extracellular matrix synthesis.

Besides, the identification of a safety concentration range up to 100 µg/mL supports the feasibility of incorporating asiatic acid into integrated strategies for controlling biofilm‑associated infections, particularly those caused by drug‑resistant pathogens. Additional studies exploring optimized delivery methods, chemical modifications, dose refinement could further enhance its bioavailability and therapeutic potential.
