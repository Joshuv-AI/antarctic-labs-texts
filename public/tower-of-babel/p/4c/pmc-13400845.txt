Secondary invasive aspergillosis following severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection in children: Report of two cases
Authors: Mengjie Yang, Hongjuan Huang, Xin Li, Yanhong Lu, Yuqing Wang, Lili Huang, Qian Yang, Mi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReports of secondary invasive aspergillosis subsequent to Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection are rare. Herein, we present two cases of invasive aspergillosis following severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection and discuss the differences in risk factors for invasive aspergillosis between pediatric and adult populations. Dynamic etiological assessment is crucial in complex clinical scenarios. Furthermore, close collaboration within the multidisciplinary team contributes to optimizing patient prognosis.Keywords: Invasive aspergillosis, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Children, Glucocorticoid, Risk factor

Introduction

In recent years, the continuous increase in medical interventions, the widespread use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and glucocorticoids, and advancements in organ transplantation and central venous catheterization have significantly increased the risk of fungal opportunistic infections [1], [2], [3]. Candida and Aspergillus spp. are the most prevalent fungi leading to invasive fungal infections (IFIs). Preterm and very low birth weight newborns are high-risk populations [4], and invasive aspergillosis (IA) is typically seen in children with immunodeficiency, but there is a growing number of cases in critically ill patients [5]. The risk factors, differential diagnosis, and treatment of IFIs in children are somewhat different from those in adults. The challenge of treatment requires more multidisciplinary involvement for children with IFIs.

Influenza virus and SARS-Cov−2 infection can increase the risk of invasive aspergillosis, because these viral infections may induce transient immune dysfunction [6]. Laura Peccini, et al. reported a case of sensitivity to Aspergillus fumigatus after Mycoplasma pneumoniae(MP) infection in children with cystic fibrosis [7]. But the existing evidence does not support a significant association between MP and Aspergillus spp. infection. However, during the previous MP epidemic season, two critically ill children with complex underlying diseases developed secondary aspergillosis after MP infection in our center. This highlighted the need to pay attention to the risk of secondary aspergillosis in children.

Case 1

A 4-year-old boy was admitted with a one-week history of fever and persistent dry cough for 4 days. He had no prior medical history. Initial laboratory examinations showed normal white blood cell and C-reactive protein levels, mild neutrophilia, and marked consolidation in the right upper lobe consistent with lobar pneumonia on chest CT. Empirical oseltamivir was initiated, and a respiratory virus panel assay was subsequently performed. Oseltamivir was discontinued once the viral test returned negative. Further serological tests yielded positive Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) IgM antibodies and negative IgG antibodies. Markedly elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, 418.5 U/L), D-dimer (2180.0 μg/L), and fibrinogen degradation product (FDP, 7470.0 μg/L) indicated severe MP pneumonia, while the ferritin concentration remained within the normal range. The interleukin−6 (IL-6) level was 86.1 pg/mL. Results of lymphocyte subset testing were as follows: CD19⁺CD23⁺ cells 1.70%, total lymphocyte count 996 cells/μL, CD3⁺CD8⁺ cell count 279.44 cells/μL, and CD3⁻CD19⁺ cell count 184.26 cells/μL. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) identified Pseudomonas aeruginosa (111468 reads) and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (72172 reads), with neutrophils accounting for 73% of cells in the BALF sample. Bronchoscopy revealed obstructive plastic bronchial casts in the posterior segment of the right upper lobe. Considering the seasonal MP epidemic, this pathogen was responsible for 70% of respiratory tract infections among recently enrolled outpatient cases. Integrating radiographic features, clinical manifestations, and laboratory data, the patient was clinically diagnosed with MP pneumonia. Treatment consisting of azithromycin, intravenous methylprednisolone (30 mg every 8 h, 5.3 mg/kg/day), and cefoperazone–sulbactam was initiated. The methylprednisolone dose was gradually tapered after 6 days of therapy (Fig. 1).

On hospital day 17, the patient developed recurrent fever without chills or convulsions. His temperature responded to oral antipyretic agents, and he presented with a mild cough. A qualitative respiratory pathogen panel detected positive nucleic acids for influenza A virus, adenovirus and human metapneumovirus. mNGS performed on BALF identified adenovirus (350 reads), MP (4784 reads) and Aspergillus fumigatus (45 reads). Intravenous peramivir was administered for three consecutive days. The patient subsequently developed neutropenia, with an absolute neutrophil count of 0.73 × 10⁹/L. Chest CT on hospital day 22 revealed new scattered infiltrates in the right lower lung with perilesional halo signs, together with diffuse cystic lesions in the upper lobes (Fig. 2). Quantitative MP-DNA testing of BALF yielded a result below 2.5 × 10 ³ copies/mL, ruling out recurrent exacerbation of MP infection; therefore, gradual tapering of glucocorticoids was continued. BALF culture grew penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae (PRSP) at approximately 3 × 10 ³ CFU/mL, alongside Aspergillus fumigatus at 9 × 10² CFU/mL. Antifungal drug susceptibility testing was not conducted. Vancomycin was added to the treatment regimen, and serum fungal serological assays were arranged. The serum 1,3-β-D-glucan level was 52.760 pg/mL, and the serum galactomannan (GM) index for mold antigen was 0.710. Although the patient had achieved a favorable response to prior anti-MP therapy, he developed recurrent fever and new radiographic abnormalities. Combined with the elevated serum GM level and positive Aspergillus culture from BALF, together with the multidisciplinary team discussion, a clinical diagnosis of pulmonary aspergillosis was established, despite the relatively low number of Aspergillus reads detected on mNGS.

The chest CT image on admission demonstrates lobar pneumonia in this patient. New scattered pulmonary infiltrates emerged on hospital day 22, which progressed to linear opacities and small necrotic cavities after treatment (days 35, 37, and 151).

Oral voriconazole was prescribed at a dose of 160 mg every 12 h (patient weight: 18.3 kg, approximately 8.7 mg/kg per dose). Repeated bronchoscopic bronchoalveolar lavage was performed during hospitalization. Fungal hyphae were visualized on BALF smear microscopy, whereas only sparse Aspergillus colonies were recovered from culture. Antifungal treatment effectively suppressed fungal proliferation and was deemed clinically efficacious. Voriconazole therapy was continued for a total of 8 weeks, and the patient eventually achieved full clinical recovery. Follow-up chest CT scans revealed faint residual linear opacities and small cavitary lesions (Fig. 2, hospital days 35, 37, and 151).

Case 2

A 1-year-old boy underwent multiple surgical procedures for pulmonary artery occlusion (type IV), ventricular septal defect, atrial septal defect, and congenital systemic pulmonary collateral circulation. The last surgical procedure was Pulmonary artery fusion (single source), systemic to pulmonary artery bypass surgery (Fig. 3, day 0). On postoperative day 3, the child developed persistent fever, accompanied by an elevated white blood cell count (WBC, 15.21 ×10⁹/L) and C-reactive protein (CRP, 11.19 mg/L). Given the development of hemodynamic instability, empirical antimicrobial therapy with vancomycin, meropenem and fluconazole was initiated. A respiratory viral nucleic acid panel was subsequently tested and returned negative results. As a nationwide and regional MP epidemic was ongoing at that time, MP infection was highly suspected. The fever persisted, with a peak temperature of 39.9 °C on postoperative day 11. Following written informed consent obtained from the patient’s guardians, the antimicrobial regimen was switched to doxycycline after confirmation of positive MP IgG and IgM serology. Despite the patient being younger than 8 years of age, azithromycin was not administered, mainly for two reasons: the child had complex hemodynamic status; intravenous azithromycin requires a large dilution volume and carries potential cardiotoxicity, leading to relatively high clinical risks in this case. Subsequent quantitative MP-DNA testing of BALF was 3.0 × 10⁴ copies/mL. After doxycycline was initiated, the patient’s clinical symptoms improved markedly, hemodynamic parameters gradually stabilized, and his body temperature returned to normal.

On hospital day 18, the patient developed recurrent high fever, leukocytosis, and progressive hemodynamic instability. Pulmonary auscultation detected prominent moist rales. Bedside chest radiography showed patchy blurred high-density opacities in the right upper lung lobe. Bronchoscopy revealed substantial viscous purulent secretions within the airway. Subsequent BALF culture yielded positive growth of Aspergillus fumigatus. mNGS further identified Aspergillus fumigatus in both BALF (36,488 reads) and peripheral blood (2 reads) specimens. Accordingly, the treatment regimen was adjusted to intravenous amphotericin B deoxycholate (D-AMB) at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg.

The child exhibited a favorable response to this treatment, with gradual defervescence, stabilized vital signs, and improved inflammatory markers. After two weeks of amphotericin B deoxycholate (D-AMB) therapy, the patient was transferred out of the intensive care unit. Following six weeks of antifungal treatment, the regimen was switched to voriconazole (VOR) due to an elevated serum creatinine level of 90.8 μmol/L. After four weeks of VOR administration, the trough blood concentration of voriconazole reached 2.11 μg/mL, and renal function returned to normal. The patient was discharged on oral VOR after a 10-week in-hospital treatment course. Routine follow-up recorded a voriconazole blood concentration of 1.06 μg/mL. Ultimately, antifungal therapy was discontinued after a total treatment duration of 16 weeks.

Discussion

The occurrence of invasive fungal infections includes three main factors: host, pathogen, and environment. More and more host-related risk factors are being included in updates to diagnostic criteria, such as granulocytopenia, congenital immunodeficiency, use of glucocorticoids, small molecule targeted drugs, cirrhosis etc [1], [2]. However, these updates are mostly focused on adult patients. There is a significant lag in the understanding of host-related risk factors in pediatric patients. The definition of risk factors for children in EORTC/MSGERC still focuses on premature birth and low body weight, which are commonly associated with candidiasis. Furthermore, EORTC/MSGERC proposes the necessity of establishing a specialized definition for invasive fungal diseases in children [3]. But there are more congenital immune deficiencies.

In addition, another issue worth discussing is glucocorticoids, which is also involved in the Case 1. The use of glucocorticoids at a dose of 20 mg or more of prednisone per day (another expression: prolonged use of corticosteroids at a therapeutic dose of ≥0.3 mg/kg corticosteroids for ≥3 weeks in the past 60 days) is recognized as a contributing host risk factor to fungal infections according to major guidelines on fungal diseases [1], [3]. However, this dose criterion may not serve as a valid risk factor for children. Although sufficient evidence is currently lacking to confirm discrepancies in dose-related exposure risk factors between children and adults, corticosteroids, often at high doses, are frequently administered to pediatric patients with respiratory infections for the management of wheezing or bronchiolitis obliterans induced by viral infections and other pathogens. In cases of severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia(MPP), varying high doses of glucocorticoids are typically prescribed based on the severity of the condition. The recommended dosage of methylprednisolone is 1–2 mg/(kg·d) for mild or moderate MPP and 4–6 mg/kg for severe MPP [8], [9]. While this dosage may be suitable for treating MPP, it can also pose a potential risk of fungal infection, particularly in cases where there is a co-infection with influenza virus, SARS-CoV−2, adenovirus, or other pathogens. The overlap of viral infection and glucocorticoid use elevates the risk of infection. Meanwhile, given that plastic bronchitis and necrotizing pneumonia may complicate MP pneumonia, cumulative exposure to high-dose corticosteroids warrants close attention. In Case 1, the patient's dose of methylprednisolone was more than 5 mg/kg, and there were no other clear host factors causing immunodeficiency. At that time, due to the widespread prevalence of MP in China, clinicians primarily focused on the treatment of MPP and overlooked the potential risks of IA.

In addition to host factors, environmental factors are also an important consideration for IFIs. Increasing evidence indicates that newborns or infants admitted to the intensive care unit face a heightened risk of developing invasive pulmonary aspergillosis. Factors like extensive surgical procedures, multiple medications, and concurrent infections can further escalate the susceptibility of these infants to fungal infections. Surgical procedures can induce multiple immune disorders. With few exceptions, a systemic postoperative immunosuppressive state is generally observed, whose severity and duration are partially determined by the degree of surgical trauma. Surgery-induced immunosuppression primarily results from impaired function of immune cell subsets. Although surgical stress increases the total peripheral leukocyte count, key leukocyte subsets exhibit quantitative and functional suppression [10]. Previous reviews have summarized cases of postoperative Aspergillus infection following cardiac, ophthalmic, and dental surgeries, indicating that invasive aspergillosis after major surgery represents a clinically noteworthy issue [11]. The difficulty of Case 2 lies in factors such as hemodynamic instability, poor cough reflex and poor airway self-purification caused by complex congenital heart disease; co-infection with MP and Aspergillus fumigatus, which exacerbates lung damage; the high metabolic rate in infants requiring high doses of antifungal drugs [12]; and the challenge of balancing antifungal drug dosage with the risk of organ damage. A multidisciplinary team comprising cardiothoracic surgeons, respiratory specialists, microbiologists, and clinical pharmacists plays a vital role in delivering personalized care. Cardiothoracic surgeons provided advanced life support; respiratory specialists performed bronchoscopy and airway management; microbiologists provided pathogen analysis and drug sensitivity tests; and clinical pharmacists conducted therapeutic drug monitoring and individualized dose adjustment. The concerted efforts of diverse disciplines are essential for the effective management of these vulnerable patients.

The two cases presented in this study do not demonstrate a definitive association between MP infection and aspergillosis, such as the causal link observed between influenza and secondary fungal infections. However, MP infection may predispose patients to mixed infections with other pathogens, with multiple confounding exposure factors involved, including complex pathophysiological conditions, environmental influences, and therapeutic medications. Therefore, clinicians should remain alert to such rare infectious complications in clinical practice, and dynamic assessment of the patient’s clinical status is essential for timely diagnosis and intervention.
