Functional inputs to the subgenual cingulate cortex differentiate levels of depressive symptom severity in neurotypical young adults: granger causality and clustering analyses
Authors: Huey-Ting Li, Yu Chen, Shefali Chaudhary, Jaime S Ide, Chiang-Shan R Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionExtant research has implicated functional connectivity of the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) in major depressive disorders or depressive traits in neurotypical populations. However, prior studies have not distinguished the inputs and outputs of the sgACC, and the “diagnostic” accuracy of these connectivity metrics remains elusive.MethodsHere, we analyzed data of 890 neurotypical young adults (459 women, age 22–35) from the Human Connectome Project using Granger causality analyses (GCA) with the sgACC as the seed and 268 regions of interest from the Shen’s atlas as targets. Depressive and other comorbid symptoms were assessed using the Adult Self Report. Individual connectivities were assessed with an F test and group results were evaluated with a binomial test, both at a corrected threshold.ResultsWe identified brain regions with significant inputs to and outputs from the sgACC. Clustering analyses of Granger causality inputs, but not Granger causality outputs or resting state connectivity features revealed distinct subject clusters, effectively distinguishing individuals with different levels of depressive symptom severity and those with comorbidities. Specifically, weaker projections from the fronto-parietal and orbitofrontal cortices, anterior insula, temporal cortices, and cerebellum to the sgACC characterized five clusters with low to high scores of depression as well as comorbid internalizing and externalizing problems. Machine learning using a logistic classifier with the significant “GCA-in” features and 5-fold cross-validation achieved 87% accuracy in distinguishing subject clusters, including those with high vs. low depressive symptom severity levels.DiscussionThese new findings specify the functional inputs and outputs of the sgACC and highlight an outsized role of sgACC inputs in distinguishing individuals’ depressive symptom severity in a neurotypical population.

INTRODUCTION

The subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC), a key hub of the emotion-regulating limbic circuit,1 is widely implicated in the pathophysiology of mood disorders.2,3 Altered sgACC activity has been observed in individuals with major depressive disorder (MDD).4–6 Greater sgACC activation to sad emotional stimuli early during treatment predicted improvement in depression scores with 10 weeks of antidepressant regimen.7 In neurotypical populations, elevated sgACC responses to social exclusion predicted later depressive symptoms in adolescents.8 These findings highlight sgACC’s crucial role in emotion processing and sgACC dysfunction as a neural marker for depression and treatment response.

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) targeting the sgACC have shown promise in treating people with depression.9 Chronic DBS of white matter tracts near the sgACC led to sustained remission for up to 6 months,10 with at least 50% reduction in depression scores.11 DBS in the sgACC also improved empathic responses to negative stimuli and reduced depression severity in treatment-resistant MDD.11 Repetitive TMS of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) modulated sgACC activity in depressed patients,12 with treatment responders showing negative sgACC-dlPFC connectivity at rest.13 Further, baseline sgACC-frontoparietal connectivity predicted response to escitalopram over 12 weeks of treatment,14 highlighting sgACC functional connectivity as a biomarker for treatment efficacy.

Resting-state functional connectivity (rsFC) reveals brain’s functional architecture by quantifying regional correlations of spontaneous neural activity, independent of task demands.15 Numerous studies have highlighted altered rsFC in neuropsychiatric disorders, including depression and anxiety, reflecting intrinsic brain networks in link with cognitive dysfunction and emotional dysregulation.16,17 Studies showed altered sgACC rsFC’s with frontoparietal, salience, limbic-striatal, and default mode networks (DMN) in depression.18 Relative to healthy controls, MDD patients showed higher sgACC rsFC’s with the prefrontal cortex (PFC)19,20 and amygdala21,22 and lower rsFC’s with the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), hippocampus, thalamus, and insula.20,23 These connectivity patterns correlated with depression severity in both clinical19,20,22,23 and subclinical24,25 populations.

Whereas the rsFC describes correlated brain activities, it does not determine the direction of influence. Granger causality analysis (GCA), a statistical method for assessing directional relationships in time-series data, helps in distinguishing inputs from outputs.26 For instance, GCA demonstrated enhanced bottom-up connections from the thalamus to cortical and subcortical regions, along with reduced top-down connections to the thalamus, in people with MDD.27 On the other hand, despite extensive evidence implicating the sgACC in depression, no studies have distinguished the potential roles of sgACC inputs and outputs in depression.

To address this issue, we investigated Granger causal connectivities of 268 regions of interest (ROIs28,29) with the sgACC in 890 neurotypical individuals curated from the Human Connectome Project (HCP). Although the HCP comprises a largely non-clinical sample, the participants varied significantly in the extent of depression and comorbid problems. Investigating sgACC connectivities in this sample may allow us to understand the neurophysiological processes early in the onset of clinical depression.30,31 After identifying ROIs with significant sgACC inputs and outputs, we used connectivity strength (F values) in cluster analysis to identify groups with distinct connectivity features. We hypothesized that these features could differentiate individuals with varying depression and/or anxiety scores and potential comorbidities. Lastly, we applied machine learning to evaluate the accuracy at which these features predicted group membership. Our overall goal was to define sgACC functional inputs and outputs and characterize their potentially distinct roles in individual depression.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Dataset: Subjects and Assessments

The HCP Young Adult (HCP-YA) S1200 release contains clinical, behavioral, and 3T magnetic resonance (MR) imaging data of 1,206 young healthy adults without severe neurodevelopmental, neuropsychiatric, or neurologic disorders. In the current study, a total of 316 subjects were excluded due to missing resting-state fMRI (rsfMRI) or clinical data (n = 110), excessive head movements and non-stationary time series (n = 206, see details in MRI Data Preprocessing and Granger Causality Analysis). Therefore, the final cohort comprised 890 subjects (459 women, age 22–35). Participants completed the Achenbach Adult Self Report (ASR32), where individual questions were rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0-Not True, 1-Somewhat or Sometimes True, and 2-Very True or Often True). We used the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual-oriented depression and anxiety raw scores in our analyses. The ASR also includes eight syndromal scales, including anxious/depressed syndromes; withdrawn syndromes; somatic complaints; thought problems; inattention problems; aggression; rule-breaking behaviors; and intrusive problems, and allowed us to evaluate comorbidities of the subject clusters. For depression, anxiety, and all comorbid syndromes, higher scores indicate greater symptom severity.

MRI Protocol

As described earlier,33,34 the details of the data collection procedures can be found in the HCP-YA S1200 Release Reference Manual. All imaging data were acquired on a customized Siemens 3T Skyra with a standard 32-channel Siemens receiver head coil and a body transmission coil. T1-weighted high-resolution structural images were acquired using a 3D MPRAGE sequence with 0.7 mm isotropic resolution [Field of View (FOV) = 224 mm, matrix = 320, 256 sagittal slices, Repetition Time (TR) = 2400 ms, Time to Echo (TE) = 2.14 ms, Inversion Time (TI) = 1000 ms, Flip Angle (FA) = 8°] and used to register rsfMRI data to a standard brain space. The rsfMRI data were collected in two sessions, using gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) with 2.0 mm isotropic resolution (FOV = 208 × 180 mm, matrix = 104 × 90, 72 slices, TR = 720 ms, TE = 33.1 ms, FA = 52°, multi-band factor = 8). Within each session, oblique axial acquisitions alternated between phase encoding in a right-to-left direction in one run and a left-to-right direction in the other run. Each run lasted 14.4 min (1200 frames).

MRI Data Preprocessing and Granger Causality Analysis (GCA)

Preprocessing was applied to reduce spurious Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) variances that were unlikely to reflect neuronal activity.35,36 The sources of spurious variance were removed by regressing out signals from the ventricular system, white matter, and whole brain, in addition to the six parameters obtained by rigid body head motion correction. Additionally, we applied a temporal band-pass filter (0.009 Hz < f < 0.08 Hz) to the time course in order to obtain low-frequency fluctuations.37 Cordes et al. (2001) suggested that BOLD fluctuations below a frequency of 0.1 Hz contribute to regionally specific38 BOLD correlations. Next, average time series were extracted for ROIs defined according to the Yale brain atlas of 268 regions covering the whole brain29 as well as for the sgACC.

Granger causality between the sgACC and each of the 268 ROIs was computed using F-values to represent the causality strength.39 With a TR of 0.72 s, we used model order 2 rather than higher orders to avoid model overfitting in GCA.40 We called the influence of sgACC on other ROIs “GCA-out,” and the influence of ROIs on the sgACC “GCA-in.” For both GCA-in and GCA-out, the connectivity significance for each individual was assessed using the F-statistics of autoregressive modeling. Multiple comparisons across ROIs were evaluated at P<.01, corrected for false discovery rate (FDR). Group results were evaluated with a binomial test of the number of subjects with versus without a significant input or output connection for each ROI, again at P<.01, Bonferroni corrected.41

Although GCA has been widely applied to rsfMRI data, we note that directional dependence estimates derived from hemodynamic signals are subject to critical limitations, including variability in the hemodynamic response function (HRF), finite temporal resolution, and assumptions of covariance stationarity. Although HRF deconvolution was recommended for GCA analyses for event-related data,42 there would be no clear events to model for rsfMRI. Thus, in the present study, we did not perform HRF deconvolution.

Throughout the manuscript, the terms “GCA-in” and “GCA-out” refer to directional statistical dependencies as estimated from BOLD time series rather than direct neural causality. Accordingly, these measures are interpreted as relative directional influences under the constraints of hemodynamic fMRI data. We used these abbreviated terms for readability and cautioned about the interpretation of the GCA findings.

In addition to GCA-in and GCA-out, we also computed the rsFC’s of the sgACC with the 268 ROIs, using the same preprocessed BOLD data and following a published pipeline.34,43 Pearson’s correlations were computed pairwise, and z-transform was applied to the coefficients for one-sample t-test in group analysis. These rsFC’s served as features in a “control” set of clustering analysis, too.

Clustering and Statistical Analyses

The connectivity metrics were divided into GCA-in and GCA-out features, each representing the F-in and F-out values, respectively, for significant ROIs, ie, those with significant sgACC inputs or outputs. We applied k-means clustering to GCA-in and GCA-out features, respectively, and determined the optimum number of clusters (optimum-k) using the GAP statistics.44 The GAP(k) statistics quantify the within-cluster dispersion of the actual data relative to randomly generated reference datasets (null distribution). The reference datasets are simulated in a way that preserves the original data (ie, the overall shape in principal component space). The optimum-k was selected for the first k where the GAP statistic was not significantly smaller than the statistic for k + 1, considering the standard deviations derived from the Monte-Carlo simulations. This is a more robust approach than simply selecting the maximum gap statistic because it considers the variability in GAP statistic estimates. The clusters represented distinct groups of subjects distinguished according to GCA-in or GCA-out metrics. In other words, subjects with similar GCA connectivity patterns were grouped together in a cluster. As a control, we also performed clustering analysis using the 268 sets of rsFC’s as features.

We next investigated if the subject clusters, with distinct GCA connectivity patterns, differed in demographic and ASR measures. We performed a one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA), followed by post-hoc t-tests pairwise of the clusters, to examine whether the subject clusters differed in age and/or different levels of symptom severity. Differences in sex composition across the clusters were examined pairwise by chi-square tests.

To examine which GCA features were associated with depression severity, we ordered the cluster-labels according to the ASR scores for correlation with the neural features (ie, GCA-in or -out F values). We ordered the cluster-labels by increasing value of depression scores, so that a significant negative correlation indicated that lower GCA connectivity (F values) of a particular ROI with the sgACC was associated with higher depressive symptom severity, and vice versa. To assess the significance of the correlations, we employed a permutation test. This non-parametric method provides a robust way to estimate empirical P-values without making strong assumptions about the data distribution, with the following procedures: i) calculate correlations: we first calculated the Pearson’s correlation coefficients between each connectivity feature and the cluster label; ii) generate null distribution: to create a null distribution, we randomly permuted the cluster labels while keeping the feature values fixed. This removed the relationships between the features and clusters, simulating a scenario where there was no association. For each permutation, we calculated the correlation coefficients between the features and shuffled labels. This process was repeated 10 000 times to generate a distribution of correlation coefficients under the null hypothesis of no association; and iii) estimate empirical P-values: for each feature, we compared the observed correlation coefficient to the null distribution. The empirical P-value was calculated as the proportion of permuted correlation coefficients that were greater than or equal to the absolute value of the observed correlation coefficient. This provides an estimate of the probability of observing a correlation as extreme as the observed one if there were no true associations between the feature and cluster labels.

To explore potential sex effects, we conducted additional analyses incorporating sex as a factor in models relating GCA-in features to cluster membership and symptom severity. Specifically, for clinical measures showing significant cluster differences, we performed two-way ANOVA with cluster and sex as fixed factors. This approach allowed us to evaluate main effects of cluster and sex as well as the cluster-by-sex interaction. These analyses were conducted for all ASR domains that differed significantly across clusters in the primary analyses. For outcomes showing a significant cluster-by-sex interaction, we conducted follow-up simple effect analyses to characterize the nature of the interaction. Specifically, we examined sex differences within each cluster using pairwise comparisons derived from the ANOVA model. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for Type-I error due to multiple post hoc tests.

Predicting Depression Using Multi-Label Classification

To assess whether and how well the GCA features could predict levels of depression, we used the ordered cluster-labels as class-labels for supervised learning. This approach has the benefit of filtering out variance present in the clinical measures. We employed two methods – multinomial logistic and naïve Bayes modeling – in the multi-label classification.

The analysis followed a two-stage pipeline: training and testing (Supplementary Figure S1). The classifier was trained on a portion of the clustered data, using 5-fold cross-validation to assess their performance on unseen data. This cross-validation procedure allowed us to evaluate the performance of the classifiers. To quantify classification performance, we used accuracy, balanced accuracy, and macro-averaged F1-score as performance metrics. Accuracy was the overall proportion of correctly classified instances. Balanced accuracy was the average recall for each class. Macro-averaged F1-score was the average of the F1-score for each class, where the F1-score was the harmonic mean of precision and recall. These metrics provided a comprehensive evaluation of the classifier’s performance across all classes, especially in cases where the classes may have different sizes or importance. The entire pipeline was implemented using Python (scikit-learn==1.0.2).

To evaluate the discriminative power of the brain regions’ significant GCA features, we additionally performed the logistic regression classifier with a nested cross-validation (CV) approach to address potential circularity issues (“double-dipping”). We implemented a 10-fold outer loop to estimate the generalization performance of the pipeline. Within each outer loop, a 5-fold inner loop was utilized for hyperparameter tuning and feature selection (SelectKBest). This ensures that the test data in the outer loop remains independent and is never seen during model training and its optimization. We used the SelectKNest implementation available in sklearn with the number of features (k) optimized within the inner loop for the range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k\in \left\{\mathrm{10,20,40,60}, all\right\}$\end{document}, and grid search for the regularization strength (C) of the logistic regression model.

To summarize the clinical characteristics of these subject clusters, C1 and C5 were characterized by the lowest and highest levels across different domains of internalizing and externalizing symptoms.

In addition, given different sex distribution across clusters, including higher female-to-male ratio in Cluster C4, we followed up with a two-way ANOVA with cluster and sex as factors. These analyses revealed significant cluster-by-sex interaction effects for aggression, rule-breaking behaviors, and intrusive problems (Table 1). Simple effects analyses indicated that men exhibited higher levels of externalizing symptoms than women in Clusters C2, C4, and C5. Additionally, within Cluster C3, men showed higher scores on intrusive problems as compared with women. In contrast, no significant cluster-by-sex interactions were observed for depression or anxiety scores, indicating that the primary clustering effects on internalizing symptoms were not driven by sex differences. For the depression scores only, the main effect of cluster remained significant (F = 2.60, P=.035) after accounting for sex, supporting the robustness of the identified cluster phenotypes.

Clustering with Significant GCA-Out Features

In Figure 2B, we show the GAP statistics for the 64 significant GCA-out features. The optimal clustering was selected at k = 2 for the GCA-out features. There were no statistically significant differences of clinical or demographic variables between the two clusters (P’s ≥ 0.130), except for sex (χ2 = 6.54, P=.010), with more men in Cluster 1 (53% of a total of 367) and more women in Cluster 2 (55% of a total of 523).

Clustering with rsFC Features

We applied the same clustering procedures to rsFC metrics (Pearson’s correlations) and obtained k = 13 as the optimum number of clusters (Figure 2C). However, inspection of the clusters revealed issues that suggested invalidity of the results.

As shown in the Supplement, we expanded the range and computed the GAP statistics for rsFC clustering up to k = 15, as well as examined the data distribution (Supplementary Figure S2A) and within-cluster sum of squared distances (Wks) to further investigate the existence of a larger number of clusters. According to the 1-SE criterion, we obtained k = 13 as the optimum number of clusters; however, inspection of the cluster distribution showed absence of well-defined clusters (Supplementary Figure S2B and 2C), indicating the existence of a non-monotone behavior that violated one of the key assumptions of GAP statistics.44 Further, the decrease of the Wks could be explained by the creation of smaller subclusters, including those with only a single metric (Supplementary Figure S2D), again suggesting overestimation of the number of clusters.45 We also estimated the optimal number of clusters using Silhouette scores and confirmed the results. Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise, another algorithm that groups data points based on their proximity to each other, likewise failed to show multiple clusters. Therefore, we concluded that rsFC’s as features did not yield significant clustering, ie, k = 1.

Finally, following the clustering results with GCA metrics as features, we arbitrarily selected k = 4 and k = 5 for clustering with rsFC as features and examined whether the four or five clusters may show any group differences in depression scores. The results were negative (t’s ≤ 1.90; P’s ≥ 0.050; pairwise t tests).

Depression-Related GCA-in Features: Identification and Prediction

The depression scores increased from GCA-in Cluster C1 to C5, with statistical differences observed between Cluster C1 and Clusters C3-5. Therefore, we examined which GCA-in features (GC strength or F values) were significantly associated with depression by computing the correlation coefficients between the F-in values and the ordered cluster-labels. The cluster labels were ordered according to the severity of depression symptoms; thus, a linear correlation allowed us to identify the neural correlates of depression without incurring the issue of multiple comparisons, which would have complicated the interpretation with other analyses, such as ANOVA. The brain regions with significant correlations as evaluated with permutation tests at P<.001, Bonferroni corrected across 84 ROIs, are shown in Figure 4 and listed in Supplementary Table S2. Note that these correlations were all negative, indicating that lower GC strength was associated with higher severity of depression symptoms. The critical GCA-in features included projections to the sgACC from the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital areas, specifically, bilateral superior frontal and middle temporal gyri, left posterior orbital gyrus and superior parietal lobule, right medial frontal cortex and precentral gyrus.

Brain regions with significant correlations between significant Granger causality analyses (GCA)-in features of the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex and cluster-labels ordered by depression. Results were evaluated at P<.001, Bonferroni corrected across 84 regions-of-interest. All GCA-in features showed negative correlation with cluster labels in increasing severity of depression symptoms. We used GCA-in features to predict the clusters with significant differences in depression. The multinomial logistic model showed an accuracy of 86.7%, a balanced accuracy of 86.5%, and an F1 score of 0.865. The Naïve Bayes model showed an accuracy of 80.4%, a balanced accuracy of 79.9%, and an F1 score of 0.800. Interestingly, after the hyperparameter tuning inherent in the nested CV pipeline, prediction performance was even higher, with an accuracy of 0.924 (±0.018), a balanced accuracy of 0.925 (±0.017), and an F1 score of 0.921 (±0.019). These results suggest the robustness of the relationships between the regional features and the underlying cluster assignments.

DISCUSSION

Significant Granger causal inputs to the sgACC enabled clustering of individuals with varying levels of depression and comorbid symptom severity. Connectivity features of multiple cortical regions best captured depression and predicted levels of depressive symptoms (ASR-derived) with 86.7% accuracy in a logistic model. Notably, the same analytics applied to Granger causal outputs or to rsFC’s failed to identify depression-related subject clusters. These findings suggest that resting-state Granger causal inputs to the sgACC covary with individual differences in depressive symptom severity in a neurotypical population and may represent candidate neural phenotypes for future studies of clinical samples. We discuss the main findings in the below, where references to “higher” or “lower” levels of depressive symptom severity reflect relative differences in ASR-measured depressive symptom severity of a neurotypical population rather than clinical diagnosis of depression.

Functional Connectivities of the sgACC: rsFC Analysis and GCA

The sgACC rsFC’s showed both positive and negative connections with frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital cortices, as well as the insula, cerebellum, and subcortical regions, in alignment with prior findings.46,47 This suggests that the sgACC partakes in large-scale functional brain networks and, by integrating information across multiple cortical and subcortical regions,48 may support emotional regulation and other cognitive processes broadly relevant to mood-related symptom dimensions.2

Adding to rsFC studies, we identified an extensive array of brain regions that were either unidirectional recipients (GCA-in) or sources (GCA-out) or of bidirectional influences of the sgACC. Regions providing inputs to the sgACC encompassed the fronto-parietal network, frontopolar cortex, and DMN (including the precuneus), ITC, and AI. This network of inputs likely regulates sgACC activity. For example, the frontopolar cortex49 and lateral PFC50,51 support emotion regulation and, with direct anatomical connections,52 may regulate sgACC activities and emotional responses. Anatomical tract-tracing in nonhuman primates showed that dlPFC fibers extend ventrally and medially, merging with cingulate bundles before terminating in the sgACC.53 These prefrontal cortical connections support sgACC’s role in cognitive-affective processing.

The precuneus, a key DMN component, may influence sgACC activity in self-referential negative emotions.54,55 The sgACC-precuneus hyperconnectivity has been associated with rumination and difficulty disengaging from negative thoughts.56,57 Brain stimulation and psychotherapy have been shown to modulate sgACC-DMN connectivity in clinical depression,58 suggesting that similar circuits as identified here may be relevant to intervention.

Involved in interoception, emotional awareness, and salience processing,59–62 the AI functions as a key limbic input region.63 GCA showed positive causal outflow from the right AI to the pgACC in both MDD patients and healthy controls.64 Our findings add to this literature and support a potential role of AI-sgACC connectivity in the regulation of interoceptive, salience and emotion responses and in the pathophysiology of depression.

In contrast, the sgACC predominantly sent outputs to medial motor cortical structures, the cerebellum, MCC, pons, and left posterior insula. The fronto-pontine-cerebellar circuits may support emotional expression,65–68 and sgACC outputs to the posterior insula modulate autonomic and visceral states of emotions.60,69 As a core region for autonomic regulation, the sgACC influences heart rate variability and stress responses70 and, with its projections to the posterior insula, may modulate bodily states during emotional processing in anxiety and depression.71

Additionally, a wide array of brain regions showed bidirectional connectivity with the sgACC, including the rostral ACC, PCC, dorsal parietal cortex, ventral striatum, hippocampus, amygdala, and right insula. These connections are linked functionally to emotional regulation, attention to emotional stimuli, and reward sensitivity.72,73 Dysfunctional sgACC-ventral striatum connectivity was associated with anhedonia74 and sgACC-hippocampal hyperconnectivity was associated with excessive emotional recall in depression.75 Together, these findings are in broad agreement with the roles of the sgACC network76,77 and extend the literature by specifying the directional interactions of the sgACC and how these directional interactions may play out in depression. However, the present findings from a neurotypical sample cannot be generalized to clinical populations. Rather, the findings may help in formulation of testable hypotheses regarding the roles of sgACC-directed pathways in the pathophysiology and treatment of depression.

Input Features of the sgACC Predict Individual Variation in Depression

Indeed, by leveraging GCA-in features in multi-label classification, we were able to distinguish subgroups of individuals differing in depressive symptom severity in a non-clinical sample. Notably, classification based exclusively on GCA-in, but not GCA-out, features effectively clustered participants by depression levels. Under the present analytic framework, GCA-in but not GCA-out of the sgACC showed greater utility for stratifying individuals by depressive symptom severity. Disruptions in how the sgACC processes inputs from the emotion circuit may contribute to individual differences in depressive symptoms.1 Prior studies of GCA reported that medial PFC and hippocampal activity predicted higher ventral ACC activity in people with MDD.78 Additionally, connectivity from the OFC to the ACC and occipital regions was associated with depression scores in patients with MDD.79

We found significant associations between the ordered cluster-labels of depression and Granger causal connectivity strength of these inputs to the sgACC, including bilateral frontal, temporal, occipital regions, left MCC, and precuneus. The strongest associations were observed for inputs from frontal and temporal regions, particularly bilateral superior frontal gyri, medial frontal cortex, and temporal poles, whereas parietal inputs showed modest effects. These findings suggest that depression-related variation in sgACC function is driven primarily by differences in higher-order prefrontal and temporal cortical inputs rather than uniformly across all afferent pathways within this neurotypical sample.

Frontal-cingulate cortical interactions have been consistently implicated in top–down regulation of affective and self-referential processing. Earlier research showed that higher dlPFC activity led to reduced activation of the ventral ACC, PCC, dorsomedial PFC (dmPFC), and ventral striatum in MDD.78 Temporal pole and middle temporal gyrus connections, also among the strongest contributors here, have been linked to socio-emotional experience.80 This literature along with the current findings underscore the relevance of frontotemporal control over sgACC activity in shaping depressive symptom severity in a neurotypical population.

In contrast, we observed reciprocal influences between bilateral amygdala and sgACC (ie, GCA-in and GCA-out both significant). However, amygdala-sgACC connections were not among those that distinguished cluster levels of depression. The sgACC-amygdala dysconnectivity was associated with heightened emotional reactivity81 and emotion regulation enhanced dmPFC-amygdala connectivity via sgACC pathways.82 These findings are also consistent with labeled neurons in the amygdala following sgACC injections in neuronal tracing studies of macaque monkeys.83 While limited research has explored sgACC Granger causal connectivity, Zhou and colleagues reported directional influences of the sgACC and pregenual ACC (pgACC) on the right amygdala during emotional face processing in healthy adults.84 Further, a study using dynamic causal modeling found weaker connectivity from the amygdala to the sgACC in adolescents with MDD.85 It remains to be seen whether Granger causality connectivities between the sgACC and amygdala may only be compromised in individuals with a clinical diagnosis of depression.

Notably, the differential performance of GCA-in versus GCA-out features should be interpreted with caution. While GCA-in measures yielded more clinically interpretable clustering, this may reflect differences in measurement variability, feature dimensionality, or signal reliability rather than biological primacy of sgACC inputs in emotional regulation and experience. Thus, our results indicate differential discriminative utility of sgACC-directed versus sgACC-originating signals under the current methodological framework but do not imply that sgACC outputs are unimportant for mood regulation. Indeed, extensive prior work implicates sgACC projections to cortical, limbic, and brainstem regions in emotional expression, autonomic regulation, and antidepressant treatment response,2,53 suggesting that projections from the sgACC remain central to affective function and dysfunction even if not captured here for symptom stratification. Future work using task-based data may better capture the role of sgACC outputs in affective regulation and clinical heterogeneity in depression.

Cluster Differences in Depression and Comorbid Problems

Clustering based on GCA-in F values of these sgACC-connected ROIs identified five clusters of individuals with distinct affective and behavioral profiles. The sgACC input-based clustering revealed distinct symptom-related phenotypes within this neurotypical sample: Cluster C1 exhibited the lowest levels of depression, anxiety, somatic complaints, and inattention problem, whereas C5 showed the highest symptom burden across both internalizing and externalizing domains. These findings align with prior evidence linking structural atrophy and altered functional connectivity of the sgACC with anxiety,86 somatic symptoms,25,87 depression-related attentional deficits,88,89 and aggressive behaviors.90 This dimensional grouping, based on functional connectivity features, supports the utility of sgACC-directed inputs as a neurobiologically-informed marker of clinical heterogeneity in depression and comorbid problems. Notably, machine learning showed that these sgACC markers are highly accurate (>85% accuracy) in distinguishing cluster labels, outperforming rsFC measures reported in previous studies.91,92

Moreover, although sex contributed to variability in selected externalizing symptom severity, as evidenced by significant cluster-by-sex interactions, the core associations between sgACC input-defined clustering and depressive symptom severity were not driven by sex differences. Notably, no sex interactions were observed for depression or anxiety, and the main effect of cluster on depression remained significant after accounting for sex as a covariate. These findings indicate that sex modulates specific behavioral expressions within connectivity-defined subgroups but does not account for the primary sgACC connectivity phenotypes identified in this study.

Limitations and Conclusions

The current study has important limitations. First, although the HCP-YA provides a large, well-characterized sample, participants were not recruited based on psychiatric diagnoses. Consequently, the present analyses do not establish biomarkers of MDD diagnosis or treatment responses. All translational interpretations linking GCA-in patterns to MDD pathophysiology, neuromodulation targets, or treatment response prediction should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating.

Second, several methodological constraints need to be considered when interpreting the present findings. GCA as applied to fMRI data may be influenced by regional HRF variability, temporal filtering, and assumptions of stationarity, and does not establish neural causality. Therefore, the GCA-in and GCA-out patterns as identified should be understood as estimates of directional dependence in hemodynamic signals rather than definitive neural inputs or outputs. Importantly, our conclusions focus on differentiable associations of GCA-in and GCA-out features with depressive symptom severity in a neurotypical sample rather than causal influences of GCA features on the pathogenesis of clinical depression.

Third, this study lacks an independent replication or external test set. The same dataset was used to derive GCA-in features, define clusters, and evaluate classifiers via cross-validation. Although cross-validation mitigates overfitting during model training, it does not address circularity arising from data-driven features or cluster definition. Accordingly, the classification accuracy as reported reflects internal separability of sgACC input-based phenotypes rather than predictive performance for unseen individuals. Validation in independent datasets will be necessary to assess generalizability of the current findings.

Despite these limitations, our findings identify directional sgACC connections that may be important to mood and behavioral regulation. Multiple regional inputs to the sgACC predicted individual differences in depression and comorbid clinical features. Confirmation and refining of these findings in clinical populations is warranted and will help clarify how sgACC-directed connectivity relates to dimensional depressive traits and their progression toward clinically significant disorders.

To conclude, our findings support the sgACC’s role in mood regulation by integrating network inputs.1 Altered sgACC inputs are associated with higher levels of depression and comorbid symptom severity in neurotypical populations.
