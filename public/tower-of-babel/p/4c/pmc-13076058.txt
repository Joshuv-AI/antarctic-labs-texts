Techno‐Economic Analysis of a Three‐Compartment CO2 Electrolyzer for Formic Acid Production
Authors: Camilo E. Peralta, Jose Antonio Abarca, Guillermo Díaz-Sainz, Esther Santos, Angel Irabien
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The electrochemical reduction of CO2 to liquid fuels or chemicals offers a sustainable route to store renewable electricity in chemical form. Among potential products, formic acid stands out for its high energy density, ease of storage, and potential as a hydrogen carrier. However, maintaining selectivity at high product concentrations remains a key challenge for economic viability. Here, we present a techno‐economic analysis of formic acid and formate production in two‐compartment and three‐compartment electrolyzers, using either water oxidation reaction or the hydrogen oxidation reaction at the anode. In two‐compartment cells, operating in acidic media to directly produce formic acid is economically favorable compared to alkaline media, as it avoids post‐protonation and improves CO2 conversion efficiency by preventing carbonate formation. Three‐compartment configurations achieve the best performance when high product concentrations are reached without compromising selectivity. Concentrations above 15 M require Faradaic efficiencies exceeding 70% for economic feasibility. Employing low‐cost renewable hydrogen for HOR in a three‐compartment cell can further reduce production costs, reaching values below $0.42 kg−1 HCOOH at 70% Faradaic efficiency and 15 M product concentration. This approach holds strong potential for deployment in hard‐to‐abate sectors such as cement, steel, and ammonia synthesis, thereby supporting industrial decarbonization.

Introduction

The industrial sector is a major contributor to global greenhouse gas emissions due to its heavy dependence on fossil fuels to produce essential chemicals and materials [1, 2, 3]. In 2022, industrial energy use generated approximately 9 Gt of CO2 emissions, highlighting the urgent need for new technologies to decarbonize these operations without compromising productivity [4]. This urgency has driven the development of low‐carbon pathways capable of meeting growing demand while mitigating climate change [5]. Carbon capture and utilization (CCU) has emerged as a promising strategy to address industrial emissions by converting captured CO2 into valuable products [6, 7]. One key CCU pathway is the electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR), which uses renewable electricity, water, and CO2 to produce fuels and chemicals in a carbon‐neutral cycle (Figure 1) [8]. This process also enables storage of intermittent renewable energy in stable molecular form, improving energy‐grid flexibility [9, 10]. Several products from CO2RR, such as carbon monoxide, formic acid, methanol, and ethanol, can be produced through multiple electron‐transfer steps using renewable energy sources (Figure 1). Among these products, two‐electron product such as formic acid (HCOOH) is particularly attractive due to its wide range of applications such as a hydrogen carrier, a chemical feedstock, and a reagent in the biomass industry (Figure 1) [11, 12, 13]. The global formic acid market was valued at $2.48 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach $3.68 billion by 2032, with an annual growth rate of 5.8% [14]. This growth is primarily driven by increasing demand in the agriculture, leather, and chemical manufacturing sectors, as well as emerging interest in formic acid as a hydrogen carrier and sustainable energy vector.

Electrochemical CO2 reduction pathway for renewable formic acid production.

Conventional HCOOH synthesis, primarily through methyl formate hydrolysis, remains heavily dependent on fossil resources, resulting in considerable greenhouse gas emissions. Life cycle assessments estimate that the average carbon footprint (CF) of this route is approximately 3.0 kg CO2‐eq per kg HCOOH, which can be reduced to around 2.0 kg CO2‐eq per kg HCOOH when powered by renewable energy sources [15, 16, 17]. In contrast, CO2RR to HCOOH offers a potentially carbon‐neutral or even carbon‐negative alternative, although its environmental benefits depend strongly on process efficiency, product concentration, and the energy source employed [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. The impact of cell components on CF has been evaluated, showing a negligible contribution (<1% of the total CF) [17, 19]. When grid electricity is used, lab‐scale CO2 electroreduction using H‐type systems exhibit higher CF values (~3.27 kg CO2‐eq per kg HCOOH) than conventional process due to poor energy efficiency and low product concentrations. Electricity use dominates the electrochemical process’s impacts (>90% of CF), and switching to renewable sources can drastically reduce the CF [18]. Under typical laboratory conditions powered by renewables, the CF may decrease to ~0.8 kg CO2‐eq per kg HCOOH, and even further to ~0.47 kg CO2‐eq per kg HCOOH if co‐products such as H2 and O2 are effectively valorized [19]. Moreover, integration with direct air capture (DAC), renewable energy, and waste heat utilization can enable net‐negative emissions, achieving a reported CF of –0.806 kg CO2‐eq per kg HCOOH [15]. While conventional formic acid production remains carbon‐intensive, electrochemical CO2 conversion powered by renewable energy represents a promising pathway toward carbon‐negative chemical manufacturing.

Progress in CO2 electrolyzer engineering for HCOOH/HCOO− production has been achieved through advances in cell configuration, evolving from simple H‐type cells to flow cells, membrane electrode assemblies (MEAs), and three‐compartment designs. For industrial applications, CO2 electrolyzers must operate at high current densities (>100 mA cm−2), maintain high product selectivity, operate at low cell potentials, and ensure long‐term stability [20]. In this analysis, two main types of polymer membrane‐based electrolyzers were evaluated: two‐compartment and three‐compartment systems. Two‐compartment systems are the most established configuration and include H‐type, flow‐cell, and MEA designs. They consist of cathodic and anodic chambers separated by an ion‐exchange membrane (IEM), typically a cation‐exchange membrane (CEM), which suppresses formate crossover to the anode, where it can be re‐oxidized to CO2, lowering selectivity [21, 22, 23]. While H‐type cells are common in fundamental studies under idealized conditions, they are limited by CO2 solubility and cannot achieve commercially relevant current densities.

Flow cells address the mass transport limitations of H‐type setups by using a gas diffusion electrode (GDE) to continuously produce HCOOH/HCOO−. In this design, the anolyte and catholyte are separated by an ion‐exchange membrane, which enables selective ion transport to maintain charge balance while reducing species crossover (Figure 2a). Aqueous electrolytes such as potassium bicarbonate or potassium hydroxide are typically used to facilitate ion conduction and to collect the HCOOH/ HCOO− product. The catholyte pH determines the product form: alkaline conditions favor HCOO₋ formation, whereas acidic operation enables direct HCOOH synthesis. Operating under acidic conditions is particularly attractive, as it eliminates the additional protonation step required to convert HCOO− into HCOOH, reduces downstream separation costs, and increases CO2 utilization by preventing salt formation [24, 25, 26, 27]. Despite these benefits, flow cells still face key challenges, including low product concentrations (<2 M) caused by dilution in the electrolyte, higher separation costs, increased ohmic resistance from the liquid catholyte, and the risk of electrode flooding [28, 29], as well as material stability and competitive hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) under strongly acidic conditions [26, 27, 30].

Schematic of (a) flow cell for cell, (b) zero‐gap membrane electrode assembly (MEA) cell (c) three‐compartment with porous solid electrolyte.

MEA electrolyzers mitigate the limitations of flow cells by removing the liquid catholyte and pressing the catalyst‐coated GDE directly against the ion‐exchange membrane (Figure 2b). This configuration reduces ohmic resistance, prevents electrode flooding, and allows operation at lower potentials compared to flow cells. Moreover, the absence of bulk electrolyte at the cathode enables higher product concentrations, with reports of HCOOH/HCOO₋ exceeding 5.5 M [31, 32, 33], representing a substantial improvement over flow cell systems [29, 34]. Despite these advantages, MEA systems face challenges: without a liquid catholyte, product removal becomes difficult, and pH regulation is limited, which can favor the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and lower selectivity.

Three‐compartment electrolyzers represent an advanced configuration for CO2RR. In this setup, a third chamber is introduced between the cathode and anode to improve ion transport, enhance reaction selectivity, and provide better control over electrolyte management (Figure 2c). Formate ions generated at the cathode pass through an anion exchange membrane (AEM) to a porous solid electrolyte (PSE), while protons produced at the anode move through a proton exchange membrane (PEM) into the same layer. Inside the PSE, these species recombine to form pure HCOOH, which can then be extracted using deionized water or an inert gas stream. This design can eliminate downstream separation [35], enable direct production of concentrated HCOOH, and recover CO2 losses in the form of bicarbonates, thereby improving overall carbon utilization [36, 37]. Recent advances have demonstrated that the product concentration in the central chamber can be tuned by controlling the water content of the extracting environment. For instance, using deionized water as the extracting medium enables formic‐acid concentrations of 1−8.3 M [13, 27, 35, 38, 39, 40, 41], although this is accompanied by a notable decrease in Faradaic efficiency at high product concentration due to product crossover and increased product dissociation [42]. This loss of selectivity can be mitigated by replacing the liquid water with humidified N2, which increases the production rate and the formic acid concentration to approximately 12−15 M [35, 43]. Operating with dry nitrogen can yield nearly pure HCOOH [35]; however, the marked loss of selectivity and the limited chemical stability of current polymeric membranes under low‐humidity conditions make this approach impractical for prolonged operation.

Each electrolyzer architecture provides a unique engineering solution to the challenges of electrochemical CO2 reduction to HCOOH, and the specific design choices within each configuration lead to distinct techno‐economic trade‐offs.

Despite rapid advances in catalyst development and reactor design, the commercial viability of CO2 electrolyzers remains uncertain, requiring a combined evaluation of performance and economic competitiveness. Techno‐economic analysis (TEA) is therefore an essential tool for bridging the gap between laboratory‐scale technical performance and real‐world economic feasibility. It provides a framework to assess the capital expenditure (CAPEX) and operational expenditure (OPEX) of a process, identifying key cost drivers and bottlenecks that may not be apparent from technical metrics alone, such as current density or Faradaic efficiency. Although some techno‐economic analyses exist for CO2RR systems [44, 45, 46, 47], the economic trade‐offs of three‐compartment reactors employing a PSE for the direct production of concentrated formic acid remain largely unexplored.

This work presents a comprehensive techno‐economic model of two‐ and three‐compartment CO2 electrolyzers for formic acid production. By combining experimental data with a voltage‐breakdown model, we identify the maximum PSE thickness that ensures economic competitiveness. Furthermore, we examine various strategies for reducing the levelized cost of formic acid, including improvements in Faradaic efficiency, the use of alternative anodic reactions, and the integration of the system with low‐cost renewable hydrogen. The results provide practical guidelines for the design, optimization, and future deployment of these electrolyzers in hard‐to‐abate industrial sectors.

Techno‐Economic Analysis Model

A techno‐economic analysis was conducted to evaluate the production of formic acid from electrochemical CO2 reduction using the operating and cost parameters summarized in Table 1. The assessment was based on methodologies reported in previous techno‐economic analyses [45, 47]. The process model considered a continuous plant with a 100‐ton day−1 production capacity and a 20‐year project lifetime, assuming a HCOOH market price of 0.77 $/kg [50]. Capital and operating costs were estimated by combining market prices of feedstocks and catalysts (CO2, H2, Ir, Pt, and PSE), electricity cost, and the system discount rate. Product purification was included in the evaluation: gas‐phase separation of unreacted CO2 and by‐products was simulated through pressure swing adsorption (PSA), while downstream liquid separation and concentration of formic acid were modeled by conventional distillation. For a two‐compartment electrolyzer configuration, an additional protonation step to convert formate into formic acid, as well as electrolyte regeneration, was also considered following the process scheme proposed by Overa et al. [49]. These calculations enabled the estimation of the levelized production cost and the sensitivity of the process economics to key design variables and market prices.

Hydrogen Oxidation Reaction as Alternative Anodic Reaction

The oxygen evolution reaction (OER) remains one of the major limitations in CO2 electrolyzers due to its high overpotential, sluggish kinetics, and the generation of low‐value diatomic oxygen (O2) as a by‐product [54, 55, 56]. Alternative anodic reactions such as glycerol oxidation (GOR) [32, 43, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 62] or hydrogen oxidation (HOR) [32, 43, 56, 57, 58, 59, 60, 61, 62] offer faster kinetics and lower thermodynamic requirements, enabling reduced cell voltages and more stable operation. Suitable anodic feeds must be low‐cost, widely available, and avoid increasing CO2 emissions [57]. In this context, hydrogen produced from low‐cost renewable electricity is particularly attractive.

HOR at the anode offers practical and operational advantages for CO2 electrolysis systems. Although most renewable hydrogen is currently produced by water electrolysis, where the oxygen evolution reaction occurs at the anode, this reaction can be performed under more favorable conditions in dedicated water‐splitting units [63]. These systems can operate with highly optimized catalysts, strongly alkaline electrolytes, or elevated temperatures, which greatly enhance reaction kinetics and overall efficiency [63, 64]. In contrast, three‐compartment electrolyzers are restricted to neutral or acidic anolytes, as alkaline conditions promote the formation of alkali‐metal formate salts in the middle compartment. Recovering formic acid therefore requires an additional acidification step [38].

Beyond these operational considerations, there is also a significant benefit in terms of system integration. Hydrogen is a valuable energy carrier but remains difficult to store and transport due to its low density in ambient conditions. Formic acid, in contrast, is a stable liquid under ambient conditions, is far easier to store and transport, and provides a high volumetric hydrogen density of 53 g of hydrogen per liter [12]. This liquid product can be readily stored and later dehydrogenated to release hydrogen on demand [65].

According to the Global Hydrogen Review 2024 [66], the International Energy Agency (IEA) projects that renewable hydrogen could reach around $2 kg−1 with an electricity price of $0.014 kWh−1 under a Net Zero Emissions (NZE) scenario by 2030, with even lower costs in regions with abundant solar resources. This creates an opportunity to couple renewable hydrogen production with CO2 reduction for hydrogen‐based energy carriers. Building on the potential of HOR as an anodic reaction, we assess the cost‐effectiveness as an alternative to OER, using experimental data from both two‐compartment [11, 24] and three‐compartment cells [42, 43].

Results and Discussion

Voltage Breakdown and Techno‐Economic Implications

The total applied voltage across an electrochemical cell can be decomposed into four main contributions: thermodynamic potential, kinetic overpotentials, ohmic losses, and mass‐transport overpotentials. Together, these components represent the electrical energy required to drive the desired reaction and overcome inherent energy losses. This breakdown is central for techno‐economic assessments, as electricity consumption typically dominates the operating cost of electrochemical processes [67]. Any reduction in cell voltage directly lowers the production cost of HCOOH.

Figure 3 compares the voltage breakdown of three CO2 electrolyzer configurations, considering thermodynamic, kinetic, and ohmic contributions (calculations can be found in the Supporting Information, equations S8–S13). As shown in Figure 3a, flow cells exhibit significant ohmic losses that increase with current density, primarily due to the resistance of the liquid electrolyte and the relatively large distance between the electrodes. In contrast, MEA cells (Figure 3b) display significantly lower ohmic losses, as they operate without a liquid catholyte and have reduced electrode spacing. However, MEA cells experience significant HER kinetic overpotentials at high current densities due to parasitic HER activity.

[31, 68], Abarca et al. [41]). Effect of middle‐layer thickness and current density on HCOOH production cost in three‐compartment cells (d).

The three‐compartment configuration, which incorporates a PSE as a middle layer, can reduce or even eliminate downstream separation steps [42, 69]. However, the additional PSE layer introduces extra ohmic resistance compared with the MEA cell (Figure 3c). This resistance depends on the middle layer thickness, its ionic conductivity, and the operating current density. For instance, a 2 mm PSE layer with an average conductivity of 65 mS cm−2 (Amberlite IR 120) [38] contributes an added resistance of approximately 3.12 Ω cm−2. This resistance becomes critical under industrially relevant conditions, where high current densities and large active areas are required. Increasing the middle layer thickness not only amplifies ohmic losses but also raises material costs due to the larger volume of resin required. Consequently, the separator thickness must be carefully optimized to keep the HCOOH production cost below the current European market price (0.77 USD kg−1) [50] at industrially relevant current densities (>200 mA cm−2).

Using the voltage breakdown data from Figure 3, the HCOOH production cost was modeled as a function of middle layer thickness and current density. As illustrated in Figure 3c, a thickness greater than 3 mm yields production costs exceeding the market price, whereas a 2 mm layer allows operation up to 600 mA cm−2. Reducing the thickness below 1 mm supports operation beyond 1000 mA cm−2 Although thinner PSE layers reduce ohmic losses and production costs, they must remain thick enough to balance ohmic resistance, product release efficiency, and mechanical stability [42]. Based on these findings, the techno‐economic analysis presented below assumes a middle layer thickness of 2 mm.

Impact of Selectivity on the Economic Viability of High Concentration HCOOH Production

When using a three‐compartment electrolyzer to produce HCOOH, increasing product concentration often leads to a drop in efficiency and selectivity. This is primarily due to two factors: the reverse reaction, which is favored at higher concentrations, and product crossover, where HCOOH passes through the membrane and is re‐oxidized at the anode [35, 42, 43]. As shown in Figure 4a three‐compartment cells can achieve significantly higher concentrations of HCOOH compared to two‐compartment cells. However, this advantage introduces new challenges.

Figure 4b illustrates that achieving these high concentrations in three‐compartment cells results in a significant loss of selectivity, as indicated by a drop in Faradaic efficiency. This inefficiency leads to significant energy losses in side reactions, directly increasing overall production cost.

Figure 4c visualizes the economic impact of this trade‐off by mapping the production cost of HCOOH against both concentration and Faradaic efficiency. These results highlight that even with a high product concentration, the production process is not economically viable if the Faradaic efficiency is too low. The red line, representing a competitive market price, shows that to be profitable, a Faradaic efficiency above 70% is essential. Therefore, for viable HCOOH production, it is crucial to balance achieving high product concentration with maintaining high selectivity.

For two‐compartment cells, operation in acidic provides a significant advantage. Total production costs can be reduced by approximately 20% compared with alkaline operation. This reduction arises from eliminating the post‐protonation required for formate in alkaline media and from higher CO2 conversion efficiency in acidic environments, where carbonate formation is avoided.

In three‐compartment electrolyzers, cost reduction is primarily driven by improved product selectivity. Increasing the Faradaic efficiency from 30% to 70% at a fixed HCOOH concentration of 15 M can reduce costs by up to 58% for both WOR and HOR systems. Higher selectivity decreases the number of electrons required per kilogram of product, reducing the electricity consumption and the number of electrochemical reactions needed to sustain the target production rate

Impact of Precious Metal Anode on Formic Acid Production in Three‐Compartment Electrolyzers

Platinum‐group metals (PGMs) such as Ir and Pt present significant sustainability and cost challenges for electrochemical technologies due to their limited global supply and escalating price. Iridium is particularly constrained, with a global production of only ~7.3 Mt per year (Figure 6a), compared with ~177Mt per year of platinum (Figure 6b). This supply imbalance has driven iridium prices to levels several times higher than those of platinum (Figure 6a,b). The impact of Ir scarcity is further amplified by the high Ir loadings required in state‐of‐the‐art OER anodes (~1–2 mgIr cm−2) to ensure adequate activity and durability [72]. At large scales, these loadings translate into substantial material demands. For instance, converting the projected 258 Mt CO2 captured from the cement industry by 2030 [73] at 500 mA cm−2 would require approximately 69 Mt of Ir, nearly ten times the current annual global production [71]. In contrast, state‐of‐the‐art Pt loadings in PEMFC anodes are much lower (~0.1 mgPt cm−2) due to the rapid kinetics of the hydrogen oxidation reaction (HOR) [74]. Under the same CO2 conversion scenario, the required Pt would be only 6.9 Mt, representing about 3.8% of the global Pt supply.

Scaling up electrolyzers for H2 or CO2 electrolysis further increases the demand for precious‐metal catalysts, leading to significant increases in system cost. Figure 6c evaluates how changes in the price of Ir and Pt influence the production cost of formic acid in a three‐compartment system operating with WOR or HOR at the anode. Any increase in the price of Ir leads to a substantial increase in the cost of the final product: a 50% increase in the price of Ir raises the cost by 16.3%, while a 100% increase raises it by up to 32.6%. In contrast, platinum shows a minimal effect: even a 100% increase in the price of Pt results in an increase of only 1.25% in the production cost. This marked contrast arises from the considerably higher Ir loading and its much higher market price relative to Pt. As a result, Ir becomes the primary cost driver in the three‐compartment electrolyzer, accounting for up to ~48% of the total system cost under strong price fluctuations. Given these constraints, relying exclusively on Ir‐based anodes for CO2 electrolysis is unsustainable, especially as deployment grows and competition with water electrolyzers intensifies. In this context, coupling CO2RR with hydrogen‐production systems that already operate Ir‐based OER anodes under optimized and stable conditions, alongside robust recycling strategies, becomes a promising pathway to maximize the utilization of scarce Ir resources. Such integration can also enhance system‐level energy management through shared hydrogen and CO2‐derived product logistics, while addressing challenges associated with hydrogen transportation.

Integrating Industrial CO2 Sources with CO2RR for Cost‐Effective Formic Acid Production

Industrial CO2 emissions vary widely in concentration and purity depending on the sector, affecting both the cost and feasibility of CCU. Integrating CO2 electro‐reduction with these emission sources presents an opportunity to decarbonize industry while producing value‐added chemicals such as HCOOH. CO2 capture costs depend on flue gas concentration [75, 76]. High‐purity streams from biomass or ammonia production (~100%) are the most cost‐effective, whereas cement plants and steel plants (3%–30% CO2) incur moderate capture costs. Direct air capture (DAC) has the highest levelized cost due to the low CO2 concentration in air (~0.042%), though advances are expected to reduce DAC costs below $100 per ton by 2030 [77] (Figure 7).

Under the NZE 2030 low‐energy price scenario, integrating industrial CO2 sources with renewable hydrogen in a three‐compartment‐HOR electrolyzer shows particularly promising economics. For instance, HCOOH production from ammonia plant CO2 streams could reach $0.28 kg−1 HCOOH, well below the current market price (Figure 6). Likewise, hard‐to‐abate sectors such as cement and iron and steel can achieve competitive production costs of $0.36 and $0.34 kg−1, respectively. These findings highlight the promise of three‐compartment electrolysis coupled with green hydrogen in decarbonizing energy‐intensive industries, offering both industrial decarbonization and the generation of valuable value‐added CO2 products.

Formic Acid as a Liquid Hydrogen Carrier

The widespread adoption of a hydrogen economy is limited by the high costs associated with hydrogen transport and storage. Due to its low volumetric density and gaseous nature, hydrogen requires energy‐intensive compression or liquefaction, adding up to $10/kg H2 in infrastructure and distribution costs [78, 79]. Formic acid presents a compelling alternative as a liquid organic hydrogen carrier, with a hydrogen density of 53 gL−1 and favorable properties: it is low in toxicity, has low flammability, and is stable under ambient conditions [79, 80]. These properties enable the use of existing liquid fuel infrastructure, significantly lowering delivery costs. Importantly, formic acid enables a carbon‐neutral hydrogen cycle through the CO2RR to HCOOH and subsequent selective dehydrogenation to release H2 and regenerate CO2.

Figure 8 compares conventional hydrogen delivery with the formic acid‐based route. In the conventional pathway, H2 is produced via water electrolysis ($2/kg H2 under NZE 2030) and then liquefied, stored, and transported, adding ~$10/kg, for a total cost of ~$12/kg H2. In the alternative route, CO2 is electrochemically converted to HCOOH using HOR. The current production cost of HCOOH is $6.3/kg H2 equivalent, with transport and storage adding only $0.5/kg H2. At the point of use, H2 is released from HCOOH via catalytic dehydrogenation at a cost of approximately $4.0/kg H2 [81, 82], leading to a total hydrogen delivery cost of $10.8/kg H2. Through improvements in electrochemical efficiency, access to low‐cost CO2 (including policy‐driven tax credits), the formic acid production cost can be reduced up to $3.31/kgH2 [78]. Additionally, with the development of more efficient dehydrogenation technologies, the overall hydrogen cost in this pathway could be further reduced. Moreover, HCOOH is a high‐value liquid with an electrochemical production cost of $0.28/kg HCOOH and has established markets in agriculture, textiles, pharmaceuticals, and energy storage, offering additional economic value beyond hydrogen transport.

Scale‐up Challenges in Three‐Compartment Electrolyzers

Three‐compartment electrolyzers face critical barriers to commercialization, beginning with the challenge of maintaining membrane stability under steep pH gradients and chemical incompatibilities inherent to the multichamber design [83]. Operational efficiency is further compromised by product crossover, as HCOOH diffuses through the membrane and is oxidized at the anode, and by the porous layer itself, which introduces significant ohmic resistance that reduces overall energy efficiency. Scaling these systems is challenged by irregular resin packing, volume changes that disrupt contact and raise resistance, and the tradeoff between layer thickness and risks of pressurization or leakage. Moreover, large‐scale operation faces difficulties in ensuring uniform flow distribution to prevent hotspots and managing high pressure drops or gas bubble accumulation, which can block ionic pathways and structurally damage cell components [83]. Despite their ability to deliver high‐purity products, these systems require advances in membrane durability, conductive porous media, and precision flow control to achieve stable, energy‐efficient industrial operation.

Conclusion

The electrochemical conversion of CO2 to formic acid offers a sustainable pathway to reduce the CF of conventional processes while contributing to industrial decarbonization. In this study, a comprehensive techno‐economic analysis was conducted to assess the feasibility of CO2 electroreduction to HCOOH in two‐ and three‐compartment electrolyzers. These results demonstrate that strategic integration of reactor design, operating conditions, and feedstock sourcing can enable CO2 electroreduction to HCOOH at costs well below current market prices.

In two‐compartment systems, operation under acidic conditions eliminates the need for downstream protonation steps and mitigates carbonate formation, leading to cost savings of approximately 20% compared with alkaline systems. In three‐compartment electrolyzers, product selectivity becomes the key determinant of economic viability, with Faradaic efficiencies above 70% required for competitiveness. Under NZE 2030 energy price projections, achieving this selectivity threshold reduces formic acid production costs to USD 0.42 kg−1, below current market values.

Optimization of the middle layer to thicknesses below 2 mm enables high‐rate operation while minimizing ohmic losses, although mechanical integrity must be ensured. Moreover, coupling renewable hydrogen with high‐purity CO2 streams from existing industrial processes not only supports economically viable green formic acid production but also provides a scalable pathway to decarbonize hard‐to‐abate sectors. However, remaining challenges, including the lifetimes of the materials, system scalability, and product selectivity, must be addressed before large‐scale deployment. Overall, these findings indicate that CO2RR to formic acid is approaching market readiness, offering strong potential as both an energy carrier and a chemical feedstock within a renewable, circular carbon economy.
