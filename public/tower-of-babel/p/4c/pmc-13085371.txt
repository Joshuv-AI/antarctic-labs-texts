Association of the atherogenic index of plasma and a novel triglyceride-glucose-body shape index with stroke: insights from NHANES and machine learning approaches
Authors: Huanmao Hong, Yongtian Guo, Tangxin Xin, Xiaojin Gan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStroke is a major global health burden. While composite indices have shown promise in cardiovascular risk assessment, their value in stroke risk assessment requires further investigation. This study aimed to develop a novel Triglyceride-Glucose-Body Shape Index (TBSI) by integrating the triglyceride-glucose index with a body shape index, and to evaluate its association with stroke risk together with the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).MethodsA total of 43,448 participants from NHANES (2003-2018) were included. The independent associations of AIP and TBSI with stroke were assessed using weighted multivariable logistic regression, with non-linear relationships investigated by restricted cubic splines (RCS). Discriminative ability was validated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore effect modification, and sensitivity analyses tested model robustness. Mediation analysis was conducted to explore whether the effects of AIP and TBSI on stroke were mediated by metabolic diseases (hypertension and diabetes) as well as oxidative stress biomarkers (gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT] and albumin). The Boruta algorithm and multiple machine learning models were applied for feature selection. The performance of the best model was further interpreted using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values.ResultsTwo indices were significantly associated with increased stroke risk. TBSI exhibited a J-shaped association, with a threshold at 7.176. Sex and race modified the associations. In terms of discriminative performance, TBSI (AUC = 0.779) proved superior to AIP (AUC = 0.769). Mediation analysis revealed statistically significant indirect effects: the association of TBSI with stroke was mediated through hypertension and diabetes, while that of AIP was mediated through hypertension. Although mediation through oxidative stress biomarkers was proposed, it did not reach statistical significance. Feature importance analysis identified TBSI as the top feature, with the random forest (RF) model demonstrating the highest accuracy in identifying stroke risk. SHAP analysis of the RF model results also showed that TBSI, AIP, and lipid-related features are key factors influencing stroke occurrence, with significant positive impacts.ConclusionThe novel TBSI and the AIP are strongly associated with stroke risk and offer complementary clinical value. Their integration into clinical screening and machine learning models may support early identification and targeted prevention, especially in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05668-1.

Introduction

Stroke remains a major acute cerebrovascular condition characterized by high incidence, mortality, and disability rates. Epidemiological data indicate a sustained increase in its global burden over the past decades, with a particularly accelerated rise in low- and middle-income countries. This trend poses a critical challenge to public health systems and socioeconomic development worldwide. The total global economic burden of stroke has been estimated to exceed USD 890 billion, accounting for approximately 0.66% of the global Gross Domestic Product [1]. Between 1990 and 2021, absolute stroke cases increased markedly, with corresponding rises of 70.0% in incidence, 44.0% in mortality, and 86.0% in prevalence. Disability-adjusted life years have also increased by 32% [2]. Notably, Asia, home to nearly 60% of the global population, bears the heaviest share of this burden [3].

Among the modifiable risk factors, metabolic dysfunctions have emerged as predominant drivers of stroke onset. Approximately 69% of stroke risk has been attributed to metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, central obesity, and chronic inflammation, underscoring the critical role of the metabolic-inflammatory axis in stroke pathophysiology [2]. Identification of clinically feasible and quantifiable metabolic markers with high sensitivity is therefore essential for precise stroke prevention strategies. In this context, two novel metabolic indices have gained attention. The Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP), calculated as the logarithmic ratio of fasting triglycerides to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), reflects underlying lipoprotein imbalance and insulin resistance [4]. Extensive evidence has established a robust association between elevated AIP levels and increased risks of stroke and cardiovascular disease, positioning AIP as a valuable marker associated with these conditions. Intervention and surveillance targeting AIP among individuals with dyslipidemia may offer risk reduction benefits [5–7]. Moreover, AIP has demonstrated potential in forecasting long-term stroke outcomes.

The Triglyceride-Glucose (TyG) index, a simplified surrogate marker for insulin resistance, has demonstrated promising applications in metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and cardiocerebrovascular risk assessment [8]. Elevated TyG index levels have been consistently linked to a higher incidence of stroke and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [9, 10]. Similarly, the A Body Shape Index (ABSI), derived from waist circumference normalized by body mass index (BMI) and height, independently reflects central adiposity and visceral fat distribution, offering superior cardiovascular risk stratification compared to conventional BMI [11]. A composite metric, the TyG-ABSI index (TBSI), which combines the TyG index and ABSI, has recently been introduced as a novel indicator capturing the interplay between glucose-lipid metabolic dysfunction and body composition. TBSI has shown superior performance in assessing cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality, highlighting its potential utility in stroke risk assessment by integrating two pivotal risk dimensions [12].

Furthermore, growing evidence indicates that oxidative stress may serve as a mechanistic bridge linking metabolic dysregulation to cerebrovascular injury. Biomarkers such as gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) and serum albumin have been widely studied as accessible indicators of oxidative stress and systemic antioxidant capacity [13]. Concurrently, hypertension and diabetes are well-established as critical factors in stroke pathogenesis. However, their potential mediating role in the association between composite metabolic indices and stroke has not been comprehensively investigated.

To address these gaps, the present study leveraged data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative and methodologically rigorous database, to evaluate the associations of AIP and TBSI with stroke prevalence. We further performed mediation analyses to elucidate the potential mediating effects of hypertension, diabetes, and oxidative stress biomarkers. Additionally, we developed and internally validated machine learning models, incorporating Boruta feature selection, to improve the early identification of individuals at high stroke risk and inform personalized prevention strategies.

Methods

Data source and study design

A cross-sectional study was conducted using data from multiple cycles (2003–2018) of the NHANES, a nationally representative survey administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [14]. NHANES employs a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to collect detailed health information from the noninstitutionalized US population, encompassing demographic, lifestyle, physical examination, and laboratory data. All procedures adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. As NHANES data are publicly available, no additional ethical approval was required for secondary analysis.

A total of 80,312 participants were initially included, enrolled in each NHANES survey cycle (Supplementary Table S1). Individuals with missing stroke outcome data or incomplete covariate information (e.g., age, gender, education, race, history of stroke, hypertension, diabetes, liver disease, cancer, heart disease) were excluded. After applying these criteria, 43,448 participants were retained for analysis. For the analysis of each primary exposure, two separate subsamples were defined based on the availability of complete data for each primary exposure: one for the analysis of AIP (n = 15,291) and another for the analysis of TBSI (n = 14,931) (Fig. 1).

Outcome variable

Stroke was ascertained based on self-reported responses to the questionnaire item “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had a stroke?” (MCQ160f).

Covariates

To control for potential confounding, a comprehensive set of demographic, lifestyle, clinical, and biochemical variables was included. Demographic variables encompassed age (< 45 years, ≥ 45 years), sex, race (Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, other Hispanic, and other races), education level (low: elementary or less; medium: middle/high school; high: college or above), and poverty-income ratio (PIR: <1.4, 1.4–3.5, ≥ 3.5). Lifestyle variables included smoking and alcohol use (both dichotomized as yes/no). Medical history included hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, liver disease, and cancer. Biochemical indicators included serum albumin (g/L), GGT (U/L), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (DBP, mmHg), and serum uric acid (UA, µmol/L).

Statistical analysis

All analyses incorporated NHANES survey weights, strata, and primary sampling units to ensure national representativeness. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (weighted percentages), while continuous variables were presented as weighted means ± standard deviation (SD). Intergroup differences were assessed using χ² tests for categorical variables and weighted t-tests for continuous variables [19].

Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among the independent variables. A VIF value below 5 was considered indicative of no significant multicollinearity. The exposure variables, AIP and TBSI, were analyzed both as continuous variables and in quartiles (Q1-Q4). Their associations with stroke were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression, with results expressed as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three progressively adjusted models were constructed for AIP: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, race, and BMI; and Model 3 was further adjusted for coronary artery disease, height, diabetes, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), liver disease, and GGT. For TBSI, Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, race, and height; and Model 3 was further adjusted for heart disease, diabetes, and liver disease [20]. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to assess non-linear associations between exposure variables and stroke risk. Subgroup and interaction analyses were conducted to evaluate potential effect modification. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess discriminative ability [21]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness and reliability of the findings.

Mediation analyses were implemented using the “mediation” package in R to explore the potential mediating effects of hypertension, diabetes, GGT, and serum albumin on the associations of AIP and TBSI with stroke. The nonparametric bootstrap method (1,000 iterations) was used to estimate direct, indirect, and total effects, as well as the proportion mediated [22].

To further refine the classification model, the Boruta algorithm was applied to identify variables with significant importance for identification. The dataset was split into training and validation subsets in a 7:3 ratio. Six machine learning models were established, including Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Decision Tree (DT), Extreme Gradient Boosting, Logistic Regression, and Light Gradient Boosting Machine [23]. All models were constructed within a unified training framework using a 5-fold cross-validation strategy, with the Area Under the Curve (AUC) serving as the primary metric for evaluating model discrimination. Furthermore, model performance was comprehensively assessed using multiple indicators such as accuracy, sensitivity, false positive rate, false negative rate, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and the F1-score. Decision curve analysis was also employed to validate their clinical utility. After identifying the optimal model, Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to interpret the model, clarifying the direction and magnitude of contribution of each predictor variable to the outcome, thereby enhancing the model’s interpretability and transparency. Hyperparameters for all models were optimized through grid search, and a fixed random seed was set to ensure the reproducibility of the experiments.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.1.2), with statistical significance defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

All baseline characteristics, except sex and GGT, demonstrated statistically significant differences between stroke and non-stroke groups (p < 0.05). In the AIP dataset (n = 15,291), 567 participants reported stroke. Stroke prevalence was higher in individuals with PIR of 1.4–3.5 (41.4%), smokers (60.0%), those with self-reported hypertension (73.9%), and elevated levels of BMI (29.82 ± 6.71), GGT (33.19 ± 42.44 U/L), UA (349.00 ± 96.07 µmol/L), and SBP (132.89 ± 22.69 mmHg) (Supplementary Table S2). A total of 14,931 participants were included in the TBSI cohort, of whom 539 had a history of stroke. Higher stroke prevalence was associated with PIR 1.4–3.5 (41.6%), smoking (61.0%), higher TBSI scores (7.45 ± 0.76), elevated BMI (29.83 ± 6.66), GGT (32.32 ± 38.29 U/L), UA (351.08 ± 96.97 µmol/L), and SBP (132.78 ± 22.79 mmHg) (Table 1). Participants excluded due to missing data were demographically and clinically comparable to those included in the final analysis (Supplementary Table S3).

Association between exposure indicators and stroke risk

All VIF for the variables ranged from 1.00 to 2.70, well below the common threshold of 5, confirming the absence of substantial multicollinearity (Supplementary Table S4). We assessed the associations of AIP and TBSI with stroke risk using multivariable logistic regression, analyzing both indices in quartiles. AIP was divided into Q1 (-1.25 to -0.28), Q2 (-0.28 to -0.06), Q3 (-0.06 to 0.16), and Q4 (0.16 to 1.79). TBSI ranged from Q1 (4.16 to 6.51), Q2 (6.51 to 7.04), Q3 (7.04 to 7.57), to Q4 (7.57 to 10.95). In the unadjusted model (Model 1, Table 2), participants in the highest AIP quartile (Q4) had significantly higher odds of stroke compared to those in Q1 (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.32–2.36, p < 0.01). For TBSI, a dose-response relationship was evident: each successive quartile was associated with progressively increased odds of stroke (Q2: OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.31–2.91; Q3: OR = 2.61, 95% CI: 1.73–3.92; Q4: OR = 4.37, 95% CI: 2.97–6.45; all p < 0.01). These associations persisted after sequential adjustment for covariates. In Model 2 and Model 3, the trends remained consistent. In the final model (Model 3), the highest quartiles of both AIP (Q4: OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.06–2.02, p = 0.02) and TBSI (Q4: OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.27–2.94, p < 0.01) remained independently associated with increased stroke risk, using Q1 as the reference.

RCS analysis

The RCS model was applied to explore potential non-linear associations between exposure indices and stroke risk. TBSI demonstrated a significant non-linear relationship with stroke (p−overall <0.001, p−nonlinear = 0.009). The risk increased across the TBSI range, with the curve exhibiting a moderately steeper ascent beyond the inflection point of 7.176 (Fig. 2B). In contrast, AIP was significantly associated with stroke overall (p−overall = 0.004), although the nonlinear relationship was not statistically significant (Fig. 2A).

Subgroup analyses

The association between AIP and stroke was significantly modified by sex (p = 0.005) and history of heart attack (p = 0.035). Among participants aged < 45 years, AIP in Q2 was associated with an elevated stroke risk (OR: 3.115; 95% CI: 1.041–9.317, p = 0.044). In males, AIP in Q4 was associated with a more than twofold increased risk (OR: 2.574, 95% CI: 1.801–3.679, p < 0.001) (Supplementary Figure S1). TBSI demonstrated significant interactions with heart disease (p = 0.004), heart attack (p < 0.001), and cancer (p = 0.022). In several subgroups, TBSI was strongly associated with increased stroke risk. Notably, among males, TBSI in Q4 was associated with nearly fivefold higher odds (OR: 4.882, 95% CI: 3.018–7.897, p < 0.001), and among individuals with a PIR between 1.4 and 3.5, Q4 of TBSI conferred a markedly elevated risk (OR: 6.775, 95% CI: 3.220-14.256, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Findings from the sensitivity analysis were consistent with those of the weighted logistic regression, further confirming AIP and TBSI as independent risk factors for stroke. In Model 1, the highest quartiles of both AIP (Q4: OR = 1.58, 95% CI: 1.24–2.02, p < 0.01) and TBSI (Q4: OR = 4.33, 95% CI: 3.19–5.86, p < 0.01) were significantly associated with stroke risk. After adjustment for covariates in Model 2, the strong association for TBSI Q4 persisted (Q4: OR = 2.64, 95% CI: 1.91–3.65, p < 0.01) (Table 3). Overall, the robustness and reliability of the logistic regression findings were validated by the sensitivity analysis.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analysis revealed distinct pathways through which AIP and TBSI influence stroke risk. For AIP, hypertension emerged as the strongest mediator (average causal mediation effect [ACME] = 0.002127, p = 0.006), accounting for approximately 40% of the total effect. In contrast, TBSI exhibited a more complex mediation pattern. Hypertension again represented the primary mediator (ACME = 0.001843, p < 0.001), with diabetes also making a significant contribution (ACME = 0.000102, p = 0.018). Although serum albumin showed statistically significant mediating effects for both indices, the proportion mediated was minimal, suggesting limited clinical relevance in these pathways (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S5).

Among the six models, RF demonstrated the best performance, achieving an AUC of 0.952 (Fig. 6B). Decision curve analysis showed that the calibration curve for RF was closest to the diagonal (Brier score = 0.01), indicating high consistency between its predicted probabilities and the actual risks (Fig. 6C). The clinical decision curve further confirmed that the net benefits of all models were significantly higher than the “no intervention” and “intervention for all” strategies, with RF exhibiting the highest clinical net benefit within the risk threshold range of 0.2–0.4 (Fig. 6D). The RF model also showed high sensitivity (93.7%) and acceptable specificity (67.5%), with both the false positive rate (32.5%) and false negative rate (6.3%) maintained at low levels, and a negative predictive value as high as 99.7% (Supplementary Table S6). Based on this comprehensive performance, RF was selected as the optimal predictive model.

Further explanation of the RF model was conducted via SHAP analysis. The beeswarm plot revealed that hypertension, TBSI, triglycerides, the atherogenic index of plasma, total cholesterol, HDL-C, and diabetes were the primary variables driving the model’s predictions; among these, hypertension, TBSI, and diabetes significantly increased the model’s predicted risk score (Fig. 6E). In an individual sample analysis, a subject with a baseline predicted risk of 0 was ultimately classified by the model as having a high risk of 76.6%. This prediction was primarily attributed to the positive contributions of total cholesterol (99 mg/dL, 23.6%), LDL-C (34 mg/dL, 13.6%), diabetes (11.2%), and hypertension (10.4%) (Fig. 6F).

Discussion

A comprehensive evaluation was conducted to investigate the associations of the AIP and TBSI with stroke, using data from the nationally representative NHANES cohort. Both indices demonstrated significant, independent associations with stroke across diverse demographic and clinical subgroups, supporting their broad applicability and utility in risk stratification. Notably, TBSI exhibited a stronger association with stroke risk than AIP, with participants in the highest quartile (Q4) experiencing a 94% increased risk compared to the lowest (Q1), versus a 46% increase for AIP. These findings offer novel insights into early identification strategies for individuals at high stroke risk, particularly within the context of escalating global stroke burden, and may contribute to the theoretical foundation for primary prevention frameworks.

AIP demonstrated a linear association with stroke risk, persisting after adjustment for conventional confounders (Model 3), with individuals in Q4 exhibiting a 46% higher risk. This aligns with previous research indicating a 84% increased risk with elevated AIP, as reported by Qu et al. [4], and a 69% increase among obese individuals, as shown by Zhai et al. [6]. AIP, as a surrogate marker for small dense LDL (sdLDL) particles and impaired reverse cholesterol transport, appears to contribute mechanistically to stroke pathogenesis [24–26]. Elevated AIP suggests a predominance of sdLDL, which more readily penetrates the vascular endothelium, undergoes oxidative modification, and initiates foam cell formation, thereby accelerating atherosclerotic plaque development [27, 28]. Additionally, AIP is reflective of diminished antioxidant capacity and impaired HDL-mediated cholesterol efflux, exacerbating endothelial dysfunction and inflammation, processes integrally linked to cerebrovascular events [29]. These biologically plausible mechanisms strengthen the case for AIP as a reliable indicator associated with stroke.

TBSI, integrating body morphology, fat distribution, and metabolic function, exhibited a non-linear “J-shaped” relationship with stroke, with a defined threshold effect at 7.176. Below this threshold, moderate visceral adiposity and insulin resistance likely contribute to early atherogenic changes, partially increased by residual adiponectin-mediated vascular protection [30, 31]. Beyond this point, excessive visceral adipose tissue accumulation drives chronic subclinical inflammation, marked by elevated TNF-α and IL-6 levels, which precipitate endothelial apoptosis and progressive vascular injury [32, 33]. The compounding effects of free fatty acid-induced insulin resistance and sustained metabolic stress likely culminate in irreversible vascular dysfunction [34]. This transition underscores a critical biological tipping point in the trajectory from metabolic dysregulation to overt stroke.

The novel index ABSI, designed to reflect the interaction between abnormal body shape and dyslipidemia, also demonstrated a positive association with stroke. Although slightly less discriminant than TBSI, ABSI highlights the importance of integrating morphological and metabolic parameters in cerebrovascular risk assessment and lays the conceptual groundwork for the future development of composite risk scores that incorporate both domains [35, 36].

Subgroup analyses revealed that the association between AIP and stroke was significant only among men (Q4: OR = 2.57), indicating sex-specific sensitivity of lipid metabolism to cerebrovascular risk. This may reflect higher baseline levels of LDL and lower HDL-C among males, compounded by the absence of estrogen-mediated lipid modulation and antioxidant protection [37, 38]. Conversely, the non-significant association in women may be attributable to estrogen’s favorable effects on lipid profiles and endothelial integrity [39]. These sex-specific differences underscore the importance of personalized risk stratification in stroke prevention. TBSI exhibited high classification accuracy across all demographic groups, with the strongest association observed among African Americans, where stroke risk was elevated by nearly 6.9-fold compared to White individuals. Ethnic disparities in fat distribution, insulin sensitivity, genetic susceptibility, and socioeconomic context may contribute to these differences [40]. These findings highlight the complex interplay of ancestry, biology, and environment in shaping metabolic stroke risk and underscore the need for population-specific precision medicine strategies. Mediation analysis revealed that hypertension serves as a critical intermediary mechanism in the pathway through which both AIP and TBSI contribute to stroke incidence. Additionally, diabetes was identified as a significant mediator in the association between TBSI and stroke. These findings collectively highlight the importance of blood pressure and glycemic management in stroke prevention strategies.

TBSI demonstrated modestly higher discriminatory ability for stroke risk (AUC = 0.779) compared to AIP (AUC = 0.769), suggesting potential differences in their clinical application. AIP, as a lipid-centric index, is well-suited for longitudinal risk monitoring across general populations, whereas TBSI, sensitive to inflection points in metabolic deterioration, may be particularly valuable in frontline screening and community-level interventions. Supportive machine learning analyses confirmed that TBSI was among the top-ranking features, with threshold-based models demonstrating its utility in stroke risk classification. A combined model incorporating both indices yielded improved interpretability and robustness, advocating for their synergistic use in refining stroke risk assessment frameworks.

The integration of machine learning methodologies provided additional validation for the composite discriminative utility of the selected metabolic indicators. Feature importance analysis via the Boruta algorithm consistently identified TBSI and AIP as top-ranking variables, indicating strong biological plausibility. While ensemble models exhibited certain limitations in the context of imbalanced data, single-threshold models (e.g., TBSI > 7.176) retained considerable clinical value due to their interpretability and physiological relevance, particularly in primary care risk stratification. Machine learning techniques significantly enhanced model discrimination by capturing complex interactions among metabolic parameters, illustrating the promise of combining biomarker profiles with advanced computational frameworks [41]. Future directions may benefit from incorporating high-dimensional omics data and dynamic physiological metrics to construct hybrid models, thereby enabling more personalized and precise approaches to cerebrovascular risk assessment.

Despite the significant associations observed between multidimensional metabolic markers and stroke risk, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The cross-sectional design of NHANES inherently restricts causal inference and fails to account for temporality or reverse causation, underscoring the need for validation in large-scale, prospective, and multicenter longitudinal cohorts. Second, the dataset does not distinguish between ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. This is a critical gap, as AIP and TBSI primarily reflect insulin resistance and dyslipidemia—pathological drivers of atherosclerosis. Consequently, grouping all stroke subtypes may have introduced heterogeneity, potentially masking a stronger, specific correlation between these markers and ischemic stroke. Dependence on self-reported stroke history, which is subject to recall bias and impedes stroke subtype classification, introduces misclassification bias and limits mechanistic interpretation. Moreover, key confounders such as physical activity, dietary intake, and medication use were based on self-reports, which may introduce measurement error and affect model stability. Temporal variability of metabolic markers and their alignment with stroke incidence were not evaluated, potentially underestimating the cumulative burden of long-term metabolic dysregulation. Genetic predisposition, lipidomic signatures, and gut microbiota, emerging determinants of cerebrovascular pathophysiology, were not accounted for, representing important avenues for future investigation. Integration of multi-omics and multimodal datasets is anticipated to enhance model performance and translational potential.

Population representativeness and ethnic heterogeneity remain critical considerations for external validation. Given the modifying effects of race, sex, and socioeconomic determinants on metabolic risk trajectories, further assessments in diverse populations are imperative. Particular focus should be directed toward subclinical younger adults, individuals with metabolic syndrome, and elderly high-risk cohorts to evaluate discriminative efficacy and clinical applicability across demographic strata, thereby enhancing generalizability and real-world utility.

Policy implications derived from these findings highlight the urgency of strengthening metabolic health surveillance within primary care systems. Incorporation of AIP and TBSI into community-based screening protocols and chronic disease management frameworks may facilitate standardized, non-invasive risk monitoring. Personalized early interventions, including lifestyle optimization, nutritional counseling, and metabolic risk control, are recommended for individuals with elevated visceral adiposity to prevent progression to major cerebrovascular events [42]. Development of machine learning-assisted screening tools, such as automated clinical decision support systems and real-time alert platforms, may further augment early detection and timely intervention, particularly in resource-constrained settings [43]. Attention to population-level heterogeneity remains essential for informing precision public health strategies tailored to sex- and ethnicity-specific risk profiles, ultimately contributing to stroke prevention through earlier identification and targeted mitigation of high-risk individuals.

Conclusion

In summary, both AIP and TBSI demonstrated significant positive associations with stroke incidence and exhibited comparable performance in risk stratification. However, their distinct risk patterns, linear for AIP and threshold-based for TBSI, suggest complementary clinical utility. A combined application is recommended in routine screening. AIP may facilitate rapid identification of individuals at moderate to elevated risk, while TBSI can aid in detecting high-risk individuals, particularly those with metabolic disturbances but without overt comorbidities. Integrating both indices may enhance precision prevention strategies, enabling a continuum from risk stratification to early warning and targeted intervention.
