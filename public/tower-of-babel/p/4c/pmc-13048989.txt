Enhancing groundwater recharge mapping in arid regions with geospatial multi-criteria analysis in the Eastern desert of Egypt
Authors: Mohamed Saber, Sameh A Kantoush, Tetsuya Sumi, Ahmed Sefelnasr, Dalal Alshamsi, Ahmed Gad, Abdelhalim Ali, Alaa Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGroundwater recharge zones should be identified in order to address water scarcity while ensuring the sustainability of aquifers. The research utilized an integrated methodology that combined Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), Remote Sensing (RS), and Geographic Information System (GIS) techniques to identify groundwater recharge potential zones within the Qift–El Quseir area of Egypt’s Eastern Desert. Eight key parameters, including drainage density, slope, topographic wetness index, lineament density, lithology, rainfall, land cover, and soil characteristics, were extracted from Sentinel-2 imagery, an ALOS PALSAR digital elevation model, long term gridded precipitation, and geological and land cover maps. AHP-based multi-criteria evaluation highlights lineament density and lithology as the dominant controls on recharge, followed by topographic and drainage characteristics. The resulting Groundwater Recharge Potential Index map classifies the basin into four categories, with 22.0% of the area characterized as excellent to very good, 34.9% as very good to good, 31.2% as good to moderate, and 11.9% as moderate to low. By combining these classes with class specific recharge ratios and mean annual rainfall (4.3 mm), the basin scale recharge is estimated at approximately 9.67 × 106 m3/year, equivalent to about 26.8% of annual precipitation, with nearly 80% of this recharge contributed by the two highest classes. Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis and Receiver Operating Characteristic evaluation, together with geophysical and hydrochemical data, confirm the robustness and hydrogeological plausibility of the mapped recharge zones. The findings show that multi-criteria spatial modeling accurately locates the optimal groundwater recharge sites, which would aid water resource planning and policymaking in arid regions.Keywords: Groundwater recharge, AHP, GIS, Multi-criteria analysis, Arid regions, Eastern desert, EgyptSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Hydrology, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

In arid and semi-arid regions, groundwater plays a critical role in sustaining various economic activities and meeting the basic water needs of the population1,2. With limited and insufficient surface water availability, identifying and managing groundwater resources has become necessary for regional development strategies3–5. Egypt, one of the world’s most water-stressed countries, is experiencing increased pressure on its water resources because of rapid population expansion, intensifying agricultural demands, unsustainable consumption, rapid and uncontrolled urbanization, and climate change6,7. The Nile River is Egypt’s main source of renewable water, and the country is reaching full usage of its River Nile water allocation due to the implementation of advanced agricultural projects such as the El Salaam and Tushka Canals, which convey River Nile water to Egypt’s deserts7,8. As a result, there is a pressing need to explore and promote more sustainable use of supplementary water resources, particularly groundwater, to face the water shortage challenge9,10.

A major challenge in managing groundwater resources is the natural variability and complexity of aquifer systems11,12. Furthermore, the highly variable and discontinuous nature of these aquifers, which are often influenced by complex geological structures and tectonic movements, makes it difficult to accurately assess their groundwater recharge potential and develop sustainable extraction strategies13–15. On top of that, the limited recharge rates of hard rock aquifers in arid regions, coupled with groundwater overexploitation, can lead to rapid depletion and salinization of these critical water resources, posing serious threats to the long-term sustainability of water supplies16–18. Consequently, identifying and mapping high-potential groundwater areas in arid environments has become an essential responsibility for water resource planners and managers. This information is vital for guiding decisions related to sustainable groundwater development, extraction, and management practices19,20.

To address these challenges, assessing groundwater recharge potential in different aquifers frequently requires the integration of multiple methodologies, each with its own set of strengths and limits. For example, remote sensing (RS) has emerged as an invaluable tool in identifying geological structures, lineaments, and other geomorphological features that can serve as indicators of groundwater-bearing formations21–24. Geophysical methods, such as electrical resistivity and electromagnetic surveys, have also proven beneficial in delineating subsurface geological formations and detecting the presence of groundwater25–28. Moreover, the integration of groundwater recharge potential mapping with other complementary approaches, such as numerical groundwater flow modelling, geophysical surveys, and hydrochemical analyses, can further enhance our understanding of the complex hydrogeological systems, thus facilitating the formulation of more effective and sustainable groundwater management plans28–32. As well, the integration of Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and geospatial modeling techniques has enabled the development of comprehensive groundwater recharge potential maps that consider a wide range of environmental and hydrogeological factors, including lithology, geomorphology, slope, drainage patterns, and precipitation patterns33,34. By integrating decision-support frameworks such as the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP), researchers can synthesize multiple thematic layers of different factors to evaluate their collective impact on recharge processes. As a structured multi-criteria decision-making approach, the AHP enables systematic factor comparison and weighting which results in comprehensive and reproducible recharge potential maps. The application of the AHP model has been demonstrated in several studies35–37, where it has been shown to be an effective tool for delineating groundwater recharge potential zones for sustainable development and planning.

Integrating multiple approaches provides deeper insights into groundwater abundance and spatial distribution. This, in turn, can improve water security and promote sustainable development in regions that lack surface water sources. While AHP–GIS techniques have been widely applied for groundwater potential and recharge mapping, their use in fractured basement aquifers of the Arabian Nubian Shield (ANS), combined with independent geophysical and hydrochemical validation and explicit recharge-volume estimation, remains limited. This research investigates the use of an integrated AHP–GIS–RS framework to delineate groundwater recharge potential zones within the geologically intricate and water-scarce Qift–El Quseir region of Egypt’s Eastern Desert. By combining hydrological, geological, and topographic datasets, the study identifies priority areas for groundwater recharge and validates the results using field and geophysical data. The findings provide spatially explicit guidance to support sustainable groundwater management in arid environments and advance groundwater recharge potential mapping in structurally complex basement terrains through integrated, field-validated AHP–GIS–RS analysis.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study area is located in the central Eastern Desert on both sides of the Qift–El Quseir Road (25° 25′–26° 20′ N and 32° 37′–34° 30′ E), with an area about 8398 km2 (Fig. 1). It extends more than 175 km meandering throughout the Eastern desert between the Nile valley and the Red Sea coast. The region is characterized by a hot, arid climate with limited precipitation. The region experiences extremely high summer temperatures and arid climatic conditions throughout the year. During the winter months, temperatures range from the 20 to 30 °C, while in the summers the average daily temperatures often exceed 40 °C. Precipitation in the Qift–El Quseir area is scarce, with annual rainfall typically less than 50 mm. The local population relies heavily on groundwater resources for their water needs33,34.

(a) Location map of the study area, (b) Digital Elevation Model, and (c) Geologic map showing the main geologic units (a, b, and c were generated with ArcGIS 10.8 38).

The study area lies within the Arabian Nubian Shield (ANS), which comprises igneous and metamorphic rocks on both sides of the Red Sea that were formed about 600 million years ago during the collision of eastern and western Gondwana39,40. The exposed rock units span from Precambrian through Quaternary periods including both Pan-African basement rocks and younger Phanerozoic sedimentary rocks. The basement rocks in the area consist mainly of ophiolitic mélange, molasse-type sediments, volcanics, and granites39–41. Phanerozoic sedimentary rocks represented by Late Cretaceous-Early Eocene, Miocene, and Quaternary deposits, which build unconformable platforms above basement rocks. Multiple deformation phases which include thrusting and shearing along the Najd Fault as well as Red Sea rifting have divided this region into structurally complex tectonic zones such as the Safaga-Qena fault zones. The Safaga–Qena fault zones constitute an E–W to ENE–WSW–striking transfer/shear system, oblique to the regionally dominant NW–SE Najd fault system. Where these faults intersect Najd‑aligned fractures and major wadis, fracture connectivity and damage zones are enhanced, creating preferential pathways for infiltration and along‑fault groundwater flow39,42.

The aquifer systems in this region consist of three hydrogeological units. Firstly, there is the Quaternary shallow unconfined aquifer, which is predominantly made up of alluvial deposits including gravel, sand, silt, and clay. This water-bearing formation is found in the Wadi El Mathula delta and along the main channels but is absent in the eastern sections of the study area. The thickness increases downstream of the wadi and reaches 100 m 43. The water quality is primarily brackish to saline, rendering it suitable solely for agricultural purposes43. The depth of the Quaternary aquifer varies from 5 m in the Wadi El Mathula delta to 54 m in the northern part of Qift City. Recharge for this aquifer occurs mainly through precipitation, return flow from irrigation, and existing faults. Secondly, the Nubian Sandstone confined aquifer is chiefly composed of sand and shale, with thicknesses ranging from less than 22.3 m in the eastern areas to 700 m in the central part of the study area. The water-bearing layers of this aquifer function as the primary aquifer (Cretaceous aquifer) in Wadi El-Mathula. The Nubian aquifer is classified as a deep aquifer. Water from the drilled wells in the study area flows with a head ranging from 1.34 to 9.30 m above the ground surface. The natural discharge of the wells varies from 168 to 1500 m3/day44. Lastly, the Fractured Basement aquifer is in the far eastern part of the study area, with thicknesses ranging from 15 m to 25.4 m. The potential of this aquifer is limited, and there are currently no drilled wells extracting water from it. The limited potential of the fractured‑basement aquifer reflects the massive, only thinly weathered crystalline rocks and low lineament/fracture connectivity, which reduce transmissivity.

Data and data processing

To develop the groundwater recharge potential model for the study area, a suite of spatial and non-spatial datasets was incorporated. These datasets were acquired from a range of sources (Table 1) and, when necessary, were further processed and refined to meet the analytical standards required through the study framework (Fig. 2). The first dataset consists of Sentinel-2 imagery, incorporating visible, near-infrared (NIR), and mid-infrared (MIR) bands, which was used as the primary source of optical data. The second dataset comprises ALOS PALSAR DEM with a spatial resolution of approximately 12.5 m, obtained from the USGS Earth Explorer website (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/), along with available geological maps (Conco, 1987). All datasets were georeferenced to the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) coordinate system using the World Geodetic System (WGS 1984) datum in ILWIS (Integrated Land and Water Information Systems) version 3.4 and ENVI 4.8 software.

The analytic hierarchy process (AHP)

The Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) is a well-established decision-making framework that allows for the evaluation and prioritization of multiple criteria in a systematic and structured manner46. This process is particularly useful when it comes to delineating groundwater recharge potential zones, as it enables the integration of various geospatial data layers and the assignment of relative importance to each factor47,48.

Eight groundwater recharge-controlling factors were considered in the multi-criteria evaluation: lineament density, lithology, topographic wetness index (TWI), slope, drainage density, soil type, land cover, and rainfall. Hydrogeological conceptual model based on the field data

Validating the groundwater recharge potential zones identified via the AHP method involves a reinterpretation of the previously published geophysical exploration results reported by Thabet et al. (2020)53 in combination with the utilization of published geochemical water well data reported by Mohallel (2020)54. The geophysical profile traces and the well and sample locations were overlaid on the GRP map to evaluate the agreement between classes and observations (low-TDS points preferentially falling within high-recharge classes, and high-TDS points within low-recharge classes). Because direct measurement of basin-wide groundwater recharge is not feasible in this arid setting, an empirical, area-weighted approach was adopted, similar to many recent GIS–MCDA studies that delineate groundwater recharge or potential zones and then aggregate them to obtain basin-scale estimates49,55. In this approach, the groundwater recharge potential map was first classified into four classes (Excellent–Very Good, Very Good–Good, Good–Moderate, and Moderate–Low) based on the integrated RS–GIS–AHP analysis. Each class was then assigned a representative recharge ratio (the average percentage of rainfall expected to infiltrate) reflecting its relative infiltration capacity and hydrogeological favorability (high lineament density, permeable lithology, low slope, and low drainage density for the higher classes, and the opposite for the lower classes). This follows the common practice of using zonal recharge coefficients or empirical fractions of rainfall derived from hydrogeological reasoning, calibration, or comparison with water balance and tracer-based recharge estimates19,56. Results

Influential factors

Groundwater identification and occurrence are determined through indirect analysis of geological and geomorphic features, along with their hydrological characteristics. In this study, eight major factors influencing groundwater recharge potentiality were investigated.

Lineaments orientation and density

Lineaments represent geomorphological linear features that ascribed to geological structures (e.g., fractures in rocks) and/or to contacts between different rock units57,58. The significance of these factors in hydrogeology cannot be overstated, as they play an influential role in the recharge and flow of groundwater. Groundwater recharge is influenced by three key principles related to lineaments, including their connectivity, length and number21,59,60. Areas with high lineament density offer significant promise for groundwater. Figure 3d shows the densest lineament networks in the central and eastern sectors of the study area, whereas the western margin exhibits comparatively lower lineament density. Comparable integrated remote‑sensing and geophysical investigations in Sinai’s basement terrains demonstrate that groundwater accumulation and recharge concentrate along major fault corridors and at intersections of shear/fault systems, a pattern consistent with the high lineament‑density clusters mapped here24,28.

Slope, TWI, drainage and drainage density

Basin slope plays a critical role in the understanding the groundwater occurrence recharge and movement. Areas with gentle slopes tend to have slow runoff, resulting in a longer residence time for rainwater to infiltrate, hence a higher potential for recharge. On the other hand, areas with steep slopes experience fast runoff, leading to less infiltration and recharge of aquifers60–62. The slope map of the study area (Fig. 4a) reveals that the eastern part, characterized by high slopes (up to 56 degrees), indicating higher surface runoff velocities and reduction of the time available for water infiltration. In contrast, the central and western regions are dominated by gentle slopes (< 5 degrees), suggesting slower surface water movement, enhancing the potential for water to infiltrate the soil and replenish groundwater reserves. Topographic variables, such as slope, aspect, and relief, are also closely connected to morphometry of the stream networks, as they determine the energy available for erosion and the efficiency of water transport63,64. By analysing the nature and shape of drainages, researchers can gain valuable insights into water infiltration and runoff patterns.

The thematic layers of the factors involved in the analysis (a) slope; (b) TWI; (c) streams; drainage density (a, b, c, and d were generated with ArcGIS 10.8 38).

The Topographic Wetness Index (TWI) layer provides insight into how topography controls the spatial distribution of potential soil moisture and surface and subsurface water accumulation in the basin. Low TWI values coincide with ridge tops, steep slopes, and upper catchment areas, where contributing areas are small and slopes are relatively high. These zones are typically well drained, promote rapid runoff, and therefore have low potential for water ponding and infiltration; in the groundwater recharge model, they tend to correspond to low recharge potential classes36. By contrast, moderate to high TWI values are concentrated along wadi bottoms, gentle footslopes, and structurally depressed areas, where flow paths converge and local slopes decrease. These settings favour water accumulation, longer residence times at the surface, and higher soil moisture content, and thus act as preferred locations for infiltration and focused recharge. In the study area, the high TWI belts generally follow the main drainage network and coincide with Quaternary alluvial plains and structurally controlled corridors, forming one of the key components of the high and very high groundwater recharge potential zones identified by the multi criteria model (Fig. 4b).

The dendritic pattern observed in the studied watershed (Fig. 4c), covering an area of 8,398 km2, have drainage density ranging from 0.1 to 0.55 (Fig. 4d), which influenced by factors including lithology, topography, lineaments, resistance to weathering, and dominant climatic conditions. Areas with high drainage density typically experience low infiltration and recharge, while those with low density exhibit the opposite52. For instance, the eastern and central areas are characterized by high drainage density, whereas low‑density zones are concentrated toward the western margin (Fig. 4d).

Soil, land Cover, and lithology

The texture of the soil plays a critical role in determining the amount of water that can infiltrate the ground, thereby influencing the recharge of groundwater. The rate of infiltration is significantly influenced by the soil texture and the hydraulic characteristics of the soils65,66. The study area is mainly covered by silty clay loam and clay loam soil in valley fill, with sandy and loamy sand soils in the central areas, and rugged rocky lands in the eastern areas (Fig. 5a). In general, soils with high porosity and permeability, such as sandy or loamy soils, generally have a higher potential for groundwater recharge as they allow water to readily infiltrate and percolate downward, replenishing the aquifer. In contrast, soils with high clay content or compacted structure tend to have lower infiltration rates, leading to increased surface runoff and reduced groundwater recharge. The organic matter content of soil also contributes to its water-holding capacity and infiltration properties, with soils rich in organic matter generally exhibiting enhanced recharge potential due to their improved structure and water-retention capabilities67.

The thematic layers of the factors involved in the analysis (a) soil; (b) landcover; (c) lithology; (d) rainfall (a, b, c, and d were generated with ArcGIS 10.8 38).

The spatial distribution and heterogeneity of soil types within a region can significantly influence the overall GRP zones. Areas with a predominance of well-drained, permeable soils are more likely to experience higher rates of groundwater recharge, while regions with a higher proportion of low-permeability soils may have reduced recharge potential, leading to increased surface water accumulation and potential for flooding. Furthermore, the depth to the water table and the presence of confining layers or impermeable bedrock can also impact the recharge dynamics, as these factors determine the vertical distance water must travel to reach the aquifer. Moreover, Fig. 5b illustrates the main land cover types within the study area, which include bare ground, built areas, crops, trees, vegetation, range lands and water bodies. The major land use type is consolidated areas, which consist of bare rock and stony soil, covering about 49% of the total area. Following this, unconsolidated areas, including stony bare soils, and loose sands, represent approximately 46% of the area. The distribution of land use is expected to support groundwater recharge based on the underlying soil and geological conditions. However, human activities such as urban development and agriculture can alter natural infiltration and percolation processes, resulting in changes to the amount and distribution of groundwater recharge59.

The lithological nature of the rocks that are exposed is of utmost importance in determining recharge, as other factors are greatly influenced by it. Erosion, weathering, resistance, and the presence of fractures all affect the ability of rocks to allow water infiltration68,69. The porosity and permeability of the different rock types are vital in determining the storage and flow of groundwater. Groundwater storage capacity is positively influenced by higher porosity, while flow and yield are enhanced by increased permeability. The geological map of the study area indicates that the western part of the area is predominantly covered by Cretaceous rocks, with Quaternary deposits occupying the lower areas44. It is evident that surface runoff is more significant in regions with fractured rocks (Cretaceous rocks) and less in areas with alluvial deposits (Quaternary sediments). As a result, a lithology map has been classified into clastic, carbonates, and hard rocks (Fig. 5c).

Rainfall

Within this area, rainfall acts as the primary mechanism for recharging various aquifers. The climate in this region is classified as arid to semi-arid, with a significant range in the availability of rainfall. Annual rainfall measurements typically fall within the range of 3.01 to 7.33 mm (Fig. 5d). Analysis of the rainfall distribution reveals that high elevation areas receive more rainfall compared to low elevation regions. Rainfall patterns are more consistent in hillslope areas than in foothill regions70. Regions with higher rainfall can facilitate soil infiltration and serve as a reservoir for groundwater.

Groundwater recharge potential (GRP) model

A spatial database was created using ArcGIS software, and the thematic layers of the parameters were converted from vector to raster format with a 30 m cell size (Figs. 6 and 7), where the selected influential factors are divided into classes. The preparation of the GRP map involved normalization, weighting, and weighted overlay analysis. It is clear from the final GRP map (Fig. 8a) that about 22.0% of the basin area is categorized as excellent to very good recharge potential, while 34.91% is classified as very good to good. Good to moderate recharge potential zones occupy 31.23% of the basin, and 11.86% of the area falls within the moderate to low category (Fig. 8b). In general, excellent to very good potential zones are located downstream in Wadi Mathula near Qift city, especially in the Al-Laqeita region, due to the very low slope gradients and low drainage density. Moreover, high groundwater recharge potentiality exists inside the basement rocks where many faults, fractures, or shear zones intersect, as the intersection of these discontinuities increase porosity and produce favourable conditions for groundwater storage in deformed basement rocks. Good and moderate categories are the central area, where they have a relatively high abundance of lineaments, a moderate slope, and a high drainage density, such as the El-Fawakhir and Biadia areas, which are located in fractured basement rocks. These zones are elongated mainly toward the NW–SE direction, which confirms the role of the Najd fault system in developing favourable areas for groundwater storage. Brittle deformation induced by Red Sea rifting also plays an important role, particularly near the contact between sediments and basement rocks. Most zones with fair to poor groundwater recharge potential are located in massive basement units that lie far from major lineaments. These findings agree with the previous study carried out by Abdalla71, who identified Wadi fillings and the Quaternary deposits, in addition to the fractured Precambrian basement rocks as very good to good prospecting zones. In addition, these results agreed with several studies who recommended AHP as a more effective and reliable approach for GRP mapping36,37,51 confirming that the high level of accuracy in the application of AHP technique in identification of the recharge potential sites in the arid regions.

Sensitivity and verification analysis

To test how strongly the multi-criteria model depends on the expert-defined AHP weights, a Monte Carlo–based sensitivity analysis (1,000 iterations; ±15% perturbation) was performed. In each iteration, the original weight of every factor was randomly perturbed within a ± 15% range and then renormalized so the total weight equaled 1. This procedure, commonly used to propagate input uncertainty in environmental models, generated an empirical distribution for each factor weight. From these distributions, the mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated, providing a quantitative measure of the stability of the AHP weights under uncertainty. The results indicate that lineament density and lithology consistently remain the most influential factors, with mean weights of 21.68% and 21.57%, respectively, and relatively low coefficients of variation (~ 7.5%). All factors exhibit CV values below 9%, indicating that the weighting scheme is reasonably stable and that moderate perturbations in the input judgments do not substantially alter the overall ranking of factors. This supports confidence that the subsequent groundwater recharge potential (GRP) mapping is not overly sensitive to small changes in the AHP weights.

After confirming the robustness of the weighting scheme, the predictive performance of the groundwater recharge potential (GRP) map was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis. The ROC curve is a standard tool for assessing classification performance and has been widely applied in groundwater recharge potential studies22,36. In this study, it was used to quantify how well the GRP map discriminates between wells that exhibit strong recharge characteristics and those with limited or negligible recharge.

Field wells reported by Mohallel (2020)54 were first grouped into two classes: Success = 1 (recharge wells), corresponding to Group I Nubian wells with a clear recharge signature, and Success = 0 (non-recharge wells), corresponding to Group II Quaternary wells with low recharge signature. Nubian Sandstone wells (Group I) were classified as ‘recharge wells’ and Quaternary wells (Group II) as ‘non-recharge wells’ following Mohallel (2020)54, who showed that the Nubian wells in this area bear a more pronounced modern recharge signature in isotopic space, whereas the shallow Quaternary wells are dominated by older or evaporatively enriched waters with limited active recharge. For each well, the corresponding GRP raster value (RV) was extracted and assigned to one of four recharge classes: 4 = excellent, 3 = good, 2 = moderate, and 1 = low. Groundwater recharge rates

Beyond relative suitability, the GRP map was also used to estimate the actual amount of rainfall recharging the aquifer. The areal integration of recharge classes provides a first-order estimate of how much rainfall effectively recharges the aquifers in the basin. For a total area of 8,398 km2 and a mean annual rainfall of 4.3 mm (0.0043 m), the annual precipitation volume is on the order of 3.61 × 107 m3/year. Applying the class-specific recharge ratios yields a total recharge volume of about 9.67 × 106 m3/year, equivalent to a basin-wide effective recharge of approximately 26.8% of annual precipitation. This recharge, however, is strongly concentrated in the most favorable zones: the “Excellent–Very Good” and “Very Good–Good” classes together occupy ~ 56.9% of the basin but contribute nearly 80% of the total recharge (≈ 3.69 × 106 and 4.01 × 106 m3/yr, respectively). In contrast, the “Moderate–Low” class covers ~ 11.9% of the area yet accounts for only ~ 3.3% of recharge (≈ 3.14 × 105 m3/yr).

Although the basin is characterized by very low mean annual rainfall (4.3 mm), the relatively high estimated recharge fraction (approximately 26.8% of annual precipitation) is reasonable in the context of an arid, structurally controlled catchment where recharge is highly focused rather than spatially uniform. The basin-average rainfall masks the occurrence of infrequent but intense storm events that generate substantial runoff along specific wadis and uplands; this water is then funnelled into limited “hotspot” areas with highly favorable hydrogeological conditions, such as fault-controlled depressions, wide wadi beds, and coarse Quaternary alluvium over permeable sandstone. In these zones, channel transmission losses can be very efficient, allowing a large proportion of floodwater to infiltrate and percolate into the aquifer, which support assigning higher recharge ratios (30–50%) to the “Excellent–Very Good” and “Very Good–Good” classes while much lower ratios are applied elsewhere. As a result, a small fraction of the basin area contributes most of the recharge, and although the percentage of rainfall converted to recharge appears high, the absolute recharge volume (9.67 × 106 m3/yr) remains modest because the total precipitation volume is itself very small.

Conceptual model and validation

The findings from the potential recharge map clearly demonstrate an increasing significance of groundwater occurrences in areas where the Quaternary and Nubian sandstone aquifers are dominant. The validity of our map was verified by comparing it with previous geophysical surveys, TDS measurements, and water depth parameters (Fig. 10). To validate the GRP map, we compared our classes with two independents, published datasets that cover the same area. Geophysical data along the Qift–El Quseir road, where integrated magnetic, vertical electrical sounding, and electrical resistivity tomography surveys delineate the main aquifer systems and promising drilling targets53. Hydrogeochemical data from 18 groundwater samples collected in Qift and Wadi El-Mathula, including TDS and major ion concentrations54. We overlaid well/sample locations and geophysical measurements on our GRP classes (Fig. 10) and observed consistent patterns: low TDS points cluster in “excellent–good” zones, whereas high TDS points coincide with “moderate–low” zones, and geophysical cross sections indicate thicker, more transmissive sediments where classes are highest.

The uppermost lithologic layer consists originally of a thin layer of alluvial sediments and wadi fills with a thickness ranging from 5 to 10 m. The Pleistocene sediments in the eastern and western regions act as the near surface zone, capable of storing water through infiltration or evaporation. The Nubian sandstone aquifer in the western and central areas serves as a primary recharge location, while the crystalline basement aquifer in the eastern areas has limited recharge potential due to filled and cemented fractures. Also, the data reveals a correlation between high TDS levels in bore wells and their placement in zones characterized by low potential, whereas bore wells with low TDS levels are typically situated in areas classified as having high potential.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the efficacy of integrating RS, GIS, and AHP for identifying groundwater recharge potential zones in arid and geologically complex environments such as Egypt’s Eastern Desert. The results highlight the importance of multi-criteria spatial modelling in supporting sustainable groundwater management, particularly in regions with limited surface water resources. In recent decades, extensive research has been conducted to identify the recharge potential zones using remote sensing, geospatial tools and AHP model51,72–74. This involves analysing various factors like lithologic features, structural parameters, water flow patterns, and geomorphic factors. While the method may be subjective due to the weightage factors, it has been effectively applied in groundwater recharge potential mapping studies. The use of such integration for mapping recharge potential locations offers advantages in terms of accuracy, cost-effectiveness, time efficiency, and data availability. This study’s findings on structural controls guiding groundwater recharge are consistent with recent research in Sinai24,28. These studies also demonstrate that fault zones and structural intersections are primary sites for groundwater accumulation. Both the Sinai work and this study use structural and geomorphic features to map potential, and both emphasize the importance of validating these models with follow-up geophysical surveys.

The integrated recharge potential mapping provides a coherent picture of the hydrogeological framework of the study area and clarifies how structural, lithological, geomorphological, and climatic controls interact to govern groundwater occurrence and recharge. The results indicate that about 22% of the area has excellent to very good recharge potential and 34.91% has very good to good potential, primarily in regions with low slope angles (< 5°), high lineament density, and Quaternary alluvial deposits. Conversely, approximately 11.86% of the area shows moderate to low recharge potential, coinciding with steep slopes (> 30°) and massive basement rocks where rapid runoff and low permeability restrict infiltration. These findings are consistent with previous regional studies50,58,60,67, confirming that the intersection of lineaments in fractured basement terrains facilitates increased porosity and permeability, enhancing infiltration. Conversely, 9% of the area showed low recharge potential, coinciding with steep slopes (> 30°) and massive basement rocks, where rapid runoff and low permeability restrict infiltration16.

The basin-scale recharge efficiency estimated here is about 26.8% of annual precipitation, corresponding to a total recharge volume of approximately 9.67 × 106 m3/year (Table 5). This fraction is within the upper range of values reported for arid and semi-arid basement terrains where recharge is strongly focused along wadi beds and structurally controlled conduits. In comparable North African settings, long-term water-balance and WetSpass-based studies have yielded basin-scale recharge fractions on the order of 15–30% of annual precipitation, depending on lithology and the degree of structural focusing56,75. Our estimates are therefore plausible given the strongly channel-focused recharge and the presence of highly permeable alluvial and sandstone units in a relatively small number of structurally favourable corridors.

The AHP results and Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis jointly highlight lineament density and lithology as the dominant factors controlling groundwater recharge potential, with mean weights of 21.7% and 21.6%, respectively, and the lowest coefficients of variation among all parameters. This findings are consistent with numerous GIS–AHP groundwater recharge potential studies in arid and semi-arid settings, where geology/lithology and lineament density repeatedly emerge as the highest-ranked criteria. High lineament density zones, particularly where major fractures and faults intersect with wadi networks, provide preferential pathways for vertical infiltration and lateral flow within both the shallow Quaternary deposits and the deeper Nubian Sandstone aquifer, in agreement with structural–hydrogeological analyses that show enhanced recharge and well productivity along fracture corridors. The strong weight assigned to lithology reflects the contrast between permeable sandy/alluvial units and more consolidated or fine-grained formations that tend to act as aquitards. In the mapped GRP, the highest recharge potential classes (excellent–very good and very good–good) align spatially with areas underlain by Quaternary alluvium and Nubian Sandstone, which are recognized regionally as the principal productive aquifers in North-East Africa. Conversely, areas underlain by less permeable formations, or where lineaments are sparse or poorly connected, systematically fall into moderate to low recharge classes. This pattern suggests a hydrogeological framework in which recharge is strongly compartmentalized: structurally and lithologically favorable zones act as focused recharge “windows” into otherwise semi-confined or poorly recharged parts of the basin.

The intermediate weights assigned to TWI, slope, and drainage density further refine this structural–lithological framework. Numerous GIS-based groundwater recharge potential and recharge mapping studies have shown that high TWI values, gentle to moderate slopes, and low drainage density coincide with zones of water accumulation, reduced runoff velocity, and enhanced infiltration. In the present basin, these conditions are concentrated in the downstream and middle segments of major wadis and in structurally depressed areas, where runoff decelerates and accumulates. These zones form the backbone of the high and very high recharge-potential belts. In contrast, steep slopes and high drainage density in upland and headwater areas favor rapid runoff, limited infiltration, and therefore low recharge potential, as also reported in Wadi Fatima (Saudi Arabia) and other arid watersheds where high-relief headwaters behave mainly as runoff source zones feeding downstream recharge areas. Land use/land cover and soil type, although ranked slightly lower, modulate these processes by controlling surface infiltration capacity: coarse-textured alluvial soils and sparsely vegetated wadi floors typically promote infiltration, whereas compacted or fine-grained surfaces and urban or disturbed land covers reduce recharge and enhance overland flow. The relatively small weight assigned to rainfall reflects the limited spatial variability of precipitation compared with the strong contrasts imposed by structure, lithology, and topography; similar findings have been reported in other semi-arid basins where spatial recharge patterns are primarily controlled by landscape properties rather than by small differences in rainfall totals.

From a resource-management perspective, this pattern underscores the importance of protecting these key recharge areas from land degradation, uncontrolled abstraction, or activities that reduce infiltration (e.g., paving, compaction, or poorly planned infrastructure). Similar recommendations have been made for other arid and semi-arid basins where AHP-based recharge or groundwater-potential maps identify a small fraction of the area as critical recharge corridors.

From an operational perspective, the mapped recharge hotspots indicate where managed aquifer recharge interventions would be most effective. The highest priority targets are the downstream reach of Wadi El Mathula and the Al Laqeita alluvial fan, where Quaternary wadi fills and Nubian Sandstone outcrops coincide with the “Excellent–Very Good” GRP class and where geophysical and hydrochemical data confirm thick, transmissive sediments and relatively low salinity groundwater. Additional suitable locations occur along structurally controlled wadis in the central basin near El Fawakhir and Biadia, and in localized depressions at the contact between Quaternary alluvium and basement highs that fall within the “Very Good–Good” class. In these zones, small recharge dams, check dams, and infiltration basins could be used to enhance channel transmission losses and promote percolation into the underlying aquifers, whereas moderate-to-low GRP areas are less suitable for artificial recharge and should instead be managed mainly as runoff source zones.

The conceptual framework derived from the GRP analysis is strongly supported by independent geophysical and hydrogeochemical data. Along the Qift–El Quseir road, integrated magnetic, vertical electrical sounding, and electrical resistivity tomography surveys show that the most promising groundwater targets occur where structurally controlled wadis intersect thick sedimentary successions comprising Quaternary alluvium and Nubian Sandstone, with enhanced fracturing and favorable basin geometry. These geophysical findings are consistent with regional syntheses of the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System, which emphasize structurally guided flow paths and localized modern recharge where outcrops and fault zones coincide. Similarly, the distribution of TDS from groundwater samples in Qift and Wadi El Mathula reinforces the recharge pattern inferred from the GRP map: wells located within “excellent–good” GRP zones predominantly exhibit lower TDS values and better hydrochemical quality, whereas wells in “moderate–low” recharge zones tend to show higher salinity and more evolved water types, reflecting longer residence times and partial isolation from active recharge. This spatial coherence between recharge potential and water quality supports a hydrogeological model in which structurally and geomorphically favored zones provide active recharge conduits, while surrounding areas host more stagnant or older groundwater.

The ROC validation further strengthens this interpretation. The AUC value of ~ 0.72 indicates an acceptable level of predictive performance, comparable to other GIS–AHP groundwater and recharge potential models that report AUC values around 0.7–0.8 when validated against well yields or field-based indicators. The monotonic increase in true positive rate with decreasing threshold, accompanied by a gradual rise in false positives, illustrates the expected trade-off between sensitivity and specificity and confirms that the GRP map discriminates meaningfully between productive (actively recharged) and less productive zones. The fact that high-recharge classes coincide with thicker, more transmissive sediments in geophysical sections and with low-TDS, better-quality water in hydrochemical data provides a multi-line, independent validation of the spatial patterns produced by the AHP–GIS model.

Taken together, the agreement among (i) the GRP map and its AHP–Monte Carlo robustness; (ii) ROC performance (AUC ≈ 0.72); (iii) geophysical indicators of aquifer thickness, fracture intensity, and structural control; and (iv) TDS and hydrochemical patterns suggests a well-organized flow system. Focused recharge occurs in structurally controlled wadies and sandstone domains with high lineament density, favorable lithology, and moderate slopes; from there, groundwater moves laterally and vertically through the Quaternary and Nubian Sandstone systems, with hydraulic connectivity enhanced along fault and fracture zones. Peripheral and structurally elevated areas are comparatively poorly recharged, host more mineralized groundwater, and contribute mainly to surface runoff rather than direct infiltration. This conceptual model is consistent with regional understanding of the Nubian Sandstone aquifer and other large arid-zone aquifers, where paleo-recharge dominates storage, but modest modern recharge still occurs locally along structurally and geomorphically favorable outcrops.

To sum up, the results from this study could assist the decision-makers, water resource authorities, and other stakeholders in effectively managing the identified GRP zone in the study area for long-term sustainability. Moreover, to improve the understanding of the GRP in the study area, it is beneficial for future research to expand the metrics used, include economic and social data, and conduct further examination of the bore wells. Additionally, it is crucial to note that this study only addresses the spatial variability that can influence the identification of potential groundwater zones. For future studies, it is advisable to consider temporal variability.

Limitations

This work is subject to several limitations that should be considered when applying the results. The recharge estimates combine long term mean rainfall from coarse resolution gridded products with class-based recharge ratios that are inferred from hydrogeological reasoning rather than calibrated against independent water balance, water level, or tracer-based recharge measurements, which introduces uncertainty into the absolute recharge volumes. Model validation is constrained by the limited number and spatial coverage of available wells and geophysical profiles, so the performance metrics and conceptual model are most reliable along the Qift–El Quseir corridor and should be extrapolated with caution to poorly monitored parts of the basin. Because the ROC analysis is based on only eight wells located along a single transect, the resulting AUC of 0.72 should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive. Additional wells distributed across the basin would be valuable for further validating the GRP map.

Conclusions

The identification of possible areas for groundwater recharge is vital for the development of effective management approaches to protect groundwater reservoirs. This study successfully delineated groundwater recharge potential zones in the Qift–El Quseir area of Egypt’s Eastern Desert, using an integrated data approach including remote sensing, GIS, and AHP. The final groundwater recharge potential map distinguishes four classes, with excellent to very good and very good to good zones covering 22.00% and 34.91% of the basin, mainly along structurally controlled wadis, alluvial plains, and Nubian Sandstone outcrops. Good to moderate and moderate to low zones occupy 31.23% and 11.86%, respectively, and are largely associated with higher-relief areas and massive basement rocks where infiltration is limited. Using class specific recharge coefficients, the basin receives an estimated total recharge of 9.67 × 106 m3/year, equivalent to 26.8% of annual precipitation, which shows that a relatively small portion of the landscape functions as recharge hotspots. Consistent correspondence between high recharge classes, thicker and more transmissive sediments, low TDS groundwater, and ROC performance (AUC ≈ 0.72) supports the reliability of the proposed map. This highlights the potential for rainwater harvesting to enhance groundwater storage. The use of dynamic variables (rainfall seasonality and land use changes) could be adopted in future studies, and more efforts could be made in the field validation. Other decision-making methods such as fuzzy AHP and TOPSIS could be explored and applied in groundwater resources assessment models. Incorporating socio-economic and environmental considerations may also enhance the practicability of the model in regional development and water resources planning. Finally, this study could contribute to groundwater resources management by providing spatial decision support techniques to identify the locations with high priority for artificial recharge, groundwater protection, and targeted groundwater exploration to build a more sustainable water system in arid and semi-arid areas. The mapped recharge hotspots provide priority targets for artificial recharge, protection from land degradation, and careful monitoring of abstraction, while the moderate-to-low recharge zones should be managed with the understanding that they contribute little to long-term aquifer replenishment.
