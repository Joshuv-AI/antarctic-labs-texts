Thiamine use and short-term prognosis in critical cerebrovascular disease: a propensity score matched retrospective analysis from the MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Tianli Zhang, Minheng Zhang, Haixia Fan, Xuan Chen, Peng Wei, Zhaoxu Yin, Xin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn the realm of critical care, cerebrovascular disease remains a major factor in patient morbidity and mortality, with limited treatment options outside of acute interventions. The importance of thiamine (vitamin B1) in energy metabolism is well-known, and it may provide neuroprotective benefits, though its role in serious cerebrovascular diseases is still uncertain.MethodsUsing data from the MIMIC-IV database, this retrospective cohort study explored the relationship between thiamine and short-term outcomes in patients with critical cerebrovascular conditions. The application of propensity score matching (PSM) helped to balance covariates between users and non-users of thiamine. The study’s primary and secondary endpoints were the all-cause mortality at 7, 14, and 28 days. To estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI), Cox proportional hazards models were used, along with analyses for subgroups, dose-response, and duration-response.ResultsAmong 11,814 patients, 1,647 received thiamine. With 1,585 matched pairs post-PSM, thiamine administration showed a significant association with decreased mortality at 7 days (HR = 0.68, 95%CI: 0.53–0.89, P = 0.004), 14 days (HR = 0.78, 95%CI: 0.64–0.96, P = 0.016), and 28 days (HR = 0.82, 95%CI: 0.69–0.98, P = 0.028) compared with non-thiamine use after adjustment. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger benefits in patients aged ≥ 65 years, females, and those without mannitol or thrombolysis. Enhanced outcomes were most consistently observed with treatment durations longer than 5 days and daily doses lower than 100 mg.ConclusionsThiamine supplementation correlated with a reduction in short-term mortality for patients with critical cerebrovascular conditions, especially with prolonged treatment and reduced daily doses. While these observations propose thiamine as a possible supportive metabolic therapy, the evidence remains insufficient to guide clinical practice and should be considered hypothesis-generating pending confirmation in randomized trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04478-w.Keywords: MIMIC-IV database, Thiamine, Short-term outcomes, Cerebrovascular disease, Propensity score matching

Introduction

Globally, cerebrovascular disease continues to be a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability, with acute ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes contributing greatly to critical care admissions [1, 2]. In spite of advancements in reperfusion and neuroprotective strategies, a considerable number of patients continue to experience adverse short-term outcomes, including high mortality and functional reliance [3–5]. The metabolic derangements accompanying acute brain injury, particularly mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired aerobic glycolysis, exacerbate neuronal damage and suggest a potential role for metabolic interventions in improving prognosis. Metabolic imbalances, such as mitochondrial dysfunction and compromised aerobic glycolysis, that occur during acute brain injury, aggravate neuronal damage and propose a possible role for metabolic strategies in improving outcomes [6]. Due to its role as a cofactor for vital enzymes in glucose metabolism, thiamine (vitamin B1) has been proposed as a therapy for its ability to improve energy production and mitigate lactate accumulation in ischemic tissues [7, 8]. Evidence for its application in critical cerebrovascular disease is still scarce, as most research is centered on sepsis or traumatic brain injury [9–11].

Recent observational data suggest that thiamine deficiency is prevalent among critically ill patients and correlates with worse neurological outcomes [12–14]. In clinical practice, small-scale trials in septic shock have reported a decrease in mortality with thiamine treatment, hypothesizing that stroke patients facing metabolic crises could see similar improvements [15, 16]. Yet, no large-scale studies have evaluated thiamine’s impact in cerebrovascular disease, particularly in heterogeneous intensive care unit (ICU) populations where confounders such as age, comorbidities, and concomitant therapies may modulate its effects. The gap in knowledge indicates the need for detailed real-world investigations to inform clinical practice.

By leveraging the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, this study investigates the relationship between thiamine usage and short-term prognosis in critical cerebrovascular disease, applying propensity score matching (PSM) to control for confounding variables. Our hypothesis is that thiamine supplementation correlates with decreased mortality and enhanced functional outcomes, particularly in individuals experiencing metabolic instability, like elevated lactate or those on mechanical ventilation. By using extensive clinical data from a diverse and sizable ICU cohort, this study aims to lay the groundwork for future randomized trials and highlight the patient populations that are most likely to gain advantages. The insights from our findings may optimize therapeutic strategies for a condition with scarce treatment alternatives beyond the hyperacute phase.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The analysis incorporated data from the MIMIC-IV (version 3.0), an open-source database created by research teams at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). The MIMIC-IV database, which encompasses the period from 2008 to 2019, provides thorough clinical data on patients in a single-center intensive care unit (ICU), with detailed information on baseline characteristics, health conditions, imaging findings, complications, medication usage, and diagnoses. In the updated MIMIC-IV database, current datasets are integrated, and several aspects of the previous MIMIC-III, which has been rigorously analyzed in academic circles, are upgraded. Following the completion of the required authorization protocol, a credentialed member of our research team (certification: 13230663) was granted access to the MIMIC-IV database. According to the data protection protocols of the institutional review board, all personal identification information was systematically removed from the MIMIC-IV dataset. Patient records were made anonymous by assigning them unique identifiers that were randomly generated. Hence, the Institutional Review Board of the second hospital of tianjin medical university granted an exemption from both individual patient consent and a full ethics review for this study.

Study population

Between the years 2008 and 2019, we located individuals in the MIMIC-IV database with a primary admission diagnosis of cerebrovascular disease, as specified by the relevant ICD codes: ICD-9 codes starting with 362.34 or ranging from 430 to 438, and ICD-10 codes starting with G45, G46, H34.0, or ranging from I60 to I69. Using diagnosis codes from the ICD’s Ninth and Tenth Revisions, subtypes of cardiovascular disease were identified [17, 18] (Supplemental Table 1). Eligibility required patients to satisfy these criteria: (1) ICU admission, (2) age of 18 years or older, (3) an ICU stay exceeding 24 h, and (4) a primary diagnosis of cerebrovascular disease. To eliminate duplication, we removed readmissions and nonindex ICU stays, ensuring only the first hospitalization and ICU admission were kept for each patient. The cohort derivation process is illustrated in Fig. 1. To summarize, 94,458 records of ICU admissions were examined. After excluding 9,216 non-index admissions, 85,242 individual patients remained. Out of these, 70,759 individuals without a cerebrovascular disease diagnosis were removed, resulting in 14,483 patients remaining. The final analytic cohort comprised 11,814 patients after excluding 2,669 patients who were either under 18 years old or had an ICU stay of 24 h or less. In the study, 1,647 people received thiamine, whereas 10,167 did not. To counteract baseline imbalances, PSM was applied using nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1 on the logit of the propensity score, leading to 1,585 matched pairs (3,170 patients).

Data extraction

Structured query language (SQL) was utilized to extract details recorded on the first day after being admitted to the ICU. In our study, we obtained multidimensional clinical data from the database, which spanned six crucial domains: (1) demographic characteristics, (2) vital signs, (3) clinical severity scores, (4) laboratory metrics, (5) comorbidity profiles, and (6) therapeutic methods. Demographic features included age, gender, and weight. Included in the physiological parameters were heart rate (HR), blood pressure (systolic [SBP], diastolic [DBP], and mean arterial pressure [MAP]), respiratory rate (RR), core temperature (T), and oxygen saturation (SPO₂). The laboratory parameters consisted of chloride, sodium, and potassium electrolyte levels, as well as coagulation profiles, specifically prothrombin time (PT) and partial thromboplastin time (PTT). To assess clinical severity, validated scoring systems were employed: charlson comorbidity index (CCI) for pre-existing conditions, glasgow coma scale (GCS) for neurological evaluation, simplified acute physiology score II (SAPS II) for physiological disruption, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score for organ dysfunction and oxford acute severity of illness score (OASIS) for overall disease severity. On the first day of ICU admission, the maximum values of vital signs and laboratory parameters were extracted, while the minimum GCS score was recorded and retained. Physiological and laboratory variables were extracted from the first 24 h of ICU admission, as this window best reflects baseline severity of illness before substantial therapeutic effects and is widely adopted in established scoring systems and contemporary predictive models [19–23]. During the first 24 h post-ICU admission, the peak values of physiological parameters were assessed, given the importance of this period for clinical risk evaluation and outcome prediction. For each patient, these maximum values (e.g., heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, body temperature, oxygen saturation, etc.) were defined as the highest recorded measurements during the index ICU stay. To avoid redundancy, only the first ICU admission was considered in cases with more than one ICU stay. The MIMIC-IV database’s time-stamped vital sign and lab records were used to extract the most severe physiological derangements observed early in the ICU stay. Initial irregularities in vital signs and lab markers are powerful predictors of how severe a disease will be and the outcomes for patients. The identification of comorbidities was done using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes from the MIMIC-IV database, and they were classified following the Charlson and Elixhauser frameworks. The list of conditions comprised heart failure, chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic pulmonary disease, cancer, moderate-to-severe liver disease, and atrial fibrillation. Only diagnoses that were documented before or at the point of ICU admission were included, distinguishing pre-existing conditions from complications that occurred during the ICU stay. In order to account for baseline health status, these variables were used in propensity score matching and multivariable regression models. Therapeutic interventions were extracted from the MIMIC-IV treatment and procedure records and included thiamine, mannitol, antiplatelet agents (e.g., aspirin, clopidogrel), anticoagulants (e.g., heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin, warfarin), thrombolytic therapy (e.g., tissue plasminogen activator), vasopressors (e.g., norepinephrine, dopamine), vasopressin, diuretics (e.g., furosemide), continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and invasive or non-invasive mechanical ventilation. Each intervention was recorded as a binary variable based on whether it was administered during the index ICU admission.

Clinical prescribing scenarios and definition of thiamine exposure

In the ICU setting, thiamine administration is most frequently initiated in the context of nutritional risk, particularly at the initiation of enteral or parenteral nutrition to prevent refeeding syndrome, and in patients with low body mass index (BMI), hypoalbuminemia, prolonged fasting, or overt malnutrition. It is also utilized in metabolic imbalances like lactic acidosis or septic shock, especially when the lactate level upon admission is at least 4 mmol/L, in patients who are hemodynamically unstable, or those needing vasopressor intervention. Given the accelerated clearance of water-soluble vitamins, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) represents another circumstance in which prophylactic supplementation may be considered. Neurological indications include altered mental status, delirium, seizures, or suspected Wernicke’s encephalopathy, which may be associated with alcohol use disorder or other predisposing conditions.

Guideline recommendations and standard ICU practices guided the categorization of doses [24, 25]. The ESPEN micronutrient guidelines recommend daily thiamine supplementation in the range of 1.5-3 mg during standard nutrition support and 10 mg/day intravenously for biochemically confirmed but clinically silent deficiency. Nonetheless, patients who are critically ill typically need much larger doses because of heightened metabolic needs. In ICU practice, thiamine is regularly given intravenously at 100–300 mg per day for metabolic support, while doses of 200–500 mg per day (administered once to three times daily) are often reported in cases of septic shock or lactic acidosis. Recommended treatment for Wernicke’s encephalopathy involves administering 200–500 mg intravenously three times per day. These references collectively justify our categorization into low- (< 100 mg/day), intermediate- (100–300 mg/day), and high-dose (>300 mg/day) groups. ESPEN’s micronutrient guidelines suggest that thiamine is generally safe, without a specified tolerable upper intake level, and excess amounts are largely excreted in urine [24]. Nonetheless, potential negative impacts should not be completely ignored. Rare instances of hypersensitivity reactions have been observed following high-dose intravenous administration, with doses over 400 mg/day potentially causing transient symptoms like nausea, anorexia, or mild ataxia. However, such events are exceedingly rare and occur much less often than the hazards of untreated thiamine deficiency.

The total daily intravenous dose of thiamine, defined as thiamine exposure, was calculated by summing all doses given within a 24-hour period.The schedule and quantity of administrations were adjusted according to the underlying indication. Thiamine was generally administered once daily to those at nutritional risk. For conditions like lactic acidosis or septic shock, increased or repeated dosing was frequently employed. Prophylactic supplementation was considered in patients undergoing continuous CRRT, and empiric treatment was applied for neurological indications including altered mental status, seizures, or suspected Wernicke’s encephalopathy. Prophylactic supplementation was contemplated for patients undergoing continuous CRRT, with empirical treatment applied for neurological indications like altered mental status, seizures, or suspected Wernicke’s encephalopathy. In the ICU, thiamine was mostly administered parenterally, with oral or enteral dosing being documented in only a minority of situations. To achieve a full analysis, these alternative routes were also taken into account. Treatment duration was defined as the total number of calendar days between the first and the last recorded thiamine administration, excluding days within this interval when thiamine was not administered.

Clinical outcomes

The study’s primary endpoint was the rate of death from any cause within 7 days after ICU entry. Secondary endpoints included death rates from any cause within 14 and 28 days of admission.

Statistical analysis

Both the extent and pattern of missing data were meticulously reviewed. As summarized in Supplementary Table 2, most clinical variables had a relatively low proportion of missing values (< 5%), whereas several laboratory measurements (chloride, sodium, potassium, PT, and PTT) had missingness ranging from 12% to 20%. For variables with ≤ 20% missingness, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was applied under the assumption of missing at random (MAR) (Supplementary Table 2). According to current recommendations, 12 imputed datasets were generated, ensuring the number of imputations is at least as high as the percentage of incomplete cases. Incorporating all covariates and outcomes, the imputation model ensured congeniality. During the descriptive analysis, medians with interquartile ranges (Q1, Q3) were used to summarize continuous variables, while percentages were used for categorical variables. To compare baseline characteristics between the thiamine and non-thiamine groups, the independent samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, while the Pearson chi-square test was applied for categorical variables. To evaluate the association between thiamine administration and clinical endpoints, Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), with selected models incorporating covariate adjustments. Using the Schoenfeld residual method and deviance residual plots, the proportional hazards (PH) assumption of the Cox regression model was examined (Supplementary Table 3). Prior to executing multivariate Cox regression, multicollinearity among the independent variables was checked using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and VIF values greater than 5 were considered to indicate significant multicollinearity (Supplementary Table 4). The present analysis included covariates with VIFs lower than the threshold. To handle potential baseline imbalances, we utilized 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement through PSM. Matching was performed on the logit of the propensity score with a caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit-PS, following the methodological recommendations of Austin [26], who demonstrated that this threshold provides an optimal trade-off between bias reduction and precision in matched samples. The choice of 1:1 matching was guided by prior methodological evidence indicating that 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching generally yields the best covariate balance and minimizes residual confounding, despite reducing the sample size compared with higher matching ratios [27–29]. Post-matching, the balance of covariates between the groups was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), where an SMD of 0.1 or lower indicated adequate balance [26, 29, 30]. According to Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2, all covariates reached the required threshold, confirming that PSM effectively minimized baseline disparities between groups. Propensity scores were calculated through logistic regression models that included a detailed set of baseline variables, selected a priori for their clinical relevance and previous literature. The variables consisted of demographic characteristics, initial severity of illness indicators, concurrent health issues, and therapeutic measures applied during the initial ICU admission. Before and after PSM, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses and log-rank tests were used to compare the outcomes of patients who used thiamine with those who did not. Within the PSM cohort, univariate and stepwise multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to examine the connection between thiamine exposure and all-cause mortality at 7, 14, and 28 days. Inclusion in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was based on variables having a p-value under 0.05 in the univariate Cox regression analysis. In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, three hierarchical models were developed, each with a progressively extensive set of covariates. Model 1 was not adjusted and acted as the baseline. Model 2 took into account demographic characteristics, physiological parameters, and disease severity indicators such as age, weight, HR, RR, SOFA score, GCS score, CCI, SAPS II, OASIS, and levels of serum chloride, sodium, potassium, PT, PTT, hypertension, and moderate to severe liver disease. Model 3 included additional adjustments for interventions related to treatment, such as CRRT, mechanical ventilation, mannitol administration, antiplatelet agents, anticoagulant therapy, thrombolytic treatment, vasoactive agents, vasopressin administration, and diuretics. Using restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions within the Cox proportional hazards model, we evaluated potential nonlinear connections between thiamine dose and short-term mortality. The daily dose of thiamine was calculated based on the total amount administered each day, and the treatment duration was defined as the total number of days from the first to the last recorded administration, excluding days without administration. Knots for the spline functions were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the dose distribution, as per standard recommendations. Models were adjusted for demographic characteristics, comorbidities, baseline severity scores, laboratory parameters, and major therapeutic interventions. Subgroup analyses were performed to further test the robustness of our findings. These analyses were prespecified a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature, focusing on demographic characteristics, baseline disease severity and comorbidities, and key therapeutic interventions, as these factors are well-established predictors of outcomes in critically ill cerebrovascular populations and potential effect modifiers. To mitigate the risk of overfitting, each interaction term was evaluated separately using likelihood ratio tests. Potential effect modification was assessed by adding interaction terms between thiamine use and key covariates, which were pre-selected based on clinical importance and previous literature. In the fully adjusted model, each interaction term was added separately, and statistical significance was assessed by comparing models with and without the interaction term using likelihood ratio tests. The statistical significance of interaction terms was evaluated using the P for interaction. However, only those with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and interpreted as evidence of effect modification. Investigations into the sensitivity of the correlation between thiamine use and short-term mortality after conducting propensity score matching. Two approaches were performed: (1) additional adjustment for sepsis, cerebral edema, and craniotomy; and (2) exclusion of patients with obvious clinical indications for thiamine supplementation, including alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, metabolic acidosis, and dialysis/CRRT, with covariate adjustment for demographic characteristics, comorbidities, severity scores, laboratory parameters, and major therapeutic interventions. To evaluate interaction effects between thiamine use and stratification variables, likelihood ratio tests were employed. R (version 4.3.3, released on February 29, 2024) and Zstats (version 1.0; www.zstats.net) were utilized for processing data and conducting statistical analyses. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Outline of patient demographic data and clinical traits

Before applying PSM, the demographic and clinical characteristics of the thiamine group differed significantly from those of the non-thiamine group (Table 1). In the thiamine group, patients were generally younger, with a median age of 65.95 years versus 70.93 years, and there was a higher percentage of males, 63.57% compared to 50.00%. Additionally, they showed higher median heart rate (103vs.97 beats/min), diastolic blood pressure (93vs.90 mmHg), respiratory rate (27vs.26 breaths/min), temperature (37.39vs.37.22 °C), SOFA score (5vs.3), SAPSII (36vs.33), and OASIS score (34vs.30), with all p-values < 0.001. The thiamine group exhibited notable rises in lab measurements, with PT at 14.10 s compared to 13.40 s and PTT at 31.80 s compared to 30.88 s. Furthermore, there was a higher rate of moderate to severe liver disease (7.65% vs. 1.16%) and chronic pulmonary disease (22.53% vs. 19.57%) in the thiamine group, along with significant differences in hypertension (47.66% vs. 51.92%) and heart failure (25.38% vs. 22.46%). After PSM, the baseline traits of the two groups were evenly matched: age, gender distribution, weight, vital signs, and severity scores (SOFA, SAPSII, OASIS) were alike, with all p-values greater than 0.05 and standardized mean differences (SMDs) close to zero (Table 2). Balanced comorbidities and lab parameters, including PT and PTT, between the groups indicate that the matched cohorts were suitable for further analysis of outcomes (Table 2).

Characteristics of patients at baseline after PSM

Abbreviation: SOFA sequential organ failure assessment, GCS glasgow coma scale, CCI charlson comorbidity index, SAPSII simplified acute physiology score II, OASIS oxford acute severity of illness score, PT prothrombin time, PTT partial thromboplastin time, CKD chronic kidney disease, HR heart rate, SBP systolic blood pressure, DBP diastolic blood pressure, MBP mean blood pressure, RR respiratory rate, T temperature, SPO2 oxygen saturation, CRRT continuous renal replacement therapy, PSM propensity score matching

By employing PSM with a caliper of 0.1, we minimized baseline imbalances and generated 1,585 well-matched pairs of patients with cerebrovascular disease. In Supplementary Fig. 1, the kernel density plots display the propensity score distributions for those using thiamine and those not using it, before and after matching. The left panel presents the distributions before matching, where the curves have minimal overlap, suggesting notable baseline heterogeneity between the two groups. The right panel depicts the distributions after matching, where the curves exhibit almost total overlap, indicating that the PSM approach effectively minimized baseline variability. This visual enhancement in overlap serves as direct evidence that the matched groups became more comparable, thereby bolstering the validity of future outcome analyses. Supplementary Fig. 2 provides additional analysis of covariate balance through SMDs. Before the matching process, a number of covariates were above the usual imbalance threshold of 0.1, hinting at possible confounding. Following matching, almost all covariates were under this limit, with SMDs nearing zero for demographic, physiological, and severity-related factors. Achieving this balance is important in observational studies, as it signifies that the two groups are statistically comparable in terms of measured confounders. Supplementary Fig. 1 looks at the overall balance in distribution, while Supplementary Fig. 2 highlights the balance for specific variables. Taken together, these figures demonstrate that the PSM procedure successfully reduced systematic differences between treatment groups. This balance strengthens the internal validity of the analysis by ensuring that the subsequent associations between thiamine use and short-term mortality are less likely to be explained by baseline disparities.

Baseline characteristics were compared between patients who received thiamine for less than 3 days (n = 215) and those treated for 3 days or longer (n = 1,370). Patients in the ≥ 3 days group were slightly younger (median age 65.82vs.68.99 years, P = 0.016) and had lower severity scores at admission (Supplementary Table 5). Specifically, they showed lower SOFA scores (median 4.00vs.5.00, P = 0.003), SAPSII scores (median 35.50 vs. 38.00, P = 0.009), and OASIS scores (median 33.00 vs. 34.00, P = 0.032). No significant differences were observed between groups in terms of gender distribution, oxygen saturation, Glasgow Coma Scale, or Charlson comorbidity index.

We further explored whether patients with more severe illnesses were preferentially given larger doses of thiamine (Supplementary Table 6). Among ICU patients, baseline comparisons showed that those receiving high-dose thiamine (> 300 mg/day) had significantly higher SOFA (median 5.0vs.4.0, P < 0.001), SAPSII (median 40.0vs.36.0, P = 0.039), and OASIS scores (median 35.0vs.32.0, P < 0.001) compared with the low-dose group (< 100 mg/day). No significant differences were observed across dose groups in age, gender distribution, GCS, or CCI.

Relationship between thiamine use and primary outcomes

In the unmatched cohort, those in the thiamine group experienced a 7-day mortality rate of 6.62%, whereas it was 7.83% in the non-thiamine group (Table 3). Once PSM was applied, the rates were 6.56% and 8.77%, respectively. Univariate Cox regression analysis was utilized to investigate the correlation between thiamine use and mortality within 7 days (Table 3). To analyze the independent association of thiamine administration with 7-day mortality, multivariate Cox regression models were formulated, adjusting for relevant clinical factors. Refer to Table 4 for the hazard ratios (HR) and the 95% confidence intervals (CI). In both the pre-PSM and post-PSM cohorts, thiamine administration was significantly correlated with a decrease in 7-day mortality (pre-PSM: HR = 0.72, 95%CI: 0.58–0.90, P = 0.004; post-PSM: HR = 0.68, 95%CI: 0.53–0.89, P = 0.004) (Model 3). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that patients receiving thiamine had a significantly higher 7-day survival rate compared to those not receiving it, in both unmatched and PSM cohorts (Fig. 2).

Relationship between thiamine use and secondary outcomes

Within the unmatched cohort, the mortality rate at 14 days was 12.39% for the thiamine group and 10.67% for the non-thiamine group, whereas at 28 days, it was 16.88% and 12.13%, respectively (Table 3). According to Table 3, following PSM, thiamine users had a 14-day mortality rate of 12.05%, compared to 13.56% for non-users. The 28-day mortality rates were 16.34% for thiamine users and 16.15% for non-users. To evaluate the link between thiamine administration and mortality at 14 and 28 days, univariate Cox regression analyses were carried out (Table 3). Subsequently, multivariate Cox regression models were formulated to analyze the independent association of thiamine administration with these secondary outcomes (Table 4). Thiamine administration was significantly correlated with a decreased 14-day mortality risk, evident both before PSM (HR = 0.83, 95%CI: 0.71–0.98, P = 0.027) and after PSM (HR = 0.78, 95%CI: 0.64–0.96, P = 0.016) (Model 3). Regarding 28-day mortality, thiamine administration remained significantly associated with lower risk both before PSM (HR = 0.88, 95%CI: 0.76–1.01, P = 0.074) and after PSM (HR = 0.82, 95%CI: 0.69–0.98, P = 0.028) (Model 3). Multivariate-adjusted models confirmed the stability of these associations, with HR often under 1, although the association with 28-day mortality was marginal in Model 3 before PSM (HR = 0.88, 95%CI: 0.76–1.01, P = 0.074). In general, these results indicate that giving thiamine is linked to better short-term survival rates in patients with cerebrovascular disease. Nonlinear dose-response association of thiamine use with 7-, 14-, and 28-day mortality

Restricted cubic spline models indicated significant nonlinear associations between the dose of thiamine and mortality rates at 7, 14, and 28 days (all P for overall and nonlinearity < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. 3A-3 C). The lowest mortality risk occurred at dose levels beneath around 50 mg/day, with hazard ratios considerably below 1. The risk of mortality rose progressively as the dose increased, reaching and surpassing 1 at moderate doses (around 50–100 mg/day), indicating a loss of protective effect. At doses exceeding 100 mg/day, the hazard ratios remained above 1. With increasing dose, the confidence intervals widened, particularly at the extremes, reflecting greater uncertainty. This dose-response pattern was consistent across all three follow-up time points. The lower-dose advantage likely reflects confounding by indication rather than biology; residual confounding may still be present even after covariate adjustment.

Multivariable-adjusted analyses of duration-response and dose-response relationships between thiamine use and outcomes

Duration-response analysis revealed that compared to patients who did not receive thiamine, those receiving thiamine for more than 5 days had significantly lower risks of 7-day, 14-day, and 28-day mortality (HR = 0.25, 95%CI: 0.15–0.41, P < 0.001; HR = 0.44, 95%CI: 0.33–0.59, P < 0.001; HR = 0.49, 95%CI: 0.38–0.62, P < 0.001, respectively) (Table 5). In contrast, short-term thiamine administration (< 3 days) was associated with increased risks of 7-day, 14-day, and 28-day mortality (HR > 1.7, all P < 0.001) (Table 5). No significant mortality difference was observed between patients treated with thiamine for 3–5 days and non-users (Table 5).

According to dose-response analysis, patients administered thiamine doses of less than 100 mg per day showed significantly lower mortality rates at 7, 14, and 28 days compared to non-users (HR = 0.52, 95%CI: 0.38–0.71; HR = 0.60, 95%CI: 0.47–0.75; HR = 0.63, 95%CI: 0.52–0.78; all P < 0.001) (Table 5). However, higher doses (100–300 mg/day and > 300 mg/day) were associated with either no significant benefit or even increased risks, with HR > 1 in several outcomes, particularly in the > 300 mg/day group (e.g., HR = 1.65, 95%CI: 1.18–2.32, P = 0.004 for 28-day mortality) (Table 5). The study’s findings reveal that both the length of treatment and the dosage of thiamine significantly affected clinical outcomes in cerebrovascular disease patients, with longer therapy durations and lower doses being most consistently associated with survival.

Subgroup analysis

According to subgroup analyses, the effect of thiamine administration on lowering mortality varied with different clinical and demographic characteristics (Supplementary Table 7). Notably, thiamine use was significantly associated with a lower risk of mortality among patients aged ≥ 65 years (HR = 0.59, 95%CI: 0.42–0.83, P = 0.003), while no significant association was observed in those aged < 65 years (HR = 0.88, 95%CI: 0.57–1.37, P = 0.581). A stronger protective effect was observed in female patients (HR = 0.51, 95%CI: 0.32–0.80, P = 0.004) compared to males (HR = 0.78, 95%CI: 0.56–1.07, P = 0.127). Thiamine administration was also associated with reduced mortality in patients without mannitol use (HR = 0.58, 95%CI: 0.43–0.78, P < 0.001) and in those not undergoing thrombolysis (HR = 0.64, 95%CI: 0.48–0.84, P = 0.001). Protective associations were further noted in patients receiving diuretics (HR = 0.62, 95%CI: 0.42–0.91, P = 0.016), antiplatelet agents (HR = 0.54, 95%CI: 0.34–0.85, P = 0.008), or anticoagulant therapy (HR = 0.72, 95%CI: 0.54–0.97, P = 0.029). In addition, a marked reduction in mortality was seen in individuals with hypertension (HR = 0.52, 95%CI: 0.34–0.80, P = 0.003), atrial fibrillation (HR = 0.52, 95%CI: 0.32–0.83, P = 0.007), heart failure (HR = 0.54, 95%CI: 0.32–0.91, P = 0.021), and moderate to severe liver disease (HR = 0.68, 95%CI: 0.52–0.90, P = 0.006).

The study analyzed interactions to see if the effect of thiamine on mortality was significantly different in various subgroups. The use of mannitol (P = 0.017), thrombolysis status (P = 0.026), and mechanical ventilation (P = 0.006) all showed statistically significant interactions. Thiamine’s protective effect was particularly lessened in patients treated with mannitol or thrombolysis and among those who were not on mechanical ventilation. No major interactions were identified concerning age, gender, diuretic use, anticoagulant therapy, or other health issues. These results emphasize the possibility of effect modification through particular clinical interventions and stress the significance of personalized treatment approaches. Important clinical insights are gained from the interaction of thiamine with other therapies.

Sensitivity analysis

For further confirmation of our findings’ robustness, we executed sensitivity analyses with added adjustments for sepsis, cerebral edema, and craniectomy/craniotomy. The results remained consistent with the primary analyses (Supplementary Table 8). Thiamine use was significantly associated with lower risks of mortality at 7 days (HR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.51–0.88, P = 0.003), 14 days (HR = 0.76, 95%CI: 0.62–0.93, P = 0.009), and 28 days (HR = 0.80, 95%CI: 0.67–0.96, P = 0.015). The evidence indicates that the association between thiamine and reduced short-term mortality persisted despite additional adjustments for crucial clinical factors. To deal with the potential confounding influence of conditions that are obvious signs for thiamine supplementation, like alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, metabolic acidosis, and dialysis/CRRT, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding these patients (Supplementary Table 9). The relationship between thiamine consumption and mortality persisted, albeit with slightly weakened effect estimates relative to the main analysis. Thiamine administration remained significantly associated with reduced short-term mortality, including 7-day mortality (HR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.44–1.02, P = 0.039), 14-day mortality (HR = 0.68, 95%CI: 0.49–0.93, P = 0.017), and 28-day mortality (HR = 0.70, 95%CI: 0.52–0.92, P = 0.012). These findings suggest that the observed survival benefit of thiamine was not solely attributable to patients with conventional indications, thereby reinforcing the robustness of the association.

Discussion

The study, which involved a large retrospective analysis using PSM, showed that giving thiamine to critically ill patients with cerebrovascular disease was consistently associated with a significant decrease in short-term mortality at 7, 14, and 28 days. Remarkably, the protective role of thiamine continued across multiple subgroup analyses and was particularly clear in patients aged 65 and over, female patients, and those without mannitol or thrombolysis treatment. Subgroup and interaction analyses further underscored the robustness of this association, highlighting the potential benefits of thiamine therapy in specific patient subpopulations. Moreover, the duration-response and dose-response evaluations suggested that treatment courses lasting over 5 days and daily doses less than 100 mg/day were most consistently related to improved results. The evidence from our study points to thiamine potentially offering survival benefits to specific high-risk groups of patients with critical cerebrovascular disorders. Considering the study’s observational design and the chance of residual confounding, these outcomes should be viewed as hypothesis-generating instead of definitive proof. In addition, sensitivity analyses suggested that our findings were generally stable. The association between thiamine use and short-term mortality remained after additional adjustment for sepsis, cerebral edema, and craniotomy, indicating that these factors had only a limited impact on the results. Furthermore, when excluding patients with potential indications for thiamine supplementation, such as alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, metabolic acidosis, and dialysis/CRRT, the associations were largely unchanged. While these further analyses reinforce the robustness of our conclusions, residual confounding cannot be entirely dismissed. Future multicenter prospective cohorts and randomized controlled trials are required to establish if thiamine supplementation can be recommended as a clinical intervention for this population.

In routine ICU practice, thiamine is not administered indiscriminately but is typically prescribed to patients with clinical features suggestive of increased risk for deficiency or heightened metabolic stress. Common indications include nutritional vulnerability such as low body mass index, hypoalbuminemia, prolonged fasting or initiation of enteral or parenteral feeding to prevent refeeding syndrome, as well as metabolic derangements like lactic acidosis or septic shock, particularly when lactate levels are markedly elevated or vasopressor support is required [8, 13, 24, 31, 32]. Thiamine is also frequently provided prophylactically in patients undergoing continuous renal replacement therapy due to accelerated vitamin clearanceand empirically for neurological concerns such as altered mental status, seizures or suspected Wernicke’s encephalopathy, which is commonly associated with alcohol use disorder or other predisposing conditions [14, 32–36]. These prescribing patterns reflect standard ICU practice and indicate that patients receiving thiamine are often more severely ill, which introduces the potential for confounding by indication in observational analyses. Although our propensity score matching and multivariable adjustments accounted for numerous severity indices and comorbidities, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors such as baseline thiamine levels or physician judgment cannot be fully excluded.

To date, relatively few studies have directly examined the relationship between thiamine and cerebrovascular disease. Prior investigations have largely addressed thiamine deficiency in sepsis, heart failure, or critical illness, with very limited findings specific to cerebrovascular disease. This scarcity of literature highlights the novelty of our study. Recently, a comprehensive cross-sectional study of NHANES data employing machine learning methods identified an inverse link between dietary vitamin B1 consumption and stroke risk, indicating population-level evidence of a protective benefit [37]. Our findings, which indicate a reduction in short-term mortality among critically ill cerebrovascular patients, are consistent with new observations and add to the knowledge by placing thiamine’s benefits in the context of cerebrovascular care. Building on this, our study’s observed mortality reduction also aligns with earlier reports of thiamine’s role in critical illness. In cases of septic shock, administering thiamine improved lactate clearance and reduced mortality [7], and it was also associated with lower in-hospital mortality for traumatic brain injury patients [11]. In contrast to sepsis studies where higher doses (200–500 mg/day) were beneficial, our findings suggest that different dosing strategies might be necessary for cerebrovascular disease [8]. The improved effectiveness in older adults corresponds with findings in heart failure and myocardial infarction populations, which might be due to a greater prevalence of thiamine deficiency or metabolic sensitivity in older individuals [38, 39]. We found a sex-specific response, with women showing stronger protection, which has not been reported before and requires examination of potential hormonal or pharmacokinetic influences. Mannitol’s diminished benefit could be attributed to thiamine depletion from osmotic diuresis or competition at cellular transporters [40]. Similarly, the lower efficacy of thrombolysis could suggest that reperfusion injury mechanisms take precedence over the metabolic benefits of thiamine [6, 41]. Our duration-response and dose-response evaluations also demonstrated that thiamine administration for over 5 days and at lower daily doses (less than 100 mg/day) were consistently connected with improved results. This observation is in agreement with current literature, which proposes that sustained, moderate doses of thiamine may offer more benefits compared to high doses taken over a short period. For example, Donnino et al. demonstrated in a randomized controlled trial that continuous thiamine administration over several days improved lactate clearance and clinical outcomes in patients with septic shock, underscoring the importance of adequate duration in metabolic support therapies [42, 43]. Therefore, our conclusions stress the importance of optimizing thiamine therapy strategies, recommending lower doses over prolonged periods to maximize therapeutic benefits while reducing potential adverse effects. Subgroup analyses by the duration of thiamine supplementation revealed that mortality was higher in patients receiving the supplement for fewer than three days compared to those with extended treatment. The pattern is unlikely to be a direct harmful effect of thiamine; rather, it is more plausibly due to confounding by indication and the severity of the underlying illness. In critical care settings, short treatment durations are typical for patients with fast-moving diseases, where death or treatment withdrawal occurs before a longer treatment can be completed. In line with our results, patients receiving thiamine for fewer than three days demonstrated markedly higher baseline severity scores (SOFA, SAPS II, OASIS) and required more intensive interventions, indicating that the apparent association is more likely a reflection of critical underlying illness rather than a causal effect of treatment duration. Despite adjusting for severity indices and comorbidities, our models cannot completely rule out residual confounding. Prospective future research with more specific data on when treatments are administered and the causes of mortality is needed to determine whether the duration of thiamine treatment independently impacts patient outcomes.

In addition to thiamine’s direct metabolic functions, several other possible explanations should be taken into account. In ICU settings, thiamine is frequently administered to patients believed to be at increased risk of deficiency or metabolic stress, which could result in confounding by indication. Residual confounding could still be present, even though we applied PSM and comprehensive covariate adjustments. Our subgroup analyses indicate that concurrent treatments like mannitol, thrombolysis, or mechanical ventilation may affect thiamine’s impact. Unmeasured elements, including initial nutritional status, vitamin levels, or variations in institutional care practices, could also affect prognosis. These aspects point out that while our findings imply a potential survival benefit of thiamine, they should be interpreted with care, and more prospective or randomized studies are required to confirm causality.

There are multiple complex mechanisms by which thiamine administration benefits short-term prognosis in patients with critical cerebrovascular conditions. Severe cerebrovascular events, like ischemic or hemorrhagic strokes, often lead to significant metabolic issues, such as mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and disrupted glucose metabolism [44–48]. Playing a key role in aerobic glucose metabolism and adenosine triphosphate synthesis, thiamine acts as a cofactor for significant mitochondrial enzymes like pyruvate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase [49, 50]. Its deficiency can exacerbate anaerobic glycolysis, leading to lactate accumulation and further energy failure, thereby intensifying neuronal injury and contributing to poor outcomes [8, 10, 50, 51]. The absence of thiamine has been shown to affect the blood-brain barrier’s integrity, increasing its permeability to plasma components, which in turn leads to brain swelling and inflammatory responses that intensify neuronal harm [52]. The lack of thiamine has been associated with brain dysfunction because it plays vital roles in synthesizing myelin and major neurotransmitters such as acetylcholine, gamma-aminobutyric acid, and glutamate [53]. Deficiency in these pathways leads to increased oxidative stress by reducing the availability of cellular reducing agents like nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, thus affecting neuronal integrity [53, 54]. Thiamine supplementation might exert protective effects on the nervous system by restoring energy metabolism, alleviating oxidative stress, and reducing inflammation, which could lead to improved short-term outcomes in patients with critical cerebrovascular disease.

This study, despite its important findings, has several limitations that need to be considered. First, the retrospective design inherently includes the risk of unmeasured confounding variables, such as nutritional status or baseline thiamine levels, which were missing from the MIMIC-IV database. Second, the research was based on data from a single-center database, which might restrict the applicability of our results. Third, the absence of comprehensive details on the timing of thiamine administration, adherence, and any adverse effects could influence how the outcomes are understood. Fourth, the database included information on significant interventions such as mechanical ventilation and anticoagulant therapy, yet it lacked detailed quantification of other factors that might influence the outcomes. Fifth, due to the absence of detailed information on endovascular interventions, including those for ischemic stroke, in the MIMIC-IV database, we were unable to analyze their potential impact on patient outcomes. Sixth, the potential confounding by indication is a limitation, as thiamine was administered based on clinical judgment, usually to patients at increased risk of deficiency or metabolic stress. Hence, these results should be treated with caution, and prospective randomized studies are needed to validate causality. Seventh, although the subgroup analyses were prespecified and evaluated using interaction tests, the possibility of overfitting and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded, particularly in subgroups with relatively few events. These results should therefore be interpreted with caution and regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Eighth, the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) was not available in the MIMIC-IV database. Although NIHSS is a useful and widely applied measure of stroke severity, its absence meant that we could not include this variable in our analyses. We have acknowledged this as a limitation of the study. Ninth, the MIMIC-IV database’s inconsistent documentation of outcomes hinders a complete assessment of competing risks, including early discharge, care withdrawal, or inter-facility transfers. Subtle differences in therapeutic practices or ward-level care, like nursing quality, nutritional support, and rehabilitation services, may still result in residual confounding. To ensure the reliability of our results, future studies should adopt multicenter, prospective cohort designs or randomized controlled trials. Future investigations ought to integrate biomarker assessments, such as measuring thiamine pyrophosphate levels, to classify patients by deficiency status and examine long-term neurological outcomes. In addition, investigations into the interaction of thiamine with other treatments, such as mannitol or thrombolytics, and its sex-specific impacts are essential for optimizing individualized treatment strategies.

Conclusions

The comprehensive retrospective study revealed a relationship between thiamine administration and a decrease in short-term mortality in critically ill patients with cerebrovascular disease. Crucially, in our analysis, treatments lasting more than five days and daily doses below 100 mg were consistently associated with superior survival outcomes. These findings indicate a potential role for thiamine in metabolic support therapy, though the study’s observational design means causality cannot be established. To validate these associations and consider the inclusion of thiamine in standard clinical practice, additional randomized controlled trials are required.
