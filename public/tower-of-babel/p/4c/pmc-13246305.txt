LncRNA Gm16023 limits M1 macrophage-mediated hyperglycemia-exacerbated septic intestinal injury via miR-377-3p/Sirt1 axis
Authors: Fang Tan, Lei Zheng, Dongdong Yuan, Shuang Wang, Weifeng Yao, Tao Wang, Bin Xia, Ningning Li, Zhang Fu, Tufeng Chen, Xinjin Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes is closely associated with inflammation and sepsis, but its clinical significance and underlying mechanisms remain obscure. Leveraging epidemiological data from 404,184 individuals in the UK Biobank cohort, we found that hyperglycemia was significantly associated with increased sepsis risk. Phenotypically, hyperglycemia enhanced macrophage infiltration and exacerbated intestinal inflammation in both septic patients and murine models. Using both a genetic CD11b-DTR model and clodronate liposome-mediated chemical ablation, we demonstrated that macrophage depletion markedly attenuated hyperglycemia-driven septic intestinal injury, underscoring their essential pathogenic role. Mechanistically, RNA sequencing analysis identified that underexpressed lncRNA Gm16023 acted as a ceRNA, binding miR-377-3p to regulate Sirt1 expression and inhibit M1 macrophage polarization. To facilitate therapeutic delivery, we engineered a lipid nanoparticle-encapsulated Gm16023 plasmid system that efficiently delivered lncRNA in vivo and conferred protection against intestinal injury in both in vitro and in vivo models. Collectively, our findings indicate that hyperglycemia promotes macrophage-mediated septic intestinal injury via the lncRNA Gm16023/miR-377-3p/Sirt1 axis, highlighting a potential RNA-based therapeutic strategy for diabetic sepsis.Keywords: hyperglycemia, intestinal injury, sepsis, macrophages, lncRNA Gm16023

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening disease characterized by multiple organ dysfunction and uncontrolled inflammatory responses.1 Intestinal barrier disruption and bacteria translocation are considered as the driving forces of sepsis.2 Epidemiological evidence indicates that diabetes is a multi-factorial risk factor for increasing morbidity and mortality in critically ill sepsis patients,3 primarily as a result of hyperglycemia.4 Nonetheless, the specific impact of hyperglycemia on sepsis-related intestinal injury as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Hyperglycemia, pathologically exaggerated glucose metabolism changes, has been shown to potentiate the intestinal injury through multiple pathogenetic mechanisms. Hyperglycemia drives intestinal barrier permeability, through GLUT2-dependent transcriptional reprogramming of intestinal epithelial cells and alteration of tight and adherence junction integrity. Consequently, hyperglycemia-mediated barrier disruption leads to systemic influx of microbial products and enhanced dissemination of enteric infection.5 Permanent hyperglycemia has been proven to alter the activities of intestinal conjugative and possibly the oxidative enzymes, thus forming a change in the metabolic pattern and eventually provoking oxidative stress.6 While immune cell dysfunction is the core link of pathological mechanism of hyperglycemia,7 its specific damage mechanism during septic intestinal injury remains to be fully elucidated.

Macrophages are the key guardians of intestinal immune homeostasis and play a crucial role in intestinal injury.8 Naive macrophages (M0) can polarize into two distinct phenotypes, the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype or the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype, under various pathological conditions.9 It is revealed that high-level DCA induced by high-fat diet (HFD) serves as an initiator to activate M1 macrophage polarization and pro-inflammatory cytokine production and drive colonic inflammation.10 A mechanistic study showed that ECM1 can regulate M1 macrophage polarization through the granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor/STAT5 signaling pathway causing intestinal inflammation and epithelial injury.9 Dietary phenolic acids alleviate intestinal inflammation by reducing M1 macrophage polarization through suppression of pyruvate kinase M 2 (Pkm2)-dependent glycolysis and inhibition of NOD-like receptor protein 3 (Nlrp3) activation.11 While it has been shown that persistent M1 macrophage activation can result in intestinal tissue damage, making regulation of the M1/M2 balance crucial for preventing excessive inflammation in sepsis.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are widely involved in pathophysiological processes, including the modulation of macrophage-mediated immune response.12 An illustrative instance is the lncRNA PTPRE-AS1, which has been demonstrated to modulate M2 macrophage activation and inflammatory conditions by epigenetically promoting protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type E.13 In the context of viral myocarditis, it has been observed that the expression of lncRNA AK085865 is upregulated and that it plays a role in promoting M2 macrophage polarization by regulating the biogenesis of interleukin enhancer-binding factor 2-interleukin enhancer-binding factor 3 complex-mediated microRNA (miRNA)-192.14 LncRNAs are also critical regulatory players during the manifestation of diabetes by modulating pathways involved in cell death, proliferation, inflammation, and fibrosis.15 LncRNA AQP4-AS1 was reported to selectively bind AQP4 mRNA in vivo and regulates AQP4 transcript levels. The downregulation of AQP4 can accelerate the progression of diabetic complications by intensifying the inflammatory response.16 In addition, lncRNAs have been implicated in the development of acute inflammation and chronic inflammatory diseases.17,18 The gut microbiota reprograms intestinal lipid metabolism through lncRNA Snhg9.19 Deficiency of lncRNA HOXA11os in colonic myeloid cells results in complex I deficiency, dysfunctional oxidative phosphorylation, and the production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), developing spontaneous intestinal inflammation.20 To date, many thousands of lncRNAs have been annotated in the human genome. However, the majority of these remain uncharacterized.

Despite thousands of annotated lncRNAs, their specific roles in the intersection of hyperglycemia, sepsis, and intestinal injury remain largely uncharacterized. Accumulating evidence suggests that high-glucose levels increase the risk of infection and are associated with a worse prognosis at sepsis onset.21,22 Therefore, the current study aimed to specifically investigate the impact of hyperglycemia on sepsis-induced intestinal injury and explore the underlying molecular mechanisms. First, large-scale cohort analysis (UK Biobank) confirmed that diabetes and hyperglycemia significantly increase the risk of sepsis. Then, we observed that hyperglycemia could promote sepsis-induced intestinal injury in both septic patients and murine models. Macrophages exhibited a crucial role in this process. Notably, the lncRNA Gm16023 acts as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA), binding miR-377-3p to regulate Sirt1 and inhibit M1 macrophage polarization. In vivo, lncRNA Gm16023 overexpression alleviates intestinal injury and improves survival outcomes in sepsis. Our work highlights the lncRNA Gm16023/miR-377-3p/Sirt1 axis as a promising therapeutic target for diabetic sepsis.

Results

Hyperglycemia aggravates intestinal damage and macrophage infiltration in septic mice

To explore the impact of blood glucose on sepsis, we first performed an epidemiological investigation between blood glucose and sepsis incidence using the UK Biobank cohort. Cox regression analysis of 404,184 individuals demonstrated that type 2 diabetes and abnormal blood glucose are significant risk factors for sepsis (Figures 1A–1C and S1A–S1D). Moreover, clinical specimens from diabetic and septic patients revealed that hyperglycemia exacerbates septic intestinal injury (Figures 1D and 1E).

To further assess the in vivo effect of hyperglycemia on sepsis, we employed the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) technique in a sepsis mouse model, incorporating both wild-type C57BL/KsJ mice (designated wt-CLP) and a diabetic model of C57BL/KsJ db/db mice (designated db/db-CLP). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed more severe intestinal tissue damage in db/db-CLP mice compared with wt-CLP mice (Figure 2A). Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining revealed a pronounced decrease in PAS-positive goblet cells, indicating compromised mucosal integrity in the db/db-CLP group (Figure 2B). Additionally, protein levels of tight junction markers occludin and zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) were significantly lower in db/db-CLP mice (Figure 2C). Consistent with these findings, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis revealed a notable increase in the expression of inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor α [TNF-α], interleukin-1β [IL-1β], and IL-6) in the intestinal tissues of db/db-CLP mice compared with controls (Figure 2D). Importantly, these pathological alterations were fully recapitulated in female db/db-CLP mice (Figures S2A–S2E). Furthermore, to rule out the confounding effects of leptin deficiency inherent to the db/db background, we validated our findings in a non-genetic model induced by a HFD and streptozotocin (HFD/STZ). Consistent with the genetic model, HFD/STZ mice displayed exacerbated intestinal barrier disruption (Figures S2F and S2G), elevated inflammatory cytokines, and pronounced M1 macrophage polarization (Figure S2H and S2I). These data collectively confirm that hyperglycemia, rather than sex or specific genetic traits, is the primary driver of the observed immunopathology.

Macrophages are key contributors to intestinal injury during sepsis. Immunofluorescence analysis showed a pronounced increase in F4/80+ macrophage infiltration in db/db-CLP mice (Figure 2E), accompanied by a significant rise in CD86-positive M1-like macrophages. A similar trend was observed in clinical specimens (Figures 1F and 1G). Collectively, these results indicate that hyperglycemia intensifies septic intestinal injury by promoting inflammatory cytokine expression and enhancing macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization under diabetic conditions.

Macrophage depletion mitigates septic intestinal injury in diabetic mice

To evaluate the role of macrophage infiltration on septic intestinal injury under diabetic conditions, we employed two complementary mouse models: C57BL/KsJ db/db mice treated with clodronate liposomes (Cls) for chemical depletion of macrophages, and CD11b-DTR diabetic mice in which diphtheria toxin (DT) was used to induce macrophage-specific ablation, followed by CLP to induce sepsis (Figure 3A). Crucially, flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood at sacrifice confirmed the sustained depletion of circulating CD11b+ monocytes, effectively ruling out rapid bone marrow repopulation (Figure S3A). Consistent with this systemic depletion, immunofluorescence staining confirmed a substantial reduction in the expression of the macrophage marker F4/80+ following both Cls and DT treatments (Figure S3B). H&E staining revealed that intestinal injury scores were significantly lower in the macrophage-depleted CLP groups in both mouse models (Figures 3B and 3C). Consistent with the histological findings, immunoblot analysis showed that depletion of macrophages markedly restored the expression of tight junction proteins occludin and ZO-1 (Figure 3D). Furthermore, depletion of macrophages led to a pronounced decrease in the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, in line with the observed improvement in intestinal injury (Figures 3E and 3F). These findings underscore the pivotal role of macrophages in mediating intestinal damage during sepsis under diabetic conditions and we next focused on elucidating the potential mechanism underlying the effect of hyperglycemia on macrophage.

Hyperglycemia promotes M1 polarization and septic intestinal injury by downregulating lncRNA Gm16023 expression in macrophages

Recently, although lncRNAs have been implicated in sepsis-induced intestinal injury, their roles under hyperglycemic conditions are poorly characterized. To identify key lncRNAs involved in the progression of diabetes and sepsis, we conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on sepsis-induced intestinal tissue from diabetic mice and wild-type (WT) mice (Figure S4A). The results revealed 812 downregulated and 2,924 upregulated lncRNAs in tissue samples (db/db-sham vs. db/db-CLP |logFC | <0.8, p < 0.05), and 1,377 downregulated and 2,531 upregulated lncRNAs in tissue samples (wt-CLP vs. db/db-CLP) (Figure S4B); the chromosomal locations of the differentially expressed lncRNAs are depicted in Figure S4C. In this study, our focus was on investigating downregulated lncRNAs. Through our analysis, we identified a total of 347 downregulated lncRNAs (Figure 4A). From the identified downregulated lncRNAs, we annotated the top 25 lncRNAs in expression levels and predicted their functions by blast2GO software. Two lncRNAs (NONMMUG031589.2 and NONMMUG006964.2) were associated with inflammatory pathways (Figure 4B). Consequently, we selected NONMMUG031589.2 (lncRNA Gm16023), which exhibited a more pronounced downregulation (log2FC = −2.04) compared with NONMMUG006964.2 (log2FC = −1.37), for further investigation. The expression of lncRNA Gm16023 in intestinal tissue was consistent with the result of RNA-seq analysis (Figure 4C). Subsequently, we assessed the expression of lncRNA Gm16023 in intestinal epithelial cells, fibroblasts, B cells, T cells, and macrophages. The results showed that lncRNA Gm16023 expression was highest in macrophages (Figures 4D, 4E, and S4D).

To assess the functional consequences of macrophage-derived lncRNA Gm16023 on septic intestinal injury, we established a transwell coculture system (Figure 4F). Overexpression of lncRNA Gm16023 in macrophages led to enhanced expression of barrier proteins occludin and ZO-1 (Figure 4G), reduced epithelial apoptosis (Figures 4H and S5A), and lowered reactive oxygen species (Figures 4I and S5B), suggesting that lncRNA Gm16023 alleviates intestinal injury in vitro.

To explore how hyperglycemia influences lncRNA Gm16023 expression in macrophages, we conducted qRT-PCR analysis on macrophages activated with high glucose (HG), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), or both (HG + LPS). The findings indicated a notable reduction in the expression of lncRNA Gm16023 in the HG + LPS group compared with LPS alone (Figure 4J). Furthermore, correlation analysis demonstrated a negative association between the expression of lncRNA Gm16023 and the M1 macrophage marker inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (Figures S5C and S5D). To further confirm the relationship between lncRNA Gm16023 and macrophage polarization, we generated an overexpression plasmid as well as three short interference RNAs (siRNAs). The efficacy of lncRNA Gm16023 overexpression and knockdown was validated by RT-qPCR (Figures S5E and S5F). As anticipated, the upregulation of lncRNA Gm16023 resulted in a notable reduction in the expression of M1 macrophage markers (iNOS and CCR7) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) (Figures 4K and 4L). Conversely, knockdown of lncRNA Gm16023 increased M1 markers and cytokine levels and decreased M2 markers (Figures S5G and S5H), indicating that lncRNA Gm16023 suppresses M1 polarization. Collectively, these data confirm that hyperglycemia-induced downregulation of lncRNA Gm16023 in macrophages drives M1 polarization, thereby worsening septic intestinal injury.

LncRNA Gm16023 serves as an miR-377-3p sponge to inhibit M1 macrophage polarization

To elucidate the downstream mechanisms of lncRNA Gm16023, we first used the RNA-fold web server to predict the secondary structure of lncRNA Gm16023, and then the web tool at “cpc2.gao-lab.org/” to predict its coding potential. Interestingly, the results indicated that lncRNA Gm16023 possessed a minimal coding capability (Figure S6A). The subcellular localization of lncRNAs plays a crucial role in its biological function. Thus, we initially predicted the subcellular localization of lncRNA Gm16023 using lncLocator (csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/lncLocator/), and the results suggested predominant cytoplasmic distribution, which was further validated by cellular fractionation analysis (Figures 5A and 5B). Given the predominant cytoplasmic localization of lncRNA Gm16023, we hypothesized that it might function as a miRNA sponge, regulating the expression of downstream target genes via ceRNAs mechanisms.

To test this, we performed RNA-seq and identified 133 downregulated and 85 upregulated miRNAs (Figures 5C and S6B). By integrating these data with the lncRNA-Base database, we identified miR-377-3p and miR-665-3p as potential targets of lncRNA Gm16023 (Figure 5D). Subsequent analysis revealed a significant upregulation of miR-377-3p in both intestinal tissue and macrophages, whereas miR-665-3p was increased only in intestinal tissues (Figures 5E and 5F). Furthermore, correlation analysis indicated a negative correlation between the expression of lncRNA Gm16023 and miR-377-3p (Figure 5G). Next, we examined whether lncRNA Gm16023 regulates miR-377-3p expression. The lncRNA Gm16023 overexpression plasmid and siRNAs were transfected into macrophages. Notably, the overexpression of lncRNA Gm16023 significantly decreased miR-377-3p levels (Figure 5H), whereas knockdown of lncRNA Gm16023 led to a significant increase in the expression of miR-377-3p (Figure 5I).

To explore the regulatory role of miR-377-3p in macrophage polarization, we transfected RAW264.7 cells with a miR-377-3p mimic (Figure S6C). It was found that the upregulation of miR-377-3p resulted in significantly increased levels of the M1 markers (iNOS and CCR7) and markedly elevated TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels (Figure 5J). Conversely, transfection with miR-377-3p inhibitors suppressed M1 polarization and reduced inflammatory cytokine release (Figure 5K).

To investigate the potential binding between lncRNA Gm16023 and miR-377-3p, we utilized the JASPAR database to predict potential binding sites and then constructed luciferase reporter vectors containing either the wild-type (WT-luc) or mutated binding regions (MUT-luc). These reporters were co-transfected into 293T cells with a miR-377-3p mimic or control oligos, followed by a dual-luciferase reporter assay. The results showed that miR-377-3p mimic significantly suppressed luciferase activity in cells transfected with WT-luc constructs, but not in those with MUT-luc constructs (Figure 5L). Thus, our findings reveal that sequestering miR-377-3p via lncRNA Gm16023 is crucial for restraining pro-inflammatory M1 macrophage activation.

Sirt1 is a downstream target of miR-377-3p in regulating macrophage polarization

We next identified downstream targets of miR-377-3p by analyzing differentially expressed mRNAs from our RNA-seq dataset (Figures S7A and S7B). Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analyses indicated that the genes were enriched in the metabolic pathway (Figures S7C and S7D). By integrating mRNA sequencing data, metabolic pathway enrichment, and TargetScan predictions, we identified Ndufs6, Sirt1, Hdc, Atp5k, and Cers5 as potential targets of miR-377-3p (Figure 6A). Utilizing the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org) to assess target mRNA expression in macrophages, we found that Sirt1 exhibited the highest expression (Figure 6A) and was similarly upregulated in our intestinal tissue and macrophages (Figures 6B and 6C). Molecular docking predicted two potential binding conformations for miR-377-3p at the extracellular binding site of Sirt1 (Figure 6D). Luciferase reporter assays demonstrated the potential binding site between miR-377-3p and the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of Sirt1.23 Furthermore, western blot and qRT-PCR analyses revealed that transfection with a miR-377-3p mimic significantly decreased Sirt1 mRNA and protein levels compared with the negative control group, whereas inhibition of miR-377-3p produced the opposite effect (Figures 6E and 6F).

To examine whether Sirt1 modulates macrophage polarization, RAW264.7 cells were treated with the Sirt1 agonist SRT1720 or the inhibitor Ex527, and subsequent qRT-PCR and western blot analyses confirmed the efficacy (Figures S7E and S7F). Sirt1 activation caused significant suppression of M1 macrophage polarization and reduction in the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Figures 6G and 6H), whereas Sirt1 inhibition promoted M1 polarization and enhanced inflammatory cytokines release (Figures 6I and 6J).

To further confirm that Sirt1 is a downstream target of miR-377-3p in macrophage polarization, we conducted rescue experiments. Macrophages were co-treated with the Sirt1 agonist SRT1720 and a miR-377-3p mimic. The expression of M1 markers (iNOS and CCR7) and inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) indicated that the pro-M1 effects induced by miR-377-3p upregulation were effectively reversed by Sirt1 activation (Figures 6K and 6L). Collectively, these results confirm that Sirt1 functions as a downstream target of miR-377-3p in regulating macrophage polarization.

LncRNA Gm16023 regulates Sirt1 expression and subsequent macrophage polarization via miR-377-3p sponging

To further validate the ceRNA network, we examined the effects of lncRNA Gm16023 overexpression and knockdown on Sirt1 mRNA and protein levels (Figures 7A–7C). Subsequently, we investigated whether the effects of lncRNA Gm16023 on Sirt1 expression and macrophage polarization were dependent on miR-377-3p. Macrophages were co-transfected with lncRNA Gm16023 siRNA and miR-377-3p inhibitor. Knockdown of lncRNA Gm16023 reduced Sirt1 expression, which was restored by miR-377-3p inhibition (Figures 7D and 7E). Moreover, the enhanced M1 polarization and increased inflammatory cytokines induced by lncRNA Gm16023 knockdown were effectively reversed by miR-377-3p inhibition (Figures 7F–7H). Overall, these data indicate that lncRNA Gm16023 modulates Sirt1 expression and suppresses M1 macrophage polarization by sponging miR-377-3p.

LncRNA Gm16023 inhibits sepsis-induced intestinal injury in vivo

Considering that RNA is a negatively charged hydrophilic macromolecule, it is difficult to enter the cell due to the electrostatic rejection of the cell membrane, and it is easily degraded by ubiquitous RNA enzymes. Therefore, a protective shell is needed to enter the cell. Since the cell membrane is mainly composed of lipids, we used liposomes to encapsulate lncRNA, which can more efficiently pass through the cell membrane and release RNA into the cytoplasm, reducing the breakdown of RNA in the process. The strategy for preparing Plasmid-Gm16023@Liposome (PGL) is illustrated in Figure 8A. First, we evaluated liposome transfection efficiency in macrophages by assessing fluorescence intensity and lncRNA expression, which confirmed high in vitro efficiency (Figure S8A). In vivo, PGL injection markedly increased lncRNA Gm16023 levels in intestinal tissue (Figure 8B). The key molecular target miR-377-3p was significantly reduced and sirt1 was markedly increased (Figures 8C and 8D).

Functionally, survival analysis revealed that PGL treatment significantly improved the survival rate of septic mice (Figure 8E), demonstrating its therapeutic potential. Histologically, PGL treatment significantly alleviated intestinal pathological damage and reduced injury scores (Figure 8F). Consistent with the structural preservation, immunoblot analysis showed that PGL markedly restored the expression of tight junction proteins occludin and ZO-1 (Figure 8G). Furthermore, PGL treatment led to a pronounced decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), consistent with the observed tissue protection (Figure 8H). Corroborating these findings, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in F4/80+ macrophage infiltration and a marked decrease in CD86+ M1-like polarization in the PGL group (Figure 8I).

Crucially, to evaluate the translational safety profile of PGL, we conducted a high-dose acute toxicity study. Healthy mice received PGL at a 2-fold therapeutic dose (3 injections within 24 h) (Figure S9A). We observed no significant body weight loss (Figure S9B), and serum biochemical analysis confirmed that liver (AST, ALT) and kidney (BUN, Cr) functions remained within normal physiological ranges (Figures S9C–S9F). Moreover, histological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) revealed no pathological damage or off-target toxicity (Figure S9G). Collectively, these data indicate that PGL effectively overcomes lncRNA instability and alleviates sepsis-induced intestinal injury. The underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms are summarized in the graphical abstract.

Discussion

At present, several studies have reported the effect of hyperglycemia on sepsis, but the epidemiological evidence is still insufficient.5,24 In our study, analysis of UKB data further supported that type 2 diabetes and abnormal blood glucose were high risk factors for sepsis. Hyperglycemia was identified to exacerbate sepsis-induced intestinal injury by promoting a pro-inflammatory (M1-like) macrophage polarization. Specifically, under hyperglycemic conditions, the expression of lncRNA Gm16023 is markedly reduced. Subsequent mechanistic investigations demonstrated that downregulation of lncRNA Gm16023 suppresses Sirt1 expression and reinforces M1 macrophage polarization via miR-377-3p sponging.

Hyperglycemia plays a critical role in initiating and sustaining inflammation, yet its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Recent evidence emphasizes that immune cell dysfunction, particularly altered macrophage phenotypes, is central to this process.25,26 In our study, both human and murine data reveal that hyperglycemia aggravates intestinal damage and increases macrophage infiltration in septic mice. Notably, macrophage depletion in two diabetic sepsis mouse models restored intestinal barrier integrity, underscoring their causal role in high-glucose-promoted intestinal injury in septic mice. Consistent with previous reports, high-glucose exposure in monocyte derived and peritoneal macrophages increased the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-1Ra) and reduced the secretion of the M2-associated cytokine CCL18.27 Moreover, mouse peritoneal macrophages cultured in a high-glucose environment exhibited an upregulation of inflammatory cytokines and diabetic kidney disease models further demonstrated that high-glucose conditions drive macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype, as evidenced by increased IL-6 secretion.28,29 These findings indicate that dysregulated macrophage polarization is a key mechanism underlying hyperglycemia-induced intestinal barrier disruption, warranting further mechanistic exploration.

LncRNAs have emerged as critical regulators of macrophage polarization. For instance, lncRNA-MM2P has been identified as a critical modulator of M2 macrophage polarization, promoting macrophage-induced tumorigenesis.30 In addition, a different lncRNA, lncRNA-Cox2, has been found to enhance macrophage M1 polarization and the inflammatory response through the CREB-C/EBPβ axis, which correlates with unfavorable survival rates in septic mice.31 In this study, RNA-seq analysis revealed that lncRNA Gm16023 is significantly downregulated in hyperglycemia-promoted septic intestinal injury models, and its function has not yet been reported. Subsequent functional assays confirmed that overexpression of lncRNA Gm16023 suppresses M1 macrophage polarization, as evidenced by a marked reduction in M1 markers and inflammatory cytokines. These findings indicate that lncRNA Gm16023 serves as an important immune-inflammatory regulator, which is consistent with previous functional predictions.

lncRNAs exhibit diverse functions, including regulation of transcription, epigenetic modifications, RNA splicing, and protein modifications. Based on their subcellular localization, lncRNAs are classified into nuclear and cytoplasmic types. Nuclear lncRNAs primarily participate in chromatin modification and transcriptional regulation, whereas cytoplasmic lncRNAs modulate mRNA stability, translation, and protein interactions. Notably, many cytoplasmic lncRNAs regulate gene expression via the ceRNA mechanism, acting as miRNA “sponges” to protect target mRNAs from post-transcriptional repression.32,33 In our study, subcellular localization analysis revealed that lncRNA Gm16023 predominantly resides in the cytoplasm, and bioinformatics analysis further predicted that it may function as a ceRNA by binding to miR-377-3p, which has also been verified as a critical promoter of the inflammatory response in previous studies.34,35 Our results further indicate that miR-377-3p enhances M1 macrophage polarization and inflammatory activation, while inhibition of miR-377-3p partially reverses the M1 polarization and inflammatory response induced by lncRNA Gm16023 downregulation. These findings suggest that lncRNA Gm16023 modulates macrophage polarization via a ceRNA mechanism by acting as a miR-377-3p sponge.

To further investigate the downstream regulatory mechanism of miR-377-3p, we integrated mRNA sequencing data, metabolic pathway enrichment analysis, and TargetScan predictions, which led us to identify Sirt1 as the most highly expressed candidate gene. Previous studies have determined the binding sites between Sirt1 and miR-377-3p using dual-luciferase reporter assays.36,37 Sirt1, a highly conserved NAD+-dependent deacetylase, is widely involved in biological processes such as glucose metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammation.38,39,40 Recent studies have demonstrated that Sirt1 modulates sepsis-mediated macrophage polarization,41,42 and it also plays a crucial role in diabetes progression by suppressing inflammation.43,44 In our study, molecular docking analysis confirmed the binding site between miR-377-3p and the 3′ UTR of Sirt1, while functional experiments revealed that suppression of Sirt1 promotes M1 macrophage polarization and inflammatory cytokine release, whereas its activation produces the opposite effects. Notably, overexpression of lncRNA Gm16023 inhibited miR-377-3p expression via a ceRNA mechanism, thereby alleviating M1 macrophage polarization and ultimately regulating Sirt1 expression.

Despite the significant advances made in this study, several aspects warrant further investigation. First, regarding the animal model, we selected CLP over LPS injection because it better reflects sepsis pathophysiology. Unlike the transient cytokine spike typical of LPS models,45 CLP induces sustained inflammation that more closely mimics the progressive nature of clinical sepsis. However, we acknowledge that CLP still possesses limitations regarding translational relevance. Etiologically, CLP is restricted to polymicrobial peritonitis, whereas clinical sepsis arises from diverse sources (e.g., pneumonia, urinary tract infections) involving viruses and fungi. Furthermore, the rapid disease progression in mice significantly simplifies the complex, protracted immune dysregulation and heterogeneity observed in human patients. Second, regarding clinical validation, we acknowledge that the human intestinal samples analyzed in this study were scarce. Larger multi-center patient cohorts are necessary to validate the clinical relevance of the lncRNA Gm16023/miR-377-3p/Sirt1 axis and to establish its diagnostic or therapeutic potential for septic intestinal injury in diabetic individuals. Third, future studies are needed to deepen our mechanistic and safety understanding. While we identified the key regulatory axis, the specific downstream signaling pathways and molecular interactions require further exploration to uncover additional therapeutic targets. In parallel, the potential off-target effects and long-term safety of therapeutic interventions targeting this regulatory axis should be thoroughly evaluated. Finally, investigating the impact of other regulatory factors and signaling pathways associated with hyperglycemia and sepsis on intestinal injury may uncover additional therapeutic targets for intervention.

In summary, our study sheds light on the intricate interplay between hyperglycemia, sepsis, and intestinal injury. Targeting the lncRNA Gm16023/miR-377-3p/Sirt1 axis may hold therapeutic potential for attenuating septic intestinal injury in diabetic individuals.

Materials and methods

Data source and study design

The current investigation employed data from the ongoing UK Biobank prospective cohort study (application 51671; ethical approval obtained August 2019). This large-scale population study enrolled more than 500,000 participants aged 40–69 years from multiple regions in the United Kingdom (England, Wales, and Scotland) during the period 2006–2010. Comprehensive descriptions of the UK Biobank’s methodological framework and assessment protocols are available in previous publications.46 Ethical clearance was granted by the National Information Governance Board for Health and Social Care (covering England and Wales) along with the Community Health Index Advisory Group (for Scotland). All enrolled individuals provided written informed consent before any data collection procedures were initiated.

The initial dataset comprised 502,527 UK Biobank participants. Exclusion criteria were applied as follows: individuals lacking HbA1c measurements (n = 37,285), those without available glucose data (n = 59,055), participants missing blood lipid profiles (n = 1,009), and cases with pre-existing sepsis at baseline assessment (n = 994). Following these exclusions, the analytical sample consisted of 404,184 eligible participants, including 191,595 male and 212,589 female subjects (refer to Figure 1A for detailed flow diagram). Specifically, “abnormal blood glucose” was defined using a fasting blood glucose reference threshold of >5.6 mmol/L, and type 2 diabetes was identified based on HbA1c ≥ 48 mmol/mol. Sepsis-related events were ascertained using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10) codes A02, A39, A40, and A41. Covariates included in the adjustment models comprised age, sex, UK Biobank assessment centers, ethnicity, index of multiple deprivation, body mass index, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, smoking status, alcohol status, physical activity, hypertension, aspirin use, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, and statin use.

Clinical specimens

Intestinal tissues and matched adjacent normal tissue were collected from nondiabetic (n = 6) and diabetic (n = 6) patients with intestinal obstruction who underwent an enterectomy between January 2020 and December 2023 at the Department of Pathology, The Seventh Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. All cases were confirmed to have intestinal obstruction-induced sepsis based on the Sepsis Diagnostic Criteria (v.3.0).47 The collection of clinical specimens was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Seventh Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University.

Mice

WT C57BL/KsJ male mice and diabetic C57BL/KsJ db/db male mice, aged 10–12 weeks, were procured from Guangdong Yaokang Biotechnology (Foshan, China). CD11b-DTR male mice, aged 3–4 weeks, were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. All animal experiments were conducted with approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-Sen University (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). The mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free barrier environment. They were provided with access to water and food ad libitum.

Diabetes model

CD11b-DRT transgenic mice were divided into cages at 21 days after birth, and their toes were also cut to identify the genotype. Pure male mice were fed a HFD for 4 weeks. Then, streptozotocin at a dose of 60 mg/kg was injected intraperitoneally for 5 days, followed by another 4 weeks of HFD feeding to establish the type 2 diabetic mouse model. Random blood glucose levels greater than 16.6 mmol/L indicated successful diabetes model establishment for further CLP modeling.

CLP-induced sepsis model

Nondiabetic mice and diabetic mice were randomly divided into four groups to investigate the impact of hyperglycemia and sepsis-induced intestinal injury. Specifically, the nondiabetic sham group (WS group, n = 6), the nondiabetic CLP group (WCLP group, n = 6), the diabetic sham group (DMS group, n = 6), and the diabetic CLP group (DMCLP group, n = 6). CLP modeling was conducted according to a previously described procedure24,48 with strict standardization to ensure consistent disease severity. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.). After disinfection with 75% ethanol, a 1-cm midline incision was made to expose the cecum. The cecum was ligated at the proximal third using silk thread and punctured once with a 22-gauge needle, followed by the extrusion of a small droplet of fecal matter. The cecum was then returned to the abdominal cavity, and the incision was sutured. Postoperatively, mice received fluid resuscitation (normal saline, 4 mL/100 g, s.c.) and analgesia (bupivacaine and butorphanol tartrate). In the sham group, the cecum was exposed but neither ligated nor punctured. Serum and intestinal tissues were collected 12 h post-surgery.

Macrophage depletion

In this study, we used both Cls (YE SEN, China) and DT (Sigma) to eliminate macrophages. The mechanism of macrophage depletion involves the uptake of Cls by macrophages through endocytosis. Subsequently, lysosomes facilitate the release of clodronate, leading to macrophage apoptosis and achieving effective macrophage depletion. DT is composed of A and B subunits. The B subunit binds to the DTR on the cell membrane and facilitates the entry of the A subunit into the cell, which inactivates transcription elongation factor 2 through adenosine diphosphate ribosylation. This blocks protein synthesis and induces apoptosis. After insertion of the human DTR gene into CD11b-DTR transgenic mice, macrophages in the mice showed high sensitivity to DT. Thus, a low dose of DT could conditionally deplete macrophages from their bodies without affecting other cells.

C57BL/KsJ db/db mice were divided into four groups to investigate the involvement of macrophages in sepsis-induced intestinal injury: Sham+phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) group (n = 6), CLP+PBS group (n = 6), Sham+Cls group (n = 6), and CLP+Cls group (n = 6). The mice received intraperitoneal injections of 200 μL of Cls or PBS (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) and, 24 h later, the CLP procedure was performed. CD11b-DTR diabetic mice were randomly assigned to the following four groups: Sham+PBS group (n = 6), CLP+PBS group (n = 6), Sham+DT group (n = 6), and CLP+DT group (n = 6). The mice were intraperitoneally injected with DT (20 ng/g) and, 24 h later, the CLP procedure was conducted. This single-dose DT protocol ensures sustained depletion for >3 days, covering the acute septic window.49,50

Cell culture and treatment

RAW264.7 cells (obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Science, Shanghai, China) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (KeyGen, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco), and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). The cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. To establish the hyperglycemia and sepsis cell model, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with 35 mM high-glucose medium and 1 μg/mL LPS (Sigma) for 24 h. Experiments in vitro were conducted in biological replicates.

Histological assessment

Intestinal tissues were collected 5 cm above the terminal ileum. The tissues were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, consecutive tissue sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared and stained with H&E. Chui’s scoring system was employed to assess the extent of intestinal mucosal damage,51 defined as follows: grade 0, normal mucosa villi; grade 1, development of the subepithelial Gruenhagen’s space at the tip of the villi; grade 2, extension of the subepithelial space with moderate epithelial lifting; grade 3, extensive epithelial lifting, possibly with a few denuded villi; grade 4, denuded villi with dilated capillaries and increased cellularity of the lamina propria and exposed capillaries; and grade 5, disintegration of the lamina propria, ulceration, and hemorrhage.

Western blotting and immunofluorescence assays

Western blotting and immunofluorescence assays were performed as described previously.24,52 The primary antibodies consisted of anti-occludin (1:1,000–1:2,000, Abcam), anti-ZO-1 (1:1,000–1:2,000, ABclonal), anti-Sirt1 (1:1,000, Abcam), β-actin (1:10,000, ABclonal), F4/80 (1:200, ABclonal), CD86 (1:200, Zen Bio), and CD163 (1:200, Servicebio). The HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000, ABclonal), FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, Servicebio), and Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200, Servicebio) used were for visualization.

qRT-PCR

The RNAs from the intestinal tissue and intestinal cells were extracted using the FastPure Cell & Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme Biotech, V2, China). For nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA separation, the Protein and RNA Isolution System (PARISTM Kit) (Invitrogen) was utilized. For miRNA analysis, cDNA was synthesized using the TransScript II All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (GeneCopoeia, Guangzhou, China). For mRNA analysis, cDNA synthesis was performed using the Evo M-MLV RT Premix (Accurate Biology, China). Subsequently, qRT-PCR was conducted using the SYBR Green PCR kit (Accurate Biology) for mRNA analysis. U6 and β-actin were used as internal controls for miRNA and mRNA, respectively. The primer sequences for all genes were obtained from Guangzhou IGE Biotechnology Corporation (Guangzhou, China), and their details can be found in the supporting material (Tables S1 and S2). Quantitative analysis was carried out using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

RNA-seq and data analysis

To identify lncRNAs associated with diabetes, we conducted RNA-seq analysis on septic and matched normal intestinal tissue samples from both diabetic and nondiabetic mice. The RNA-seq analysis was performed by the BGI (Shenzhen, China) on two pairs of samples. Given the exploratory nature of this screening, we adopted a nominal significance threshold of |fold change| ≥ 0.8 and p < 0.05, rather than strict false discovery rate correction, to minimize the risk of type II errors (false negatives). Crucially, the reliability of the identified targets was subsequently validated using qRT-PCR in independent samples. The prediction of target miRNAs for the identified lncRNAs was performed using the LncBase database. Furthermore, for the identification of differentially expressed miRNAs, a miRNA microarray assay was carried out by the BGI. The predicted target enrichment analysis was performed using the TargetScan database.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

To investigate the direct binding capacity between lncRNA Gm16023 and miR-377-3p, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was conducted. The WT and mutant (Mut) sequences of lncRNA Gm16023 were separately cloned into the GV272 vector (GENE, China). HEK293T cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 24-well plates and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37°C with 5% CO2. Upon reaching 70%–80% confluence, co-transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). For each well, the transfection mixture consisted of 100 ng of the reporter plasmid (WT or Mut), 50 ng of the miR-377 expression plasmid (or negative control), and 10 ng of the Renilla luciferase internal control plasmid. The culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 8 h post-transfection. After 48 h, luciferase activity was quantified using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell transfection

The lncRNA Gm16023 overexpression plasmid and shRNA plasmid (GENE), miRNA-377 mimic, and inhibitor (KeyGen), as well as their corresponding negative controls, were transfected into macrophages using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) as per the manufacturer’s recommendations. The sequences are shown in Table S3. Lipofectamine 2000 was diluted with Opti-MEM and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The transfection reagent mixture was subsequently combined with diluted plasmid or miRNA at room temperature for 20 min. After incubation for 6 h, the complete culture medium was replaced. After 48 h, macrophages were harvested for subsequent tests.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed to evaluate the apoptosis of intestinal mucosal epithelial cells and to characterize macrophage phenotypes. For the apoptosis assay, HCoEpiC cells (5 × 105) were resuspended in 500 μL of binding buffer containing 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of propidium iodide (KeyGen). Cells were incubated for 15 min in the dark at room temperature, followed immediately by flow cytometric analysis. For the analysis of intestinal macrophages, septic intestinal tissues were mined into small pieces and digested using a mixture of collagenase II, collagenase IV, DNase I, and Dispase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The resulting suspension was filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to obtain single cells. Cell viability was assessed using the Zombie Violet Fixable Viability Kit (catalog. no. 423113, BioLegend) to exclude dead cells. Samples were then incubated with the following anti-mouse cell surface antibodies: FITC anti-CD45 (103108), PE/Cyanine7 anti-CD11b (101216), APC anti-F4/80 (123116), and PE anti-CD86 (105007) from BioLegend. After washing, cells were resuspended in PBS for analysis. For peripheral blood analysis, 100 μL of blood was collected, processed to obtain single-cell suspensions, and stained using the same antibody panel described above. Data were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Reactive oxygen species analysis

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in treated macrophages were detected using a Reactive Oxygen Species Detection Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, Jiangsu, China). Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates suitable for fluorescence microscopy. The culture medium was removed, and cells were washed gently three times with PBS. Subsequently, cells were incubated with the DCFH-DA probe (diluted 1:1,000) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. After removing the probe solution, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular DCFH-DA. ROS generation was visualized and captured using a fluorescence microscope.

PGL

PGL complexes were prepared using Hieff Trans Liposomal 2000 (Yeasen), a standardized commercial transfection reagent that has been rigorously optimized for stable nucleic acid encapsulation and validated in numerous high-impact studies.53 The PGL nanoparticles are synthesized in a fast, green way. Briefly, the 30 μg plasmid or vector were dissolved in 30 μL liposomes consisting of cholesterol and phospholipid. After 60 s of vortex mixing, the mixture was left to stand at room temperature for 10 min. The obtained plasmid@liposome suspension was collected into it and stored at 4°C until further use.

PGL animal experiment

For animal experiments, C57BL/KsJ db/db mice were randomly divided into four groups to investigate the involvement of PGL in sepsis-induced intestinal injury (n = 6 per group): saline, NPs, NPs_vector, and PGL. Mice received intraperitoneal injections of NPs, NPs_vector, or PGL (10 mg/kg) for 3 consecutive days. The CLP procedure was performed 24 h after the final injection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 13.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL) and Sigma-plot 10.0 (Systat Software, Chicago, IL). Student’s t test was used to evaluate differences between two groups. For multiple comparisons among different groups, one-way analysis of variance was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. The quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. A significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
