Segment-specific PTV margins for rectal cancer radiotherapy on the MR-Linac: characterization of rectal motion and dosimetric validation
Authors: Wang Yang, Lei Yu, Yiwen Hu, Hui Zhang, Menglong Zhou, Yajie Chen, Yaqi Wang, Jian Qiao, Yanju Yang, Jun Zhao, Weigang Hu, Zhen Zhang, Fan Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDose escalation in rectal radiotherapy shows promise for improving complete response rates and organ preservation, yet optimal expansion margins for rectal lesions remain unclear. This study aims to utilize high-resolution real-time imaging from MR-guided radiotherapy to characterize rectal motion uncertainties, optimize planning target volume (PTV) margins, and validate dosimetric outcomes, thereby enhancing the precision in rectal cancer dose escalation.MethodsA cohort of 22 advanced rectal cancer patients undergoing neoadjuvant radiotherapy on a 1.5T MR-Linac. Pre-treatment, intra-fraction, and post-treatment MR images were acquired for each fraction, with the rectum delineated according to the distance from the anus (upper, middle, lower). Inter- and intrafractional margins were determined by isotropic expansion of the segmented clinical target volume (CTV), with adequacy defined as covering 95% of the CTV volume in 90% of all fractions. Dosimetric validation was performed on 15 patients (10 internal, 5 external), comparing the segmental margins with literature-based uniform margins to assess target coverage and organs at risk (OARs) sparing.ResultsRectal motion variability was segment-dependent, with the upper rectum requiring the largest margins and the lower rectum the smallest. Interfractional margins for the upper, middle, and lower rectum were 13 mm, 7 mm, and 6.5 mm, respectively. Intrafractional analysis revealed that treatment duration also influenced margin requirements, with 7 mm for the upper rectum and 3 mm for the middle and lower rectum sufficient for durations under 30 min; longer durations necessitated slightly larger margins. Dosimetric analysis confirmed that the segmented margin expansion strategy achieves adequate target coverage while substantially sparing OARs with dose reductions ranging from 7.4% to 44.5%.ConclusionsSegment-specific margin expansion suggest a potential improvement for rectal radiotherapy, ensuring target coverage while reducing OAR dose, thereby facilitating organ and function preservation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02806-z.Keywords: Rectal cancer, Margin expansion, Segmented, Dosimetric validation, MR-Linac, Interfraction, Intrafraction

Introduction

Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) followed by surgical resection has been established as the standard treatment for locally advanced rectal cancer (LARC), significantly improving tumor regression and local control [1, 2]. Nevertheless, only approximately 10–15% of patients attain pCR after standard CRT [2, 3], prompting the exploration of treatment intensification strategies. Dose escalation in radiotherapy has emerged as a promising approach to improve tumor response, with established dose-response relationships supported by preclinical and clinical evidence [4–6]. Nevertheless, rectal irradiation is challenging due to the considerable motion of rectal targets, which manifest as both interfractional (between fractions) and intrafractional (during treatment) uncertainties. In non-adaptive radiotherapy workflows, where the treatment plan remains unchanged throughout the course, interfractional variations are typically addressed by adding margins to the clinical target volume (CTV). In contrast, adaptive radiotherapy (ART) workflows enable daily modification of the treatment plan based on real-time anatomical changes. While interfractional uncertainties can be mitigated throughART, intrafractional uncertaintiesremain challenging to assess accurately using conventional non-real-time CT imaging, highlighting the need for more advanced motion management and precision radiotherapy techniques.

The integration of MR imaging with linear accelerators (MR-Linac) represents a breakthrough in radiotherapy, offering high soft-tissue contrast and nearly real-time adaptive treatment strategies [7]. It enables daily plan adjustments based on anatomical changes [8], thereby enhancing dose-escalation precision in rectal cancer. Nevertheless, it also results in longer treatment duration and greater intrafractional motion uncertainty.

Treatment goals vary by tumor location, with organ preservation being critical for low rectal cancers, emphasizing the need to minimize unnecessary margin expansions. While prior studies have identified variations in rectal tumor motion across anatomical locations using MR-linac [9], these investigations mainly used isotropic margin expansions [9, 10], without considering segment-specific variations. Furthermore, the dosimetric benefits of segmented-specific margins have not been quantitatively validated. This underscores the necessity for further research to optimize treatment precision. This study addresses these gaps by introducing a novel, segment-specific approach to characterize rectal motion uncertainties and define tailored margins for dose escalation. Notably, it integrates dosimetric validation to rigorously evaluate the adequacy of target coverage and the potential clinical benefits of segmented margins, offering a step forward in refining MR-Linac-based approaches for rectal cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Patients identification and treatment delivery

Data were prospectively acquired from 22 patients diagnosed with rectal cancer who underwent neoadjuvant short-course radiotherapy (SCRT) using a 1.5T MR - Linac (Unity, Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden) between August 2024 and May 2025 for margin determination. Among them, 13 cases received conventional daily treatment, while 9 cases underwent metronomic fractionated therapy (irradiation administered once every three weeks) per our institutional clinical practice. All 22 patients were included in the analysis of intrafractional margin expansion. The 9 patients receiving metronomic fractionation were excluded from the interfractional analysis because their prolonged treatment schedule introduces substantial volume changes due to tumor regression; these changes confound the assessment of motion uncertainties arising from daily anatomical variations. 10 patients were randomly selected from the enrolled cohort for internal dosimetric validation, and an additional 5 patients diagnosed with LARC between June 2025 and August 2025 were included for external dosimetric validation. This research was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the medical ethics committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center and all patients provided signed informed consent.

An online adaptive workflow on a 1.5 T MR-linac was utilized in the management of patients. Briefly, pre-treatment simulation CT and daily MRIs were obtained. Approximately one to two weeks after simulation, treatment was administered in 5 or 6 fractions of 5 Gy (either consecutively or metronomically) in accordance with our institutional standard, using either online adapt-to-position (ATP) or adapt-to-shape (ATS) workflows.

Imaging acquisition and delineations

For each treatment fraction, three online 3D T2-weighted MRI images were captured: pre-treatment scans (MRIpre), intrafraction scans (MRIintra), and post-treatment scans (MRIpost), respectively (Fig. 1a). Ultimately, 113 fractions with 334 MR scans were collected from 22 patients. The MRI scanning protocol for all treatment phases incorporated the T2 weighted (T2W) 3D Tra sequence (FOV: 360 × 480 × 250 mm, Voxel: 1.1 × 1.1 × 2.0 mm, Slice thickness: 1 mm, TE/TR: 87/1300 ms; Matrix: 328 × 437 × 250 slices).

Schematic workflow of this study, which comprises three key components: (a) image acquisition, (b) CTV delineation and margin determination, and (c) dosimetric validation. Abbreviations: CTV, clinical target volume

To account for segment-specific anatomical variations and motion patterns, the rectum was delineated according to the distance from the anus verge (upper rectum: ≤5 cm, middle rectum: 5–10 cm, lower rectum: >10 cm) in the simulation CT and MR image based on the work by Nougaret [11] using the contouring toolbox in MIM (version 7.0; MIM Software, Inc., Cleveland, OH). (Supplementary Fig. 1). Organs at risk (OARs) were contoured in accordance with the contouring guidelines of the Radiation Therapy Oncology Group [12]. To ensure the reliability of our delineations, we implemented a rigorous quality assurance protocol. This included standardized training for the operator, with all contours reviewed and approved by another senior radiation oncologist.

Assessment of inter- and intrafractional margin

According to the widely adopted van Herk margin recipe [13], a planning target volume (PTV) margin is deemed adequate if the CTV receives 95% of the prescribed dose in 90% of the patient population. In this study, inter- and intrafractional margins were determined by isotropically expanding the segmented CTVs and evaluating the geometric coverage adequacy for each fraction, as shown in Fig. 1b. Geometric coverage was defined as the ratio of overlapping voxels between the expanded structures on initial images and the corresponding segments on subsequent images. Coverage was deemed adequate when the overlapping volume reached 95% of the CTV volume on subsequent images in 90% of the cases across all fractions. The inter- and intrafractional assessments are detailed below.

Interfractional margin assessment: The MRIpre of each fraction was rigidly registered to the simulated CT scans based on bony anatomical landmarks. For each segmented CTV (upper/middle/lower) on MRIpre, geometric coverage was calculated using isotropic margins ranging from 0 to 20 mm in 0.5-mm increments. Interfractional margin reflect the uncertainties associated with conventional non-adaptive radiotherapy procedures.

For each segmented CTV (upper/middle/lower) on MRIpre, geometric coverage was calculated using isotropic margins ranging from 0 to 20 mm in 0.5-mm increments. Interfractional margin reflect the uncertainties associated with conventional non-adaptive radiotherapy procedures.

Intrafraction margin assessment: For each treatment fraction, MRI scans were systematically paired in all possible combinations (MRIpre/MRIintra, MRIpre/MRIpost, MRIintra/MRIpost). Note that MR images were not registered, as the motion occurring during the treatment process ought to be considered as well. For each segmented CTV (upper/middle/lower) on the earlier images in each pair, geometric coverage was calculated for the expanded volumes on the subsequent image, employing margins from 0 to 10 mm in 0.5-mm increments. Data were categorized into 10-minute intervals based on acquisition time differences, and intrafraction margins were analyzed accordingly. Intrafractional margin reflect the uncertainties associated with adaptive radiotherapy procedures. Margins exceeding 20 mm for interfractional margin evaluation or 10 mm for intrafractional were regarded as outliers. Each outlier case was independently reassessed by a specialized team comprising a radiation oncologist and a medical physicist, who re-evaluated the delineation accuracy and analyzed anatomical changes. The geometric coverage for these outliers was then manually recalculated using the same isotropic expansion principle, with careful verification to ensure precision. All expansion and coverages were calculated using UIH (version R001.3.0; United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China).

Dosimetric analysis

The derived segmental margins were dosimetrically compared with the conventional uniform margins from literature, which employed 6 mm and 17 mm PTV margins for intra- and interfractional motion, respectively [9]. This comparison focused on differences in target coverage and OAR sparing, as illustrated in Fig. 1c. Dosimetric analyses were performed on 10 enrolled patients for internal assessment and an additional 5 patients for external validation, with detailed procedures outlined in Supplementary Fig. 2. Since the primary objective of this study was to evaluate rectal motion during dose escalation to rectal lesions, the CTV was defined as the primary rectal lesion and the lumen at the involved level and was segmentally recontoured on simulated CT scans, as well as MRIpre and MRIpost from the first fraction. A prescription dose of 25 Gy in 5 fractions was applied to all treatment plans. All plans were originated using the Monaco treatment planning system (version 5.40.01; Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden) and were evaluated and approved by radiation oncologists.

Statistics

Data are recorded as the mean (SD) or median (range) for continuous variables and numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. Patient characteristics and dosimetric analysis were studied using the chi-square test, independent sample t-test, or Wilcoxon rank sum as applicable. All statistical analyses were carried out using the R statistical software package (version 3.6.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Inter- and intrafractional coverage and margin determination

A total of 192 datasets of inter- and 1002 datasets of intrafraction were analyzed. The coverage rates for inter- and intrafraction are depicted in Fig. 2a, with the overall required expansion margins being 9 mm for interfraction and 3.5 mm for intrafraction. Given that this study identified substantial variations in rectal motion among different segments, Fig. 2b and c illustrates the inter- and intrafractional data corresponding to distinct segments. For interfraction, the upper rectum required an expansion margin of 13 mm, the middle rectum 7 mm, and the lower rectum 6.5 mm. Regarding intrafraction, the upper rectum required a 6.5 mm expansion margin, the middle rectum 3 mm, and the lower rectum 2.5 mm. The upper rectum requires a larger expansion margin, whereas the lower rectum is relatively fixed and requires a smaller margin.

Expansion margins required to encompass 95% of voxels for (a) both inter- and intrafractional variations in all enrolled patients, (b) interfractional variation categorized by anatomical location, (c) intrafractional variation categorized by anatomical location, and intrafractional variation stratified by time intervals in the (d) upper, (e) middle, and (f) lower rectal segments

The time interval-related intrafraction coverage rates are shown in the Fig. 2d and f. Among all 22 patients, the median procedure time was 24 min (range 14–82 min), with ATP and ATS cases being 24 min (range 14–37 min) and 35 min (range 24–82 min), respectively. For cases with a treatment duration of less than 30 min, a margin of 7 mm for the upper rectum and 3 mm for the middle and lower rectum were sufficient to cover 90% of the CTV within 30 min. However, for cases with longer durations (more than 30 min), the margins need to be increased to 8 mm, 4.5 mm and 3 mm, respectively. Moreover, the required margin for rectum with tumors was relatively smaller compared to those without tumors (7.5 mm vs. 11.5 mm for interfractional, and 3 mm vs. 4.5 mm for intrafractional), possibly because the infiltrative growth of tumors makes the rectum more fixed (Supplementary Fig. 3). Tumor infiltration depth also affects the required margin expansion, with deeper infiltration associated with smaller margin requirements (Supplementary Fig. 4).

In the case of insufficient intrafractional CTV coverage, 11 out of 334 fractions (3.3%) in the upper rectum required an expansion margin exceeding 10 mm, with these outliers observed in 5 patients. For the middle and lower rectum, the maximum expansion margins were 10 mm and 6.5 mm, respectively, both occurring in the same patient. Visual analysis attributed the upper rectum’s insufficient coverage to its inherently greater mobility due to its relatively free anatomical structure. Additionally, rectal gas distension and bladder filling also influence mobility, which are the main contributing factors to insufficient coverage in cases of the middle and lower rectum.

A comparison of target volumes was conducted between conventional uniform margins (literature-based: 17 mm interfraction, 6 mm intrafraction) and our segment-specific margins. The results indicated a significant reduction in target volume for the middle and lower rectal segments, regardless of treatment duration (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Dosimetric analysis

The geometric coverage analysis provided the foundation for determining the segment-specific expansion margins, which were then rigorously validated through dosimetric assessments. For uniform expansion protocol [9], 17 mm and 6 mm margins were adopted for inter- and intrafractional assessments. For the segmented expansion protocol, as established in the previous subsection, the interfractional margins were defined as 13 mm/7 mm/ 6.5 mm for the upper/middle/lower CTVs, respectively. Correspondingly, the intrafractional margins were determined as 7 mm/3 mm/3 mm, based on the margin expansion requirements for the median treatment duration (20–30 min). Statistical characteristics of target coverage and OARs sparing between the two strategies are summarized in Table 2. The segmented expansion method exhibits a distinct advantage in reducing the irradiated volume compared to the uniform approach, achieving approximately 50% and 17% reductions in target volume for inter- and intrafractional variations, respectively. Both protocols provided adequate dosimetric coverage of the CTV (V95% > 99% and V100% > 95%), with no significant differences observed between the two protocols in either internal or external validations. Case-by-case analysis revealed that, with the segmented margin expansion method, all patients in the internal and external cohorts received sufficient CTV dose during the interfractional assessment. In the intrafractional assessment, 14 patients maintained sufficient CTV coverage, with only one exception in the internal cohort.

Discussion

This study presents the first detailed analysis of rectal motion based on rectal segments using a 1.5T MR-linac, proposing an optimized segment-specific margin expansion strategy. By analyzing 192 interfractional and 1002 intrafractional datasets, the current study reveals that for patients undergoing daily ART with treatment durations not exceeding 30 min, intrafractional expansion margins of 7.5 mm, 3 mm, and 3 mm for the upper, middle, and lower segments, respectively, are sufficient to ensure adequate target coverage. However, for patients with anticipated prolonged treatment durations (e.g., due to time-consuming target delineation or complex planning on MR-linac), the intrafractional expansion margins should be appropriately increased to 8 mm, 4.5 mm, and 3 mm for the upper, middle, and lower segments, respectively. For patients who receive routine non-adaptive treatments, the required interfractional margins are 13 mm, 7 mm, and 6.5 mm, respectively. These findings indicate that the upper rectum exhibits greater mobility compared to the middle and lower rectum, necessitating a larger expansion margin. While previous studies have acknowledged regional differences in rectal motion [9, 14], they have not systematically explored these variations based on specific rectal segments. The upper rectum, owing to its relatively free anatomical position, is substantially influenced by bladder filling, respiratory movements, and intestinal peristalsis, resulting in larger motion. This necessitates a larger expansion margin to ensure sufficient coverage of the target volume during radiotherapy. In contrast, the lower rectum is more fixed and exhibits minimal motion, thus requiring a smaller expansion margin. This study reveals that the overall required margins should be 9 mm for interfraction and 3.5 mm for intrafraction, which slightly differs from prior findings. Hidde et al. reported that margins of 17 mm for interfraction and 6 mm for intrafraction margins were required to encompass 90% of tumor motion [9]. The reduced margins observed in this study may be attributed to the advanced disease stage of the enrolled patients, with 81.8% presenting with clinical stage III disease. The deep tumor infiltration, extending into the perirectal adipose tissue or invading adjacent organs, results in relative rectal fixation, consequently reducing the required expansion margin. Additionally, the CTV delineated in this study, which encompasses both the gross tumor volume (GTV) and the rectal wall at the tumor level, results in a larger volume compared to GTV-only delineation, potentially enabling 90% geometric coverage with smaller expansion margins. Another study focusing on dose escalation demonstrated that an isotropic intrafractional margin of 3 mm was sufficient to cover 95% of the target volume. Notably, all patients in this study were managed using the ATP workflow, with a median treatment time of only 13 min. This finding is consistent with the results observed in our subgroup of patients with treatment durations of 10–20 min [10]. In addition, another study investigating mesorectal motion patterns also conducted a segment-specific analysis. Their findings align with our observed segment-specific motion trends, with greater mobility in the upper segment, further supporting the necessity of individualized margins [15].

Several previous studies have adopted statistical error-based approaches such as the van Herk recipe to propose distinct margin expansions along different anatomical directions. For instance, Kensen et al. calculated differential margin requirements for the primary tumor, reporting larger values in the anterior-posterior and cranial-caudal directions [16]. Similarly, Boldrini et al. found that mesorectal motion is direction-dependent, recommending anisotropic margins with the largest expansion (20 mm) in the anterior direction for the superior mesorectum [15]. However, the current study use isotropic margins mainly because the core innovation and primary aim of this study were to propose and validate the strategy of “segment-specific margin” and to evaluate its potential superiority over the conventional method of applying a uniform margin. The simplified isotropic model directly highlights the dosimetric benefits of the segment-specific strategy, avoiding unnecessary complexity and reliance on statistical assumptions regarding error distributions.The geometric coverage analysis provided the foundation for determining the segment-specific expansion margins, which were then rigorously validated through dosimetric assessments. Internal and external dosimetric validations confirmed that the geometric coverage directly translates into dosimetric robustness ensuring optimal target coverage while significantly improving OAR sparing. This was particularly evident for the sphincter complex, which exhibited dose reductions of 44.5% for interfractional and 18.2% for intrafractional margins. Notably, the sphincter complex plays an important role in anal function preservation, which is a key therapeutic goal in rectal cancer treatment, particularly for low rectal cancers. Previous studies have demonstrated that the radiation dose to the sphincter is a predictive factor for anal incontinence and lower anterior resection syndrome [17], yet it is often overlooked in conventional treatments. In the present study, the implementation of segmented expansion irradiation markedly reduced the radiation dose delivered to the sphincter complex across patients with diverse tumor locations, thereby achieving substantial preservation of anal function. These insights provide valuable guidance for clinicians in tailoring expansion margins in accordance with tumor location, thereby optimizing radiotherapy planning and enhancing treatment efficacy.Based on the findings of this study, a structured workflow for MR-Linac-based rectal radiotherapy can be established. For non-adaptive approaches with an unmodified target volume, the interfraction margins established herein should be applied. For adaptive strategies, the expected treatment duration should be estimated in advance, taking into account target recontouring complexity and replanning requirements, to facilitate the selection of appropriate intrafraction margins. To minimize intrafractional motion, every effort should be made to streamline the adaptive process, particularly the time-consuming steps of re-delineation and re-planning. Future research should focus on artificial intelligence(AI)-driven automated contouring and planning to accelerate workflow efficiency. Although adaptive workflows reduce inter-fractional errors, re-planning significantly prolongs treatment time and may increase intrafractional errors; thus, careful consideration of whether re-planning is necessary is essential, and developing AI or radiomics-based decision-making frameworks is highly valuable [18]. Furthermore, while conventional CT-guided linacs lack real-time soft-tissue visualization capabilities, the segment-specific margin framework established in this study remains applicable on such platforms. It is noteworthy that the corresponding margin expansion should be applied based on the estimated treatment time interval.

The conclusions derived from this study are applicable not only to dose-escalation strategies in rectal radiotherapy, but also to treatments involving localized irradiation of rectal lesions, such as lymph node-sparing radiotherapy. As an emerging approach derived from the combination of immunotherapy and radiotherapy, lymph node-sparing radiotherapy is based on the rationale that tumor-draining lymph nodes are classical sites for T-cell priming and activation [19]. Preserving these lymph nodes during irradiation of the primary lesion may thus enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. This concept has been demonstrated in a prospective phase II single-arm clinical trial involving 46 patients with LARC. The study demonstrated that lymph node-sparing modified short-course radiotherapy combined with CAPOX and tislelizumab significantly increased the pCR rate to 78.8% in MSS/pMMR LARC patients, suggesting a promising and tolerable treatment modality.

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, the sample size is relatively small, with only 22 patients included for margin assessment. To address this limitation, data were collected during simulation and at three distinct time points throughout each treatment fraction, yielding a total of 22 CT scans and 334 MR scans for analysis. We believe these data are sufficient for a systematic evaluation of both interfraction and intrafraction expansion margins. Secondly, our study did not perform a formal inter-observer variability analysis, as the contouring was performed under a unified protocol designed to inherently minimize variation, and the analysis prioritized evaluating geometric changes over time rather than comparing individual observers’ delineations. Thirdly, We utilized isotropic margins and did not account for directional variations since the design principle of this method inherently assumes uniform uncertainty in all directions. Finally, the margins determination and dosimetric validation were performed exclusively at a single center, which introduces potential institutional bias. To strengthen the findings, further studies should involve multicenter collaborations with larger cohorts to validate the robustness of the proposed segment-specific margins.

Conclusions

This study proposed segment-specific margins required for both adaptive radiotherapy (determined by intrafraction margin) and non-adaptive radiotherapy (determined by interfraction margin). Dosimetric validation suggested that these segment-specific margins ensure adequate target coverage while reducing radiation exposure to normal tissues, particularly the sphincter complex. The strong correlation between geometric coverage and actual dosimetric performance may support the clinical adoption of tailored margins based on tumor location and treatment duration, providing a foundation for future prospective clinical trials on dose escalation to rectal lesions. However, given the single-center design and limited sample size, further validation through multicenter studies with larger cohorts is warranted to refine the conclusions, facilitating their robust application in clinical practice.
