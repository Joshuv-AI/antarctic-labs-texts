Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma of the uterine corpus in a postmenopausal Woman: A case report and literature Review
Authors: Misako Omuro, Yuichi Imai, Yuki Ogawara, Taichi Mizushima, Erika Muraoka, Shoji Yamanaka, Satoshi Fujii, Etsuko Miyagi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) of the uterine corpus is extremely rare in postmenopausal women.•Complete surgical resection is crucial for favorable outcomes.•Adult-onset RMS generally carries a worse prognosis than pediatric cases.•Individualized treatment is essential due to the absence of standardized protocols.

Introduction

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a malignant soft tissue sarcoma derived from mesenchymal cells capable of skeletal muscle differentiation. RMS predominantly affects children and adolescents, with a median age at diagnosis of around 15 years; occurrences in adults are rare (Committee et al., 2020). Primary sites commonly include the genitourinary tract, head and neck, and extremities. RMS arising in organs lacking skeletal muscle, such as the uterus, is particularly uncommon.

Uterine RMS accounts for 0.2–0.5 % of all uterine malignancies (Pop et al., 2023). Among these, approximately 53 % arise from the cervix and fewer than 20 % from the uterine corpus (Luguta et al., 2024). The median age at diagnosis is typically during adolescence, making adult-onset cases—particularly in postmenopausal women—exceedingly rare.

Histologically, RMS is classified into embryonal, alveolar, pleomorphic, and spindle cell/sclerosing subtypes. The embryonal subtype is the most common in the genitourinary tract, comprising approximately 71 % of cases (Odai et al., 2024, Ferguson et al., 2007). In pediatric patients, a multimodal treatment approach—including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy—is standard. The VAC regimen—vincristine, actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide—is a cornerstone of pediatric chemotherapy. Risk-adapted protocols established through the IRS studies, particularly IRS-V, allow for stratification into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, guiding treatment intensity accordingly (Malempati and Hawkins, 2012 Jul). However, treatment strategies for adult RMS remain poorly defined and are often extrapolated from pediatric protocols due to the rarity of disease in adults.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old nulligravid woman presented with a 3-month history of persistent abnormal genital bleeding. Her medical history included hypertension and herpes zoster. Her sister had endometrial cancer, although the histological subtype and stage were unknown. The patient had undergone menopause at age 55. Initial pelvic examination at a local clinic revealed a prolapsing vaginal mass, and biopsy results were consistent with rhabdomyosarcoma. She was referred to our institution for further management. On examination, a polypoid tumor was observed protruding through the cervical os into the vaginal canal (Fig. 1). Bimanual examination revealed an effaced cervical canal, and the tumor appeared to originate from the uterine corpus, with no continuity to cervical tissue and no evidence of parametrial invasion.Fig. 1Tumor protruding into the vaginal canal and occupying the cervical canal.

Transvaginal ultrasound demonstrated a 45 × 40 mm mass extending from the uterine body to the cervix, with prominent vascularity in the anterior uterine wall. Although the bleeding had ceased, the patient remained anemic (Hb, 7.7 g/dL). Serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, 142 U/L; reference range, 124–222 U/L), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA, 0.7 ng/mL; <5.0 ng/mL), cancer antigen 125 (CA125, 7 U/mL; <35 U/mL), cancer antigen 19–9 (CA19-9, 3 U/mL; <37 U/mL), and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC, 0.7 ng/mL; <1.5 ng/mL) were all within normal limits.

Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a smooth-surfaced, well-demarcated 57 × 48 × 38 mm mass attached to the uterine corpus and protruding into the vaginal canal, with some areas of diffusion restriction. Cervical involvement could not be excluded (Fig. 2). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) from chest to pelvis revealed no distant metastasis.Fig. 2Sagittal T2-weighted pelvic MRI showing a uterine tumor extending into the vaginal canal.

A biopsy performed at our institution showed a proliferation of large round cells with irregular, eccentrically located nuclei and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were positive for Desmin and Myogenin. No carcinoma components were identified on hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining or immunohistochemical staining for CK AE1/AE3, supporting the diagnosis of rhabdomyosarcoma (Fig. 3). Based on the 2011 Japanese Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology and 2008 FIGO criteria, the preoperative diagnosis was uterine rhabdomyosarcoma, Stage IB. Following the 2023 Japanese Clinical Practice Guidelines for Endometrial Cancer and the NCCN Guidelines for Uterine Neoplasms, lymphadenectomy was not performed, and total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and upper vaginectomy was selected as the standard surgical procedure for uterine sarcomas (Japan Society of Gynecologic Oncology, 2023, National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 2024).

The operation lasted 304 min with an estimated blood loss of 497 mL. During surgery, the patient received 8 units of packed red blood cells (1,120 mL) and 6 units of fresh frozen plasma (720 mL). Gross pathology showed a 70 × 65 × 35 mm polypoid mass attached to the posterior uterine wall (Fig. 4). As part of the upper vaginectomy, the vaginal wall was resected, measuring approximately 2 cm at its shortest and 4 cm at its longest.Fig. 4Macroscopic view of the resected specimen showing a polypoid tumor measuring 70 × 65 × 35 mm attached to the posterior wall of the uterine corpus.

Macroscopic view of the resected specimen showing a polypoid tumor measuring 70 × 65 × 35 mm attached to the posterior wall of the uterine corpus.

A total of 25 histological blocks were prepared from the tumor-bearing area for microscopic examination. Histologically, the tumor consisted of a mixed proliferation of large round cells with irregular, eccentrically located nuclei and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, along with small round to spindle-shaped primitive mesenchymal cells. The tumor proliferated within the endometrial stroma, sparing non-neoplastic endometrial glands. No carcinoma component was identified, and immunohistochemical staining for epithelial markers (CAM5.2, AE1/AE3, EMA) was negative. Myometrial invasion was superficial, extending only 1 mm into the 13-mm-thick myometrium. No lymph vascular invasion was observed. The surgical margin was negative, and peritoneal washing cytology was also negative.

Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were positive for Desmin, diffusely and strongly positive for Myogenin, and focally weakly positive for MyoD1. The Ki-67 labeling index was approximately 30 % (Fig. 5). The final diagnosis was embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma of the uterine corpus, Stage IB (pT1bNxM0). One cycle of adjuvant VAC chemotherapy was administered but later discontinued at the patient’s request. The patient remains under close surveillance and has shown no evidence of disease for 12 months following surgery.

Discussion

Clinical and molecular characteristics of RMS

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a rare, aggressive malignancy that can arise in atypical anatomical sites, including the uterus, although it more commonly occurs in the genitourinary tract, head and neck, and extremities (Malempati and Hawkins, 2012 Jul). Histologically, RMS is categorized into four subtypes according to the World Health Organization (WHO): embryonal, alveolar, pleomorphic, and spindle cell/sclerosing. In pediatric cases, the embryonal and alveolar subtypes are most common, with alveolar RMS frequently harboring PAX3-FOXO1 or PAX7-FOXO1 fusion genes, whereas embryonal RMS shows heterogeneous genetic alterations, including FGFR4, RAS, and AKT pathway alterations, PTEN and TP53 mutations, and chromosomal copy number variations (Japan Society of Gynecologic Oncology, 2023).

Pleomorphic RMS is observed more often in adults, although its genetic background remains poorly characterized. Prognosis generally correlates with histologic subtype, with embryonal RMS showing relatively favorable outcomes and alveolar or pleomorphic subtypes associated with poorer prognosis.

RMS in the female genital tract

In the female reproductive tract, RMS can occur as part of a biphasic tumor, such as carcinosarcoma, or as a pure heterologous sarcoma. Pure RMS most commonly arises in the vagina, often manifesting as sarcoma botryoides, with the average age at diagnosis being in the teenage years. Pure RMS originating in the uterus in adults is exceedingly rare, accounting for only 0.2–0.5 % of all uterine malignancies. Among these, approximately 53 % arise from the cervix and only 20 % from the uterine corpus (Luguta et al., 2024).

Histologically, embryonal and pleomorphic subtypes are most frequently observed in uterine RMS. In a series of 25 cases of cervical RMS, the median age was 36 years (range: 19–75 years), with a median tumor size of 6.0 cm (range: 3.3–18.0 cm) (Odai et al., 2024, National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 2024). In contrast, among 41 reported cases of uterine corpus RMS, the median age was 65 years (range: 21–90 years), with a median tumor size of 10.0 cm (range: 5.0–20.0 cm), suggesting a trend toward older age and larger tumors compared to cervical cases (Japanese Society of Pediatric Hematology/Oncology, 2016).

Pleomorphic RMS accounts for more than half of uterine corpus RMS cases, with a poor prognosis; 42.1 % of patients died within 6 months of surgery or diagnosis, and the mean overall survival was reported to be only 12.9 months. In contrast, approximately 22 % of uterine corpus RMS cases are of the embryonal subtype, with a 70 % survival rate, highlighting the influence of histological subtype on prognosis in adult uterine RMS (Odai et al., 2024).

Treatment strategies

RMS is an extremely rare malignancy, and currently, no disease-specific treatment guidelines have been established. A multimodal treatment strategy, including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, is generally considered the standard approach.

Treatment plans are determined based on risk stratification derived from large cooperative studies such as IRS-V and IRS-VI, which incorporate histologic subtype, pretreatment stage classification, postoperative group classification, and patient age. Treatment intensity is adjusted accordingly.

According to the NCCN Clinical Practice Guidelines for Uterine Neoplasms, total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is the recommended initial treatment for resectable uterine sarcomas. However, there is no clear consensus on the necessity of lymphadenectomy (National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 2024). Even in cases where complete resection is achieved, adjuvant multi-agent chemotherapy, including the VAC regimen (vincristine, actinomycin D, and cyclophosphamide), is recommended to reduce the risk of recurrence (Malempati and Hawkins, 2012 Jul, Japanese Society of Pediatric Hematology/Oncology, 2016). Radiotherapy is recommended in the NCCN guidelines as a local control strategy for unresectable or recurrent disease (National Comprehensive Cancer Network, 2024). Similarly, the Japanese Pediatric Hematology/Oncology Guidelines for Rhabdomyosarcoma suggest considering radiotherapy when residual tumor remains after surgery (Malempati and Hawkins, 2012 Jul, Japanese Society of Pediatric Hematology/Oncology, 2016).

Treatment outcomes and prognostic factors

Even in patients who underwent complete resection, combination therapy with chemotherapy has shown survival benefits. A previous study reported that the 5-year overall survival (OS) was 61.5 % in patients who received complete surgery and more than eight cycles of chemotherapy, compared to 47.1 % in patients who underwent incomplete surgery or did not receive chemotherapy (Aminimoghaddam et al., 2021). Prognostic factors include age ≤ 20 years, tumor size ≤ 5 cm, absence of distant metastasis at initial diagnosis, favorable histologic subtype, and responsiveness to chemotherapy (Ferrari et al., 2003, Little et al., 2002, Hawkins et al., 2001). Two main factors may explain the poorer prognosis of RMS in adults compared to children. First, the most common primary sites differ between age groups. In pediatric patients, orbital RMS—a favorable site classified as Stage 1 under IRS-V—is common. In contrast, adult patients more frequently present with tumors in parameningeal sites, which are classified as Stage 3 and associated with worse outcomes. Second, histologic subtypes differ. While children more often have embryonal RMS, which has a relatively favorable prognosis, adults more frequently develop alveolar or pleomorphic RMS, which are associated with poor outcomes.

Case Context and clinical Implications

In the present case, although the tumor was classified as embryonal RMS—a histologic subtype generally associated with a more favorable prognosis—certain adverse prognostic factors were present, including advanced age, large tumor size, and discontinuation of chemotherapy. In light of these factors and the patient's preferences, further treatment beyond the first cycle of adjuvant VAC chemotherapy was not pursued.

Given the extreme rarity of uterine RMS and the lack of disease-specific treatment guidelines, individualized treatment planning is essential. This involves tailoring the therapeutic strategy by carefully balancing prognostic factors, treatment-related risks, and patient preferences, rather than adhering strictly to standard protocols. In this case, the decision to prioritize surgical resection followed by close surveillance exemplifies such an approach.

Pathological and diagnostic considerations

Differential diagnoses include carcinosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, adenosarcoma with sarcomatous overgrowth, low-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma, rhabdoid leiomyoma, alveolar soft part sarcoma, rhabdoid tumor, and undifferentiated uterine sarcoma (Esnaola et al., 2001). Accurate histopathological assessment is essential for the diagnosis of pure RMS. Extensive and thorough sampling of the resected specimen is required to rule out the presence of any epithelial components. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis is also useful for evaluating suspicious areas, particularly when distinguishing RMS from carcinosarcoma with heterologous elements (Aminimoghaddam et al., 2021).

While preoperative diagnosis via cytology or biopsy is crucial, it remains challenging due to tumor necrosis, which is commonly seen in adult uterine RMS (Takazawa, 2018). Even in rhabdomyosarcoma of the uterine cervix, several reports have indicated that cervical cytology alone is frequently insufficient for a definitive diagnosis (Sohsuke, 2016). In such cases, the use of cell block preparations and the application of IHC staining are often required to reach an accurate diagnosis (Wei and Li, 2024). In our case, a diagnosis of rhabdomyosarcoma was successfully established at the time of biopsy. However, when tumors exhibit extensive surface necrosis, obtaining adequate diagnostic tissue can be difficult, potentially resulting in delayed diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, in suspected cases of rhabdomyosarcoma, securing an adequate amount of tissue and performing comprehensive histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation are essential.

Although hysterectomy was feasible in this postmenopausal patient, in younger women with fertility desire, thorough preoperative assessment is critical, as hysterectomy represents both the definitive diagnostic and therapeutic modality.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of embryonal RMS of the uterine corpus in a postmenopausal woman. This case highlights the importance of early diagnosis, complete surgical resection, and individualized consideration of adjuvant therapy in adult uterine RMS, given the extreme rarity and lack of standardized treatment protocols. Accumulation of additional case reports and long-term studies is essential to improve understanding of clinical behavior, optimal management, and prognosis in adult-onset uterine corpus embryonal RMS.
