Comparing the effect of dispositional and contextual ethical sensitivity in ethical decision-making of medical students: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Na Zhang, Xiang Sun, Zhen Xu, Qi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As awareness of medical ethics is the first step in the successful implementation of ethical decision-making, the importance of ethical sensitivity has been frequently highlighted. This study aimed to explore the different influencing mechanisms of dispositional and contextual ethical sensitivity (CES) in medical students’ ethical decision-making. Based on Rest’s four-stage ethical decision-making model, this research highlights the importance of ethical sensitivity in ethical decision-making. Using the Credamo platform, we generated a questionnaire link and randomly selected 678 medical students from 3 cities in China as our research sample. We constructed a mediation model for a comparative study to analyze the mediating roles of ethical judgment in the relationship between dispositional ethical sensitivity/CES and ethical intention. The results showed that medical students’ dispositional ethical sensitivity positively affects their ethical intentions, and CES negatively affects their ethical intentions; the relationship between dispositional/CES and ethical intention is mediated by ethical judgment. Our research indicates that medical schools ought to implement a variety of strategies to enhance the ethical sensitivity of their students, thereby improving their capacity to make ethical decisions. These findings hold important implications for the progression of medical ethics education.

1. Introduction

1.1. Background

Physicians often face ethical dilemmas in their work, which may lead to becoming morally numb to frequent clinical ethical dilemmas.[1] Ethical sensitivity is the ability to perceive and understand the ethical issues contained in work contexts, which is the initial psychological component before the occurrence of ethical behavior.[2] Medical students, as a reserve force for physicians, face similar tests of ethical decision-making.[3] Therefore, they must possess a high level of ethical sensitivity to address the ethical tests they may encounter in their future work.

To ensure that medical students can make appropriate ethical decisions in professional practice, current medical education should focus on cultivating and enhancing their ethical sensitivity. This approach will help students develop the necessary sensitivity and coping skills as they progress in their learning. By guiding medical students to confront ethical issues in their work and boosting their ethical sensitivity, they can be better motivated to adhere to ethical norms and engage in morally upright behaviors.[4]

Rest initially introduced the four-stage model for ethical decision-making.[5] He suggested that there are 4 stages leading up to engagement in ethical behavior: ethical sensitivity (recognizing the scenario), ethical judgment (judging whether the behavior is ethically right or not), ethical intention (choosing which behavior to enact), and ethical behavior (carrying out the ethical action). Although he indicated that while the 4 stages of ethical decision-making follow a clear logical sequence, they may not always occur in a predetermined chronological order in practice. This is because there are complex feedback loops and interactions among them. However, few studies have investigated medical students’ ethical decision-making through the lens of the four-component model. Consequently, the relationship between the stages of ethical decision-making among medical students remains unclear. To effectively enhance the ethical behavior of medical students, it is crucial to clarify the logical relationships between these stages.

Moreover, Zheng noted that ethical sensitivity is an important part of the ethical decision-making process.[6] It contains 2 important components: dispositional ethical sensitivity (DES) and contextual ethical sensitivity (CES). DES is the tendency shown by an individual’s attitude toward ethical issues. CES is a realistic, ethical psychological ability. In terms of the relationship between the 2, the former is the foundation, and the latter is the expression.[7] However, previous research on ethical decision-making has focused predominantly on specific scenarios, primarily examining the influence of CES in this process. In contrast, the impact of static DES on ethical decision-making has been largely overlooked. Thus, what kind of relationship exists between general DES and the other variables in ethical decision-making contexts? Consequently, distinguishing the varying effects of these types of ethical sensitivity in ethical decision-making is necessary.

1.2. Objectives

Based on Rest’s four-stage ethical decision-making model, this research aims to explore the different ways in which DES/CES influences the ethical decision-making processes among medical students, as well as the mediating role of ethical judgment.

1.3. The relationship between ethical sensitivity and ethical intention

Rest first put forward the four-stage model of ethical decision-making. Wotruba reported that these stages typically occur sequentially.[5] Specifically, individuals must first identify ethical problems, then make ethical judgments, followed by formulating ethical intentions, and finally implement ethical behavior.[8]

As per the Rest four-stage model of ethical decision-making, Rest believes that individuals must be aware of the existence of ethical issues.[5] He also considers it necessary to take action on ethical issues before the next 3 processes occur. After recognizing an ethical issue, an individual will make an ethical judgment. Ethical judgment is the decision to determine what is right and what is wrong.[5] After making an ethical judgment, the individual will develop an ethical intention. That is the individual decision to perform or not to perform the action. Ethical intention is the direct determinant of an individual’s ethical behavior. After the formation of ethical intention, the individual will transform ethical intention into ethical behavior.[5]

Ethical sensitivity refers to the ability of an individual to identify ethical issues in a certain context. It is also the awareness of the ethical content of the contexts and the awareness of how his or her behavior affects others. That is the sensitive realization that “this is an ethical issue.” It includes the ability to choose and empathize with the views of the parties involved as to how various actions affect them. It also includes the ability to imagine the cause and effect of events or to take into account specific ethical norms or principles that can be applied to the context.[5] Therefore, one must first be aware of the ethical implications of a context, and only then can one confirm the roles of all parties in the context and the influences they receive.[9]

Hunt suggested that the perception and identification of ethical issues (ethical sensitivity) are prerequisites for the generation of ethical intentions.[10] Singhapakdi empirical research demonstrated a strong connection between ethical sensitivity and ethical intention.[11] Furthermore, Haines et al research found that the perception and recognition of the importance of ethical issues are predictive variables for ethical intentions.[12] Milliken noted that ethical sensitivity is a crucial factor that fosters ethical behavior.[13] Lee asserted that ethical sensitivity serves as a prerequisite for ethical behavior.[14] Chen Qin indicated that an increased level of ethical sensitivity enhances one’s ability to discern the ethical dimensions of situations and facilitates more rational ethical behavior.[15]

Thus, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H1a: Medical students’ DES positively affects their ethical intention.

H1b: Medical students’ CES positively affects their ethical intention.

1.4. The mediating role of ethical judgment

Many studies have shown that ethical sensitivity has an important influence on ethical judgment. Once an individual becomes aware of the existence of ethical issues, he will make ethical judgments.[16] Jordan has also confirmed that ethical sensitivity has a positive effect on ethical judgment.[17] Thus, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H2a: Medical students’ DES has a significant positive impact on their ethical judgment.

H2b: Medical students’ CES has a significant positive impact on their ethical judgment.

Furthermore, ethical judgments lead to ethical intentions.[18,19] Several studies have shown that ethical judgment positively affects ethical intention.[16,19–21] Sweeney conducted an experimental study on accounting students and found that ethical judgment positively affects ethical intention.[20] Specifically, the more one can judge that the behavior in a context is ethical, the more the same behavioral intention will be generated. Furthermore, Wu identified a notable positive relationship between ethical judgment and ethical intention. In particular, the greater an individual’s ability to recognize an unethical decision, the lower the likelihood of that person intending to participate in the same conduct.[21] Consequently, the subsequent hypothesis is put forth:

H3: Medical students’ ethical judgment positively affects their ethical intention.

Therefore, ethical sensitivity can help individuals recognize ethical issues, make better ethical judgments, and ultimately enact more ethical intentions. In other words, ethical judgment plays a mediating role between ethical sensitivity and ethical intention.[22] Thus, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H4a: Ethical judgment mediates the relationship between DES and ethical intention.

H4b: Ethical judgment mediates the relationship between CES and ethical intention.

2. Methods

2.1. Ethical considerations

Prior to initiating the investigation, we sought ethical approval from the Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research at the Medical College of Hebei University of Engineering (No. 2023[K]030-20), which was granted. All participants were provided with an information sheet detailing the research, and the questionnaire was performed after obtaining informed consent from all the participants involved in the study.

2.2. Study design

This study employed a quantitative design and survey research methodology. A simple random sampling approach was utilized, with 3 medical colleges randomly selected from Hebei, Guangxi, and Guangzhou. Medical students were then randomly chosen from these 3 institutions.

2.3. Setting

Between January and March 2024, the third author, aided by the teacher, sent out survey questionnaires to medical students through the Credamo platform. This platform is designed specifically for professional data collection in China. The introductory page of the survey detailed the objectives of the study, as well as its potential risks and benefits. Students were informed that the survey purpose was strictly academic. Participation was entirely voluntary, and no personal information was gathered. Participants filled out all questionnaires in the classroom, which the third author collected after they were completed. Finally, 678 valid questionnaires were eventually collected, for an effective recovery rate of 86.9%.

2.4. Measurement

All the scales utilized mature questionnaires that demonstrated strong reliability and validity. All items related to the variables in the study have been translated and back-translated following the procedures proposed by Brislin to ensure the accuracy of the Chinese measurement items.[23]

DES. Medical students’ DES was measured using the dispositional ethical sensitivity questionnaire compiled by Zheng.[7] The survey included 28 items rated on a five-point scale (from 1 “strongly disagree” to 5 “strongly agree”). A higher score indicates better identification of ethical issues and stronger sensitivity. The Cronbach α in this study was 0.952.

As in many previous studies,[22,24] each scenario included 4 related questions designed to assess the stages of ethical decision-making, namely, CES, contextual ethical judgment, and contextual ethical intention. Participants were requested to express their degree of agreement on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1, indicating “strongly agree,” to 5 representing “strongly disagree.”

The scenarios employed in our study were adapted from the literature.[25] Because the use of contexts allows researchers to show special decision contexts that are more like real life, researchers usually combine contexts with well-developed scales to study individual ethical decision-making processes.[11,26,27] Using questionnaires can provide standardized social stimuli to different participants and make decision-making scenarios more realistic.[28] This approach helps us obtain high-quality data.[29]

To reduce social desirability bias, we included an actor in the scenario instead of asking the participants to act as ethical decision-makers.[30] Moreover, to reduce the reaction bias caused by scenarios that are too long, all the scenarios were limited to 50 to 100 words.[27]

CES. Medical students’ CES was measured using a questionnaire adapted from Singhapakdi and Barnett to allow participants to indicate the degree of consent to the item “the decision-making scenario involves ethical issues.”[18,31,32] The higher the score for this item is, the stronger the respondent’s ethical sensitivity. The Cronbach α in this study was 0.853.

As both ethical judgment and ethical intention are evaluated within particular contexts, the terms contextual ethical judgment and contextual ethical intention will be referred to simply as ethical judgment and ethical intention in the subsequent text.

Ethical judgment. Medical students’ ethical judgment was measured using a questionnaire adapted from Morris.[33] The scale allows participants to indicate the degree of consent to the item “the behavior is ethical.” The higher the score, the more the participant considered the ethical dilemma to be ethical. The Cronbach α in this study was 0.878.

Ethical intention. Medical students’ ethical intention was measured using a questionnaire adapted from Singhapakdi.[31] The items are “In this scenario, I would do the same” and ‘In this scenario, my colleagues around me would do the same’. The average score of the 2 items was used to represent the ethical behavior intention of the subjects. The higher the score is, the stronger the subject’s intention to perform the behavior. The Cronbach α in this study was 0.933.

2.5. Statistical methods

Data analysis was carried out with the assistance of SPSS 27.0 and Mplus 8.0. The statistical methods implemented included the following steps: Initially, the common method bias test was executed through SPSS 27.0. Subsequently, confirmatory factor analysis was conducted using Mplus 8.0. Next, SPSS 27.0 was employed once more for conducting descriptive statistical analysis and correlation analysis. Lastly, hypotheses were tested via structural equation modeling utilizing Mplus 8.0.

3. Results

3.1. Participants

Among the respondents who participated in the study, 391 were male, and 287 were female, accounting for 57.6% and 42.4%, respectively. Regarding whether they were only children, 148 persons were only children, and 530 were not. Regarding the place of birth, 502 were from rural areas, 84 were from cities, and 92 were from towns. Regarding whether they had student cadre experience, 472 respondents had student cadre experience, and 206 respondents did not.

3.2. Common method bias test

In the process of editing and distributing the questionnaire, emphasis was placed on ensuring the anonymity of the respondents and randomizing the sequence of the items. This approach seeks to prevent uniform thinking among subjects during the completion of the questionnaire and to manage common method bias. Subsequently, to assess the extent of potential common method bias, the Harman one-way test was employed to examine the variations. The initial principal component analysis accounted for 29.238% of the variation (below the 40% threshold), suggesting that significant common method variance was not present in this research.[34]

3.3. Confirmatory factor analysis

In this study, Mplus 8.0 was used to process the data, and a competition model was constructed using 4 key variables: DES, CES, ethical judgment, and ethical intention to test the validity of the model. The results are shown in Table 1. Compared with the other models, the four-factor model (DES, CES, ethical judgment, and ethical intention) had the best fit (χ2/df = 4.956, root mean square error of approximation = 0.076, Comparative Fit Index = 0.986, Tucker–Lewis index = 0.975, standardized root mean square residual = 0.025). It satisfied the statistical requirements and was better than the other models, indicating that the above variables have good discriminant validity.

3.5. Main results

Initially, we examined DES’s role in ethical decision-making processes. Once we adjusted for all control variables, we found that DES significantly enhanced ethical intention (b = 0.277, standard error [SE] = 0.034, P < .001), thereby supporting Hypothesis 1a.

The findings from the structural equation modeling are illustrated in Figure 2. DES was found to have a positive impact on ethical judgment (b = 0.265, SE = 0.066, P < .001), which in turn supported Hypothesis 2a. Furthermore, ethical judgment positively influenced ethical intention (b = 0.790, SE = 0.028, P < .001), thus confirming Hypothesis 3.

Results of the multilevel path analysis.

Furthermore, the bootstrap procedure was employed to determine the 95% confidence interval for the mediating effect to evaluate its influence. A total of 5000 resamples were conducted. The findings indicate that DES significantly indirectly impacts medical students’ ethical intentions via ethical judgment (b = 0.206, SE = 0.054, P < .05, 95% confidence Interval = (0.102, 0.315)), with the confidence interval not including 0. Therefore, Hypothesis 4a was supported. After controlling for the ethical judgement, the direct effect of DES on ethical intention was significant (b = 0.068, P < .05). Thus, ethical judgement plays a partially mediating role between DES and ethical intention. Thus, H4a was supported.

We subsequently examined how the CES of medical students influenced their ethical decision-making processes. The findings indicated that medical students’ CES significantly negatively impacted their ethical intentions (b = −0.449, SE = 0.036, P < .001). Thus, Hypothesis 1b was not supported.

As shown in Figure 2, CES negatively affects ethical judgment (b = −0.485, SE = 0.055, P < .001), and Hypothesis 2b was not supported. Ethical judgment positively affects ethical intention (b = 0.790, SE = 0.028, P < .001), and Hypothesis 3 was supported.

Furthermore, the bootstrap technique was employed to determine the 95% confidence interval for the mediating effect, facilitating the assessment of this effect. A total of 5000 repeated samples were analyzed. The findings indicate that CES significantly influences medical students’ ethical intentions indirectly via ethical judgment (b = −0.383, SE = 0.043, P < .001, 95% confidence Interval = [−0.468, −0.298]), with the confidence interval not encompassing 0. Consequently, Hypothesis 4b is validated. After controlling for the ethical judgment, the direct effect of CES on ethical intention was significant (b = −0.066, P < .05). Thus, ethical judgment plays a partially mediating role between CES and ethical intention. Thus, H4a was supported.

4. Discussion

4.1. Key results

This study is based on Rest’s four-component model of ethical decision-making and takes medical students as the research objects. It aims to explore the different influencing mechanisms of DES/CES on the ethical decision-making process of medical students by using ethical judgment as the mediating variable. The results show that medical students’ DES positively affects their ethical intentions, and CES negatively affects their ethical intentions. The relationship between dispositional/CES and ethical intention is mediated by ethical judgment.

4.2. Interpretation

First, medical students’ DES has a positive impact on their ethical intentions. This is consistent with the results of Chen Qin et al[15] He found that the greater the respondent’s level of ethical sensitivity, the more keenly he was able to perceive whether the issue itself was ethical, and thus, the greater the ability to act ethically. This also corresponds to the results of Kim and Choe.[35,36] He found that if we cannot sensitively detect ethical issues, it will be impossible to achieve positive ethical behavior naturally.

Ethical judgment mediated the relationship between DES and ethical intention. It shows that medical students with greater ethical sensitivity are better able to make correct ethical judgments and tend to engage in more ethical behaviors. It further confirms the Rest ethical decision-making model and aligns with the findings of previous research.[22,24]

Second, medical students’ CES has a negative impact on their ethical intentions. This may be due to the intensity of ethical issues. CES refers to an individual’s ability to perceive ethically relevant contexts and their ability to recognize and understand ethical issues. It is not an absolutely stable psychological ability but is context-specific and unstable.[2] The intensity of ethical issues refers to the severity and urgency of an ethical issue or ethical dilemma.[37] Some ethical issues may involve life and death, major injustices, or deep ethical conflicts, while others may be relatively minor or less urgent. This may affect an individual’s ethical sensitivity, as more intense ethical issues may be more easily perceived and understood by the individual.[37] Thus, it stimulates deeper ethical reflection and judgment as well as generates ethical intentions.[37] Hence, the intensity of ethical issues may affect medical students’ CES. In addition, they may make different ethical judgments and form different ethical intentions when faced with different ethical decision-making dilemmas.

Ethical judgment mediated the relationship between CES and ethical intention. Although CES negatively affects ethical judgment, ethical judgment positively affects ethical intention. This further validates the complexity of the Rest four-component model: “Although the 4 components have a more obvious logical order in process, they are not necessarily presented in a fixed chronological order in reality. This is because there are complex feedback loops and interactions among them.”[5]

In addition, the social information processing theory suggests that a person’s activities are shaped by complex external factors in the social environment. Individual perceptions, attitudes, and behaviors are developed based on the information they receive. Furthermore, interpreting and processing this information has a significant impact on subsequent behavior.[38] The complex scenarios are packed with a lot of information, which may encompass factors irrelevant to morality and even misleading ones. When medical students attempt to process this information, they are likely to be distracted by the irrelevant factors and overlook ethical principles. Meanwhile, ethical judgment is influenced by an individual’s internal ethical values. These internal values act as a filter and guide during the information processing stages; thus, they can help medical students prioritize ethical considerations even in the face of overwhelming information.

Finally, in the present study, the effects of DES and CES on ethical judgment were both significant but in opposite directions. This may be because when medical students encounter various scenarios, they tend to become more reflective and cautious. It may diminish their ethical intentions and potentially increase unethical behavior.[39] Warner’s study suggested that intuition and spontaneous decision-making can lead to stronger ethical intentions.[40] Therefore, identifying ethical issues in specific scenarios and then going through a process of careful consideration may ultimately reduce the medical students’ ethical intentions.

4.3. Contributions to literature

First, we distinguished between 2 different kinds of ethical sensitivities.[6] We examined the relationship and influencing mechanism between the 2 different ethical sensitivities and ethical intentions separately. This is helpful for further detailed study of medical students’ ethical sensitivity. This study also provides theoretical guidance and support for cultivating medical students’ ethical sensitivity and urging them to engage in more ethical behaviors.

Second, the existing empirical studies all used the Rest four-component model as the default theoretical model.[41] They tested the influences of the specific personal, organizational, and ethical issue intensity variables on the decision-making model based on the theory itself. Few studies have investigated the theoretical model itself and the relationships between the components inside the model.[42]

This study used medical students as research subjects to study the interrelationship between the internal components of the model. It fills the gap in the existing research and shifts the focus from external factors to the interrelationship between the internal components of the model. This study reveals the intrinsic links among medical students’ ethical sensitivity, ethical judgment, and ethical intention. It also shows how these components interact and affect medical students’ ethical decision-making. The results expand the theoretical framework of medical students’ ethical sensitivity and enrich the relevant research on medical students’ ethical decision-making theory.

4.4. Practical implications

First, the results show that medical students’ DES has a positive impact on their ethical intentions. This study provides some suggestions for medical ethics education to improve medical students’ ethical sensitivity and thus prompts them to engage in more ethical behaviors.

Ethical sensitivity is a key element of ethical cognition. It is also the ability to recognize and understand ethical issues, which can be improved through systematic training and education.[43] Medical colleges should emphasize ethical education through systematic and comprehensive ethical education courses. These courses cover medical ethical principles, ethical decision-making, codes of professional conduct, etc, and introduce interdisciplinary educational content, such as ethics and psychology. It will help students to comprehensively understand ethical issues and master relevant decision-making tools and skills.

Additionally, Zhang research indicates that an organization ethical climate can have a positive impact on ethical sensitivity.[44] Medical colleges can enhance ethical awareness and ethical behavior by organizing campus cultural activities, such as lectures, seminars, and cultural festivals, to promote positive values and ethical standards, and to build an excellent campus culture.

Second, the results show that in both the DES pathway and the CES pathway, ethical judgment positively affects ethical intention. This study provides theoretical guidance for prompting medical students to engage in more ethical behaviors and points out a direction for their career development. Greene research has shown that emotions can influence ethical judgment.[45] Therefore, medical schools should offer courses related to emotional management, aimed at enhancing students’ self-awareness,emotional recognition, emotional regulation, and stress management skills. It can help them to improve their emotional intelligence, thereby improving their ethical judgment abilities.

Furthermore, studies have indicated that stress can also affect an individual’s ethical judgment, such as time pressure and social pressure.[46] Medical colleges can offer relevant courses aimed at educating students on how to effectively manage their time, including setting priorities, goals, and time planning, to improve study efficiency and reduce stress caused by time constraints. Additionally, mental health education should be strengthened to help medical students understand the sources of stress and learn methods to cope with it, thereby enhancing their self-regulation abilities and ethical judgment abilities.

5. Limitations

This study adopts the methods of previous empirical research, focusing on variables and exploring the interrelationships among them. Although theoretically appealing, it is overly simplified for managers in practice.[47] Future research could consider employing latent profile analysis, which is centered on individuals and focuses on the heterogeneity among individuals. This approach ensures that differences between categories are maximized and differences within categories are minimized, providing important information for behavioral interventions.[48]

Second, this study used the scenario research method widely used in the field of ethical decision-making to measure CES, ethical judgment, and ethical intention. However, the study did not adequately consider the consistency of the ethical intensity of the contexts set up, nor did it specifically measure the ethical intensity of each context.[37]

In future research, there is a need to include a measure of ethical issue strength to consider the effect of ethical issue strength on ethical judgment and ethical intention. This will help to more accurately assess the role of situational factors in the ethical decision-making process, thus providing more insight into medical education and ethical development.

Finally, the participants in the study were mainly from Guangxi Province. Medical students in other provinces were not investigated. In future studies, consideration should be given to expanding the scope and number of samples to cover medical students from more regions. Furthermore, there remain significant socio-cultural elements that we haven’t considered. Consequently, focusing on additional aspects, like elements of Chinese culture, represents another avenue for future research.

6. Conclusions

The results show that medical students’ DES positively affects their ethical intentions, and CES negatively affects their ethical intentions. The relationship between dispositional/CES and ethical intention is mediated by ethical judgment. The findings of this study enhance the theoretical framework regarding medical students’ ethical sensitivity through the lens of ethical decision-making. Additionally, this research offers a theoretical foundation and direction for fostering ethical sensitivity among medical students, encouraging them to adopt more ethical behaviors in practical scenarios.
