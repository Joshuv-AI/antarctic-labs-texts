New persistent plant RNA virus carries mutations to weaken viral suppression of antiviral RNA interference
Authors: Li‐Juan Zhu, Yu Zhu, Chengwu Zou, Lan‐Yi Su, Chong‐Tao Zhang, Chi Wang, Ya‐Ni Bai, Baoshan Chen, Rongbai Li, Qingfa Wu, Shou‐Wei Ding, Jian‐Guo Wu, Yan‐Hong Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent plant viruses are widespread in natural ecosystems. However, little is known about why persistent infection with these viruses may cause little or no harm to their host. Here, we discovered a new polerovirus that persistently infected wild rice plants by deep sequencing and assembly of virus‐derived small‐interfering RNAs (siRNAs). The new virus was named Rice tiller inhibition virus 2 (RTIV2) based on the symptoms developed in cultivated rice varieties following Agrobacterium‐mediated inoculation with an infectious RTIV2 clone. We showed that RTIV2 infection induced antiviral RNA interference (RNAi) in both the wild and cultivated rice plants as well as Nicotiana benthamiana. It is known that virulent virus infection in plants depends on effective suppression of antiviral RNAi by viral suppressors of RNAi (VSRs). Notably, the P0 protein of RTIV2 exhibited weak VSR activity and carries alanine substitutions of two amino acids broadly conserved among diverse poleroviruses. Mixed infection with umbraviruses enhanced RTIV2 accumulation and/or enabled its mechanical transmission in N. benthamiana. Moreover, replacing the alanine at either one or both positions of RTIV2 P0 enhanced the VSR activity in a co‐infiltration assay, and RTIV2 mutants carrying the corresponding substitutions replicated to significantly higher levels in both rice and N. benthamiana plants. Together, our findings show that as a persistent plant virus, RTIV2 carries specific mutations in its VSR gene to weaken viral suppression of antiviral RNAi. Our work reveals a new strategy for persistent viruses to maintain long‐term infection by weak suppression of the host defence response.Keywords: antiviral RNAi, new polerovirus, P0 protein, persistent RNA virus, synergistic infection

INTRODUCTION

Persistent infection, wherein a pathogen is continually present in a host individual asymptomatically or cryptically, is widespread in virus–host systems (Goic & Saleh, 2012; Honjo et al., 2020; Pagan et al., 2010; Prendeville et al., 2012; Roossinck, 2012). However, the mechanism of such persistence is not yet fully understood. Emerging and re‐emerging plant viruses pose significant threats to various economically important crops (Ertunc, 2020; Martelli & Gallitelli, 2008). The advent of high‐throughput sequencing (HTS) technologies, also known as next‐generation sequencing, has revolutionized plant virus diagnosis (Maree et al., 2018; Villamor et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2010, 2024). Wild rice, a perennial plant, serves as a potential reservoir for novel endemic viruses that can subsequently incite epidemics in cultivated rice, a crop of paramount importance in China (Yan et al., 2023). Using HTS, a novel polerovirus, rice tiller inhibition virus (RTIV), has been identified from wild rice and shown to establish virulent systemic infection in cultivated rice (Yan et al., 2023).

Poleroviruses are economically important plant pathogens affecting cereal, vegetable, and fruit crops, leading to substantial yield and quality losses globally. The protein P0, encoded by ORF0 of poleroviruses, has been identified as a viral suppressor of RNA silencing (VSR) in many poleroviruses (Agrofoglio et al., 2019; Akinyuwa & Kang, 2024; Almasi et al., 2015; Barrios Barón et al., 2021; Baumberger et al., 2007; Bortolamiol et al., 2007; Cascardo et al., 2015; Delfosse et al., 2014; Dou et al., 2022; Filardo & Sharman, 2019; Fusaro et al., 2012; Goodin et al., 2002; Han et al., 2010; Johansen & Carrington, 2001; Kozlowska‐Makulska et al., 2010; Li et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2012; Mangwende et al., 2009; Qiao et al., 2023; Sun et al., 2020; Voinnet et al., 2000; Wang et al., 2018; Zhuo et al., 2014). Several regions within P0 are essential for the silencing suppression activity, including a consensus F‐box‐like motif (LPXX(L/I)X10–13P) and the Phe/Trp (FW) residues within the C‐terminal (Delfosse et al., 2021). The amino acid sequence of P0 proteins exhibits considerable sequence variability within the Polerovirus genus, resulting in differential efficacies in RNA silencing suppression (Cai et al., 2023; Han et al., 2010; Mangwende et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2015, 2021, 2023). P0 plays a critical role in subverting host antiviral defence, specifically by mediating the degradation of Argonaute protein (Honjo et al., 2020), a pivotal component in antiviral RNA silencing (Clavel et al., 2021; Pazhouhandeh et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2015).

Poleroviruses are restricted to the vascular phloem tissues of their host plants (Mayo & Ziegler‐Graff, 1996; Taliansky et al., 2003) and are typically not transmissible through mechanical means, such as rubbing plants with virus particles (Nurkiyanova et al., 2000; Peter et al., 2009). Poleroviruses are generally transmitted by aphids in a persistent, circulative, and non‐propagative manner (Esau & Hoefert, 1972; Gray & Gildow, 2003), except for a newly reported whitefly‐transmitted pepper polerovirus (Ghosh et al., 2019). Viral synergism often occurs from the co‐infection of two unrelated viruses within the same host, leading to more severe symptoms, elevated titres of one or both viruses (Pruss et al., 1997; Syller, 2012; Xia et al., 2016), modifications in tissue tropism (Ryabov et al., 2001), host range (García‐Cano et al., 2006) and/or transmission rate (Li et al., 2014). Increasing evidence indicates that viral synergy between phloem‐restricted poleroviruses and umbraviruses is prevalent, with devastating effects on crop plants (Gao & Simon, 2016; Taliansky et al., 2000). When co‐infected with umbraviruses, poleroviruses can overcome phloem restriction, disseminate extensively in non‐vascular tissues, and initiate systemic infection by mechanical inoculation (Mayo et al., 2000; Robinson et al., 1999; Ryabov et al., 2001; Zhou et al., 2017). For instance, co‐infection of poleroviruses potato leafroll virus (PLRV) or brassica yellows virus (BrYV) with the umbravirus pea enation mosaic virus 2 (PEMV2) results in more severe symptoms, with enhanced BrYV accumulation in Nicotiana benthamiana plants, and allows PLRV and BrYV to infect mesophyll tissues and to initiate infection by mechanical inoculation (Mayo et al., 2000; Ryabov et al., 2001; Zhou et al., 2017). In addition, co‐infection of PLRV with carrot mottle virus (CMoV) can increase the titre of PLRV (Barker, 1987), and beet western yellows virus (BWYV) becomes mechanically transmissible in the presence of lettuce mottle virus (LeMoV) (Falk et al., 1979).

In this study, we identified a new species of positive‐strand RNA virus, designated Rice tiller inhibition virus 2 (RTIV2), using virus discovery by deep sequencing and assembly of virus‐derived small silencing RNAs (vdSAR; Wu et al., 2010). We successfully rescued RTIV2 from an infectious cDNA clone and demonstrated systemic symptomatic infection of rice cultivars by RTIV2 following Agrobacterium‐mediated inoculation. Furthermore, we observed that co‐infection of RTIV2 with PEMV2 or tobacco bushy top virus (TBTV) led to increased accumulation of RTIV2 in N. benthamiana plants and that RTIV2 infection was established by mechanical inoculation in the presence of PEMV2. Moreover, we identified P0 of RTIV2 as a weak VSR that carries alanine substitutions of two amino acids broadly conserved among poleroviruses. Replacing the alanine at either one or both positions of RTIV2 P0 enhanced the VSR activity, and RTIV2 mutants carrying the corresponding substitutions replicated to much higher levels in both rice and N. benthamiana plants. Together, our findings show that as a persistent plant virus, RTIV2 carries specific mutations in its VSR gene to weaken viral suppression of antiviral RNAi. Our work reveals a new strategy for persistent viruses to maintain long‐term infection by weak suppression of the host defence response.

RESULTS

Virus discovery from wild rice

Guangxi University in Nanning, China, houses a genetic reserve of wild rice accessions collected across China that have been vegetatively maintained in net houses since 1999 (Li et al., 2010). Based on the deep sequencing of large and small RNAs from 1000 accessions of wild rice, we focused on one accession with a combination of suspected polerovirus and umbravirus and resequenced total small RNAs from these plants. After trimming adapter sequences, we obtained a total of 29,067,612 high‐quality reads ranging from 18 to 30 nucleotides that were subsequently assembled into contigs by vdSAR. BLAST searches revealed the presence of multiple contigs with high sequence similarity to Pa nicum distortion mosaic virus (PDMV) from the genus Polerovirus in the Solemoviridae family. Based on the sequences of the assembled contigs, primers (Table S1) were designed, and the full genomic sequence was obtained by rapid amplification of cDNA ends (RACE) to determine the viral 5′ and 3′ terminal sequences (Figure S1a,b) combined with Sanger sequencing of overlapping fragments.

The results revealed that the viral genome is 5822 nucleotides (nt) in length, encoding seven open reading frames (ORFs) consistent with known poleroviruses (Sõmera et al., 2021) (Figure 1a). The virus was designated as Rice tiller inhibition virus 2 (RTIV2) based on the disease symptoms induced in cultivated rice plants (see below in Figure 3g), and the sequence was deposited into the NCBI GenBank database under accession number OM835626. Further phylogenetic analysis of the viral RNA‐dependent RNA polymerases (RdRP) demonstrated that RTIV2 is closely related to PDMV (Figure 1b). Sequence identity comparisons of individual RTIV2 proteins and their genomic nucleotide sequences to known poleroviruses showed that RTIV2 and PDMV exhibited the highest sequence identities, ranging from 51.2% to 84.2% (Table 1), which is below the new virus demarcation criterion of 90% homology in the genus Polerovirus. Thus, we propose Rice tiller inhibition virus 2 as a new species in the genus Polerovirus.

Total RNAs were further extracted from three individual plants from the resequenced accession for northern blot detection of RTIV2 high and low molecular weight RNAs. RTIV2 virus‐derived small interfering RNAs (vsiRNAs) in the size range of 21–22 nt accumulated to readily detectable levels in all three wild rice plants (Figure 1c). Based on the positions of RNA markers, the largest and most abundant RNA molecule detected in these plants was approximately 5800 nt in length, likely corresponding to the full‐length genomic RNA of RTIV2 (Figure 1c). A smaller viral RNA species detected in wild rice plants was identified as the 2388‐nt subgenomic RNA (sgRNA) through 5′ RACE and northern blot hybridization with region‐specific probes (nt 3451–4120 or 156–899) (Figure 1c, Figure S1c,d). These results demonstrate production of both the viral genomic and subgenomic RNAs in individual wild rice plants, indicating an active RTIV2 infection of the Asian wild rice colony.

The total small RNA reads from resequenced wild rice mapped to the full‐length genome of RTIV2 displayed strong size preference for 21 and 22 nt, with a minor peak at 20 nt (Figure 1d). This size distribution pattern is typical of vsiRNAs produced by a plant antiviral RNA interference (RNAi) response to RNA virus infection (Guo et al., 2019). Furthermore, the full‐length genomic RNA of RTIV2 was targeted by both positive‐ and negative‐strand vsiRNAs, with RTIV2 vsiRNAs exhibiting approximately equal ratios of positive and negative strands (Figure 1d). These properties of RTIV2 vsiRNAs are consistent with the model of plant vsiRNA biogenesis (Guo et al., 2019), in which viral long double‐stranded RNA (dsRNA) precursors are processed into 21‐ and 22‐nt vsiRNAs by Dicer‐like 4 (DCL4) and DCL2, respectively.

Together, our findings discovered a new polerovirus that established a persistent infection in wild rice colonies maintained vegetatively for decades in the net house.

Infectivity and pathogenicity of RTIV2 infectious clone in N. benthamiana

An infectious cDNA clone of RTIV2 was developed to verify its infectivity and pathogenesis. The full‐length cDNA of the RTIV2 RNA genome was cloned between a CaMV 35S promoter and a ribozyme in the plasmid vector pXT, yielding the binary plasmid, pXT‐RTIV2. To test the infectivity of pXT‐RTIV2, leaves of both wild‐type (WT) and DCL2‐ and DCL4‐RNAi knockdown N. benthamiana plants (DCL2/4i) were inoculated by leaf infiltration with Agrobacterium tumefaciens that carried pXT‐RTIV2.

In the inoculated leaves of DCL2/4i plants, agroinfiltration with pXT‐RTIV2 caused chlorosis by 5 days post‐inoculation (dpi), which gradually developed into necrosis. The inoculated leaves of WT plants also developed similar symptoms with a delay of 3–5 days (Figure 2a). No obvious symptoms were observed in the upper non‐inoculated leaves of either WT or DCL2/4i plants inoculated with RTIV2 (Figure 2a,b). Total RNA was extracted from upper leaves at 15 and 28 dpi for reverse transcription (RT)‐PCR, which detected the expected PCR fragments in both WT and DCL2/4i plants (Figure S2). Northern blot analysis revealed that the viral RNAs accumulated to the highest level at 30 dpi in the upper non‐inoculated leaves of DCL2/4i plants (Figure 2e). By comparison, RTIV2 genomic and subgenomic RNAs as well as its vsiRNAs were readily detectable at 28 dpi in the upper leaves of DCL2/4i plants but not WT plants (Figure 2c). Further RT‐quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR) analysis showed that RTIV2 replicated to 1000 times higher levels in DCL2/4i plants than WT plants (Figure 2d). These results indicate that the cloned RTIV2 is infectious and develops systemic infection that is potently suppressed by antiviral RNAi in N. benthamiana plants.

Infectivity and pathogenicity of RTIV2 infectious clone in cultivated rice

To determine whether the cloned RTIV2 can infect cultivated rice, two lines of rice (Oryza sativa subsp. japonica ‘Nipponbare’ and O. sativa subsp. indica ‘9311’) were inoculated using an Agrobacterium‐mediated inoculation procedure. Total RNA from the upper non‐inoculated leaves was extracted at different time points for northern blot detection of RTIV2 genomic and subgenomic RNAs (Figure 3a,b). In Nipponbare, viral RNAs became detectable at 30 dpi, and virus accumulation decreased afterward (Figure 3a). In line 9311 plants, however, RTIV2 replicated to detectable levels at earlier time points and remained at similarly high levels for approximately 1 month (Figure 3b). Both vsiRNAs (Figure 3c) and coat protein (CP) (Figure 3d) were readily detectable in the non‐infiltrated leaves of Nipponbare and line 9311 plants at 44 dpi, indicating systemic infection of both rice cultivars by RTIV2.

We named this virus as RTIV2 because, like RTIV (Yan et al., 2023), RTIV2 infection in both Nipponbare and 9311 plants caused reduced tillering, an important trait for rice yield (Figure 2g). In addition, RTIV2‐infected 9311 plants displayed dwarf symptoms (Figure 3g). The earlier high concentration of the virus may explain the more severe symptoms in 9311 compared to Nipponbare. We sequenced total small RNAs in symptomatic Nipponbare and 9311 plants infected with RTIV2. The Nipponbare and 9311 small RNA libraries contained 25,279,901 and 24,379,655 reads, respectively, 1,867,637 (about 7.4%) and 368,202 (about 1.5%) of which mapped to the RTIV2 genome. The size distribution and strand ratio of the RTIV2 vsiRNAs from the infected Nipponbare and 9311 plants (Figure 3e,f) were similar to those from the infected wild rice plants (Figure 1d). In both cases, the majority of small RNAs of both polarities were in the size range from 20 to 22 nt, with a predominant peak at 21 nt (Figure 3e,f). Analysis of 5′‐terminal nucleotides of vsiRNAs showed uridine (U) as the most abundant in RTIV2‐infected Nipponbare, while no obvious preference was observed in 9311 plants (Figure 3e,f). These findings demonstrate that the persistent virus isolated from wild rice can establish virulent systemic infection in two cultivars of the Asian cultivated rice species, indicating that native plants harbour an endemic virus with the potential to become a new pathogen in cultivated rice (O. sativa).

Mixed infection with umbraviruses enhanced RTIV2 accumulation

We next investigated whether the weak systemic infection of N. benthamiana by RTIV2 could be enhanced by co‐infection with an unrelated virus, which often induces virus synergism because of more efficient RNAi suppression. To this end, N. benthamiana plants were agroinfiltrated singly with an empty vector (EV), RTIV2, TBTV, or PEMV2 and in combination with RTIV2 + TBTV or RTIV2 + PEMV2. Co‐infiltration of RTIV2 + TBTV or RTIV2 + PEMV2 induced necrotic symptoms earlier than RTIV2 alone in inoculated leaves of both WT and DCL2/4i plants (Figure 4a). Co‐infiltration of RTIV2 with either PEMV2 or TBTV caused more severe symptoms than RTIV2 alone in both N. benthamiana WT and DCL2/4i, but the symptoms were due to PEMV2 or TBTV (Figure 4a, Figure S3a). Total RNA was extracted from the upper non‐inoculated leaves at 28 dpi for RT‐PCR and northern blot detection of viral RNAs (Figure 4b, Figure S3b). The results showed that co‐infection with either TBTV or PEMV2 enhanced the accumulation of RTIV2 genomic and subgenomic RNAs in both WT and DCL2/4i plants (Figure 4b, Figure S3b). Unlike RTIV2 single infection, RTIV2 was readily detectable by northern blotting in the systemically infected leaves of WT plants co‐infected with either umbravirus at 28 dpi (Figure 4b). These findings demonstrate that mixed infection with umbraviruses enhanced RTIV2 accumulation.

To test mechanical transmission, total RNA extracted from plants infiltrated with EV, RTIV2, RTIV2 + TBTV, and RTIV2 + PEMV2 was mixed with carborundum and rubbed onto the leaves of DCL2/4i N. benthamiana seedlings at the 3‐ to 4‐leaf stage. At 17 dpi, the upper non‐inoculated leaves from these plants were harvested for RNA extraction and virus detection by RT‐PCR (Figure S3c) and northern blot hybridization (Figure 4c). As expected, RTIV2 alone failed to establish systemic infection in DCL2/4i plants by mechanical inoculation (Figure 4c, Figure S3c). However, RTIV2 systemic infection was detected in plants mechanically inoculated with total RNA extracted from plants co‐infected with PEMV2, but not with TBTV (Figure 4c, Figure S3c), demonstrating that co‐infection with PEMV2 also promoted the mechanical transmission of RTIV2 in N. benthamiana.

P0 of RTIV2 is a weak viral suppressor of RNAi

The P0 proteins have been identified as VSRs in many poleroviruses (Delfosse et al., 2021). Multiple sequence alignments of P0 proteins revealed several conserved regions (shaded in colours in Figure S4), especially the N‐terminal, including the F‐box motif, that is highly related to VSR activities (Wang et al., 2023). Alanine substitution (Ala24, Ala55, and Ala61) of RTIV2 P0 at three positions that are conserved in other poleroviruses was revealed (indicated as bold red characters) (Figure 5a). Based on the above sequence alignment, we made the consensus substitutions A24R (RTIV2A24R), A55R (RTIV2A55R), and A61L (RTIV2A61L) in the RTIV2 infectious clone and tested for infectious activities. RT‐PCR results showed that both RTIV2A24R and RTIV2A55R lost viral systemic infectivity (Figure S5). We then made the substitutions A24F (RTIV2A24F) (Han et al., 2010) and A55V (RTIV2A55V) (the only substitution we could make without altering the coding of the overlapping RdRP), and both RTIV2A24F and RTIV2A55V showed systemic infectivity in N. benthamiana (Figure S5). Further RT‐qPCR analysis showed that viral RNA levels of RTIV2A24F and RTIV2A55V replicated to a much greater extent than wild‐type RTIV2, while RTIV2A61L showed no significant difference to RTIV2 in N. benthamiana (Figure 2b). These results indicate that residues Ala24 and Ala55 play a critical role in RTIV2 RNA accumulation.

For VSR activity detection, the most widely used assay has been to visualize the induction and suppression of green fluorescent protein (GFP) RNA silencing in the leaves of wild‐type or GFP‐transgenic N. benthamiana plants based on Agrobacterium‐mediated transient expression (Johansen & Carrington, 2001; Voinnet et al., 2000). Systemic silencing suppression was analysed using the GFP‐expressing line 16c of N. benthamiana, in which local GFP transgene silencing induced by 35S‐GFP infiltration spreads to newly emerged leaves. Compared to the efficient suppression of systemic silencing by the VSR P19, we observed silencing suppression in 6 of the 18 plants co‐infiltrated with the plasmid directing expression of RTIV2 P0 (Figure 5c). In the co‐infiltration assay conducted in N. benthamiana (line 16c), the P0 protein of RTIV2 exhibited very weak VSR activity (Figure 5d). These results indicate that RTIV2 P0 displayed a weak VSR activity to suppress both local and systemic RNA silencing in the dicot plants. We found that replacing Ala24 with phenylalanine (A24F), Ala55 with valine (A55V, without altering the coding of the overlapping RdRP), or both residues increased its VSR activity to suppress local RNA silencing in the co‐infiltration assay, as indicated by detecting green fluorescence (Figure 5d), GFP mRNA, protein, and siRNA accumulation (Figure 5e). These results indicated that residues Ala24 and Ala55 decreased the RNA silencing suppression activity of RTIV2 P0. Notably, infectious RTIV2 mutants carrying the corresponding A24F, A55V, or both, designated RTIV2A24F, RTIV2A55V, and RTIV2A24F+A55V, replicated to higher levels in rice line 9311 (Figure 5f). We conclude that RTIV2 found in persistently infected wild rice plants carries specific mutations to decrease in‐vivo virus accumulation by weakening the VSR activity of RTIV2 P0.

DISCUSSION

Our study identifies and characterizes a new polerovirus, rice tiller inhibition virus 2 (RTIV2), that persistently infected wild rice. Further analysis of sRNA reads revealed that the virus has polymorphisms, but the sequence we cloned has the highest frequency of occurrence (Table S3). The findings provide significant insights into the infectivity, pathogenicity, and interactions of RTIV2, highlighting its potential threat to cultivated rice species.

The discovery of RTIV2 from wild rice populations underscores the importance of genetic reserves in identifying new pathogens. The development of an infectious cDNA clone of RTIV2 allowed us to test its infectivity and pathogenicity. In cultivated rice, RTIV2 infected both Nipponbare (japonica) and 9311 (indica) varieties, with detectable levels of viral RNAs and vsiRNAs. The virus caused significant tiller inhibition, a critical yield trait, in both rice varieties, with more severe symptoms in 9311. This differential response suggests that genetic factors in the host may influence the severity of RTIV2 infection. Current rice varieties popularized in production in China generally lack antiviral function (Wu et al., 2022), which increases the potential outspread of RTIV2.

Agrobacterium‐mediated inoculation of N. benthamiana plants, including DCL2/4i and WT plants, demonstrated that RTIV2 could systemically infect these plants, causing chlorosis and necrosis. Interestingly, RTIV2 showed higher accumulation and more severe symptoms in DCL2/4i plants, indicating that DCL2 and DCL4 are crucial for curbing RTIV2 propagation through the biogenesis of vsiRNAs.

Our results also revealed a synergistic interaction between RTIV2 and umbraviruses, specifically PEMV2 and TBTV, in N. benthamiana. Co‐infiltration of RTIV2 with either PEMV2 or TBTV enhanced the accumulation of RTIV2 RNAs, especially in WT plants, from undetectable to readily detectable by northern blotting (Figure 4b), which indicated that umbraviruses help to overcome host antiviral RNAi. Remarkably, PEMV2 co‐infection enabled RTIV2 to be mechanically transmitted in N. benthamiana. This kind of synergism may enhance the spread and impact of RTIV2 in natural environments.

Two lines of evidence indicate that RTIV2 may establish nonvirulent persistent infection in wild rice by evolving a P0 protein variant with weak VSR activity. First, replacing one or both alanine residues of the P0 protein of RTIV2 enhanced its VSR activity in the classical co‐infiltration assay. Further analysis of sRNA reads from wild rice at positions 24A (nucleotide positions 225–227) and 55A (nucleotide positions 318–320) of P0 are essentially identical to the reference sequence, but all bases have a small number of mutations (Table S4), indicating that these two residues have the potential to become any amino acid, including phenylalanine and valine substitutions we created in the study. Second, RTIV2 mutants carrying one or both substitutions increased virus titres in rice plants. Due to the overlapping nature of P0 and RdRP ORFs, one nucleotide change was introduced in the RTIV2 genome to change Ala55 of P0 to valine without altering the RdRP sequence. Coincidentally, for cereal yellow dwarf virus RPV (CYDV‐RPV) and CYDV‐RPS (Figure 5a), two important viral pathogens that infect barley (Hordeum vulgare) also encode valine at this conserved residue.

In conclusion, our study provides critical insights into the biology of RTIV2, its interactions with host plants and other viruses, and its potential impact on rice cultivation. Moreover, our work reveals a new strategy for persistent viruses to maintain long‐term infection by weak suppression of the host defence response.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Plant materials

Wild rice plants were maintained in the outdoor genetic reserve net houses at Guangxi University, China, as described previously (Li et al., 2010). N. benthamiana plants were cultivated in a walk‐in incubator under 16 h light/8 h dark at 24°C with 60% relative humidity. Wild‐type (WT) and GFP transgenic line 16c were generously provided by Dr. David Baulcombe and DCL2/4‐RNAi line by Kriton Kalantidis (Dadami et al., 2013). Nipponbare and 9311 were grown in a walk‐in incubator maintained at 25°C under 16 h light/8 h dark at 24°C with 70% relative humidity.

Small RNA sequencing, analysis of siRNAs, and contig assembly by vdSAR

Wild rice and rice samples were sent to BGI (Wuhan, China) for small RNA (sRNA) library construction and deep‐sequencing. sRNA sequencing was performed on the MGISEQ2000 platform to obtain single‐end sRNA reads. sRNA raw data were firstly checked by fastQC and then processed using software cutadapt to trim adapters and remove low‐quality reads. Only 18–30 nt clean reads were retained and were further de novo assembled by using velvet software (Zerbino & Birney, 2008). Afterwards, sRNA contigs ≥100 nt were aligned to the nt and nr databases for virus identification using Blastn and Blastx, respectively. According to the alignment, we extracted those putative viral contigs and used PCR and 3′/5′ RACE to obtain the completely viral genome of RTIV2. To detect virus‐derived siRNA of RTIV2, clean sRNA reads were mapped to the complete genome of RTIV2 using Bowtie (Langmead et al., 2009), with the limitation of two mismatches. Later, we calculated the number, length distribution, strand, and the first nucleotide of sRNA reads and visualized them in the R program by using the R script. All data are available at the GEO portal of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) by the accession number PRJNA1104427.

Determination of the full‐length genome of the virus

Based on the contigs obtained from the sRNA library, overlapped primers (Table S1) were designed to amplify RTIV2. After determining the sequence of the amplified fragments, the 5′ and 3′ termini of RTIV2 and the subgenome were determined using a 5′ RACE kit (Invitrogen) and a 3′ RACE kit (Takara) through adding a poly(A) tail using an Escherichia coli poly(A) polymerase (NEB). Amplicons were cloned and sequenced to obtain at least three‐fold genome coverage. The sequences of all clones were identical to the assembled contigs. The assembled sequences were then deposited into the NCBI GenBank under the accession number OM835626.

Phylogenetic analysis

The full‐length genome and the amino acid sequences of RdRP of selected members (Table S2) from Solemoviridae were downloaded from NCBI. Alignment was performed with the ClustalW method, the phylogenetic tree was assembled using MEGA 10.0 and the maximum‐likelihood method based on the JJTT+G+I+F model. Mushroom bacilliform virus (genus Barnavirus family Barnaviridae) was included as an outgroup. Numbers on the nodes indicate the percentage of bootstrap replicates supporting the branch (n = 1000). The bootstrap consensus tree inferred from 1000 replicates is taken to represent the evolutionary history of the sequences analysed. Branches corresponding to partitions reproduced in less than 50% of the bootstrap replicates are collapsed. The percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa clustered together in the bootstrap test (1000 replicates) are shown next to the branches.

Construction of cDNA infectious clone and other plasmids

The viral cDNA synthesis from total RNA was performed using SuperScript II reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). PCR was performed in a total volume of 50 μL in an S1000 thermal cycler (Bio‐Rad) using Phusion high‐fidelity DNA polymerase (NEB). The viral fragments were generated with the primer pairs pXT001F and PA‐3267R for the first fragment (1–3267 nt) and the primer pairs PA‐3248F and pXT3R for the second (3248–5822 nt), respectively. The viral fragments generated have a 20‐nt overlap; the 5′ viral genome fragment was supplemented with a 17 ‐nt overlap to the 35S end, whereas the 3′ viral fragment with the same amount of overlap to the 5′‐end of pXT vector by BamHI digestion. PCR products were recovered using a DNA Gel Extraction kit (Omega) and recombined into linearized pXT vector using the StuI and BamHI restriction enzymes (Vazyme) to generate pXT‐RTIV2, which was then transformed into Escherichia coli JM109. Mutated infectious clones of RTIV2A24F, RTIV2A55V, RTIV2A61L, and RTIV2A24F+A55V were obtained by reverse PCR based on pXT‐RTIV2. Briefly, fragments were generated with the primer pairs; DpnI was then added for template digestion. The amplified fragments were then purified after phosphorylation (Takara), followed by self‐ligation performed with T4 ligase (Takara) and transformation into E. coli JM109.

P0 of RTIV2 was amplified with primer pairs XT‐P0‐F/R or CflgP0F/R using pXT‐RTIV2 as template. The PCR product was digested with DpnI, purified with a DNA Gel Extraction kit, inserted into pXT vector treated by StuI and BamHI or p2300‐35S‐C‐ 2 × FLAG vector treated by XbaI and SalI, and then transformed into E. coli JM109 to obtain recombinant vector pXT‐P0 or p2300‐P0‐2 × FLAG.

Mutants P0A24F, P0A55V, P0A61L, and P0A24F+A55V were obtained through PCR amplification with primer pairs CflgP0F/R based on RTIV2A24F, RTIV2A55V, RTIV2A61L, and RTIV2A24F+A55V, respectively.

Primers used are shown in Table S1.

Agrobacterium‐mediated infection

Plasmids were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 and then used for agroinoculation on N. benthamiana plants as previously described (Han et al., 2010), whereas agroinoculation of Nipponbare and 9311 was performed with minor modifications according to the method of geminivirus (Chen et al., 2020). Agrobacteria carrying plasmids were grown overnight at 28°C on Luria Bertani (LB) medium (containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin and rifampicin) and then transferred into culture medium supplemented with 10 mM MES pH 5.6, 45 μM acetosyringone, 50 μg/mL kanamycin, and rifampicin. Cells were then collected by centrifugation, resuspended in LB to a final OD600 of 2–3, smeared on LB plates, and incubated for 24–48 h. A 30G needle was used to scrape Agrobacterium and apply it to the leaves and stems (10–15 days of seedling age), while also scraping the leaf veins with the needle to create microwounds.

RNA extraction and northern blotting

Total RNA was extracted using the hot‐phenol method for N. benthamiana as previously described (Scholes & Lewis, 2020) or TRIzol reagent for rice according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Northern blot of genomic and subgenomic RNAs was carried out as previously described with minor changes (Zhu et al., 2023). Total RNA (10 μg) was separated by electrophoresis in a 1.2% (wt/vol) agarose‐formaldehyde gel and transferred to a Hybond N+ membrane (GE Amersham) by capillary transfer. The membrane was cross‐linked by UV light and stained with methylene blue to assess loading control. The membrane was then hybridized with a biotin‐11‐dUTP (Thermo Scientific)‐labelled RTIV2‐specific (nt 3451–4120 or 156–899) or eGFP‐specific DNA probe prepared by a Taq‐based PCR process.

Northern blot of small RNAs was carried out as previously described with minor changes (Han et al., 2010; Zhu et al., 2023). Low molecular weight RNAs (5 μg) obtained after LiCl and isopropanol precipitation were fractionated on a denaturing 15% polyacrylamide gel containing 8 M urea, electroblotted onto Hybond‐NX membranes (GE Amersham), chemically cross‐linked (Sigma‐Aldrich), and hybridized with biotin‐11‐UTP (Thermo Scientific)‐labelled probe prepared by in vitro transcription and partially hydrolysed in sodium carbonate buffer. 5S rRNA stained with nucleic acid dye was used as loading control. The membrane was then hybridized at 42°C overnight with the above probe in PerfectHyb Plus Hybridization Buffer (Sigma).

Detection of hybridized material was conducted with the Chemiluminescent Nucleic Acid Detection Module Kit (Thermo Scientific). Chemiluminescent signals were collected in an Imager 600 (GE Amersham).

Protein extraction and western blotting

Proteins were extracted as previously described (Yan et al., 2023). Proteins were detected using a rabbit polyclonal antiserum raised against the CP of RTIV2, GFP (generated in‐house) or FLAG (LABLEAD), and subsequently detected by goat anti‐rabbit horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated antibody (LABLEAD). For antibodies generated in‐house, the proteins were expressed in a prokaryotic system and then sent to BGI Biotech to immunize rabbits, followed by obtaining serum.

qPCR for viral RNA accumulation

Total RNA was extracted from systemic leaves of N. benthamiana. cDNAs were synthesized by using a first‐strand cDNA synthesis supermix (TransGen Biotech). Quantitative PCR was carried out in the presence of SYBR Green Supermix (TransGen Biotech). The relative abundance of RTIV2 was normalized to an internal control (actin). Primers used are shown in Table S1. Relative abundance was estimated by the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak & Schmittgen, 2001).

Transient co‐expression assay and observation of RNA silencing

Plasmids were transformed into A. tumefaciens GV3101 using the freeze–thaw method (Wise et al., 2006). Co‐infiltration assays were performed as previously described (Han et al., 2010); each A. tumefaciens culture was grown to about OD600 = 1 and mixed in equal volumes of pGDG (Goodin et al., 2002) prior to infiltration. GFP fluorescence in the infiltrated leaves and systemic leaves was illuminated under a 100 W hand‐held long‐wave UV lamp (Black Ray model B 100AP/R; UV Products) and photographed using a digital camera (D750; Nikon) with a yellow filter (Kodak Wratten gelatin filter, no. 15) at 3 and 14 dpi.
