Switching from Conventional Fibrates to Pemafibrate Improves Lipid Profiles, Liver Enzymes, Renal Function, and Fibrosis Markers in Dyslipidemic Patients with MASLD: A Multicenter Real-World Study
Authors: Takao Sato, Toshihiro Miyamoto, Ryoma Fukuoka, Kazumasa Sugimoto, Yoko Tanimoto, Takashi Nakayama, Kojiro Tanimoto, Koichiro Sugimura, Yoshihide Fujimoto, Takahiko Kiyooka, Akio Kawamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aims: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is highly prevalent worldwide. Conventional fibrates effectively lower triglycerides but may elevate liver enzymes and creatinine, limiting long-term use. Pemafibrate, a selective PPARα modulator (SPPARMα), offers potent triglyceride reduction with favorable hepatic and renal safety. This study evaluated the effects of switching from conventional fibrates to pemafibrate on lipid profiles, liver enzyme, fibrosis indices(Fibrosis-4 index) with particular emphasis on renal safety in clinical practice. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed 144 patients with dyslipidemia, including those with MASLD or chronic kidney disease, who were switched from bezafibrate 200mg (N = 24) or 400mg (N = 30), or fenofibrate 80mg (N = 51) or 160mg (N = 39), to pemafibrate (0.2mg/day). Laboratory parameters were assessed at baseline and 6 months post-switch. Results: Triglycerides decreased significantly, particularly in the bezafibrate 200 mg and fenofibrate 80 mg groups, while other lipid profiles remained stable. Serum alanine aminotransferase decreased significantly with bezafibrate 200mg (29±27 to 17±13U/L,p＜0.05) and fenofibrate 80mg (26±18 to 20±10 U/L,p＜0.05), whereas treatment with bezafibrate (400mg) and fenofibrate (160mg) showed a trend toward reduction. The glutamyl transferase showed a similar trend. The Fibrosis-4 index showed reductions across all groups, reaching statistical significance in bezafibrate200mg (1.51±0.63→1.36±0.47,p = 0.02) and fenofibrate 80mg (1.17±0.37→1.07±0.33,p = 0.001). Renal function remained stable across all groups, with no clinically significant deterioration in estimated glomerular filtration rate, supporting the renal safety of pemafibrate. Conclusion: Switching from conventional fibrates to pemafibrate improved triglyceride levels, liver enzymes, and fibrosis indices in patients with MASLD, while maintaining a favorable renal safety profile.

Background

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease/ steatohepatitis (MASLD/MASH) which is associated with cardiovascular disease affects approximately 30% of the global adult population, with prevalence varying by region 1) .

PPARα agonists, such as fibrates, have been used as hypolipidemic agents for decades, demonstrating their safety and efficacy in lipid lowering—an essential factor in preventing cardiovascular diseases. However, due to their low selectivity, fibrates have been reported to increase creatinine levels, raising concerns about their potential impact on renal function and limiting their clinical utility as a therapeutic option 2) . Pemafibrate is a novel selective PPARα modulator (SPPARMα) with high receptor selectivity and unique binding properties, enabling potent triglyceride (TG) reduction and favorable modulation of atherogenic dyslipidemia while minimizing off-target effects seen with conventional fibrates 3) . Preclinical studies have shown hepatoprotective actions beyond TG lowering, including reduced hepatic inflammation and fibrogenic gene expression in rodent models of steatohepatitis and fibrosis, partly via suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome signaling 4 , 5) . Early clinical studies suggest that pemafibrate not only improves lipid profiles but also reduces liver enzymes, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and may favorably affect noninvasive fibrosis indices 6 , 7) . However, most trials have compared pemafibrate with placebo in treatment-naïve or add-on contexts, and few have assessed the effects of switching from conventional fibrates (bezafibrate or fenofibrate) in real-world practice. Longitudinal studies evaluating lipid, liver, renal, and fibrosis markers after such a switch are still needed 8 , 9) . Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of switching from conventional fibrates to pemafibrate on lipid parameters, including triglyceride levels, hepatic markers, and fibrosis indices, with a particular emphasis on renal safety, while accounting for the doses of the respective conventional fibrates.

Methods

Study Design and Ethics

This was a retrospective observational study based on medical records from two affiliated hospitals of our institution in Japan, conducted between January 2021 and December 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (approval number: 25-CN-035, approval date: Feb 25, 2025). Informed consent was obtained through an opt-out process permitted under national ethical guidelines, with IRB approval for a waiver of written informed consent.

Patient Selection

Patients who were receiving conventional fibrate therapy (e.g., fenofibrate or bezafibrate) and were switched to pemafibrate at the discretion of their treating physicians were screened for inclusion. The decision to switch therapy was based on clinical considerations such as inadequate triglyceride control, potential adverse effects, or physician preference for the safety and efficacy profile of pemafibrate. These decisions were made independently of this retrospective study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥ 18 years; diagnosis of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD); stable use of a conventional fibrate for at least 8 weeks before switching; and availability of baseline laboratory data within 4 weeks prior to the switch. MASLD was diagnosed based on imaging evidence of hepatic steatosis (ultrasound or CT) accompanied by at least one cardiovascular risk factor, including diabetes mellitus, obesity, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, or low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) 10) . Exclusion criteria were acute hepatic injury at baseline, active malignancy with an expected survival of less than 6 months, acute infectious disease at the time of switching, or any medication changes known to significantly affect lipid or hepatic profiles (e.g., initiation of high-dose corticosteroids, SGLT-2 inhibitors, or GLP-1 receptor agonists) during the study period.

Intervention and Follow-up

All eligible patients were switched from their conventional fibrate to pemafibrate at the standard approved dose in Japan (0.2 mg twice daily). As this was a retrospective study, concomitant medications—including statins, ezetimibe, and antidiabetic agents such as SGLT-2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists—were maintained at stable doses throughout the observation period. Consequently, although residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, the observed changes in lipid profiles, liver enzyme levels, and renal function were likely associated with the switch to pemafibrate.

Laboratory Assessments

Blood samples were collected at baseline (within 4 weeks prior to switching) and approximately 3 and 6 months after initiation of pemafibrate. The following parameters were measured: fasting triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), HDL-C, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT). The small dense LDL (sd-LDL) was estimated using Sampson’s equation 11) . Liver enzyme markers were defined as ALT, AST, and GGT. The Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index was calculated using the formula: age × AST / [platelet count × √ALT], as a marker of hepatic fibrosis.

Renal function was assessed via serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) at baseline and 6 months. All parameters were compared to baseline values at follow-up. Clinically relevant safety outcomes, including adverse events leading to treatment discontinuation, serious adverse events, or significant elevations in liver enzymes, were documented and evaluated.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range) depending on distribution; categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for overall group differences. When significant, pairwise comparisons were performed with Dunn’s test adjusted for multiple testing. Within-patient changes from baseline to each follow-up time point were assessed using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests as appropriate. A two-sided p-value ＜0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using EZR version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 144 patients with dyslipidemia who were switched from conventional fibrates to pemafibrate (0.2 mg/day) were included in the analysis. The follow-up period was six months, during which no changes or additions to medications—including pemafibrate or antidiabetic drugs—were made. The overall mean age was 59±12 years, and 95 patients (66%) were male. Comorbidities included diabetes mellitus in 59%, hypertension in 66%, and chronic kidney disease in 39% of patients, while a history of cardiovascular disease, such as prior coronary revascularization, was noted in 2.7%. At baseline, the mean triglyceride (TG) level was 254±309 mg/dL (median [interquartile range], 167 [120–269] mg/dL), HDL-C was 54±20 mg/dL, and LDL-C was 118±92 mg/dL. The mean ALT, AST, and GGT levels were 30±29, 30±26, and 47±36 U/L, respectively, the mean FIB-4 index was 1.27±0.54, and the mean eGFR was 66.3±18.2 mL/min/1.73 m², and no patients had an eGFR ＜30 mL/min/1.73 m². Changes in Lipid Profiles

Switching from bezafibrate (200 mg) or fenofibrate (80 mg) to pemafibrate led to significant reductions in TG at both 3 and 6 months compared with baseline (all p＜0.05). In contrast, switching from bezafibrate (400 mg) or fenofibrate (160 mg) showed only a trend toward TG reduction at both time points ( Fig.1 ) . LDL-C remained stable, with fluctuations of less than 10% during the study period.

In addition, sd-LDL significantly decreased after switching from bezafibrate (200 mg) or fenofibrate (80 mg) to pemafibrate (0.2 mg) (from 41.0±14.9 mg/dL to 36.5±12.6 mg/dL, p＜0.01; and from 45.1±16.0 mg/dL to 39.2±11.6 mg/dL, p = 0.03, respectively), but not after switching from bezafibrate (400 mg) or fenofibrate (160 mg) to pemafibrate (0.2 mg) (from 48.8±14.5 mg/dL to 45.1±15.3 mg/dL, p = 0.30; and from 41.8±15.6 mg/dL to 38.8±10.9 mg/dL, p = 0.23, respectively).

Changes in Liver Enzymes and in Hepatic Fibrosis Markers

ALT, AST, and GGT levels decreased significantly after switching to pemafibrate, with improvements observed as early as 3 months and maintained at 6 months. Significant reductions were seen particularly in patients previously receiving bezafibrate 200 mg and fenofibrate 80 mg compared with baseline ( Fig.2 ) . The FIB-4 index significantly decreased at 6 months in patients switched from bezafibrate 200 mg (p = 0.02) and fenofibrate 80 mg (p = 0.001), while downward trends, though not statistically significant, were observed in those switched from bezafibrate 400 mg and fenofibrate 160 mg ( Fig.3 ) .

Renal function, as assessed by serum creatinine levels and eGFR, showed a significant decrease in serum creatinine and an increase in eGFR at 6 months after the switch to pemafibrate in all groups (bezafibrate 200 mg, 400 mg, fenofibrate 80 mg, and 160 mg; all p＜0.01 vs. baseline) ( Fig.4 ) .

Discussion

The main findings of the present study are as follows; 1) The TG-lowering efficacy of pemafibrate (0.2 mg/day) was comparable to that of bezafibrate (400 mg/day) and fenofibrate (160 mg/day), with a significant reduction in AST, ALT levels compared with conventional fibrates; 2) FIB-4 index values, indicative of hepatic fibrosis, decreased even in patients who were switched from conventional fibrates; and 3) Renal function was preserved in more than 95% of patients after switching to pemafibrate, irrespective of the doses of prior conventional fibrates.

To our knowledge, few studies have evaluated the detailed clinical effects of switching from conventional fibrates (bezafibrate or fenofibrate) to pemafibrate in real-world practice, particularly taking into account prior conventional fibrate doses.

TG-lowering Efficacy of Pemafibrate

Fibrates, as PPARα agonists, are widely used to treat dyslipidemia and may play a protective role in the pathogenesis of NAFLD. However, conventional fibrates have limited clinical utility for NAFLD/NASH due to dose-dependent adverse effects, including elevations in transaminases, homocysteine, and creatinine 12 , 13) . Pemafibrate, a novel selective PPARα modulator (SPPARMα), exhibits approximately 2,500-fold higher binding affinity for PPARα than fenofibrate and demonstrates robust TG-lowering efficacy at low doses (0.2–0.4 mg/day) 14 , 15) . Clinical trials indicate minimal renal adverse effects and good tolerability with statin co-administration 16 , 17) . In the present study, switching from conventional fibrates (fenofibrate 80–160 mg or bezafibrate 200–400 mg) to pemafibrate effectively reduced TG levels, consistent with prior reports, while LDL-C and HDL-C remained relatively stable. These findings suggest that pemafibrate provides a potent and safe lipid-modifying option in a real-world setting.

Effects on Liver Enzymes

Pemafibrate may exert effects beyond lipid modulation, including anti-inflammatory actions that contribute to hepatoprotection 18 - 20) . In the randomized PORTRAIT trial 19) , pemafibrate improved liver enzymes (including ALT), non-invasive fibrosis biomarkers (M2BPGi and FIB-4), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) more effectively than omega-3 ethyl ester. Mechanistically, as a selective PPARα modulator (SPPARMα), pemafibrate enhances triglyceride hydrolysis and suppresses VLDL secretion, potentially reducing intrahepatic lipid accumulation and alleviating oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling in MASLD/MASH 20 , 21) . Although our study did not assess direct markers of inflammation or oxidative stress, consistent reductions in ALT and FIB-4 were observed after switching from conventional fibrates—even in the absence of significant triglyceride reduction. These findings may be suggestive of potential anti-inflammatory benefits of pemafibrate, though further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Renal Safety and Clinical Advantages

In our cohort, switching from conventional fibrates to pemafibrate was associated with preservation of renal function, as reflected by modest changes in eGFR; however, detailed assessments of tubular injury or other markers of nephrotoxicity were not performed. Because patient age remained unchanged during the study period, reductions in serum creatinine levels likely contributed to the observed increase in eGFR. Conventional fibrates, such as bezafibrate and fenofibrate, are effective in lowering triglycerides and increasing HDL-C levels but have been linked to increases in serum creatinine and eGFR reduction 12) because of almost completely renally excretion, causing to renal dysfunction or rhabdomyolysis. Several mechanisms have been proposed for the transient renal impairment observed with fibrates, including increased creatinine production 22) , and reduced vasodilatory prostaglandin concentrations resulting from decreased renal cyclooxygenase-2 expression 23) . Furthermore, previous clinical and preclinical studies have shown that pemafibrate effectively improves dyslipidemia while maintaining a favorable renal safety profile, even in patients with chronic kidney disease 24) . Preclinical studies also suggest potential renoprotective effects in models of fatty acid overload nephropathy 25) . Therefore, as no patients with severe renal dysfunction (eGFR ＜30 mL/min/1.73 m²) were included in the present study, the influence of advanced renal injury on the renal outcomes is likely limited. The observed changes in renal parameters after switching should be interpreted in the context of the known reversibility of fibrate-associated creatinine elevation, rather than as evidence of a direct renoprotective effect. Overall, these findings support the renal safety and functional preservation of pemafibrate in routine clinical practice. Pemafibrate, as a selective PPARα modulator (SPPARMα), exhibits higher selectivity and potency, with minimal statin interactions and negligible muscle-related toxicity 17 , 26) . In the present study, no adverse events such as rhabdomyolysis or elevations in creatine kinase were observed. Collectively, these findings suggest that pemafibrate may preserve lipid-lowering efficacy while minimizing renal and muscular safety concerns compared with conventional fibrates.

Clinical Implication

These findings reaffirm the lipid-lowering efficacy and hepatic and renal safety of pemafibrate and provide real-world evidence in a population rarely assessed in previous studies—patients switched from conventional fibrates. When patients were stratified according to prior fibrate dose, pemafibrate was associated with preserved triglyceride-lowering efficacy and with improvements in liver enzymes, fibrosis indices, and renal function in routine clinical practice.

Although speculative, the elevated baseline TG levels observed in the present study may reflect safety-related limitations of conventional fibrate therapy, particularly with respect to renal function and concerns regarding concomitant statin use. In this context, our findings suggest potential clinical benefits of switching from conventional fibrates to pemafibrate, consistent with previous reports in patients with concomitant MASLD or chronic kidney disease 19 , 27) .

Recent study has further suggested that reductions in sd-LDL may contribute to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk reduction associated with TG lowering, particularly in patients with elevated LDL levels 28) . These observations may provide a potential mechanistic framework for interpreting the neutral cardiovascular outcomes of the PROMINENT trial 29) . Although pemafibrate 0.2 mg/day appeared to reduce sd-LDL to a degree comparable to that achieved with high-dose conventional fibrates in the present study, its substantially more favorable safety profile supports further investigation of higher-dose pemafibrate therapy (0.4 mg/day) as a potentially safer strategy for cardiovascular risk reduction.

Limitations

There are several limitations to the present study. First, it was a retrospective study. Second, although the overall sample size was relatively large, the number of patients in each group was small. Third, small dense LDL cholesterol (sd-LDL-C) was not directly measured, but estimated using a calculation method. Fourth, previous studies have reported improvements in insulin resistance with pemafibrate treatment 30) ; however, this outcome was not evaluated in the present study. Fifth, liver function, including prothrombin time, was not evaluated alongside liver enzymes. Sixth, abdominal ultrasound or CT scans were not performed at follow-up to assess the extent of MASLD. Sixth, the mechanisms underlying changes in renal function, including potential tubular effects, were not specifically investigated. In addition, no stratified analyses according to chronic kidney disease status were performed. Nonetheless, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effects of switching to pemafibrate while accounting for the doses of the respective conventional fibrates. Finally, longitudinal studies following the switch are warranted to further clarify the long-term effects of pemafibrate.

Conclusion

Switching from conventional fibrates to pemafibrate effectively maintains robust triglyceride-lowering efficacy, improves liver enzymes and fibrosis indices, and demonstrates a favorable renal safety profile. These findings highlight pemafibrate as a clinically valuable therapeutic option for patients with dyslipidemia, particularly those with concomitant MASLD, while supporting the preservation of renal function in real-world clinical practice.
