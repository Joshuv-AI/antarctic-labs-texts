Ultrasound for Diagnosing Hand and Digital Nerve Trauma: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Jonah W. Perlmutter, Adham Elsherbini, Jacob Wise, Adam Mosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility of ultrasound in traumatic hand and digital nerve injuries and to compare its performance with conventional diagnostic modalities such as EMG and nerve conduction studies (NCS).MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were performed in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines (PROSPERO CRD420251107777). MEDLINE and Embase were searched to July 18, 2025. Eligible studies included English-language retrospective studies assessing ultrasound for traumatic hand or digital nerve injury with a surgical, clinical, or electrodiagnostic reference standard. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and assessed quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Sensitivity and specificity were synthesized when extractable; additional outcomes included time to diagnosis, injury characterization, and impact on surgical planning.ResultsTwelve retrospective studies encompassing 607 injuries were included. Nine studies provided extractable diagnostic data, with a pooled ultrasound sensitivity of 93% across diverse trauma types. Specificity was reported in only two studies and remained limited because of small disease-negative cohorts. Sonographic findings included complete transection (29%), neuroma in continuity (24%), increased cross-sectional area (18%), and partial transection (8%). In six studies directly comparing modalities, ultrasound and EMG/NCS demonstrated sensitivities ≥90%, with full concordance in 2 series and near-equivalence in others. Ultrasound additionally identified structural features such as neuromas, fascicular disruption, and perineural scarring that guided operative referral. Studies using ultrahigh-frequency (70 MHz) probes achieved sensitivities approaching 98% for superficial digital nerves. Overall study quality was moderate, with common limitations including retrospective design and incomplete reporting of specificity.ConclusionsUltrasound is a highly sensitive, noninvasive tool for diagnosing traumatic hand and digital nerve injuries, offering advantages over electrodiagnostic studies by directly visualizing structural pathology. An “ultrasound-first” strategy in the acute phase, followed by EMG/NCS for functional assessment in the subacute phase, may optimize timely diagnosis, guide referral, and assist surgical planning.Type of study/level of evidenceDiagnostic III

Traumatic injuries to the hand and digital nerves are among the most frequent peripheral nerve injuries and represent a major cause of functional limitation, psychosocial morbidity, and overall decreased quality of life.1 A loss of protective sensation, fine tactile feedback, and motor coordination can lead to reduced dexterity and difficulties performing activities of daily living in up to 70% of moderate to severe cases.1,2 Persistent neuropathic pain and sensory deficits further contribute to disease burden and can have significant socioeconomic consequences, particularly in working-age populations.1 Timely recognition and repair of nerve trauma is critical, as outcomes decline with delayed intervention, and earlier repair is associated with improved sensory recovery and functional restoration.3

The mechanisms of traumatic hand and digital nerve injury are diverse, with important implications for both presentation and management. Sharp penetrating trauma, most often from glass, knives, or power tools, remains the most common cause and is typically associated with clean transections or partial lacerations.4 Stretch and traction injuries, such as those seen in sports or motor vehicle accidents, may lead to axonotmesis with nerve elongation, fascicular disruption, or perineural scarring.2 Compression and contusion injuries, including iatrogenic causes from casts, surgical retractors, or postoperative fibrosis, often result in focal nerve flattening and proximal swelling.4 Ballistic and blast trauma represent a distinct category, frequently producing mixed injury patterns that include neuroma formation, discontinuity, and scarring, in addition to concomitant vascular, soft tissue, and bone trauma.5 Over half of hand or digital nerve injuries have concomitant soft tissue, bone, or vascular injuries.4

Accurate diagnosis of hand and digital nerve injuries, however, remains challenging.6 Physical examination may be confounded by pain, swelling, or concomitant injuries and lacks the ability to directly visualize nerve integrity.6 Both, EMG and nerve conduction studies (NCS) provide physiologic information but often require a delay of several weeks before abnormalities can be detected, limiting their utility in acute settings. Furthermore, these tests can be uncomfortable for patients and provide limited spatial resolution.7 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can demonstrate nerve continuity and surrounding soft tissue changes, but its use is constrained by high cost, longer acquisition times, and limited availability.6,8 In pediatric patients, the need for sedation or general anesthesia poses additional risks.8 Although surgical exploration is considered the gold standard, its invasive nature, high-resource demands, and lack of necessity when repair is unwarranted limit its role in routine diagnosis.6

Ultrasound (US) has emerged as a promising alternative, offering a noninvasive, cost-effective, and real-time imaging modality that allows direct visualization of peripheral nerves.9 With high spatial resolution, US can detect nerve discontinuity, caliber changes, neuromas, and foreign bodies while also enabling dynamic evaluation during movement.9 It is portable, widely available, and well tolerated across age groups, including children. Importantly, US can be performed at the point of care, expediting diagnosis and facilitating early surgical planning.9, 10, 11

Despite these advantages, US remains underused for the evaluation of hand and digital nerve injuries, and its performance in clinical diagnosis and decision-making has not been comprehensively synthesized.12 To address this gap, this systematic review and meta-analysis aims to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy and clinical utility associated with US in the diagnosis of traumatic hand and digital nerve injuries compared with conventional diagnostic techniques.

Materials and Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was registered in the PROSPERO database (registration code: CRD420251107777). Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, MEDLINE and Embase were searched by an investigator (AE) using predefined search terms (Table S1–S2, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org). The search included all studies published up to July 18, 2025, with no restrictions on the start date. Study selection was performed using Covidence, where 2 independent reviewers (AE and JW) screened titles and abstracts to exclude irrelevant studies. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were then assessed against predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria by both reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (JP).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they were published in English and evaluated the use of US for diagnosing traumatic hand or digital nerve injuries, including sharp transections, stretch injuries, or ballistic trauma. Only retrospective studies were included to maintain methodological consistency across data sets and enable a uniform approach to bias analysis. Studies were excluded if they were prospective studies, randomized controlled trials, case reports, case series with <10 patients, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, qualitative studies, or conference abstracts without full texts. Studies were also excluded if they evaluated nontraumatic neuropathies, injuries outside the hand/fingers, or US used exclusively intraoperatively.

Data extraction and outcomes

Two independent reviewers (AE and JW) extracted data using a standardized extraction form adapted from the Cochrane Data Extraction and Assessment Form. Extracted data included study characteristics (author, year, study design, country), population demographics (age, sex, injury type, clinical setting), intervention details (US modality, probe type, and operator), comparators (surgical exploration, MRI, EMG/NCS, or clinical examination), and outcomes. The primary outcome was diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity, specificity, predictive values). Secondary outcomes included time to diagnosis, impact on surgical planning, procedural success rates, and patient outcomes (pain, function).

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which assesses study quality across selection, comparability, and outcome domains.13 Two reviewers (AE and JW) independently appraised each study, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (JP).

Data synthesis

Studies were categorized based on study design and type of nerve trauma. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.3). Diagnostic accuracy measures (true positives, false negatives, false positives, and true negatives) were extracted from each eligible study, and sensitivity and specificity with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated where possible. Pooled sensitivity of US for diagnosing traumatic hand and digital nerve injuries was calculated using a random-effects model to account for between-study heterogeneity, with forest plots generated using the meta (version 6.5-0) and metafor (version 4.8-0) packages. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, with thresholds of 25%, 50%, and 75% indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity. Comparative sensitivity values between US and EMG were synthesized descriptively. When studies did not provide complete 2 × 2 contingency data, sensitivity values were incorporated narratively rather than quantitatively, and specificity was not pooled because of insufficient disease-negative cohorts. Outliers were assessed by visual inspection of forest plots, and small or extreme studies were retained to maintain comprehensiveness, with their potential impact on pooled results noted.

Ethics

This study did not include the use of human subjects or identifiable personal information and was exempt from institutional review board ethical approval.

Participant characteristics

The pooled mean age was 42.1 years (549/611, 89.9%) with a range of 2–87 years and reflecting 278 males (59%, 278/557). Mechanism of nerve trauma included lacerations (47.8%, 119/249), compression injuries (38.1%, 95/249), and traction/stretch injuries (14.1%, 35/249). There was right-hand involvement in 50% (83/166) of patients. Initial clinical presentation included decreased muscle bulk or muscle atrophy (38.3%, 51/133), combined motor and sensory deficits (31.6%, 42/133), pure motor deficits (18.8%, 25/133), and pure sensory deficits (11.3%, 15/133). The average time from trauma to US was 47.9 days. Nerves involved included median (22.2%, 76/343), ulnar (21.6%, 74/343), radial (15.2%, 52/34), and digital (14.9%, 51/343).

US characteristics and findings

Ultrasound probe frequencies varied across studies, most commonly in the high-frequency range (15–22 MHz, 67%, 8/12). Lower-frequency probes (<15 MHz) were used in 25% of studies (3/12), whereas 8.3% (1/12) used an ultrahigh-frequency 70 MHz probe. Operators included radiologists (85.7%, 6/7) and physiatrists (14.3%, 1/7) with an average experience of 15.5 years (range 8–35 years) with US.

Sonographic findings were heterogeneous, including complete transection (28.6%, 44/154), neuroma in-continuity (24%, 37/154), increased cross-sectional area (18.2%, 28/154), axonotmesis (14.3%, 22/154), partial transection (12/154, 7.8%), associated orthopedic injury (5.8%, 9/154), stump neuroma (4.5%, 7/154), nerve edema (1.3%, 2/154), associated vascular injury (0.6%, 1/154), and hypoechogenicity (0.6%, 1/154).

Diagnostic performance of US

Nine studies (462 nerve injuries) provided sufficient data to calculate sensitivity. Using a random-effects model, the pooled sensitivity of US for diagnosing traumatic hand and digital nerve injuries was 93.3% (95% CI, 87.8% to 96.4%), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 39.9%) (Fig. 2; Table S3, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org). Comparison with EMG

Six studies (469 nerves) directly compared US with electrodiagnostic studies. Cha et al14 evaluated 67 digital nerves, with electrodiagnosis detecting all injuries (sensitivity 100%), demonstrating complete concordance. Lu et al15 similarly reported perfect agreement across 59 injuries (100% sensitivity for both modalities), with only 1 misclassified neuroma versus partial tear noted on US. Mologne et al16 reported that US detected 14 of 16 injuries (sensitivity 87.5%), whereas electrodiagnosis identified all 16 (100%), highlighting a small performance gap in this smaller series. Shields et al17 assessed 22 patients with gunshot-related upper-extremity injuries; both US and electrodiagnosis were diagnostic in the majority, with US sensitivity estimated at 95% versus 91% for electrodiagnostic studies, and US provided structural details such as neuromas and fascicular disruption. Amr et al18 examined 66 traumatic nerve injuries, reporting sensitivities of 90% for US and 85% for electrodiagnosis, with US further stratifying severity grades and detecting neuromas (15%). Marco et al19 compared conventional high-frequency (18 MHz) and ultrahigh-frequency (70 MHz) probes in 61 patients, using electrodiagnosis as the reference. Ultrasound with ultrahigh-frequency probes achieved 98% sensitivity, nearly identical to electrodiagnostic studies (98%–100%), whereas standard probes performed slightly lower at 82%.

Clinical utility of US

Several studies highlighted the broader role of US in injury characterization and surgical planning beyond binary diagnostic accuracy. Toia et al20 demonstrated that US informed diagnosis or operative planning in 72.2% of traumatic neuropathies, whereas it was noncontributory in approximately 10.1%, predominantly in cases of mild compressive lesions. Shields et al reported the distribution of US findings in 16 gunshot injuries: 11/16 (69%) had enlarged and/or hypoechoic nerves (often with prominent fascicles), 4/16 (25%) had neuroma in continuity, and 1/16 (6%) showed neurotmesis. Additional features included epineural or perineural thickening, loss of the fascicular pattern, and focal discontinuity. Amr et al18 similarly characterized US findings while grading their severity. Grade 4 findings (36%) correlated with severe EMG abnormalities and muscle atrophy, whereas neuromas were identified in 15% of cases. Amr et al’s 66-nerve series demonstrates the value of US for severity stratification and clinical linkage. Ultrasound grades were 18.2% (12/66) grade 1 (thickened, distinct fascicles), 22.7% (15/66) grade 2 (thickened, matted fascicles), 22.7% (15/66) grade 3 (interstitial neuroma), and 36.4% (24/66) grade 4 (transection); neuromas were seen in 15.1% (10/66).18 Although US grade did not significantly track duration of injury or EMG findings (both P > .05), all repaired nerves were grade 3 on ultrasound, highlighting its potential role in flagging lesions likely to need intervention.18

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes the available evidence on the diagnostic performance of US in traumatic hand and digital nerve injuries (distal to the brachial plexus), comparing its accuracy with electrodiagnostic modalities such as EMG and NCS. Across nine studies with extractable sensitivity data, the US achieved a pooled sensitivity of 93.3% (95% CI, 87.8% to 96.4%), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 39.9%). This analysis confirms that US is a reliable diagnostic tool for identifying nerve pathology in the setting of trauma, with performance comparable to more established electrophysiological techniques. In six studies that directly compared US and EMG/NCS, both modalities demonstrated high sensitivities (≥90% to 100%), further underscoring these findings. Importantly, US offered unique added value by directly visualizing structural pathology, such as neuroma formation, fascicular discontinuity, perineural scarring, and hardware impingement, that electrodiagnostic methods cannot specifically detect.9 These morphological insights enhance surgical planning by identifying the precise location, type, and severity of injury, supporting operative decision-making in real time.

The findings of this review have immediate clinical relevance for the management of traumatic hand and digital nerve injuries. EMG/NCS has long been considered the functional gold standard for confirming axonal injury and assessing reinnervation.25 However, these methods cannot provide reliable information in the acute phase until Wallerian degeneration is complete at approximately 2 weeks posttrauma.26 This delay limits the rapid diagnostic clarity needed to evaluate the extent of injury and the need for surgery. In contrast, ambulatory US can be performed within days of injury, offering immediate visualization of nerve continuity, caliber changes, or neuroma formation.12 Its capacity to dynamically assess nerves along their course, and in relation to hardware, fracture callus, or scar tissue, further supports its use as a first-line modality.12 Moreover, Amr et al demonstrated that US grading of nerve injury severity correlates with the need for surgical intervention, underscoring its value in guiding referrals. This synthesis supports a complementary diagnostic pathway (Fig. 3): US should serve as the initial modality in acute trauma, allowing early identification of discontinuity or entrapment that necessitates surgical intervention. This approach is anecdotally commonplace among practicing peripheral nerve surgeons but presently lacks level 3 evidence. EMG/NCS, in turn, provides valuable functional information regarding axonal integrity, denervation, and reinnervation potential, making it most useful in subacute and chronic stages. Together, these modalities offer a comprehensive evaluation of both structure and function. This review also highlights how sonographic findings align with distinct mechanisms of injury and how technological advances enhance diagnostic precision. Sharp penetrating injuries, such as those caused by glass or knives, frequently result in clean nerve transections.26 On US, these present as abrupt caliber loss with proximal and distal stump enlargement, often accompanied by hypoechoic neuroma formation at the transected ends.12 Recognition of these features has clear operative implications, prompting timely surgical repair or grafting.31 In contrast, traction and stretch injuries may produce fascicular disruption, diffuse swelling, or hypoechoic changes without frank discontinuity.9 These cases often warrant close monitoring or delayed repair, and US can help track structural evolution over time. Compression injuries, whether iatrogenic (casts and retractors) or traumatic (callus and fibrosis), characteristically demonstrate focal flattening with proximal swelling, findings that can be subtle but critical in explaining persistent deficits.12,16, Ballistic and blast injuries frequently produce complex mixed patterns, including discontinuity, neuromas, and perineural scarring, in which US is uniquely positioned to delineate the extent and type of pathology in ways that EMG cannot.18

Advances in probe technology further improve diagnostic capability. In Marco et al,19 ultra-high-frequency ultrasound (UHFUS, 30 MHz +) achieved 98% sensitivity and 95% overall accuracy, considerably outperforming high-frequency ultrasound (HFUS, 15-30 MHz). The higher spatial resolution of UHFUS enables visualization of fascicular architecture and detection of small neuromas or subtle scarring that would otherwise be missed. Although penetration depth remains limited, restricting UHFUS to superficial structures such as digital nerves, these findings suggest an important role for UHFUS in specialized centers and highlight a trajectory of ongoing technological refinement that may broaden clinical adoption.19

The strength of the present findings is tempered by several important limitations. First, the majority of included studies were retrospective and single-center in design, with inherent risks of selection bias and incomplete reporting. Risk of bias assessment confirmed moderate study quality overall, with NOS scores ranging from 3 to 6 out of 9 (mean 5.1), highlighting limitations in comparability and cohort selection. Few studies provided full 2 × 2 diagnostic data, limiting the ability to pool specificity estimates or calculate predictive values. Specificity was rarely reported, reflecting a bias toward studying patients already selected for surgical exploration or EMG, rather than unselected clinical cohorts. This spectrum bias inflates apparent sensitivity while underestimating false-positive rates. Second, heterogeneity was evident across studies in terms of reference standards, operator expertise, probe frequencies, and patient populations. Third, publication bias cannot be excluded.

Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter diagnostic accuracy studies that apply standardized protocols and report complete diagnostic metrics. Such studies should include both injured and uninjured nerves, enabling robust estimates of both sensitivity and specificity. The development of a standardized sonographic taxonomy, for example, consensus definitions for neuroma, discontinuity, and cross-sectional area ratios, would enhance consistency and facilitate meta-analysis.

Technological innovation also warrants ongoing evaluation. UHFUS and elastography represent promising frontiers that may expand diagnostic accuracy and prognostic utility. Elastography, which assesses tissue stiffness under compression, could improve the detection of fibrotic scarring or differentiate neuromas from surrounding soft tissue.32 Cost-effectiveness analyses are likewise needed, particularly in resource-limited settings, to determine whether widespread adoption of US for peripheral nerve trauma is feasible and sustainable.33 Finally, implementation studies examining training requirements, learning curves, and integration into emergency and surgical workflows will be critical for translating evidence into practice.34

In summary, this systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that US is a highly sensitive diagnostic tool for traumatic hand and digital nerve injuries, with pooled sensitivity exceeding 93% and performance comparable to EMG/NCS. Beyond diagnostic accuracy, US uniquely provides morphological detail that informs surgical planning and guides clinical management. Advances in probe technology, particularly ultrahigh-frequency US, further enhance its sensitivity and utility. Although current evidence is limited by retrospective designs, heterogeneity, and incomplete specificity data, the weight of evidence supports an “ultrasound-first” approach in the evaluation of suspected digital and hand nerve trauma, complemented by EMG/NCS for functional assessment and follow-up.
