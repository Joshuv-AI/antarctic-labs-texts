Introduction

Wrapping an ileal loop around the base of the redundant sigmoid colon, and vice versa, is known as ileosigmoid knotting (ISK)[1,2]. In some African, Asian, and Middle Eastern countries, it is very widespread, but in the West, it is an uncommon entity. ISK’s regional distribution closely matches that of communities that consume a diet high in fluids and mostly composed of large vegetables and carbohydrates. ISK appears to be caused by an ileal loop encircling the base of a redundant sigmoidal loop in most cases, which is thought to be a variation of midgut volvulus[1].

Although the frequency of ISK is unknown, it typically occurs in regions where sigmoid volvulus (SV) is common[3]. In Eastern countries, 18–50% of SV cases are caused by ISK, whereas in Western countries, 5–8% are caused by ISK[3,4].

Initially, ISK was classified by Alver into four types. Type I: The active component, the ileum, encircles the passive component, the sigmoid colon, either clockwise (1) or counterclockwise (2). Type II: The active component, the sigmoid colon, rotates either clockwise or counterclockwise around an ileal loop, the passive component. Type III: The sigmoid colon (passive component) is encircled by the ileocecal segment (active component)[1]. It remains an uncertain or indefinite type (type IV) when it is impossible to identify the active or passive component[1,5]. The previous classification method does not provide detailed information about the management or prognosis of ISK. Here we present a 70-year-old female patient diagnosed with ISK upon intraoperative findings and successfully treated with surgery, derotation, and postoperative surgical intensive care unit (ICU) follow-up. This work has been prepared in line with the SCARE guideline[8].

Case presentation

A 70-year-old female patient, who was relatively healthy 4 days ago, started experiencing infraumbilical abdominal pain of 4 days’ duration, which was colicky and non-radiating in nature. Associated with this, she had a history of abdominal distension, which was progressive, and failure to pass feces for the same duration. She also reported a history of two episodes of vomiting of ingested matter and failure to pass flatus for 1 day. For these complaints, she visited a nearby private clinic, where she was given unspecified medication and referred to Ayder Comprehensive and Specialized Referal Hospital. She also had a history of anorexia and easy fatigability for 4 days. On physical examination, she appeared acutely sick-looking. Her pulse rate was 106 bpm, while other vital signs were within the normal range. Her abdomen moves with respiration, was grossly distended, hyper-tympanic to percussion, had hyperactive bowel sounds, and exhibited mild tenderness in the lower abdomen.

With the impression of small bowel obstruction secondary to primary volvulus, she was investigated with a complete blood count (hemoglobin: 6.1 g/dL, white blood cell count: 22 000 cells/µL, granulocyte: 92.6%) and an abdominal X-ray (multiple air-fluid levels with dilated bowel loops; Fig. 1). She was managed with resuscitation using three units of crystalloids, Ceftriaxone 1 gm IV BID, Metronidazole 500 mg IV TID, and a transurethral catheter, and she was prepared for surgery (exploratory laparotomy).

Intraoperative finding: 100 mL of reactive fluid in the general peritoneum, a 360° clockwise rotated small bowel over the sigmoid colon mesentery (Type IA ISK [Fig. 2]), and a distended sigmoid colon, which was dusky. Derotation of the small bowel was performed; the reactive fluid was suctioned out; the dusky sigmoid colon was packed with warm normal saline-soaked gauze for 20 minutes and regained its color; the abdominal cavity was closed in layers after the correct count was declared and hemostasis was secured; and the patient was transferred to the surgical ICU. She was discharged from our hospital on her 12th postoperative day, improved. She visited our referral clinic during her 1st, 2nd, and 3rd postoperative months, and her recovery was uneventful. She was discharged with advice regarding danger signs for follow-up.

Discussion

Studies showed ISK being common in the region called the “volvulus belt,” which includes Africa, South America, Russia, Eastern Europe, the Middle East, India, and Brazil[9]. According to a study by Abebe et al conducted in Ethiopia, the average age of ISK was 41.7 years (SD ± 19.5), with a range of 18–80 years. Unlike our case, ISK is more prevalent in men (a male-to-female ratio of 3:1)[9]. In Turkey, ISK is responsible for 1.7 of cases of mechanical intestinal obstruction[1,10].

For its etiology, it requires the unusual anatomical prerequisites of a movable small intestine with a long mesentery and a redundant sigmoid colon with an elongated and narrow-based mesentery[1,2,9]. The knot is initiated by a hyperactive ileum that encircles itself around the pedicle of the passive sigmoid loop. Other predisposing factors incriminated as triggering events are bulk feeding on an empty small intestine, which plays an important role in stimulating bowel activity; late pregnancy; intestinal malrotations; postoperative adhesions; internal herniation; and Meckel’s diverticulum[9,11,12].

All patients began with abdominal pain and vomiting, which were followed by abdominal distention (24, 85.7%) and obstipation (23, 82.1%)[9]. The (range 1–6) days was the average amount of time that symptoms persisted prior to presentation, which is similar to our case[1,10,13]. Because the phenomenon is uncommon and the radiologic results are atypical, preoperative diagnosis is frequently challenging[2]. In less than 20% of individuals, ISK is diagnosed prior to surgery. Perforated viscus (7.2%), SV (14.2%), and small bowel obstruction (50%) were the three most common misdiagnoses[9,14].

ISK cannot be diagnosed with specific blood tests. It has been demonstrated that gangrenous bowel disease is correlated with a higher white cell count (>18 000 × 109⁄L)[14]. A plain abdominal X-ray may reveal disproportionate distension with air-fluid levels in the sigmoid colon. The small intestine’s numerous air-fluid loops on the left side of the abdomen are enlarged in conjunction with this[1,14].

Effective surgery, adequate antimicrobial therapy, aggressive preoperative resuscitation, and postoperative metabolic care all contribute to a lower incidence of morbidity and mortality[10]. There is a lot of disagreement over the best surgical technique for ISK. It has been suggested that the sigmoid colon be deflated by needle deflation, colostomy, or en-bloc excision of the gangrenous colon because untwisting the knot is challenging and could result in toxin release and perforation[1,3,4,14]. A study done by Abebe et al shows that the most commonly performed procedure is resection of the gangrenous segments with primary ileoileal anastomosis and sigmoid end colostomy (57.1%); in 7.2% of patients with both viable ileum and sigmoid colon, untying only was done, similar to our case[9].

The speed at which a diagnosis is made, and surgery is performed determines the prognosis of ISK[14]. Higher mortality is linked to a number of factors, including advanced age, gangrenous bowel, delayed presentations and diagnosis, comorbidities, pregnancy, and shock (at presentation or after the administration of anesthesia). Despite the early presentation and lack of gangrene, the death rate after ISK is between 15 and 73%[15].

Conclusion

ISK is a rare but catastrophic cause of closed-loop bowel obstruction, with a high morbidity and mortality rate. The key to a good outcome is early diagnosis, aggressive resuscitation, adequate antimicrobial therapy, and immediate surgical intervention. Do not delay surgery for an extensive diagnostic workup if there is a high index of clinical suspicion.
