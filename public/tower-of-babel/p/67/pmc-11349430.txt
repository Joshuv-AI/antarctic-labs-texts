Both mOTS-words and pOTS-words prefer emoji stimuli over text stimuli during a lexical judgment task
Authors: Alexia Dalski, Holly Kular, Julia G Jorgensen, Kalanit Grill-Spector, Mareike Grotheer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe visual word form area in the occipitotemporal sulcus (here OTS-words) is crucial for reading and shows a preference for text stimuli. We hypothesized that this text preference may be driven by lexical processing. Hence, we performed three fMRI experiments (n = 15), systematically varying participants’ task and stimulus, and separately evaluated middle mOTS-words and posterior pOTS-words. Experiment 1 contrasted text with other visual stimuli to identify both OTS-words subregions. Experiment 2 utilized an fMRI adaptation paradigm, presenting compound words as texts or emojis. In experiment 3, participants performed a lexical or color judgment task on compound words in text or emoji format. In experiment 2, pOTS-words, but not mOTS-words, showed fMRI adaptation for compound words in both formats. In experiment 3, both subregions showed higher responses to compound words in emoji format. Moreover, mOTS-words showed higher responses during the lexical judgment task and a task–stimulus interaction. Multivariate analyses revealed that distributed responses in pOTS-words encode stimulus and distributed responses in mOTS-words encode stimulus and task. Together, our findings suggest that the function of the OTS-words subregions goes beyond the specific visual processing of text and that these regions are flexibly recruited whenever semantic meaning needs to be assigned to visual input.Keywords: visual word form area, reading, occipitotemporal sulcus, category selectivity, vision

Introduction

Reading text is a fundamental building block of communication in our society, and an inability to do so has profound negative consequences (Weiss et al. 1991; Cho et al. 2008; Ardila et al. 2010). An essential neural substrate of reading is the visual word form area (Cohen et al. 2000), a left-lateralized region (Cai et al. 2008; Dehaene et al. 2010; Strother et al. 2017; Canário et al. 2020) which responds more strongly to text than other visual stimuli (Cohen et al. 2000; Dehaene and Cohen 2011) and is critical for reading (Gaillard et al. 2006; van der Mark et al. 2009; Sandak et al. 2018). Here, we refer to this region as OTS-words due it its anatomical location in the occipitotemporal sulcus (OTS) and its functional preference for text.

Importantly, the functional role of OTS-words is not without debate. While the predominant view suggests that it is selectively involved in processing text (Cohen et al. 2000; Cohen and Dehaene 2004; Szwed et al. 2011), several studies found evidence against an exclusive text preference (Ben-Shachar et al. 2007; Mei et al. 2010; Vogel et al. 2012; Wright et al. 2008; for reviews see Price and Devlin 2003; Vogel et al. 2014; Dȩbska et al. 2023). Recent work further proposes a posterior-to-anterior increase in processing level along the OTS (Vinckier et al. 2007; Thesen et al. 2012; Carreiras et al. 2014; Caffarra et al. 2021), and in fact, it has been suggested that OTS-words can be divided into two subregions (middle mOTS-words and posterior pOTS-words) that differ in their functional properties, white matter connections, and functional connectivity (Stigliani et al. 2015; Lerma-Usabiaga et al. 2018; Yablonski et al. 2023). Here, we hypothesize that the debate on the functional role of OTS-words may be resolved by evaluating the two subregions separately, as it is possible that only one of the subregions is selective for text. Moreover, we postulate that it is important to also take the participants’ task into consideration. In many prior experiments, only the text stimuli were readable, so the observed text preference may have been confounded with a preference for lexical processing. Indeed, recent studies have found higher responses for reading than other tasks in OTS-words supporting this notion (Grotheer et al. 2018; Grotheer et al. 2019; Qu et al. 2022; White et al. 2023). We hence derived three hypotheses (Fig. 1a–c): H1: Both OTS-words subregions prefer text over other stimuli, irrespective of task. H2: Both OTS-words subregions prefer lexical tasks over other tasks, irrespective of the stimulus. H3: There is an anterior-to-posterior gradient in processing level, indicating that mOTS-words is selective for lexical tasks and pOTS-words is selective for text stimuli.

Tasks: Subjects were instructed to either read the word pair and indicate if they form a meaningful English compound word (lexical judgment task) or to compare the color hues of the two stimuli (color judgment task).

To distinguish between these hypotheses, in three fMRI experiments, we systematically varied the stimulus and the participant’s task and assessed mean and distributed responses in mOTS-words and pOTS-words (Fig. 1d–f). In experiment 1, we contrasted responses to text with other visual categories. We used experiment 1 data to identify mOTS-words and pOTS-words based on their text preference, as in previous studies (Stigliani et al. 2015; Rosenke et al. 2021). In experiment 2, we used fMRI adaptation to probe the stimulus sensitivity of mOTS-words and pOTS-words, comparing text and non-text stimuli (emojis). In experiment 3, subjects performed a lexical and a color judgment task on text and non-text stimuli (emojis).

Our data show that mOTS-words and pOTS-words can be identified based on their preference for text. Strikingly, however, even though regions were identified through their sensitivity towards text, our univariate analyses showed higher responses for emojis than texts in both subregions. In pOTS-words, we further observed fMRI adaptation for both text and emoji stimuli, while mOTS-words showed a preference for the lexical judgment over the color judgment task. Multivariate analyses aligned and showed that pOTS-words encodes the visual stimulus, while mOTS-words encodes both the visual stimulus and the performed task. These results expand our understanding of the OTS-words subregions, suggesting that they are flexibly recruited for processing a wider array of symbolic stimuli.

Materials and methods

Participants

Eighteen right-handed volunteers (11 female, 7 male, mean age 28.4 years, SD = 12.8 years) were recruited from Stanford University and surrounding areas and participated in two experimental sessions (session 1: experiments 1 and 2; session 2: experiment 3), conducted on different days. Two participants had to be excluded due to excessive head motions (>3 voxels within and/or 3.5 voxels between scan motion), and one participant was excluded due to an incidental finding. The final data set contained 15 subjects (8 female, 7 male). Subjects had normal or corrected to normal vision and gave their informed written consent. The Stanford Internal Review Board on Human Subjects Research approved all procedures.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Acquisition

Data were collected at the Center for Cognitive and Neurobiological Imaging at Stanford University, using a GE 3-T SIGNA Scanner with a 32-channel head coil. A total of 48 slices were acquired, covering the occipitotemporal and most of the frontal cortex using a T2*-sensitive gradient echo sequence (resolution: 2.4 mm × 2.4 mm × 2.4 mm, TR: 1,000 ms, TE: 30 ms, FoV: 192 mm, flip angle: 62°, multiplexing factor of 3). A whole-brain, anatomical volume was acquired as well, once for each participant, using a T1-weighted BRAVO pulse sequence (resolution: 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm, TI = 450 ms, flip angle: 12°, 1 NEX, FoV: 240 mm).

Preprocessing

The anatomical brain volume of each participant was segmented into gray and white matter using FreeSurfer (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). Manual corrections were made using ITKGray (http://web.stanford.edu/group/vista/cgi-bin/wiki/index.php/ItkGray), and each subject’s cortical surface was reconstructed. Functional data were analyzed using the mrVista toolbox (http://github.com/vistalab) for MATLAB. Regions of interest were defined on inflated cortical surfaces. The fMRI data from each experiment were motion corrected, both within and between runs, and then aligned to the anatomical volume. To increase precision, all analyses were conducted in the native brain space of each participant, and no spatial smoothing was applied. The time course of each voxel was high-pass filtered with a 1/20-Hz cutoff and transformed from arbitrary units to percentage signal change. For each experiment, we created a separate design matrix and convolved it with the hemodynamic response function implemented in SPM (http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) to generate predictors for each experimental condition. Regularized response coefficients (betas) were estimated for each voxel and each predictor using a general linear model (GLM) indicating the magnitude of response for that condition. Motion was not treated as a regressor in the general linear models.

Experimental design

Experiment 1: localizer

The first experiment was designed to detect voxels which show higher responses for text compared to other visual stimuli, as in previous work (Stigliani et al. 2015).

Stimuli

Stimuli were images belonging to one of five different categories: text, faces, limbs, objects, and houses. In order to also detect color-sensitive voxels, the stimuli were presented either in grayscale or in color.

Trial structure

All stimuli were shown for 500 ms, with an interstimulus interval of 0 s, in blocks of 8 stimuli. Each participant completed 4 runs; each run was 5 min long and included 10 repetitions of each stimulus category.

Task

Participants were asked to fixate a black dot in the center of the screen and to indicate immediate repetitions of a stimulus by pressing a button (1-back task, Fig. 1d). For further details on the experimental design, see Stigliani et al. (2015).

Experiment 2: fMRI adaptation

The goal of the second experiment was to leverage fMRI adaptation effects to probe stimulus preferences in the previously identified OTS-words and control regions, with high spatial accuracy. fMRI adaptation leverages the phenomenon that repeated exposure to a specific stimulus leads to decreased neural activity if voxels within the evaluated brain region are engaged in processing that stimulus. By comparing repeated and non-repeated trials of different stimuli, we could hence investigate what type of stimulus information the OTS-words subregions are sensitive to.

Stimuli

Stimuli consisted of two pairs of images, presented at the center of the screen, whereas each pair formed an English compound word (e.g. raincoat). The compound words were presented in three different formats, as emojis, as text, or in a mixed format, in which one pair was an emoji and one pair was text. The compound words primarily represented a wide range of everyday objects, including food, clothing, tools, household items, and animals. They consisted of 40 unique words that were combined into 36 image pairs, each representing a compound noun. All stimuli were either white or black on a gray background. Half of the stimuli were presented in white; the other half in black. A full overview over all the image pairs used in this experiment can be found in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S1a).

Trial structure

In each trial, two image pairs were presented, which were either repeated (e.g. “raincoat” followed by “raincoat”) or non-repeated (e.g. “raincoat” followed by “sunflower”). Each pair was presented for 1 s (Fig. 1e), with an interstimulus interval of 0.2 s and an intertrial interval of 1 s. Participants completed 6 runs; each run had a duration of ~ 5 min and contained 72 experiment trials. Trial conditions were balanced in regard to stimulus format and repetition within each run.

Task

The image pairs were presented either in black or white in ~ 50% of the trials. Subjects had to press a button whenever they saw a white image pair (black/white detection).

Experiment 3: task and stimulus preferences

The goal of experiment 3 was to probe the influence of different tasks (lexical judgment task vs. color judgment task) and stimuli (emoji vs. text stimuli) on neural responses in the OTS-words subregions.

Stimuli

Participants were presented with two stimuli shown consecutively, each stimulus pair depicting an English compound word. The stimuli were either both in text or emoji format. The compound words primarily represented a wide range of everyday objects, including food, clothing, tools, household items, and animals. They consisted of 39 unique words that were combined into 24 compound nouns. Each stimulus pair was either shown in the correct order, forming a meaningful English compound (sun + flower), or in the reversed order, forming a meaningless term (flower + sun). The meaningless terms were always inverted real words. All pairs were presented in color on a gray background. The pairs had either the exact same color or similar, yet distinguishable, hues (Fig. 1f). Behavioral pilot experiments performed on Amazon Mechanical Turk (MTurk) and in the laboratory were used to (i) ensure that the compound words are easily identifiable even in emoji format and (ii) titrate the hues used in the color task, until task difficulty was matched between the color and the lexical task. An overview of the stimulus set can be found in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S1b). Stimulus and task conditions were balanced across runs, with each compound noun appearing equally often in each stimulus and task condition (emoji vs. text; color vs. lexical). Matching (color judgment: same color nuance; lexical judgment: meaningful compound) and mismatching (color judgment: different color nuance; lexical judgment: reversed compound order) trials were balanced across runs as well.

Trial structure

At the beginning of a trial, participants were given a cue indicating which task should be performed (“Read” for the lexical judgment or “Color” for the color judgment task), which was shown for 1 s. After the cue, the two images were shown consecutively, with an interstimulus interval of 0 s, each for 1 s, followed by an answer screen presented for 2 s (Fig. 1f, bottom). The interstimulus interval between the cue and the stimuli, as well as between stimuli and answer screen, was randomized and jittered with durations of 1, 2, or 3 s. The intertrial interval was randomized to 1, 2, or 3 s. Subjects completed 8 runs, each lasting ~ 5 min and containing 24 trials. Stimulus and task conditions were balanced across runs so that each compound word was presented equally often in each experimental condition. In 16% of the trials, we presented only the cue or only the answer screen. These “blank trials” were used to remove any impact of the cue and answer screen on the BOLD responses measured for the different stimuli and tasks.

Task

The experiment included two tasks, a lexical judgment and a color judgment task. When performing the lexical task, participants had to decide whether the stimuli form a meaningful English compound word (match) or not (mismatch). The color judgment task required participants to carefully compare the hues of the two presented stimuli, and to decide whether they are the same (match) or slightly different (mismatch). Participants responded by selecting “match” or “mismatch” on the answer screen via a button press, while the correct answer was presented randomly either on the left or the right side of the answer screen.

Regions-of-interest definition

Functional regions of interest (fROIs) were defined in the native brain space of each participant, using both functional and anatomical criteria. We used all runs from the first experiment (localizer) to define the fROIs. The fROIs defined during experiment 1 were then used to evaluate responses in the other two experiments. We did not apply any spatial smoothing and avoided group-based analyses, as those increase the risk of spurious overlap between closely neighboring fROIs (Weiner and Grill-Spector 2013). We defined several fROIs in the ventral temporal cortex.

OTS-words

We localized the OTS-words subregions bilaterally by contrasting responses to text stimuli with responses to other visual categories (limbs, faces, objects, and houses). Consistent with prior results (Lerma-Usabiaga et al. 2018), we found two self-contained regions in the left middle and posterior occipitotemporal sulcus (OTS) that showed a preference for text over other visual stimuli (T = 3, voxel level, uncorrected). We refer to them as middle OTS-words (mOTS-words) and posterior OTS-words (pOTS-words). We were able to define left pOTS-words in all 15 participants. On average, the size of left pOTS-words was 722.8 mm3 (SD = 459.9 mm3, n = 15). We were also able to identify left mOTS-words in all 15 subjects. The average size of left mOTS-words was 444.2 mm3 (SD = 350.7 mm3, n = 15). In the right hemisphere, we were able to identify mOTS-words (average size: 234.6 mm3, SD = 313.5 mm3) in 11 subjects and pOTS-words (average size: 266.9 mm3, SD = 163 mm3) in all 15 subjects.

Color-sensitive region

Previous studies described three color-sensitive patches along the left midfusiform sulcus, containing voxels which showed significantly higher responses for colored compared to grayscale images (T = 3, voxel level, uncorrected) (Lafer-Sousa et al. 2016). For this study, we focus on the central color region (Cc-color), as it closely neighbors the OTS-words subregions. Cc-color could be defined bilaterally for all 15 subjects, spanning on average 409.4 mm3 (SD = 238.7 mm3, n = 15) on the left and 399.7 mm3 (SD = 193.8 mm3, n = 15) on the right hemisphere.

Additionally, we also defined constant size disk fROIs, with a diameter of 7.5 mm, for mOTS-words, pOTS-words, and Cc-color in both hemispheres, to match fROI sizes across participants for the multivoxel pattern analyses (MVPAs, see below). These disk fROIs were centered on the respective region, and the radius of the disk was chosen to match the average size of the OTS-words subregions and Cc-color.

Additional control ROIs CoS-places, mFus-faces, and pFus-faces

In addition, three other control ROIs were defined in the left hemisphere: two patches in the fusiform gyrus that showed stronger responses to faces in contrast to limbs, text, houses, and objects (T = 3, voxel level, uncorrected); one in the midfusiform gyrus (mFus-faces); and one in the posterior fusiform gyrus (pFus-faces). Furthermore, we defined a ventral place-selective region, in the collateral sulcus (CoS-places), based on higher responses toward houses compared to other visual categories. Results of these additional control ROIs can be found in the Supplemental Material (Figs. S4, S6, S9, S11, and S13).

Statistical analysis

Univariate analysis

We first extracted the average time course in percentage signal change for each condition from mOTS-words, pOTS-words, and the control regions. Then, we applied a GLM to estimate betas, indicating the magnitude of response for each condition. Consequently, the bar graphs in Figs. 2 and 4 show betas in units of percent signal change ± SEM. Importantly, we used independent data for fROI definition (experiment 1) and signal extraction (experiments 2 and 3). We conducted repeated measures analyses of variance (rmANOVAs) on the betas from each fROI. Exp 2: rmANOVAs used stimulus (emoji, text, mixed) and repetition (repeated vs. non-repeated) as factors; Exp 3: rmANOVAs used stimulus (emoji or text) and task (lexical or color judgment) as factors. Initially, we also included the matching condition (matched vs. mismatched) as a factor, but we did not observe any differences in either the neural or behavioral data between match and mismatch trials and we hence averaged across matched and mismatched trials for the analyses presented here. We also conducted rmANOVAs for both Exp 2 and Exp 3 where we added ROI (mOTS-words, pOTS-words) as an additional factor to compare responses across the two OTS-words subregions. Post hoc tests were applied, when the rmANOVAs revealed significant main effects or interaction effects between factors. Where applicable, we used Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Multivoxel pattern analysis

We conducted MVPAs (Haxby et al. 2001) on distributed responses across the 7.5-mm disk fROIs for mOTS-words, pOTS-words, and Cc-color. A GLM was calculated to estimate the responses to each experimental condition separately for each voxel. The responses were then normalized by subtracting each voxel’s mean response and z-standardized.

For each experiment and each condition, we calculated the correlation among each pair of multivoxel patterns (MVPs), using a leave-one-run-out procedure. These correlations were summarized in representational similarity matrices (RSMs). By using the leave-one-run-out procedure as cross-validation, we were able to evaluate the reliability of the observed MVP across runs. The RSMs were calculated for each subject separately and then averaged across subjects. In addition to that, we trained winner-takes-all (WTA) classifiers separately on the responses of experiments 2 and 3, to observe which experimental conditions can be decoded from the MVPs of each fROIs. In experiment 2, classifiers were initially trained to decode stimulus (emoji, text, mixed) and repetition (repeated vs. non-repeated). However, as the mixed condition includes both emoji and text stimuli, the classifier could not learn patterns associated with each stimulus format when this condition was included. We therefore reran the MVPAs for experiment 2 excluding all mixed trials. For experiment 3, WTA classifiers were trained to decode stimulus (emoji vs. text) and task (lexical judgment vs. color judgment). All classifiers used a leave-one-run-out procedure, where the training set consisted of all runs but one, and the test set included the left-out run. Results report the aggregated classifier performances across all iterations of leave-one-run-out combinations. To quantify the classifiers’ performance, we computed paired t-tests against chance level. Where applicable, Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied.

We also created RSMs combining responses from experiments 2 and 3, to test MVPs for stimulus (emoji vs. text) and tasks (lexical, color, black/white detection) across experiments. We included all conditions from experiment 3, and all emoji and text trials from experiment 2, averaging repeated and non-repeated trials. We normalized the data by subtracting the mean response separately for each experiment before merging them, as the average response levels differed between experiments. As experiments 2 and 3 also differed in their number of runs, a leave-one-run-out procedure was not feasible, which is why we divided the data set into even and odd runs for the multivoxel pattern analysis. RSMs were calculated for each subject separately and then averaged across subjects.

Finally, we conducted additional rmANOVAs on the WTA classifier decoding accuracy with decoding type (Exp 2: stimulus and repetition, Exp 3: stimulus and task) and ROI (mOTS-words, pOTS-words) as factors, to compare decoding performance across the OTS-words subregions. Post hoc tests were applied, when the rmANOVAs revealed significant main effects or interaction effects between factors. Where applicable, we used Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Analysis of behavioral responses during fMRI

We calculated each subject’s average performance (measured in % correct ± SE) and reaction times separately for each experiment. Furthermore, we tested for significant performance differences between experimental conditions by conducting rmANOVAs on the behavioral responses. Exp 1: rmANOVAs used stimulus (words, limbs, objects, houses, faces) and color (colored vs. grayscale) as factors; Exp 2: rmANOVAs used stimulus (emoji, text, mixed) and repetition of stimulus (repeated vs. non-repeated) as factors; Exp 3: rmANOVAs used stimulus (emoji or text) and task (lexical or color judgment) as factors. Post hoc tests were applied when the rmANOVAs revealed significant main effects or interaction effects between factors. Where applicable, Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied.

Results

Task difficulty was well matched across conditions in each of the three experiments

The goal of the first experiment was to localize mOTS-words and pOTS-words in each participant by contrasting responses to text stimuli with those elicited by other visual stimulus categories. Subjects (n = 15) were presented with images of text, limbs, faces, objects, and houses while they performed a 1-back task. We used rmANOVAs to compare performance and reaction time across conditions. On average, participants’ accuracy (±SE) was 60% (±4%). Participants had to respond within 1 s, which could explain the relatively low accuracy during the 1-back task. There was no significant difference in accuracy between the different stimulus categories (F(4,11) = 0.45, P = 0.77) or between colored and grayscale images (F(1,14) = 0.81, P = 0.38) and no interaction between stimulus and color (F(4,11) = 1.68, P = 0.17). Participants’ reaction time (±SE) was on average 260 ms (±40 ms) and did not differ between stimulus categories (F(4,11) = 0.47, P = 0.76) or between colored or grayscale images (F(1,14) = 2.13, P = 0.12). There was also no interaction between stimulus and color (F(4,11) = 0.53, P = 0.71).

The goal of the second experiment (fMRI adaptation) was to test whether there is a difference in neural adaptation for emojis compared to text stimuli in mOTS-words and pOTS-words. The images were shown either in white or black and the participant’s task was to press a button when white images instead of black images were shown. We tested for differences in subjects’ performance (±SE) as well as in their reaction times (±SE) by conducting rmANOVAs of behavioral responses with repetition and stimulus as factors. On average, participants responded correctly in 91% (±1%) of the trials. Participants’ performance did not significantly differ between emoji, text, and mixed trials (F(2,13) = 0.40, P = 0.68) or between repeated and non-repeated trials (F(1,14) = 0.20, P = 0.66). We found no interaction effect between stimulus and repetition (F(2,13) = 0.70, P = 0.55). The subjects’ reaction time (±SE) was on average 304 ms (±10 ms). We found no significant differences in response time between emoji, text, and mixed trials (F(2,13) = 0.99, P = 0.38) or between repeated and non-repeated trials (F(1,14) = 0.10, P = 0.75) and no interaction between stimulus and repetition (F(2,13) = 0.69, P = 0.5).

The third experiment aimed to uncover the influence of different tasks (lexical vs. color judgment) and stimuli (text vs. emoji) on responses in mOTS-words and pOTS-words. We performed rmANOVAs, using task and stimulus as factors to evaluate performance and reaction times. On average, subjects’ performance (±SE) was 95% (±2%) correct. There were no effects of task (F(1,14) = 0.33, P = 0.67) or stimulus (F(1,14) = 0.22, P = 0.65) and no interaction effect between stimulus and task (F(1,14) = 0.21, P = 0.65) on participants’ accuracy. Participant’s average reaction time (±SE) was 669 ms (±25 ms). We found a significant main effect of stimulus (F(1,14) = 8.17, P = 0.01) and task (F(1,14) = 6.50, P = 0.02) and a significant interaction between stimulus and task (F(1,14) = 7.99, P = 0.01) on participant’s reaction times. Post hoc analyses revealed significantly longer reaction times for emoji compared to text stimuli in the lexical judgment task (P = 0.0004).

mOTS-words and pOTS-words were defined in the left hemisphere based on higher responses to text than other visual stimuli

We identified mOTS-words and pOTS-words in the left hemisphere in all 15 participants by contrasting responses to text stimuli with responses elicited by other visual categories (faces, objects, limbs, and houses). In order to identify the color patches along the midfusiform sulcus, in experiment 1, we contrasted responses to colored images with grayscale images. Three color regions were defined. Here, we focus on Cc-color, as it was successfully identified in all 15 subjects and is adjacent to both OTS-words subregions in the left hemisphere. Cc-color was used as a control region to evaluate the specificity of the response patterns observed in mOTS-words and pOTS-words. Figure 2a shows mOTS-words, pOTS-words, and Cc-Color in a representative subject in the left hemisphere; an overview of all subjects can be found in the Supplementary Material Fig. S2. Examples for the additional control regions mFus-faces, pFus-faces and CoS-places can be found in Supplementary Fig. S4.

pOTS-words, but not mOTS-words, shows fMRI adaptation for both emoji and text stimuli

In experiment 2, we leveraged an fMRI-adaptation design to test if the OTS-words subregions are only responsive toward text stimuli or also other symbolic non-text stimuli, such as emojis. We conducted rmANOVAs with factors of repetition (repeated, non-repeated) and stimulus format (emoji, text, mixed). For pOTS-words, we found no significant main effect of stimulus (F(2,13) = 1.53, P = 0.23), but a main effect of repetition (F(1,14) = 14.99, P = 0.002), as well as an interaction between repetition and stimulus (F(2,13) = 5.30, P = 0.01). Post hoc tests revealed a significant effect of repetition for both text (P = 0.001) and emoji stimuli (P = 0.03), but not for the mixed condition (P = 0.39) (Fig. 2b). mOTS-words did not show significant effects of repetition (F(1,14) = 1.12, P = 0.31), stimulus format (F(2,13) = 0.90, P = 0.42), or an interaction (F(2,13) = 2.47, P = 0.10) (Fig. 2c).

After examining mOTS-words and pOTS-words separately, we wanted to explicitly test if there are significant differences across these regions. To this end, we conducted additional rmANOVAs adding ROI (mOTS-words, pOTS-words) as a factor. In experiment 2, we found no main effect of stimulus (F(2,13) = 1.56, P = 0.23), but a main effect of repetition (F(1,14) = 5.10, P = 0.04) and ROI (F(1,14) =34.00, P < 0.0001). We also observed a significant interaction between stimulus and repetition (F(2,13) = 4.94, P = 0.01). Post hoc tests revealed significant differences between repeated and non-repeated trials for text stimuli only (text: P = 0.008, emoji: P = 0.09, mixed: P = 0.23). We found no interaction between repetition and ROI (F(1,14) = 1.88, P = 0.19) or stimulus and ROI (F(2,13) = 0.63, P = 0.58). The three-way interaction of ROI, stimulus, and repetition was not significant as well (F(2,13) = 0.17, P = 0.85).

For the first control ROI, Cc-color, we found a main effect of stimulus (F(2,13) = 32.98, P < 0.0001) and a main effect of repetition (F(1,14) = 5.37, P = 0.04). Furthermore, we found a significant interaction between repetition and stimulus (F(2,13) = 4.11, P = 0.03) (Fig. 2d). Post hoc tests revealed a significant difference between repeated and non-repeated trials for the emoji stimuli (P = 0.007), but not for the text (P = 0.39), or the mixed condition (P = 0.94).

Detailed results of the right hemisphere can be found in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S3). In brief, for pOTS-words a main effect of repetition (F(1,14) = 5.68, P = 0.03) and stimulus (F(2,13) = 12.38, P < 0.0001) was found, along with a significant interaction (F(2,13) = 4.93, P = 0.02) (Fig. S3b). Post hoc tests showed significant differences between repeated and non-repeated trials for text (P = 0.03) and emoji stimuli (P = 0.01), but not mixed stimuli (P= 0.78). For mOTS-words in the right hemisphere, no main effect of repetition (F(1,10) = 2.49, P = 0.15) or stimulus (F(2,9) = 1.32, P = 0.3) and no interaction (F(2,9) = 0.75, P = 0.48) was observed (Fig. S3c). For Cc-color, main effects of stimulus (F(2,13) = 42.73, P < 0.0001) and repetition (F(1,14) = 5.65, P = 0.03) were observed, but no significant interaction effect was found (F(2,13) = 2.84, P = 0.07) (Fig. S3d).

Results for the additional control ROIs CoS-places, mFus-faces, and pFus-faces are also shown in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S4). In brief, we found for CoS-places (n = 13) no significant main effect of repetition (F(1,12) = 0.54, P = 0.47), no significant interaction between stimulus and repetition (F(2,11) = 2.02, P = 0.15), but a significant main effect of stimulus (F(2,11) = 9.67, P < 0.0001) (Fig. S4b). For mFus-faces (n = 15), we found significant main effects of both repetition (F(1,14) = 5.18, P = 0.03) and stimulus (F(2,13) = 3.44, P = 0.04) with no significant interaction effect (F(2,13) = 2.24, P = 0.13) (Fig. S4c). For pFus-faces (n = 15), a significant main effect of repetition (F(1,14) = 5.78, P = 0.03) was observed, but no significant main effect of stimulus (F(2,13) = 1.02, P = 0.37) or an interaction effect (F(2,13) = 2.71, P = 0.08) were found (Fig. S4d).

Distributed responses in mOTS-words and pOTS-words encode stimulus but not repetition

In addition to the univariate results presented above, we also probed what information can be decoded from distributed responses within mOTS-words, pOTS-words, and the control regions. For this, we performed MVPA within 7.5-mm disk ROIs centered on these ROIs. We used a constant disk ROI to equate the number of voxels used in the MVPA across participants.

In experiment 2, we generated an RSM comparing repeated and non-repeated emoji and text trials using a leave-one-run-out-approach. Each cell in the RSM reflects the correlation between distributed responses over the ROI across 2 conditions. Thus, the diagonal of the RSM indicates similarity with the same condition across runs, and the off diagonal indicates similarity among different conditions across runs. For left pOTS-words, the RSM revealed positive correlations for the same stimuli across repeated and non-repeated trials (Fig. 3a). To quantify if information about stimulus and/or repetition can be successfully decoded from the distributed responses, we trained WTA (winner-takes-all) classifiers. We found that the stimulus but not repetition can be successfully decoded from distributed pOTS-words responses. Classifier performance reached an accuracy (±SEM) of 87.30% (±4.33%) for stimulus, significantly exceeding 50% chance level (P < 0.0001, significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.01). Classifier performances for distinguishing between repeated and non-repeated trials reached an accuracy (±SEM) of 50.67% (±4.53%), which did not exceed chance level (P = 0.86, not significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.01) (Fig. 3b). Accordingly, a direct comparison showed that the classifier decoding stimulus yielded a significantly higher performance than the classifier differentiating between repeated and non-repeated trials (P < 0.0001).

For left mOTS-words, we observed high MVP correlations for the same stimuli across repeated and non-repeated trials (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, WTA classifier performances (±SEM) reached 78.89% (±3.89%) accuracy for decoding stimulus, significantly exceeding 50% chance level (P < 0.0001, significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.01) (Fig. 3d). Classifier performances (±SEM) for distinguishing between repeated and non-repeated trials was 52.5% (±1.76%) and did not exceed 50% chance level (P = 0.21, not significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.01). Accordingly, the classifier decoding stimulus performed significantly better than the one decoding repetition (P < 0.0001).

To further investigate if there are significant differences in the information contained in the distributed responses across the OTS-words subregions, we conducted rmANOVAs on the WTA classifier decoding accuracy with decoding type (stimulus and repetition) and ROI (mOTS-words, pOTS-words) as factors. In experiment 2, we found a main effect of decoding type (F(1,14) = 47.96, P < 0.0001), but no main effect of ROI (F(1,14) = 1.29, P = 0.28) and no interaction effect (F(1,14) = 3.18, P = 0.10).

For left Cc-color, the RSM revealed a clear stimulus effect, showing positive correlations between repeated and non-repeated trials when the same stimulus was presented (Fig. 3e). We again trained WTA classifiers to test what kind of information (stimulus, repetition, or both) can be successfully decoded from distributed responses in Cc-color (Fig. 3f). Classification performance (mean ± SEM) for decoding stimulus yielded 81.69% (±5.61%). Decoding accuracy was significantly above 50% chance level (P < 0.0001, significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.01). Classification performance (±SEM) for detecting repeated trials was 58.25% (±3.56%) and exceeded 50% chance level (P = 0.003, significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.01). When comparing the average performances for decoding stimulus versus repetition, we found that the classifier decoding stimulus performed significantly better (P = 0.001).

For the right hemisphere, we observed similar results. Detailed results are presented in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S5). In brief, the RSM created for right pOTS-words showed high MVP correlations for the same stimulus across repeated and non-repeated trials (Fig. S5a). Furthermore, we found high classification accuracy (mean ± SEM) for stimulus (85.03% ± 4.79%), significantly exceeding chance (P < 0.0001). This was not the case for repetition (53.78% ± 3.1%), where decoding accuracy did not exceed chance level (P = 0.11) (Fig. S5b). Stimulus decoding outperformed repetition decoding (P < 0.0001). A similar correlation pattern was found for mOTS-words in the right hemisphere (Fig. S5c). We observed a high classification accuracy for stimulus decoding (74.24% ± 4.39%), exceeding chance level (P < 0.0001). Repetition decoding (53.79% ± 5.32%) did not exceed chance level (P = 0.15) (Fig. S5d). Stimulus decoding was significantly higher than repetition decoding (P < 0.0001). For Cc-color, in the right hemisphere, we observed high MVP correlations when the same stimulus was presented (Fig. S5e). A high classification accuracy for decoding stimulus (85.8% ± 4.04%) significantly exceeded chance level (P < 0.0001). Decoding accuracy for repetition was lower and did not exceed chance level (54.17% ± 3.13%, P = 0.09) (Fig. S5f). Stimulus decoding outperformed repetition decoding (P < 0.0001).

For additional control regions, left mFus-faces (n = 15) and left pFus-faces (n = 15), we observed a similar pattern of MVP correlations in the RSMs (Fig. S6c, e) and classifier performances for decoding stimulus were significantly higher than chance level for both ROIs (mFus-faces: 85.11% ± 3.5%, P < 0.0001; pFus-faces 88.03% ± 4.1%, P < 0.0001) (Fig. S6d, f). This was not the case for decoding repetition (mFus-faces: 51.25% ± 3.69%, P = 0.67; pFus-faces: 51.19 ± 4.75%, P = 0.73). Stimulus decoding significantly outperformed repetition decoding (mFus-faces: P < 0.0001; pFus-faces: P < 0.0001). The RSM for CoS-places (N=13) revealed higher MVP correlations for conditions involving the same stimulus (Fig. S6a) and classifier performance was significantly above chance for decoding stimulus (68.75% ±5.3%, p < 0.0001), but not for decoding repetition (55.93% ±2.85%, p = 0.73) (Fig. S6b). Decoding stimulus was significantly better than decoding repetition (p < 0.0001).For this multivariate analysis, we chose not to include the mixed trials, as this condition included both emoji and text stimuli, preventing the classifier from learning information about the stimulus. RSMs including this condition for mOTS-words, pOTS-words, and Cc-color in both hemispheres can be found in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S7).

Both OTS-words subregions prefer emojis over text and mOTS-words shows higher responses during the lexical judgment task

In experiment 3, we probed task and stimulus preferences in the OTS-words subregions and the control regions by manipulating the participant’s tasks and the presented stimuli orthogonally. In pOTS-words, rmANOVAs using stimulus and task as factors revealed a main effect of stimulus (F(1,14) = 12.37, P = 0.003), with a preference for emoji stimuli, but no main effect of task (F(1,14) = 3.85, P = 0.07) and no significant interaction between task and stimulus (F(1,14) = 1.91, P = 0.18) (Fig. 4a). Post hoc tests revealed higher responses for emoji stimuli than text stimuli in pOTS-words (P = 0.003). For mOTS-words, we found a main effect of stimulus (F(1,14) = 7.54, P = 0.02) and a main effect of task (F(1,14) = 13.28, P = 0.003) with higher responses during the lexical judgment task (Fig. 4b). We also observed an interaction between task and stimulus (F(1,14) = 7.66, P = 0.02). Post hoc tests revealed higher responses for emoji than text stimuli during the lexical judgment task (P = 0.0005).

After evaluating left mOTS-words and pOTS-words separately, we added ROI as an additional factor to the rmANOVAs to directly compare task and stimulus preferences across these subregions. We found a main effect of task (F(1,14) = 11.56, P = 0.004), a main effect of stimulus (F(1,14) = 27.48, P = 0.0001), and a main effect of ROI (F(1,14) = 6.86, P = 0.02). No significant interaction effect between ROI and stimulus (F(1,14) = 0.776, P = 0.39) was found. The interaction effect between task and stimulus was marginally significant (F(1,14) = 4.53, P = 0.05). The interaction between task and ROI was not significant, although a trend was observed (F(1,14) = 3.35, P = 0.09). The three-way interaction between task, stimulus, and ROI was not significant (F(1,14) = 2.13, P = 0.17).

Left Cc-color showed a main effect of stimulus (F(1,14) = 81.62, P < 0.0001) with a preference for emoji stimuli over text stimuli. Moreover, we found a significant main effect of task (F(1,14) = 20.85, P < 0.0001), with a preference for the color judgment task over the lexical judgment task in Cc-color. This established a double dissociation of task preferences with mOTS-words (Fig. 4c). We found no interaction between task and stimulus (F(1,14) = 2.16, P = 0.16) in Cc-color.

Detailed results for the right hemisphere can be found in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S8). In brief, for right pOTS-words no main effect of task (F(1,14) = 0.01, P = 0.92) was observed, but a main effect of stimulus (F(1,14) = 44.75, P < 0.0001) and an interaction between task and stimulus (F(1,14) = 7.34, P = 0.02) were found (Fig. S8a). Post hoc tests showed a preference for emojis over text in both tasks (lexical: P < 0.0001, color: P = 0.0003). For right mOTS-words, a main effect of stimulus (F(1,10) = 15.73, P = 0.003) was found, but no main effect of task (F(1,10) = 1.57, P = 0.70) and no interaction (F(1,10) = 0.7, P = 0.41) were seen (Fig. S8b). The univariate analyses for Cc-color in the right hemisphere revealed significant stimulus (F(1, 14) = 106.65, P < 0.0001) and task effects (F(1,14) = 10.62, P = 0.0085), with higher responses for emojis and the color judgment task (Fig. S8c). No interaction effect (F(1,14) = 0.00, P = 0.94) was found.

For the additional control ROIs CoS-places, mFus-faces, and pFus-faces, results are presented in the Supplementary Material (Fig. S9). In brief, for CoS-places, we found no main effect of task (F(1,12) = 1.63, P = 0.23), but a main effect for stimulus with a preference for emoji stimuli (F(1,12) = 18.42, P = 0.001) (Fig. S9a). No interaction effect between task and stimulus (F(1,12) = 0.00, P = 0.97) was found. Similarly, for mFus-faces we found no main effect of task (F(1,14) = 1.58, P = 0.23), but a stimulus effect (F(1,14) = 10.9, P = 0.005), with a preference for emoji stimuli (Fig. S9b). No significant interaction effect was found (F(1,14) = 3.84, P = 0.07). For pFus-faces, no main effect of stimulus (F(1,14) = 0.002, P = 0.96) or task (F(1,14) = 0.38, P = 0.54) and no interaction effect (F(1,14) = 1.23, P = 0.29) were observed (Fig. S9c).

Task and stimulus decodability differ between the OTS-words subregions

We also performed multivariate analyses on experiment 3 data to further investigate task and stimulus encoding in the OTS-words subregions. We created RSMs comparing response correlations for the different stimuli (text vs. emoji) and tasks (lexical vs. color), again using a leave-one-run-out approach. In addition, WTA classifiers were trained separately for stimulus and task to determine which kind of information (stimulus, task, or both) can be successfully decoded from distributed responses. As Fig. 5a shows, in pOTS-words, the RSM revealed positive correlations for the same stimulus under different tasks. Classification performance (±SEM) for decoding stimulus was 86.67% (±4.59%) and was significantly higher than 50% chance level (P < 0.0001, significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.01). When differentiating between tasks, the classifier’s performance (±SEM) was on average at 65.21% (±3.71%) with decoding accuracy significantly exceeding 50% chance level (P < 0.0001, significant with Bonferroni-corrected threshold of P < 0.01) (Fig. 5b). A direct comparison showed that decoding accuracy for decoding the stimulus was significantly higher than for decoding the task (P < 0.0001). These results suggest that while both the stimulus and task are decodable from distributed responses in pOTS-words, pOTS-words contains more information regarding the visual stimulus than the task.

Joined MVPAs of experiment 2 and 3 reveal differential sensitivities to tasks and stimuli across the OTS-words subregions

Finally, we merged data from experiments 2 and 3 to further probe distributed responses elicited by different stimuli and tasks across experiments. For experiment 2, we averaged across repeated and non-repeated trials. For pOTS-words, the resulting RSM showed that MVPs for the same stimulus (emoji and text) are positively correlated across experiments, suggesting that these stimuli induce stable and reproducible MVPs across the three tasks (lexical judgment, color, black/white detection) (Fig. 6a). In contrast, there is no similarity in responses across stimuli within a task. Thus, our results suggest that pOTS-words is predominantly involved in encoding stimulus, not task. We observed a similar pattern in the right hemisphere (Fig. S12a).

Discussion

Here, we assessed task and stimulus preferences in the OTS-words subregions. In experiment 1, we identified mOTS-words and pOTS-words by contrasting text with other visual stimuli. Experiment 2 showed fMRI adaptation for text and emoji stimuli in pOTS-words. In mOTS-words, this effect did not reach statistical significance. Finally, in experiment 3, pOTS-words showed a preference for emojis independent of task and mOTS-words showed a preference for emojis during a lexical judgment task as well as an overall preference for lexical judgments. Corresponding multivariate analyses showed that pOTS-words encodes the visual stimulus, while mOTS-words is sensitive to both stimulus and task.

Our finding that mOTS-words is sensitive to lexical processing while pOTS-words is sensitive only to the visual stimulus aligns with previous studies proposing an increasing processing level along the OTS (Carreiras et al. 2014; Taylor et al. 2019; White et al. 2019; Woolnough et al. 2021). First, while the posterior OTS is proposed to process letters, the anterior OTS is suggested to be responsible for perceiving words as language units (Vinckier et al. 2007; Thesen et al. 2012). Accordingly, the local combination detector model (Dehaene et al. 2005) posits that words are encoded through a posterior-to-anterior hierarchy of neuronal populations, which are tuned to process increasingly more complex features of words. Indeed, recent work suggests that the more posterior pOTS-words is involved in visual processing, while mOTS-words plays a role in integrating vision and language (Lerma-Usabiaga et al. 2018), which is supported by differential structural (Lerma-Usabiaga et al. 2018) and functional connectivity (Yablonski et al. 2023) of the subregions. This increasing processing level also appears to be functionally relevant, as impairments in the OTS-words processing hierarchy have been associated with dyslexia (van der Mark et al. 2009). Together, these differences in processing level suggest that mOTS-words and pOTS-words should be evaluated separately. For this, it is helpful to perform analyses in the native brain space of the participants without spatial smoothing, as normalization and smoothing can induce spurious overlap between neighboring regions (Weiner and Grill-Spector 2013).

We found that both OTS-words subregions prefer symbolic non-text stimuli (emojis) over text stimuli, which is surprising, as most prior studies have argued for a text selectivity in OTS-words (Cohen et al. 2000; Cohen and Dehaene 2004; Szwed et al. 2011). However, other prior studies have questioned this exclusive preference for text, showing that activations in OTS-words can be elicited by other visual stimuli as well (Price and Devlin 2003; Ben-Shachar et al. 2007; Mei et al. 2010). Here, we identified the OTS-words subregions in the same fashion as previous studies (Stigliani et al. 2015; Rosenke et al. 2021; Keller et al. 2022) but in addition introduced emojis as symbolic non-text stimuli. This allowed us to disentangle task and stimulus preferences, which is not possible if the only stimulus in the experiment that can be processed lexically is text. Hence, the difference between our findings and the prior literature may be explained by the following: (i) in our study, text was compared to another stimulus that can be processed lexically, (ii) we introduced a task that required participants to perform a lexical judgment on all stimuli in the experiment, and (iii) we presented emojis with which the participants have prior experience and which relate to archaic forms of writing. We discuss each of these differences separately in the paragraphs below. The emoji stimuli presented in the current study differed from previous work in that they were “symbolic,” i.e. the two stimuli together formed meaningful English compound words on which a lexical task could be performed. The observed preference for emojis over text in mOTS-words and pOTS-words provides evidence that these regions are not solely selective for text but rather are flexibly recruited for processing any kind of symbolic stimulus. This relates to previous research highlighting the importance of semantic content in reading: fMRI studies have shown that instead of following simple rule-based mechanisms, word naming is partially driven by a semantic component, which goes beyond basic lexical and phonological rules (Binder et al. 2005). This is further supported by a study finding neural evidence for two distinct pathways for reading out aloud: one for direct mapping between spelling and sound, and another for indirect mapping through semantic processing. This demonstrated importance of semantic content is an important consideration for the current study, as it underscores the importance of investigating how we interpret meaningful symbolic stimuli beyond traditional text (Graves et al. 2010). Similar to learning a new writing system, symbolic stimuli can become “readable” when we learn to retrieve semantic meaning from previously insignificant visual features. For example, in a recent study subjects were trained to read “HouseFont,” an artificial writing system in which English phonemes were presented as different houses (Martin et al. 2019). Strikingly, authors found higher activations in OTS-words for HouseFont than an artificial control script once training was completed. This aligns with our findings, suggesting that the OTS-words subregions are activated when semantic meaning needs to be assigned to a symbolic stimulus. One important difference to our study is that Martin et al. used stimuli that initially were not related to the word that they represent, whereas our emoji stimuli were pictograms of the objects themselves. These differences raise the question which kind of stimuli can be turned into meaningful lexical units. Future research could investigate if readability might also be evoked for stimuli such as photographs of objects or natural scenes. Another difference is that, in the study by Martin et al. each house was mapped onto a phoneme, which then needed to be combined into words. In our study, each emoji represented an entire word, and two emojis needed to be combined into a compound noun. Interestingly, analogous to these differences, across different scriptures, symbols can either represent individual letters (e.g. English), phonemes (e.g. Finnish), or words (e.g. Japanese kanji). This suggests that OTS-words might be surprisingly flexible regarding the mapping between symbols and language units.

Our findings highlight the importance of the performed task in driving responses in the OTS-words subregions. For example, while we did not find effects of stimulus or repetition in mOTS-words in experiment 2, we found both task and stimulus effects in experiment 3 where a lexical task was introduced. This aligns with recent studies showing higher responses for reading over other tasks in OTS-words (Grotheer et al. 2018, 2019; Qu et al. 2022). Moreover, the lexical task employed in the current study may even have contributed to the surprising preference for emoji stimuli observed in OTS-words, as conjoining evidence from intracranial recordings (Nobre et al. 1998; Woolnough et al. 2021), EEG (Strijkers and Bertrand 2015), PET (Starrfelt and Gerlach 2007), and fMRI (Mano et al. 2013; Kay et al. 2015; White et al. 2023) suggest that the task relevance of a stimulus can affect visual processing in OTS-words. Importantly, as outlined in the Interactive Account hypothesis (Price and Devlin 2011), these task effects may not originate in OTS-words, but rather result from top-down modulation from higher level regions in the language and/or attention networks, as supported by recent functional connectivity (White et al. 2023) and fMRI modeling analyses (Kay et al. 2015). An open question is what kind of task is needed to drive responses in OTS-words. Considering that the OTSs is also activated during object naming (Bookheimer et al. 1995; Moore and Price 1999; Murtha et al. 1999), the question arises if the observed preference for emojis is driven by lexical processing or by silently naming the presented objects. However, recent work has indicated higher responses for reading than object naming in OTS-words (Quinn et al. 2017) and as our participants had to combine two emojis into a semantic unit, the lexical judgment developed in the current study likely required a higher level of processing that goes beyond object naming.

The observed preference for emoji stimuli in OTS-words may also be explained by the extensive experience our participants have with emojis. Previous studies showed higher activations in OTS-words for familiar compared to unfamiliar scripts (Szwed et al. 2011; Dehaene et al. 2015; Martin et al. 2019), highlighting the importance of familiarity in driving OTS-words. Indeed, OTS-words is absent in illiterate adults (Dehaene et al. 2010, 2015; Braga et al. 2017) and proposed to emerge due to training during our childhood education (Ben et al. 2011; Dehaene et al. 2018; Nordt et al. 2021). As our participants stem from a demographic that likely encounters emojis daily (Prada et al. 2018), and as emojis convey critical social information (Kaye et al. 2017; Boutet et al. 2021), expertise for emojis might hence have contributed to the observed preference in OTS-words. Moreover, while most modern scripture systems are based on morpheme and phonographic signs, the earliest scripture systems, the Sumerian cuneiform and the Egyptian hieroglyphs, were iconographic (Trigger 1998). They were composed of pictograms expressing objects, actions, and ideas, similar to modern-day emojis. Emojis may hence actually represent a prior, more fundamental way of written communication (Alshenqeeti 2016).

Nonetheless, as we observed longer response times when participants performed the lexical task on emojis compared to text, one might still argue that processing emojis leads to an increased cognitive load resulting in higher responses in OTS-words. Previous research, examining differences in neural responses and reaction times for words and pseudowords, revealed that the pronunciation of pseudowords requires longer reaction times, which, in turn, has been proposed to indicate greater neural effort (Taylor et al. 2014). We theorize that the observed increase in response times for emoji stimuli, particularly in the lexical judgment task, could be attributed to the novelty of interpreting those visual symbols within a lexical context. This increased cognitive demand could explain the observed differences in processing time. For future research, taking into consideration differences in response time might be a fruitful avenue to further our understanding of how the OTS-words subregions process different lexical stimuli. This aligns with the suggestion of Mumford et al. (2024), who propose that response time differences should be considered in the fMRI time-series models to avoid spurious correlations.

More research is required to fully understand why OTS-words shows a preference for emojis compared to text, for example, by evaluating if there are distinct populations of voxels within these regions that selectively process one stimulus or the other. After all, the fact that we did not observe fMRI adaptation in the mixed condition in Exp 2 and that we could reliably decode the visual stimulus in both subregions across experiments may support this scenario. In addition, we did not find a preference for emoji stimuli solely in the OTS-words region, but also in the control ROIs Cc-color, CoS-places, and mFus-faces. We theorize that Cc-color shows this preference as a result of the emoji stimuli containing more colored pixels than the text stimuli. Furthermore, we can speculate that as some of the emojis depict places (e.g. tree house) and others faces, this might drive responses in CoS-places and mFus-faces more strongly than text-stimuli.

We conclude that both OTS-words subregions contribute to fluent reading, and that they are flexibly recruited whenever semantic meaning needs to be assigned to abstract visual stimuli. Our work further suggests that both stimulus features and task demands should be considered when investigating the OTS-words subregions.
