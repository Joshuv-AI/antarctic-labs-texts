Introduction

Gastric adenocarcinoma is a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide and is characterized by a high propensity for metastatic dissemination, most commonly involving the liver, lungs, peritoneum, and bone.1–3 Cardiac metastases, although more common than primary cardiac tumours, remain an uncommon clinical entity and are frequently clinically silent, with an incidence of 2%–18% reported in autopsy series.4–6 Gastrointestinal malignancies account for only a small fraction of cardiac metastases, and involvement of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) is exceptionally rare.4,7

Cardiac metastases may occur via haematogenous, lymphatic, transvenous, or direct extension pathways.5 Clinical presentation is highly variable and depends on tumour location and extent, ranging from asymptomatic disease to heart failure, arrhythmias, or obstruction of cardiac inflow or outflow tracts.5,8 Owing to their non-specific presentation, cardiac metastases are frequently underdiagnosed during life. Multimodality imaging plays a central role in detection and characterization, with echocardiography as the first-line modality and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) providing superior tissue characterization and assessment of local invasion.8,9

Here, we report a rare case of a clinically silent probable metastatic right-sided cardiac mass involving the RVOT and right ventricular free wall, incidentally detected during routine pre-chemotherapy cardiovascular assessment. This case illustrates how baseline cardio-oncology imaging, performed for pre-treatment cardiovascular assessment, may incidentally reveal clinically relevant structural cardiac abnormalities and highlights the complementary role of multimodality imaging in lesion characterization.

Summary figure

Case presentation:

A 60-year-old man with newly diagnosed metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma was referred for baseline cardiovascular assessment prior to initiation of systemic chemotherapy, in accordance with contemporary cardio-oncology recommendations. He had no prior history of cardiovascular disease and denied dyspnoea, chest pain, palpitations, or syncope. Physical examination revealed normal vital signs and no signs of heart failure.

Transthoracic echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a normal left ventricular size (LVEDVI 48 mL/m2) with preserved systolic function (LVEF 50%–55%) and no regional wall motion abnormalities. Left ventricular filling pressures were within normal limits.

In contrast, the right ventricle (RV) was mildly dilated and exhibited moderate systolic dysfunction. Regional wall motion analysis revealed akinesia of the mid-to-apical RV segments. Notably, the RV free wall appeared abnormally thickened (∼1.1 cm), raising strong suspicion for infiltrative myocardial involvement rather than isolated functional impairment.

A large intracavitary mass was identified within the RVOT, measuring ∼3.1 × 2.0 cm (Figure 1A–C). The mass was heterogeneous in echogenicity, predominantly immobile, and appeared broadly attached to the RVOT wall. It protruded into the RVOT lumen, resulting in flow turbulence on colour Doppler imaging, although without haemodynamically significant obstruction (peak gradient 16 mmHg).

(A) Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiography (2D TTE), parasternal short-axis view at the level of the aortic valve, demonstrating a large mass within the RVOT. (B) Two-dimensional transoesophageal echocardiography (2D TEE), mid-oesophageal ∼90° view, showing a heterogeneous RVOT mass measuring ∼3.1 × 2.0 cm, with broad-based attachment to the RVOT wall. (C) Three-dimensional transoesophageal echocardiography (3D TEE) providing enhanced spatial delineation of the lesion, confirming an irregular, sessile mass with a wide attachment. Overall, the mass appears large, heterogeneous, and non-pedunculated, firmly attached to the RVOT wall, without causing significant RVOT obstruction or interference with pulmonary valve function.

Three-dimensional echocardiography further delineated the morphology of the lesion, demonstrating an irregular, non-pedunculated mass with a broad-based attachment to the RVOT and adjacent free wall, features highly suggestive of a neoplastic rather than thrombotic process.

Additional findings included mild mitral regurgitation, mild pulmonic insufficiency, and mild-to-moderate tricuspid regurgitation. The tricuspid regurgitation gradient was 40 mmHg, and the estimated right atrial pressure was 5 mmHg based on normal inferior vena cava size with >50% respiratory collapse, yielding an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure of ∼45 mmHg. After accounting for the RVOT peak gradient of 16 mmHg, the estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure was ∼29 mmHg, suggesting no significant pulmonary arterial hypertension. A small circumferential pericardial effusion was present. A separate linear echo density was noted at the tip of the indwelling port catheter, most consistent with catheter-associated thrombus, providing an important internal comparator for thrombotic morphology.

Overall, the constellation of findings—including RV wall thickening, regional akinesia, and a large, heterogeneous, immobile RVOT mass—was highly suggestive of infiltrative cardiac involvement in the context of known malignancy.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging

CMR imaging was performed for further tissue characterization and assessment of the extent of cardiac involvement.

Cine steady-state free precession sequences demonstrated an infiltrative mass involving the right ventricular (RV) free wall and extending into the RVOT, with both intracavitary and extracardiac components (Figure 2A and B). The lesion appeared poorly demarcated with evidence of myocardial invasion and extension beyond the cardiac contours, consistent with an aggressive infiltrative process.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging of probable metastatic right-sided cardiac involvement. (A–B) Cine steady-state free precession images demonstrating an infiltrative mass involving the right ventricular free wall and extending into the right ventricular outflow tract, with both intracavitary and extracardiac components. (C) First-pass perfusion imaging demonstrating contrast uptake within the lesion. (D) Late gadolinium enhancement imaging using a phase-sensitive inversion recovery sequence showing heterogeneous enhancement of the lesion, supporting probable neoplastic involvement. Arrows indicate the lesion in panels B–D.

On T2-weighted imaging, the mass exhibited high signal intensity, suggestive of increased water content and tumour-related oedema. T1-weighted fat-suppressed sequences demonstrated relatively low signal intensity.

First-pass perfusion imaging revealed contrast uptake within the lesion, indicating vascularity (Figure 2C). Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging using phase-sensitive inversion recovery sequences demonstrated heterogeneous enhancement (Figure 2D), supporting probable neoplastic involvement rather than thrombus.

No additional intracardiac masses were identified. A small pericardial effusion was confirmed. Additionally, subendocardial-to-transmural LGE was noted at the left ventricular apex, consistent with prior myocardial injury.

Overall, in the context of known gastric adenocarcinoma, the multimodality imaging findings—including an infiltrative growth pattern, myocardial and extracardiac extension, contrast uptake, and heterogeneous LGE—were highly suggestive of probable metastatic cardiac involvement. Histological confirmation was not pursued because of the lesion location, procedural risk, absence of haemodynamic compromise, and the need to avoid delaying systemic therapy.

Clinical course

The patient had newly diagnosed metastatic gastric adenocarcinoma and was referred for baseline cardio-oncology assessment prior to the initiation of systemic chemotherapy. Following the echocardiographic identification of an RVOT/right ventricular mass, CMR imaging was performed for further anatomical and tissue characterization. Therapeutic anticoagulation with enoxaparin 80 mg twice daily was initiated for the catheter-associated thrombus. The patient remained asymptomatic from a cardiovascular standpoint and haemodynamically stable throughout the diagnostic evaluation. After completion of cardiac assessment, systemic chemotherapy was initiated under close cardio-oncology follow-up. No follow-up cardiac imaging was available at the time of manuscript revision. At the latest available clinical follow-up, the patient was alive and receiving systemic chemotherapy.

Discussion

Cardiac metastases are more common than primary cardiac tumours but remain underrecognized due to their frequently silent clinical course and incidental detection.5,10 Gastrointestinal malignancies account for only a small proportion of cardiac metastases, and right-sided cardiac involvement extending to the RVOT is particularly uncommon.5,10

The present case is distinguished by imaging findings highly suggestive of probable metastatic cardiac involvement, characterized by right ventricular free wall infiltration with extension into the RVOT, incidentally identified in an asymptomatic patient during routine baseline cardio-oncology evaluation prior to systemic therapy. Baseline cardio-oncology imaging is recommended primarily for cardiovascular risk stratification and assessment of cardiac function before potentially cardiotoxic therapy. In the present case, this evaluation incidentally revealed an unsuspected structural cardiac abnormality, later characterized by multimodality imaging.11

Multimodality imaging was central to the diagnostic assessment. TTE enabled initial detection of the intracardiac mass, while transoesophageal and three-dimensional echocardiography provided detailed morphological characterization, including a sessile, non-pedunculated configuration with broad-based attachment. CMR further characterized the anatomical extent of involvement by demonstrating right ventricular free wall infiltration with extension into the RVOT, including intracavitary and extracardiac components, as well as heterogeneous LGE.12 Notably, the presence of a catheter-associated thrombus in the same patient served as an internal comparator, reinforcing the distinction between thrombotic and neoplastic masses based on morphology and contrast behaviour.

The differential diagnosis of a right-sided intracardiac mass in a patient with malignancy includes bland thrombus, catheter-associated thrombus, tumour thrombus, primary cardiac tumours, and secondary cardiac involvement.10,12,13 In the present case, bland thrombus was considered less likely because the lesion was heterogeneous, sessile, and broadly attached to the RVOT/right ventricular free wall, with associated right ventricular wall thickening and regional dysfunction. CMR further supported probable neoplastic infiltration by demonstrating myocardial and extracardiac extension, high T2 signal intensity, first-pass contrast uptake, and heterogeneous LGE.12,13 The separate catheter-associated thrombus at the port catheter tip provided an internal comparator, as it had a more linear morphology and lacked the infiltrative features observed in the RVOT/right ventricular free wall lesion. Although histological confirmation was not obtained, the overall clinical context and multimodality imaging findings were highly suggestive of probable metastatic cardiac involvement.10,12,13

This case underscores the need to consider probable metastatic involvement in the differential diagnosis of right-sided intracardiac masses in patients with known malignancy, even in the absence of cardiovascular symptoms. It also illustrates that baseline echocardiography performed for pre-treatment cardio-oncology assessment may incidentally identify clinically relevant structural abnormalities. In this setting, multimodality imaging can support lesion characterization, differential diagnosis, and clinical decision-making.10,12

Conclusion

This case demonstrates an unusual presentation of probable metastatic right-sided cardiac involvement, detected incidentally in an asymptomatic patient undergoing baseline cardio-oncology assessment. It emphasizes the importance of careful interpretation of baseline cardio-oncology imaging performed for established pre-treatment cardiovascular evaluation, particularly when unexpected structural abnormalities are identified. Multimodality imaging can support differentiation of intracardiac masses and guide appropriate clinical management.
