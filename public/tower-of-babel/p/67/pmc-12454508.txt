Real-World Outcomes of T790M Mutation Testing and Sequential Osimertinib in EGFR-Positive Advanced Non-small Cell Lung Cancer: A Revisited Strategy
Authors: Kelvin Yan, Arizah Bakhtiah, Shweta Hota, Joanne Evans, Frankie Mo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-driven non-small cell lung cancer (eLC) is a leading cause of death. The FLAURA study showed that upfront osimertinib (U-OSI) led to better overall survival (OS) than gefitinib or erlotinib, regardless of T790M status in advanced disease. However, if sequenced optimally, sequential OSI (S-OSI) in T790M-positive patients after first- or second-generation EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (F-S-EGFR-TKI) should theoretically lead to better OS than U-OSI.ObjectiveTo identify the best sequencing strategy in this group of patients.Patients and MethodsA multicentre retrospective study was conducted on treatment-naive eLC patients who had received an F-S- EGFR-TKI between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2020 in three tertiary NHS hospitals in the UK. Compliance to national recommendation of T790M testing was analysed. Survival outcomes of T790M testing and S-OSI were estimated with the Kaplan–Meier and Cox Proportional Hazard models.ResultsIn 84/122 evaluable patients, after F-S-EGFR-TKI, only 50% of the patients were offered a T790M biopsy, owing to rapid progression and reduced fitness. Of which, 59.5% of the patients tested positive and had S-OSI. Median OS for the T790M-tested cohort, regardless of positivity and S-OSI, was 54.0 months vs 8.9 months in those not tested (P = < 0.001). Median OS of S-OSI in T790M-positive patients was 64.0 months vs 34.9 months in the T790M-negative cohort (P < 0.0001). On multivariable analysis, S-OSI was associated with better OS (HR 1.841; 95% CI 1.052–3.221; P = 0.0325), whereas performance status (HR 2. 256; 95% CI 1.151–4.422, P = 0.0178), presence of baseline intracranial disease (HR 2 .022; 95% CI 1.144–3.575, P = 0. 0115), the male sex (HR 2.265; 95% CI 1.302–3.939; P = 0.0038) and non-exon 19 deletion mutations (HR 1.610; 95% CI 1.112–2.331, P = 0.0116) were associated with a higher risk of death.ConclusionsHigh performance status and intracranial disease should be indications for U-OSI for a higher chance of response. For fitter patients, F-S-EGFR-TKI followed by T790M biopsy +/− S-OSI appears to confer better-than-expected OS in the entire cohort in the real-world setting, regardless of T790M positivity. Given the clinical benefit and potential cost-effectiveness of this approach, S-OSI should be considered a favourable option in this group of patients, especially in resource-deprived settings.

Key Points

Upfront osimertinib for untreated EGFR-positive advanced non-small cell lung cancer has become the preferred treatment option in recent years. However, if sequenced optimally, sequential osimertinib after a first- or second-generation EGFR-TKI should theoretically lead to better survival.This real-world study identified better-than-expected survival outcomes in the sequential osimertinib group as well as in those who started on a first- or second-generation EGFR-TKI but did not access sequential osimertinib.The results of this study have significant implications on both the policy and clinical levels regarding the best sequencing strategy for this group of patients.

Introduction

Lung cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the major subtype of lung cancer accounting for over 85% of all cases [2]. NSCLC with an activating mutation is a distinct biological and treatment subgroup, with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations being the most common [3–5]. EGFR mutations, particularly the exon 19 deletion and the exon 21 L858R point mutation, account for around 9–14% of Caucasian and up to 50% of East Asian NSCLC cases [3–6]. Since the emergence of phase III evidence of an overall survival (OS) benefit of EGFR-targeting tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) against chemotherapy alone, EGFR TKIs have been recommended by international guidelines as a first line treatment in EGFR-positive advanced NSCLC patients [7–9]. However, patients invariably develop resistance to first- and second-generation EGFR TKIs with the T790M mutation being a major pathway [10]. The AURA3 study subsequently demonstrated that osimertinib (OSI), a third-generation EGFR TKI, conferred an OS benefit of in patients who developed the T790M mutation after first- or second-generation EGFR TKIs (F-S-EGFR-TKI) [11]. This led to the recommendation of T790M mutation testing and the potential subsequent treatment with osimertinib by various international guidelines [12].

The FLAURA study subsequently showed that first-line osimertinib, when compared against gefitinib or erlotinib, led to better OS regardless of the T790M mutational status [13]. This finding led to the recommendation of osimertinib as a first-line option for EGFR-positive NSCLC [12, 14]. However, in 2017, the AURA3 trial had previously demonstrate superior progression-free survival (PFS) for osimertinib over platinum-based therapy (10.1 months versus 4.4 months) in patients with a T790M mutation who had disease progression after first-line treatment with F-S-EGFR-TKI. Previously, it had been shown that the first-generation EGFR-TKI, gefitinib, resulted in longer OS than chemotherapy (30.5 months versus 23.6 months) in previously untreated patients with EGFR-mutated cancers [15]. As such, based on the survival benefits shown in these two trials, patients in whom T790M mutations develop while they are receiving F-S-EGFR-TKI theoretically could have an OS of more than 40 months with second-line osimertinib if the therapies were sequenced optimally [16].

The treatment landscape for NSCLC, especially in the activating mutation and EGFR space, is evolving rapidly. A recent phase III study demonstrated a PFS benefit with upfront osimertinib plus chemotherapy against osimertinib alone in EGFR-positive advanced NSCLC, with their OS data awaited [17]. It is not surprising that PFS is improved with the combination. However, whether or not this translates to OS and meaningful clinical benefits awaits data maturity and critical scrutiny of the mutational status, subsequent treatments and toxicity from the trial. Optimal sequencing is a vital strategy in oncology and therefore the use of upfront versus sequential osimertinib in this space remains an important question to be answered. Real-world data on the utility of T790M testing and the outcomes of sequential use of osimertinib will, at least in part, shed light on the theoretical longer OS in patients who are sequenced optimally.

This multicentre study therefore aimed to (1) audit the compliance of T790M testing for sequential osimertinib as per the national guideline in the UK [18, 19], and (2) compare the outcomes for the patients in whom an upfront F-S-EGFR-TKI was given, with respect to T790M testing, mutation results and sequential osimertinib use.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

We conducted this retrospective cohort study on treatment-naive EGFR-positive NSCLC patients who had received a F-S-EGFR-TKI between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2020 at three tertiary centres in London, UK. Compliance to the National Institute of Clinical Excellence guideline of T790M testing and subsequent access to sequential osimertinib on progression of a F-S-EGFR-TKI were reviewed [18, 19]. Clinical outcomes between patients who had a T790M biopsy and those who did not were compared. A separate analysis of clinical outcomes between the groups of ‘T790M biopsy’, ‘negative T790M’ and ‘positive T790M’ with subsequent sequential osimertinib, was undertaken. The study was approved as a clinical audit at Imperial College Healthcare NHS Trust (registration number: O&P_102). It was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Collection and Data Accuracy

The hospital master record for systemic anticancer was used to identify eligible patients treated with a F-S-EGFR-TKI. Electronic hospital records of eligible patients were accessed. Baseline characteristics, clinical data and treatment summaries were extracted manually. To ensure data accuracy, every patient record was accessed and checked by at least two authors of the study.

Statistical Analysis

Overall survival was estimated with the Kaplan–Meier method from the date of F-S-TKI initiation to the date of death. Progression-free survival was measured from the date of F-S-TKI initiation to the date of first disease progression or death of any cause. Intracranial disease progression was calculated from the date of F-S-TKI initiation to the date of first intracranial progression. For all survival calculations, patients without events were censored at last follow-up time.

Compliance to T790M testing was calculated using descriptive statistics. The impact of T790M testing on survival was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with log-rank tests. Potential prognostic factors for OS, PFS and intracranial progression were evaluated with univariable and multivariable analyses and were conducted with the Cox proportional hazard regression model. Variables from univariable analyses were selected in order of significance to be included in the multivariable analysis using a stepwise approach. A two-tailed P value of < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

Results

A total of 122 eligible patients were identified. Patient records of 38 patients were incomplete and were therefore excluded from the analysis. The majority of patients were female (female: n = 61; 72.6%; male: n = 23; 27.4%). The predominant ethnic group was white Caucasian (n = 39; 46.4%), followed by Asian (n = 28; 33.3%). Of the 84 patients, 10 (12%) received afatinib, 5 (6%) received gefitinib and 69 (82%) received erlotinib. The most common mutation was the Exon 19 deletion (n = 48; 57.1%), followed by the L858R mutation (n = 28; 33.3%).

Compliance to National Guidance

With regards to compliance to T790 testing, only half (42/84; 50.0%) of patients were offered a test on disease progression. Of those, 25/42 (59.5%) tested positive for the T790M mutation and all of these patients were started on sequential osimertinib.

Of the non-compliance cohort, 40/42 (95.2%) were due to rapid progression of disease resulting in the unfitness for a biopsy. The other reasons include a change of healthcare providers or loss to follow up.

Outcomes

Overall Survival

With a median follow up of 86.4 months, the median OS in the entire cohort was 25.2 months. The median OS in patients who were not tested for T790M was 8.4 months versus 54.0 months in those who were tested, regardless of positivity (P < 0.001) (Fig 1). The median OS in those who were tested negative was 34.9 months versus 64.0 months in positive patients (P < 0.0001) (Fig 2).

Progression-Free Survival

The median PFS for patients who were not tested for T790M was 5.5 months versus 18.0 months in those who tested positive and 18.0 months in those who tested negative (P < 0.0001) (Fig 3).

Progression-free survival by T790M biopsy

On multivariate analysis, having had a T790M biopsy was associated with better PFS (HR: 0.413; 95% CI 0.253–0.675; P = 0.0004). A higher performance status (PS) was independently associated with worse PFS (HR: 2.342; 95% CI 1.214–4.520; P = 0.0112) (Table 2).

When adjusted for the T790M biopsy, only PS was associated with a risk of progression (HR 3.478; 95% CI 1.858–6.510, P < 0.0001).

Intracranial Progression

On multivariate analysis, the presence of existing intracranial disease was independently associated with intracranial progression (HR: 12.909; 95% CI 3.531–47.197; P = 0.0001). The L858R and other rarer mutations were also independently associated with an increased risk of brain progression (HR: 0.082; 95% CI 0.014–0.478; P = 0.0054) (Table 2).

Discussion

Upfront osimertinib is considered the preferred standard option in many parts of the world [12, 14]. The use of sequential osimertinib after a F-S-EGFR-TKI, albeit still used as a standard option, is falling out of trend especially in developed countries. However, sequential osimertinib still theoretically leads to better OS outcomes versus upfront osimertinib [16]. To access this benefit, a robust adherence to national guidance on T790M testing is paramount. Our multicentre audit shows that only 50.0 of our patients were tested for T790M on progression of first-line F-S-EGFR-TKI. The compliance rate is much lower than the 80–100% reported by other centres [20–22]. The main reason of a lack of testing was rapid progression of disease and a resultant unfitness or inappropriateness for a repeat biopsy. Over half of these patients did not access any subsequent treatment owing to rapid progression and/or a high PS and most of the remainder had chemotherapy owing to the need for rapid control. This underlies the findings of our study in that there was a higher representation of poor PS in those who did not access a T790M biopsy, highlighting the importance of rapid control in unfit patients.

Further, a higher PS is independently associated with worse PFS and OS. As access to T790M biopsy likely reflects a combination of better baseline PS, less aggressive biology of disease and the subsequent use of sequential OSI, when adjusted for T790M biopsy testing, PS became statistically significant in its association with OS and remained the only significant factor in PFS association. As access to T790M testing should not affect PFS on F-S-EGFR-TKI, this confirms our observation that higher PS is associated with a lack of treatment response and rapid progression, thereby affecting the ability to access T790M testing and potential sequential osimertinib. It is also clear from the Kaplan–Meier charts that there is a rapid drop in both the PFS and OS curves in those who did not have a T790M biopsy, likely reflecting a combination of rapid progression, poor PS and limited response to treatment. This resonates with our interpretation that in these patients where sequential osimertinib is unlikely to be possible, they should be offered a treatment that allows the highest chance of response. Whilst these patients might nonetheless have a poor response to treatment regardless of T790M testing or TKI choices, osimertinib tends to have a high response rate in patients with poor PS with good PS recovery and reasonable PFS, which may yet offer the best chance of therapeutic benefit in this subset of patients [23, 24]. Further, the FLAURA 2 study identified a high response rate of 83% and better PFS with upfront Osimertinib + chemotherapy versus osimertinib alone, albeit in PS 1/2 patients and immature OS data [17]. This novel approach, if proven safe in this subset of patients, may well be an option for patients that require rapid control of disease. In the context of a median OS of 25.2 months in the entire cohort, which is in line with published evidence of 19.4–26.9 months in various populations in Europe and Asia, our findings certainly support the differentiation of patients with rapidly progressive disease who likely needed a more active compound or intensive treatment, which, alongside higher PS, were likely negatively impacting the overall OS in this cohort when analysed together with patients who were fit enough to access a T790M biopsy and potential sequential osimertinib [25–27]. It is, however, important to make an informed and shared decision with patients regarding the benefit-to-risk balance of therapy in patients in whom treatment may be futile.

Our audit shows that of those that were tested for T790M on progression, 59.5% returned a positive result. This is higher than much of the current published data of around 50% and therefore has an important implication on the choice of first line treatment [28]. In the subgroup analysis, our results show that median OS was 64.0 months in those who received sequential osimertinib and 34.9 months in those who tested negative for T790M. This is much longer than the reported median OS of 38.6 months in the upfront osimertinib group from the FLAURA trial [13]. In the 59.5% of patients who tested positive for T790M, the OS is almost a double of those who were T790M-negative. This representation and the resultant OS benefit with sequential osimertinib were strong enough to drive the OS in the T790M-tested group, regardless of positivity, to 54.0 months. Considering the potential activity of osimertinib regardless of T790M mutational status, sequential osimertinib appears to have a larger role in patients with EGFR-positive NSCLC, despite the FLAURA study. Further, the APPLE trial demonstrated that osimertinib showed activity in patients who did not identify a T790M mutation after progression on a F-S-EGFR-TKI [29]. This finding suggests that, in addition to those who develop a resistance T790M mutation, for those that test negative for T790M, there may still be a benefit for sequential osimertinib, possibly due to a stronger affinity of osimertinib to EGFR [30]. This is particularly relevant given the higher than expected T790M mutational rate in our study and the potential effect with the negative group. When considering the population benefit of almost doubling the OS in 59.5% of patients, alongside a robust 34.9-month OS even in those that do not access Osimertinib and the implications of cost differences between osimertinib and other EGFR TKIs, this study certainly opens up a debate on both policymaking and clinical strategies regarding capturing the maximum benefit for the entire EGFR population and balancing the cost-effectiveness in this arena.

Previous studies, including meta-analyses, have shown that females tend to respond better to EGFR TKIs and have better survival outcomes than males [31]. Our study confirms this finding in that male sex confers a higher risk of than female sex. In the context of our real-world study, this may imply that when choosing first-line treatment options, expected response rates versus potential benefit from sequential osimertinib should be considered. Our study, however, did not identify a PFS benefit with female sex, even after adjusting for fitness.

On this note, as the presence of brain disease is also independently associated with OS, it highlights the importance of utilising osimertinib, which crosses the blood–brain barrier more readily, as an upfront treatment [32]. This is particularly true as PS is associated with intracranial progression and OS, where rapid control with upfront osimertinib appears plausible.

Another important finding of this study is that exon 19 deletion appears to confer a favourable effect on OS and intracranial progression. This is consistent with previous evidence that exon 19 deletion leads to better OS and PFS; it also appears to respond better to EGFR-TKI [33–35]. Although our study did not demonstrate a consistent PFS benefit with exon 19 deletion in the multivariate analysis, this may be explained by the small sample size. Nonetheless, evidence consistently supports the notion that exon 19 deletion and L858R are two separate entities [36], which may influence our decision in utilising upfront osimertinib in patients with the L858R mutation. On the other hand, if sequential osimertinib is contemplated, a more intense follow up schedule for these patients whilst on F-S-EGFR-TKI and a more proactive testing strategy for the T790M mutation would be reasonable to enable timely access to osimertinib.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study had several strengths. It reflects the real-world treatment practice and outcomes of utilising T790M testing for sequential osimertinib in EGFR-positive NSCLC. It also identifies that T790M remains an important tool in the NHS setting, and that it is closely related to fitness, a seemingly surrogate for rapid progression, as evidenced by the 50.0% compliance. Given the much lower EGFR mutation rates in the Western populations compared with Asian counterparts, this study serves as an important insight into this unique patient demographic, especially in the NHS setting. The study was limited by the retrospective nature, with limited late-line options especially in the more historical cohort, which might have skewed the OS data. Other potential options, such as the erlotinib and ramucirumab combination, were also not available for comparison [37]. The relatively small sample size, despite the much lower rates of EGFR mutations in western populations, also limits the generalisability of the findings. Nonetheless, it provides an important basis for decision making between F-S-EGFR-TKI versus upfront osimertinib.

Conclusions

In the UK and beyond, clinicians have a choice of using upfront osimertinib versus other EGFR TKIs, with both options reimbursable. In the decision-making process, this study suggests that for those where a rapid response is needed, upfront osimertinib should still be considered the preferred option, given the potential loss of opportunity in accessing sequential osimertinib owing to the turnaround time for a re-biopsy for T790M. Notwithstanding, our real-world study in the NHS setting shows that in patients that were fit enough to have a T790M test, regardless of positivity and sequential osimertinib, the robust 54.0-month OS is better than that of the control group reported in FLAURA. This finding, alongside the better-than-expected 64-month OS in those who accessed sequential osimertinib, should certainly enable this approach as a reasonable option in the decision-making process, from both the policy-making and clinical perspectives, especially in resource-deprived healthcare settings.
