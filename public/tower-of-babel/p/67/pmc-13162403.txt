Explainable machine learning model for identifying key risk factors in congenital heart disease prediction using questionnaire data: a retrospective case-control study
Authors: Yunyan Wang, Tiezheng Li, Jiajia Chen, Jinghua Ruan, Qiang Shu, Weize Xu, Die Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent neonatal congenital heart disease (CHD) screening strategies face significant challenges in low-income or underdeveloped regions due to a shortage of experienced physicians. Therefore, we aim to develop a cost-effective method to identify high-risk populations, supplementing neonatal screening.MethodsA multicenter case-control study was conducted in three hospitals in Zhejiang, China, from September 2022 to July 2024. Pregnant women were surveyed using a self-designed questionnaire, and newborns were diagnosed using echocardiography. We utilized three steps to establish a prediction model for neonatal CHD. Initially, LASSO regression was used to screen variables. Subsequently, five representative machine learning (ML) algorithms, including SVM, XGBoost, Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR) and LightGBM, were applied to establish the CHD risk prediction models. Finally, the Shapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) method and logistic regression were adopted to identify key risk factors for CHD.ResultsA total of 1,633 mother-infant pairs were included, with 437 infants diagnosed with CHD. Among the five machine learning models, XGBoost showed the best performance, with an AUC of 0.724 on the internal test set and 0.706 on the external validation cohort. The internal AUCs for RF, SVM, LightGBM, and LR were 0.673, 0.647, 0.672, and 0.649, respectively. The key risk factors for CHD were identified as COVID-19 infection within the three months prior to the last menstrual period, gestational medication use and viral infections during pregnancy. The odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were 8.31 (4.86–14.72), 2.24 (1.65–3.03), 1.49 (1.13–1.97), respectively.ConclusionsOur CHD prediction model is non-invasive, cost-effective, and demonstrates robust performance, making it a valuable supplement to routine neonatal screening for identifying newborns at high risk of CHD. The ML model may contribute to guiding better clinical risk assessment and decision making for neonatal CHD.Clinical trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03458-5.

Background

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is a congenital malformation caused by abnormal development of the heart and great vessels during the embryonic stage or by failure of the closure of certain fetal circulatory structures after birth [1]. It is the most frequent congenital defect in newborns. The global incidence of CHD in live-born infants is 0.94%, and this rate has been increasing over time [2]. Therefore, the accurate identification of fetuses at high-risk of CHD, along with a comprehensive investigation of the risk factors associated with CHD, is essential for early intervention and the implementation of effective preventive strategies.

Currently, the etiology of CHD is not clear, generally considered to be the interaction of genetic and environmental factors. Many previous studies have shown that many risk factors may be associated with CHD, such as chromosomal disorder like Down syndrome [3]; multiple births, pregnant women with advanced maternal age, obesity, non-local household registration or a history of CHD [4, 5]; gestational disease like diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, hypertension, infection, and influenza [6, 7]; behavior factors such as excessive alcohol consumption and smoking [8]; lack of essential supplementation like vitamin A and folic acid [9]; Medications such as antihypertensive agents [10]; moderate, high or a very high altitude are also considered risk factors for CHD [11, 12]. These studies often focus only on the impact of single factors on CHD, lacking a comprehensive analysis of maternal risk factors.

Pulse oximetry (POX) is widely used for screening method for CHD, but it is limited to detecting CHD with hypoxia symptoms and is not suitable for use at the high altitude [13, 14]. Cardiac auscultation is a common measure for first-line screening [15]. However, it requires extensive training to master and is best performed by an experienced physician [16]. Therefore, we need adaptable, pediatrician-independent, and cost-effective screening methods. Machine learning (ML) can analyze large amounts of complex data and identify previously unknown relationships. Various ML methods are being utilized to create models for predicting CHD, demonstrating effective forecasting capabilities [17–19]. Most of the existing CHD prediction models have relied on data from fetal electrocardiogram, fetal cardiac ultrasound, and maternal plasma proteomic analysis [20–22]. However, these methods are costly, difficult to generalize in low-income areas, and cannot be widely used as routine screening.

Although machine learning is powerful, it is limited by its lack of interpretability, commonly referred to as a “black-box”, causing clinicians to hesitate in using it for medical decisions due to opacity [23]. To address the ‘black-box’ issues, the Shapley Additive exPlanation (SHAP) method offers a unified framework for explaining ML model outputs [24]. SHAP values determine feature importance by evaluating the output that includes all combinations of features and providing a consistent contribution score for each feature in the model, aiding users in gaining a deeper understanding of the model’s decision-making process. Additionally, this explainable ML has been effectively utilized in diverse medical domains, such as predicting acute kidney injury in critically ill children [25] and major adverse outcomes after pediatric CHD [26]. Nevertheless, little research has been reported on neonatal CHD predictive models with the SHAP method applied to interpret these models.

Hence, the objective of this study was to develop and validate machine learning models based on questionnaire data for predicting neonatal CHD. The best-performing prediction model was chosen to interpret and identify significant risk factors for CHD using SHAP method.

Methods

Study design and participants

A multi-center hospital-based case-control study was conducted between September 2022 and July 2024 in three hospitals (Jiaxing Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital, Dongyang Maternity and Child Health Hospital, Ninghai Maternal and Child Health Hospital ). Participants were recruited among pregnant women who delivered in these three collaborated hospitals. All newborns participating in this study underwent both ‘Double-Indicator’ screening for CHD and cardiac ultrasound examinations conducted by professional echocardiographers. All pregnant women underwent a survey on risk factors during pregnancy, conducted by trained personnel using a self-designed questionnaire. The case group included mothers whose newborns were diagnosed with CHD by echocardiography. In addition, the control group was involved mothers whose newborns were diagnosed without cardiac defect. Meanwhile, Infants and their mothers were matched at a 1:3 ratio based on infant sex and maternal age. The study flow chart is shown in Fig. 1.

Exclusion criteria for our project were as follows: (1) multiple pregnancy; (2) premature birth; (3) newborns with chromosome disorders; (4) newborn with other birth defects. Finally, 365 CHD newborns and their mothers were included in the case group, and 983 healthy newborns and their mothers were included in the control group. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University School of Medicine Children’s Hospital (2023-IRB-0139-P-01). Participants were fully informed about the study and signed an informed consent form before the investigation.

To assess the external generalizability of our predictive model, we additionally included an independent external validation cohort from Yongkang Maternal and Child Health Hospital, which was not involved in model development or internal testing. The same inclusion criteria, diagnostic procedures, and data collection protocols were applied to ensure consistency across cohorts. A total of 285 mother-infant pairs were included in this external validation cohort.

Questionnaire design

This study employed a questionnaire survey method. The questionnaire was designed by the research team, based on previous literature on risk factors for adverse birth outcomes. It included questions on parental demographic information, physical measurements, genetic predispositions, maternal health conditions during pregnancy, prenatal exposures, and the intake of prenatal supplements. The questionnaire also included viral infections, smoking, alcohol, and caffeine consumption in the three months before the last menstrual period and during pregnancy, maternal lifestyle behaviors, medication use during pregnancy, and emotional and sleep status. In total, the questionnaire explored 12 key aspects related to neonatal CHD risk factors and early warning patterns. The questionnaire used in this study is available as Supplementary_File_Questionnaire. The detailed information is presented in Supplementary Table 1, which outlines the full list of risk factors assessed in the questionnaire.

Development of machine learning models

These data were randomly split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). Given that multicollinearity can diminish model accuracy, we performed Spearman’s correlation analysis to select features with high correlation coefficients (r > 0.7) and removed variables with weak correlation to the outcome variable. Due to more than 25% missing data, the feature of annual average household income was excluded to prevent model bias by excessive missing values. As a high-dimensional feature set may lead to overfitting in machine learning models, we applied LASSO regression to select relevant features from the available variables.

In this study, five machine learning algorithms widely used for disease prediction were used to train a CHD prediction model. The five machine learning algorithms include Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Logistic Regression (LR), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). To optimize the prediction model, a combination of grid search and manual fine-tuning was applied to obtain the final hyperparameters [27].

We used the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as a key metric to evaluate the discriminatory ability of each prediction model. To assess whether differences in AUC between models were statistically significant, we performed pairwise DeLong tests using the pROC package in R. Additionally, to enhance the interpretability and clinical utility of the predicted probabilities, model calibration was performed using Isotonic Regression. Subsequently, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was applied to evaluate the net clinical benefit of the model across a range of threshold probabilities. DCA was implemented using the dcurves package in R.

Model interpretation

We employed the SHAP algorithm and logistic regression to enhance model interpretability and elucidate the clinical relevance of key features. The SHAP algorithm calculates the SHAP value for each variable of this model, ranks the importance of the input variables, and assesses their contribution to the predictive model, helping to overcome the “black-box” limitation of ML models. Logistic regression was used to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) to further analyze the impact of significant risk factors on CHD in newborns.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using R software, version 4.0.1. Continuous variables (e.g., maternal age, maternal pre-pregnancy weight) were summarized as the median and interquartile range, and the Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test was applied. Categorical variables (Folic acid supplementation, Smoking during pregnancy) were summarized as percentages, and Pearson’s Chi-squared test was used. A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The ‘randomForest’, ‘xgboost’, ‘e1071’, and ‘lightgbm’ packages were used to develop the RF, XGBoost, SVM, and LightGBM models, respectively. SHAP values for each feature were calculated using an R package “shapviz”.

Results

Study population description

In this study, a total of 1,488 valid responses were collected. After excluding cases with additional birth defects or preterm births, 1,348 questionnaires were included, comprising 365 individuals in the CHD group and 983 in the control group. Participants were randomly split into training and test sets at a 7:3 ratio.

The training set, used to develop the predictive model, included 945 participants (462 males and 483 females), while the test set comprised 403 participants (201 males and 202 females) for internal performance evaluation. An independent external validation cohort of 285 participants (145 males and 140 females), comprising 72 CHD cases and 213 controls, was used to evaluate the model’s generalizability. Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics of participants by case/control status and across the development and external validation cohorts. No significant demographic differences were observed between the CHD and non-CHD groups.

LASSO regression for variable screening

After excluding features with excessive missingness and collinearity, 105 candidate variables remained. To prevent overfitting due to high dimensionality, we applied LASSO regression with 10-fold cross-validation for feature selection. The optimal regularization parameter (λ.min = 0.017), corresponding to the minimum mean squared error, was chosen, resulting in the selection of 21 features (Fig. 2A, B). Although the more parsimonious λ.1se retained only 3 variables, it markedly reduced predictive performance, so λ.min was adopted for subsequent modeling. Finally, we ultimately selected 21 variables, including family history of heart disease, family history of chronic diseases, family history of diabetes, infection with COVID-19 in the 3 months prior to the last menstrual period, viral infections during pregnancy, diagnosed thyroid disease during pregnancy, gestational medication use, antiviral medication use during pregnancy, vitamin D supplementation, iron supplementation, calcium supplementation, exposure to pesticides and agricultural chemicals during pregnancy, exposure to microwaves during pregnancy, emotional stability during mid-pregnancy, maternal age, paternal ethnicity, paternal occupation, maternal pre-pregnancy BMI, use of multivitamins during mid-pregnancy, maternal weight gain during pregnancy, and paternal education level.

Performances of machine learning models

We developed CHD prediction models for newborns using five machine learning algorithms (SVM, RF, LR, XGBoost, and LightGBM) based on 21 selected features. Model performance on the internal test cohort is summarized in Table 2. Among the five models, XGBoost achieved the highest AUC (0.71), followed by LightGBM (0.67), LR (0.65), RF (0.67), and SVM (0.65). The ROC curves of these models are shown in Fig. 3, and their optimal hyperparameters are listed in Supplementary Table 2. To assess statistical differences between AUCs, we performed pairwise DeLong tests. As shown in Supplementary Table 3, no statistically significant differences were found between XGBoost and the other models (p > 0.05). However, XGBoost consistently outperformed others in terms of accuracy (69.6%), specificity (60.2%), and positive predictive value (83.4%), suggesting better balance and practical utility.

Based on its superior overall performance and robustness, we selected XGBoost for further optimization. To address class imbalance, we applied the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) prior to model retraining. The optimized XGBoost model achieved an AUC of 0.81 on the training set, 0.72 on the internal test set, and 0.71 on the external validation cohort, demonstrating stable performance and improved generalizability. The final hyperparameter settings are detailed in Supplementary Table 4. The ROC curves of the final XGBoost model across all datasets are presented in Fig. 4. Performance metrics of the final XGBoost model on the training, internal test, and external validation sets are summarized in Table 3. All evaluations were conducted using a unified probability threshold (cutoff = 0.429) optimized on the internal test set. The model achieved robust sensitivity across datasets, particularly in the external validation cohort (91.1%), underscoring its potential as a screening tool for early identification of CHD.

To assess the clinical utility of the prediction model, we performed DCA on the external validation dataset. After applying Isotonic Regression to calibrate predicted probabilities, the XGBoost model demonstrated a net benefit over “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies across a clinically meaningful range of threshold probabilities (25–60%) (Fig. 5).

Furthermore, to evaluate the model’s generalizability across different CHD subtypes, we assessed its performance in classifying atrial septal defect (ASD), ventricular septal defect (VSD), and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) cases within the external validation dataset. The XGBoost model achieved AUCs of 0.736 for ASD, 0.851 for VSD, and 0.682 for PDA, indicating good discriminative ability across these common CHD subtypes (Supplementary Table 5).

Important features and clinical interpretation of model

To better comprehend the contribution of features in the CHD model and identify key risk factors, we applied the SHAP algorithm to evaluate contribution of each feature in the XGBoost-based CHD prediction model. Figure 6 shows the ten most significant risk factors in the model on the training set. The contribution of each feature to the model was calculated using the mean SHAP value and displayed in descending order. The top ten ranked features include viral infection during pregnancy, maternal pre-delivery BMI, COVID-19 infection within the three months prior to the last menstrual period, gestational medicine use, father work, emotional stability in second trimester, maternal age, weight gain during pregnancy, iron supplementation, family history of diabetes (Fig. 6A, B). Additionally, the SHAP dependence plot helps illustrate how individual features influence the output of the prediction model. The real values of maternal pre-delivery BMI and maternal age and their SHAP values are displayed in Fig. 7. Positive SHAP values correspond to a relationship between the prediction model and the positive class. For example, maternal age > 35 years or pre-delivery BMI > 30 results in a SHAP value greater than 0, which pushes the prediction towards the CHD category.

We performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis with the top ten features obtained from the SHAP method to assess their association with CHD. The odds ratios (OR) for each variable and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated, and statistical significance tests were conducted. Figure 8 shows the OR values for the top 10 features. Among these variables, COVID-19 infection within the three months prior to the last menstrual period, gestational medication use and viral infections during pregnancy showed statistically significant associations with OR (95%CI) are 8.31 (4.86–14.72), 2.24 (1.65–3.03), 1.49 (1.13–1.97), respectively.

Notably, some features such as maternal BMI and emotional stability, while ranked highly by SHAP, did not reach statistical significance in the regression analysis. This discrepancy reflects the differences between machine learning and traditional statistical approaches: SHAP values quantify a feature’s marginal contribution to model predictions, often capturing non-linear effects and interactions that may not be significant in linear models. These results underscore the complementary value of SHAP and logistic regression in understanding and validating feature importance.

Discussion

In this study, we obtained 1,633 pregnant women comprehensive pregnancy risk factor data through a questionnaire survey. Using most up-to-date methodology, we established and validated five ML models for predicting CHD based on questionnaire data. The ML models performed well in the external validation cohort, with AUC of 0.706. In addition, we applied the SHAP method to interpret our model and in conjunction with multivariate logistic regression identified the key risk factors for neonatal CHD. We found COVID-19 infection in the three months prior to pregnancy, medication use during pregnancy, and viral infections during pregnancy were significantly associated with CHD in newborns.

Predictive models for various adverse perinatal outcomes, such as non-reassuring fetal heart rate, fetal growth restriction (FGR), and birth asphyxia, have demonstrated promising performance in previous studies [28–30]. However, many of these studies relied on coarse-grained data extracted from administrative systems, which often lack detailed clinical and behavioral variables [31]. Our predictive model differs from the models developed in other studies in several ways. Several previous studies have developed CHD predictive models that perform better than ours. Yet, some of those models may have overfitting due to a lack of feature selection, while others were developed using extremely small samples of CHD cases [32, 33]. Qu et al. using retrospective medical data reported an AUC of 0.76 [33]. The study directly incorporated all variables into the construction of the machine learning model without conducting collinearity checks and variable selection, which may cause overfitting [34]. In contrast, our study excluded collinear variables before constructing the model and applied the LASSO algorithm to select variables. Kaur et al. found superior performance (accuracy = 0.857) in their prediction model [32], but our dataset, with 437 CHD cases compared to their 18, provides a more reliable performance estimate. Most CHD prediction models achieve good performance, but they are not suitable for low-resource areas or broad-scale screening [18, 22, 35]. This is because they rely on ultrasound and metabolomics data, which are either less cost-effective or require assistance from experienced ultrasound physicians. In contrast, our model uses questionnaires to obtain the input variables for the model, which can be easily mastered by medical personnel after simple training and enables the identification of CHD high-risk infants within the general population.

We identified that maternal viral infection during gestation is the primary risk factor for neonatal CHD outset in our predictive model through SHAP method. Several previous literatures confirmed our finding [25, 26]. A systematic review and meta-analysis found that mothers with a history of viral infection during early pregnancy had 1.83 to 2.28 times higher risk of CHD in their children compared to those without such virus infections [36]. A prospective cohort study in central China found that maternal viral infection was independently linked to the risk of total CHD and several major CHD phenotypes in offspring, particularly in first trimester [37]. Many studies have revealed the mechanism by which maternal viral infection causes defection on fetus [36, 38, 39]. Viruses, including Cytomegalovirus, rubella, and herpes simplex virus, can cross the placenta and cause intrauterine infections that lead to fetal malformations [39]. Most of the previous literature has focused on the impact of viral infections in the first trimester on the fetus [36]. Our study has shown that viral infections throughout conception increase the risk of CHD in newborns. Meanwhile, Previous studies also have found that viral infections during the second and third trimesters may also cause fetal damage [40].

In recent years, COVID-19 has attracted the attention of numerous researchers, and some studies have found that gestational COVID-19 infection rises the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. A meta-analysis of 42 researches indicated that maternal COVID-19 infection during pregnancy raises the risk of preeclampsia, preterm birth, and stillbirth compared to pregnancies without COVID-19 [41]. A single-center study found that gestational COVID-19 infection may negatively affect the growth of the fetus’ heart [42]. Existing researches have mainly focused on the effects of gestational COVID-19 infection on the fetus, with limited research on the effects of pre-pregnancy COVID-19 infection. Our study found that maternal COVID-19 infection in the three months prior to the last menstrual period significantly increases the risk of CHD in newborns, suggesting that COVID-19 may have long-term effects on the mother. Despite the current lack of high-quality evidence regarding the impact of COVID-19 on human oocytes and early embryos, previous studies have uncovered significant findings. Specifically, these earlier studies have discovered the co-expression of Angiotensin-converting enzyme II (ACE2) and Transmembrane protease, serine 2 (TMPRSS2) in gametes, fertilized eggs, and trophectoderm cells [43]. ACE2 serves as the receptor for COVID-19 while TMPRSS2 facilitates virus proliferation and dissemination [44–46]. This co-expression may trigger immune responses that are detrimental to embryonic development [47].

In our study, gestational exposure to medications, including anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antihypertensive drugs, antiviral agents, antipyretics, and antibiotics, was identified as an important risk factor for the onset of CHD in neonates. The use of anticonvulsants, antidepressants, antihypertensive drugs, and antibiotics has been shown in multiple studies to be associated with infant heart defects. A meta-analysis of 131 studies showed that anticonvulsants increase the risk of cardiovascular malformations in offspring [48]. The conclusion of a birth cohort research revealed that exposure to antidepressants in early pregnancy increased the prevalence of cardiac birth defect [49]. Yakoob et al.’s study showed that first-trimester antihypertensive drugs use was associated with CHD [50]. A cohort study found maternal exposure to macrolide antibiotics during the first trimester was associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular malformations [51]. However, our review of previous literature has failed to reveal an association between exposure to antipyretics or antiviral drugs during gestation and the occurrence of CHD in offspring.

The unique advantage of this study is that we have applied five ML algorithms to CHD prediction, establishing a non-invasive and cost-effective CHD screening model based on questionnaire data, with a model AUC exceeding 0.7. In addition, we applied SHAP values to our predictive model to address the ‘black box’ issue, thereby enhancing both predictive performance and model interpretability. However, our study has certain limitations. First, although CHD encompasses multiple phenotypes, we did not develop subtype-specific prediction models due to limited sample sizes. Nonetheless, the general CHD model showed acceptable discrimination across subtypes, with AUCs ranging from 0.680 to 0.736 in external validation. Second, maternal exposure data were collected via self-reported questionnaires, which is subject to recall bias. To mitigate this, trained personnel conducted face-to-face interviews using standardized procedures to enhance data accuracy and consistency.

Conclusion

We have established an explainable ML model for predicting CHD in newborns using questionnaire data that can be readily acquired by medical personnel. Our proposed prediction model is cost-effective, with variables that are easily obtainable, rendering it suitable for low-resource areas or large-scale neonatal CHD screening. Additionally, key risk factors identified by the SHAP method and logistic regression include COVID-19 infection within the three months prior to the last menstrual period, gestational medication use, and viral infections during pregnancy.
