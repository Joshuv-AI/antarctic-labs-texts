Optimizing drilling fluid rheology with hybrid nanoparticles boron nitride and graphene nanosheets: an experimental study
Authors: Rashid Pourrajab, Mohammad Behbahani, Seyed Nasser Moosavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rheological performance of oil-based drilling fluids was enhanced using graphene nanosheets and graphene–boron nitride hybrid nanoparticles. Optimized rheology is critical for efficient cuttings transport, wellbore stability, and cost-effective drilling. Graphene nanosheets (100–1500 mg/L) increased apparent viscosity (AV) by up to 90% and plastic viscosity (PV) by up to 106% compared to the base mud across 140–240 °F, with negligible density change. The graphene–boron nitride hybrid system exhibited concentration-dependent nonlinear behavior: viscosities decreased at low concentrations (100–500 mg/L) but rose markedly at higher concentrations (1000–1500 mg/L), achieving up to 164% increase in AV and 71% in PV at 1500 mg/L and 240 °F relative to the base fluid. This synergistic effect arises from graphene’s lubricating properties combined with boron nitride’s structural reinforcement, enabling formation of a robust nanoparticle network that resists thermal thinning. These findings demonstrate that hybrid nanoparticles offer a tunable, effective strategy to customize drilling fluid rheology, improve hydraulic efficiency, reduce torque and drag, and lower operational costs in high-temperature environments.Keywords: Drilling hybrid nanofluid, Rheology, Graphene nanosheets, Boron nitride, Viscosity enhancementSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Drilling fluids are essential in hydrocarbon recovery operations, providing wellbore stabilization, facilitating cuttings removal, and maintaining downhole pressure control. Rheological properties are particularly important for effective hydraulic cleaning and suspension of drill cuttings. Oil-based drilling fluids (OBDFs) are often selected for challenging formations due to their superior shale inhibition, lubricity, and high-temperature stability. However, controlling their rheological behavior under extreme downhole conditions remains a significant engineering challenge. Elevated temperatures can cause substantial thinning, which compromises performance and increases non-productive time and costs1. In recent decades, nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative approach, enabling modifications at the atomic and nanoscale levels that result in novel material properties. Specifically, reducing material dimensions from the microscale to the 2–100 nm range increases surface area by up to a thousandfold. Looking forward, nanotechnology is expected to have broad applications across industry, commerce, and daily life. The integration of scientific advancements with technology can drive product development and economic growth, thereby enhancing societal standards of living2.

A nanofluid is defined as a solution of nanoscale particles dispersed in base fluids such as water, oil, ethylene glycol, diethylene glycol, and other solvents. Recent research has focused on the rheological behavior of nanofluids, with efforts to suspend nanoparticles in these fluids to enhance their effects in the medium3. However, a primary limitation of base fluids is their low thermal conductivity, which reduces heat transfer rates. To address this, incorporating nanoparticles into these fluids is expected to improve thermal conductivity, thereby increasing heat transfer rates and thermal efficiency4. Consequently, nanofluids offer advantages such as improved heat transfer, enhanced rheological properties, and increased thermal efficiency compared to conventional base fluids. Typically, nanofluids are prepared by suspending nanoparticles in fluids such as water, ethylene glycol, or oil, with particle sizes less than 100 nanometers5. Comparing nanofluids to microfluids reveals that reducing particle diameter from one micrometer to one nanometer increases the cross-sectional area by nearly a thousandfold. This larger surface area in nanoparticles enhances thermal conductivity due to increased contact surfaces. Furthermore, because nanoparticles are approximately a thousand times smaller than microparticles, they form stronger chemical and physical surface bonds. Consequently, these properties give nanofluids distinct advantages including greater stability and improved thermal performance not observed in microfluids. Thus, nanofluids are expected to exhibit greater stability than microfluids6.

Nanoparticles are used to modify and improve rheological properties, save costs, and serve as suitable replacements for viscosifiers in drilling oil-based muds. In this regard, an oil-based drilling mud with an 80/20 ratio of oil and brine is used, and its rheological parameters are measured based on the Power Law model7. Many studies have been done in this area. Karakosta et al.8 investigated the effect of nanopolymers on drilling fluids. In comparison to conventional drilling muds (polymer-based drilling systems), rheological properties such as plastic viscosity and density were enhanced. However, in high-temperature and high-pressure conditions, some stability-related issues may occur because of inappropriate physical and chemical properties, such as the mechanical and thermal properties of the polymers. Na’em Ahmad et al.9 examined the effects of adding iron oxide nanoparticles combined with potassium chloride, glycol, and polymer-based drilling mud to the base fluid (6% by weight). Adding 3% nanoparticles to the base fluid increases plastic viscosity. Also, an optimal concentration of 0.5% by weight of hematite nanoparticles can improve plastic viscosity by up to 10%. Moraveji et al.10 designed a stable glycol-based drilling fluid to replace conventional oil-based drilling fluids. Their results showed that the use of silica nanoparticles improves the rheological properties of glycol-based drilling fluids. Adding silica nanoparticles decreases fluid loss and increases the thermal stability of the drilling fluid. Additionally, they can effectively block nanoscale pores in the Gurpi formation shale. Therefore, the infiltration rate of glycol-based drilling fluid into shale samples of the Gurpi formation decreases. Katende et al.7 studied the performance of silica nanoparticles in reducing fluid loss and improving rheological properties in water-based and oil-based systems under high-temperature conditions. The results showed a positive impact on all rheological properties of both water-based and oil-based muds at ambient temperature. These properties included lubrication, electrical stability, and filtration under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions. Kevin and Mahmud11 examined silica nanoparticles under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions in synthetic invert emulsion-based drilling fluids in a gas reservoir. Their results showed that with a maximum nanoparticle concentration of 40% plastic viscosity and yield point decreased. However, other rheological properties improved in this study. Perween et al.12 synthesized bismuth ferrite nanoparticles using non-aqueous wet chemical techniques and used them as additives for water-based mud along with bentonite and other polymeric additives. These nanoparticles showed good compatibility with clay, leading to improved mud rheology. The interlayer interaction of the nanoparticles with clay and polymers resulted in increased apparent viscosity, plastic viscosity, yield point, and fluid loss control. It was found that increasing nanoparticle concentrations from 0 to 0.3 w/v% led to a 25% improvement in apparent viscosity, plastic viscosity, and yield point. Also, the fluid loss at 20 °C decreased by 35%. The combination of nanoparticles with bentonite platelets provides good thermal stability to the drilling fluid. Pourkhalil and Nakhaee13 examined the application of zinc oxide nanoparticles in preventing mud filtration and reducing water infiltrations into shale formations. Zinc oxide nanoparticles are known for their appropriate size and surface properties. Their results showed that zinc oxide nanoparticles significantly improved shale stability. This effect can be attributed to the size and other properties of these nanoparticles, such as hydrophilicity and positive charge. Edalatfar et al.14 studied the effect of titanate nanoparticles in water-based mud. They used viscosity tests to determine the rheological behavior of the mud. Based on a rheological study, the optimized sample contained 5% by weight of bentonite, 2% by weight of salt, and 1% by weight of titanate nanoparticles. Adding 1% by weight of titanate nanoparticles to the mud reduced the fluid loss volume by nearly 7.7%. Kamali et al.15 studied the use of carboxymethyl cellulose–iron oxide nanocomposite as a rheology modifier and fluid loss control additive. The results showed that adding 57% nanocomposite to the base drilling fluid significantly improved the viscosity. However, the filtration volume decreased. Adding this compound to the water-based drilling fluid for fluid loss control improves viscosity in both brine and deionized water conditions. Jayachandran et al.16 studied the reaction product of glycerin with long-chain fatty acids and graphene nanoparticles on the lubrication and rheological properties of water-based drilling fluids. Overall, 14 different drilling fluid samples were prepared for detailed analysis. The samples had concentrations of 1%, 2%, and 3% by volume. The temperatures of the samples were 60, 30, and 90 C. According to their results, all the samples showed Bingham plastic model behavior. Also, the viscosity of all the samples decreased as the temperature increased. Arian et al.17 examined the performance of graphene nanoplatelets in an inverse emulsion drilling fluid in wells with high temperatures. The temperature range was 120, 100, 80, 50, and 25 C. The experiment pressure was ambient pressure. By adding 0.3 parts per billion of nanoparticles, the plastic viscosity improved by up to 11% and the yield point increased by up to 42%. Additionally, the fluid loss decreased by up to 62%. Naderi et al.18 studied the application of copper oxide nanoparticles on the rheological properties and drilling fluid loss of water-based drilling fluids. The results showed that the nanofluid with a pH of 8 could be a good additive for improving fluid rheology. The yield point, apparent viscosity, gel strength at 10 s and 10 min, and fluid loss were improved by 45%, 33%, 200%, 100%, and 44% respectively.

Recent molecular dynamics (MD) studies provide valuable microscopic insights into the mechanisms behind enhanced thermophysical properties in nanofluids, particularly thermal conductivity improvements through interfacial effects and nanoparticle interactions. These findings offer a theoretical basis for understanding the synergistic rheological enhancements observed in our graphene–boron nitride hybrid oil-based drilling fluids. For example, Zhang et al.19 employed non-equilibrium MD simulations to explore hybrid nanofluids, such as Cu–Al in argon and glycerol–ethylene glycol bases. Similarly, another study by Zhang et al.20 applied MD to propylene glycol-based nanofluids incorporating metallic nanoparticles, revealing that layered adsorption structures around nanoparticles significantly boost thermal conductivity, with the inner adsorption layer primarily composed of base fluid molecules playing a key role in heat transfer augmentation, while outer layers involve mixed propylene glycol and water molecules. Furthermore, Zhang et al.21 investigated copper nanofluids using NEMD simulations, finding a linear increase in thermal conductivity with nanoparticle volume fraction. The primary mechanism was identified as a high-density quasi-solid adsorption layer on Cu surfaces, which expands effective thermal conduction pathways, as evidenced by radial distribution function peaks and number density profiles.

Recent studies on hybrid nanofluids, particularly those incorporating graphene oxide (GO) with other nanomaterials, have provided valuable insights into concentration-dependent enhancements in thermal conductivity and viscosity22,23. For instance, Kanti et al.24 examined the influence of pH on GO–CuO hybrid nanofluids, revealing optimized stability and thermal performance through adjusted surface chemistry and particle interactions. Additionally, a comprehensive study by Kumar et al.25 investigated the thermal conductivity and viscosity of water-based nanofluids containing graphene oxide (GO), silicon dioxide (SiO₂), titanium dioxide (TiO2), and their hybrids. Their results demonstrated that hybrid nanofluids, particularly GO-TiO2, exhibited superior performance, with thermal conductivity and viscosity ratios exceeding 43% and 144%, respectively, compared to the base fluid at 1 vol% concentration. While research on GO-based hybrids in aqueous systems is advancing26,27, the application of graphene-boron nitride hybrids in oil-based muds remains largely unexplored, presenting a critical research gap this study aims to address. Recent advancements in nanoparticle-enhanced drilling fluids have focused on high-temperature stability and hybrid formulations. Arain and Ridha28 investigated the effect of boron nitride nanoparticles on oil-based drilling fluids for high-temperature unconventional formation drilling. Their results demonstrated significant improvements in rheological properties, including a 24% increase in plastic viscosity and a 27% enhancement in yield point at 0.2 ppb concentration, alongside a 67% reduction in filtration losses at 0.5 ppb under high-pressure, high-temperature conditions. Abdolahi et al.29 evaluated the thermal and rheological performance of Behran oil-based nanofluids incorporating functionalized boron nitride (BN), carbon nitride (CN), and boron carbon nitride (BCN) nanoparticles at concentrations of 0.1–0.5 wt%. Key findings included a 14.92% enhancement in thermal conductivity with BN at 0.3 wt% and 50 °C, a remarkable 414% increase in specific heat capacity with CN at 25 °C, and minimal viscosity increases of less than 1% across all temperatures tested. Gokapai et al.30 provided a comprehensive review of nanoparticles in drilling fluids, covering types such as metal oxides, carbon nanotubes, and graphene nanoplates, with mechanisms for improving rheological, filtration, and thermal properties; the work emphasized boron carbide and nitride nanofluids for enhancing viscosity and shale inhibition, noting that hexagonal boron nitride increases thermal conductivity and viscosity with rising concentration, offering prospects for sustainable drilling operations.

Previous research has explored the potential of incorporating various nanoparticles into drilling fluids, which can improve viscosity, filtration control, and thermal stability. However, the combined use of graphene nanosheets and boron nitride nanoparticles in hybrid form has not been systematically investigated in oil-based drilling muds. Graphene offers exceptional lubricating ability and high surface area for network formation, while hexagonal boron nitride provides superior thermal stability and structural rigidity properties that, when synergistically combined, are expected to yield tunable, concentration-dependent rheological behavior and enhanced resistance to thermal thinning beyond what either material can achieve alone. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has explored this specific graphene–boron nitride hybrid system in oil-based formulations.

The present work addresses this gap by experimentally evaluating the effects of graphene nanosheets alone and graphene–boron nitride hybrid nanoparticles on the density and rheological properties of an oil-based drilling mud across a wide concentration range and temperature range. The primary objectives are to (i) quantify viscosity enhancements and thermal stability improvements, (ii) elucidate the concentration-dependent synergistic mechanisms in the hybrid system, and (iii) propose optimal nanoparticle formulations for improved hydraulic performance, torque/drag reduction, and drilling efficiency in high-temperature environments. By introducing and characterizing this novel hybrid nanomaterial approach, the study aims to provide new insights into nanoparticle synergy for advanced drilling fluid design.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemicals used in this study were of high analytical purity (> 99%) and sourced from commercial suppliers. The base oil-based drilling mud (OBDM) was formulated using diesel (density: 0.82 g/cm3), saturated brine (NaCl solution, 20% w/v), primary emulsifier (fatty acid-based), secondary emulsifier (amide-based), lime (Ca(OH)2), and barite (BaSO4) as a weighting agent. Graphene nanosheets (average thickness: 5–10 nm, lateral size: 1–5 μm, purity: >99%) and boron nitride nanoparticles (hexagonal, average size: 50–100 nm, purity: >99%) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Preparation of oil-based drilling mud

The OBDM was prepared with an oil-to-water ratio of 80:20 (diesel: saturated brine) to achieve a target density of 85 lb/ft3 (10.2 ppg). The procedure followed API Recommended Practice 13B-2 standards. Briefly, 181.5 mL of diesel was added to a laboratory mixing vessel, followed by 10.5 mL of primary emulsifier under continuous stirring at 6000 rpm using a Hamilton Beach mixer for 5 min. Subsequently, 8 g of lime was incorporated and mixed for 10 min. Saturated brine (45 mL) was then slowly added while stirring, followed by the secondary emulsifier (5 mL) and barite (as needed to adjust density). Emulsion stability was verified using an electrical stability meter (API 13B-2), and a pilot test confirmed the mud’s integrity, with no phase separation observed after 24 h of static aging at 150 °F.

Preparation of nanofluids and hybrid nanofluids

Graphene nanosheets and boron nitride nanoparticles were dispersed individually or as a hybrid (1:1 mass ratio for hybrid) into the OBDM at concentrations of 100, 200, 500, 1000, and 1500 mg/L. The 1:1 mass ratio for graphene-boron nitride hybrid nanofluids was carefully selected to maximize synergistic interactions between the two nanomaterials. This equal-mass configuration aligns with established practices in hybrid nanofluid research, where balanced compositions have been shown to optimize rheological enhancement through complementary particle interactions31,32. Nanoparticle masses (0.045, 0.090, 0.225, 0.450, and 0.675 g per 450 mL mud, respectively) were weighed using an analytical balance (Ohaus AV264, precision: ±0.0001 g). The nanoparticles were gradually added to the OBDM under manual stirring. Homogenization involved a three-stage process: (i) ultrasonication for 20 min using a Hielscher UP400S ultrasonic processor (400 W, 24 kHz, 50% amplitude); (ii) mechanical stirring for 20 min with an IKA overhead stirrer (2000 rpm); and (iii) repeated ultrasonication for 20 min. This ensured stable dispersion without agglomeration, as confirmed by visual inspection and zeta potential measurements (> 30 mV). Samples are shown in Fig. 1.

Density and rheological measurements

Mud density was measured using a pressurized mud balance (OFITE Model 140) at ambient conditions, following API 13B-2. No significant density changes were observed post-nanoparticle addition. Rheological properties were evaluated using a Fann 35 viscometer (six-speed rotational) in accordance with API 13B-2. Samples (350 mL) were preheated to test temperatures (140, 160, 180, 200, 210, and 240 °F) using a hot plate with magnetic stirring, and measurements were conducted immediately to minimize thermal degradation. Dial readings (θ) were recorded at shear rates of 600, 300, 200, 100, 6, and 3 rpm. All rheological measurements were conducted in triplicate (n = 3) for each nanoparticle concentration and temperature condition using freshly prepared and homogenized samples. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation. The uncertainty associated with each measurement instrument was obtained from manufacturer specifications: Fann 35 viscometer dial readings (± 0.5°), temperature control (± 1 °F), analytical balance (± 0.0001 g), and filtrate volume (± 0.1 mL). For derived parameters (AV and PV), uncertainty was propagated using standard error propagation formulas.

Results and discussion

All reported rheological values represent the mean of three independent replicates (± standard deviation), confirming the reliability and reproducibility of the observed trends. Variability was low, consistent with standardized API 13B-2 procedures and similar nanoparticle-enhanced drilling fluid studies using rotational viscometers33. These nanoparticles have unique properties and can improve the drilling process, reduce equipment maintenance costs, and increase productivity.

Characterization of nanoparticles

Based on the TEM image of graphene sheen (Fig. 3a), the characteristic, crumpled silk-like structure of graphene nanosheets is observed. This appearance is a direct result of the material’s incredible thinness and high two-dimensional flexibility. The sheets are not flat but appear folded and rippled, which is a typical and expected feature of free-standing, few-layer graphene. Numerous wrinkles and folds are visible across the sheets. This is not a defect but an intrinsic property. Because the graphene is only a few atoms thick but extends over a large area (microns), it bends and folds to stabilize itself, minimizing its surface energy. This texture gives it a large, accessible surface area. Different regions of the sheets show different levels of darkness or contrast. This is a critical observation: lighter, more translucent areas indicate zones where the sheet is very thin likely consisting of just one, two, or three layers of graphene. These areas allow more of the electron beam to pass through. Darker, more opaque areas correspond to regions where the sheets are stacked (multiple layers) or folded over themselves, creating a locally thicker structure that absorbs more electrons. The edges of the sheets appear smooth and well-defined in visible areas, suggesting good structural integrity. The sheets are continuous over large distances without obvious tears or holes, indicating a sample of reasonable quality suitable for application-based studies. The continuous gradient of contrast from very light to dark confirms that the sample consists of few-layer graphene (FLG). It is not a single, uniform sheet but a material with a variable number of layers, typically ranging from 1 to perhaps 5–10 layers in the thickest, folded regions.

TEM images of graphene nanosheet (a) and hexagonal boron nitride (b) and SEM images of graphene nanosheet (c) and hexagonal boron nitride34,35 (d).

This observed nanostructure is highly advantageous for modifying drilling fluid rheology: The wrinkled, high-surface-area morphology allows graphene sheets to interact extensively with oil-based mud molecules, creating greater friction and additional network points, which directly explain the measured increases in viscosity (AV and PV). The flexibility and large lateral size enable the sheets to act as effective “bridges” within the fluid, reinforcing its structure even at high temperatures and contributing to better thermal stability. The combination of thin, transparent regions and thicker, folded zones provides a versatile structure that can interact with the fluid on multiple scales.

The provided TEM image (Fig. 3b), reveals aggregated, plate-like nanoparticles characteristic of hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN). Unlike the large, continuous, and wrinkled sheets seen in the graphene sample, these BN particles appear as smaller, distinct, and stacked plates or flakes. This indicates a fundamental difference in their nanostructure and behavior compared to the graphene nanosheets. The primary particles are irregularly shaped plates with lateral dimensions typically in the tens to a few hundred nanometer range (as indicated by the 150 nm scale bar). They do not form the extensive, micron-scale continuous sheets typical of graphene but exist as discrete, flake-like units. A dominant feature is the strong tendency of these plate-like particles to aggregate and stack, forming thicker clusters or agglomerates. This stacking is evident from the overlapping edges and the varied contrast within the clusters. This behavior is common in h-BN due to van der Waals forces between layers and suggests that the particles consist of multiple atomic layers (multilayer h-BN flakes). Similar to the graphene image, variations in electron-beam contrast are evident: the thinner edges of individual flakes appear lighter and more translucent. The centers of aggregates and overlapping regions appear much darker and more opaque, confirming increased thickness due to the stacking of multiple BN layers. The particles exhibit sharp, well-defined edges and a relatively uniform plate-like geometry, which suggesting a crystalline or semi-crystalline structure consistent with hexagonal boron nitride. The lack of a highly crumpled, paper-like texture differentiates them morphologically from the flexible graphene sheets. This observed nanostructure is crucial for understanding its role in the hybrid graphene-BN system: The smaller, thicker, and more rigid BN platelets are likely structural spacers or supports within the fluid and between the larger graphene sheets. They can physically prevent the re-stacking or excessive aggregation of graphene, helping to maintain its dispersed, high-surface-area state. The combination of the lubricating, flexible graphene sheets and the more rigid, plate-like BN particles can create a more complex and tunable internal network in the drilling fluid. The BN may provide anchor points or modify the flow dynamics differently than graphene alone, leading to the non-linear, concentration-dependent rheological changes (e.g., viscosity reduction at low concentrations and increase at high concentrations). The SEM images of graphene sheet and h-BN were shown in Fig. 3 (c) and (d) for more morphological characterization of these materials. The SEM image shows graphene nanosheets (Fig. 3c) at a scale of 2 micrometers. The thin, sheet-like structures with visible folds and edges reflect the flexible, two-dimensional nature of the material. These surface features are typical of graphene and indicate a good distribution and structural quality on the substrate. SEM image of h-BN (Fig. 3 (d)) exhibits extensive formation of irregular, micron-scale agglomerates, a phenomenon commonly observed in nanomaterials due to high surface energy and prominent van der Waals interactions that promote particle cohesion34,35.

Dispersion stability assessment

To further confirm the dispersion stability of graphene nanosheets, boron nitride nanoparticles, and their hybrid in the oil-based drilling mud, zeta potential and dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were conducted on representative samples post-homogenization. Zeta potential values ranged from + 35 to + 45 mV for graphene-containing systems and + 50 to + 66 mV for BN and hybrid formulations (measured at 25 °C using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS), well above the ± 30 mV threshold commonly accepted for good colloidal stability via electrostatic repulsion and steric contributions in non-aqueous media. These values remained stable with < 5% variation after 24 h standing, indicating minimal aggregation during the period relevant to our rheological experiments. Complementary DLS analysis revealed hydrodynamic diameter distributions consistent with primary particle sizes (average ~ 100–200 nm for BN-dominant and broader for graphene due to lateral dimensions), with polydispersity indices (PDI) below 0.3 and no significant increase in mean size or appearance of large aggregates over 24 h, confirming effective dispersion achieved through the three-stage ultrasonication-mechanical process.

Rheological properties of oil-based drilling mud

The apparent viscosity (AV) of the base oil-based drilling mud (OBDM) decreased with increasing temperature from 140 °F to 240 °F, exhibiting an overall reduction of 45% (Table 2). Similarly, plastic viscosity (PV) declined by 56% over the same temperature range (Table 2). These trends show a gradual decrease at lower temperatures, which accelerates at higher temperatures, indicating diminished solid particle interactions. Scientifically, this temperature-dependent thinning occurs because elevated temperatures increase the kinetic energy of molecules, expanding intermolecular distances and reducing internal friction within the fluid matrix. In oil-based systems, this is exacerbated by the thermal expansion of the continuous phase, weakening van der Waals forces and reducing lower resistance to flow36.

Effect of graphene nanofluid (graphene sheets/oil-based drilling mud)

Incorporation of graphene nanosheets increased AV significantly with concentration, as shown in Table 2; Fig. 4a,b, across all tested temperatures (140–240 °F). PV also rose markedly (Table 2), with enhancements of 36% at 100 mg/L and 140 °F, escalating to 106.2% at 200 mg/L and 240 °F relative to the base mud; however, the rate of PV increase was less pronounced than AV, and temperature-induced decreases remained minimal. This viscosity augmentation stems from graphene’s high aspect ratio and surface area, which facilitate the formation of a percolating network within the fluid, enhancing shear resistance through particle bridging and entanglement. At higher concentrations, aggregation intensifies these interactions, counteracting thermal thinning by stabilizing the fluid structure. The subdued PV response and sustained values under temperature fluctuations are attributable to graphene’s inherent lubricating properties, which reduce inter-particle friction while maintaining overall viscosity, making 1500 mg/L optimal for balanced performance37,38. The uncertainty analysis confirms that all observed viscosity increases exceed measurement uncertainty by a factor of 5–10. The relative uncertainties for AV measurements range from 0.3 to 1.3% (base mud) to 0.4–0.9% (graphene nanofluid), while PV measurements show relative uncertainties of 0.6–1.8% (base mud) and 0.3–0.9% (graphene nanofluid).

Effect of hybrid nanofluid (graphene-boron nitride/oil-based drilling mud)

The hybrid nanofluid exhibited concentration-dependent rheological behavior: at lower concentrations (100–500 mg/L), AV and PV decreased with temperature, while at higher concentrations (1000–1500 mg/L), they increased (Table 3; Fig. 5a,b). The nonlinear rheological behavior can be interpreted through the framework of DLVO theory and steric hindrance effects39. Although DLVO theory was originally developed for aqueous colloids, it has been extended to explain stability and rheological transitions in non-aqueous nanofluids and drilling fluids, where van der Waals attraction dominates due to the low dielectric constant of oil, while additional steric and morphological factors play key roles.

At low nanoparticle concentrations, the average interparticle distance remains relatively large, allowing Brownian motion to promote dispersion; however, the incorporation of rigid, plate-like BN nanoparticles (as evidenced in TEM images, Fig. 3b) disrupts the percolating network formed by flexible graphene nanosheets, reducing effective hydrodynamic volume and weakening particle bridging, which leads to a net decrease in apparent viscosity (AV) and plastic viscosity (PV) compared to the base mud or single-graphene system. This network-disrupting or dilution-like effect at low loadings has been observed in hybrid nanofluids with mismatched particle morphologies.

At higher concentrations, reduced interparticle spacing enhances attractive van der Waals forces while steric hindrance arising from the larger, flexible graphene sheets and rigid BN platelets prevents excessive aggregation and promotes formation of a robust, entangled structural network through particle bridging, entanglement, and steric stabilization. This results in pronounced viscosity enhancement and improved resistance to thermal thinning, with the hexagonal BN platelets acting as rigid spacers that limit graphene restacking while graphene contributes lubrication within the network. The observed concentration threshold for synergistic behavior aligns with DLVO-based interpretations of interparticle force balances governing rheological transitions in nanofluid systems40,41. Also, this nonlinear trend reflects synergistic interactions between graphene and boron nitride, where low concentrations allow Brownian motion and particle dispersion to dominate, potentially disrupting the fluid network and leading to viscosity reduction akin to dilution effects. At higher concentrations, enhanced particle-particle and particle-matrix bridging form a robust structural network, increasing viscosity through steric hindrance and improved load transfer. Boron nitride’s hexagonal layers provide additional lubrication and thermal bridging, mitigating thinning. This shift underscores the concentration threshold for synergy, where hybrid effects outweigh individual nanoparticle contributions, resulting in up to 95% AV enhancements at an optimal level31,32. The statistical uncertainty analysis confirms that all observed rheological enhancements exceed measurement uncertainties by a factor of 5–10, with relative uncertainties ranging from 0.3 to 1.8% for direct viscosity measurements and 3.5–5.7% for percentage enhancement calculations.

The complementary selection of graphene nanosheets (for network formation and lubrication) and boron nitride nanoparticles (for structural reinforcement and dispersion stability) was validated by the experimental results. Graphene’s high aspect ratio enabled efficient viscosity enhancement at elevated concentrations, while BN’s rigid platelet morphology prevented graphene restacking and maintained colloidal stability. This synergistic interaction, hypothesized during material selection based on their distinct physicochemical properties, produced concentration-dependent rheological behavior unattainable with either material alone42. Limitations and implications for HPHT conditions

While the present experiments were limited to atmospheric pressure and temperatures up to 240 °F, real-world high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) drilling often involves conditions exceeding 300 °F and pressures above 10,000–20,000 psi, where thermal thinning, emulsion instability, and barite sagging become more pronounced in oil-based muds. Boron nitride (BN) nanoparticles exhibit exceptional inherent thermal stability (stable up to ~ 900 °C in controlled environments) and have demonstrated significant performance improvements in oil-based drilling fluids under high-temperature conditions, including up to 67% reduction in HPHT filtration losses, 24% enhancement in plastic viscosity, and reduced barite sagging at concentrations as low as 0.2–0.5 ppb28.

Hybrid systems combining BN with graphene-based nanomaterials (such as graphene nanoplatelets) in invert emulsion fluids for high-temperature shale formations further enhance rheological stability, viscoelastic properties, and filtration control, often outperforming single-nanoparticle additives by forming synergistic networks that mitigate thermal degradation. In general, nanoparticle additives like BN and graphene counteract thermal thinning by reinforcing internal structural networks, though extreme HPHT conditions may still induce gradual viscosity reduction due to intensified thermal expansion, potential nanoparticle agglomeration if dispersion is insufficient, or surfactant/emulsion breakdown in the oil phase47.

The synergistic G-BN hybrid network in our study with BN providing thermal bridging and structural rigidity while graphene contributes lubrication and bridging is anticipated to offer superior resistance to thermal degradation compared to conventional muds, consistent with trends in nitride- and carbon-based nanofluids where viscosity maintenance and filtration control improve or stabilize at optimized concentrations under HPHT.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that graphene nanosheets and graphene-boron nitride hybrid nanoparticles significantly enhance the rheological properties of oil-based drilling muds without affecting density, providing a cost-effective strategy to optimize drilling operations at elevated temperatures (140–240 °F). Key findings include increases of up to 90% and 106% in apparent and plastic viscosities, respectively, for graphene nanosheet nanofluids at 200 mg/L. For graphene-boron nitride/oil-based hybrid nanofluids at 1500 mg/L, these values increase by 164% and 71%, respectively, compared to conventional muds. The observed concentration-dependent improvements in hybrid systems are attributed to network formation, lubrication, and synergistic interactions. These enhancements facilitate improved cuttings transport, wellbore cleaning, and friction reduction, which are especially valuable in directional and horizontal wells for reducing non-productive time and equipment wear. The novelty of this work lies in the first reported use of graphene-boron nitride hybrids in oil-based muds, addressing a gap in the existing literature. For field-scale application, graded concentration strategies based on wellbore temperature profiles are recommended to optimize cost-performance balance, with 1500 mg/L hybrid formulation suggested for high-temperature sections (> 200 °F) and lower concentrations (200–500 mg/L) for cooler intervals. Future research should prioritize HPHT validation, long-term stability assessment, and comprehensive ecotoxicity evaluation of hybrid nanoparticles to ensure environmentally responsible field deployment.
