Diverse and significant microbe-mediated mineral transformation in deep-sea hydrothermal vent indicated by Methylophaga, Sulfitobacter and Roseovarius
Authors: Minghua Liu, Yingwen Zhong, Yi Ding, Kuojian Ma, Can Chen, Yuehong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Long-term organic-free enrichment with hydrothermal sulfide/iron-rich sediment successfully constructed diverse sulfur-oxidizing communities.•36 potential sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (including 10 novel species) were isolated from the mineral-enriched community.•Sulfitobacter sp. l-8 and Roseovarius sp. B-10 achieved 68%–93% thiosulfate consumption and mediated diverse and significant mineral transformation.•This study connects microbial sulfur-oxidizing metabolisms with sulfide weathering, expanding insights into microbial roles in sulfide mineral weathering and utilization.•SOB act as key drivers of sulfide transformation, biogeochemical cycling and energy dynamics in deep-sea hydrothermal vents ecosystems.

Introduction

Deep-sea hydrothermal vents sustain unique chemosynthetic ecosystems on Earth. Venting fluids are enriched with hydrogen sulfide, hydrogen, methane, and various metal ions (e.g., Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺, Zn²⁺), providing abundant energy sources for microorganisms (Dick, 2019). Traditionally, these venting fluids containing bioavailable chemical substrates were considered the major energy source supporting hydrothermal vent ecosystems. Chemoautotrophic microbes fix carbon using energy derived from oxidation of reduced compounds, thereby forming the foundation of the hydrothermal food webs and exerting a profound influence on deep-sea biogeochemical cycles (Zeng et al., 2021). However, recent studies have revealed that polymetallic sulfide deposits may also support primary production (Fallon et al., 2017; Meng et al., 2021; Yvenou et al., 2025). Even without active venting, deep-sea microorganisms were able to colonize on solid polymetallic minerals (Fallon et al., 2018), forming mineral-associated chemoautotrophic communities sustained by mineral oxidation, and establishing novel chemoautotrophic communities for organic production (Hou et al., 2020). The oxidation of sulfide mineral and the associated mobilization of elements by such mineral-associated community can influence biogeochemical cycles over long timescales, and are particularly important in inactive or extinct hydrothermal areas where hydrothermal fluids are absent (Hou et al., 2020; Meier et al., 2019).

Although studies employing metagenome sequencing and onboard incubations techniques have highlighted the “mineral-driven” ecosystems sustained by microbial sulfur and iron oxidation (Achberger et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2022), the underlying dynamic mechanisms of microbe-mineral interaction remain unresolved. Currently, microbial involvement in sulfide transformation is primarily inferred from metagenomic predictions of metabolic potential, morphological features of in situ samples, and in situ colonization experiments with mineral substrates (Dong et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2020; Li et al., 2017; Meier et al., 2019; Mitsunobu et al., 2021). However, these approaches are inherently limited in characterizing the actual physiological capabilities of the microorganisms and their direct interactions with mineral substrates. Without isolated functional strains and experimental validation, the definitive link between microbial sulfur metabolism and mineral weathering remains elusive. Direct experimental evidence is still lacking to demonstrate how microorganisms mediate the weathering and transformation of polymetallic sulfides. Therefore, isolating functional strains involved in microbe-mineral interaction and verifying their capabilities to participate in mineral weathering are crucial for elucidating how mineral supports the energy conservation in hydrothermal ecosystem, as well as their role in cryptic element cycling in deep-sea hydrothermal fields.

In this research, sulfide mineral and polymetallic sediment samples were obtained from the hydrothermal field along the Carlsberg Ridge (CR) and Southwest Indian Ridge (SWIR) in the Indian Ocean and subjected to long-term enrichment experiments simulating microbe-mineral interactions. High-throughput sequencing was employed to characterize microbial community composition and infer metabolic potential. A total of 38 non-redundant bacterial species were isolated using multiple isolation strategies, of which 36 were predicted as sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (SOB) based on genomic analysis. Three strains named Sulfitobacter sp. L-8, Roseovarius sp. B-10, and Methylophaga sp. F7 were chosen to conduct in vitro thiosulfate oxidation assays and microbe-mineral interaction using ion chromatography (IC) and scanning electron microscopy equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), respectively. These SOB strains exhibited efficient thiosulfate-metabolizing capabilities and directly participated in weathering of sulfide mineral. Nanoscale weathering morphologies including bioerosion and production of novel secondary mineral were observed. Overall, this study established direct link between microbial sulfur oxidizing metabolisms and the morphological transformation of polymetallic sulfide minerals, highlighting the importance of SOB in mineral weathering and oxidation process in hydrothermal environments.

Material and methods

Sample collection and mineralogical analysis

Hydrothermal chimney and sediment samples were collected from the CR and SWIR (Fig. 1; Table S1). Chimney sample JL218 was obtained from an inactive vent from the Tianxiu hydrothermal area using machine arm of the Jiaolong HOV and was stored in a sealed biobox during dive (Shenhai-1, DY72, June 2022). On board, JL218 was sectioned with sterile shovels. The outer layer comprised red oxidized crust were named JL218O, while the inner black sulfide portion was named JL218I. Sediment sample BC13 and BC14 were collected near active hydrothermal sites of Tianxiu region using box corer, at distance 1.5 km and 4.3 km from the nearest vent, respectively. Another four sediment samples from Yuhuang-1 hydrothermal region in the SWIR were collected during DY85 cruise using a television (TV) grab (Dayang Hao, DY85, May 2024). The distances of these sediment samples from the known vent site (34II-TVG23, Fig. 1) ranged from 0.47 to 3.03 km (Liang et al., 2023; Liao et al., 2018). For mineralogical analysis, sulfide mineral and sediment sample were first freeze-dried (Alpha 1–4 LDplus) and ground into particles <53 μm in diameter. X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted using a Bruker D8 Venture Ius3.0 (Bruker) with the following parameters: 42 kV × 100 mA power, Cu Kα radiation, 2θ scanning range of 5° to 80°, step size of 0.02°, and scan speed of 0.2 s/step. Downstream measurement was performed in one-dimensional continuous scanning mode. X-Ray Fluorescence (XRF) analysis was performed using a PANalytical Epsilon 1 spectrometer to determine the elemental compositions. Powders of each sample were pressed into pellets using a hydraulic press at 40 tons for 10 s. After instrument calibration and quality control, measurements were performed in solid-pellet mode. Sample thickness and mass were entered into the software before initiating the automated analysis. Semi-quantitative results were derived from acquired spectra, with compositions reported in both elemental and oxide forms.

Mineral-Interacted microorganism enrichment

A total of 140 modified mineral-microbial enrichment experiments were conducted using artificial seawater (ASW) as the basal medium. The experimental design included the following controlled variables: anaerobic/aerobic, 30 °C/40 °C, with/without mineral substrates (chalcopyrite (CP), pyrite (PY), or sphalerite (SP)), and with/without samples (BC13, BC14, JL218O, JL218I, TVG2, TVG3, TVG4, TVG5).

The composition of 1 L ASW medium included: NaCl, 30.00 g; K2HPO4, 0.14 g; CaCl2·2H2O, 0.14 g; MgSO4·7H2O, 3.40 g; MgCl2·6H2O, 4.18 g; KCl, 0.33 g; NH4Cl, 0.25 g; Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6 H2O, 0.01 g; modified Wolin's mineral solution, 1 ml; Wolin's vitamin solution, 1 ml. The modified Wolin’s mineral solution (1 L) contained: nitrilotriacetic acid, 1.50 g; MgSO4·7 H2O, 3.00 g; MnSO4·H2O, 0.50 g; NaCl, 1.00 g; FeSO4·7 H2O, 0.10 g; CoSO4·7 H2O, 0.18 g; CaCl2·2 H2O, 0.10 g; ZnSO4·7 H2O, 0.18 g; CuSO4·5 H2O, 0.01 g; AlK(SO4)2·12 H2O, 0.02 g; H3BO3, 0.01 g; Na2MoO4·2 H2O, 0.01 g; NiCl2·6 H2O, 0.03 g; Na2SeO3·5 H2O, 0.0003 g; Na2WO4·2 H2O, 0.0004 g). The Wolin's vitamin solution (1 L) included: biotin, 0.02 g; folic acid, 0.02 g; pyridoxine hydrochloride, 0.1 g; thiamine HCl, 0.05 g; riboflavin, 0.05 g; nicotinic acid, 0.05 g; calcium D-(+)-pantothenate, 0.05 g; vitamin B12, 0.001 g; p-aminobenzoic acid, 0.05 g; (DL)-alpha-lipoic acid, 0.05 g).

Mineral substrates were ground into particles with diameter <53 μm and added into ASW medium to a final concentration of 200 mmol/L. The composition of all mineral substrates was verified by XRD and XRF as described above. All mineral substrates were sterilized prior to use.

All enrichments were conducted in 100 mL bottle containing 50 mL medium and sealed with butyl rubber stopper. For anaerobic enrichments, culture media were flushed with 99.999% nitrogen for 30 min to remove dissolved oxygen. All media were then autoclaved in 121 °C, for 20 min. After cooling, filter-sterilized vitamin solution was added. 3 mL 5% (w/v) filter-sterile NaHCO3 solution and 0.5 mL of 2 g/L Na2S·6 H2O were added. The headspace was composed of a CO2: N2 ratio of 1:7 at 16 psi. For aerobic enrichment, bottles were maintained under atmospheric condition. The pH of all media was adjusted to 7.0, and no organic carbon source was added.

Prior to incubation, sediment sampled were mixed 1:1 (w:v) with autoclaved bottom seawater collected in Tianxiu hydrothermal region under sterile conditions. Chimney samples JL218O and JL218I were ground with autoclaved pestle. For aerobic enrichments, incubation was performed in UV-sterilized clean bench with 2.5 mL of the sediment-seawater mixture. For anaerobic enrichments, incubation was completed in an isopropanol-wiped anaerobic chamber (American, Coy Lab). All enrichments were incubated statically in the dark for 90 days.

Composition and function profile of enrichment community

For microbial community analysis, 10 mL of enrichment culture was withdrawn using a sterile syringe and filtered through a 0.22 μm polycarbonate membrane (Millipore, USA). Total genomic DNA was extracted using the ALFA-SEQ Magnetic Soil DNA Kit (FINDROP, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primer pair 515F/806R (515F: 5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′, 806R: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) (Caporaso et al., 2011; Parada et al., 2016) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR products were verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and the target DNA bands were excised and purified using the E.Z.N.A.® Gel Extraction Kit (Omega, USA). Concentration and purity of recovered DNA products were examined using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) and a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Sequencing libraries were constructed using the ALFA-SEQ DNA Library Prep Kit (FINDROP, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Amplicon sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 250 bp reads (PE250).

For metagenomic analysis, DNA extraction, library construction, and quality control were performed using the same protocol described above. Metagenomic sequencing was conducted on an MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads (PE150).

rRNA amplicon and metagenome analysis

QIIME2 pipeline (Bolyen et al., 2019) was used for ASV (amplicon sequence variant) identification and 16S rRNA data analysis. Taxonomic classification was performed using the “feature-classifier” plugin with a pre-trained SILVA 138.1 (SSURef) database file downloaded from the QIIME2 website.

For metagenome data, clean reads were trimmed using fastp v0.23.2 (Chen, 2023; Chen et al., 2018). High-quality reads were assembled using MEGAHIT v1.2.9 with default parameters (Li et al., 2015). Protein coding sequences (CDSs) were predicted using Prodigal. Functional annotation of CDSs was performed using METABOLIC v4.0 (Zhou et al., 2022), KofamScan v1.3.0 (Aramaki et al., 2020) and eggNOG-mapper v2.1.12 (eggNOG 5.0.2) (Cantalapiedra et al., 2021; Huerta-Cepas et al., 2019). Taxonomy of CDSs was presumed by the best hit retrieved from NCBI-NR annotation using DIAMOND, with an e-value threshold of 10−5 (Buchfink et al., 2021; Sayers et al., 2025). Read counts mapped to CDSs were calculated using featureCounts v2.0.6 (Liao et al., 2014) and normalized to TPM (Transcripts Per Million) (Wagner et al., 2012) using a custom Python script. Metagenome binning was conducted using MetaWRAP (Uritskiy et al., 2018), which employs the following three binning tools: MaxBin2 (Wu et al., 2016), CONCOCT (Alneberg et al., 2014) and MetaBAT2 (Kang et al., 2019). Resulting bins were refined by the MetaWRAP refinement module, with threshold of completeness >50% and contamination <10% (Uritskiy et al., 2018). Taxonomy of metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) passing the quality thresholds were analyzed by GTDB-Tk v2.1.1 (release R214) (Chaumeil et al., 2020).

Microorganism isolation and identification

Isolation strategies were guided by the 16S V4 amplicon sequencing results. Given the enrichment of both heterotrophic and potential autotrophic microorganisms, three microorganism isolation techniques were used in this research: i) Enrichment cultures were serially diluted and plated on 2216 agar (BD Difco, USA). Single colonies were purified by repeated streaking to isolate heterotrophic bacteria. ii) Enrichment cultures were serially diluted in 5 mL ASW medium supplemented with 200 mmol/L mineral substrates to enrich and isolate autotrophic bacteria. iii) Enrichment cultures were serially diluted in 5 mL ASW supplemented with 200 mmol/L mineral substrates as original enrichment supplemented with 1% (v/v) methanol to isolate dominant Methylophaga species. All isolates reported in this research were cultivated under aerobic conditions at 30 °C. Strain purity was judged by colony physiology on agar plate and 16S rDNA sequencing.

Genome sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

Total genomic DNA was first extracted from pure isolates using a Bacteria DNA Kit (Omega, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality was assessed using the methods described above. Whole-genome sequencing was performed on an MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform with paired-end 150 bp reads (PE150). Clean genome reads were assembled by MEGAHIT (Li et al., 2015). Genome completeness and contamination were assessed using CheckM (Parks et al., 2015). ANI (average nucleotide identity) was calculated by fastANI (Jain et al., 2018), with reference genomes from the same genus downloaded from the NCBI Genome database. Phylogenetic trees of MAGs and isolate genomes were built by IQ-TREE 2 (-B 1000, -m LG+F+R10) (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017; Minh et al., 2020) with concatenated protein sequences of bac120 markers identified by GTDB-Tk (Parks et al., 2018).

Chemical characterization of thiosulfate-oxidizing capabilities in SOB

Prior to thiosulfate-oxidizing tests, all isolates were subjected to a purity check by full-length 16S rDNA sequencing to exclude potential contamination. Growth curves were monitored by measuring OD600 using a UV/visible spectrophotometer (Ultrospec 6300 pro, Amersham Biosciences) to determine the time for characterizing thiosulfate-oxidizing capabilities. Appropriate observation windows began with inoculation (1%, v/v) of log‑phase cells and ended at the late exponential (pre‑stationary) phase to avoid bias caused by cell lysis during the decline phase. For Sulfitobacter sp. L-8 and Roseovarius sp. B-10, thiosulfate-oxidizing assays were performed by inoculating 50 μL log-phase cultures to 5 mL ASW medium supplemented with 0.1% (w/v) yeast extract, 0.5% (w/v) peptone and 1.5 g/L sodium thiosulfate (∼6.05 mmol/L, Na2S2O3·5H2O) for simultaneously monitoring cell growth and thiosulfate and sulfate concentration. For Methylophaga sp. F7, assays were conducted in ASW medium supplemented with 0.3% (v/v) methanol and 1.5 g/L sodium thiosulfate. Samples were collected at 24-hour intervals, with three biological replicates. Cell free supernatants were obtained by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 10 min) and analyzed by ion chromatography (ICS-5000+/900, Thermo Fisher) to determine thiosulfate and sulfate concentrations. All ion chromatography measurements were conducted at the Instrumental Analysis Center in Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Microbe-Mineral interaction experiments and SEM-EDS characterization

To investigate microbe-mineral interaction, pure cultures of Roseovarius sp. B-10, and Sulfitobacter sp. L-8 were incubated in ASW medium supplemented with chalcopyrite or pyrite powders, 0.1% yeast extract and 0.5% peptone as mentioned above. Methylophaga sp. F7 was incubated with ASW medium supplemented with 0.3% methanol. Abiotic controls consisted of only ASW medium and mineral powders. All cultures were aerobically incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 150 rpm.

For SEM observation, 1 mL of cultures incubated for 30 and 90 days were collected. Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), with each step lasting 5 min, followed by freeze-drying for 24 h under vacuum (Alpha 1–4 LDplus). Morphological features and elemental composition of the samples were examined with a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Ultra 55) operated at 10 kV and equipped with an energy dispersive spectrum (EDS, Oxford-Inca X-Max 20). Prior to SEM-EDS analysis, all samples were coated with a thin platinum (Pt) layer for enhancing conductivity.

Results

Mineralogy of hydrothermal chimney and sediment

Chimney sample JL218 was primarily composed of chalcopyrite (CuFeS2) and sphalerite (ZnS), with high abundance of S (45.14%), Zn (33.16%), and Fe (13.7%) (Fig. S1; Table S2). Sediment samples BC13 and BC14 were enriched in Ca (31.17%−51.02%), Si (18.87%−30.37%), Fe (7.09%−7.85%), and Na (5.04%−7.36%). Small brown and black particles were also observed in sediment and presumed as Fe-Mg-Si minerals based on XRD results (Fig. S1; Table S2). Composition of the sediments collected from the SWIR was more diverse. The gray sediment TVG2 was enriched in Fe (34.64%), Si (24.37%), Al (19.45%), and Mg (12.11%) with detection of chalcopyrite. For the brown TVG4, in addition to Fe (36.13%), it contained high Cu (28.22%) and S (12.6%), and was characterized by the presence of pyrite (FeS2) and goethite (FeO(OH)). The brown TVG5 and yellow-brown TVG3 were mainly composed of Fe (30.73%−36.11%), Si (22.52%−24.37%), and Mg (7.93%−12.11%). Serpentine-related minerals were detected in TVG3. Overall, samples analyzed in this study exhibited characteristic hydrothermal sedimentary features such as enrichment in Fe and the presence of sulfide minerals (Fig. S1; Table S2).

Enrichment community composition and function

As revealed by 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing, the enrichment communities were dominated by the class Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, Bacteroidia and Rhodothermia (Fig. 2A). At the genus level, Marinobacter, Halomonas, Alcanivorax, Methylophaga and Muricauda were the major bacterial taxa, with average relative abundance ranging from 5.93% to 24.88% (Fig. 2B). Unclassified sequences were also detected, suggesting the presence of potentially novel lineages within Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria. In addition, the taxonomic resolution was further expanded by metagenome sequencing, as 433 MAGs (completeness >50% and contamination <10%) were recovered. Phylogenomic analysis assigned these MAGs to 19 phyla, including two clades that remained uncharacterized (UBA10199, BMS3Abin14). Detected MAGs were from diverse bacterial lineages, including Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Actinomycetota, Deinococcota, Gemmatimonadota, Myxococcota, Planctomycetota, Chloroflexota, Methylomirabilota, Nitrospirota, Desulfobacterota, Marinisomatota, Omnitrophota, Patescibacteria, Bipolaricaulota, and Bacillota_G, indicating substantial phylogenetic diversity (Fig. 2C). Chemoautotrophic microorganisms were also enriched in these communities as no carbon source was supplemented in culture medium. Key genes involved in the Calvin-Benson-Bassham cycle, including ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase genes (rbcSL), were detected, and were affiliated with Methylophaga, Thiomicrospira, Gracilimonas, Rhodomicrobium, and Thiogranum (Fig. 3B). Notably, Methylophaga-related ASVs were also highly abundant in most enrichment communities, with average abundance of 5.29% in 90-day enrichments (Fig. 2) and 21.69% in 180-day enrichments (data not shown). Combined with the presence of sox cluster genes, these results suggested that Methylophaga might perform chemoautotroph with energy conserved from thiosulfate oxidation, producing organic matter to support the enrichment communities. This organic contribution from Methylophaga likely facilitated the growth of other fast-growing heterotrophic bacteria, such as Sulfitobacter, Thalassospira and Roseovarius (Fig. 2).

Isolation of SOB strains

Strains from Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, Balneolia, Cytophagia, Flavobacteriia, and Bacilli were isolated. Most of highly enriched taxa were isolated by streaking plate on 2216 agar, including Marinobacter, Paracoccus, Sulfitobacter, Flagellimonas, and Gracilimonas. In addition, serial dilution cultures supplemented with 1% (v/v) methanol enabled the isolation of two Methylophaga species (F2&F7). In total, 38 non-redundant strains were obtained and purified in this study (Table 1). Based on 16S rDNA sequence analysis, these strains shared 98.84−100% identity with their closest cultured relatives (Table 1). Pairwise average nucleotide identity (ANI) analysis, the common methodology used for novel taxonomy identification, showed that 10 strains exhibited <95% ANI values relative to available reference genomes (Chaumeil et al., 2020), which included Methylophaga sp. F7 (90.15%), Marinobacter sp. B-12 (92.83%), Sulfitobacter sp. L-8 (79.32%), Paracoccus sp. L-17 (82.50%), Paracoccus sp. L-5 (82.50%), Paracoccus sp. B-1 (82.58%), Paracoccus sp. L-6 W (80.27%), Maricaulis sp. B-26 (80.27%), Flagellimonas sp. B-3 (94.4%), and Gracilimonas sp. B-17 (82.8%) (Table 1). Evaluation of thiosulfate-oxidizing capabilities

Sulfitobacter sp. L-8 (Fig. 5A–C), Roseovarius sp. B-10 (Fig. 5D–F) and Methylophaga sp. F7 (Fig. 5G–I) were selected for thiosulfate-oxidizing capabilities evaluation assays based on the presence of (near-)complete sox clusters or exhibited microbial mineral weathering feature (described in the “microbe-mineral interaction feature” section). B-10 (Fig. 5A, Fig. 5D), respectively, suggesting efficient utilization of thiosulfate. For Sulfitobacter sp. B-10 only generated 0.31 mmol/L SO42− (Fig. 5E), suggesting the presence of alternative unknown thiosulfate transformation pathways. Both species exhibited active cellular growth (Fig. 5C, Fig. 5F) and clear biomass production in thiosulfate-supplemented 2216 media. Methylophaga sp. F7 also grew in medium containing methanol and thiosulfate, and was able to oxidized S2O32− to SO42− (Fig. 5G–I). After 30 days of incubation of Methylophaga sp. F7 with chalcopyrite, nano-scale metallic oxide particles and rod-like structures enriched in C, O, S, and Fe (Fig. 6G, red arrows) were observed on the mineral surface. These particles were deposited in the pericellular area, with diameters ranging from 20 to 50 nm (Fig. 6G–H), and interconnected via extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), forming unique network-like and filamentous biomineralization structures (Fig. 6H, red arrows). For Sulfitobacter sp. L-8, irregular nano-sized pits were observed on the mineral surfaces after 90 days of incubation (Fig. 6I, yellow arrow), as the result of the direct microbe-induced dissolution.

Mineral spherules were also observed in cultures of Roseovarius sp. B-10 incubated with pyrite after 30 days of incubation (Fig. 6J, red arrow). With prolonged incubation (90 days), more significant weathering characteristics were observed. Similar to those occurred on chalcopyrite culture samples, dissolution pits with comparable size of microbe cells were also observed on pyrite surfaces (Fig. 6K, yellow arrows). Additionally, smooth amorphous secondary mineral precipitates (1–2 µm in diameter) containing C, O, P, S, and Fe were observed (Fig. 6K–L, red arrows), which might form through mineral dissolution and subsequent reprecipitation. The high abundance of O (46.5%) and Fe (22.0%) suggested that these mineral precipitates are predominantly composed of iron oxides.

Discussion

SOB participation in microbe-mineral interaction

This research combined large-scale microbe-mineral incubation experiment with 16S rDNA amplicon and metagenome sequencing to characterize microbial community composition and functional potential (Fig. 2; Fig. 3). Diverse SOB predominated in these microbe-mineral incubation experiments (Fig. 2B; Fig. 3B), suggesting their important role in mineral utilization. Potential autotrophic bacteria were also enriched, indicating sulfide minerals served as an energy source for chemotrophic growth. The following thiosulfate oxidation assay (Fig. 5) and mineral morphological inspection (Fig. 6) further confirmed their versatile metabolic capabilities in thiosulfate oxidation and mineral transformation, which provided solid evidence for SOB participating in sulfide mineral interaction characterized by oxidation, dissolution, and secondary mineral generation.

Metagenomic analyses revealed enrichment of key sulfur oxidation genes, including sqr, fccAB, and sox (Fig. 3). The sqr and fccAB genes encode sulfide-quinone oxidoreductase (Sqr) and flavocytochrome c sulfide dehydrogenase (FCSD), respectively. These enzymes can catalyze the oxidation of sulfide to elemental sulfur (or polysulfide) while transfer electrons to the respiratory chain (Chen et al., 2025; Lü et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2021). Notably, the relative abundance of fccB was lower in JL218 (chimney, CR) and TVG (sediment, SWIR) enrichments compared to BC (sediment, CR) (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the sox gene cluster remained consistently abundant across all enrichments, indicating that thiosulfate-oxidizing metabolism represents a dominant sulfur oxidation strategy during sulfide mineral utilization (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

As microorganisms interacting with minerals might adhere to the surface, sediment and mineral samples were directly added into medium for enrichment. Sediment particles were homogeneously mixed with sulfide mineral substrate (chalcopyrite, pyrite and sphalerite), to stimulate the growth of microorganisms that can metabolically interact with mineral. The initial design of enrichment medium excluded the addition of common artificial organic sources such as yeast extract or peptone in order to select chemolithoautotrophic microorganisms. However, the residual organic matter from sediment could not be neglected even after long-term starvation, and supported the growth of diverse heterotrophic bacteria (Fig. 2B–D) with distinct morphological signatures on sulfide minerals. These heterotrophic bacteria might either survive on the organic substrates from the sediments or the organic carbon produced by several potential autotrophic species, such as Thiogranum and Methylophaga (Fig. 2, Fig. 3B). Although the isolation of Thiogranum via plate-streaking and dilution-to-distinction was unsuccessful, Methylophaga sp. F7 was successfully isolated and possesses the potential for chemoautotrophy via sulfur oxidation (Liu and Zhong, in prep). Chemoautotrophic microorganisms oxidize sulfide minerals and perform primary production, supporting the growth of heterotrophic bacteria such as Roseovarius. These heterotrophic bacteria can further promote the mineral weathering through sulfur oxidation metabolism. In addition, metabolites (e.g., organic acids) produced by heterotrophic bacteria can accelerate mineral dissolution and weathering (Ullman et al., 1996). This coupling highlights the integration of carbon and sulfur cycling within microbe-mineral interactions, potentially enhancing metabolic efficiency and ecological stability in deep-sea hydrothermal environments.

In total, 36 strains of SOB were isolated (Table 1; Fig. 4) in this research. Three strains (Sulfitobacter sp. L-8, Roseovarius sp. B-10 and Methylophaga sp. F7) were selected here due to their genomic potential for sulfur oxidation (Fig. 4C) and strong activities in mineral transformation (Fig. 6). Sulfitobacter sp. L-8 and Roseovarius sp. B-10 exhibited high efficiency in thiosulfate consumption (956 mg/L – 1399 mg/L in less than five days) (Fig. 5A, D) as cell population reached maximum level. These oxidation rates exceeding previous reports on Pseudomonas sp. IOP_13 and Halomonas sp. IOP_14 with thiosulfate oxidation rates of ∼625 mg/L – 750mg/L in nine days (He et al., 2022).

Sulfur oxidation has been widely studied in microbial sulfur cycling at mid-ocean ridges (e.g., the Southwest Indian Ridge) (Cao et al., 2014; Zhou, 2023). Members of Methylophaga, Sulfitobacter and Roseovarius are known to participate in sulfur metabolism (Boden et al., 2010; Ding et al., 2023). Notably, the Roseobacter clade, including Sulfitobacter and Roseovarius, widely distributed in marine environments, and have been recognized as major thiosulfate consumers in deep-sea hydrothermal vent settings, implying their important roles in sulfur oxidation within deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystems (Ding et al., 2023; Major et al., 2025). However, their direct roles in mineral weathering and mineral-associated sulfide oxidation remain largely unexplored, particularly in the context of microbe-mineral interactions within hydrothermal deposits. In this study, three strains not only oxidized thiosulfate, but also caused pronounced weathering on the surfaces of chalcopyrite and pyrite. These specific morphological changes were clearly revealed by SEM, including dissolution pits, nanoscale oxide particles, and other secondary mineral generation (Fig. 6G–L). These morphologies might result from the extraction and oxidation of sulfur elements mediated by microorganisms. The weathering patterns observed in our study, characterized by dissolution pits and oxide particles, are consistent with previous results on the morphological features of mineral weathering mediated by SOB (Liu et al., 2020; Liu and Zhang, 2015). Compared with abiotic controls (Fig. 6A–F), the extent of mineral weathering in microbe-mineral treatments was substantially higher (Fig. 6G–L), confirming the active contribution of these strains to sulfide oxidation and mineral transformation. Collectively, these findings provide direct experimental evidence linking microbial sulfur oxidation to mineral weathering processes, and demonstrate that widely distributed hydrothermal taxa, particularly members of the Sulfitobacter and Roseovarius can actively mediate the transformation of solid-phase sulfur. This expands their ecological role from dissolved sulfur cycling to direct participation in mineral-associated sulfur processes, highlighting their previously underappreciated contribution to solid-phase sulfur biogeochemistry.

Despite these findings, it is important to recognize that laboratory cultivation conditions differ substantially from in situ deep-sea hydrothermal environments, such as hydrostatic pressure and physical fluid flow. Previous studies have demonstrated that hydrostatic pressure can change microbial metabolic rates compared with cultures incubated at atmospheric pressure (Wu et al., 2013), suggesting that microbial sulfur oxidation and associated mineral transformation processes may differ under in situ hydrothermal conditions.

SOB mobilized sulfide minerals and enhanced resource-sharing for community

As sulfide minerals are formed through hydrothermal fluid precipitation, reduced sulfur species in solid phase are retained in these minerals. In comparison to the dissolved sulfide input from hydrothermal fluids, mineral-bound sulfur may exhibit low bioavailability, although they are abundant and widely distributed in deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystem. Microbial sulfur oxidation can effectively increase the bioavailability of these otherwise recalcitrant mineral-bound sulfur resources by transforming them into soluble or metabolically accessible forms. The metabolic strategies employed by SOB may primarily metabolize the sulfur species embedded within sulfide minerals (Liu and Zhang, 2015), which increases the overall bioreactivity of sulfur minerals for microbial communities. Through microbially mediated mineral dissolution, a larger contact area may be created to form micro-niches for bacterial colonization (Fig. 6I, K, yellow arrows) (Banfield et al., 1999). Metallic elements are also microbially mobilized and released either as dissolved ions dispersing via plume or as amorphous and poorly crystalline minerals with much higher bioavailability for microorganisms, such as goethite. Transformation of sulfur element might also be diverse, as intermediate species (S0, S4O62−, S2O32−) and organic sulfur compounds might also be produced (Kelly et al., 1997; Wu et al., 2021). In the case of Roseovarius sp. B-10 and Sulfitobacter sp. L-8, not all supplemented S2O32− were transformed into expected end product, sulfate (SO42−). As no other inorganic sulfur species were detected, it is possible that S2O32− might be assimilated into organic sulfur molecules, such as cysteine, via assimilatory sulfate reduction (Fig. 4C) (Chen et al., 2025; Li et al., 2020). In this way, the recalcitrant sulfide mineral might be transformed into bio-available organic compounds (e.g., sulfur-containing amino acids such as cysteine and methionine), making them accessible to a wider range of bacterial consumers within sediment communities (Vermeij and Kertesz, 1999). The mobilization, sharing, and exchange processes of both organic sulfur compounds and mineral-derived sulfur might be enhanced within microbial communities, facilitating stable and efficient nutrient cycling and energy conservation network under oligotrophic conditions.

Microbial communities on the Carlsberg Ridge and the Southwest Indian Ridge play vital roles in primary production and elemental biogeochemical cycling in inactive hydrothermal fields via the oxidative dissolution of sulfide minerals. These chemolithoautotrophic microbes form the base of the food web in hydrothermal ecosystems, and the sulfur transformation processes mediated by them are crucial for maintaining deep-sea biodiversity and ecosystem stability.

Overall, this study demonstrates that SOB play a dual role in deep-sea hydrothermal systems by simultaneously driving sulfur oxidation and mediating sulfide mineral transformation. Through these processes, they regulate both energy flow and element mobilization, highlighting the central importance of microbe-mineral interactions in shaping biogeochemical cycles in inactive hydrothermal environments.

Conclusion

This study aimed to narrow the knowledge gap concerning the roles of microorganisms in sulfide mineral weathering and utilization within deep-sea hydrothermal sediment. Through large-scale microbe-mineral incubation experiments, 36 sulfur-oxidizing bacterial strains were isolated. Representative strains, Methylophaga sp. F7, Sulfitobacter sp. L-8, and Roseovarius sp. B-10, exhibited efficient thiosulfate consumption and induced mineral transformation with distinct morphological features. These findings suggest that SOB might contribute to nutrient cycling and energy conservation in hydrothermal microbial communities by driving the mobilization of recalcitrant sulfide mineral into more bioavailable sulfur compounds. Overall, this research highlighted the role of SOB as key drivers of sulfide mineral transformation, biogeochemical cycling, and energy dynamics in hydrothermal vents ecosystems.
