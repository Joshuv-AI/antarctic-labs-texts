Robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery for bilateral ureteral fibroepithelial polyps in children: a case report
Authors: Jin Zhang, Rui Zhou, Yu Ou-yang, Wen Fu, Zhang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUreteral fibroepithelial polyps (FEPs) are rare, benign lesions in children that may cause ureteral obstruction. Bilateral ureters involvement is especially uncommon, and the optimal surgical approach remains debated.Case presentationA 3-year-old boy of East Asian ethnicity presented with intermittent abdominal pain for 2 weeks. Ultrasound and computed tomography (CT) imaging indicated severe hydronephrosis and dilatation in the bilateral upper ureters. Robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery (RALS) identified multiple FEPs in both ureters, and bilateral partial ureterectomy with end-to-end anastomosis was performed. At 12-month follow-up, no symptoms had recurred and hydronephrosis had significantly improved.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first reported case of bilateral ureteral FEPs treated via RALS in a single procedure, suggesting that RALS is feasible and safe for complex pediatric ureteral FEPs. Long-term follow-up is warranted to evaluate the risk of recurrence.

Background

Ureteral fibroepithelial polyps (FEPs) are benign lesions originating from the mesoderm. The incidence of FEPs is 2–6% [1]. FEPs frequently occur unilaterally and involve the upper ureter, with a prevalence of approximately 0.5% in children with ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO) [2]. Most reports of ureteral FEPs in children are case reports or small series [3], bilateral ureters involvement is especially uncommon. Since Bartone et al. [4] reported the first case of bilateral FEPs causing UPJO in a child, only four cases have been identified in the English literature [5].

Ureteral FEPs are usually characterized by abdominal pain, hematuria and urinary tract infections. It is unlikely that ureteral FEPs can be precisely diagnosed preoperatively; Adey reported a preoperative detection rate of 28.5% [6]. Most FEPs are identified during surgery, and the traditional approaches include open surgery, laparoscopic surgery or endoscopic surgery. Currently, robot-assisted laparoscopic surgery (RALS) has been widely used and has been shown to have a high success rate for treating pediatric urinary tract diseases in a minimally invasive manner, thus providing an additional treatment option for FEPs. Case presentation

A 3-year-old boy of East Asian ethnicity was referred to a tertiary medical center after complaining of intermittent upper abdominal pain for 2 weeks. The abdominal pain was colicky and resolved spontaneously. Each episode of pain lasted for 20 to 30 minutes. His basic vital signs were stable, abdominal physical examination revealed a flat abdomen, tympanic to percussion, remarkable finding was mild tenderness in the upper abdomen, with no palpable mass, no rebound tenderness or guarding was elicited, bladder not distended. The patient has no family history of genetic disease and no history of trauma. He had no prior urological symptoms, no history of urinary tract infections, and no previous surgical interventions. The patient lives with both parents in a stable family environment, and his growth and developmental milestones were age-appropriate. The imaging appearance was linear soft tissue attenuation‌, different from tumor, calculi or inflammatory lesion‌, ureteral FEPs were suspected, and the obstruction was relieved via RALS, but the final diagnosis remained to be determined by intraoperative exploration and histologic examination.

The case report was approved by the Regional Ethics Committee of our center. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, an informed consent form was obtained from the patient’s legal guardian.

Surgical technique

The surgery was performed using the da Vinci Xi robotic surgery system, with the following surgical instruments: fenestrated bipolar forceps, monopolar curved scissors, and a large SutureCut needle driver.

After general endotracheal anesthesia, the patient was placed in the right lateral position. The umbilical incision was made, the laparoscope was inserted through this approach, and the other 2 trocars were placed 8 cm below the umbilicus level and near the right rectus abdominis muscle, the robotic arms were inserted. Adopted the mesenteric approach, opened the colonic mesentery, and fully exposed the left kidney and ureter. The dissection range was from the ureteropelvic junction to the middle ureter. Then a longitudinal cut was made along the stenotic ureteral wall. Multiple FEPs were identified, and the lumen was completely blocked (Fig. 2). We resected the entire diseased segment of the ureter (approximately 2 cm in length on the left side) while carefully preserving the periureteral adventitia and its blood supply. Adequate perfusion of the remaining ureteral ends was confirmed by visual assessment of the mucosal color and active capillary bleeding at the cut margins. The specimen was maintained for histologic examination. Next, the lowest point of the proximal ureter and the highest point of the distal ureter were trimmed to a wedge shape, and the posterior walls of the proximal and distal ureters were anastomosed with a continuous suture using 5-0 Vicryl sutures along the margin. The patient was turned to the left lateral position, the robotic arms were reinserted to explore the right ureter. Multiple FEPs were identified in the proximal ureter, with a diseased segment measuring approximately 1.5 cm. We performed a partial ureterectomy of the diseased segment, using end-to-end anastomosis similar to that of the opposite segment. After ensuring that no damage occurred, the robotic arms were removed, and the incision was closed.

The surgery procedures took a total of 245 minutes, and the volume of blood loss was 5 ml.

Pathological diagnosis

Gross examination of the resected ureteral segments revealed multiple polypoid lesions with smooth surfaces arising from the ureteral mucosa, the largest lesion measured approximately 1.0 cm in length. Histologic examination showed polypoid structures lined by hyperplastic urothelium, overlying a fibrovascular stroma composed of loose connective tissue with scattered chronic lymphocytic infiltration and mild submucosal edema (Fig. 3). Hospital course and follow-up

On the 5th day after surgery, the patient recovered well and was discharged, no complications were reported. The double J stents were removed 2 months after surgery. At the 12-month follow-up assessment, no symptoms recurred, and bilateral hydronephrosis improved significantly. The most recent B-ultrasound scan revealed hydronephrosis and dilatation in the bilateral upper ureters (the maximum diameters were 0.9 cm on the left and 1.6 cm on the right). The patient and his parents are satisfied with the treatment outcome. Although no complications occurred after stent removal, further follow-up into adulthood is still necessary due to the risk of disease recurrence [7, 8].

Discussion

The differential diagnosis of proximal ureteral obstruction in children includes UPJO due to intrinsic stenosis or crossing vessels, ureteral calculi, ureteral stricture secondary to trauma or infection, and rarely, malignant neoplasms. In our case, preoperative contrast-enhanced CT revealed linear soft tissue attenuation within the proximal ureteral lumen bilaterally, which differs from the irregular wall thickening seen in malignancies, and the extrinsic compression pattern of crossing vessels. The absence of calcification on CT helped exclude calculi, while the smooth, homogeneous enhancement pattern and the patient's age made malignancy unlikely. The bilateral involvement and imaging characteristics led us to suspect FEPs preoperatively, but the definitive diagnosis required intraoperative exploration and histological confirmation.

Although surgical treatment is advised for ureteral FEPs, there is a debate on surgical methods [9]. In the past, nephroureterectomy has been performed for ureteral FEPs in children because of potential malignancy, since FEPs were confirmed to be benign, and more minimally invasive methods were advocated [10]. A systematic literature review [11] concluded that endoscopic resection should be considered for the treatment of FEPs, but no study on children was included in the systematic literature review. Additionally, the feasibility and effectiveness of endoscopic surgery for children have been queried due to operative difficulty in the narrow lumen and a long learning curve for surgeons. Fukui et al. reported successful endoscopic treatment using thulium laser for multiple ureteral FEPs in an 11-year-old boy [12], to the best of our knowledge, this is the first pediatric case of ureteral FEPs successfully managed with this technique. However, endoscopic ablation for FEPs in situ of the ureter is associated with a higher risk of residual disease and recurrence compared to resection of the diseased ureteral segment [7, 8].

Regarding surgical approaches, endoscopic treatment is the least invasive but is technically challenging in the narrow pediatric ureteral lumen, with a higher risk of incomplete resection and recurrence [7, 8, 12]. Open surgery remains reliable for complex reconstructions but entails greater tissue trauma. Conventional laparoscopic surgery allows complete excision but is limited by restricted instrument articulation for precise ureteral anastomosis, so it is difficult to finish bilateral ureteral anastomosis via open or laparoscopic surgery at one time. To date, all reported cases of bilateral ureteral FEPs in children have been managed by staged pyeloplasty or ureteroscopic approaches. Notably, Romesburg et al. [5] described recurrent obstruction following initial ureteroscopic treatment, which ultimately required secondary open pyeloplasty.

Since the number of applications of RALS in pediatric urology has increased rapidly, showing advantages of minimal invasiveness, improved manipulation and visualization, a recent study [13] confirmed the panoramic view offered by the robotic technique, which was highly sensitive for identifying tiny anatomical structures such as the crossing vessel during robot-assisted laparoscopic pyeloplasty. RALS was recommended for the treatment of UPJO and ureterovesical reflux [14]. Ureteral FEPs have also been reported to be successful treatments in children [15], but all relevant studies included small series, and there are no precedent cases of bilateral ureteral FEPs treated via RALS in a single procedure.

In our case, flexible robotic instruments and panoramic views are helpful for locating the lesions precisely, dissecting the ureters and relieving the obstruction, thus minimizing the operating time as much as possible, even without reinsertion of the trocars. Our preliminary experience suggests that RALS may be an effective and safe method for treating ureteral FEPs in children, even for complex cases such as bilateral ureteral FEPs. However, age and weight could be contraindications for RALS; in some cases, the lack of robotic instruments available for young and low-weight children limits the advantages of RALS [16], based on clinical experience, RALS demonstrates greater advantages in older children. For surgeons, the lack of force feedback in robotic arms may result in excessive clamping force, leading to tissue damage, which represents one of the current limitations of RALS. Moreover, increasing the operation time could lead to increased anesthesia risk compared with staging surgery. Therefore, we suggest a comprehensive evaluation of the surgical approach on the basis of the patient's details and the surgeon's preferences.

At the 12-month postoperative follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with satisfactory ultrasound findings. However, we acknowledge that this follow-up period is still insufficient to fully assess long-term outcomes, particularly regarding the risk of FEP recurrence and late ureteral stricture formation. Kojima et al. [8] reported metachronous FEP recurrence in children occurring years after initial treatment, emphasizing the need for prolonged surveillance. We plan to continue follow-up with annual renal ultrasound and clinical assessment into adulthood.

Conclusion

Based on this single case, RALS appears feasible and safe for treating bilateral ureteral FEPs in children in a single procedure. However, given the single-case design and relatively short follow-up period, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Long-term follow-up is essential to evaluate recurrence and ureteral stricture formation. Further studies comparing different surgical approaches in larger pediatric cohorts are warranted to establish evidence-based recommendations.
