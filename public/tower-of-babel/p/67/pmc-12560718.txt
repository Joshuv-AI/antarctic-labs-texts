Greater Health Resources Are Used for Direct-to-implant Breast Reconstruction in Women With Higher Body Mass Index
Authors: Alexander F. Mericli, Carrie K. Chu, Margaret S. Roubaud, Rahul Khanna, Alia Khaled, Yiran Rong, Patrick B. Garvey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Healthcare resource use associated with breast reconstruction may vary by body mass index (BMI). We hypothesized that obese women undergoing direct-to-implant (DTI) reconstruction used more resources than healthy-weight women.Methods:Using the Premier Healthcare Database, a retrospective cohort study was conducted among women aged 21 and older who had mastectomy and concurrent DTI breast reconstruction from 2012 to 2020. Patients were classified into obese (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) and healthy-weight (BMI 20–25 kg/m2) groups. Outcomes, including cost, operating room time, and 12-month breast reconstruction revision rate, were assessed and compared between the study cohorts. The inverse probability of treatment weighting method was used to balance the covariates between the 2 cohorts. Study outcomes were assessed using weighted generalized estimating equation (GEE) models. Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare study outcomes between morbidly obese patients (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2) and healthy-weight patients.Results:After applying study criteria and inverse probability treatment weighting, 1150 patients were identified (682 obese patients and 468 healthy-weight patients). Compared with healthy-weight patients, obese patients had significantly higher procedure costs (mean: $26,778.53 [SD: $21,973.63] versus $24,209.93 [SD: $12,16.90], GEE P = 0.03) and significantly longer operating room time (mean: 285.86 [SD: 110.20] versus 248.02 [SD: 89.43] min, GEE P < 0.001). Significant differences in 12-month revisions were observed between morbidly obese patients and healthy-weight patients (22.9% versus 14.5%, GEE P = 0.02).Conclusions:Among women undergoing immediate DTI breast reconstruction postmastectomy, those who were obese had significantly higher resource use compared with healthy-weight women.

Takeaways

Question: How does obesity impact healthcare resource use and costs in women undergoing direct-to-implant breast reconstruction after mastectomy?

Findings: This retrospective cohort study using a nationwide hospital database (2012–2020) found that obese women had significantly higher procedure costs (+11%) and longer operating room times (+38 min) compared with healthy-weight women, though revision rates were similar.

Meaning: Obese women undergoing direct-to-implant breast reconstruction require more healthcare resources, underscoring the need for tailored strategies to address their unique challenges.

INTRODUCTION

A large proportion of women in the United States are obese (41.9%–43.3% across age groups) with a body mass index (BMI) of 30 kg/m2 or greater, and this population has steadily increased over the years.1,2 The World Health Organization has subclassified degrees of obesity based on BMI: class I (30–34.99 kg/m2), class II (35–39.99 kg/m2), and class III (≥40 kg/m2).3 Class III, or severe obesity, affects 11.5% of the US population.2 Among women presenting for mastectomy and reconstruction, approximately 28% meet the diagnostic criteria for obesity.4–6 Many prior studies have demonstrated that obesity is a risk factor for postoperative complications among women undergoing breast reconstruction postmastectomy. In their examination of the 2005–2010 American College of Surgeons—National Surgical Quality Improvement Program databases, Fischer et al5 observed that obese patients undergoing breast reconstruction had a higher incidence of comorbid conditions, higher American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status, longer operative times, and longer hospital stays compared with healthy-weight patients (BMI 20–29.9 kg/m2). Studies have also reported a higher risk of surgical complications, medical complications, and reoperation among obese women undergoing breast reconstruction postmastectomy.7–10 When examining the association between obesity and total healthcare costs within 1 year of breast reconstruction, Huo et al11. observed the adjusted mean total healthcare costs (adjusted to 2014 US dollars) for obese class II and III patients to be significantly higher than those for healthy-weight patients (BMI ≤ 29.9 kg/m2) ($90,804 versus $81,490; P = 0.02).

Much of the data on resource use comparison among obese and healthy-weight women are outdated. Further, there is limited information on the procedural cost comparison among these women. Given the increasing prevalence of breast cancer and obesity in women, it is vital to understand the impact of obesity on resource use for women undergoing breast reconstruction after mastectomy. Based on prior studies and our own clinical experience, we hypothesized that direct-to-implant (DTI) reconstruction in obese women was significantly more costly, required more operating room (OR) time, and was associated with a greater revision rate compared with DTI reconstruction in healthy-weight women.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Data Source

This retrospective cohort study used the Premier Healthcare Database (PHD) from January 1, 2011, to December 31, 2021. The PHD is a nationally representative, all-payer US hospital database that maintains data on inpatient and outpatient encounters from nonprofit, nongovernmental, and community and teaching hospitals and health systems.12 It contains complete clinical coding, hospital cost, and patient billing data from more than 1000 hospitals throughout the United States. The included hospitals are representative based on bed size, geographic region, location (urban/rural), and teaching status. The database contains all billed items by the cost-accounting department, including medications, laboratory, diagnostic, and therapeutic services, and primary and secondary diagnoses for each patient’s hospitalization. Patient demographic and payor information is also available and can be linked to enrollment files. This analysis of the PHD was conducted under an exemption from institutional review board oversight for US-based studies using de-identified healthcare records, as dictated by Title 45 Code of Federal Regulations (45 CFR 46.101(b)(4)) (https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2011-title45-vol1/pdf/CFR-2011-title45-vol1.pdf).

Study Population

The study included female patients aged 21 years or older at the time of mastectomy concurrently with DTI breast reconstruction (Current Procedural Terminology 19340, International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-9] procedure codes 85.53, 85.54, and International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification [ICD-10] procedure codes 0HRT0JZ, 0HRT0KZ, 0HRT3JZ, 0HRT3KZ, 0HRU0JZ, 0HRU0KZ, 0HRU3JZ, 0HRU3KZ, 0HRV0JZ, 0HRV0KZ, 0HRV3JZ, 0HRV3KZ) in inpatient or outpatient settings between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2020. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the codes for breast reconstruction, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E431.) The first reported admission was considered the index admission. Patients had to incur the index admission in a hospital that continuously contributed inpatient and outpatient data to PHD in the 12-month pre- and postindex periods. In addition, eligible patients should have had a diagnosis of breast cancer or a genetic susceptibility to breast cancer, identified through coding, during the index admission or 12-month preindex admission period. Patients were excluded from the study if they (1) experienced autologous reconstruction or tissue expander insertion at the index episode; (2) had missing BMI information during the index admission and 12-month period before the index date; (3) were classified as underweight (BMI < 20 kg/m2) or overweight (BMI 25–30 kg/m2); and (4) had missing values in any outcome variable.

Based on the World Health Organization obesity classification system, patients were classified into the healthy-weight group (BMI 20–25 kg/m2) or obese group (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2) using primary or secondary BMI ICD-9/10-CM diagnosis codes and BMI calculated from weight and height assessed at the index and 12-month preindex periods. For patients with multiple codes, the one closest to the index procedure date was considered when assigning the BMI category. Patients with BMI values outside the ranges of healthy or obese weight were not included in our study to achieve cleaner cohorts that enable the direct evaluation of the differences in outcomes between healthy-weight and obese patients.

Study Outcomes

Study outcomes included index breast reconstruction procedure cost, index breast reconstruction supply cost, index breast reconstruction OR time, and 12-month postreconstruction revision rate. Index procedure cost was defined as the total cost of treating the patient during the index mastectomy and DTI admission, including all supplies, labor, depreciation of equipment, and so on. Index supply cost was defined as the cost of all supplies for the index DTI admission. Costs were adjusted to 2021 US dollars based on the US Consumer Price Index for medical care. Index breast reconstruction OR time included the total time that a patient spent in the OR for their index mastectomy and reconstruction procedure. In the PHD database, cost is defined as the total cost for a charged item, encompassing all supplies, labor, and equipment depreciation. It aligns with the managerial accounting definition of total cost, which is the sum of variable and fixed costs. Therefore, the assessed cost reflects the expenses incurred for the procedure itself, excluding the hospital markup.

Study Covariates

Patient demographics, including age, race/ethnicity, and insurance status, were reported. Index breast reconstruction characteristics were identified, including procedure setting (inpatient/outpatient), year of index reconstruction, reconstruction laterality (unilateral/bilateral), concurrent breast reduction procedure (yes/no), use of acellular dermal matrix (yes/no), and use of fat grafting (yes/no). Patient clinical characteristics were identified, including Elixhauser comorbidity score13 (which is a composite score calculated based upon the presence or absence of diagnoses codes for 31 different comorbidities), history of radiation therapy, history of chemotherapy, diagnosis of breast cancer, diagnosis of genetic susceptibility to breast cancer only, family history of breast cancer, and history of breast cancer–related lymph node surgery. Furthermore, hospital characteristics, including region (Midwest/South/Northeast/Midwest), hospital teaching status (teaching/nonteaching hospital), and number of beds (000–299/300–499/500 or more), were also assessed.

Statistical Analysis

To compare outcomes between the 2 study groups (obese versus healthy weight), the inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) approach based on propensity scores was performed to control for the covariate imbalance, with the average treatment effect on the treated patients estimated.14 The IPTW method is a propensity score–based technique that allows for balancing patients’ baseline characteristics, which may have been disproportionately distributed between patients with obesity and those with a healthy weight. The propensity score weighting method preserves the sample size both before and after weighting. Consequently, propensity score matching was avoided to prevent the removal of many patients from the analysis and to minimize sample attrition, thereby maintaining generalizability. The propensity score for being obese was first estimated using a logistic regression model, with all study covariates mentioned earlier acting as covariates. Stabilized weights were calculated based on the propensity score of each subject. Patients were trimmed if their probability of being obese was less than 0.1 or greater than 0.9. To evaluate the “goodness of fit” in IPTW modeling (which refers to the effectiveness of the weighted sample in balancing the distribution of observed covariates across treatment groups), standardized mean difference (SMD) values were used to examine the balance of study covariates before and after weighting. An SMD greater than 0.25 or less than −0.25 was considered balanced.

Bivariate analyses were performed to examine differences in baseline characteristics between the 2 groups before and after weighting. Generalized estimating equation (GEE) models with appropriate link functions (logit link for revision and log link for cost outcomes) and distribution functions (gamma distribution for cost outcomes, Gaussian distribution for OR time, and binomial distribution for revision) were conducted to examine differentials in outcomes between the weighted study cohorts. GEE analyses were adjusted for hospital clustering and any unbalanced covariates (based on the SMD threshold) postweighting. To understand the impact of morbid obesity on breast reconstruction outcomes, a subgroup analysis compared the study outcomes between women with a BMI of 35 kg/m2 or greater and healthy-weight women. In all analyses, a 2-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R for Windows, version 4.0.2.

DISCUSSION

Using a nationwide, multihospital dataset, our study highlighted the significant resource use burden associated with obesity among women undergoing immediate DTI-based breast reconstruction postmastectomy. In our study, obese patients experienced 11% higher breast reconstruction–related procedure costs and 16% higher supply costs compared with healthy-weight patients. It has been reported that costs for obese patients undergoing general surgical procedures were significantly higher than nonobese patients in the United States.15 Though there are no prior studies with comparable cost outcome metrics among women who underwent breast reconstruction, a few studies reported related metrics. In one such study by Sun et al,16 the authors examined inpatient data from 2004 to 2010 using the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project. They estimated that an additional $1826 (95% CI: $1405.19–$2064.12) in healthcare costs were associated with mastectomy for obese patients compared with those without obesity.16 In their analysis of a commercial claims dataset, Huo et al11 observed obese class II and III patients (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2) to have 11.4% higher total healthcare costs in the 12 months post breast reconstruction compared with nonobese patients. Our results, together with these prior studies, suggest that healthcare costs are higher for obese women compared with healthy-weight women undergoing breast reconstruction. As seen in our study, the incremental supply cost among obese women could potentially be a contributing factor to higher procedural costs in these patients.

After mastectomy, breast reconstruction among patients with large frames, obesity, and macromastia can be challenging.17 Obese patients have been found to experience higher rates of complications related to mastectomy, such as infections, skin breakdown, and wound complications, compared with nonobese patients.18–20 For implant-based breast reconstruction, obesity has also been established as a critical risk factor for developing surgical complications, including infection, wound dehiscence, seroma, mastectomy flap necrosis, and prosthesis loss in the perioperative period or long-term postoperative period.7–9,21–25 According to a meta-analysis by Panayi et al,7 obese women were 2.64 times more likely (risk ratio: 2.64; 95% CI: 2.25–3.09; P < 0.00001) to experience surgical complications during implant-based breast reconstruction. These complications may lead to a higher risk of reoperation, rehospitalization, or reconstruction failure.7 High BMI is also associated with a greater risk of complications in other breast reconstruction techniques as well, suggesting it may play a role in the increased use of healthcare resources.26–28 Our study findings highlighted that the higher risk of complications could result in additional interventions that contribute to higher procedure costs for obese patients.

Another significant shortcoming pertinent to this patient population is the lack of available implant sizes larger than 800 cm3 to achieve breast contours proportionate to their body habitus.29 Evidence suggests that surgeons may use alternative strategies to achieve symmetry and address volume loss associated with reconstructions following a unilateral mastectomy in women with larger breasts in the absence of adequately sized commercially available implants. These strategies may include off-label approaches such as inserting more than 1 implant per pocket30 or hyperinflation of saline implants.31 These off-label treatment strategies could contribute to the higher supply costs seen in obese women in our study and add to the overall procedure costs. Another strategy that surgeons use is to favor breast reconstruction with autologous tissue over implants to provide sufficient volume to achieve a proportionate breast reconstruction. Although autologous reconstruction can address the volume deficiency of current commercially available breast implants, it does require a significantly more involved index reconstruction and the creation of a donor site wound. When faced with these options, some women with larger breasts choose not to undergo immediate breast reconstruction following mastectomy or opt for segmental mastectomy and radiation therapy with oncoplastic breast reconstruction.

One major cost driver for breast reconstruction is OR time. We identified a significantly longer OR time associated with immediate implant-based breast reconstruction postmastectomy among obese women compared with healthy-weight women. Our results are consistent with the Fischer et al5 study, which reported OR time to be significantly longer in obese patients compared with nonobese patients following immediate implant-based breast reconstruction and showed that OR times continued to increase based on the level of obesity for a given patient. The complexity of breast reconstruction procedures in women with larger breasts could have led to longer OR times among obese women. When examining 12-month revision rates postreconstruction, obese women had higher revision rates than healthy-weight women (21.6% versus 16.4%), although this finding was not statistically different (P = 0.07).

Our study has several limitations. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, there may have been unmeasured confounders, such as implant size, that could have contributed to the differences in outcomes. Similarly, we could not compare the costs of complications for different types of mastectomies (eg, skin-sparing versus nipple-sparing), as these data were not included in PHD and are therefore beyond the scope of this study. Another limitation is the inability to evaluate BMI as a continuous variable in the PHD, as BMI classification is defined by ICD-9/ICD-10 codes that only reflect the range of a patient’s BMI. For instance, the ICD-9-CM code “V85.35” represents “BMI 35.0–35.9,” whereas the ICD-10-CM code “Z68.4” corresponds to “BMI 40.0–44.9.” As a result, a continuum-focused analysis of BMI is not feasible due to the restrictions of the ICD coding system and the PHD database. To mitigate the impact of underreporting, we also used BMI information based on weight and height information from the index admission or the date closest to the index admission. In addition, there was a potential for misclassification of patients whose BMI information was not recorded on the index procedure date, even though we referred to the value closest to the index date. Perioperative complication rates were not reported in this study. Still, they may have impacted the cost and OR time results given previous research, which has reported higher rates of complications in obese women.16 In the PHD, readmissions among patients admitted to a hospital (irrespective of whether that hospital contributes data to the PHD) other than the one where the index admission was made are not reported. Consequently, our revision rates could be underestimated in terms of the actual rate of revisions among the study sample. Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited given the nature of the database, which has the primary purpose of tracking hospital billing data.

CONCLUSIONS

In this examination of a large US multihospital database, significantly higher resource use was observed among obese women undergoing immediate DTI breast reconstruction postmastectomy compared with healthy-weight women. Procedure, supply costs, and OR time were incrementally higher for obese women, reflective of the unique challenges associated with performing reconstruction in women with high BMIs and the unmet need for larger commercially available implant solutions for this patient sample.
