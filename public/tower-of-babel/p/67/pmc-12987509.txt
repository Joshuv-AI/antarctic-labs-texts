Predictors of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis among cirrhotic patients with ascites in Dodoma, Tanzania: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Emmanuel Marcel Sindato, Salum Ali Mwinyi, James Joseph Yahaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSpontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is a fatal complication which occurs among decompensated cirrhotic patients which is also associated with high morbidity and mortality. Despite the complications of decompensated cirrhotic patients are common and fatal, there is limited information regarding prevalence and predictors of SBP in Tanzania.AimThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence and predictors of SBP in cirrhotic patients with ascites.Materials and MethodsA prospective cross-sectional study was undertaken in Dodoma city, Tanzania from November 2022 to June 2023.ResultsThe present study included a total of 269 patients with median age of 46.0 (IQR = 39.0–53.0) years. The prevalence of SBP in this study was (19.0%, n = 51). Child-Pugh score C, international normalized ratio (>1.5 ± 0.3), and severe ascites were the independent predictors (factors) associated with SBP in this study.ConclusionsThe findings of this study indicate that SBP is a health problem of concern among cirrhotic patients. Factors including Child-Pugh C and raised international normalized ratio have shown ability to strongly predict the diagnosis of SBP, and therefore, such factors may be used to stratify cirrhotic patients into high risk and low risk cirrhotic patients for SBP.

Introduction and objectives

Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is a common condition in cirrhotic patients especially, who are in decompensated stage. Studies have shown that 10%–30% [1–3] and 1.5%–3.5% [4] of hospitalized decompensated cirrhotic patients and cirrhotic outpatients end up developing SBP. More importantly, SBP has also been reported to contribute up to 40% of cirrhotic inpatients with ascites [5]. SBP is a spontaneous infection of the ascitic fluid for either patients with liver cirrhosis or those with fulminant hepatic failure associated with ascites [6,7]. This form of infection was described for the first time in 1964 by Harold O Conn [8]. Despite lack of a known source of SBP in decompensated cirrhotic patients, studies have shown that the main cause of SBP is intestinal bacterial translocation, in which bacteria move from the bowels and enter the blood circulation and finally gain access into the ascitic fluid found in the peritoneum [9–11], and gram-negative bacteria form the main causative agents such as Escherichia coli and Klebsiela pneumoniae [12]. Decompensated cirrhotic patients with SBP as a complication normally have poor prognosis due to high risk of developing adverse outcomes including, acute kidney failure, acute-on-chronic failure of the liver, and even death [13,14].

In Tanzania, data regarding prevalence of SBP among patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites are lacking. However, studies done from similar settings, have shown a wide range even over 60% [15] compared to the reported global burden of SBP which is approximately 17% [16]. Delayed diagnosis, existing fragile healthcare systems, and numerous sociodemographic factors stand as the driving factors for the increased risk of cirrhotic patients in low-and-middle income countries (LMICs) to have SBP. Studies have shown that various factors contribute to the development of SBP among patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites. For example, studies have shown that increased Child-Pugh score (CPS), high model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score, increased bilirubin levels, and having viral hepatitis infection increase the risk of cirrhotic patients with ascites do develop SBP [17–19]. Other factors that contribute to SBP among decompensated patients include, gastrointestinal bleeding, electrolyte derangements, alcoholism ascites, hepatic encephalopathy [20], and increased level of international normalized ratio (INR) among many others [21].

Lack of data regarding the burden of SBP in cirrhotic patients in Tanzania as well as documentation of the predictors of such a complication in this cohort is a setback in terms of formulation of mitigation. This increases the possibility of delayed suspecting of possible cases of decompensated cirrhotic patients that may develop SBP. This in turn may contribute to delay of initiation of treatment hence causing poor outcome of the patients. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence and predictors of SBP among cirrhotic patients with ascites.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional analytical study design which was conducted at two public health facilities; Benjamin Mkapa hospital (BMH) and Dodoma regional referral hospital (DRRH) in Dodoma city for a duration of 8 months (November 2022–June 2023). Dodoma city is among the cities which are currently having increased population in the country due to increased urbanization. This has been found to be associated with changing of the lifestyle characteristics including, increased alcohol intake and indulging in sexual activities among many others. All these factors increase the risk of developing liver cirrhosis among individuals in the city.

Patients’ characteristics

We recruited cirrhotic patients in decompensated stage (having ascites) who were aged 18 years or over. Diagnosis of liver cirrhosis was based on abdominal ultrasound and/or abdominal CT scan together with other laboratory investigations. The following were the inclusion criteria: having 18 years or over, being diagnosed with liver cirrhosis, and having ascites. On the other hand, all patients with secondary bacterial peritonitis, being on antibiotics for more than 12 hours prior recruitment in the study, having history of abdominal surgery for the last 4 weeks, and having peritoneal malignancy were excluded from the analysis.

Sample size estimation and sampling procedure

Estimation of the sample size was done using a formula for calculation of prevalence of a single population as it was done in a previous study [22], n = Z2 × p (100 − p)/e2 assuming standard normal variables (z score) of 1.96 at 95% confidence interval, marginal error (e) of 5% and prevalence (p) of 17.7% of SBP among cirrhotic patients which was reported in the study that was done in Cameroon [23]. The obtained sample size was 223, and therefore considering a non-response rate of 20%, the total sample size was 269. Sampling of the patients in the study was done using convenience method, in which all participants who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled simultaneously until the sample size was achieved.

Data collection and research tool

Prior data collection, the purpose of the study was explained clearly to the study subjects including the related procedures. A written informed consent was obtained first from every patient before data collection, and privacy of the patients was observed throughout the study period by anonymizing the identities of the patients. The process of data collection was done from a separate room for the purpose of maintaining privacy of the patients. Data were collected by the researchers using a self-made structured form which was interviewer-administered. The form was prepared based on the available literatures. Validation of the research tool was done after conducting a pilot study among 20 participants within the study sites for obtaining accuracy and relevance of the items which were composing the research tool. The research tool consisted of five parts including, sociodemographic (age of the patient, sex, and residence) and clinical characteristics (abdominal pain, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, rebound tenderness, hepatic encephalopathy, fever, blood pressure, imaging findings, laboratory investigations (white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (Hb) level, international normalized ratio (INR), bilirubin level, albumin level, serum creatinine level, model for end-stage of liver disease score, and Child-Pugh score, and outcome variable (presence or absence of SBP) as measured by leucocyte esterase dipstick and then polymorphonuclear (PMN) cell count by cytology.

Serological tests and manual counting of the PMN cells

Of the collected blood sample collected from every patient was placed in red top vacutainers for serum creatinine, albumin level, total bilirubin and INR, and liver enzymes (alanine transaminase (ALT) and aspartate transaminase (AST)) testing. A clinical chemistry automatic analyzer (Erba machine XL-180, model 160239, Germany) was used for analysis. For serological test of hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV), blood samples were collected from peripheral veins aseptically from each patient’s index finger. HCV infection was tested using iCARE One Step Anti-HCV Rapid Test (Jal Medical, Singapore) kit, and the results were recorded after 15 min. A purplish test band in the region was interpreted as positive whereas absence of a purplish test band in the test region was read as negative result. Presence of only a purplish control band in the control region regardless of the result was considered invalid and the test was repeated. Detection of HBV infection was done qualitatively using iCARE one step HBsAg Rapid Test (Jal Medical, Singapore) which is an immunochromatography. A blood sample from the pricked index finger was collected using plastic micropipettes and placed in a well. Then two drops of a diluent was added into the same sample well and results were recorded after every 15 min. Interpretation of the results was done based on the manufacturer’s instructions.

A total of 10 mL of the ascitic fluid which was collected by means of abdominal paracentesis was inoculated into heparin containing tubes, then the tubes were sent for cytological evaluation for quantification of PMN cells through centrifugation within four hours of collection. The fluid was stirred gently so as to spread the suspended cells followed by centrifugation for four minutes. Each sample was placed on four glass slides by dropping at least two drops of the suspension. The processed cytological slides were quickly fixed in 95% ethanol for 10 min. Staining of the glass slides was done using Pap stain. Counting of the PMN cells was done by using chamber counting method, and ≥250 cells/mm3 was used as a diagnostic cutoff for establishing positive and negative results for SBP [24]. Therefore, patients with SBP were treated empirically using antibiotics including ceftriaxone, merenopenem, and cefotaxime based on the LERS positive results as well as clinical manifestations indicative of SBP as it was agreed previously by the International Ascites Club in 2000 [25].

Statistical analysis

Data were first entered in the excel sheet and then exported into SPSS programme version 27.0 for analysis. Before analysis, data were first cleaned by running frequencies crosstabs, and percentages for identifying any missing, duplications, and wrongly entered data. All continuous variables were summarized in mean ± standard deviation (SD) after testing for assumption of normality of the data using Shapiro–Wilk W test. Bivariate analysis for categorical variables was done using Chi-square test whereas for continuous variables t-test was used including Cohen’s d value. Then backward stepwise selection was applied in assessing the predictors of SBP under multivariable analysis of the binary logistic regression. Only variables with p < 0.05 under bivariate analysis were moved to the multivariable model. Nagelkerke R2 value was also assessed for measuring the goodness of fit of the model. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Bivariate analysis of the association of sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the patients with SBP

The median age of the patients was 46.0 (IQR = 39.0–53.0) years. Majority of the patients (61.3%, n = 165) were males. Our study has found that having jaundice was significantly associated with SBP (95% CI = 1.10–4.52, p = 0.023). Patients who were presenting clinically with rebounding tenderness were also statistically significantly associated with SBP (95% CI = 0.11–0.20, p < 0.001). Also, patients with Child-Pugh score C were significantly associated with SBP (95% CI = 3.86–20.33, p < 0.001). Additionally, there was increased trend of SBP detection in cases with severe ascites compared to cases with moderate ascites, however, the difference between the two compared groups of the variable did not reach statistical significance (95% CI = 0.92–3.71, p = 0.082). Other variables were not statistically significant (Table 1).

Bivariate analysis of the association of laboratory investigations of the patients with SBP

The mean level of white cell count (WBC) among patients with SBP was significantly higher than the mean level of WBC of patients without SBP (95% CI = −5.78 to (−1.59), p < 0.001), however the effect size was low (Cohen’s d = -0.6). The mean level of bilirubin among patients with SBP was significantly higher than that of patients without SBP (95% CI = −155.92 to (−46.56), p < 0.001) though the effect size was small (Cohen’s test = −0.6). The mean level of the MELD score for patients with SBP was statistically significant compared to their counterparts (95% CI = −6.86 to (−2.82), p < 0.001), however, the effect size was also small (Cohen’s d = −0.9). Other variables were not statistically significant (Table 2).

Multivariable analysis for the predictors of SBP using backward stepwise manual selection method

After adjusting for WBC, jaundice, rebounding tenderness, MELD score, NIR, bilirubin, Child-Pugh score, and ascites during multivariable regression analysis using manual backward stepwise selection method (elimination), INR, CPS and ascites remained the predictors of SBP. Patients who had raised mean level of INR (1.5 ± 0.3) were almost 6 times more likely to be diagnosed with SBP than patients that had low mean level of INR (1.3 ± 0.3) (AOR = 5.8, 95% CI = 1.415–23.417, p = 0.014). Also, patients with Child-Pugh class C were more than 10 times likely to have SBP compared to their counterparts (AOR = 10.2, 95% CI = 3.927–26.310, p < 0.001). The risk of developing SBP in the group of patients with severe ascites was 4 times more likely than of the patients in the group of patients with moderate ascites (AOR = 4.1, 95% CI =3.501–12.883, p = 0.032) (Table 3).

Discussion

SBP remains one of the most common fatal complication among patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites. Various factors determine the risk of cirrhotic patients to contract SBP. In countries like Tanzania where diagnostic services are still a challenge especially, in remote areas, identifying risk factors of developing SBP in this cohort may help in one way or another to stratify patients and be able to improve their prognosis, because by considering the risk of developing SBP they can be treated empirically. The prevalence of SBP among cirrhotic patients who are decompensated stage varies considerably across different settings depending on the availability and distribution of different etiological factors. In this study, the etiological factors of liver cirrhosis in patients with SBP consisted of non-viral factors including alcoholism similar to the studies of Popoiag et al. [26], Gonçalves et al. [27], and Gyunda et al. [28] which were done in Romania, Brazil, and Tanzania, respectively. However, other studies from Africa and other developing countries have documented that over 30% of liver cirrhosis cases are caused by viral hepatitis especially, hepatitis B virus (HBV) [29,30]. The inconsistency of the etiological factors across various settings globally may be partly due to spatial distribution of such risk factors and preventive practices among individuals as well as variation of the levels of awareness, knowledge and attitude, all together help to explain the variation of the risk factors of liver cirrhosis.

The prevalence of SBP in this study of almost 20% was higher than 11.7% and 11.3% levels of SBP among cirrhotic patients with ascites which were reported in France [31] and UK [32], however, close to 18% and 17.1% that was reported in Egypt [33] and India [34], respectively. However, increased burden of SBP of 33.3%, 25.2%, 62%, and 43.4% were reported in various settings [15,18,21,26]. In another study of Elsadek et al. which was done in Egypt reported significantly high positive result of SBP of 34.5% in patients with liver cirrhosis and ascites [19]. Variation in accuracy of different LERS such as Siemens Multistix, Roche Chemtrip, Combur 10TestM, Combina 13 among many others also contribute to the discrepancy of the reported prevalence of SBP. There is a difference in the ability to detect presence of bacteria for these reagent strips, which contributes to the discrepancy of detection of SBP. Although there is a known cutoff level of >250 cells/mm3 based on the available guidelines [35], still there are challenges including lack of experience of performing manual cell count among reporters which contributes to subjectivity of the positive results. This is because manual cell count approach has been found to be associated with operator dependency, transport-induced cell lysis, and the potential for delays [36,37]. Interobserver difference in reporting the prepared cytological smears from the ascitic fluid under microscopy also contributes to variation of results when counting manual counting of the cells. Additionally, detection by culture as opposed to using dipsticks which are more likely to give high false positive results [38,39], and this may bring about differences of the prevalence of SBP across compared studies.

There is a variation of the predictors of SBP among patients with liver cirrhosis with evidence of decompansation. This poses a challenge in using risk factors of SBP in this population of patients rather than testing. In this study, there was increased risk of being detected with SBP for cases that had a high mean value of INR compared to cases that had low mean value of INR. This observation has also been reported in the studies of Popoiag et al, Duah et al., and Juhi et al. which were done in Romania, Ghana, and India, respectively [21,26,34]. In another study which was done in Ajmer, India it was found that INR was a predictor of SBP in asymptomatic cirrhotic patients [40]. Increased mean levels of INR in cirrhotic patients with ascites has been linked to hepatic synthetic failure (HSF) and not true hypercoagulable state [41,42]. Therefore, HSF contributes to SBP in cirrhotic patients with ascites based on a number of possible mechanisms including impaired host defense and favoring of bacterial translocation as a result of portal hypertension [43,44].

Another significant and potential predictor of SBP in our cohort was CPS. Other studies have also reported a significant association of class C of CPS with increased risk of developing SBP among cirrhotic patients with ascites [45–47]. CPS is one of the measures of the integrity of the function of the liver, and high CPS indicates impaired integrity of hepatic function [5,48]. This contributes to compromised body immunity hence leading to bacterial translocation from the bowels into the peritoneum and this maps the clinical picture of SBP. Considering the evidence communicated from previous studies as well as findings, it is important to understand, CPS may be a valuable clinical test in taking preventive measures including prophylaxis in the process of combating SBP which is one of the ominous complications among cirrhotic patients with ascites.

Other clinical factors like MELD score which is a well-known significant and important predictor of end-stage of damage of the liver as it has been reported in several studies [34,49,50]. Also, in the studies of Kraja et al and that of Desai et al it was found MELD score (>21) was associated with increased risk of getting SBP among cirrhotic patients [51,52]. Lack of association between increased MELD score and SBP among cirrhotic patients which was observed in the present study was also reported in the study of Haddad et al. [53]. Also, in the study of Haddad et al it was argued that, clinical status of the refractory ascites significantly determine the chance of acquisition of SBP and not MELD score [53]. This implies that the association between SBP and MELD score among decompensated cirrhotic patients depends on recurrence of ascites and not a direct one. Additionally, Thiele et al and Wehmeyer et al. also found no association between high MELD score and SBP from studies which were done in Brazil and Germany, respectively [54,55]. Furthermore, we found no association between ascites and possibility of developing SBP among study subjects in the cohort. This contradicts to the findings of the number of studies reported in the literature which have shown a positive association between the two variables [21,52,56].

Additionally, in this study, we also observed that patients with severe ascites had increased odds of developing SBP which is similar to the report in the studies of Duah et al. [21], Trad et al. [57]. Severe ascites contributes to development of SBP among cirrhotic patients with ascites because it acts as source of potential nutrients for bacterial growth, increases intestinal permeability, and also supports bacterial translocation [58,59]. Furthermore, it has been observed that low ascetic fluid total protein level (<1.5 gm/dL) and involvement of ascitic fluid C3 contribute to the development of SBP among decompensated cirrhotic patients [60].

Study limitations

The study limitations for this study included failure to including culture test for the patients due to lack of funds. Selection bias could also have influenced the findings due to lack of probability sampling of the study participants. Generalizability of the findings of this study were also limited due to small sample size and lack of representativeness of the sample.

Conclusion

Our study has shown that SBP is a significant health issue among patients with decompensated liver cirrhosis in Tanzania. Therefore, there is a need of ensuring thorough monitoring and testing so as to prevent mortality. Factors like increased mean level of INR and class C of CPS have provided strong evidence that they can be used in identifying decompensated cirrhotic patients with high risk of getting SBP. This may help early initiation of empirical treatment and hence improve clinical outcomes of the patients.
