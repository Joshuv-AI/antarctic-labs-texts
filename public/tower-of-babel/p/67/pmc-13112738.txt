Association between metabolic dysregulation and frailty in four prospective cohorts
Authors: Weiwei Sun, Xiao ling Lang, Ke Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe relationship between metabolic dysregulation and frailty remains incompletely understood. This study examines the association between metabolic dysregulation and frailty in middle-aged and older adults.MethodsData from four cohorts (CHARLS, HRS, SHARE, MHAS) were analysed. Frailty was assessed using a 30-item deficit accumulation frailty index, treated as a continuous variable ranging from 0–100. Metabolic dysregulation was defined based on the presence of central obesity, high blood pressure, and/or hyperglycaemia, and was analysed both as a categorical count variable (0, 1, 2, or 3 dysregulations) and as specific combinations of individual metabolic components. Multivariable linear regression models were used to examine associations, adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors.ResultsAmong 416,693 participants, 63.7% had at least one metabolic dysregulation. A dose–response pattern was observed: β-values for frailty were 4.31 (95% CI: 4.22–4.40) for one dysregulation, 8.36 (95% CI: 8.24–8.49) for two, and 12.92 (95% CI: 12.79–13.16) for three. Hyperglycemia had the strongest effect, especially when combined with high blood pressure, and remained significant after adjustment.ConclusionMetabolic dysregulation synergistically accelerates frailty progression, underscoring the need for early intervention in metabolic disorders to prevent frailty.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01290-1.

Introduction

The global trend of population ageing has created significant challenges to public health systems all over the world, the increasing proportion of older adults exacerbating this burden to a chronic level [1, 2]. Metabolic dysregulation has become increasingly common in ageing populations, reflecting a constellation of interconnected metabolic abnormalities including central obesity, high blood pressure (HBP), hyperglycaemia, and dyslipidaemia [3–5]. The high prevalence of metabolic dysregulations among older adults serves to elevate the risk of cardiovascular diseases and diabetes, and are strongly linked to accelerated functional decline, reduced quality of life, and increased healthcare expenditure in later life [6, 7].

Frailty is a geriatric syndrome characterised by a decline in physiological reserve and heightened vulnerability to stressors and adverse outcomes [8, 9]. The core features of frailty include sarcopenia (loss of muscle mass and strength), reduced physical function and heightened susceptibility to stressors including illness and injury [10–12]. Epidemiological studies have indicated that approximately 10–15% of older adults globally are affected by frailty, with a higher prevalence in those with comorbid conditions such as metabolic dysregulations [13, 14].

The pathophysiological mechanisms that link metabolic dysregulations to frailty involve a complex interplay of chronic inflammation, oxidative stress and endocrine dysregulation [15, 16]. Chronic low-grade inflammation, which is driven by adipokine imbalance and pro-inflammatory cytokines, is a contributing factor to muscle atrophy and functional decline [17]. Oxidative stress, which is exacerbated by hyperglycaemia and dyslipidaemia, serves to further damage cellular structures and impair mitochondrial function [18]. In addition, insulin resistance and hormonal imbalances disrupt anabolic processes, which accelerates sarcopenia and reduces physiological resilience [19, 20]. These interlocking mechanisms generate a self-perpetuating pathophysiological cycle that may potentiate frailty onset and progression [21].

Although numerous studies have investigated the relationship between metabolic syndrome and frailty, the findings remain inconsistent. For instance, a study from Ireland found that metabolic syndrome increased the risk of frailty by 57% (based on the Fried frailty phenotype) [22], whereas a study from Japan indicated that both obesity and metabolic syndrome independently elevated frailty risk [23]. However, in individuals with diabetes, a 5%-10% weight loss was associated with a reduced frailty risk. Additionally, some studies have identified hyperglycemia as the most significant factor influencing frailty, while others have emphasized the dominant role of high blood pressure or central obesity [24–26]. These discrepancies may stem from variations in research methodologies, population characteristics, and follow-up durations.Therefore, evidence synthesised across multiple countries and population structures is needed.

To address this research gap, this study integrates four well-established international ageing cohorts—CHARLS, HRS, SHARE, and MHAS—to evaluate the association between metabolic dysregulation and frailty, and to determine whether specific combinations of metabolic abnormalities confer greater frailty risk.

Methods

Study design and population

This integrated multicohort analysis uses data from four longitudinal ageing cohorts: the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the Survey of Health, Ageing, and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), and the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS).The geographic regions covered by the different cohorts included in this study are summarised in Supplementary Table 1.

The selection of the specific time frames from each dataset was driven by several key considerations. First, these time periods correspond to the most recent and complete survey waves available for each cohort, ensuring the inclusion of the most up-to-date and comprehensive data. Second, we aimed to select overlapping time frames across the different cohorts, which helps to enhance data comparability and mitigate the impact of large temporal differences. This overlap allows for a more robust integrated analysis across cohorts, ensuring consistency in the temporal coverage of the datasets. Additionally, the data for these time frames are publicly accessible and have undergone rigorous quality control, which aligns with the study’s standards for data harmonization. The time frames for each dataset are as follows: HRS covers waves 10 to 15 (2010–2020), CHARLS includes waves 1 to 3 (2011–2015), MHAS spans waves 4 to 5 (2015–2018), and SHARE encompasses waves 4 to 8 (2011–2020) (Supplementary Table 2). Participants aged 50 or above were included, with exclusions applied to those with missing data on metabolic dysregulations or frailty index. The flowchart for the investigation population screening procedure in HRS, CHARLS, MHAS and SHARE can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 1–4.

Exposure assessment

In the context of metabolic dysregulations, the study has a specific focus on central obesity, high blood pressure and hyperglycaemia as these conditions all have the potential to exacerbate frailty.

Central obesity

According to the Harmonized Definition of metabolic dysregulations, the diagnostic criteria for central obesity vary in different regions. In China, it is defined as a waist circumference (WC) of ≥ 85 cm for men or ≥ 80 cm for women [27], while the thresholds are ≥ 90 cm for men or ≥ 80 cm for women in Mexico [28]. In contrast, there are more stringent diagnostic criteria for the United States and Europe, with waist circumference thresholds of ≥ 102 cm for men or ≥ 88 cm for women [28, 29].

In this study, waist circumference was considered the preferred measure for assessing central obesity due to its more direct reflection of visceral adiposity. However, due to the absence of waist circumference data in certain waves of some databases, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 was used as a surrogate indicator for central obesity in these instances. This approach was informed by previous studies [30, 31], which have demonstrated that BMI is a robust and reliable marker for identifying populations with excess body fat, particularly in epidemiological research. While waist circumference remains the gold standard for assessing central obesity, BMI has been widely accepted as a suitable alternative, especially when waist circumference data is unavailable. Its use allows for consistency across different datasets and ensures methodological uniformity in multi-cohort analyses.

High blood pressure

High blood pressure was assessed by self-reported diagnoses and clinical blood pressure measurements. In accordance with earlier studies [32, 33], participants were classified as having high blood pressure if they gave the response of “yes” to the question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with hypertension by a doctor?” In addition, individuals who gave responses related to the treatment of hypertension and its complications, including “Traditional Chinese medicine/Western medicine/Physical therapy/Acupuncture/Occupational therapy/None of the above”, were also categorised as having high blood pressure.

Participants with a baseline average systolic blood pressure (SBP) of ≥ 130 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of ≥ 85 mmHg were classified as hypertensive based on established diagnostic criteria [34].

Hyperglycaemia

The assessment of hyperglycaemia was based on self-reported diagnoses and clinical measurements. Participants with a baseline fasting blood glucose (FBS) level of ≥ 110 mg/dL or haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 5.7% were classified as having hyperglycaemia according to established diagnostic criteria [35]. In addition, those who reported having received treatment for diabetes and its related complications were also categorized as having hyperglycaemia. Baseline tables classified by central obesity, high blood pressure and hyperglycaemia are provided in Supplementary Table 5.

Outcome assessment

The frailty index, a well-established metric that quantifies the cumulative burden of age-related health deficits, was used to assess frailty [36]. In accordance with previous studies, items were selected that could be consistently applied across all four cohorts [37, 38]. After data screening, 30 items from the CHARLS, HRS and SHARE surveys and 28 from the MHAS survey were used to construct the frailty index. In MHAS, two items were unavailable and therefore excluded: self-reported physician-diagnosed emotional/nervous/psychiatric problems, and self-reported physician-diagnosed memory-related conditions (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, dementia or other cognitive impairment)(Supplementary Table 3). Most of the items were dichotomised based on established cut-off values, with a score of 0 indicating the absence of a deficit and a score of 1 indicating its presence. Self-reported measures of general vision, hearing, health status and cognition were scored on a scale from 0 to 1, where higher scores reflected more severe deficits. The frailty index was calculated by summing the deficits for each individual, dividing the total by 30 (for CHARLS, HRS and SHARE) or 28 (for MHAS) and then multiplying by 100. The frailty index is theoretically a continuous variable that ranges from 0 to 100. Any participants with missing data for any of the 30 items (CHARLS, HRS and SHARE) or 28 items (MHAS) were excluded from the frailty index calculation.

Data collection

To ensure the robustness and interpretability of the regression model, the selection of covariates was grounded in both the theoretical foundations and empirical findings from existing literature. We incorporated key demographic variables (such as age, sex, education level, and marital status), lifestyle factors (including smoking, alcohol consumption, and physical activity), and clinical variables (such as cancer, heart disease, and lung disease). These factors have consistently been identified in previous studies on metabolic dysregulations and frailty as potential significant confounders.

The following information was gathered for this study: (i) Demographic information consisting of marital status, sex, age and educational attainment. Three groups were created based on educational attainment: lower secondary education or below, upper secondary, and higher than upper secondary. Married and other marital statuses (including separated, single, widowed and divorced) were the two categories of marital status. (ii) Lifestyle information was gathered relating to drinking and smoking patterns. Physical activity was defined as the engagement by participants in moderate or vigorous exercise at least once per week. (iii) Medical history information was gathered pertaining to the presence of heart disease, lung disease and cancer. Further details are provided in Supplementary Table 4. In the data preprocessing of this study, we employed a complete case analysis method, including only samples with complete data for analysis. Variables or samples with a high proportion of missing values were subject to rigorous screening and exclusion to ensure data integrity.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables with a normal distribution, the data was presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and group differences were assessed using ANOVA. For continuous variables that were non-normally distributed, data were summarised as median (Q1, Q3).Categorical variables were expressed as numerical values (percentages) and intergroup differences were analysed through the use of Pearson’s chi-square test.

A series of linear regression models were employed for investigating the association between metabolic dysregulations and the frailty index. Several models were constructed, each of which incorporated a distinct set of covariates to elucidate the nuanced influence of the variables on the observed relationship. Model 1 was unadjusted and served as the baseline, Model 2 introduced adjustments for age and gender and Model 3 further expanded the covariates to include age, gender, marital status, educational attainment, cancer, heart disease, lung disease, physical activity and current drinking and smoking levels. The impact metabolic dysregulations have on frailty was evaluated through an examination of the cumulative number of metabolic dysregulations (ranging from 0 to 3) and specific combinations of these dysregulations. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess whether the association between metabolic dysregulations and the frailty index varies in strength across different populations. Participants were divided into different subgroups based on their gender, age (less than 65 years vs. more than 65 years), drinking and smoking habits. Linear regression analyses were performed using the “stats” package (lm function) in R (version 4.3.1), and the “regress” module was used for modelling in MSTATA. A two-sided P-value of below 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

We observed significant differences in baseline characteristics among participants with no metabolic dysregulations, a single metabolic dysregulation, and multiple metabolic dysregulations. Notably, there was a clear upward trend in mean age corresponding to the number of metabolic conditions, rising from 64.19 years in the group without any metabolic dysregulations to 68.35 years in the group with multiple metabolic dysregulations. This pattern suggests that advancing age constitutes a critical risk factor for the accumulation of metabolic conditions.

Regarding sex distribution, the proportion of male participants decreased slightly with increasing metabolic burden—from 44.7% in the metabolically healthy group to 42.7% in the group with multiple metabolic dysregulations. Furthermore, educational attainment appeared to be inversely associated with metabolic multimorbidity; participants with higher levels of education were less frequently represented in the group with multiple metabolic dysregulations. The distribution of the frailty index across the various databases can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 5.

In the analysis, participants without metabolic dysregulation were used as the reference group. The results revealed that participants with a single metabolic condition exhibited a modest but statistically significant increase in the frailty index, with an unadjusted β value of 7.64 (95% CI: 7.53 to 7.76). Moreover, as the number of metabolic conditions increased, the elevation in the frailty index became more pronounced. Specifically, participants with two metabolic dysregulations demonstrated a significant increase in the frailty index (unadjusted β value of 13.41 [95% CI: 13.26 to 13.55]), while those with three metabolic dysregulations showed an even more significant rise (unadjusted β value of 19.21 [95% CI: 19.07 to 19.41]). In addition to the comprehensive analysis, the association between the prevalence of metabolic dysregulations and frailty was further investigated across the four datasets. A clear dose–response relationship was observed in the HRS between the number of metabolic dysregulations and the frailty index, with β values demonstrating a progressive increase as the number of comorbidities escalated. More specifically, the β values were as follows: single metabolic dysregulation (β = 4.73 [95% CI: 4.55 to 4.90]), two metabolic dysregulations (β = 9.09 [95% CI: 8.89 to 9.28]) and three metabolic dysregulations (β = 13.85 [95% CI: 13.60 to 14.11]). A consistent parallel trend was observed in the CHARLS, MHAS and SHARE datasets, as Fig. 3 shows. In the subgroup analysis, we employed interaction tests (P for interaction) to assess the differences in the strength of the association between metabolic dysregulations and the frailty index across different subgroups. The results showed that the interactions between gender (P for interaction < 0.01) and current smoking (P for interaction < 0.01) were statistically significant, while the interactions between age (P for interaction = 0.152) and current drinking (P for interaction = 0.346) were not significant. Specifically, in the female subgroup, the association between metabolic dysregulations and the frailty index was stronger (β = 5.90, 95% CI: 5.74–6.09, P < 0.01), whereas in the male subgroup, the association was weaker (β = 5.11, 95% CI: 4.92–5.27, P < 0.01). Similarly, in the current smoking subgroup, the association was more pronounced (β = 5.53, 95% CI: 5.34–5.78, P < 0.01), while in the no current smoking subgroup, the association was weaker (β = 4.74, 95% CI: 4.52–4.99, P < 0.01). These results suggest that gender and current smoking may be important moderating factors in the relationship between metabolic dysregulations and the frailty index, while age and drinking do not significantly impact this relationship (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Discussion

This study employed multivariable linear regression analysis to assess the association between metabolic dysregulations and frailty, with results expressed as β values and 95% confidence intervals. The β value offers a clear and quantitative measure of the impact of independent variables on the dependent variable and is widely utilized in multi-cohort studies to evaluate the strength of associations. Therefore, we argue that the β value effectively captures the cumulative effects of metabolic dysregulations and their relative influence, providing a robust and precise representation of their association with frailty.This study found there to be a significant positive association between metabolic dysregulations – specifically central obesity, hypertension and hyperglycaemia – and the frailty index, with a dose–response relationship being observed as the number of metabolic dysregulations increased. This suggests that the accumulation of metabolic abnormalities serves to exacerbate frailty in a progressive and additive manner, which highlights the synergistic impact of multiple comorbidities on health deterioration in ageing populations.

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research, which has found there to be a clear link between metabolic dysregulations and its individual components, including hyperglycaemia, hypertension and central obesity, and an increased risk of frailty. For example, a study by López-Otín et al. highlighted that metabolic dysregulations, particularly hyperglycaemia, are a contributing factor to chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, which are key drivers of frailty among older adults [39]. In addition, Fried et al. demonstrated the association hypertension and central obesity have with reduced physical function and increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, which further supports the role of metabolic dysregulations in frailty development [40]. Blaum et al. also found older women with multiple metabolic abnormalities to exhibit a higher prevalence of frailty, which is in alignment with the dose–response relationship that was observed in this study [41]. In addition to confirming these established associations, this study extends the current literature by systematically analysing the cumulative and combinatorial effects of metabolic dysregulations.

Hyperglycaemia is a key component of metabolic dysregulations and has a profound impact on the development of frailty through multiple interconnected mechanisms. Chronic hyperglycaemia induces a state of systemic inflammation through the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines including interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-alpha, which promote muscle catabolism and impair muscle regeneration. This can lead to sarcopenia [42, 43]. Hyperglycaemia also exacerbates oxidative stress by increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which damages cellular structures, including mitochondria, and impairs muscle function [18, 44]. This oxidative damage serves to further accelerate the decline in muscle mass and strength, which contributes to physical frailty. Furthermore, hyperglycaemia disrupts insulin signalling pathways, which leads to insulin resistance and impaired glucose uptake in skeletal muscle, which compromises energy metabolism and muscle performance [45]. These mechanisms make a collective contribution to the accelerated progression of frailty in those with hyperglycaemia.

Central obesity and HBP also play significant roles in the development of frailty through distinct mechanisms. Central obesity, characterized by excess visceral fat, contributes to inflammation by releasing pro-inflammatory adipokines, such as leptin and resistin, which impair muscle function and regeneration [16]. This chronic inflammation accelerates muscle wasting and sarcopenia, a key feature of frailty [46]. Additionally, obesity-induced insulin resistance disrupts metabolic processes, leading to decreased energy availability for muscle maintenance.

HBP exerts its impact on frailty through increased arterial stiffness and reduced blood flow to tissues, including skeletal muscles. The resulting hypoperfusion limits nutrient and oxygen delivery to muscle cells, impairing muscle function and contributing to weakness [47, 48]. Furthermore, HBP induces oxidative stress, causing cellular damage, including to muscle tissue, further contributing to muscle degeneration [49]. Over time, these effects result in a decline in muscle mass and strength, key components of frailty in individuals with HBP [50].

The findings of this study show that the coexistence of hyperglycaemia and HBP has the most pronounced impact on the frailty index, which suggests that there is a synergistic effect between these two metabolic dysregulations. This synergistic effect arises primarily from the combined impact they have on cardiovascular and metabolic burden. Hyperglycaemia induces endothelial dysfunction and promotes chronic inflammation and HBP exacerbates vascular stiffness and increases cardiac workload, which leads to microvascular and macrovascular complications [35, 51]. The combination of these conditions amplifies oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, which further impairs muscle function and accelerates sarcopenia [52, 53]. In addition, the dual burden of hyperglycaemia and HBP serves to disrupt insulin signalling and adipokine balance, which exacerbates metabolic dysregulation and reduces physiological resilience [54].

The consistency of findings across HRS (United States), CHARLS (China), MHAS (Mexico), and SHARE (Europe) underscores the cross-cultural validity of this association. Despite regional variation in lifestyle, healthcare access, and socioeconomic environment, the dose–response pattern remained stable, suggesting shared biological pathways linking metabolic dysregulation to frailty across diverse ageing populations.

There are several limitations to this study that are worthy of consideration. Firstly, although waist circumference was utilised for the diagnosis of central obesity, the lack of this data in certain waves of the database (where BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 served as a surrogate measure) may have resulted in an underestimation of its true impact, as waist circumference is a more accurate reflection of visceral adiposity. Although our study employed complete case analysis to ensure the robustness of the data, we recognize that methods such as multiple imputation could further enhance data utilization and reduce potential selection bias. Secondly, although this study have adjusted for multiple confounding factors, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables, such as genetic factors and environmental exposures, may still be present. Furthermore, while databases such as CHARLS, HRS, SHARE, and MHAS include weighted variables to improve representativeness, our study did not adjust for these weights as the primary focus was on exploring individual-level associations rather than making population-level inferences. However, we recognize that this may affect the representativeness of the different databases at the population level. Therefore, future research could benefit from further investigation into the application of survey weights to enhance the external validity of the findings. Thirdly,this study utilized a cross-sectional design, which inherently limits the ability to establish causal relationships between metabolic dysregulations and frailty. Therefore, although our analysis reveals a significant association between these variables, caution is warranted in drawing causal inferences. Future longitudinal studies will be essential to further elucidate the role of metabolic dysregulations in the progression of frailty and to delineate its underlying biological mechanisms. In addition, despite the consistency of findings across diverse cohorts, regional variations in healthcare infrastructure, cultural practices and genetic backgrounds may serve to modulate the relationship between metabolic dysregulation and frailty. Healthcare access, dietary habit and physical activity level disparities across populations could introduce heterogeneity in the observed associations. Furthermore, hypertension was treated as a binary variable in this study, and information on hypertension control based on guideline-defined targets (e.g., AHA/ESH) was not consistently available across cohorts, which may have underestimated the impact of poorly controlled hypertension relative to hyperglycaemia.Finally, the frailty instrument used in this study was based on the Rockwood Frailty Index, which reflects deficit accumulation and includes comorbidities, raising the possibility of partial conceptual overlap with metabolic components. Future studies should incorporate physical frailty scales such as the Fried phenotype, which may provide a more direct measure of physiological performance when investigating the metabolic origins of frailty.

Conclusion

In conclusion, metabolic dysregulations are significantly associated with an increased risk of frailty, with hyperglycaemia and HBP exhibiting the strongest synergistic associations.
