Timing and Frequency of Surveillance Colonoscopies After Resection for Colorectal Cancer in Queensland, Australia: A Retrospective 10‐Year Analysis
Authors: Yiu Ming Ho, Katharina M D Merollini, Louisa G Collins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo compare the timing intervals of surveillance colonoscopies after resection for colorectal cancer with recommendations in the Australian Clinical Practice Guidelines for Surveillance Colonoscopy.Study DesignA retrospective, observational study.Data SourcesAdults who had a resection for colorectal cancer from the Costs of Surviving Cancer—Queensland Study (COS‐Q), which is a retrospective population‐based cohort study of all individuals diagnosed with a primary cancer from 2005 to 2015.ResultsIn total, 7265 patients that underwent resection of colorectal cancer were included for analysis. Most patients had a pre‐operative colonoscopy (6636, 87.6%) while 899 patients (12.4%) had no pre‐operative colonoscopy. 82.1% of patients had their first surveillance colonoscopy as recommended by the Guidelines. In contrast, after the first post‐operative surveillance colonoscopy without polypectomy, 978 of 1015 (96.4%) had a second surveillance colonoscopy early by at least 6 months. Thirty seven (1.7%) had the surveillance colonoscopy on time or later than recommended. Being female or having a pre‐operative colonoscopy was associated with an early second surveillance colonoscopy (odds ratio 2.2, p < 0.05). Early surveillance colonoscopies incurred costs up to AU$3.78 million.ConclusionsMost post‐resection surveillance colonoscopies for patients with colorectal cancer are performed earlier than recommended. Since colonoscopies in patients at low risk of cancer recurrence were generally performed too early, there is evidence of the potential overuse of healthcare resources and the opportunity to improve hospital efficiency.Keywords: adenomatous polyps, clinical guidelines, colonoscopy surveillance, colorectal cancer, surveillance frequency

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) has the fourth highest incidence of cancer worldwide, affecting 32 per 100 000 people in 2020 [1]. By 2070, CRC is projected to increase steeply and be the most common cancer globally with 4.7 million expected cases [2]. CRC poses a high burden to healthcare systems. For example, CRC was the second most burdensome cancer in 2015, following breast cancer, and was associated with 96 936 disability‐adjusted life years in Australia [3]. Up to 98% of patients can be successfully treated if CRC is detected early [4]. However, with the increasing incidence of CRC and the expansion of bowel screening programs to younger people, the demand for colonoscopy services continues to grow [5], placing further pressures on healthcare systems.

Follow‐up colonoscopy after CRC resection is an essential component of CRC surveillance. It plays a critical role in detecting metachronous and synchronous cancers [6], particularly in cases where a pre‐operative colonoscopy is not conducted. Additionally, it aids in early identification of mucosal recurrence at the anastomosis [7]. The current Australian surveillance guidelines on “Follow‐up colonoscopy after colorectal cancer resection” have remained unchanged since 2011 [8]. Its recommendations were extracted from “Clinical Practice Guidelines for Surveillance Colonoscopy” [8], which were based on systematic reviews in 2010.

The Guidelines recommend that a post‐resection colonoscopy (C1) should be performed within 1 year after the resection of a sporadic cancer when a pre‐resection colonoscopy (Cpre) was performed. Most patients have a Cpre, but not always. If no Cpre was performed, such as in the event of emergency resection, the post‐resection colonoscopy should be performed within 6 months after the resection. The timing of a subsequent colonoscopy will be based on the findings of C1. In the absence of polyps at C1, which constitutes a low risk of recurrence, the next surveillance colonoscopy is recommended to occur at 5 years (see Schematic illustration of the Australian Guidelines' recommendation on the use of colonoscopy in the management of CRC resections in Appendix 1). If polyps are found at C1, the subsequent colonoscopy (C2+) will depend on the number and the presence of adverse features on the polyp(s) removed.

Internationally, evidence suggests that the first surveillance colonoscopy interval recommendations are not well adhered to [9]. In the US, the first surveillance colonoscopy after a colorectal resection is due at 12 months, but 28% did not have a surveillance colonoscopy [10]. In a recent meta‐analysis [9], the overall compliance rate of polyp surveillance was as low as 40%, with many surveillance colonoscopies performed earlier than recommended intervals. There is evidence that CRC‐related mortality rates do not reduce with increasing frequency of colonoscopies [11]. Therefore, the additional colonoscopies are largely unnecessary.

Evidence for the timing of surveillance colonoscopies in Australia is scarce, yet healthcare costs are high and procedural waiting lists are growing [12]. In the context of limited healthcare resources, understanding how often surveillance colonoscopies are performed is important. Therefore, the aim of this study was to quantify the surveillance intervals after colectomy for CRC and compare these to the recommended intervals in the Guidelines and explore factors that may be associated with colonoscopies performed unnecessarily early. We also estimated the financial burden associated with the more frequent colonoscopy with the view that these costs could be postponed to future budget periods.

Methods

This study follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement for reporting observational studies [13]. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of the Sunshine Coast (Ethics Approval A/17/941) and from the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) Human Research Ethics Committee (EO2017/3/348). Approval for Queensland data extraction and linkage, including a waiver of consent, has been sought from Queensland Health under the Public Health Act from 2005 and was granted by the Director‐General (grant RD007281) due to de‐identified, routinely collected administrative data being used for this project.

Study Design

This is a retrospective, observational study.

Setting

We used data from the Costs of Surviving Cancer—Queensland Study (COS‐Q) study, a large data‐linkage project of patients with cancer in Queensland linked to routinely collected administrative datasets. COS‐Q was designed to analyze the long‐term health and cost outcomes of all individuals diagnosed with a primary cancer in Queensland on a population level from 1997 to 2015 [14]. State and national healthcare databases were linked to Queensland Cancer Register records to capture all health service utilization and associated costs for Queensland residents over 20 years. The state of Queensland has a population size of 5.2 million, representing 20.2% of the Australian population [15]. About one‐third of the population lives outside the metropolitan South East Queensland area [16].

Participants

Patients aged 18 years or older with a documented resection for CRC between 2005 and 2015 were included. This time frame was selected to ensure sufficient follow‐up time to assess long‐term outcomes. CRC resection was based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) procedure codes [17]. Patients in the database with non‐resection surgeries based on the ICD procedure codes were excluded, such as stoma closure, formation of reservoirs, insertion of colonic stents, and resections where no colonic mucosa remained to be surveyed (e.g., total proctocolectomy).

Demographic and Clinical Variables

Socio‐demographic information was extracted, including the age at resection, sex, marital status, geographical residence, and socio‐economic status. Geographic remoteness was measured using the Accessibility/Remoteness Index of Australia (ARIA) based on road distance to service centers. Socio‐economic status was measured using the Socio‐Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) summarizing the relative socio‐economic characteristics of Australian communities. Both ARIA and SEIFA measures were created by the Australian Bureau of Statistics [18, 19].

Colonoscopy data, including procedure ICD codes, were used to differentiate procedures with and without polypectomy (see Appendix 2). Procedure dates were extracted for further analysis. Patients with a CRC code as the primary site code (C180‐C209) were selected from the COS‐Q study. Patients who had a resection but received no surveillance colonoscopy were included in the overall assessment of compliance with the Guidelines.

Findings during the colonoscopy procedure, such as the presence and removal of colonic adenoma, will change the recommended colonoscopy surveillance interval according to the Guidelines. For an example, removing a hyperplastic polyp was counted as polypectomy, but it does not change the recommended surveillance interval. Information on polyp removal, including number, size, and the presence of advanced features will impact surveillance intervals; however, they were not included in the COS‐Q database. Therefore, only colonoscopies without polypectomy (i.e., patients with low‐risk disease) could be meaningfully compared with the Guidelines.

Measurement of Guideline Adherence

For patients who had no pre‐operative colonoscopy, the interval between the resection and the first surveillance colonoscopy (C1) was calculated in days and should be less than 6 months. C1 was late if the interval was more than 365 days using a grace period of 6 months. In the event of no pre‐operative colonoscopy, C1 was late if more than 547 days (or 18 months) had elapsed since the resection. The interval between C1 and the second surveillance colonoscopy (C2) was calculated as the difference in days only if the first post‐operative surveillance colonoscopy did not include polypectomy. The Guidelines recommended an interval of 5 years (1825 days) between the C1 and C2 interval (C1C2). No subsequent colonoscopy intervals after C1C2 were analyzed in this study. Intervals more than 6‐months before the due date or < 1642 days were defined as ‘early’. Intervals within 6 months either side of due date, or between 1643 and 2007 days were on time or “as per recommendation,” and intervals more than 6‐months after the due date or > 2007 days were defined as ‘late’. The 6‐month grace period used is arbitrary but has been used previously by others [9]. Patients who did not undergo any surveillance colonoscopy were included in the non‐compliance group in relation to the Guidelines (Appendix 3).

Polypectomies at C1 were counted when a polypectomy ICD code was recorded. A second resection after C1 was counted if a resection ICD code was identified after C1. Subsequent resections and polypectomies were counted if the relevant ICD codes were recorded. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Statistical Methods

Summary statistics were used to describe socio‐demographic, clinical, and colonoscopy timing characteristics. Continuous variables were summarized and presented as means and standard deviations (SD) while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Counts less than 10 were reported as < 10 to avoid individuals potentially being identified. Data were analyzed for subgroups of patients with or without a pre‐operative colonoscopy, and differences tested using Chi square tests. Logistic regression analyses were conducted separately for patients with or without a pre‐operative colonoscopy to identify any socio‐demographic factors associated with patients having early surveillance. Factors considered in the regression analysis were age at the resection, sex, marital status, SEIFA and ARIA, and clinical data (i.e., with or without polypectomy, and/or second resection). Statistical significance was defined by p < 0.05. Missing data were reported in the results.

Financial Burden

Hospital costs associated with early surveillance were estimated by multiplying the number of patients who underwent early surveillance colonoscopies by a unit cost per procedure. The unit cost was derived from national estimates and adjusted based on the presence or absence of complications, as reported in the Australian Refined Diagnosis Related Group (AR‐DRG) classifications [20]. The weighted cost per colonoscopy ranged from AU$2330 to AU$3824 (2024 values [21]), depending on billing practices across various Hospital and Health Services. All reported costs are presented in current Australian dollars.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 11 087 patients who underwent CRC resection were identified in the COS‐Q study to assess overall compliance with the Guidelines. Of these, 3822 patients did not receive a first surveillance colonoscopy (C1) and were therefore classified as non‐compliant with the Guidelines. The remaining 7265 patients who had both a resection and a recorded C1 were included in the analysis of surveillance intervals (see Figure 1). The mean age of patients at the time of resection was 66 years (SD 12, interquartile range 16) and 48.9% were female. Most patients were married or de facto (70.6%) and lived in metropolitan areas (59.9%).

Patients selection.

Most patients had a pre‐operative colonoscopy (87.6%). A total of 1015 (12.4%) patients had their first post‐operative surveillance colonoscopy without polypectomy. Of these, the majority (88.2%) had a pre‐operative colonoscopy (Table 1). Among patients aged 60–79 years, a higher proportion of patients had a pre‐operative colonoscopy (63%) compared to those who did not (59%), p < 0.05. In contrast, patients younger than 50 years were more likely not to have a pre‐operative colonoscopy (11%) compared to those who did (7%), p < 0.05. Additionally, a greater proportion of patients residing in metropolitan areas did not have a pre‐operative colonoscopy (64%) compared to those who did not (60%), p < 0.05. The average time between the resection and the first colonoscopy was 341 days (SD 345) or 11.2 months. 75% of the sample had a colonoscopy within 435 days or 14.3 months (Figure 2).

Timing of the First Surveillance Colonoscopy

Among all patients, 55.8% underwent their first surveillance colonoscopy within the timeframe recommended by the Guidelines. Of those who received a surveillance colonoscopy, 17.9% had the procedure more than 6 months later than the recommended interval. Notably, three‐quarters of the cohort underwent their first follow‐up colonoscopy within 435 days of resection (Figure 3). The mean was 340.8 days and the standard deviation was 344.8. The median was 366 days and IQR was 125 days (Figure 2). Among those who had a late first surveillance colonoscopy, 25.3% (n = 330) did not have a pre‐operative colonoscopy while 74.7% (n = 972) had one (OR 3.2 CI:2.8–3.8, p < 0.001) (Table 2). The year of colectomy and marital status were associated with the first surveillance colonoscopy being late (Table 3). Colectomies performed in earlier years were associated with a higher likelihood of a late C1 (OR 1.2 CI: 1.1–1.2, p < 0.05), whereas being widowed (OR 0.8 CI: 0.7–0.9, p < 0.05) or being never married (OR 0.8 CI: 0.6–1.0, p < 0.05) had a lower likelihood of a late C1.

Timing of the Second Surveillance Colonoscopy

The mean interval between the first and second surveillance colonoscopies was 26.4 months (or approximately 2.2 years), with a standard deviation of 14.0 months. The median, 25th and 75th percentiles were 25.1, 13.7, and 35.9 months, respectively. After the first post‐operative surveillance colonoscopy without polypectomy, 96.4% had a second surveillance colonoscopy early. Thirty‐seven (3.6%) had the surveillance colonoscopy on time or late (Figure 4). Being female or having a pre‐operative colonoscopy was associated with an early second surveillance colonoscopy (Table 3).

The Overall Assessment of Compliance to the Guidelines

Of the 11 087 patients evaluated, 3822 (34.5%) did not undergo a surveillance colonoscopy following resection. A total of 1302 patients (11.7%) received their first surveillance colonoscopy later than recommended. For the second surveillance colonoscopy, 978 patients (96.4%) received it earlier than recommended, while 19 (1.9%) received it later than the recommended interval. Overall compliance with the national guidelines was 55.1% (6114 out of 11 087 patients) (Tables 4 and 5).

Hospital Costs of Early Colonoscopies

Our results found that there were 1000 early surveillance colonoscopies without polypectomy, among people with low‐risk CRC in Queensland from 2005 to 2009. These were associated with an expected financial burden in Queensland in the range of AU$2.30 million to AU$3.78 million, when adjusted to 2025 value.

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate colonoscopy surveillance intervals following CRC resection in Queensland, utilizing population‐based data. Our analysis revealed that the timing of the first surveillance colonoscopy was similar regardless of whether a pre‐operative colonoscopy had been performed, which contrasts with guideline recommendations suggesting surveillance within 6 months for patients without a pre‐operative colonoscopy and within 12 months for those with one. Nonetheless, overall compliance with guideline‐recommended timing was high when allowing for a six‐month “grace period.” More than 95% of patients with low risk of recurrence underwent the second surveillance colonoscopy earlier than the advised five‐year interval, particularly when no polypectomy was performed during the initial surveillance.

The Guideline recommendations are based on the theoretical risk of synchronous or metachronous cancer or significant polyps in areas of the colon that have not been examined [22]. Our study suggests that there are challenges in clinical execution. The observed poor compliance with the Guidelines may be attributed to various clinical factors. Patients who had a resection without a pre‐operative colonoscopy often present as emergencies. Emergency resections are associated with higher morbidities [23] which potentially delay the first surveillance colonoscopy. In our study, the majority of the first surveillance colonoscopy was performed within 12 months after the bowel resection, regardless of the different intervals recommended by the Guidelines.

Our findings are consistent with a prior meta‐analysis indicating that up to 40% of colonoscopies performed for polyp surveillance in Australia, Europe, and North America were conducted earlier than guideline recommendations [9]. In our study, over 95% of second surveillance colonoscopies following CRC resection occurred earlier than the recommended five‐year interval and as frequently as annually. While the exact reasons for early surveillance remain difficult to determine, the authors speculate that the underlying causes are likely multifactorial, including clinician‐, system‐, and patient‐related influences. Historically, clinicians have largely determined surveillance intervals based on clinical judgment and perceived patient risk [24]. While guidelines have influenced clinical decision‐making [25], their effect was not clearly demonstrated in the current study. Although financial incentives have been proposed as a potential driver of early or more frequent surveillance, there is limited supporting evidence [26].

System‐level factors may also play a role. For instance, the National Bowel Screening Program in Australia targets individuals aged 45–64 years, sending routine invitations for fecal occult blood testing. This system does not account for individuals already undergoing colonoscopy surveillance—whether for polyps or post‐cancer follow‐up. As a result, patients who have recently undergone surveillance colonoscopy may still receive a stool test kit. A positive result can lead to an additional, and often unnecessary, colonoscopy.

Patient‐related factors further contribute to early surveillance, particularly among those who perceive themselves to be at elevated risk [27]. This perception is understandable among individuals with a history of CRC and surgery. Notably, demographic factors, specifically female sex, were significantly associated with earlier surveillance. This observation offers further insight into the drivers of early surveillance, highlighting the role of patient‐related factors in addition to the commonly emphasized influence of clinician risk perception. Although our findings do not support financial incentives as a primary motivator, the financial implications of early and potentially unnecessary surveillance colonoscopies are nonetheless substantial; our study period revealed significant costs associated with surveillance colonoscopies performed without polypectomy, with even higher costs anticipated if procedures involving polypectomy were included. Our findings suggest revising surveillance guidelines to perform a colonoscopy within 12 months after a CRC resection with or without a pre‐operative colonoscopy to reflect the current clinical practice.

In addition to the financial burden on the health system, the overall poor compliance to the national guidelines and the overuse of surveillance colonoscopies impede access for patients with genuine needs. The prognosis following curative colorectal resection is improving [28], resulting in an increasing number of CRC survivors requiring surveillance. The recent reduction of the screening age from 50 to 45 years in Australia is likely to create a substantial demand for colonoscopy at a population level [29]. Low‐value colonoscopies, including those performed earlier than current surveillance guidelines recommend, should be scrutinized to ensure that healthcare resources are utilized effectively.

One strength of this study is the use of the COS‐Q dataset to identify and select all CRC cases and associated treatments using DRG codes. While there is a theoretical possibility of missing cases not captured by the Queensland Cancer Register, this is considered unlikely in both public and private healthcare settings due to the mandatory reporting of malignancies by pathologists.

This study was limited by the data items in COS‐Q and the inability to know why surveillance intervals might differ to the Guidelines for valid clinical reasons. Assessing the frequency interval against the recommendations was considered reasonable only for patients who underwent colonoscopies without polypectomy so that a more homogeneous group was studied.

Conclusions

Most surveillance colonoscopies performed in Queensland hospitals did not align with the recommended intervals of current Guidelines. There is potential to significantly optimize surveillance timing and resource use and enhance patient care in CRC surveillance.
