Network pharmacology and molecular docking analysis on mechanisms of Euphorbia Lathyris L. seed in treating colorectal cancer
Authors: Xiaohong Gao, Hongjuan Zhang, Yilin Wang, Peizhen Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEuphorbia Lathyris L. Seed (ELLS) is a Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), which has long been used in China. This study was designed to reveal the synergistic mechanism of ELLS in the treatment of colorectal cancer (CRC) by using network pharmacology method and molecular docking. In addition, related in vitro experiments will be conducted to verify the efficacy of ELLS.MethodsELLS related compounds were obtained from TCMSP database. Then active compounds were screened by ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion). Additionally, TCMSP, BATMAN-TCM, STITCH, Swiss Target Prediction and literatures were used to capture the relationships between drugs and targets. A compound-target (C-T) network was established by Cytoscape. Target genes related to CRC were acquired from GeneCards, TTD and OMIM databases. Correlations about compound-target-pathway (C-T-P) were visualized by Cytoscape. The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed by STRING. Gene survival analysis came from the GEPIA2. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed via metascape. Molecular docking analysis was constructed by the AutoDock Vina. And the efficacy of ELLS in combating CRC was verified using HCT116 and SW620 cells.ResultsA total of 12 active compounds and 173 associated targets of ELLS compounds were identified. Sixty-three common genes were obtained by matching 173 potential genes of ELLS with 1554 CRC related genes and PPI network screened out key targets, including AKT1, CASP3, ESR1, TNF, HSP90AA1. Five core compounds were beta-sitosterol, stigmasterol, euphol, Artemetin and lathyrol. Eight core targets were PRKACA, PRKCA, AR, BAX, GSK3B, NFKB1, RXRA and NCOA2 in the C-T-P network. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that ELLS effectively treated CRC through regulation of pathways in cancer, Epstein-Barr virus infection, thyroid hormone signaling pathway, bile secretion, and transcriptional misregulation in cancer. Gene survival analysis showed that 7 genes (APAF1, APOE, CASP3, HDAC2, NFKB1, PGR, and SNAI1) were significantly related in CRC patients’ survival and prognosis. Molecular docking results suggested that almost all of the core compound-targets had an excellent binding activity (affinity < − 5 kcal/mol). CCK8 results indicated that ELLS (20 µg/mL, 24-hour treatment) significantly inhibited the proliferation of HCT116 cells, while it had minimal impact on the viability of normal NCM460 cells under the same conditions (survival rate ≥ 80%).ConclusionKey targets of ELLS could regulate multiple signaling pathways and biological process in treating CRC which provided a scientific basis for further elucidating the mechanism of molecules and screening drug targets.Keywords: Euphorbia Lathyris L. Seed, Network Pharmacology, Molecular Docking Analysis, Colorectal Cancer, Drug Targets

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second most common cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide and ranks fourth among all malignant tumors in terms of overall cancer mortality, causing approximately 900,000 deaths each year [1]. It poses a persistent and severe challenge to global public health. Currently, the clinical treatment of CRC mainly includes surgical operation, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy, etc [2, 3]. are treatment options for patients with CRC. Novel therapies and drugs are urgently needed because CRC remains incurable and current therapeutic methods have limitations. Although these methods have achieved certain therapeutic effects in early-stage and some patients in the middle and advanced stages, there are still significant limitations. Surgical treatment has limited efficacy for patients with metastasis in the advanced stage; radiotherapy and chemotherapy often come with obvious toxic side effects and drug resistance issues; while immunotherapy and targeted therapy are usually only applicable to patients with specific biomarker positivity, the overall response rate still has room for improvement [4]. Furthermore, numerous studies have indicated that TCM provide meaningful chemoprevention in CRC patients [5, 6]. Therefore, in the context of the high incidence of CRC and prominent treatment bottlenecks, in-depth research on the pathogenic mechanism, accelerating the development of new drugs, and exploring innovative treatment strategies have become urgent and important tasks for the global oncology community. Developing more efficient, less toxic, and treatment-resistant solutions is the key to improving patient prognosis and enhancing quality of life. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), with its unique theoretical framework and rich practical experience, is increasingly demonstrating significant value in the comprehensive treatment of CRC. Its core advantage lies in the use of compound or single herbs containing numerous bioactive components. Through a multi-component, multi-target, and multi-pathway synergistic mechanism, it plays a crucial auxiliary and synergistic role in modern conventional therapies such as surgical treatment, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [7]. Furthermore, numerous studies have indicated that TCM provide meaningful chemoprevention in CRC patients [7, 8]. Euphorbia Lathyris L. Seed is widely used as TCM [7] in Asia, especially in China [8] which exhibits influence on multi-drug resistance, apoptosis [9], antitumor activity, antioxidant activity [10], cytotoxic activities [11–13], p-glycoprotein [14], adipogenesis [15], tyrosinase inhibitory activities [16] and anti-inflammatory property [17, 18] against CRC and other cancers.

Network pharmacology, as an emerging systematic biology research method, has been widely regarded by the academic community as a highly promising approach due to its ability to effectively elucidate the synergistic mechanism of TCM’s multi-components, multi-targets, and multi-pathways, and to predict potential new therapeutic targets for diseases [19, 20]. Therefore, it has been widely used to investigate intrinsic interaction between drug and target in the previous research studies [21, 22]. Furthermore, this method has been widely applied in predicting the mechanism of action of traditional Chinese medicine on various types of cancers such as breast cancer and liver cancer [23, 24]. Currently, there is a study on the network pharmacology analysis of the cytotoxic triterpenoids isolated from Euphorbia [25]; another study is based on network pharmacology to investigate the mechanism of action of Euphorbia fischeriana Steud on liver cirrhosis [26]. However, there are relatively few studies on the relevant network pharmacology of Euphorbia Lathyris L. Seed for CRC.

Therefore, this study was intended to search the potential bioactive compounds, targets, and pathways of ELLS for the treatment of CRC. The detailed workflow was showed in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

Determination and screening of the active compounds of ELLS

All compounds of ELLS were obtained from the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database (TCMSP, https://tcmsp-e.com/). TCMSP is a unique systems pharmacology database of Chinese herbal medicines that collects the relationships between drugs, diseases, and targets. In TCMSP, pharmacokinetic properties of natural compounds involve oral bioavailability, drug-likeness, aqueous solubility, blood-brain-barrier, and intestinal epithelial permeability etc. We retrieved all compounds of ELLS by name. Then the active compounds of ELLS were screened by ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) which were key process of drug development [27]. Among these pharmacokinetic parameters, oral bioavailability (OB), and drug-likeness (DL) were the most critical two items applied to identify the potential active compound in our study. The active compounds in ELLS were screened with a criterion of OB ≥ 30% and DL ≥ 0.18 for further study [28].

Target prediction of active compounds

First, PubChem CID and InChI were obtained from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Next, TCMSP (http://tcmspw.com/.

tcmsp.php) and BATMAN-TCM (http://bionet.ncpsb.org/batman-.

tcm/), STITCH (http://stitch.embl.de/), Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) and a literature [15] were used to capture the relationships between drugs and targets. “Homo sapiens” was chosen in the species item and cutoff score ≥ 20 was selected in BATMAN-TCM. In order to convert targets proteins names into official gene symbols and IDs, we used the authoritative database of protein sequences, UniProtKB (https://www.uniprot.org/) confining the species to “Homo sapiens”.

Construction and Analysis of C-T network

A compound-target (C-T) network was established by Cytoscape (http://www.cytoscape.org/, ver3.7.2) [29] to reflect the complicated interactions between active compounds and their corresponding targets. Cytoscape was a powerful platform to analyze and visualize the various types of biologic data and complicated networks [30].

Potential target collection of colorectal cancer

Colorectal Cancer related therapeutic target genes were acquired from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), OMIM (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, http://www.omim.org/) and TTD (http://bidd.nus.edu.sg/group/cjttd/) databases. “Colorectal Cancer” was used as a keyword and the species was limited to “Homo sapiens”. Only the targets with relevance score ≥ 50 in GeneCards were selected. All the genes obtained above the three databases were summarized as the core targets of colorectal cancer.

Venn analysis

A Venn diagram showing the common targets between active compounds and disease was generated by Bioinformatics and Evolutionary Genomics system (bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/).

PPI network construction and analysis

The above 59 common core targets from the Venn analysis were uploaded to the STRING (The Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes, https://string-db.org/, version 11.0) database which provides protein interactions to obtain the PPI network. The species was confined to “Homo sapiens” and minimum required interaction score was medium confidence (0.4) with hiding the disconnected protein nodes. Then a bar plot diagram showing nodes degree was operated with R 4.1.0.

Analysis of GO and KEGG pathway enrichment

Metascape which is an efficient and useful tool updated monthly for analyzing functional enrichment, gene annotation (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html) [31] was applied to investigate the potential molecular mechanisms of ELLS on CRC by GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. “Homo sapiens” was selected as input analysis species and P value cutoff was set 0.01. Then a compound-target-pathway (C-T-P) network was visualized to explore the interaction between compounds, targets and pathways through Cytoscape.

Survival analysis

The survival analyses data of these genes came from the GEPIA2 (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis, http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/) [32] database which contains the RNA sequencing expression data of 9,736 tumors and 8,587 normal samples from the GTEx and the TCGA projects which provides patient survival analysis, similar gene detection, and gene expression levels. COAD (Colon adenocarcinoma) and READ (Rectum adenocarcinoma) were chosen as the analytical cancer types.

Compound-target molecular docking

AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 [33, 34] which is an open source software for molecular docking was used to evaluate the interactional reliability of the active compounds and their corresponding targets. The specific method was as follows: (1) The 2D structures of active compounds (ligands) were acquired from PubChem database and saved as SDF file format. (2) Chem3D was used to convert the above-mentioned PDB format to MOL2 format. (3) The crystal structures of the core targets were downloaded from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) in a PDB format. (4) PyMOL 1.7.2.1 was used to remove the water, extract original ligands. (5) The compounds, original ligands, and target proteins were transformed to PDBQT format and the docking box parameters were set by AutoDock Tools 1.5.6. (6) The final docking simulation was performed via AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 and the docking conformation was visualized in PyMOL 1.7.2.1.

Reagent and Instruments

The HCT116 and SW620 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC); The NCM460 was purchased from INCELL Corporation LLC; Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, C04001), DMEM (C3110) 200 and RPMI-1640 (C3010) were obtained from VivaCell Biosciences (Shanghai, China); CCK-8 reagent(Beyotime, C0039).

Cell culture

The human colorectal cancer cell lines HCT116 and SW620 were purchased from the ATCC; the human normal intestinal epithelial cell line NCM460 was purchased from INCELL Corporation LLC. The HCT116 and NCM460 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution, while the SW620 cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution. These three cell lines were cultured in a 37 °C, 5% carbon dioxide incubator.

Cell viability assay

HCT116 cells (5 × 10³ per well), SW620 cells (1 × 10⁴ per well), and NCM460 cells (1 × 10³ per well) were seeded into 96-well plates, respectively. During the logarithmic growth phase, the cells were treated with ELLS at concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 µg/mL at 37 °C for 12, 24, and 48 h. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Target prediction of active compounds and C-T Network

A total of 173 targets related to 12 active compounds were gathered from databases. An intuitive C-T network which contained 185 nodes and 322 edges was constructed by Cytoscape (Fig. 2). The “degree” value referring to a node’s number of edges connecting to it was calculated to evaluate the importance of a node. The larger node meant more importance. Prediction therapeutic targets of CRC and the PPI network

A total of 1554 possible targets of CRC were predicted from GeneCards (1468), TTD (103) and the OMIM (184) databases after removing the repetitive genes. Sixty-three common targets were collected between 173 compound-related targets and the 1554 CRC related targets by Venn Analysis. The common genes were uploaded to the STRING database to get a PPI network and the free node (PRSS1) was removed (Fig. 3). The network had 63 nodes and 358 edges and the top 30 genes with higher degree were visualized by a bar plot diagram (Fig. 4).

Analysis of GO and KEGG pathway enrichment

GO enrichment included BP (biological), MF (molecular function), CC (cellular component). A total of 1295 BPs, 83 MFs, 30 CCs and 180 KEGG pathways were obtained. The respective top 10 items were shown in Fig. 5 based on P value. GO analysis indicated that the CCs of ELLS on CRC were connected to mitochondrial outer membrane, membrane raft, cell body and so on. BPs were related to cellular response to organic cyclic compound, regulation of lipid metabolic process, apoptotic signaling pathway and others. MFs were involved in nuclear receptor activity, DNA-binding transcription factor binding, and protein kinase activity. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that several pathways were correlated with the CRC, including Pathways in cancer, Epstein-Barr virus infection, Thyroid hormone signaling pathway, Bile secretion, Transcriptional misregulation in cancer and others.

Analysis of C-T-P network

According to the KEGG enrichment results, a C-T-P network indicating the mechanism of ELLS acting on CRC was constructed. The network included 74 nodes and 213 edges. The purple hexagons represented 12 bioactive compounds of ELLS, the pink circle represented 48 corresponding targets of the 12 compounds targets, and green V-shapes represented 15 key pathways (Fig. 6). Five core compounds were beta-sitosterol, stigmasterol, euphol, Artemetin and lathyrol. Eight core targets were PRKACA, PRKCA, AR, BAX, GSK3B, NFKB1, RXRA and NCOA2 with higher degree score. The inner complex regulatory relationship of the network indicated that the bioactive compounds of ELLS may be of great importance in treating CRC through these core pathways.

Survival analysis

In order to elucidate the impact of these PPI core genes on survival and prognosis of CRC, survival analysis was performed by GEPIA2. Among these top 30 genes of higher degree, 7 genes (APAF1, APOE, CASP3, HDAC2, NFKB1, PGR, SNAI1) were of importance in CRC patients’ survival and prognosis with results of P<0.05 (Fig. 7). The survival analysis suggested that some active compounds in ELLS played an important role on these genes to affect the prognosis of patients.

Results of the molecular docking

In order to verify if the 5 active compounds play a significant role in regulation of their corresponding targets, molecular docking was performed showing that most of the compounds in the network had strong affinity with their targets. A total of 25 pairs of docking results were acquired and the affinities were shown in Table 2. Affinity was an index to evaluate the binding capacity. A lower affinity meant a stronger binding capacity. An affinity < -5 kcal/mol was regarded as a good binding capacity and when the affinity < -7 kcal/mol, the binding capacity was stronger. According to the docking results, there were 24 pairs’ affinities were under − 5 kcal/mol and 19 pairs’ docking affinities were lower than − 7 kcal/mol. These results indicated that the interaction’s reliability between active compounds and targets were verified. Six pairs’ docking results with higher scores were visualized by Pymol (Fig. 8a-f).

ELLS can inhibit the proliferation of CRC cells

The CCK8 test results show that ELLS has the effect of inhibiting the proliferation and cell viability of CRC cells HCT116 and SW620, and it has dose-dependent effects at 12 h, 24 h and 48 h of treatment. For HCT116, the IC50 of ELLS at 12 h, 24 h and 48 h treatments was 50.00, 20.00 and 10.00 µg/mL respectively (Fig. 9a-c, P < 0.001). However, in SW620, the IC50 of ELLS at 12 h, 24 h and 48 h treatments was 80.00, 60.00 and 50.00 µg/mL respectively (Fig. 9d-f, P < 0.001). Additionally, at the 12 h, 24 h and 48 h time points, the doses of ELLS that maintained the survival rate of NCM460 cells ≥ 80 were 10.00, 20.00 and 2.50 µg/mL respectively (Fig. 9g-i, P < 0.001). Therefore, ELLS at a condition of 20.00 µg/mL for 24 h was selected for the subsequent study.

Discussion

The initiation of this study is based on the current clinical situation where the treatment options for CRC are limited and new drugs are urgently needed [35]. Traditional Chinese medicine has attracted attention as a potential auxiliary and preventive measure, and Euphorbia Lathyris L. Seed has been reported to possess various anti-tumor biological activities [36]. Moreover, the research on the mechanism of action of the Euphorbia genus on liver cirrhosis through network pharmacology analysis has been reported [26]. However, there are relatively few studies on the relevant network pharmacology of Euphorbia Lathyris L. Seed for CRC. To systematically explore its mechanism of treating colorectal cancer, this study employs network pharmacology methods, aiming to predict the active components, target sites, and related pathways of kinnikinnick.

In this study, 12 bioactive compounds of ELLS were identified by TCMSP. 63 common targets were selected between targets of ELLS and CRC. KEGG and GO enrichment results indicated that ELLS may act on CRC by several signaling pathways including pathways in cancer, Epstein-Barr virus infection, thyroid hormone signaling pathway, bile secretion, transcriptional misregulation in cancer and others. Eight core targets and five core compounds were confirmed by C-T-P network analysis. Among them, Beta-sitosterol showed anticancer capacities including breast cancer, colon cancer, lung cancer etc. and exhibited antioxidant potential [37]. Moreover, studies showed that Euphol exerted cytotoxic against sorts of cancer cell lines via apoptosis [38] and anticancer ability through cell cycle [39]. Studies about stigmasterol indicated its anti-cancer activities including suppressing tumor growth in cholangiocarcinoma [40], overcoming chemoresistance in endometrial cancer [41], suppressing cell migration and angiogenesis genes in ovarian cancer [42] and gastric cancer [43]. Inhibiting cell cycle and inducing apoptosis activity of artemetin was revealed in human myeloid leukemia cells [44]. Artemetin also exhibited cytotoxic activity and microtubule disassembling property in breast cancer cells [45]. Lathyrol acted as a potential P-gp (P-glycoprotein) inhibitor which played an important role in MDR (multi-drug resistance) which influence cancer chemotherapy effect [46, 47]. Taken together, these findings indicate that the core compounds of ELLS could participate in various fields in treating cancer including CRC and may be potential targets in CRC treatment.

Furthermore, we identified 8 core targets of ELLS on CRC using network analysis, and these targets were mainly correlated to CRC or other cancers. GSK3B (Glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta) was screened as top 10 genes of PPI network and was also one of the eight core targets in C-T-P network. GSK3B was a potential diagnostic biomarker related to CRC stage-IV progression by stage-based meta-analysis [48]. Polymorphisms of GSK3B were associated with CRC TNM stage, tumor location and the risk of promoting or preventing CRC [49]. NFKB1 (Nuclear factor NF-kappa-B p105 subunit) which was significantly related with survival and prognosis in CRC patients was also one of the 8 core genes in C-T-P network. NFKB1with − 94 ins/del ATTG (rs28720239) polymorphism significantly promoted the risk of sporadic CRC in Malaysian patients [50]. The relative mRNA expression of NFKB1 detected by RT-qPCR in lymph node metastasis CRC tissues was significantly higher compared with normal tissues [51]. PRKACA (cAMP-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit alpha) was interacted with β-catenin and the liver regenerative response [52] which can be regarded as therapeutic target. The expression of PRKACA mediated resistance to anti-HER2 therapy in breast cancer [53]. Upregulation of PRKCA (Protein kinase C alpha type) expression meant poorer prognosis in lung adenocarcinoma [54]. Previous research showed that CRC was related with hormone as males suffering from CRC were more than females and AR hypomethylation in PBL was correlated with higher CRC risk [55]. Previous studies indicated that AR (Androgen receptor) expression was lower in normal colonic mucosa than colorectal adenoma [56] and higher AR expression was significantly related with poorer overall survival which indicated that AR could act as a novel prognostic target of CRC patients [57]. The proapoptotic gene BAX had a lower expression in resistant derivate cells and tumor tissues than wild HT-29 cells and normal adjacent tissues which exhibited the importance of BAX in CRC carcinogenesis [58]. BAX may participate in the early stages of CRC by influencing cancer infiltrating to blood and lymph systems [59].

Allelic variation [60] and SNPs [61] of RXRA (Retinoic acid receptor RXR-alpha) participated in metachronous colorectal neoplasia. Study showed that NCOA2 was a potential growth regulatory gene inhibited Wnt/β-catenin pathway in CRC [62].

Then, GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were performed to better understand the interaction of these common targets. The results demonstrated that the molecular mechanisms of ELLS on CRC were mostly involved in cancer, Epstein-Barr virus infection, thyroid hormone signaling pathway, bile secretion, Transcriptional misregulation in cancer. Among them, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) was an oncovirus related with CRC [63]. Activated thyroid hormone (TH) stimulated cell differentiation [64] and chemosensitivity in CRC stem cells through BMP4 expression and Wnt signaling [65]. In addition, TH promoted cell proliferative and induced apoptosis in CRC [66]. Bile acids (BAs) were crucial compounds of bile which promoted cancer progression by inhibiting apoptosis, accelerating cell proliferation, invasion, angiogenesis, and damaging colonic epithelial cells of CRC [67] .

Then, molecular docking was performed to further uncover the interaction of the active compounds and their corresponding targets. The results illustrated that 96% of the binding affinities were good (affinity < -5 kcal/mol) and 76% were strong (affinity < -7 kcal/mol), reinforcing the reliability of the network pharmacology prediction.

In addition, we further explored the inhibitory effect of ELLS on the proliferation of colorectal cancer cells and its potential molecular mechanism. The results of this study demonstrated that ELLS could significantly inhibit the proliferation of CRC cell lines HCT116 and SW620 in vitro, and this inhibition was time- and dose-dependent. The inhibitory intensity of ELLS on the two cell lines was different, with a more significant effect on HCT116, which might be related to the heterogeneity of the two cells in terms of genetic background and the activation status of signaling pathways. This finding is consistent with previous studies, suggesting that the sensitivity of ELLS may be influenced by the specific molecular phenotype of the cells [10, 68]. Additionally, at lower concentrations, ELLS had a relatively small impact on the survival rate of normal colon epithelial cells NCM460, providing a preliminary safety basis for its subsequent development as a candidate drug for CRC.

In conclusion, the research results indicated that ELLS may exert its effects through the synergistic action of multiple active compounds, therapeutic targets, and diverse pathways. This provides a scientific basis for the research and clinical application of ELLS. While network pharmacology and in vitro experimental methods have been widely applied in the study of TCM mechanisms for various cancers, the novelty of this research is primarily demonstrated in the following three aspects. First, this study systematically explores ELLS, a TCM herb with relatively scarce modern research on its anti-CRC effects, thereby supplementing the understanding of its potential mechanisms in CRC. Second, moving beyond conventional “component-target-pathway” predictions, we constructed a C-T-P network to precisely identify an interactive module consisting of five core compounds, such as β-sitosterol and lathyrol, and eight key targets, including GSK3B, NFKB1, and AR. Finally, following the network predictions, we were the first to confirm the proliferation-inhibitory activity of ELLS extract on two CRC cell lines with distinct characteristics, HCT116 and SW620, through molecular docking and in vitro experiments. Additionally, we preliminarily indicated its differential safety profile on normal colon epithelial cells, providing new experimental evidence for the dual “efficacy-toxicity” nature of ELLS. Therefore, this study not only offers the first relatively systematic mechanistic prediction map for ELLS against CRC but also highlights its unique therapeutic aspects compared to other anti-cancer TCM herbs, laying a theoretical foundation for its further development as a candidate drug or adjuvant agent for CRC treatment.

However, this study still has limitations. Firstly, the in vitro verification in this paper is only a preliminary exploratory experiment and has not been deeply discussed; secondly, the pathways of the target points for its function have not been thoroughly explored. Future research will focus on the separation and identification of active monomers in ELLS, using gene knockout/overexpression techniques to validate key targets, and verifying the therapeutic efficacy and systemic toxicity in both in vivo and in vitro settings, in order to comprehensively promote its translational application prospects.

Conclusion

In summary, the results of network pharmacology indicate that ELLS may exert its effects through the synergistic action of multiple bioactive compounds, therapeutic targets, and diverse pathways, potentially providing a scientific basis for the research and clinical application of ELLS. Additionally, this study preliminarily confirmed that ELLS is a natural extract with anti-CRC cell proliferation activity. These findings provide important experimental evidence and mechanism clues for developing ELLS as a new anti-CRC drug or adjuvant therapy.
