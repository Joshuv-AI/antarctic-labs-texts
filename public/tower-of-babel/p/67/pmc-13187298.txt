Three-dimensional ex-vivo visualization of normal and inflamed small intestine and colonic tissue using optical coherence tomography
Authors: Abigail Matt, Yilin Li, Andrew Song, Billy D. Nix, Katherine Huang, Umang Jain, Changqing Ma, Chao Zhou, Parakkal Deepak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inflammatory bowel diseases, including Crohn’s Disease (CD) and Ulcerative Colitis (UC), are characterized by inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract. Early diagnoses are critical in the determination of an effective treatment. Current methods of tissue assessment remain two-dimensional and limited by lack of information under the surface of the mucosal layer. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a noninvasive, label-free, volumetric imaging modality which can visualize tissue structures down to the micrometer scale. Previous study has explored OCT as a method for endoscopic and benchtop assessment of colonic tissues. Here, we explore OCT in an ex vivo capacity to visualize features in IBD. Samples from human subjects with normal (not inflamed), UC, and CD diagnoses were imaged, where OCT was able to differentiate between normal ileal and colonic tissue and those from patients with CD or UC. Our study shows the promise of higher-resolution OCT to visualize both small details and overall structures in three-dimensions, informing development of future technologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46293-4.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases are characterized by inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract and affect approximately 2.4 million people in the United States alone1. Two common phenotypes, Crohn’s Disease (CD) and Ulcerative Colitis (UC), are typically diagnosed by a combination of endoscopic, histological, and serological approaches. Brightfield endoscopic methods of tissue assessment allow visualization of surface structures and inflammation in the colon and terminal ileum2. From an endoscopic view, some cases of CD can be characterized by involvement of any part of the bowel tract, with lesions appearing in discontinuous cobblestone patterns3. Lesions in UC can be viewed endoscopically, typically starting near the rectum and spreading proximally in the colon4. However, these characteristics can sometimes overlap between UC and CD in up to 15% of cases, when a distinction between UC and CD cannot be made, and are diagnosed as inflammatory bowel disease unclassified (IBDU)5. It is important to differentiate between these diseases in early stages, so that more targeted treatments can be applied to minimize complications5.

Histological methods of tissue examination have high molecular sensitivity and can aid in making a CD or UC diagnosis. In CD, inflammation tends to be transmural, meaning that it spans the length of the mucosa and submucosa. Granulomas may be present in CD, but are not a reliable marker in all cases3. In UC, inflammation may be more confined to the mucosa, which may be structurally distorted, including the presence of crypt abscesses6. In both UC and CD, inflammation can lead to fibrosis, where extracellular matrix accumulates, depositing collagen in tissue7. In UC, this can lead to the thickening of the muscularis mucosae layer8. Current methods for brightfield endoscopic assessment of tissue for inflammation and fibrosis in patients with IBD insufficiently differentiates between the two entities. A new method for aiding diagnosis should overcome the limitations of brightfield endoscopic approaches, which provide only information about the surface layer of tissue, and histological methods, which can be sensitive to different cell types but are invasive, requiring resection, and two-dimensional.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a high resolution, label-free, non-invasive, three-dimensional (3D) imaging modality. Introduced in 1991, OCT relies on the interference between reflected light from a reference mirror and backscattered light from the sample of interest to form volumetric images9. Current OCT systems can provide micron-scale resolution with 1–2 mm depth penetration in thick tissues. Clinically, OCT has been applied to ophthalmic imaging, given its ability to reliably visualize structures and vasculature in the eye10. In an ex vivo setting, OCT has been used to characterize renal, breast, brain, ovarian tissues, and more, where it provided close correlation with gold standard histological staining11–14. In the colon, OCT has been largely applied to cancer detection. In vivo patient studies of endoscopic OCT systems have shown the potential for clinical translation in cancer diagnosis15–18. The development of micro-OCT has provided systems with improved axial resolution (1–2 μm) through the use of ultra-wide bandwidth light sources19. Micro-OCT has been used in the colon to visualize crypt architecture and individual goblet cells with high correlation to histology20,21. Machine learning models have also shown promise for real-time differentiation of benign and malignant tissue in OCT images22–24.

However, OCT has also been used to assess tissue morphology in IBD. In 2004, Shen et al. published two studies, one in vivo and one ex vivo, that demonstrated the promise of using an OCT endoscopic probe to detect changes between healthy and diseased tissue, going as far as recognizing transmural inflammation in a cross-sectional image of a CD sample25,26. Later, another endoscopic OCT study was published, focusing on the ability of OCT to visualize tissue in 3D, analyzing differences between normal and UC tissue in both cross-sectional and en face images27. Quantitative measures of OCT images in IBD have been extracted, mainly focusing on the attenuation of light by mucosal tissues as a marker of disease28. Most recently, OCT has been applied to image tissues from rodent models of IBD, obtaining three-dimensional datasets demonstrating that OCT can visualize differences in normal and diseased tissue29–31. OCT has the potential to combine the advantages of endoscopic and histological means of diagnosis, producing 3D, depth-wise information about tissue without the need for excising or labelling tissue.

This study aims to evaluate our high-resolution OCT system’s ability to recognize structures in the colon and ileum, in moving towards classifying inflamed tissue in CD and UC. Here, we imaged ex vivo normal, CD, and UC tissues using OCT with resolution on the single-micron scale. With this, we were able to visualize disruptions in 3D, including in the layered structure in OCT cross-sectional images and in crypt structures in OCT en face images as a result of IBD. This study provides a foundation for morphological assessment of IBD tissues using 3D OCT.

Methods

OCT imaging system

Samples were imaged with a custom-made 1300 nm SD-OCT system. Imaging light was sent from a SLD light source (EBD291023-02, Exalos) centered at 1300 nm with a 175.6 nm bandwidth to a 50:50 fiber coupler (Hi780, AC Photonics, Inc – CA), which split the light into sample and reference arms. The sample arm was fitted with a 5x objective lens, resulting in a 3.9 μm lateral resolution. Interfered light from sample and reference arms was detected by a spectrometer (Cobra 1300, Wabash Photonics) with a 2048-pixel InGaAs line-scan camera (GL2048, Sensors Unlimited). This system setup resulted in a 4.0 μm axial resolution in tissue and 98.2 dB maximal sensitivity.

Sample imaging procedure

Patients undergoing bowel resections at Washington University in St. Louis School of Medicine were recruited into our study. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable regulations governing research involving human subjects. The research protocol, including the collection and use of patient tissue samples, was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Washington University School of Medicine under protocol number IRB 201,111,078. All participants were informed of the purpose, procedures, and potential risks of the study, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Consent included permission for the collection, storage, and research use of tissue samples. All collected data and specimens were de-identified to protect patient confidentiality.

After tissues were collected, they were placed into RPMI solution at 4 °C and transported for imaging. Samples were cut into a roughly 1 × 1 cm piece and then placed into a modified histology cassette (21RK90, Grainger). This cassette was fitted with an imaging window by using a drill press to create a 3/8” circular hole in the lid and placing a 22 mm x 22 mm coverslip (CS-S22-100, AmScope) under the lid of the cassette. The mucosal layer of the tissue was oriented upward towards the imaging beam, and the modified cassette was closed before imaging, creating a flat surface. The cassette with the sample was placed into a 50 mm diameter dish, covered with 1xPBS, and oriented in the imaging system. To minimize sensitivity roll-off deeper into tissue, we imaged the flipped sample positioned close to the top of imaging range (e.g., zero optical delay position). The imaging beam was focused just below the surface of the mucosa. For the 1300 nm system, a 2.9 mm by 2.8 mm FOV was sampled with 1000 A-scans and 1000 B-scans. Multiple different FOVs were imaged around the sample with approximately 20% overlap between adjacent scans.

OCT image processing

Custom software collected and processed images. OCT images were filtered using a median filter with a 3 × 3 window and displayed with log compression. To account for sample stage tilt, image flattening was performed through custom written MATLAB code. In this code, each frame in the image is binarized such that the flat line established by the coverslip on the OCT image can be recognized. This line is fitted, and the image is flattened according to the resultant fit.

To stitch tiled OCT images, the ImageJ stitching plugin, as described by Preibisch et al. in 200932 was used with a 20% overlap and linear blending settings selected. In cases where the stitching plugin failed to correctly stitch tiles, a modified algorithm utilizing restricted offsets was used to eliminate erroneous tile alignments. This customized stitching program is available at https://github.com/andrewprivate/stitching for public use.

For analysis of spatial patterns, the 2D fast Fourier transform was used. A region of ~ 8.1 mm2 was selected, and an en face projection was taken of successive slices in the mucosal layer. In MATLAB, the “fft2()” function was performed on the image of interest. The resulting spectrum is the result of a cropping to include a box of the center 150 pixels. For group analysis of the 2DFFT patterns, we first fit each sample’s mean radial PSD using the “fit()” function with the “power2” option. We subtracted the fit line from the mean radial PSD and define “Ring power” as the mean of the positive resultant values.

Histology

After imaging, samples were fixed in 4% PFA solution at 4 °C. Tissues were then paraffin embedded, sectioned, and stained using a routine H&E staining procedure through the Washington University Digestive Diseases Research Core Center. The sections were scanned using a Ziess Axio Scan brightfield slide scanner and a 10x objective lens at the Washington University Center for Cellular Imaging. These images were white-balanced and contrast adjusted for ideal image quality.

OCT and histological correlation

OCT and histology image pairs were correlated to match features seen in OCT images to their corresponding structures in H&E histology images. A total of 7 normal samples, 4 CD samples, and 4 UC samples were sent for histology. OCT images were rescaled such that pixel size in the x, y, and z directions were the same size. Then, the OCT image set was matched to its corresponding H&E image qualitatively. H&E was used to assess the accuracy and specificity of OCT to differentiating features.

Statistical analysis

The statistical comparison of ring power between normal and inflamed samples was completed using a two-tailed student’s t-test. Results were considered significant when p < 0.05.

Results

Normal tissue

After resection, samples were placed in RPMI and transported to the research lab for OCT imaging. The sample was further cut to fit a modified histology cassette, and 3D OCT datasets were obtained with a benchtop system through the circular imaging window. Normal samples and samples from UC and CD cases were obtained, where IBD samples were taken from patients with active disease status.

These villous structures are visualized in the en face view in Fig. 1A-D, where Fig. 1A shows a large field of view of many of these structures, and Fig. 1B zooms in on an area of interest. Within the H&E stain, different cell groups are identified, including epithelial cells and a cross section of the lamina propria. Figure 1C shows a large field of view OCT en face projection image, and Fig. 1D zooms into an area of interest within this image. Similar to the H&E-stained sample, OCT differentiates cells in the epithelial layer from cells in the lamia propria.

Just as in the normal ileum tissue, normal colon tissue contains organized layered structures which can be visualized in the cross-sectional view. Figure 2E shows an example of an H&E-stained cross-section of a normal colon, where the mucosa, muscularis mucosae, and submucosa can be visualized. This is correlated with the OCT image in Fig. 2F, where these same layers can be differentiated. The mucosa consists of long crypt structures in the OCT image. Individual crypt structures can be resolved, and each crypt takes on relatively the same size and shape. In the OCT image, the mucosa appears thicker than in the H&E image, likely due to the effect of tissue fixation and processing for histology. Supplementary Video 2 displays the entire volume of the normal colon.

In both normal ileum and colon tissue, the muscularis mucosae presents as a thin, brighter band of tissue under the mucosa that is thin enough it does not greatly attenuate the imaging beam. Submucosa can also be visualized beneath the muscularis mucosae at a depth of up to approximately 1 mm. The submucosa in normal colon and ileum samples appears largely similar to each other in both H&E and OCT images. Submucosa is visualized in the OCT image with sparse, almost circular structures, representing the connective tissue found in this layer. OCT can also visualize inhomogeneities in the thickness of the connective tissue of the submucosa.

In the OCT image, the mucosal layer can be observed, but it attenuates the light so greatly that the submucosal layer cannot be resolved. Spatial frequency analysis

We utilized a 2D fast Fourier transform (2D FFT) to analyze spatial frequency in en face projection images. In IBD colon, the mucosal structure becomes disrupted, resulting in changes to the en face crypt pattern in OCT images. Figure 6A displays an example OCT en face projection of the mucosal layer in normal colon. Here, there is good contrast between crypts with higher pixel grayscale values and the darker background. As noted earlier, in the normal colon, the crypts are well organized with similar spacing and sizes. In Fig. 6B-C, the crypt structure in samples from CD and UC are distorted. The en face projections of the mucosal layer from these tissues display crypts that are less uniform and have grayscale values that are closer to the background.

The well-organized pattern in normal tissue results in a 2D FFT pattern that contains a ring-like structure around the center, shown in Fig. 6D with the white dotted circle. In inflamed samples, the 2D FFT pattern does not contain the ring band around the center as is displayed in normal tissue due to lack of an organized crypt structure (Fig. 6E-F). When average radial profiles were taken, the intensity profile from normal tissue could be distinguished from both CD and UC tissues by the presence of a higher power spatial frequency band, shown in Fig. 6G-H. However, CD and UC tissues could not be distinguished from each other from these intensity profiles.

Through group analysis, we show how the 2D FFT can be used to quantitatively differentiate normal (n = 5) and inflamed tissue (n = 5). The mean radial power spectral density (PSD) can be fit using an inverse function. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows each sample with its respective line fitting. In normal samples where a uniform and repeated crypt pattern is present, the ring band will appear above the fitted line. To compare normal and inflamed tissue, we defined “Ring power” as resultant mean from the positively valued result of the subtracted fit line from the mean radial PSD. This mean was taken from the minimum sampling frequency, 0.7 cycles/mm, to the maximum sampling frequency where the group mean radial PSDs intersected (Supplementary Fig. 2), 7.5 cycles/mm. Normal samples had higher ring power (p = 0.02), representing more positive deviation from the fit line in the crypt spatial frequency range.

Discussion

Our study shows the promise of high-resolution OCT to visualize both small details and overall structures in three-dimensions to lend to a differentiation between normal ileal and colonic tissue and those from patients with CD or UC. The high resolution of our systems allows visualization of small structures, down to individual epithelial cells in the mucosal crypts in normal tissue. The overall crypt structure can also be visualized. In normal tissues, it appears ordered, with crypts of relatively the same size. In UC and CD tissues, crypt organization is highly disrupted with crypts in the OCT en face projection having different sizes and shapes. In CD, we also analyze samples from the ileum, which has not been previously studied with OCT.

From a cross-sectional view, disruptions can be observed between normal and inflamed tissues in OCT images. In normal tissues, layered structures are well defined to differentiate the mucosa, muscularis mucosa, and upper portion of the submucosa. In ileum and colonic tissue from patients with CD, disruption in the mucosal layer structure can be observed in en face images from the ileum and the colon. In the normal ileum, the mucosal structure appears very mosaic, with defined boundaries between villous structures. In the cross-sectional view, individual villous structures can be identified. In the CD ileum sample, it is harder to differentiate individual villous structures in both views. Similarly in the normal colon, mucosal structures are well defined, but in the CD colon sample, they become disrupted, and horizontal layered structures can be found below the mucosal layer, which may indicate disruption in the submucosa.

In OCT imaging of the UC sample, the mucosal layer is largely visible, and crypt abscesses, which are more common in UC than CD, can be identified in this layer. In the mucosal layer, it is difficult to identify normally organized crypt structures in the cross-sectional view, which translates to crypt structures that are different sizes and shapes in the en face view. In this sample, however, the muscularis mucosae and submucosa were not visible, due to the strong attenuation of light by the mucosal layer.

Normal and inflamed tissue can be further differentiated using a 2D FFT. The disruptions in the mucosal layer due to inflammation result in overall distortion in crypt structure. Though this can be viewed in the cross-section, where in inflamed tissue the crypts are less uniform and the layer thickness may change, OCT affords the ability to view tissue additionally in the en face direction. Here, crypts can be visualized as evenly spaced, uniform circular structures in normal colonic tissue. In inflamed tissue, the size and spacing between the crypts changes, which results in changes to the spatial frequency pattern. By using a 2D FFT, the changes in spatial frequency can be further exemplified, where in uniform normal tissue, the 2D FFT pattern results in a ring around the center spectra, owed to the regular and repeated frequency pattern the crypts appear in. In 2D FFT spectra from CD and UC samples, the ring around the center spectra does not appear due to the distortion of the crypt structures in the mucosal layer. The mean radial PSDs could be fit with an inverse power function. By analyzing the difference between the mean radial PSD and the fit for each sample, the power from the ring pattern in the normal samples could be isolated and quantified, providing a sample-specific metric to quantitatively differentiate normal and inflamed tissues.

Our study provides visualization of normal and inflamed datasets with a large field of view (FOV), achieved by stitching together adjacent smaller FOVs (2.8 × 2.9 mm each). Our 1300 nm system operates with a comparatively high lateral (3.9 μm) and axial resolutions (4.0 μm in tissue) to other implementations of spectral-domain OCT, such as micro-OCT (axial resolution of 1–2 μm in tissue)19,21. When considering clinical translation, our study demonstrates the potential of OCT as a method for volumetric tissue analysis if implemented in a high-speed manner, such as through space-division multiplexing33.

The extent of inflammation can be used to differentiate between CD and UC, where in CD, there is more transmural inflammation present deeper to the submucosal layer. Thus, visualization of as much of the submucosa as possible is critical to the adoption of OCT as a diagnostic imaging tool for IBD. OCT still remains limited by depth penetration, as the entirety of the submucosal layer cannot be visualized. Absorbing dyes such as tartrazine, ampyrone, and indocyanine green (ICG) have recently been reported to offer reversable optical clearing, which may improve OCT imaging depth in colonic tissues34–36. In our study, we found that OCT alone did not have high enough sensitivity to differentiate tissue with and without fibrosis in the submucosal layer.

Future studies may go beyond structural OCT alone to perform dynamic contrast imaging to obtain information about tissue activity or integrate optical coherence elastography to obtain information about tissue mechanical properties. Improved axial resolution, provided through implementations such as micro-OCT, may provide more information about disruptions to crypt architecture and organization of goblet cells. Additionally, a multimodal system could combine OCT with other imaging modalities, such as brightfield imaging or Raman spectroscopy to provide additional contrast.

In summary, we used OCT to perform label-free assessment of fresh normal, CD, and UC small intestine and colonic tissues. We found that OCT could visualize disruptions in normal structures, further distinguished in 2D FFTs of the en face images of the mucosal layer. This work informs future development of imaging technologies that will work to differentiate CD from UC, with higher sensitivity and specificity, with the goal of differentiating inflammation from fibrosis completely non-invasively to tissue.
