Transcranial direct current stimulation as an augmentation therapy in patients with obsessive-compulsive disorder: A case series
Authors: Ipsita Basu, Srikrishna Prasad Panda, Prateek Yadav
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterized by intrusive, distressing thoughts and/or repetitive behaviors. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a promising neuromodulation technique for augmenting pharmacotherapy in OCD. This case series identified patients diagnosed with OCD, who showed residual symptoms even after two adequate trials of pharmacotherapy corroborated using the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) (score of eight and above taken as cutoff). tDCS was given in the form of two milliamperes current with anode at SMA and cathode at the right orbitofrontal area, for twenty minutes, for six sessions (one session per day) in addition to pharmacotherapy. Clinical assessment was performed using the Y BOCS at baseline and post treatment. The results revealed a reduction in OCD symptom severity in all the patients (as per both subjective report and Y-BOCS scores). The patients did not have any severe adverse effects. tDCS appears to be a potential augmentation therapy for individuals with OCD. However, further research is warranted to optimize stimulation parameters and elucidate the long-term effects of tDCS as part of a comprehensive treatment approach for OCD.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) consists of repetitive, intrusive thoughts, images, impulses, and repetitive behaviors performed in response, to relieve resultant anxiety.[1] First-line pharmacotherapy for OCD is high-dose serotonergic drugs, rarely needing augmentation with antipsychotics, antiepileptics, or glutamate modulators.[2] For refractory cases, various noninvasive neuromodulation techniques, including electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) have been used to augment treatment, with promising results. However, because of a lack of adequate evidence, tDCS is still not accepted into mainstream clinical practice. This series was conceptualized to study tDCS in the Treatment-resistant OCD. A dysfunction in parallel and partially segregated corticostriatothalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuits and front-limbic circuits has been implicated in cognitive, affective, and behavioral symptoms of OCD, although in functional neuroimaging, both hyperactivity and hypoactivity have been seen in various brain areas.[3] Therefore, various modes of tDCS have been tried. However, most RCTs have shown positive results with anode at supplementary motor area (SMA)/pre-SMA and cathode at right orbitofrontal/supraorbital area.[4] tDCS delivers one to two milliamperes current through a portable device with conducting electrodes placed on the scalp to either stimulate (through anode) or inhibit (through cathode) certain brain areas, depending on the disease targeted, using the adaptive neuroplasticity of the human brain. It is safe and well-tolerated unanimously, with only occasional adverse effects in the form of local reactions and headaches. It is inexpensive and easy to use and may even be used by patients at home with precontrolled settings.[5,6] In this series, tDCS was given as per the protocol of two milliamperes current for twenty minutes, with anode at the supplementary motor area[7] and cathode at the right orbitofrontal area,[3,8] for six sessions (one session per day) on consecutive days, in addition to ongoing pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy.

CASE REPORTS

Case 1

Forty-year-old male, on tab fluoxetine 80 mg/day and tab clomipramine 75 mg/day, along with psychotherapy, for around two years, continued to have obsessive symptoms in the form of persistent recurrent intrusive thoughts about his hands being dirty, compeling him to repeatedly wash his hands or perform spitting rituals whenever he would be in a situation where hand washing was not possible, to relieve the distressing anxiety symptoms, also gave a history of occasional difficulty in focusing on tasks and would commit minor mistakes such as in counting items, or forgetting tasks assigned at the workplace because of his preoccupation with obsessive thoughts, secondary to which he had depressive symptoms and passive death wishes. His baseline Y-BOCS score was 24 (obsessive subscore-12 and compulsive subscore-12). Repeat Y-BOCS score, after completion of tDCS protocol as outlined above, was 5 (obsessive subscore-3 and compulsive subscore-2). He reported a tingling sensation in the area of electrode placement for the first two days, after which he had no further adverse effects. He was continued on the same treatment, managed as an in-patient owing to the risk of harm to self, and after discharge, was kept on regular follow-up.

Case 2

A thirty-six-year-old man, a diagnosed case of OCD (refractory) and schizophrenia (in remission) since the last fifteen years, on regular treatment with tab clozapine 200 mg/day as well as cap fluoxetine 60 mg/day, reported persistent thoughts that surfaces were dirty, and might spoil his hands if touched, compeling him to repeatedly wash his hands. He also had secondary depressive symptoms because of the above. He was managed in the outpatient department because of no risk of harm. The initial Y-BOCS score was 20 (obsessive subscore-12 and compulsive subscore-8). Repeat Y-BOCS score after completion of tDCS protocol was 4 (obsessive subscore-2 and compulsive subscore-2). He had a mild headache after the second session but had no other adverse effects.

Case 3

A seventeen-year-old boy, diagnosed case of OCD since the last five years, on tab sertraline 100 mg/day and clomipramine 150 mg/day, who had persistent doubt that he must not have read a line he had already read, compeling him to read the same line again and again. He would also feel that his hands were dirty even after washing them and would thus have to repeat the hand-washing multiple times, despite the treatment, causing difficulty in his board examination preparation, leading to significant distress. He was planned for tDCS after taking assent from him and consent from his parents. Y-BOCS score was 23 (obsessive subscore-12 and compulsive subscore-11) before starting, and 11 (obsessive subscore-5 and compulsive subscore-6) after completion of the tDCS protocol. He was managed as an outpatient, continuing on previous treatment, and kept on follow-up. He had a tingling sensation on his scalp after administration, which spontaneously resolved within a few hours. After the six days of tDCS, the patient was able to focus on his board examinations and kept performing better in academics, which he was previously unable to do earlier.

Case 4

A twenty-five-year-old man, on treatment for OCD and severe depressive episode without psychotic symptoms, having consulted multiple psychiatrists over the past four years without relief in symptoms, presented with persistent thoughts that he had not heard something spoken to him and being compelled to ask for things to be repeated; thoughts that his hands were unclean and being compelled to wash them repeatedly; and thoughts that he had not carried out a task properly, and thus being compelled to repeat the tasks over and over again. This led him to quit multiple jobs as he was unable to perform any task without having to repeat it. He was started on tab fluvoxamine 100mg/day, uptitrated to 200mg/day, and tab clomipramine 75mg/day was added when there was inadequate response and further uptitrated to 150mg/day. However, the patient reported minimal improvement even after three months of the above and also developed active suicidal ideations. He was admitted and taken up for tDCS. Y-BOCS score at baseline was 25 (obsessive subscore-13 and compulsive subscore-12) and, after completion of the tDCS protocol, was 8 (obsessive subscore-3 and compulsive subscore-5). The patient was kept on regular follow-up, and he finally took up a job and has been functioning well adequately.

Case 5

A thirty-four-year-old man, on treatment for OCD for three years, who had persistent obsessional ruminations in the form of endless indecisive or quasi-philosophical ruminations about trivial and unimportant details, and compulsive asking and reassurance seeking, despite taking tab clomipramine 75mg/day and cap fluoxetine 80mg/day since the last three years, leading to considerable distress and passive death wishes. He was managed as an inpatient because of the risk of harm to himself and was planned for tDCS. Y-BOCS score was 32 (obsessive subscore-20 and compulsive subscore-12) before starting and 17 (obsessive subscore-9 and compulsive subscore-8) after completion of the tDCS protocol. The patient had a global headache associated with a burning sensation on the region of electrode placement after the first session, which subsided on administering tab paracetamol 500mg stat. He had no other adverse effect. Was kept on regular follow-ups, and he was able to resume writing his book and was motivated to publish it. His medications were continued as usual.

DISCUSSION

This case series identified patients diagnosed with OCD, who showed residual symptoms even after two adequate trials with serotonergic drugs as well as psychotherapy, corroborated using Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) (with less than 35% improvement taken to mean resistance).[9] The results revealed a reduction in OCD symptom severity by at least 35% in all the patients (as per both subjective report and Y-BOCS scores). The only adverse effects reported were headache and transient local irritation at the electrode sites. This is consistent with the reports of previous studies which have also shown the fast, safe, and efficient effect that tDCS has. This case series, in line with previous studies, has confirmed the efficacy of the position of electrodes in the form of the cathode being on the right orbitofrontal area, and the anode being in the supplementary motor area, this montage being derived from the theory that OCD symptoms are associated with increased neuronal activity in the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and decreased neuronal activity in the supplementary and presupplementary motor area (pre-SMA) responsible for inhibitory control.[10,11] Other montages that have been used include bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, cerebellum, and single electrode cathodal/anodal stimulation at SMA/pre-SMA.

CONCLUSION

This case series studied the use of transcranial direct current stimulation on patients with treatment-resistant OCD as an augmentation to pharmacotherapy, and it seems to be a potential augmentation therapy for individuals with OCD. However, further research is warranted to optimize stimulation parameters and elucidate the long-term effects of tDCS as part of a comprehensive treatment approach for OCD.
