Examining the associations among adults’ physical activity, serum cotinine, and the risk of metabolic syndrome
Authors: Xiao Hou, Zan Gao, Tingting Sun, Xiaosheng Dong, Liya Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction/objectiveThis study aims to assess the associations among tobacco exposure, confirmed through serum cotinine (SC) levels, physical activity (PA), and the likelihood of developing metabolic syndrome (MetS).MethodsThis study involved 8,423 adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning years 2007 to 2018. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate the associations among SC, PA and the risk of MetS with its components. We also investigated the sex differences on these outcome variables via analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Chi-square test.ResultsThe covariate-adjusted logistic regression analysis results suggest that SC concentration (unit: ng/mL) was significantly and positively associated with MetS (OR: 6.41, 95%CI: 5.81–7.08), abdominal obesity (OR: 1.92, 95%CI: 1.75–2.11), hypertension (OR: 2.70, 95%CI: 2.46–2.96), hyperglycemia (OR: 12.50, 95%CI: 10.63–14.71), hypertriglyceridemia (OR: 2.10, 95%CI: 1.90–2.33), and low serum high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) (OR: 1.61, 95%CI: 1.46–1.77). PA energy consumption (unit: MET*h/wk) was significantly and inversely associated with MetS (OR: 0.56, 95%CI: 0.51–0.61), abdominal obesity (OR: 0.71, 95%CI: 0.65–0.78), hypertension (OR: 0.55, 95%CI: 0.50–0.60), hyperglycemia (OR: 0.59, 95%CI: 0.52–0.67), and hypertriglyceridemia (OR: 0.84, 95%CI: 0.76–0.93). For males, an increase of approximately 6 MET*h/wk in PA energy consumption was negatively associated with the higher odds of MetS linked with a 1 ng/mL higher SC level. Similarly, for females, the corresponding estimate was 7 MET*h/wk.ConclusionsThis study indicates a positive relationship between SC concentration and MetS risk, whereas higher PA energy expenditure was inversely associated with MetS risk. Moreover, the findings indicate a potential compensatory association between PA and SC levels in relation to MetS risk. These estimates are based on statistical modeling and should be viewed as illustrative approximations rather than definitive causal or prescriptive thresholds. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to confirm these relationships.Keywords: Compensatory association, Dose-response association, Metabolic syndrome, Physical activity, Serum cotinine

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a clinical condition characterized by the disruption of metabolic processes involving proteins, fats, carbohydrates, and other bodily substances. The National Cholesterol Education Program-Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III) identifies the key features of MetS as abdominal obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and reduced high-density lipoprotein (low HDL) [1]. According to data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) collected between 2011 and 2016, it is estimated that around 34.7% of the adult population in the United States is affected by MetS [2, 3]. The growing prevalence and mortality associated with MetS have elevated it to a significant public health challenge worldwide, garnering increasing scrutiny for its risks. MetS is known to be a precursor to various health issues, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and kidney and liver disorders [4, 5]. Considering the profound impact of MetS and its components on cardiovascular diseases and a range of cancers, extensive investigations have been undertaken to explore the factors influencing MetS, encompassing both risk and protective elements [6–9].

Recent research within the academic sphere has highlighted tobacco exposure as a significant lifestyle-related factor contributing to MetS, in addition to factors like diet and sleep [10]. Smoking is recognized as a primary factor in premature mortality and is associated with a range of diseases including coronary heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, bronchitis, and lung cancer [11–13]. Both direct and indirect tobacco exposure have been identified as causal factors in the onset of MetS [14]. Research studies consistently indicate a link between smoking and a heightened risk of MetS [15–17]. A study by Miyatake et al. on 3,177 Japanese individuals aged 20–79 years old revealed a significantly higher incidence of smoking among those with MetS compared to those without [16]. Additionally, a comprehensive meta-analysis conducted in China, which included an aggregate of 56,691 participants across various cohort studies, found a notable positive relationship between smoking and MetS risk (overall relative risk [RR]: 1.26, 95%CI: 1.10–1.44) [17]. Nicotine, a key harmful component in tobacco, is known to trigger MetS. It activates the sympathetic nervous system and raises cortisol levels, leading to the accumulation of visceral fat and subsequent disruption of lipid metabolism. Additionally, nicotine is implicated in the development of MetS by promoting insulin resistance [18].

In recent epidemiological studies, data on individual tobacco exposure and semi-quantitative estimates of such exposure are commonly gathered through questionnaire surveys. These surveys offer the benefits of time, cost, and labor efficiency. However, they present challenges as respondents might struggle to accurately recall their smoking habits, potentially leading to biases in assessing tobacco exposure and its biological effects [19, 20]. Recognizing passive smoking exposure is also problematic for survey participants. A study utilizing data from the NHANES revealed that 72% of individuals who reported no passive tobacco exposure still had detectable serum cotinine levels, a primary metabolite of nicotine [21]. This indicates that relying solely on self-reported smoking status might result in misclassification of passive smokers as non-exposed to tobacco. Serum cotinine (SC) is a key metabolite of nicotine and is considered one of the most reliable biochemical markers for measuring nicotine levels in the blood [22]. Nicotine, the primary component in tobacco, is metabolized in the liver by the cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP2A6, converting 70–80% of nicotine into cotinine [23]. Cotinine has a half-life of approximately 18 h, and due to its high sensitivity and specificity, it is a more favored biomarker than nicotine, which has a much shorter half-life [24].

Beyond tobacco exposure, insufficient PA is another crucial behavioral factor contributing to MetS. A study by Honda et al., which conducted a 3-year follow-up on 430 office employees aged 40–64, identified a strong link between prolonged sedentary behavior and the onset of MetS (hazard ratios [HR]: 2.85, 95%CI: 1.33–6.18) [25]. Furthermore, other research has highlighted the benefits of increased PA in lowering the risk of MetS and related health conditions [26, 27]. Specifically, heightened aerobic PA (40% – 80% VO2max, for 150–170 min per week, over a period of 20 weeks to 3.2 years) has been observed to decrease the prevalence of MetS by 23%–47% among physically inactive older adults [28]. PA effectively enhances metabolism by elevating the resting metabolic rate, improving blood lipid profiles, reducing blood pressure, and increasing insulin sensitivity [29]. As such, PA is a vital non-pharmacological approach for the prevention and treatment of MetS and its associated complications. However, the effectiveness of PA in reducing the risk of MetS among individuals exposed to tobacco remains understudied.

This research aims to examine SC and PA data from the NHANES database covering the period from 2007 to 2018. The goal is to assess the correlations among tobacco exposure, PA, and MetS. Furthermore, the study seeks to quantitatively determine if PA can mitigate the adverse effects of SC on MetS. By establishing the precise relationships, this analysis intends to offer insights for crafting tailored PA recommendations for individuals with varying levels of tobacco exposure.

Materials and methods

Study population

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is an initiative designed to evaluate the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. It is conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics and the Health and Nutrition Examination Survey Department under the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Each year, NHANES recruits approximately 5,000 non-hospitalized civilians from across the US, using a complex, multi-stage probability sampling method. Participants initially undergo health interviews at home, which are followed by clinical assessments, dietary interviews, and physical examinations at a mobile examination center (MEC) [30]. The survey encompasses a range of data, including social demographics, physical examination results (like blood pressure, hearing tests), laboratory data (such as blood sugar, blood lipids), and questionnaire responses (covering prescription drug use, health status, etc.) [30, 31].

Our study focused on adults aged 20 and older who completed physical activity surveys and had measurements taken for SC and MetS-related indicators. We excluded those with incomplete covariate data and individuals diagnosed with cancer. The research protocols for all NHANES studies have received approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board. Ultimately, this analysis incorporated a total of 8,423 adult participants (Fig. 1).

Measurements of SC and PA

SC levels were measured using isotope dilution-high-performance liquid chromatography/atmospheric pressure chemical ionization tandem mass spectrometry (ID HPLC-APCI MS/MS), providing an accurate representation of environmental tobacco exposure [32, 33]. For detailed information about the SC measurement process, please refer to the NHANES study [34, 35]. We adopted the updated cutoff point of 3 ng/ml, as recommended by Benowitz et al., to distinguish between individuals exposed to tobacco and those not exposed [22].

In the study, participants were inquired about their engagement in moderate-intensity physical activity (MPA) and vigorous-intensity physical activity (VPA) related to work (PAQ610, PAQ615, PAQ625, and PAD630) and recreational activities (PAQ655, PAD660, PAQ670, and PAD675), including walking and bicycling (PAQ640 and PAD645), during an average week. The intensity of these physical activities was measured in Metabolic Equivalents of Task (METs). One MET is defined as the energy expenditure at rest in a seated position, equivalent to a consumption of 3.5 ml of oxygen per kg of body weight per minute. The NHANES assesses physical activity using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [36]. For the purpose of this analysis, the following MET intensities were applied: walking and bicycling = 4 METs, MPA = 4.0 METs, and VPA = 8.0 METs. The total weekly physical activity energy expenditure (expressed in MET-hours/week, or MET*h/wk) is calculated as the sum of energy expended in walking, bicycling, MPA, and VPA. The specific formulas used are outlined below.

The latest World Health Organization (WHO) PA guideline recommend that adults should do at least 150–300 min of MPA, or at least 75–150 min of VPA, or an equivalent combination of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activity throughout the week for substantial health benefits [37]. It means that the PA energy consumption for adults should be a minimum of 10–20 MET*h/wk. We utilized the guideline-recommended minimum threshold of PA energy consumption (i.e., 10 MET*h/wk) to separate sufficient-PA individuals from insufficient-PA individuals.

The definition and components of MetS

Participants were diagnosed as patients with MetS, if three or more of the following NCEP-ATP III criteria for MetS were met [1]: (1) waist circumference (WC) ≥ 102 cm in males or ≥ 88 cm in females; (2) serum triglyceride (TG) ≥ 1.7 mmol/l; (3) serum high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) < 1.03mmol/l in males and < 1.29 mmol/l in females; (4) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 85 mmHg, receiving anti-hypertensive treatment, or diagnosed with hypertension by a doctor; (5) fasting glucose (FG) ≥ 100 mg/dL, receiving antihyperglycemic treatments, taking insulin, or being diagnosed with diabetes. Among these indices, the information on anti-hypertensive, anti-diabetic drugs and disease diagnosis were self-reported and collected from the questionnaire in NHANES. Other indices were obtained by clinical tests.

The determination of covariates

In this study, the covariates taken into account were age, body mass index (BMI, measured in kg/m^2), gender, ethnicity, education levels, the period of the NHANES survey, sedentary behavior times (SBT), and total energy intake (TEI). We used the TEI values as calculated by the National Center for Health Statistics, which are included in the NHANES dataset [38]. The collection of food consumption data (energy intake) in NHANES was carried out using the automated multiple-pass method (AMPM) [38]. Each participant in NHANES was asked to provide a dietary recall. The first recall occurred at the mobile examination center (MEC), and the second was conducted via telephone 3 to 10 days later. The average of the two days’ recalls for each individual was used to determine their average daily TEI. Among these covariates, age, BMI, and SBT were treated as continuous variables. The other covariates were categorized as follows: gender (male, female), ethnicity (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and others), education levels (less than 9th grade, 9-11th grade, high school graduate, some college or AA degree, college graduate or above), and TEI (low: males < 2000 kcal/day, females < 1600 kcal/day; adequate: males 2000–3000 kcal/day, females 1600–2400 kcal/day; high: males >3000 kcal/day, females >2400 kcal/day) [39].

Statistical analyses

The mean (standard deviation, SD) and median (interquartile range, IQR) were calculated for continuous variables (e.g., age, BMI, SBT, WC, SBP, DBP, FG, TG, HDL-C). Given the right-skewed distributions of SC and PA, both variables were transformed using the natural logarithm as log (SC + 0.01) and log (PA + 0.01), respectively. These transformed variables were used in subsequent regression analyses. All categorical variables (e.g., gender, ethnicity, education levels, TEI) were described in frequencies (n) and percentage (%). The analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Chi-square test were conducted to evaluate the trends in continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

Logistic regression analysis was used to examine the associations between exposures and the risk of MetS. Two models were constructed: Model 1, unadjusted; and Model 2, adjusted for age, gender, race, BMI, total energy intake (TEI), sedentary behavior time (SBT), and either SC (for PA models) or PA (for SC models), based on their known associations with metabolic syndrome and their potential to confound the examined relationships. To assess multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIFs) were calculated for all independent variables in the logistic regression models, with a VIF > 5 considered indicative of potentially concerning collinearity. The logistic regression coefficients represent changes in the log-odds of MetS, and odds ratios (ORs) were calculated by exponentiating these coefficients (i.e., OR = exp (coefficient)). We additionally performed sensitivity analyses by excluding BMI from the covariate set to evaluate potential overadjustment.

The SC and PA were divided into quartiles as four levels (i.e., Q1, Q2, Q3 and Q4) and the outcomes of MetS were regarded as diagnosed MetS or undiagnosed MetS. The subjects in the Q1 group were determined as the reference group. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate the dose-response associations and to explore the potential compensatory relationship among SC, PA, and the risk of MetS and its components. In addition to abdominal obesity, BMI was included as a covariate to capture overall adiposity, given its distinct physiological relevance as an established risk factor for metabolic dysregulation [40]. A P value less than 0.05 represented a statistically significant difference between compared data sets. All data were analyzed using Statistical Package of the Social Sciences (SPSS version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All analyses were conducted using NHANES complex survey design procedures, incorporating sampling weights, stratification (SDMVSTRA), and clustering (SDMVPSU), to ensure accurate estimates of variance and confidence intervals. To assess potential non-linearity, restricted cubic spline models were fitted for log-transformed SC and PA levels in relation to MetS risk. For categorical analyses, the lowest quartile (Q1) or the group representing lower exposure or guideline adherence (e.g., SC ≤ 3 ng/mL, PA < 10 MET*h/wk) was used as the reference category, unless otherwise specified. To account for multiple comparisons across the five MetS components and subgroup analyses, the false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, with the significance threshold set at Q < 0.05.

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, this study finally included 8423 subjects, of which 3132 patients (37.2%) diagnosed as MetS. The participants with MetS in the study included 1,470 males (average age 54.3 ± 15.5 years) and 1,662 females (average age 54.1 ± 15.8 years), representing 17.5% and 19.7% of the total sample size (8,423 subjects), respectively. Associations between SC and MetS (components)

Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no concerning collinearity among independent variables in the logistic regression models. All VIF values ranged from 1.001 to 1.166, well below the commonly accepted threshold of 5. The initial univariate logistic regression analysis (Model 1), which did not adjusted for other covariates, evaluated the association between log-transformed SC levels and MetS as presented in Table 2. To reduce skewness, SC was transformed using ln (SC + 0.01), and the resulting values were categorized into four quartiles. For each quartile of log-transformed SC, the median and IQR were calculated to better characterize the distribution within groups, as follows: Q1 median − 4.6031 (IQR: −4.6035 to − 4.6027), Q2 median − 4.5999 (IQR: −4.6013 to − 4.5977), Q3 median − 4.4768 (IQR: −4.5750 to − 3.4835), and Q4 median − 1.3168 (IQR: −1.6412 to − 1.0134). Notably, the median and IQR in Q1 and Q2 were nearly identical, reflecting a clustering of low SC values near the detection limit. Compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the odds of MetS were significantly higher in Q2 (OR: 2.30, 95% CI: 2.03–2.61), Q3 (OR: 10.02, 95% CI: 8.69–11.56), and Q4 (OR: 21.71, 95% CI: 18.24–25.83).

After adjustment for age, gender, race, BMI, TEI, SBT, and PA in Model 2, these associations remained significant. Compared with Q1, the odds of MetS were higher in Q2 (OR: 2.25, 95% CI: 1.98–2.55), Q3 (OR: 9.58, 95% CI: 8.29–11.07), and Q4 (OR: 20.84, 95% CI: 17.48–24.85). In sensitivity analyses excluding BMI from the covariate set, the associations between SC and MetS remained materially unchanged. Compared with Q1, the odds of MetS were higher in Q2 (OR: 2.29, 95% CI: 2.02–2.60), Q3 (OR: 9.88, 95% CI: 8.56–11.41), and Q4 (OR: 21.44, 95% CI: 17.99–25.56), which were nearly the same as those observed in the main model. Furthermore, dose-response analysis indicated that each SD increase in log-transformed SC levels (SD = 1.43) was associated with an approximately 2.50-fold increase in the risk of MetS (OR: 2.50, 95% CI: 2.38–2.63, P < 0.001). The restricted cubic spline analyses did not reveal significant departures from linearity for the associations between log-transformed SC and MetS risk, supporting the use of log-linear models in the primary analyses.

We divided the participants into tobacco-exposed individuals and non-tobacco-exposed individuals based upon the newly recommended SC cut-off point of 3 ng/ml. The results showed that SC was significantly positive-associated with MetS (OR: 6.41, 95%CI: 5.81–7.08), abdominal obesity (OR: 1.92, 95%CI: 1.75–2.11), hypertension (OR: 2.70, 95%CI: 2.46–2.96), hyperglycemia (OR:12.50, 95%CI: 10.63–14.71), hypertriglyceridemia (OR: 2.10, 95%CI: 1.90–2.33), and low HDL-C (OR: 1.61, 95%CI: 1.46–1.77) (Fig. 2). All associations reported for SC with MetS and its individual components remained statistically significant after controlling for the false discovery rate at Q < 0.05 using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Associations between PA and MetS (components)

Logistic regression analysis was performed to examine the association between log-transformed PA levels and MetS, as presented in Table 3. PA was transformed as ln (PA + 0.01) to reduce skewness and subsequently categorized into quartiles. For each quartile of log-transformed PA, the median and IQR were calculated to better characterize the distribution within groups, as follows: Q1 median 1.1019 (IQR: 0.6981 to 1.3888), Q2 median 2.3036 (IQR: 1.8986 to 2.6398), Q3 median 3.6378 (IQR: 3.2962 to 3.9892), and Q4 median 5.1240 (IQR: 4.7186 to 5.5854). Notably, the distribution of log-transformed PA values was more evenly spread across quartiles compared to SC, with less clustering at the lower end. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), compared with the lowest quartile of PA (Q1), the odds of MetS were significantly lower in Q2 (OR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.75–0.96), Q3 (OR: 0.61, 95% CI: 0.53–0.69), and Q4 (OR: 0.47, 95% CI: 0.42–0.53).

Discussion

Drawing from a comprehensive and representative cross-sectional survey of adults in the United States, we quantified the complex relationships among PA, SC, and MetS, offering new insights into how PA may be linked with MetS risk in the context of tobacco exposure. Our findings reveal a significant positive dose-response association between SC levels and the risk of MetS, alongside a significantly inverse association between PA energy expenditure and the risk of MetS. Additionally, our results suggest that higher levels of PA energy expenditure were positively associated with lower MetS risk among individuals with elevated SC levels, suggesting a potential compensatory relationship derived from regression modeling. To our knowledge, this study is among the few to explore such a potential statistical association between PA and SC in relation to MetS risk.

In this study, we utilized SC concentration as an indicator of individual tobacco exposure and established that SC levels are significantly positively associated with the risk of MetS and its components, including abdominal obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and low HDL-C, even after adjusting for relevant covariates. The observed positive link between tobacco exposure and MetS risk could stem from several mechanisms. A widely accepted theory is that SC elevates insulin resistance, thereby increasing MetS risk [41, 42]. Tobacco exposure is thought to decrease insulin sensitivity by elevating insulin-antagonizing hormones (like catecholamine, cortisol, and growth hormone) and promoting lipolysis, which leads to higher levels of serum free fatty acids [43–45]. This increase in serum free fatty acids can impair muscle and liver function, reducing hepatic insulin extraction and resulting in elevated fasting glucose levels [42, 46–48]. Persistently high fasting glucose can cause hyperglycemia, a key component of MetS.

Additionally, SC can contribute to abdominal obesity by disrupting fat metabolism [49], thus elevating MetS risk. Tobacco exposure is linked to increased activity in gluteal adipose tissue lipoprotein lipase, enhancing the uptake and storage of triglyceride fatty acids in adipocytes and consequently increasing fat mass [50]. Prior research has indicated that smokers, compared to non-smokers, tend to have a more metabolically adverse fat distribution profile, characterized by higher central adiposity [51]. Another mechanism suggests that nicotine, a component of SC, can induce lipolysis, raise TG levels, and reduce the synthesis of HDL-C [52–54]. Moreover, nicotine can stimulate the heart and adrenal glands to release large amounts of catecholamines, leading to an accelerated heart rate, vasoconstriction, and increased blood pressure [54]. Vallée et al. found that higher cigarette consumption correlates with increased hypertension among smokers [55], indirectly supporting our findings.

Our study revealed that physical activity (PA) energy consumption is inversely associated with metabolic syndrome (MetS) and its various components, such as abdominal obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and low HDL-C. PA, being a primary form of energy expenditure, is essential for maintaining energy balance and controlling body weight [56], effectively preventing abdominal obesity. In the context of hypertension, PA has been shown to lower blood pressure [57], attributed to its role in enhancing vascular elasticity, reducing the synthesis of vascular endothelin [58], and inhibiting the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) [59, 60]. Regarding hyperglycemia, PA is beneficial as it facilitates blood glucose consumption by lowering insulin resistance [61, 62]. Studies, such as those by Manson et al., have demonstrated the impact of PA on reducing diabetes risk [63]. Regular PA also positively affects lipid profiles by increasing HDL-C and decreasing triglycerides, thereby diminishing the risk of MetS. A linear dose-response relationship exists between increased exercise intensity and distance and the elevation of HDL-C levels [64]. In summary, PA is recognized as a protective factor against multiple components of MetS. The inverse relationship between PA and the risk of diagnosed MetS has been supported by various studies. For example, research by Laaksonen et al. in a prospective cohort study highlighted the significantly lower odds ratio of MetS in men engaging in moderate to vigorous physical activity compared to those with less frequent PA involvement, after controlling for major confounders [65]. This finding strongly supports our conclusion about the negative association between total PA energy consumption and the risk of MetS.

Our study not only explores the associations between SC or PA and the risk of MetS but also highlights a potential compensatory relationship suggested by statistical modeling. As reported, each cigarette increases SC concentration by about 13 ng/ml [66]. According to WHO recommendations, the minimum weekly PA energy expenditure for adults should be at least 10 MET*h/wk [37]. Our findings suggest that for males, approximately 6 MET*h/wk of PA energy expenditure were positively associated with lower odds of MetS linked with a 1 ng/mL increase in SC, and for females, the corresponding estimate was about 7 MET*h/wk. The potential compensatory association of PA and SC on the risk of MetS that we explored in this study could provide new possible ideas for reducing the risk of MetS in tobacco-exposed individuals. However, it is crucial to emphasize that this estimation is derived from statistical modeling and should be interpreted as an illustrative approximation rather than a precise or causal threshold. It should not be extrapolated into specific calculations of hours of moderate or vigorous PA required per cigarette smoked, as such extrapolations are biologically implausible and may misrepresent the complex health impacts of tobacco exposure. Moreover, it’s crucial to emphasize that these findings should not be misconstrued as a justification for smoking or exposing others to second-hand smoke. The detrimental effects of tobacco are not limited to MetS but also cause irreversible damage to other organs, including the lungs [47], heart [46], liver [67], and throat [68]. Thus, reducing tobacco exposure and simultaneously increasing PA energy expenditure could collectively contribute more effectively to lowering the risk of MetS.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of a causal link between tobacco exposure, PA, and the development of MetS. There is a need for future prospective studies to explore this relationship further. Second, the study utilizes SC concentration as a metric for quantifying tobacco exposure, which does not differentiate between active smokers and passive smokers. Consequently, it does not provide specific PA energy consumption recommendations tailored to these distinct groups. Third, the NHANES relies on self-reported PA data, which may be affected by recall errors and the desire of respondents to present themselves in a favorable light. Future studies could benefit from employing wearable devices, such as accelerometers, for a more precise and objective measurement of PA. Finally, we did not adjust for variables such as alcohol consumption, sleep duration, broader socioeconomic factors, dietary quality, and medication use, due to substantial missing data or inconsistent measurement across NHANES survey cycles. This omission may contribute to residual confounding, and future studies should seek to incorporate these important covariates where feasible. Although abdominal obesity is a component of the MetS definition, we included BMI to account for overall adiposity as an independent risk factor for metabolic abnormalities. It is important to emphasize that this study’s cross-sectional design cannot establish causal relationships, and the estimated compensatory associations are illustrative statistical approximations rather than causal or prescriptive thresholds. These behavioral recommendations are consistent with prior observational trends reported in the literature; however, they should be considered preliminary and warrant confirmation in future longitudinal and interventional studies.

Conclusion

To summarize, this population-based study found that higher SC levels are positively associated with an increased risk of MetS, whereas higher PA energy expenditure is inversely associated with MetS risk. Additionally, the findings suggest a potential compensatory relationship between PA and SC levels in relation to MetS risk. Based on model estimates, for men, an increase of approximately 6 MET*h/wk of PA was statistically associated with offsetting the higher odds of MetS linked with a 1 ng/mL rise in SC levels, and for women, the corresponding estimate was about 7 MET*h/wk. However, it is important to emphasize that these estimates are derived from statistical modeling and should be interpreted as illustrative approximations rather than precise or causal thresholds. These findings should not be construed as justification for smoking, given the extensive health harms associated with tobacco use. While physical activity may help reduce MetS risk, it cannot negate the broad and severe health harms of smoking, which include cancer, cardiovascular disease, and chronic respiratory conditions. Smoking cessation remains the most effective strategy for reducing tobacco-related health risks. Promoting physical activity and reducing tobacco exposure may collectively contribute to lowering MetS risk.
