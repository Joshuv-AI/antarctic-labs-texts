Panax notoginseng saponins inhibit platelet activation and thrombosis while preserving hemostasis: involvement of platelet oxylipin metabolic reprogramming
Authors: Wenting Wang, Changkun Li, Mingming Wang, Menghuan Xia, Xiangjun Yin, Lei Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlatelets play a central role in hemostasis and thrombosis. Panax notoginseng saponins (PNS), the bioactive components of Panax notoginseng, have demonstrated promising potential in regulating platelet function. Platelet oxylipins are critically involved in thrombotic and pro-coagulant activities. This study aimed to investigate the impact of PNS on platelet function, thrombosis and hemostasis and to explore the mechanisms regarding platelet oxylipin metabolism regulation.MethodsThe effects of PNS on platelet function and ischemia-induced myocardial injury were evaluated in a rat acute myocardial infarction (AMI) model. Agonist-induced platelet GPIIb/IIIa activation, degranulation and aggregation were tested by flow cytometry and light transmission aggregometry. Myocardial injury, infarct expansion and cardiac function post-myocardial infarction (MI) were evaluated by histopathology, 2,3,5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride staining and echocardiography. In vivo analysis of thrombus formation was conducted by FeCl3-injured mesenteric arteriole thrombosis model. Bleeding time and coagulation function were assessed in a mouse tail bleeding model. Platelet oxylipin metabolism was characterized by targeted lipidomic analysis.ResultsEnhanced platelet reactivity was observed post AMI. PNS dose-dependently inhibited platelet activation and aggregation via suppressing platelet degranulation, which was partly attributed to down-regulation of GPIIb/IIIa activation. Interestingly, high-dose PNS exhibited superior inhibition of platelet aggregation induced by adenosine diphosphate compared with aspirin. PNS ameliorated myocardial injury, MI expansion and ischemia-related heart failure. These findings were consistent with PNS-mediated attenuation of in vivo thrombus formation. Moreover, various doses of PNS preserved basal hemostasis in phycological conditions, which might be associated with shortened activated partial thromboplastin time. Platelet lipidomic analysis revealed perturbation of oxylipin profile post AMI, demonstrated by significant upregulation of thromboxane B2, 12-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (12-HETE) and leukotrienes (LTs), indicating propagation of thrombo-inflammation. PNS favored generation of anti-thrombotic lipids and mitigated pro-inflammatory cascades, facilitating the resolution of thrombo-inflammatory milieu.ConclusionsPNS exerted anti-platelet and anti-thrombotic effects in arterial ischemic conditions while preserving basal hemostasis, which was mechanistically linked to alleviation of thrombo-inflammation via reprogramming platelet oxylipin metabolism and regulation of coagulation. The findings unraveled therapeutic potential of PNS in the management of thrombotic cardiovascular diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05401-7.

Background

Thrombotic cardiovascular diseases, manifesting as myocardial infarction (MI) and ischemic stroke, account for almost a third of all deaths globally and are the leading cause of death worldwide [1, 2]. Platelets are small anucleate cells derived from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow [3]. Under physiological conditions, platelet activation and aggregation are essential for maintaining vascular integrity and blood flow at the site of endothelial injury, a process named hemostasis [4]. However, enhanced platelet reactivity triggered by high shear stress, typically after atherosclerotic plaque rupture or other damage to the vascular wall, contributes to arterial thrombosis, leading to the development of thrombotic cardiovascular diseases [3]. Given the fundamental role of platelet in mediating thrombus formation, it has been a primary target in the prevention and treatment of arterial thrombosis. Despite major pharmacological advances in antiplatelet therapies, unwanted side effect of bleeding remains to be a challenge [5], ranging from minor skin bruising to serious bleeding such as intracranial hemorrhage and gastrointestinal bleeding [6]. Major bleeding events have been associated with an adverse impact on prognosis [7]. Of note, the global burden of arterial thrombosis is anticipated to further grow in the near future [2]. Therefore, the unmet needs for novel antiplatelet strategies that inhibit pathological thrombosis while preserving primary hemostasis highlight the significance for further investigation in this area. Recently, novel targets have been identified and new drugs developed to suppress platelet-dependent thrombosis with fewer effects on hemostasis, such as inhibitors of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-β (PI3Kβ), protein disulfide-isomerase, activated glycoprotein IIb/IIIa (GPIIb/IIIa), GPIIb/IIIa outside-in signalling, protease-activated receptors, and platelet glycoprotein VI (GPVI)-mediated adhesion pathways [5, 8]. Of note, recent evidence has revealed that natural compounds like Ginsenoside Rg5 [9] and Xanthium strumarium L. [10] exhibited potent antiplatelet and antithrombotic effects by targeting the GPVI and PI3K signaling pathways, respectively.

Panax notoginseng (PN), the steamed roots of Panax notoginseng (Burk.) F.H. Chen, belongs to the species of the genus Panax, family Araliaceae [11]. PN is well-known for its efficiency in promoting blood circulation, providing analgesia and wound healing [12, 13] as documented in the Grand Compendium of Materia Medica. PN has been widely used for the treatment of hematoma and trauma in Asian countries since the late 16th century [14]. Panax notoginseng saponins (PNS) are the major bioactive ingredients of PN [11] among the 200 chemical compositions that have been identified from PN. According to Chinese Pharmacopoeia, five dammarane-type triterpenoid saponins, notoginsenoside R1, ginsenoside Rg1, Rd, Re, and Rb1, are recognized as the main active constituents of PNS by high-performance liquid chromatography fingerprint analysis. Recent research has revealed that PNS exert pleiotropic pharmacological properties including antioxidation, anti-inflammation, lipid metabolism regulation and anti-ischemic effects, targeting key pathological processes in the development of thrombotic cardiovascular diseases [15–18] and provide evidence for future study in this field. Moreover, several lines of evidence from in vivo models uncovered direct cardioprotective effects of PNS against acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, which was mediated through regulation of autophagy [19, 20] and mitigation of oxidative stress [21] and adverse remodeling [22]. In addition to its direct cardioprotective effects, PNS was also reported to facilitate cardiac repair by promoting the homing of C-kit+ bone mesenchymal stem cells in the setting of AMI [23]. However, the involvement of platelet-driven thrombogenesis in the therapeutic effects of PNS against AMI has yet to be elucidated.

Intriguingly, several clues have suggested potential regulatory effect on platelet function by PNS and its bioactive triterpenoid saponins. Wang et al. reported PNS intervention resulted in abolished platelet activation induced by injured endothelial cells (ECs) in vitro [24]. Moreover, this experiment outlined prominent inhibition of PNS on platelet adhesion to injured ECs, which is the preliminary phase in thrombogenesis [24]. Another study uncovered anti-aggregatory effect of PNS in rat hypercoagulable model [25] using single platelet agonist. This evidence was in accordance with an in vitro finding that ginsenoside Re exerted antiplatelet effect through impeding high shear stress-induced platelet aggregation [26]. Notably, further decreased platelet aggregability was achieved by combining PNS to aspirin without increased bleeding events in clinical settings [27]. Nevertheless, the efficacy of PNS on platelet activation and arterial thrombosis in ischemic condition and the impact of PNS on hemostasis have scarcely been addressed.

Lipids represent a diverse family of biomolecules that orchestrate essential physiological and pathophysiological cellular events. Platelets, in particular, harbor an abundant and dynamic lipid reservoir. Platelet function is critically dependent on specific lipid composition [28]. Dysregulation of platelet lipids is associated with impaired platelet function and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease (CVD) [29]. A specific class of platelet lipids, namely oxylipins, arise from the oxygenation of ω-3 or ω-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) [30]. Platelet oxylipins are important signaling molecules that exhibit either pro- or anti-thrombotic effects [31]. Recently, advanced lipidomics have unraveled oxylipins as pivotal actors in platelet biology, thrombogenesis and as novel biomarkers in CVDs [31]. Chatterjee identified significant alterations of platelet lipidome in patients with symptomatic coronary artery disease (CAD) compared with age-matched controls, which was associated with thrombotic disposition in CAD [32]. The metabolic disorder of platelet oxylipin may play important roles in platelet activation, platelet-dependent thrombus formation and the pathophysiology of acute coronary syndrome [33].

Beyond their classical roles in hemostasis and thrombosis, platelets are increasingly recognized as key players in thrombo-inflammation [34, 35]. There is a growing recognition that platelets exert prothrombotic and proinflammatory functions independent of their hemostatic effects [35]. Consequently, platelets have emerged as emerged as a promising therapeutic target to attenuate thrombosis and inflammation without exacerbating hemorrhage. Importantly, oxylipins play a major role in platelet biology and the progression of thrombo-inflammation [36]. Some oxylipins possess pro-inflammatory properties, while others act as pro-resolving mediators [31]. In light of the extensive regulatory role of platelet oxylipins in thrombosis and inflammation, recent evidence highlights the translational implications of modulating the platelet atherothrombotic lipidome via the ACKR3/CXCR7 axis [37] or applying novel oxylipin analog CS585 [38] to attenuate platelet hyperactivation and thrombosis without an increased risk of bleeding. Therefore, regulating platelet oxylipin metabolism holds promise for uncoupling pathological thrombosis from physiological hemostasis, emerging as a cutting-edge strategy for novel antiplatelet strategies [8].

Our previous study uncovered that combining PNS with aspirin (ASA), compared with ASA alone, markedly changed arachidonic acid (AA)-derived platelet lipid profile in chronic coronary syndrome patients by targeted lipidomic analysis, which was associated with reinforced antiplatelet effect of the combined therapy without increasing bleeding complications [39]. These results supported the hypothesis that PNS may exert anti-thrombotic effect while preserving hemostasis. In the present study, the effects of PNS monotherapy on ex vivo platelet function, in vivo arterial thrombosis, ischemia-induced myocardial injury, and basal hemostasis were evaluated in a dose-dependent manner to determine the antiplatelet efficacy and safety of PNS in the setting of AMI. Furthermore, utilizing platelet lipidomic analysis, we aimed to provide novel mechanistic insights into platelet lipidomic reprogramming in response to PNS intervention.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

PNS was purchased from Kunming Shenghuo Pharmacy Co., Ltd (Kunming, China). High-performance liquid chromatography fingerprint has detected notoginsenoside R1, ginsenosides Rb1, Rg1, Rd and Re as bioactive ingredients of PNS in our previous study [1]. Aspirin (ASA) was purchased from BAYER (Beijing, China). AA and Adenosine diphosphate (ADP) were purchased from Chrono-log Corporation (Havertown, USA). PE-conjugated anti-CD62P for flow cytometry was purchased from BD Biosciences (San Jose, USA). FITC-conjugated PAC-1 for flow cytometry was purchased from Biolegend (San Diego, CA). Internal standards for lipidomic analysis were purchased from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA). Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-grade solvents, methyl alcohol, acetonitrile, formic acid and ethanol were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Calcein was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (California, USA). Ferric chloride (FeCl3) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. louis, MO, USA).

Animals and ethics

Male SD rats (weighed 180–220 g) and male C57BL/6 mice (aged 6–8 weeks) were purchased from the Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center (SLAC), Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). All animals were kept in a dedicated specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility at Zhejiang Chinese Medical University Laboratory Animal Research Center. All animal protocols were approved by Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (approval NO. IACUC-20231023-07). Animals were allowed to acclimatize for at least 7 days before experiment.

AMI model and treatment

Rats were weighed between 220 and 250 g before surgery. AMI model was established by ligation of left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery as previously described [40]. Briefly, rats were induced with 3.0% isoflurane delivered in fresh air at a flow rate of 1 L/min. Following induction, anesthesia was maintained with 2.0% isoflurane at a reduced flow rate of 0.5 L/min, and the animals were subsequently intubated for mechanical ventilation. LAD coronary artery was permanently ligated by 8–0 line to induce AMI. Rats for sham group subjected to the same surgery except for ligation. Rats underwent ligation were randomly divided into 5 groups: (1) AMI group, (2) ASA group, which was orally administered with ASA at loading dose of 27 mg/kg/d (for only one day) and maintenance dose of 9 mg/kg/d (for day 2–7), (3) PNS low-dose group, (4) PNS intermediate-dose group, (5) PNS high-dose group, which were orally administered 7 days with PNS 10.8 mg/kg/d, 21.6 mg/kg/d, 43.2 mg/kg/d, respectively. The intermediate dose of PNS was equivalent to 240 mg daily for humans according to manual recommended and as reported previously [39]. PNS was dissolved in saline and administered by oral gavage. Rats in sham and AMI group were given the same volume of saline for 7 days.

At the end of the study, rats were euthanized by overdosing with pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg, i.p.). Deep unconsciousness was confirmed by the loss of the pedal reflex. Sacrifice was then completed by exsanguination via the abdominal aorta followed by heart harvest.

Myocardial histopathology

The harvested fresh heart was rinsed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and then fixed in formalin at 4℃ for 24 h. The fixed heart was cut transversally and embedded in paraffin. Five µm-thick sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or with Masson’s trichrome according to standard protocols. Myocardial slices were viewed with a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and images were analyzed and taken by SlideViewer 2.5.0 (Budapest, Hungary). The severity of cardiac injury was evaluated by myocardial histopathological scoring according to the previously reported criteria [41] the inflammatory cell infiltrates degree, striation loss, cardiomyocyte degeneration, and inter-muscular hemorrhage and edema. Scoring was calculated after H&E staining according to the intensity and frequency as (0) absent, (1) weak to low, (2) mild to moderate, and (3) high to frequent.

Infarct expansion

The viable and infarcted myocardium was differentiated by 2,3,5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining. The fresh harvested heart was flushed with saline and then sectioned into 5 slices along the left ventricle (LV) long axis. After processing, the slices were incubated with 0.25% TTC at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. The stained slices were photographed with a digital camera. Echocardiography

Rat cardiac function was assessed by M-mode echocardiography on the 6th day of intervention (Vevo 1100, VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) after anesthetization with 2.0% isoflurane. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (FS) was analyzed in the parasternal short-axis plane.

Platelet aggregometry

Platelet aggregation in response to agonists was determined by light transmission aggregometry (Chrono-Log, Havertown, USA) as previously described [39]. Briefly, platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was obtained by centrifuging rat whole blood anticoagulated with 3.8% trisodium citrate (260 × g, 10 min). PRP was further centrifuged (760 × g, 10 min) to obtain platelet-poor plasma (PPP). Platelet counts were adjusted to 1 × 108/mL by diluting PRP with PPP. Max platelet aggregation in response to AA (10 µg/ml) and ADP (5µM) was measured as the increase in light transmission, and records were taken 5 min after the induction of aggregation.

Platelet P-selectin and activated integrin αIIbβ3 abundance

P-selectin expression and integrin αIIbβ3 (GPIIb-IIIa) activation were analyzed by flow cytometry [42] in platelets at rest and stimulated with thrombin. Fluorophore-labelled antibodies were used to detect P-selectin expression (PE-conjugated anti-CD62P) and GPαIIbβ3 activation (FITC-conjugated PAC-1). 5 µL of 3.8% trisodium citrated rat whole blood was diluted with 100 µL of PBS and then stained with fluorophore-labelled anti-CD62p-PE, anti-CD61-APC, anti-PAC-1-FITC or isotype control antibodies at room temperature for 20 min in the dark. In some experiments, platelets were stimulated with thrombin (0.25U/ml) for 3 min before staining. After washing and suspension with PBS, the samples were analyzed by a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, California, USA). P-selectin and integrin GPIIbIIIa abundance were determined as the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI).

In vivo thrombus formation in mesenteric arterioles induced by FeCl3

Mice were pre-treated with saline (arterial thrombosis model group), or ASA (9 mg/kg/d), or either dosage of PNS (10.8 mg/kg/d, 21.6 mg/kg/d, 43.2 mg/kg/d) by oral gavage for 7 days before in vivo thrombus formation was induced. FeCl3-injured thrombus formation in mice mesenteric arteriole was performed as previously described [43]. Briefly, calcein-labeled platelets were injected into mice by the lateral tail vein. Mice were anesthetized with Zoletil 50 (a 1:1 combination of tiletamine and zolazepam, 50 mg/kg, i.p.). A stable surgical plane of anesthesia was confirmed by the loss of the pedal withdrawal reflex. The mice mesentery arteries were exteriorized and injured by topical application of a filter paper saturated with 15% FeCl3 for 30 s. Sham mice were subjected to topical application of a filter paper saturated with saline for 30 s. Thrombus formation was monitored by an eclipse intravital microscope (Nikon, New York, USA) for 90 min or until the complete cessation of blood flow (blood flow stopped for 1 min). The time to first thrombus (larger than 20 µm2) and time to vessel occlusion (defined as the cessation of blood flow for at least 15 s) were measured.

In vivo bleeding time

Mice were pre-treated with saline, or ASA (9 mg/kg/d), or either dosage of PNS (10.8 mg/kg/d, 21.6 mg/kg/d, 43.2 mg/kg/d) by oral gavage for 7 days. For the tail bleeding assay, mice were anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of Zoletil 50 (50 mg/kg). To ensure a surgical plane of anesthesia and the absence of pain, the depth of anesthesia was monitored by the loss of the pedal withdrawal reflex before procedures. Once the absence of pain was confirmed, tails were amputated 5 mm from the tip and immediately immersed into saline at 37 °C. Tail bleeding time was defined as the time taken for the bleeding to stop (no rebleeding within 1 min). Otherwise experiments were stopped after 10 min. Following the tail bleeding assay, mice were euthanized via the intraperitoneal administration of excessive pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg). Once deep unconsciousness was confirmed by the loss of the pedal reflex, blood was collected via cardiac puncture.

Coagulation testing

The prothrombin time (PT) and the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of mice were analyzed using a Hemostasis Analyzer (Sysmex CS-2400, Tampines Grande, Singapore) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Hematologic analysis

Mice red blood cell counts, hemoglobin concentration, platelet counts were determined with an automatic cell counter (Sysmex XN-1000 V, Tampines Grande, Singapore) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Mice alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), serum creatinine (Scr), urea nitrogen (Bun) were analyzed by an automatic biochemical analyzer (HITACHI 3100, Tokyo, Japan) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Lipidomic analysis

Platelet isolation and lipid extraction

Platelets were isolated from rat whole blood for lipidomic analysis as previously described with modification [39]. Whole blood from abdominal aorta was anticoagulated with 3.8% trisodium citrate and centrifuged at 280g for 8 min to obtain PRP. PRP was then centrifuged at 760g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded and the platelet pellet was suspended in Tyrode-HEPES buffer (134 mM NaCl, 12 mM NaHCO3, 2.9 mM KCl, 0.34 mM Na2HPO4, 1.0 mM MgCl2, 10 mM HEPES and 5 mM glucose) containing Acid-Citrate-Dextrose solution (85 mM trisodium citrate, 65 mM citric acid and 100 mM glucose) at a volume ratio of 9:1.

Lipid extraction was carried out according to standardized protocols [39]. The platelet pellet (3 × 108 cells) was diluted with ice-cold acidic ethanol and gently sonicated to obtain platelet lysates. A set of 12 isotopically labelled internal standards including 6-keto- Prostaglandin (PG) F1α- d4, Thromboxane B2-d4, PGF2α-d4, PGE2-d4, PGD2-d4, L Leukotriene (LT) C4-d5, LTB4 d4, 15-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (HETE) -d8, 12- HETE-d8, 5-HETE -d8, Oleoylethanolamine (OEA) -d4, AA-d8 were spiked into platelet lysis at the final concentration of 83 ng/mL (except for AA-d8 of 166 ng/mL, OEA-d4: 41.5 ng/mL). After vortexed for 3 min, the samples were centrifugated (4000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was added with 4 mL 0.1% formic acid and lipids were extracted by solid-phase extraction on Phenomenex cartridge (10 mg/mL, California, United States). Extractions were washed with 0.1% formic acid, 15% ethyl alcohol and evaporated under nitrogen prior to storage.

Lipid profiling

Evaporated lipid extract was resuspended in 100 µl methanol and lipid profiling was conducted by a robust, broad-spectrum targeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometer (LC-MS) protocol (LCMS-8050, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) adopted from Yamada et al. [44]. A Phenomenex Kinetex C8 column (150 × 2.1 mm inner diameter, 2.6 μm film thickness) was used for chromatographic separation. Two mobile phase solutions, (A) 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile were used for a gradient elution with 0.0 min 10% B, 5.0 min 25% B, 10.0 min 35% B, 20.0 min 75% B, 20.1 min 95% B and then held at 95% B for 4.9 min and returned to 10% B over 0.1 min. For each run, the injection volume was 5 µL. Measurements were performed in full scan mode for both positive and negative ionization. Lipid extracts were analyzed in multiple-reaction monitoring mode with continuous ionization polarity switching. As general MS parameters, flow rate of drying gas, nebulizing gas and heating gas was set to 5 L/min, 3 L/min, and 10 L/min, respectively. Argon gas used as collision gas was set at 270 Kpa. Temperatures for heat block, ESI interface and desolvation line were 400 °C, 300 °C and 250 °C, respectively.

Data processing and lipidomic analysis

For the lipidomic raw data, pre-processing including peak picking, alignment, noise filtering and structural annotation were performed with LabSolutions (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) software. All lipid data was normalized as the peak area ratio of the target analyte to the internal standard [45]. The processed data were log-transformed and imported to SIMCA-P 14.1 (Umetrics, Umea, Sweden) for multivariate statistical analysis. Orthogonal projection to latent structure-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was carried out to visualize the differences of lipid profile between groups and to uncover the driving forces among the variables detected in lipidome. 200-repeated permutation test were conducted to assess the robustness of OPLS-DA model. Data of potential markers, defined as the variable importance in the projection (VIP) value (threshold > 1), was further subjected to Mann-Whitney U test. Significantly differential lipids between two groups were identified based on the criteria of VIP>1, median fold changes >1.5 (or<0.67) and P value<0.05. Lipid data was back-transformed for presentation by Origin (Massachusetts, USA). Heatmap was created using R (version 4.3.3).

Statistical analysis

Statistics analysis was performed using R (version 4.3.3) and SPSS (version 21.0, Illinois, USA). Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± SEM. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range). Multiple group comparisons were performed by one-way ANOVA with LSD as post hoc correction or Kruskal-Wallis H Test with All pairwise for multiple comparisons where appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant at P<0.05.

Results

PNS inhibited GPIIb/IIIa activation, platelet α granule release and platelet aggregation ex vivo

We performed several functional assays to determine whether PNS intervention had an impact on platelet reactivity in the setting of myocardial ischemia. GPIIb/IIIa is a highly abundant heterodimeric platelet receptor essential for regulating platelet function [46]. In the resting state, GPIIb/IIIa is maintained with low affinity to ligands. Upon stimulation, GPIIb/IIIa rapidly switches to a high-affinity state and subsequently stimulates “outside-in” signaling, consequently driving platelet activation processes such as spreading, secretion (degranulation) and thrombus consolidation. In contrast to rats with sham operation, AMI rats exhibited markedly increased GPIIb/IIIa activation on platelets with and without ex vivo stimulation of thrombin (p < 0.01, Fig. 1A-B).

P-selectin is rapidly secreted from platelet α granule and mobilized to platelet surface upon activation [47]. Here, the membrane expression of P-selectin, reflection of platelet activation and secretion, was studied by flow cytometry in the basal state and after ex vivo agonist stimulation. As expected, a significantly higher expression of P-selectin was observed in AMI rats than in sham rats (p < 0.01, Fig. 1C-D). The above results suggested enhanced platelet reactivity in the setting of myocardial ischemia. We detected markedly decreased abundance of P-selectin and activated GPIIb/IIIa on both unstimulated and stimulated platelets in rats treated with intermediate- and high-doses of PNS when compared with AMI group (all p < 0.01, Fig. 1A-D). Additionally, high dose of PNS resulted in a further impaired P-selectin expression when compared with low dose of PNS in basal condition (p < 0.01, Fig. 1C) and following stimulation with thrombin (p < 0.01, Fig. 1D). Moreover, rats receiving high dose of PNS showed evidently reduced GPIIb/IIIa activation on stimulated platelets in contrast to those receiving low dose of PNS (p < 0.01, Fig. 1B). The findings demonstrated that under myocardial ischemia condition, PNS dose-dependently inhibited platelet activation by suppressing platelet degranulation, which was associated with modulation of GPIIb/IIIa-mediated “outside-in” signaling.

P-selectin expression stabilizes GPIIb/IIIa-fibrinogen interactions, leading to the formation of stable platelet aggregates [48, 49]. Since GPIIb/IIIa activation and P-selectin secretion synergistically orchestrate stable thrombus consolidation, we next performed light transmission aggregometry to evaluate whether this suppression of activation translated into reduced platelet aggregation. In this functional assessment, platelet aggregation was defined as the primary outcome, while degranulation marker and GPIIb/IIIa activation served as secondary mechanistic endpoints. Permanent ligation of LAD coronary artery triggered a significant enhancement of platelet aggregation induced by AA (p < 0.01, Fig. 1E) and ADP (p < 0.05, Fig. 1F). Rats treated with low dose of PNS showed a trend towards decreased platelet aggregation in response to AA (p > 0.05, Fig. 1E), whereas intermediate and high dose of PNS produced a substantial reduction of platelet aggregation induced by AA (all p < 0.01, Fig. 1E). Moreover, AA-induced platelet aggregation was lowered to a greater extent by high dose of PNS than that by intermediate dose of PNS (p < 0.01, Fig. 1E). In addition, PNS intervention resulted in a markedly decreased platelet aggregation upon stimulation with ADP across all groups in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1F). Intriguingly, we found that PNS was superior to ASA in diminishing platelet aggregation induced by ADP in ischemia conditions (p < 0.01, Fig. 1F).

PNS ameliorated in vivo thrombosis and infarct expansion

Following the observation of its anti-platelet activity ex vivo, we next evaluated whether this suppression translates into attenuated arterial thrombosis in vivo. For these in vivo evaluations, thrombus formation time and infarct size were established as primary outcomes. Using intravital microscopy, we found accelerated time to first thrombus and time to vessel occlusion in the arterial thrombosis (AT) model group in contrast to mice receiving sham operation (all p < 0.01, Fig. 2A-B), suggesting enhanced initial and complete thrombus formation in mesenteric arterioles induced by FeCl3. In vivo thrombus formation was profoundly affected by PNS intervention. In detail, initial thrombus formation was impaired upon pretreatment with intermediate and high dose of PNS (all p < 0.01 versus AT model group, Fig. 2A). Moreover, PNS exerted an evident inhibition on the formation of stable occlusive thrombus at both the intermediate dosage (p < 0.05, Fig. 2B) and high dosage (p < 0.01, Fig. 2B), demonstrated by prolonged occlusion time as compared with AT model. Of note, the anti-thrombotic effect of PNS appeared to be moderate, as no significant prolongation of occlusion time was observed compared with sham controls (PNSM vs. sham, p > 0.05; PNSH vs. sham, p > 0.05, Fig. 2B). On the contrary, stable thrombus formation was impeded substantially by ASA, evidenced by markedly prolonged occlusion time as compared with sham control, indicating increased risk of unwanted bleeding (p < 0.01, Fig. 2B).

Given that vascular thrombosis directly drives tissue ischemia, we next investigated whether the in vivo anti-thrombotic efficacy of PNS would limit the progression of MI. TTC staining revealed transmural infarction in rats of model group after ligation of LAD artery, with an infarct size of approximately 29% of the ischemic region (Fig. 2C-D). MI expansion was slightly alleviated by low dose of PNS (PNSL vs. AMI, p = 0.111, Fig. 2C-D) and was substantially attenuated by intermediate and high dose of PNS (all p < 0.01, Fig. 2C-D). Infarct size was further mitigated by 33% in response to high dose of PNS relative to low dose of PNS (p < 0.01, Fig. 2C-D). The findings unraveled ameliorated arterial thrombosis and MI progression by PNS intervention, which was consistent with the effects on platelet activation, secretion and aggregation.

PNS attenuated ischemia-induced myocardial injury and heart failure post AMI

Given the efficacy of PNS to limit infarct expansion, we next investigated whether this structural preservation extended to the rescue of cellular myocardial injury. As shown in Fig. 2E, sham-operated controls exhibited normally arranged myocardial fibers with normal capillary cross-sections, indicating well-preserved cardiac tissue architecture and intact microcirculation. Hearts in AMI group had histopathological evidence of severe myocardial damage, showing coagulative ischemic necrosis of myocardial fibers, loss of nuclei, infiltration of neutrophils in the interstitial area. As shown in Fig. 2F, PNS attenuated myocardial injury in a dose-dependent manner, characterized by decreased necrotic area, reduced microcirculation hemorrhage and alleviated inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 2F). Since ischemic injury triggers pathological cardiac fibrosis and remodeling, we further evaluated collagen deposition using Masson’s trichrome. Myocardial tissue in the Sham group exhibited brick-red staining with regular arrangement of cardiomyocytes by Masson’s trichrome and no blue-stained fibrotic tissue was observed. In contrast, hearts of AMI rats displayed extensive blue-stained regions in the infarcted area of the left ventricle, indicating substantial collagen fiber deposition and severe fibrosis post-infarction. PNS treatment led to a dose-dependent attenuation of myocardial fibrosis (Fig. 2G).

Cardiac fibrosis is a pathophysiologic feature in myocardial diseases, and is associated with systolic and diastolic dysfunction [50]. Mortality rate was recorded throughout the whole study. 31% of the rats that underwent ligation of LAD coronary artery died from cardiogenic shock in the very early phase of AMI (within 6 h following surgery). No mortality was observed in the AMI or treatment groups during intervention period. Heart function was designated as the primary outcome, while histopathological and fibrosis assessments served as secondary outcomes. Echocardiography was performed to evaluate the effects of PNS on cardiac function in AMI rats. Seven days post-infarction, rats of AMI group exhibited severe heart failure symptoms, with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of only 29.58% and a fractional shortening (FS) of 21.26%. low-dose PNS treatment resulted in a modest increase of LVEF and FS (LVEF: PNSL vs. AMI, 47.37% vs. 29.58%, p > 0.05; FS: PNSL vs. AMI, 26.27% vs. 21.47%, p > 0.05; Fig. 2G-I). Notably, intermediate- and high-dose of PNS treatment significantly improved LVEF and FS (LVEF: PNSM vs. AMI, 50.86% vs. 29.58%, p < 0.05; PNSH vs. AMI, 57.25% vs. 29.58%, p < 0.01; FS: PNSM vs. AMI, 31.00% vs. 21.47%, p < 0.01; PNSH vs. AMI, 37.25% vs. 21.47%, p < 0.01; Fig. 2G-I). The findings suggested that PNS attenuated ischemic-induced myocardial injury and improved cardiac function post AMI.

PNS preserved hemostasis in tail bleeding mice

A major limitation of conventional anti-platelet agents is the concomitant increased risk of bleeding. Having established the anti-thrombotic and cardioprotective efficacy of PNS, we next evaluated its impact on basal hemostasis. In our safety evaluation, tail bleeding time was designated as the primary outcome for physiological hemostasis, while coagulation parameters and hematological counts served as secondary safety endpoints. To evaluate the impact of PNS on hemostasis, a tail bleeding model was employed to mimic the physiological hemostatic process in wild-type C57BL mice. ASA intervention yielded a significantly prolonged bleeding time, indicating an increased risk of bleeding (ASA vs. normal control: 162.5s vs. 109.25s, p < 0.05, Fig. 3C). Intriguingly, PNS-treated mice showed slightly shortened bleeding time compared with the normal control, and no significant differences were observed (all p > 0.05, Fig. 3C). These findings suggested that PNS preserved hemostasis under physiological conditions. In addition, rats receiving ASA exhibited longer bleeding time compared with those receiving any dose of PNS (all p < 0.05, Fig. 3C). We then evaluated the effect of PNS on coagulation function. PT did not differ significantly across all experimental groups (p = 0.802, Fig. 3A). However, the changes in APTT reached significance when comparing PNS groups and the normal control (p = 0.015, Fig. 3B). In detail, low-, intermediate-, and high-dose of PNS resulted in slightly shortened APTT compared with normal control (PNSL vs. control: 35.13s vs. 37.34s, p < 0.01; PNSM vs. control: 35.88s vs. 37.34s, p < 0.05; PNSH vs. control: 35.61s vs. 37.34s, p < 0.05; Fig. 3B).

Platelet lipidomic profiling revealed perturbation of oxylipin metabolism post AMI

Given the central role of platelet oxylipins in orchestrating platelet reactivity, we utilized a targeted LC-MS lipidomic platform to profile the intraplatelet oxylipin metabolome post AMI. Platelet lipidomics provided an open analytical window to illustrate a characteristic fingerprint of lipid profile in platelets, where overall oxylipin alterations served as secondary outcomes to provide mechanistic insights The lipidomic platform identified 31 oxylipins across 5 subclasses in platelets isolated from rats subjected to LAD ligation or sham operation (Table S1). Among the extractable oxylipins, 16 were derived from ω-6 PUFA (AA) via cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), lipoxygenase-5 (5-LOX), 12-LOX, 15-LOX and cytochrome P450 (CYP) enzymatic pathways; 7 oxylipins were generated from ω-6 PUFA linoleic acid (LA) through 5-LOX, 15-LOX, and CYP pathways; 4 oxylipins were derived from ω-3 PUFA eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) via COX-1, 12-LOX, and non-enzymatic oxidation; and 3 derived from ω-3 PUFA docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) were synthesized through free radical-mediated oxidation (Table S1). Furthermore, 1a1b-dihomo-PGF2α, the downstream metabolite from ω-6 PUFA adrenic acid (ADA), was identified (Fig. 5). Additional information regarding these oxylipins is available in the Lipid Maps database (https://www.lipidmaps.org/).

To investigate whether platelet lipidome altered in response to myocardial ischemia, we compared the platelet lipid profiles between rats with AMI and sham ligation. The preprocessed LC/MS data were subjected to OPLS-DA and samples with similar lipid profiles were clustered closely in the OPLS-DA score plot. As shown in Fig. 6A, OPLS-DA uncovered distinct metabolic signatures of platelet oxylipins between the AMI and sham groups, indicating perturbation of oxylipin metabolism under ischemic conditions. The robustness of OPLS-DA model was validated by 200-repeated permutation test (Fig. 4B). The VIP value of the individual oxylipin was illustrated in Fig. 4C, with 11 oxylipins exhibiting VIP value higher than 1. Subsequently, fold change was calculated as the ratio of median lipid abundance in AMI group versus sham control. Nine out the 11 oxylipins showed a fold change (FC) >1.5 (or<0.67) in platelets from AMI rats in contrast to those in sham controls, including AA, thromboxane (TX) B2 (TXB2), prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), leukotriene C4 (LTC4), 11-trans-LTC4, leukotriene B4 (LTB4), leukotriene D4 (LTD4), 12-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (12-HETE) and 14,15-dihydroxyeicosatrienoic acid (14,15-DiHETrE), which were considered as potential metabolic biomarkers ascribed to myocardial ischemia. The 9 oxylipins were further confirmed as significant differentiated metabolites by Mann-Whitney U test. As shown in Fig. 4D, metabolite discriminations among AMI and sham groups were visualized by heatmap matrix. Myocardial ischemia induced a 6.6-fold increase in platelet AA levels (FC = 6.6, Fig. 5A), along with a profound remodeling of downstream oxylipin metabolism. Notably, the concentration of COX-1 derived TXB2 was elevated nearly 20-fold; whereas that of PGD2, another COX-1 derived lipid, was reduced by approximately 90%, relative to sham controls (FC = 20.3 and 0.11, respectively; Fig. 5A). A series of lipids generated from 5-LOX pathway were substantially up-regulated post AMI, including LTC4, LTD4, and LTB4 (FC = 8.03, 17.91, and 11.95, respectively, Fig. 5B). In addition, there was a significant increase in the abundance of platelet 12-HETE, downstream metabolite from 12-LOX pathway, in AMI rats compared with sham controls (FC = 6.12, Fig. 5C). Moreover, the level of platelet 14,15-DiHETrE, a downstream metabolite from AA-CYP pathway, in AMI group dropped to nearly one-third of that observed in sham controls (FC = 0.33, Fig. 5D).

Discussion

Thrombotic cardiovascular diseases lead to > 14 million deaths annually and remain the leading cause of death globally [2, 51]. Platelets play a key role in arterial thrombosis and antiplatelet therapy is crucial in the treatment of thrombotic cardiovascular diseases. Despite the use of antiplatelet regimens, high on-treatment platelet reactivity persists in a wide range of patients, characterized by impaired platelet inhibition, which is closely associated with an increased risk of thrombosis reoccurrence [51]. Of note, platelets are vital mediators for maintaining hemostasis. All current antiplatelet agents carry an inherent risk of bleeding, which is the major challenge for their use. Novel strategies that impede platelet activation and pathological thrombosis with an emphasis on preserving hemostasis are urgent. PNS, the bioactive ingredient of PN, is well-known and highly prized herb product used in Chinese medicine, with the main active components being ginsenoside Rb1, Rd, Rg1, and Re, and notoginsenoside R1 (Fig. S1). Accumulating data have shown protective roles of PNS in thrombotic cardiovascular diseases, including ischemic stroke [16] and myocardial ischemia [19], while the effect of PNS on thrombosis and hemostasis remains unexplored. The traditional theory and previous findings prompt the hypothesis if PNS is involved in regulation of platelet function, arterial thrombosis and maintaining hemostasis.

In this study, we performed a series of assays to dissect the effect of PNS on platelet function under ischemic conditions. The functional assays (flow cytometry and light transmission aggregometry) utilized whole blood and PRP, respectively, to strictly adhere to established methodological gold standards [42, 52]. Here, platelet hyperfunction was observed in rats subjected to LAD ligation, which mimicked the acute ischemic state of myocardium. The hyperfunction of platelets post-ischemia was characterized by pronounced activation of GPIIb/IIIa. GPIIb/IIIa is the most abundant integrin in platelets [53]. Various receptor-specific platelet activation signaling will converge into common events that ultimately stimulate GPIIb/IIIa activation [54]. The activation of GPIIb/IIIa triggers the “outside-in” signaling and facilitates essential platelet activities such as secretion (degranulation), spreading, and thrombus consolidation [55], which are central in thrombogenesis. PNS substantially mitigated GPIIb/IIIa activation in a dose-dependent manner in the presence or absence of ex vivo stimulation of platelet agonist, implying that platelet hyperfunction was partly ameliorated under ischemic state. Selectins (P-, E-, L-selectin) represent a family of glycoproteins that modulate platelet activity and augment thrombosis [48]. While some selectins are expressed constitutively, P-selectin is only expressed on activated platelets [56]. Upon activation, P-selectin is secreted from platelet α granules and rapidly mobilized to the surface of the cell [48]. In this study, PNS resulted in pronounced reduction of P-selectin expression on platelets under AMI milieu in basal state (without ex vivo stimulation). Furthermore, the overexpression of P-selectin was impeded by PNS following ex vivo stimulation of thrombin. These findings outlined platelet hyperfunction post acute myocardial ischemia and the perturbation toward overactivation was mitigated by PNS intervention. In our previous study, PNS was shown to reinforce platelet inhibition and attenuated high platelet reactivity in patients with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS). These results provided evidence that PNS inhibited platelet over-activation in ischemic conditions via modulating platelet secretion, which may be mechanistically linked to down-regulation of GPIIb/IIIa mediated “outside-in” signaling.

P-selectin expression on activated platelets stabilizes the interaction of GPIIb/IIIa and fibrinogen, contributing to the growth of stable platelet aggregates [49]. Currently, a series of platelet aggregatory test was performed. In keeping with the results from GPIIb/IIIa activation and P-selectin expression, PNS was shown to mitigate platelet aggregation in AMI rats across several stimuli (AA and ADP) in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, ADP-induced platelet aggregation was impeded more substantially by PNS than by ASA. Interestingly, in another study, PNS was found to exert stronger inhibition on platelet adhesion (preliminary phase of thrombogenesis), than did ASA [24]. Thus, the findings demonstrated the effect of PNS on alleviating platelet hyper-reactivity in ischemia states by suppressing activation and aggregation, which was related to the regulation of GPIIb/IIIa activation and degranulation. In addition, PNS was superior, at least in part, to ASA in depleting platelet adhesion and aggregation in experimental ischemic state. Furthermore, previous in vitro studies employing purified washed platelets showed that PNS and its principal bioactive components exerted direct inhibitory effects on platelet activation and aggregation in response to other stimuli, such as collagen [57], thrombin, U46619 [25] and pathological high shear stress [26]. Collectively, the established in vitro evidence and our ex vivo data are mutually complementary, providing an integrated view of the antiplatelet effect of PNS.

The pathogenesis of arterial thrombosis (AT) is mediated by platelets, which serve as the primary driver of thrombus growth [58]. Upon stimulation, P-selectins are secreted and translocated to the surface of activated platelets and incorporated into a growing thrombus. P-selectins have been investigated as biomarkers of platelet activation and stabilizers of thrombosis [48, 59]. There has been a recognition that platelet activation and aggregation are implicated in all stages of atherosclerosis and play a central role in atherothrombotic events [60]. Currently, the impact of PNS on thrombogenesis was evaluated by a mouse model of arterial thrombosis induced by FeCl3. Mice in AT group rapidly displayed complete thrombus occlusion in the mesentery arteries. In contrast, a significant delay in time to first thrombus and time to vessel occlusion was observed in mice pretreated with intermediate and high dose of PNS. Thus, the formation of initial and occlusive thrombi was ameliorated by PNS in a dose-dependent manner. The effect was likely ascribed to the attenuation of prothrombotic activity of platelets in ischemic states via regulating platelet hyper-reactivity and aggregation. Uncontrolled platelet activation contributes to arterial thrombogenesis, leading to the worsening of myocardial injury and the progress of MI [61]. Here, rats receiving PNS were significantly protected from severe cardiac damage and MI expansion compared with those of model group. Of note, mortality and morbidity are increased when more than 20% of the left ventricle is infarcted [62]. Rats subjected to LAD ligation without intervention exhibited extensive infarction, involving approximately 29% of the left ventricle, and 31% died from cardiogenic shock in the very early phase of AMI (within 6 h after surgery). The infarction area was decreased to below 20% by PNS treatment though no significant reduction in mortality was observed over the 7-day experiment period. MI triggers cardiac fibrosis and ventricular remodeling, which is associated with continued decline of cardiac function [50]. PNS significantly attenuated early pathological fibrosis at day 7, which is associated with improved myocardial compliance and systolic function in a dose-dependent manner. The impact of PNS on long-term chronic heart failure and late-stage hypertrophic remodeling is of great clinical significance and remains to be evaluated in the future. Taken together, under experimental ischemic states, PNS yielded prominent inhibition of platelet activation and aggregation via modulating GPIIb/IIIa expression and platelet secretion, paralleled with pronounced reduction of arterial thrombosis, which translated into a benefit in preserving cardiac function.

Platelets play a central role in thrombosis and hemostasis. Currently available antiplatelet agents, including ASA and P2Y12 receptor inhibitors, have proven efficacy in the management of thrombotic diseases, albeit at the cost of increased risk of bleeding. Bleeding was strongly associated with a higher risk of mortality within 1 year after the event [63]. Novel strategies are highly needed to achieve the balance between inhibiting thrombosis and preserving hemostasis. The direct effect of PNS, the main bioactive component of Panax notoginseng, on hemostasis was demonstrated here by tail bleeding model, which mimicked the hemostatic process under physiologic condition. ASA induced a markedly prolonged bleeding time in mice subjected to tail cutting, indicating an increased risk of bleeding. Mice receiving three doses of PNS displayed slightly shortened bleeding time compared with normal control although this difference did not reach significance. Bleeding time did not differ significantly among mice receiving low-, intermediate-, or high dose of PNS. These findings indicated the pronounced effect of PNS on preserving hemostasis in experimental bleeding conditions at doses of 10.8–43.2 mg/kg/day. Additionally, PNS-treated mice exhibited shorter APTT, but a similar PT to that in normal control, suggesting modulation of intrinsic and common pathways of coagulation cascade by PNS intervention. Recently, PNS was shown to enhance endothelial progenitor cell angiogenesis and facilitate tissue regeneration [64]. Therefore, it is tempting to speculate that PNS preserved hemostasis under physiologic conditions by regulating coagulation and vascular repair, which are fundamental in the hemostatic system [65]. In another study, Yu et al. reported that PNS promoted the meningeal lymphatic system-mediated hematoma absorption in intracerebral hemorrhage [66]. We previously found that PNS attenuated gastric mucosal injury induced by ASA [39] and dual antiplatelet therapy [67]. The aforementioned evidence is of clinical significance, as bleeding complications- particularly intracranial and gastrointestinal bleeding- are major and potentially fatal. PNS may offer incremental benefits by maintaining the balance between thrombogenesis and hemostasis in thrombotic cardiovascular diseases.

Platelets are a separate compartment of lipid metabolism, possessing the capacity for de novo lipogenesis [68]. Recent work has uncovered the crucial role of platelet lipidome in platelet structure, signaling, and ultimately, function, including platelet activation [69]. A key class of platelet lipids are oxylipins, bioactive molecules derived from ω-3 or ω-6 PUFAs by COX, LOX, CYP enzymes, or via nonenzymatic oxidation (Fig. S2). Intriguingly, some of them have pro-thrombotic properties, whereas others act as anti-thrombotic mediators [70]. Moreover, platelet oxylipins display both pro-inflammatory and pro-resolving activities [68], and their dysregulation has been implicated in cardiovascular and inflammatory diseases. Although accumulating reports have addressed the oxylipin profile in biofluids, especially in plasma, investigations of intraplatelet oxylipins using modern LC-MS techniques remain limited.

The present study explored the metabolic alteration of platelet oxylipin in response to acute myocardial ischemia. Platelets were washed, isolated and extracted to minimize the interference of systemic confounding factors with a tailored procedure for accurate lipidomic analysis in AMI rats. A total of 31 oxylipins were identified from the PUFA families of AA, LA, EPA, DHA and ADA. Acute ischemia in cardiomyocytes induced a pronounced remodeling of oxylipin profile in platelets, with 29% undergoing significant up- or down-regulation. The predominant altered lipids were derived from AA metabolic pathway, indicating distinct triggering of pathway-dependent events. AA is one of the most abundant ω-6 PUFA present in the esterified form in platelet membrane. It is released from phospholipids in response to various activating or inhibitory stimuli [71]. AMI markedly enhanced platelet activity, alongside with a 6.6-fold increase in intraplatelet AA levels. In addition, a major metabolic shift of AA derivatives was observed. These findings were consistent with a recent report indicating that approximately 20% of the platelet lipidome underwent significant modifications upon platelet activation, primarily affecting AA-containing species [68]. AA serves as the precursor of a wide range of bioactive autacoids, including PGs, TXA2, LTs, and epoxyeicosatetraenoic acids, collectively referred to as eicosanoids. Eicosanoids act as local mediators that regulate both the physiological functions and the pathophysiological responses of platelets [71]. The stimulation of AA release post-MI resulted in a substantial perturbation of eicosanoid metabolism. TXB2, an AA-COX-1 derived metabolite, was elevated at least 20-fold, while the generation of PGD2, another COX-1 downstream product, was significantly blocked. TXB2 is the biological metabolite of TXA2 generated through the rapid non-enzymatic hydrolysis. TXA2 is a short-lived, chemically unstable eicosanoid with potent platelet activating properties and has an established role in atherothrombosis [72]. The modifications in AA-LOX pathway should also be noticed. 12-HETE, a downstream product from 12-LOX pathway, was nearly sixfold higher than in controls. 12-HETE was found to potentiate platelet aggregation in vitro [73]. Platelet-derived 12-HETE has been shown to play an important role in mediating thrombotic process [74]. These findings highlighted a lipidomic reprogramming of platelets toward a pro-thrombotic phenotype in the AMI milieu, which may underlie platelet hyper-reactivity and contribute to infarct expansion. Furthermore, several 5-LOX derived LTs (LTC₄, LTD₄, and LTB₄) were highly upregulated following AMI, which was approximately 8-,18- and 12-fold higher than the concentration found in sham rats, respectively. Overall, LTs are pro-inflammatory mediators and have a major regulatory role in the inflammatory and thrombotic responses in atherosclerosis [75]. Inflammation and thrombosis are tightly connected processes and the interplay of thrombosis and inflammation, named thrombo-inflammation, generates a systemic prothrombotic environment, with typical clinical manifestations including MI, stroke and venous thromboembolism [76]. Collectively, acute myocardial ischemia induced profound platelet lipidomic reprogramming that initiated an inflammation cascade and created a self-amplifying thrombo-inflammatory milieu, thereby contributing to the propagation of platelet activation, thrombogenesis and MI progression. A decline in PGD₂ and 14,15-DiHETrE was observed, yet their minimal impact on platelet function suggested these changes might reflect collateral effects of the systemic metabolic perturbations.

Furthermore, we tested the impact of PNS on platelet lipidome. Comparison of AMI rats with or without PNS intervention revealed major modifications of oxylipin profile by targeted lipidomic technology. AMI-induced increase of AA generation was substantially counteracted by PNS consumption (43.2 mg/kg/d), leading to a 90% reduction in AA levels relative to untreated control.

The metabolic pattern of AA-COX-1 pathway was altered, characterized by a significant up-regulation of PGE2, markedly down-regulated of PGF2α, alongside with a trend toward decreased production of TXA2 (TXB2 FC = 0.74), although the latter did not reach statistical significance. PGF2α facilitates platelet activation and aggregation by acting on PGE2 receptor subtype EP3 and TXA2 receptor TP in mice [77]. PGE2 is produced by vascular endothelium, macrophages in atherosclerotic plaques, and activated platelets, which is involved in modulating vascular physiology, platelet reactivity and thrombogenesis [78]. These data are in line with our previous observation in CCS patients, where the addition of PNS to ASA reduced platelet TXB2 production. However, the present findings in AMI rats revealed a divergent effect on PGE2 regulation, whereas PNS treatment enhanced intraplatelet PGE2 generation, in contrast to the downregulation observed when adding PNS to ASA in CCS patients [39]. PGE2 exerts either stimulatory or inhibitory roles in human platelets depending on experimental conditions [79]. The impact of PNS on PGE2 metabolism and its implications for platelet function remain incompletely understood and deserve further investigations. PNS blocked the generation of 12-HETE, which was actually 83% lower in PNS treated rats compared with PNS-naive controls, a finding that correlated well with results observed in CCS patients [39]. PNS mitigated the surge of 5-LOX–derived LTs triggered by AMI, as evidenced by the dramatical fall in the levels of LTC4, LTD4, and LTB4. Importantly, the present study uncovered PNS as a novel regulator of EPA metabolism. PNS robustly increased EPA-derived oxylipins, with PGE3 and PGD3 showing nearly 10- to 13-fold elevations. ω-6 PUFAs, including EPA, contribute to the generation of PGs and specialized pro-resolving mediators, thereby facilitating the resolution of tissue damage and inflammatory responses [80]. PGD3 and PGE3 have been shown to inhibit platelet activation and aggregation and hold promise as a circulating antithrombotic mediator [81]. Overall, PNS intervention favored generation of antithrombotic lipids over prothrombotic ones, in parallel with the resolution of pro-inflammatory cascades. PNS attenuated platelet lipidomic perturbations provoked by AMI, leading to an amelioration of the thrombo-inflammatory milieu, a hallmark of ischemia that fuels platelet activation and thrombogenesis. Recent studies have unraveled that platelets, beyond adhering to vascular injury sites, actively respond to inflammation [34] via pattern recognition receptors and the receptor for advanced glycation end products [82, 83]. Of note, PNS was previously shown to ameliorate thrombosis by suppressing endothelial inflammation via the Piezo1/YAP signaling axis [84], thereby attenuating inflammation-driven platelet activation. Importantly, platelet granules also store numerous proinflammatory molecules (including serotonin, platelet factor 4, and P-selectin) [34]. Thus, inflammation activates platelets, which in turn stimulate further inflammation. Beyond its known capacity to suppress endothelial inflammation, the present study demonstrated that PNS mitigated ischemia-induced platelet hyper-reactivity and pathogenic lipid remodeling, thereby abrogating the platelet-driven thrombo-inflammatory cascade. In light of this bidirectional framework, PNS exerted pleiotropic effects across the thrombo-inflammatory network. Therefore, modulation of thrombo-inflammatory cross-talk might serve as a potential mechanism underlying the antiplatelet efficacy of PNS.

As observed here and in previous studies, PNS exhibited a broad-spectrum inhibitory effect on platelet aggregation induced by diverse stimuli, suggesting its anti-platelet action may not be pathway specific. This pleiotropic inhibition may be mechanistically linked to the abrogation of a platelet lipid-driven amplification loop. Initial platelet activation significantly alters membrane lipids, generating diverse bioactive oxylipins. In this study, AMI induced profound platelet lipidomic reprogramming, creating a self-amplifying thrombo-inflammatory milieu that drove platelet activation, thrombogenesis, and MI progression. Crucially, PNS counteracted the AMI-induced surge in AA and pro-inflammatory LTs, favoring the generation of antithrombotic lipids (PGE3 and PGD3) over prothrombotic mediators (12-HETE), mostly derived from AA metabolism. AA-derived oxylipins mediate critical costimulatory signaling within platelets, resulting in the sustained activation of GPIIb/IIIa- a final common pathway shared by diverse agonists-which ultimately facilitates stable platelet aggregation and thrombus formation [85]. Collectively, PNS mitigated the self-amplifying loop in platelet activation by remodeling oxylipin metabolism, which may partly underpin its antiplatelet effect in response to various stimuli.

Furthermore, lipidomic reprogramming provided a mechanistic basis for how PNS exerted broad-spectrum platelet inhibition with limited impact on physiological hemostasis. Pathological thrombus propagation relies heavily on thrombo-inflammatory cross-talk, particularly the intrinsic coagulation cascade amplified by neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [86, 87], which are triggered by platelet-derived chemoattractants (e.g., LTs and 12-HETE). PNS may disrupt the intrinsic amplification cascade by mitigating the generation of these lipid mediators, as evidenced by our in vivo finding of moderately prolonged APTT. Conversely, the tissue factor-driven extrinsic pathway, which is essential for rapid physiological hemostasis, appeared to remain functionally preserved, as suggested by the unaltered PT in our study.

Some limitations should be considered. The effect of PNS on platelet function and arterial thrombosis in the setting of arterial ischemia was addressed here. We also investigated the hemostatic profile of PNS under physiological bleeding condition. The impact of PNS on venous thrombus formation remained unexplored. An early study demonstrated that PNS increased plasma concentration of warfarin and prolonged its pharmacokinetic exposure, thereby potentiating its anticoagulant effect [88]. Whether PNS affects venous thrombogenesis and its clinical relevance to venous thromboembolism and pulmonary embolism remains to be determined and merits further investigation. The capacity of PNS to inhibit platelet activation and thrombosis while preserving hemostasis-seemingly contradictory effects-is likely attributed to its multi-component, multi-target nature underpinned by the presence of diverse bioactive saponins. Platelet lipidomic analysis uncovered extensive remodeling of oxylipin metabolism following PNS consumption, linking to the resolution of platelet over-activity and thrombo-inflammation; yet the molecular basis remains to be elucidated. Although our findings suggested that PNS exerted antiplatelet and antithrombotic effects by integrating platelet inhibition with the modulation of thrombo-inflammatory cascade, future studies dissecting the multicellular crosstalk among platelets, leukocytes, and the endothelium are warranted to further elucidate its antithrombotic mechanisms.

Conclusions

Currently, we evidenced that PNS suppressed platelet over-activation and aggregation under ischemic conditions by modulating platelet degranulation, which was mechanistically linked to the downregulation of GPIIb/IIIa activation. PNS yielded prominent inhibition of arterial thrombosis, leading to the attenuation of MI expansion and functional impairment post-AMI, while maintaining basal hemostasis. Acute myocardial ischemia induced extensive perturbations in the platelet oxylipin profile that amplified thrombo-inflammation, which might predispose to the propagation of platelet activation, thrombogenesis, and MI progression. PNS shifted the platelet lipidomic profile toward an antithrombotic phenotype, in parallel with the attenuation of pro-inflammatory cascades. The lipidomic reprogramming facilitated the resolution of thrombo-inflammation and underpinned the antiplatelet and antithrombotic actions of PNS. The present study sheds light on novel mechanisms underlying platelet hyperfunction and arterial thrombosis under an ischemic state. PNS may offer therapeutic benefits in thrombotic cardiovascular diseases by restoring the balance between thrombosis and hemostasis.
