Bezafibrate for severe refractory intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy: a case report and literature review

Background

Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy (ICP) is the most common hepatic disorder unique to pregnancy, with a reported prevalence of 0.2-2% depending on geographic and ethnic factors [1]. It typically presents in the second or third trimester with maternal pruritus and increased serum total bile acids (TBA), with spontaneous resolution postpartum. Although maternal outcomes are generally benign, ICP carries significant fetal risks—including spontaneous preterm birth, meconium-stained amniotic fluid (MSAF), and stillbirth—especially when TBA concentrations exceed 100 µmol/L [2–5].

Ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) remains the first-line treatment, with efficacy in alleviating maternal symptoms and improving liver function tests [6]. In the largest randomized placebo-controlled study in UDCA (605 women) did not reduce a composite of perinatal death, preterm birth, or neonatal unit admission; however, most participants had mild disease, potentially limiting applicability to severe cases [7]. In treatment-refractory cases or those with severe disease (TBA > 100 µmol/L), additional therapies such as rifampin, cholestyramine, and therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) have been considered [8–10]. Fibrates, well-established in the management of other cholestatic liver diseases, have only recently been explored as a potential therapeutic option for ICP [11, 12]. This report describes a case of refractory, early-onset ICP with extreme bile acid elevation and progressive hyperbilirubinemia, successfully managed with bezafibrate.

Case presentation

A 33-year-old gravida 4 para 3 woman presented at 19 + 3 weeks’ gestation with generalized pruritus. She had an unremarkable medical and obstetric history with three term uncomplicated vaginal deliveries. She denied taking any medications and reported no personal or familial history of liver disease. Laboratory testing revealed AST 150 IU/L, ALT 169 IU/L, ALP 148 IU/L, GGT 30 IU/L, total bilirubin 1.8 mg/dL, and markedly increased TBA 301 µmol/L. Her platelet count, blood pressure, and urinalysis were normal with no clinical, laboratory or fetal evidence of preeclampsia. A liver biopsy was not performed, as the diagnosis was made clinically based on typical features of ICP and comprehensive biochemical evaluation, with resolution occurring postpartum. A thorough investigation included a liver ultrasound and MRCP which were reported as normal. Serologies for viral hepatitis (HAV, HBV, HCV, HEV) and other viruses (CMV, EBV, HSV), autoimmune markers (ANA, AMA, LKM, ASMA), autoimmune markers (ANA, AMA, LKM, ASMA), and metabolic parameters, including ceruloplasmin and immunoglobulin concentrations, were unremarkable.

A diagnosis of ICP was established, and UDCA (15 mg/kg/day) was initiated in combination with cholestyramine. After a few days of treatment, cholestyramine was discontinued due to intolerance, resulting in UDCA monotherapy from 19 to 21 weeks’ gestation. By 21 weeks’ gestation, TBA concentrations had increased to 401 µmol/L, AST to 249 IU/L, ALT to 320 IU/L, and bilirubin to 4.2 mg/dL, prompting initiation of rifampin (150 mg twice daily), and escalation of UDCA to 20 mg/kg/day. Liver transaminases showed partial improvement, and TBA decreased to 250 µmol/L. However, bilirubin continued to rise, reaching 8.7 mg/dL at 23 + 4 weeks’ gestation, prompting rifampin discontinuation.

Genetic-indicated amniocentesis revealed markedly turbid amniotic fluid, raising concern for meconium-staining or bile acid-related discoloration. A liver biopsy was not performed during pregnancy due to its invasive nature and associated risks [13], and because the diagnosis was made clinically based on typical features of ICP and comprehensive biochemical evaluation.

Given the persistence of severe pruritus and ongoing biochemical deterioration, and after excluding autoimmune and other hepatic pathologies, bezafibrate 400 mg daily was initiated. Within 18 days, TBA concentrations fell below 10 µmol/L, liver enzymes normalized within 3 weeks, and bilirubin decreased progressively to normal concentrations at 33 weeks’. Pruritus improved gradually in parallel with liver function recovery and resolved completely upon normalization. At no stage was clinical jaundice observed. Bezafibrate was discontinued, and the UDCA treatment was tapered to UDCA 10 g daily. She delivered a healthy infant at 36 weeks’ gestation without complications. The neonate had an uneventful hospitalization and was discharged on time. Apart from a self-limited transient neonatal jaundice, no adverse outcomes were recorded, and he remained otherwise healthy. UDCA was discontinued a month postpartum. Maternal liver function tests and bile acid concentrations remained normal after delivery, and a postpartum liver biopsy was therefore not indicated.

Discussion

This case represents an unusually severe and early presentation of ICP, with TBA concentrations > 400 µmol/L, marked transaminase elevation, and progressive hyperbilirubinemia. The initial treatment with high-dose UDCA, rifampin, and cholestyramine failed to achieve adequate biochemical or symptomatic control. The turning point was the introduction of bezafibrate, which resulted in rapid and sustained improvement across all clinical and laboratory parameters.

The association between increased serum TBA and adverse perinatal outcomes is well documented. However, there is a paucity of data on the highest-risk group—patients with TBA concentrations exceeding 150–300 µmol/L. Most existing studies analyze risks using broader categories, such as TBA ≥ 100 µmol/L, but do not provide outcome stratification at more extreme concentrations. In fact, the literature contains very few detailed reports of maternal or fetal outcomes in ICP patients with TBA above 300 µmol/L [2–4, 14–16]. This case contributes to bridging that gap by highlighting the importance of assessing both bile acid concentrations and liver function indices in predicting perinatal outcomes.

Furthermore, while TBA concentrations are a critical marker, the presence and severity of liver dysfunction—reflected in markedly increased transaminases, progressive hyperbilirubinemia, and signs of synthetic impairment—may carry independent prognostic value. Severe hepatic involvement in ICP can worsen maternal morbidity and may be associated with increased fetal risk [2, 3]. These observations support the need for a broader clinical approach that includes close monitoring of hepatic indices alongside bile acids to better stratify risk and guide management. Severe ICP may also have long-term implications. Women are at increased risk of future hepatobiliary disease [17], and offspring—particularly from early-onset cases—may have higher odds of neurodevelopmental conditions [18], highlighting the importance of ongoing surveillance.

While UDCA remains the gold standard treatment for ICP, its impact on fetal outcomes remains uncertain [7]. Second-line agents such as rifampin may be used for symptom relief but their use is limited by potential hepatotoxicity and concerns about rising bilirubin, as observed in this case [19]. In this context, fibrates offer a mechanistically sound alternative. Through PPAR-α activation, they reduce bile acid synthesis and promote bile flow. Bezafibrate is now recommended as first-line pharmacologic therapy for moderate to severe cholestatic pruritus in primary sclerosing cholangitis and is increasingly used in other cholestatic liver diseases—though clinical experience is largely confined to non-pregnant populations [20]. A large cohort study found no significant increase in congenital malformation risk with short-term fibrate use in pregnancy, though the study excluded stillborn pregnancies, leaving uncertainty regarding fetal mortality [21].

Grady et al. recently reported two cases of ICP managed with fenofibrate after UDCA failure, both demonstrating substantial clinical and biochemical responses [11]. This current case is, to our knowledge, the first published report of bezafibrate use in ICP, with rapid resolution of pruritus and normalization of bile acids and bilirubin strongly supporting its anticholestatic effect. Additionally, Harrington et al. described a similar presentation—initially resembling ICP but ultimately diagnosed as primary biliary cholangitis—successfully treated with combined UDCA and bezafibrate, and highlighted that combination therapy may be a promising strategy in cholestatic liver disorders during pregnancy [12].

The use of bezafibrate was also considered in light of the patient’s rising bilirubin concentrations, which are atypical in ICP and more commonly associated with hepatic dysfunction or drug-induced liver injury. Following extensive exclusion of alternative etiologies and in the absence of liver biopsy (deferred due to pregnancy), the favorable response to bezafibrate further supports a cholestatic mechanism.

Although a comprehensive cholestasis gene panel testing 77 genes associated with hereditary cholestatic liver disorders revealed no pathogenic variants in our patient, the extreme severity, early onset, and treatment resistance raise the possibility of non-coding or yet-unidentified genetic contributors. Mutations in ABCB4, ABCB11, and ATP8B1 have been implicated in ICP, particularly in early, familial, or oral contraceptive–induced cases. Yeap et al. reported multiple pregnancies affected by severe ICP with transporter gene mutations showing poor response to UDCA and variable benefit from rifampin or naso-biliary drainage. Their findings support the relevance of genetic evaluation in select ICP cases and the potential for genotype-guided therapy [22].

This case underscores the need for expanded therapeutic options for patients with severe, treatment-refractory ICP. While additional data are needed on the safety of fibrates during pregnancy, the present report and others suggest a favorable benefit-risk ratio in select cases, particularly where maternal or fetal risk is high. The absence of complications in this case and a delivery near-term support the careful consideration of bezafibrate in similar scenarios.

Conclusion

This case describes a rare and severe form of ICP with extreme TBA concentrations elevation and marked liver injury, unresponsive to standard therapies. The dramatic and sustained improvement following bezafibrate introduction highlights its potential role in managing refractory ICP. This experience supports the consideration of fibrates as part of a broader, individualized approach. A multidisciplinary strategy remains essential to optimizing maternal and fetal outcomes. Further research is needed to establish the safety, efficacy, and optimal timing of fibrate use in pregnancy.
