Feasibility and Safety of a Novel 4 Fr Sheath Transradial and Transbrachial Approach for Mechanical Thrombectomy in Selected Patients
Authors: Yoichiro NAGAO, Takaomi SINGU, Masaki NAGANUMA, Yuichiro INATOMI, Yoichiro HASHIMOTO, Toshiro YONEHARA, Seigo SHINDO, Mitsuharu UEDA, Makoto NAKAJIMA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The transradial/transbrachial approach for mechanical thrombectomy offers potential reductions in access-site complications, but the feasibility of downsizing to a 4 Fr guiding sheath remains to be systematically assessed. To evaluate the feasibility and safety of mechanical thrombectomy performed through a 4 Fr transradial/transbrachial approach in selected patients and to compare procedural metrics with those of a contemporaneous transfemoral approach cohort treated at the same center, we retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients who underwent mechanical thrombectomy between December 2023 and December 2024. Overall, 17 patients were treated with a 4 Fr sheath through the right transradial/transbrachial approach and 87 with transfemoral approach. Baseline demographics, procedural times, recanalization rates, and access-site complications were compared using the Mann-Whitney U or χ2 tests, as appropriate. Successful recanalization (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade ≥ 2b) was achieved in 14 of 17 cases (82%) in the 4 Fr group, comparable to 78 of 87 cases (90%) in the transfemoral approach group (p = 0.39). Median puncture-to-recanalization time was 34 min (interquartile range 27.8-67) versus 36 min (interquartile range 23-56.5) (p = 0.78). No symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage occurred in the 4 Fr cohort. Access-site complications were limited to 1 minor superficial hematoma (6%), whereas the transfemoral approach group experienced 3 access-site events (3.4%), all pseudoaneurysms. A 4 Fr transradial/transbrachial approach for mechanical thrombectomy is feasible and indicates a favorable safety profile without prolonging procedure time in carefully selected right-sided or posterior circulation lesions with low thrombus burden. Larger prospective studies are warranted to confirm these signals and to explore applicability to broader patient populations.

Introduction

The transradial approach (TRA) is increasingly recognized as a safe and effective alternative to the traditional transfemoral approach (TFA) in mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for acute ischemic stroke. Several studies have reported the comparable efficacy and safety profiles of TRA and TFA in selected cases.1-5) The TRA offers notable advantages, including a reduced risk of access-site complications, earlier patient mobilization, and improved patient satisfaction, making it a preferred technique in specific neuroendovascular procedures.6-9)

The TRA typically uses 5 Fr or 6 Fr sheaths; however, large-bore (5 Fr-6 Fr) radial sheaths in both coronary10) and neurointerventional settings8) have been linked to access-site complications reaching 6%-10%, and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rates of 8% have been reported in a multicenter 6 Fr-TRA series.11) Downsizing to a 4 Fr system may therefore mitigate these risks.

SOFIAFLOW (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan) and 5 Fr Esperance (Wallaby, Shanghai, China), despite being 5 Fr aspiration catheters, are compatible with 4 Fr sheaths, allowing the application of combined thrombectomy techniques. This compatibility has the potential to reduce access-site complications using smaller sheaths while maintaining procedural efficacy. Nevertheless, there have been no reports on the use of combined techniques with a 4 Fr sheath in the TRA for neuroendovascular thrombectomy.

In the present study, we aimed to report our initial experience with a technique using a 4 Fr sheath through the right TRA/transbrachial approach (TRA/TBA) for thrombectomy in patients with acute ischemic stroke. Specifically, we compared procedural efficacy and safety in a 4 Fr TRA/TBA cohort and a contemporaneous TFA cohort treated at the same center. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to systematically evaluate the safety and efficacy of this approach, highlighting its potential as a viable and less invasive alternative to traditional methods.

Materials and Methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study included consecutive patients with acute ischemic stroke who underwent MT between December 2023 and December 2024. This single-center study compared 2 contemporaneous cohorts: patients treated with a 4 Fr sheath through the 4 Fr TRA/TBA group and those treated with the TFA group during the same period.

Patients were selected for endovascular therapy on the basis of the following inclusion criteria:

- Age ≥18 years, with no upper limit applied

- Pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≤ 2, indicating functionally independent before stroke onset

- Neurological severity: National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) ≥6 at admission,12) consistent with moderate-to-severe neurological deficit

- Imaging findings: Large vessel occlusion confirmed by computed tomography (CT) angiography or magnetic resonance angiography involving the anterior circulation (middle cerebral artery [MCA] M1, M2) or posterior circulation (basilar artery [BA], posterior cerebral artery [PCA])

- Time window: Onset-to-door time within 6 h for anterior circulation strokes and within 24 h for posterior circulation strokes or wake-up strokes based on clinical-radiological mismatch

- Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) score ≥3 for anterior circulation strokes or Posterior Circulation-ASPECTS (PC-ASPECTS) ≥613) for posterior circulation strokes

The TRA/TBA approach using a 4 Fr sheath for MT was initiated in December 2023. The decision to perform treatment using a 4 Fr sheath through the TRA/TBA was made at the discretion of the surgeon. In particular, the 4 Fr approach was used in cases in which the operator deemed that MT could be sufficiently achieved using a 5 Fr aspiration catheter on the basis of preprocedural imaging and clinical factors. The decision to perform TBA instead of TRA was based on preprocedural assessment. Specifically, in cases when the radial artery was not palpable or considered difficult to cannulate, or when Allen's test was negative, the brachial artery approach was selected to ensure safe and reliable access. Specifically, we selected cases involving the right MCA, BA, or PCA.

On the basis of the previously reported literature, cases with anterior circulation occlusions on the left side were excluded.6,14) Moreover, the gradient-echo susceptibility vessel sign on T2*-weighted imaging was used to evaluate thrombus burden, and cases with smaller thrombus volumes were prioritized for treatment.15) This study was approved by our institutional review board (Approval Number 1336), which waived the requirement for written informed consent. For comparative purposes, we also identified all patients who underwent MT through the TFA during the same study period (December 2023 to December 2024). This contemporaneous TFA cohort (n = 87) served as the control group for procedural and safety analyses.

Procedure using a 4 Fr sheath through the right radial artery

In this study, we used a 4 Fr sheath for neurointervention involving the right radial artery.

The radial artery was punctured using the single-wall technique with a 20-gauge needle. After successful cannulation of the right radial artery with a 4 Fr sheath, a radial artery angiogram was performed to visualize the brachial bifurcation. The 4 Fr sheath was replaced with a 4 Fr FUBUKI guiding sheath (Asahi Intecc, Tokyo, Japan) (Fig. 1A).

mTICI: modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction

The 4 Fr FUBUKI was carefully advanced into the right subclavian artery using a 5 Fr MSK inner catheter (Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) and a standard 0.035-inch guidewire (Radius Guidewire, 0.035 inches, 150 cm [Terumo, Tokyo, Japan]). The 4 Fr FUBUKI and 5 Fr MSK catheters showed compatibility, allowing the 5 Fr MSK to be coaxially inserted into the 4 Fr FUBUKI sheath. The outer diameter (OD) of the 5 Fr MSK is 1.70 mm (0.067 inches), which fits within the inner diameter (ID) of the 4 Fr FUBUKI, measuring 1.80 mm (0.071 inches). This compatibility enables seamless insertion while maintaining a minimally invasive approach. If feasible, the FUBUKI was advanced directly into the right common carotid artery using an MSK catheter in the coaxial system. If this proved challenging, we formed a Simmons shape by inverting the guidewire and MSK catheter at the aortic valve; subsequently, the guiding sheath was pulled back and directly cannulated into the right common carotid artery. Subsequently, a FUBUKI guiding sheath was inserted into the internal carotid artery (ICA). In patients requiring access to the posterior circulation, the sheath was navigated directly into the right vertebral artery (VA).

Endovascular procedure

All neurointerventions were performed under local anesthesia, and the procedures were performed by Y.N. and T.S, certified specialists in endovascular treatment.

After setting the 4 Fr FUBUKI guiding sheath at the targeted ICA or VA, MT was performed (Fig. 1B). The specifications of the SOFIAFLOW aspiration catheter (Terumo) and 4 Fr FUBUKI guiding sheath indicated that the SOFIAFLOW can be coaxially inserted into the 4 Fr FUBUKI guiding sheath. The OD of the SOFIAFLOW is 1.70 mm (0.067 inches), which fits within the ID of the 4 Fr FUBUKI, measuring 1.80 mm (0.071 inches). This compatibility allows the use of the SOFIAFLOW catheter inside the 4 Fr FUBUKI sheath, enabling minimally invasive treatment through radial artery puncture. Similarly, the 5 Fr Esperance aspiration catheter (Wallaby, Shanghai, China) is a mid-lumen catheter with an ID of 0.055 inches, which can be inserted into the 4 Fr FUBUKI by virtue of its maximum OD of 1.68 mm. The AXS Vecta46 aspiration catheter (Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA), with an inner lumen of 0.046 inches and a maximum OD of 1.48 mm, is another suitable option for this configuration.

Therefore, SOFIAFLOW, 5 Fr Esperance, and AXS Vecta 46 were coaxially navigated into the occluded vessel using a microcatheter and a 0.014-inch guidewire. After removing the microcatheter and guidewire, a direct aspiration first-pass technique (ADAPT)16) was performed. A combined technique was selected at the operator's discretion. The microcatheter was used to cross the lesion; next, a stent retriever was deployed, and the aspiration catheter was advanced to the occlusion site to capture and retrieve the clot in a single unit (Fig. 1C).

After successful recanalization (Fig. 1D), hemostasis was achieved using a TR Band (Terumo) for radial artery compression to ensure proper closure and minimize the risk of complications.17) The compression was applied using the TR Band for 4 h.

Data collection and outcome measurement

Baseline characteristics and angiographic results were obtained from the medical records. The same variables were extracted for the contemporaneous TFA cohort to enable groupwise comparison. All patients underwent MT using available devices. The choice of method and device was left to the treating physician.

The following parameters were included in this study: demographic data, such as age, sex, vascular risk factors, pre-stroke mRS score, NIHSS score at admission, ASPECTS and PC-ASPECTS scores, stroke subtype, and the use of intravenous recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rt-PA) were recorded. The mRS score at 90 days was used as the primary clinical outcome measure. The primary angiographic outcome was successful recanalization (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction [mTICI] ≥ 2b). Secondary outcomes comprised complete recanalization (mTICI 3), puncture-to-recanalization time, first-pass effect, and access-site complications.

The location of the vessel occlusion was categorized by laterality and into specific arterial segments: M1 proximal (M1P), M1 distal (M1D), M2, M3, BA, and PCA segments P1 and P2.

The procedural details included the puncture site (right radial, right brachial, or right femoral converted to right brachial), thrombectomy technique (ADAPT, stent retriever, or combined technique), and number of device passes. Time metrics were also recorded: onset-to-door, door-to-imaging, door-to-needle, door-to-puncture, puncture-to-guide, puncture-to-recanalization, and onset-to-recanalization (all minutes).

Recanalization was assessed using the mTICI grading system. Successful recanalization was defined as a final mTICI grade ≥2b, with further stratification into complete recanalization (mTICI 3). First-pass effect was also noted.

Procedural complications were evaluated, including access-site-related events, such as superficial hematoma and pseudoaneurysm formation. A follow-up CT scan was performed immediately after the procedure and on the following day. Intracranial complications included postoperative subarachnoid hemorrhage, intracranial hemorrhage within 24 h, and symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage within 24 h.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared with the unpaired Student's t-test; non-normal data are expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and were compared using the χ2 test; Fisher's exact test was applied when any expected cell count was <5. To explore the independent association between access route (4 Fr TRA/TBA vs TFA) and successful recanalization (mTICI ≥ 2b), we constructed a univariate logistic regression model. Given the limited sample size, multivariable adjustment was not performed. All tests were 2-sided, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted with JMP Pro 17.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Results

A total of 598 patients with ischemic stroke were admitted, of whom 219 had code stroke activations, 66 received rt-PA therapy, and 113 underwent endovascular therapy procedures between December 2023 and December 2024.

Among these, 26 cases were initially approached through the upper limb, and 87 cases were approached through the femoral artery. There were no cases in which the puncture site was changed from the 4 Fr radial approach to the femoral approach. Of the cases initially approached through the femoral artery, the puncture site was changed during treatment in 2 cases. One case was switched to radial artery puncture using a 4 Fr sheath and the other to brachial artery puncture using a 4 Fr sheath. Moreover, in 2 cases, the puncture site was switched from the femoral artery, bringing the total number of cases treated using a 4 Fr sheath to 17. In the 4 Fr TRA/TBA cohort (n = 17), the median age was 83 years (IQR 76-90.5), and 53% were male, whereas the contemporaneous TFA cohort (n = 87) had a median age of 81 years (IQR 75-88), with 55% being male; none of these differences reached statistical significance (all p > 0.60). The median pre-stroke mRS score was 1 (IQR, 0-2) vs 0 (IQR 0-2) in the TFA group (p = 0.38), whereas the mRS score at 90 days was 4 (IQR 0-5) vs 4 (IQR 1-5) (p = 0.98). On admission, the median NIHSS score was 14 (IQR 10-23) versus 21 (IQR 15-29) (p = 0.04). Right-sided occlusions predominated in the 4 Fr cohort (88% vs 38%, p < 0.01). The median ASPECTS and PC-ASPECTS scores were 7.5 (IQR 5.5-9.3) and 9 (IQR 9-9), respectively. Cardioembolic stroke was the most common subtype observed in 10 patients (59%); 1 patient (6%) had large artery atherosclerosis; 2 (12%) had other determined etiologies, and 4 (24%) had an undetermined etiology. Intravenous thrombolysis was administered in 6 patients (35%).

All MCA cases involved occlusions in the right hemisphere, with the most commonly occluded vessels being the MCA (M1P, 3 cases; M1D, 6 cases; M2, 5 cases). One patient had a BA occlusion; 2 patients had a PCA occlusion. The median time intervals were as follows: onset-to-door, 202 min (IQR 51-366); door-to-imaging, 27 min (IQR 22-37); door-to-intravenous thrombolysis, 59 min (IQR 52-82); and door-to-arterial puncture, 71 min (IQR 67-92). The puncture-to-guide catheter placement was 18 min (IQR 14.3-28.3), and the puncture-to-recanalization time was 34 min (IQR 27.8-67). The overall onset-to-recanalization time was 318.5 min (IQR 167.5-547). Corresponding medians in the TFA group were 14 min, 36 min, and 267 min, respectively (all p > 0.05). Successful recanalization (mTICI ≥ 2b) was achieved in 14 of 17 cases (82%) in the 4 Fr group and in 78 of 87 cases (90%) in the TFA group (p = 0.39). Complete recanalization (mTICI 3) occurred in 59% vs 62%, respectively. The median puncture-to-recanalization time was 34 min (IQR 27.8-67) for 4 Fr versus 36 min (23-56.5) for TFA (p = 0.78). The number of device passes was comparable (median 2 vs 2, p = 0.39), and the modified first-pass effect was 53% vs 56% (p = 0.8), respectively.

The ADAPT technique was used in 12 patients (71%), whereas a combined approach was used in 5 patients (29%). Postoperative subarachnoid hemorrhage occurred in 1 patient (6%), and intracranial hemorrhage within 24 h was observed in 2 patients (12%). No symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage occurred in the 4 Fr group, whereas 3 cases (3%) were noted after TFA (p = 0.44). Postoperative subarachnoid hemorrhage was 6% vs 23% (p = 0.11). Access-site complications differed substantially: the 4 Fr cohort had no pseudoaneurysms, whereas the TFA cohort experienced 3 pseudoaneurysms (3.4%). No conversions from 4 Fr radial to femoral access were required (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

In the present study, we performed thrombectomy using a combined technique with aspiration catheters of 5 Fr or smaller through a 4 Fr sheath through TRA/TBA in 17 patients with acute ischemic stroke. The step-by-step workflow of this minimally invasive technique is illustrated in Fig. 1. Successful recanalization defined as final mTICI ≥ 2b was achieved in 82% of the patients, with a complete recanalization (mTICI 3) rate of 59%. The median number of passes was 2, and the first-pass effect was achieved in 35% of the patients. Regarding safety, postoperative subarachnoid hemorrhage occurred in 1 patient (6%), and intracranial hemorrhage within 24 h occurred in 2 patients (12%), none of whom were symptomatic. Access-site complications were minimal, with only 1 superficial hematoma and no pseudoaneurysms. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to describe the use of a combined technique through TRA/TBA using a 4 Fr sheath in a case series of this size.

The 4 Fr group showed non-inferior procedural efficacy to that in the contemporaneous TFA cohort: final mTICI ≥ 2b was 82% vs 90% (p = 0.39), and the puncture-to-recanalization time was 34 min vs 36 min (p = 0.78), indicating that downsizing the sheath did not compromise recanalization success or prolong the procedure. These findings support positioning our work as evidence of feasibility and safety.

Previous studies have assessed the efficacy and safety of TRA in MT, often in comparison with traditional TFA. Siddiqui et al.18) reported a lower recanalization rate in the TRA group (mTICI ≥ 2b: 79.6%) than in TFA (91.4%) but also observed fewer symptomatic hemorrhagic complications (spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage [sICH]: 0.8% vs 2.3%) and access-site issues (0.8% vs 3.9%).

Similarly, Phillips et al.4) showed non-inferior procedural outcomes with TRA and a significantly lower rate of access-site complications (0% with TRA vs 6.5% with TFA).

In our study, the recanalization rate (mTICI ≥2b: 82%) and first-pass success (35%) were consistent with or exceeded those in previous TRA reports, despite the exclusive use of a 4 Fr sheath and a high median age (83 years). Notably, we observed no sICH (0%) and only 1 minor subcutaneous hematoma that required no additional treatment. No major complications were observed, further supporting the safety of this minimally invasive approach. In a randomized coronary trial, 6 Fr-TRA produced a 6% access-site complication rate, whereas no complications were observed with 4 Fr sheaths.10) Similarly, radial-artery spasm occurred in 6%-10% of 5 Fr-6 Fr neuro-angiography cases.8) The multicenter STAR registry reported symptomatic ICH in 8% of 6 Fr-TRA thrombectomy cases and distal emboli in 23%.11) By contrast, our 4 Fr cohort experienced only 1 minor superficial hematoma (6%) and no symptomatic ICH, underscoring the potential safety benefit of sheath downsizing.

Compared with earlier studies in which TRA was reserved for patients with tortuous vascular anatomy, our series also included patients with standard arch anatomy, showing the broader applicability of 4 Fr TRA, particularly for right MCA occlusions.

In recent years, TRA and TBA have also been used in neurointervention. Several studies, including that by Feldman et al.,19) have indicated that TRA reduces the risk of vascular complications and bleeding, particularly during coronary interventions. In the field of neurointervention, large-scale studies, such as those by Li et al.,20) have consistently shown that TRA is associated with lower complication rates than those in TFA, particularly in flow-diverting stent placement. Khanna et al.21) reported that TRA yielded procedural outcomes similar to those of TFA while maintaining a favorable safety profile.

Parallel to the growing use of TRA and TBA, attention has also been paid to reducing the sheath size to further minimize invasiveness. There have been several reports on the use of a 4 Fr sheath for intracranial aneurysm treatment, including 3 cases by Mizokami et al.22) and the use of a 4.3 Fr guiding sheath system in neuroendovascular therapy by Hori et al.23) Mizokami et al.22) reported that all 3 procedures were successful, with no procedural or access-site complications. The authors concluded that the use of a 4 Fr sheath through the transbrachial approach was safe and effective in older patients. Similarly, Hori et al.23) reported a procedural success rate of 98.6% across 74 patients, with only 3 instances (4.1%) of minor subcutaneous hematomas and no major complications, and noted the potential for reduced post-procedure bed rest in some cases. This report also included 16 patients with acute cerebral artery occlusion; however, the specific techniques used were not described.

To date, there have been no reports of treatments using a 4 Fr sheath that use advanced techniques, such as the combined technique or ADAPT. In this study, we revealed that the use of a 4 Fr sheath might not only minimize access-site complications but also allow the insertion of an aspiration catheter into the 4 Fr sheath, enabling the application of thrombectomy using the combined technique or ADAPT. This supports the potential for the wider adoption of the radial approach in MT.

One of the key advantages of the 4 Fr sheath is its minimally invasive nature, which aligns with recent trends in neurointervention aimed at reducing procedural invasiveness. Smaller sheath size is associated with fewer complications.8,10) Moreover, the 4 Fr TRA allows quicker postprocedural recovery, with patients being able to mobilize sooner than those who underwent the femoral approach.23) This is particularly relevant for older patients, who comprised a significant proportion of our cohort.

In the present case, we intentionally avoided TBA/TRA in a patient with left MCA occlusion. Notably, the right radial artery approach is not suitable for all patients. When accessing left-sided intracranial artery lesions through the right radial artery approach, navigational difficulties and an extended time for guide placement are anticipated because of anatomical limitations. In time-sensitive treatments, such as acute-phase recanalization therapy, the right radial artery approach may not be ideal for left-sided occlusive lesions. The development of devices that allow smoother access is eagerly awaited. Furthermore, given balloon-guiding catheters cannot be used with this approach, there is an increased risk of distal embolization in patients with a large thrombus burden. Therefore, this approach is more suitable for lesions with a smaller thrombus burden, such as peripheral occlusions.

Despite these promising results, this study has some limitations. First, the study population was highly selected: almost 90% of lesions were right-sided, and cases with a large thrombus burden were excluded; this selection bias limits generalizability. This was a retrospective, single-center study with a small sample size and a potential selection bias. Second, this technique has only been applied to right-sided occlusions; its applicability to left-sided lesions remains unclear. Third, balloon-guiding catheters, which are compatible with 4 Fr systems, may limit the embolic protection in some cases. Fourth, although safety advantages of sheath downsizing are supported by coronary and diagnostic neuro-angiography data, direct head-to-head trials in stroke thrombectomy are lacking, and the external data sets are heterogeneous.

In addition, large-bore aspiration catheters cannot be used with a 4 Fr system, which may reduce the efficacy of the procedure, particularly in cases involving ICA occlusion.

Future prospective, multicenter studies with propensity-matched or randomized comparisons with TFA are warranted to validate the feasibility and safety signals observed here.

Conclusions

The 4 Fr TRA/TBA for MT in acute ischemic stroke indicated a high rate of successful recanalization without symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage or major access-site complications. These findings suggest that the 4 Fr radial and brachial approaches may be safe, effective, and less invasive alternatives to femoral access in selected cases. These results highlight the potential of 4 Fr TRA and TBA to become preferred techniques in neurointerventions for selected cases.
