Preventing secondary primary malignancies (SPMs) in CAR-T cell therapy through site-specific transgene integration into genomic safe harbors (GSHs)
Authors: Pooria Safarzadeh Kozani, Pouya Safarzadeh Kozani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy has revolutionized oncology by achieving durable remissions in refractory hematologic malignancies. However, emerging reports link this therapy to second primary malignancies, including CAR+ lymphomas and leukemias, driven by insertional mutagenesis from semi-random viral vector integration near oncogenes or tumor suppressor loci. These rare but serious complications underscore the dual challenge of eradicating primary tumors while mitigating delayed genotoxic risks. Conventional CAR-T cell manufacturing, reliant on gamma-retroviral or lentiviral vectors, introduces genomic instability through integration into fragile sites or transcriptionally active regions. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing further amplifies risks via off-target double-strand breaks and chromosomal rearrangements. This review evaluates genomic safe harbors (GSHs)—such as AAVS1, TRAC, CCR5, ROSA26 and CLYBL—as loci validated for stable, high-level CAR transgene expression without oncogenic disruption. GSHs meet stringent criteria: distal from cancer-related genes, resistant to epigenetic silencing, and transcriptionally permissive. Preclinical studies demonstrate that site-directed CAR integration into GSHs preserves antitumor efficacy while eliminating malignant transformation risks. Challenges persist in optimizing homology-directed repair efficiency, mitigating residual dsDNA toxicity, and standardizing regulatory frameworks for long-term genomic surveillance. Emerging technologies—base/prime editing, hybrid nucleases, and rigorous monitoring—promise enhanced precision and safety. By reconciling therapeutic innovation with genomic integrity, GSH-engineered CAR-T cells herald a paradigm shift toward precision immunotherapies, offering curative potential while preempting secondary oncogenesis. Collaborative efforts to refine manufacturing, harmonize global standards, and prioritize patient-specific risk stratification will be critical to advancing this transformative approach.Keywords: Cancer immunotherapy, Secondary primary malignancies, Genomic safe harbors, Insertional mutagenesis, Chimeric antigen receptor

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy represents a paradigm shift in cancer treatment, offering durable remissions for patients with refractory hematologic malignancies. By genetically engineering T cells to express synthetic receptors targeting tumor-associated antigens, CAR-T cell therapies bypass conventional human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-restricted recognition, enabling potent antitumor responses. The clinical success of CD19-targeted CAR-T cells in relapsed/refractory B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) has solidified their role as a cornerstone of precision oncology [1, 2]. For example, axicabtagene ciloleucel and tisagenlecleucel, US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved CD19-directed CAR-T cell products, achieve complete response rates of over 50% in DLBCL and 80% in pediatric B-ALL, with many patients remaining disease-free beyond 5 years [1–3]. This efficacy stems from CAR-T cells’ ability to proliferate in vivo, persist long-term, and establish immunological memory. Beyond CD19, CAR-T cell platforms targeting B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) for multiple myeloma and CD22 for ALL have expanded therapeutic applications, underscoring the adaptability of this approach [4, 5]. The modular design of CAR constructs—comprising antigen-binding domains (human, animal-derived, or humanized scFvs or nanobodies), costimulatory motifs (e.g., CD28, 4-1BB), and activation signaling domains—enables fine-tuning of T-cell activation, persistence, and exhaustion profiles, further enhancing clinical outcomes [6–8].

Despite its transformative potential, CAR-T cell therapy is increasingly associated with a rare but serious complication, secondary primary malignancies (SPMs). These are distinct, therapy-induced cancers arising months to years post-treatment, often linked to genotoxic insults from viral vector integration or off-target genome editing [9]. In January 2024, the US FDA announced an investigation into several cases of T-cell malignancies, including CAR+ tumors, in patients treated with BCMA- or CD19-redirected CAR-T cell products [9, 10]. A 2024 study documented the first comprehensive genomic analysis of T-cell lymphoma (TCL) developing after commercial CAR19 therapy (axi-cel/brexu-cel) in a 59-year-old patient with DLBCL [11]. Post-infusion, EBV expansion and a dominant T-cell Receptor (TCR) clone emerged alongside novel chromosomal alterations (chr1q amplification, chr6q deletion) in ctDNA, absent in pre-treatment samples. scRNA-seq confirmed a clonal T-cell population without CAR19 expression, suggesting a pre-existing TCL clone activated post-therapy [11]. Ongoing research aims to clarify lineage relationships (shared vs. secondary malignancy) through genotyping, EBV analysis, and retroviral mapping, highlighting late toxicity risks despite CAR-T cell therapy efficacy [11]. While current SPM incidence rates are estimated at < 1%, these events underscore the dual challenge of eradicating primary tumors while mitigating delayed genotoxic risks.

The principal mechanism of SPMs lies in the semi-random integration of viral vectors—lentiviral or gamma-retroviral—used during CAR-T cell manufacturing. Gamma-retroviruses preferentially integrate near transcription start sites and proto-oncogenes (e.g., LMO2) in gene-dense regions, which can be linked to insertional mutagenesis risks [12]. On the other hand, lentiviruses favor gene bodies (intragenic regions) rather than promoters [12, 13]. While safer than gamma-retroviruses, integration into fragile genomic sites or tumor suppressors (e.g., TP53) can theoretically disrupt gene function, though documented cases are rare [12, 13]. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing, employed in next-generation CAR-T cell platforms, introduces additional risks: off-target double-strand breaks (DSBs) may lead to chromosomal translocations or inactivation of tumor suppressors, particularly in hematopoietic progenitors [14]. Host factors further modulate risk; patients with preexisting genomic instability from prior therapies (e.g., fludarabine, radiation), germline mutations, or inherited DNA repair deficiencies face heightened susceptibility [15, 16]. Prolonged CAR-T cell persistence, though therapeutically beneficial, exacerbates cumulative genotoxic stress, creating a permissive environment for malignant transformation.

Conventional CAR-T cell manufacturing, reliant on semi-random viral integration, inherently risks oncogenic disruption of critical genes. This has spurred interest in site-directed transgene integration into genomic safe harbors (GSHs)—loci validated for stable, high-level transgene expression without oncogenic consequences [17]. GSHs such as AAVS1 (19q13.42), CCR5, and TRAC (T-cell receptor alpha constant) meet stringent criteria: they reside distal to oncogenes, avoid fragile sites, and resist epigenetic silencing [17]. For instance, targeting the TRAC locus enables dual benefits; CAR integration disrupts endogenous TCR expression, reducing graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) risk in allogeneic products, while maintaining uniform CAR activity [18].

Site-directed integration leverages CRISPR/Cas9, transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), or zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs) to precisely insert CAR constructs into GSHs via homology-directed repair (HDR) [18, 19]. Compared to viral vectors, this approach eliminates positional variability in transgene expression, reducing clonal dominance and exhaustion. Researchers have engineered CAR-T cells using AAV-based (closed-ended linear duplex DNA) CELiD vectors for precise, safe integration into the AAVS1 locus. Co-electroporating CD19-CAR CELiD vectors with pCMV-Rep achieved 22.8% site-specific integration [20]. Engineered cells (selected via G418) produced elevated cytokines and demonstrated target-cell killing in vitro [20]. This non-viral method minimizes genomic risks, enhancing CAR-T cell safety by avoiding random viral integration [20]. Furthermore, GSH platforms enable multiplexed editing (e.g., combined CAR integration and PD-1 knockout) without cumulative genotoxicity, addressing limitations of conventional CAR-T cell and transient RNA-based systems. In this review, we critically evaluate the mechanisms underlying CAR-T cell-related SPMs, assess the role of GSHs in mitigating genotoxic risks, and discuss emerging genome-editing strategies to harmonize therapeutic efficacy with long-term genomic safety. We further analyze preclinical and clinical evidence supporting GSH-targeted CAR-T cell platforms, address persistent challenges in manufacturing scalability, and outline regulatory frameworks essential for advancing safer immunotherapies. By reconciling durable therapeutic activity with genomic safety, GSH-based engineering represents a critical advance toward precision-engineered immunotherapies.

SPM after CAR-T cell therapy

Clinical evidence and case reports

The emergence of secondary malignancies following CAR-T cell therapy, though rare, has raised significant clinical and regulatory concerns. Recent case reports and pharmacovigilance data highlight instances of T-cell lymphomas, leukemias, and myelodysplastic syndromes arising months to years post-treatment [10]. According to a recent report by Ghilardi and colleagues, a patient developed CD8+ TCL with a JAK3 mutation three months after CD19-redirected CAR-T cell therapy for B-cell lymphoma [10]. The CAR transgene was minimal, and the T-cell clone was detected pre-infusion [10]. Among 449 CAR-T cell recipients (targeting CD19 or BCMA), 3.6% (16 patients) developed secondary malignancies, with median onset times of 26.4 months for solid tumors and 9.7 months for hematologic types [10]. Projected 5-year risks were 15.2% for solid and 2.3% for hematologic malignancies. A single TCL case suggests such events are rare after CAR-T cell therapy [10]. According to another investigation, Wang et al. reported two secondary malignancies (myelodysplastic syndrome and acute myeloid leukemia) emerging 25.2 and 37.5 months after therapy [21]. These cases were not linked to KTE-X19, and no replication-competent retrovirus instances were identified [21]. The study highlights late-onset hematologic malignancies post-treatment but confirms no direct association with the CAR-T cell therapy itself, underscoring the need for long-term monitoring while affirming the therapy’s safety profile regarding retroviral risks and treatment-related secondary cancers [21].

Mechanisms

Direct genotoxic mechanisms from insertional mutagenesis by viral vectors

The development of secondary malignancies following CAR-T cell therapy is a rare but serious concern, with insertional mutagenesis representing the primary biological mechanism (Fig. 1). This process occurs when viral vectors used to deliver the CAR transgene integrate into the host genome in a semi-random manner. Such integrations can disrupt critical regulatory regions, such as promoters or enhancers, or directly inactivate tumor suppressor genes or activate proto-oncogenes [22]. Lentiviral and gamma-retroviral vectors, the two most common delivery systems in CAR-T cell manufacturing, exhibit distinct genomic integration patterns that influence their oncogenic potential. Gamma-retroviral vectors preferentially integrate near transcriptionally active regions, including promoters of proto-oncogenes like LMO2, which has been linked to leukemogenesis in early gene therapy trials [22]. According to a report by Howe and colleagues from a SCID-X1 gene therapy trial, gamma-retroviral vector integration upstream of LMO2 led to its aberrant overexpression and clonal T-ALL [23]. Leukemogenesis was further driven by cooperating mutations, including NOTCH1 activation, CDKN2A deletion, and TCR-beta translocation [23]. These findings underscore the genomic instability risks posed by enhancer-containing vectors and the potential for emergence of pathogenic T cell clones [23]. In contrast, lentiviral vectors tend to integrate within gene bodies rather than promoter regions, theoretically reducing but not eliminating the risk of oncogenic disruption. However, lentiviral integrations near genomic "fragile sites"—regions prone to breaks or rearrangements during replication—can still destabilize the genome, particularly if insertions occur near tumor suppressor loci. For example, integration of the CAR transgene into the TP53 locus, a tumor suppressor gene frequently mutated or deleted in cancers, can be linked to post-CAR-T cell therapy lymphomas. This highlights how even a single disruptive integration event can synergize with acquired somatic mutations to drive malignant transformation.

Secondary primary malignancies (SPMs) arising from random CAR transgene integration. The semi-random integration of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) transgenes into the T-cell genome generates a heterogeneous population of CAR-T cells with variable integration loci. Current FDA-approved CAR-T cell products are manufactured using gamma-retroviral or self-inactivating (SIN) lentiviral vectors, which deliver CAR transgenes into autologous T cells via semi-random genomic integration. This process yields a polyclonal CAR-T cell product wherein individual T cells harbor distinct integration sites. However, the insertion of CAR transgenes into functionally critical genomic regions—such as proto-oncogenes, tumor suppressor loci, or fragile sites—may induce clonal expansion of malignant CAR+ T cells through insertional mutagenesis. Such events represent a rare but significant risk of therapy-related SPMs directly attributable to CAR-T cell treatment

Genomic instability from genome editing

Emerging next-generation CAR-T cell platforms that employ CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing introduce additional risks. While CRISPR enables precise knock-in of CAR constructs or disruption of immune checkpoint genes (e.g., PD-1), off-target effects remain a concern [24, 25]. CRISPR-guided nucleases can induce unintended DSBs at genomic loci with partial sequence homology to the guide RNA [24, 25]. Such off-target edits may lead to chromosomal translocations, such as those involving the T cell receptor loci, or inactivation of tumor suppressors like TP53 or PTEN [25]. In a study by Kosicki and colleagues, it was revealed that CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing in normal cells (mouse embryonic stem cells, human RPE1, and progenitor cells) frequently causes extensive on-target genomic damage, including large deletions (up to megabases), insertions, inversions, and complex rearrangements, even with single gRNAs [25]. These lesions, often missed by standard short-range PCR assays, pose significant risks in clinical applications due to potential pathogenic consequences like cancer [25]. The findings highlight the necessity for comprehensive genomic analysis to detect such unintended mutations before therapeutic use, challenging current safety assumptions of CRISPR/Cas9-based therapies [25].

Immune dysregulation and non-mutagenic clonal expansion

Recently, Albittar et al. described a novel association between prolonged cytopenias and clonal T-cell large granular lymphocyte (T-LGL) expansions involving CAR-T cell populations in patients receiving CD19-targeted CAR-T cell therapy for relapsed/refractory large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL) [26]. In their case series, three patients treated with tisagenlecleucel, axicabtagene ciloleucel, or lisocabtagene maraleucel developed persistent cytopenias (grade 1–4) weeks to months after infusion, accompanied by oligoclonal or clonal T-LGL populations detected in peripheral blood and bone marrow [26]. Strikingly, flow cytometry confirmed CAR transgene expression within the T-LGL subsets in two cases (43.9% and 65.0% CAR positivity, respectively), suggesting CAR-T cell-derived clonality [26]. Notably, canonical STAT3 or STAT5B mutations—frequently implicated in T-LGL leukemias—were absent in all patients [26]. These cases highlight T-LGL proliferation as a potential driver of late hematologic toxicity post–CAR-T cell therapy, with clonal expansions persisting up to 52 months in one patient [26]. Albittar et al. propose that CAR-T cell activation dynamics, rather than traditional oncogenic mutations, may underlie this phenomenon, warranting further investigation into CAR-T cell-mediated lymphoid dysregulation as a contributor to prolonged cytopenias [26].

Host and environmental contributing factors

Beyond these mechanisms, other factors may indirectly contribute to genomic instability. NF-κB activation, as seen in human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1)–associated adult T-cell leukemia (ATL), induces DNA DSBs via R-loop accumulation, triggering senescence [27]. However, cells deficient in transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair (TC-NER) factors escape this arrest, enabling clonal expansion despite genomic damage [27]. Such mechanisms may foster genomic instability and promote emergence of pathogenic T-cell clones under selective pressures [27]. Moreover, fever, common during CAR-T-cell therapy, may influence T cell genomic stability. At 39 °C, CD4+ T cells show increased proliferation and effector function, while regulatory T cell suppression decreases [28]. However, T helper 1 (TH1) cells uniquely undergo mitochondrial stress and DNA damage, activating p53 and interferon pathways [28]. Surviving TH1 cells adapt with increased mitochondrial mass and activity, suggesting fever may promote emergence of pathogenic clones via selective pressure and genomic instability [28]. Additionally, CAR-T cells with dysregulated apoptosis pathways (e.g., due to BCL-2 overexpression) may evade normal turnover, increasing the likelihood of accumulating oncogenic mutations [29, 30]. In a study, Cox and colleagues demonstrated that by preventing apoptosis, BCL-2 enables survival of oxidatively damaged cells, promoting tumorigenesis through impaired clearance of compromised cells [29].

Host susceptibility

The risk of SPMs is modulated by interrelated factors, including vector design, host genomics, and prior therapies. Host factors also play a critical role; patients with preexisting genomic instability from prior DNA-damaging therapies (e.g., alkylating agents, radiation) or inherited mutations in DNA repair genes (e.g., BRCA1, ATM) may exhibit heightened susceptibility to insertional mutagenesis [16]. Additionally, prolonged CAR-T cell persistence, while beneficial for sustained antitumor activity, increases cumulative exposure to genotoxic stressors. Fludarabine, a purine analog known to induce chromosomal breaks, has been implicated in the development of therapy-related myeloid neoplasms, particularly when used in combination with other DNA-damaging agents [31]. Campos-Nebel and colleagues explored how fludarabine, causes DNA DSB repaired primarily by error-prone non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), not homologous recombination (HR; a precise repair mechanism) [32]. Fludarabine-treated cells showed no Rad51 activity (a HR marker) or sister chromatid exchanges (SCE, HR-associated events) [32]. NHEJ-deficient cells were hypersensitive to fludarabine, while HR-deficient cells resisted. This reliance on NHEJ may drive genomic instability in T cells, promoting pathogenic clones [32]. According to a study by Rosenwald and colleagues, fludarabine induces apoptosis via p53-dependent pathways, as shown by gene expression profiling in patients and in vitro [16]. Both in vivo and in vitro treatments activated p53 target genes and DNA repair genes, mirroring responses to ionizing radiation [16]. Using p53 wild-type and null cell lines, researchers confirmed p53’s role in regulating these genes [16]. By triggering a p53-driven response, fludarabine may select for drug-resistant p53-mutant cells, which are linked to aggressive diseases. This p53-mediated selection could synergize with fludarabine-induced genomic instability, exacerbating clonal evolution [16]. In the context of CAR-T cell therapy, prior exposure to fludarabine, used as a preconditioning regimen, may similarly contribute to an increased risk of secondary malignancies; however, in-depth investigations are warranted in this regard [31]. Mitigating these risks requires a multifactorial approach, including improved vector design, patient stratification based on genomic risk profiles, and integration of safety-engineered CAR-T cell platforms. Risk factors for secondary malignancies post-CAR-T cell therapy

GSHs

Definition, criteria, and validation

GSHs are regions of the genome deemed suitable for stable transgene integration due to their minimal risk of disrupting endogenous genes or regulatory elements. Papapetrou et al. established GSH criteria to minimize risks from transgene integration. The first two criteria address insertional oncogenesis by mandating a distance of ≥ 50 kb from any gene (1st) and ≥ 300 kb from cancer-related genes (2nd) to reduce pro-tumor gene activation [33]. The 3rd criterion requires ≥ 300 kb from miRNA genes, reflecting their critical role in cellular regulation [33]. The 4th criterion excludes integration within transcriptional units to avoid disrupting tumor suppressor function or generating aberrant gene products [33]. Lastly, the 5th criterion excludes ultraconserved regions, which may harbor essential functional elements [33]. These rules aim to ensure safe, stable transgene expression while mitigating oncogenic risks [33]. In a recent investigation, Odak et al. developed an algorithm to identify extragenic genomic safe harbors (eGSHs) in human T lymphocytes, aiming to enable stable CAR transgene integration while averting unintended CAR activation and premature T-cell differentiation [34]. Their framework incorporated several design criteria to mitigate risks associated with transgene integration, including avoidance of functional genomic regions, prevention of transgene silencing, and optimization of CRISPR/Cas9 targeting efficiency [34]. Notably, a 6th criterion was introduced to safeguard non-coding RNA (ncRNA) loci, given their critical roles in gene regulation, chromatin remodeling, and cellular differentiation, as well as their implication in oncogenesis and immune dysfunction when dysregulated (e.g., HOTAIR lncRNA) [34, 35]. A 7th criterion prioritized nuclease accessibility to ensure precise integration, while an 8th criterion addressed chromatin architecture to sustain transgene expression fidelity [34]. Preclinical validation demonstrated that CD19-redirected CAR-T cells engineered at GSH6—a site fulfilling all criteria—exhibited potent antitumor activity in B-ALL murine models at low doses, with durable tumor control observed 100 days post-infusion [34]. These cells matched the efficacy of TRAC locus-integrated CAR-T cells, underscoring the translational potential of eGSHs [34]. This work highlights the utility of systematic GSH identification in advancing cell therapies with enhanced safety profiles and predictable functionality, providing a roadmap for future refinements in precision genome engineering [34]. These criteria were established through decades of research on viral integration patterns and gene therapy outcomes, particularly following early trials where semi-random vector integration led to leukemogenesis in SCID-X1 patients [22, 23]. Validation of putative GSHs involves functional assays in primary cells, long-term engraftment studies in animal models, and computational analyses to exclude regions associated with cancer driver mutations or genome-wide association study (GWAS) hits [33].

Identified GSH loci in the human genome

Several genomic loci have been characterized as candidates for GSHs because they tend to combine relatively open chromatin, transcriptional permissiveness, and genomic contexts that minimize proximity to known oncogenes, fragile sites, or essential regulatory elements—attributes intended to promote sustained transgene expression while reducing insertional risk, although empirical, cell-type specific validation and long-term monitoring are always required. The most prominent candidates currently under investigation for CAR-T cell engineering include the AAVS1 locus, the classical adeno-associated virus (AAV) integration site that is transcriptionally permissive in many settings but whose behavior can be cell-type dependent; the CCR5 locus, which is best framed primarily as a clinically tested therapeutic knockout target (well tolerated in some human trials and conferring resistance to CCR5-tropic HIV) rather than a neutral insertion site for arbitrary transgenes; the ROSA26 locus, the canonical ubiquitous expression site in mouse models (for which a human homolog has been proposed); the emerging CLYBL locus, which has shown strong transcriptional activity and appears genomically tolerant in pluripotent stem-cell work but is less longitudinally validated in clinical settings; and the TRAC locus, whose targeted knock-in strategy uniquely enables uniform CAR expression from a native T-cell promoter while simultaneously disrupting the endogenous T-cell receptor—a dual benefit that reduces variability and addresses GVHD risk in allogeneic products. Understanding each locus’s distinct attributes, limitations, and level of clinical or preclinical validation is critical for selecting the optimal GSH strategy to reconcile potent antitumor activity with long-term genomic safety.

AAVS1

AAV is distinguished by its ability to establish latent infection through site-specific integration into the human genome, predominantly targeting a locus termed AAVS1 (chromosome 19q13.42) [36]. This locus, first identified in the 1990s, has been a focal point of research due to its unique role in AAV biology [36]. The viral Rep78 and Rep68 proteins are central to this process, binding to a Rep-binding site (RBS) within AAVS1 to mediate integration [36]. Early studies proposed that chromatin accessibility at AAVS1 facilitates Rep-mediated targeting, a hypothesis further supported by subsequent investigations [36]. Notably, a study by Lamartina revealed that the AAVS1 RBS resides adjacent to a DNase I hypersensitive region—a hallmark of "open" chromatin—suggesting this structural feature enables Rep protein accessibility [36]. This open conformation was conserved in transgenic rat models harboring a 3.5-kb AAVS1 DNA fragment, which retained competence for Rep-directed integration [36]. Intriguingly, the core of the DNase I hypersensitive site overlaps with a sequence exhibiting transcriptional enhancer-like activity, implying that AAVS1 occupies a transcriptionally active genomic niche [36]. These findings not only reinforce the model of Rep-mediated, chromatin-dependent integration but also highlight AAVS1’s potential as a safe harbor for transgene delivery [36]. The convergence of accessible chromatin, enhancer-like elements, and site-specific integration underscores AAVS1’s relevance to gene therapy, offering insights into optimizing vectors for stable, predictable genome editing—a consideration critical to advancing CAR-T cell engineering and other therapeutic applications [36]. Recent studies have leveraged genome-editing tools like TALEN and CRISPR/Cas9 to exploit AAVS1’s properties as a GSH, circumventing random integration and positional variability [37]. By targeting the AAVS1 locus, researchers achieved precise, single-copy transgene integration under the control of the CAG promoter, ensuring reproducible and scalable expression (~ twofold increments) across cell populations [37]. Critically, transgene expression remained stable during long-term human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) culture and persisted robustly through differentiation into diverse lineages [37]. This approach, supported by standardized donor vectors and protocols for iPSC culture, drug selection, and genotyping, underscores AAVS1’s utility as a reliable platform for predictable transgene delivery [37]. However, recent work highlights both the promise and limitations of AAVS1 as a GSH [38]. Researchers engineered human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC) master lines using FLPe recombinase-mediated cassette exchange (RMCE) in AAVS1, achieving 100% transgene integration efficiency without random insertions and enabling rapid transgenic line generation within 15 days [38]. While this system successfully incorporated transgenes for lineage tracing, toxicity studies, and hepatocyte differentiation, variable transgene suppression was observed in vitro in both undifferentiated hPSCs and differentiating hepatocytes [38]. This inhibition correlated with DNA methylation and other unidentified mechanisms, challenging the assumption of AAVS1 as a universally reliable locus for stable transgene expression in vitro [38]. These findings emphasize the need for rigorous functional validation of individual transgenes when targeting AAVS1 [38]. Recent advancements have further validated AAVS1’s utility in enhancing the safety and precision of CAR-T cell engineering [20]. A 2023 study by Chen and colleagues demonstrated the use of a non-viral, AAV-derived CELiD vector for site-directed integration of CD19-redirected CAR transgenes into AAVS1 in human T cells [20]. By co-electroporating CELiD vectors encoding the CAR construct with Rep-expressing plasmids, followed by G418 selection, researchers achieved site-specific integration efficiencies of up to 22.8% within 20 days [20]. CAR-T cells engineered via this method exhibited robust CAR expression, secreted elevated effector cytokines, and demonstrated potent in vitro cytotoxicity, eliminating ~ 75% of target cells [20]. Importantly, this approach leveraged AAVS1’s accessible chromatin and Rep-mediated targeting to minimize random integration, addressing safety concerns associated with conventional viral vectors [20]. The study highlights AAVS1’s adaptability for non-viral, Rep-dependent strategies, offering a scalable platform to enhance the precision of CAR-T cell therapies while mitigating genotoxic risks [20].

CCR5

The C–C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5) locus (chromosome 3p21.31) has gained prominence as a candidate GSH due to its dispensability in normal immune function and its association with a naturally occurring protective mutation (CCR5-Δ32) that confers resistance to HIV-1 infection [39, 40]. First identified in the 1990s for its role in HIV entry, CCR5’s therapeutic potential expanded with the observation that homozygous CCR5-Δ32 individuals exhibit no significant adverse health effects, suggesting safe disruption or editing of this locus [39, 40]. It is important to note that many CAR-T cell therapy clinical trials explicitly exclude HIV+ patients from participation [41]. This exclusion often stems from concerns about potential interactions between HIV infection and engineered T-cells, as HIV directly targets CD4+ T cells. Researchers cite theoretical risks of HIV altering CAR-T cell efficacy or persistence, as well as uncertainties about how immunosuppression from untreated HIV (or even well-controlled HIV with antiretroviral therapy) might compromise safety outcomes [41]. Additionally, regulatory frameworks often prioritize excluding comorbid conditions in early-phase trials to minimize confounding variables, though this limits understanding of CAR-T cell applicability in HIV+ populations [41]. Recent advances in HIV-focused gene therapy have leveraged CCR5’s safety profile to engineer HIV-resistant CAR-T cells (αHIV-CAR-T cell) [42]. A study demonstrated the feasibility of combining CCR5 disruption via megaTAL nuclease-mediated gene editing with αHIV-CAR expression targeting multiple HIV envelope epitopes [42]. While αHIV-CAR-T cells effectively eliminated > 80% of HIV-infected cells, the CAR itself posed a vulnerability by enabling HIV entry [42]. To mitigate this, CCR5—the major HIV co-receptor—was disrupted using mRNA-delivered megaTAL nucleases, achieving bi-allelic knockout rates sufficient to block viral entry [42]. Two strategies were employed: lentiviral (LV) delivery of αHIV-CAR cassettes or AAV-mediated homology-directed integration of CAR constructs into the CCR5 locus [42]. Both approaches yielded CAR+ T cells resistant to HIV infection, with CCR5-edited cells outperforming LV-only counterparts in suppressing viral replication [42]. Notably, this dual-editing strategy was successfully adapted to non-human primate (NHP) models, achieving 50% CCR5 disruption and ~ 70% CAR expression in NHP T cells, which retained potent anti-HIV activity [42]. These findings validate CCR5 as a tractable GSH for engineering HIV-resistant CAR-T cells while preserving their therapeutic function, aligning with the locus’s established safety and therapeutic relevance [42].

Human ROSA26

The ROSA26 locus, originally identified in mice as a constitutively active genomic site for transgene expression, has been adapted as a GSH in humans due to its favorable characteristics for stable and predictable transgene integration [43]. In humans, the homolog (chromosome 3p25.3) shares conserved features with its murine counterpart, including an open chromatin structure, ubiquitous transcriptional activity across cell types, and a lack of association with oncogenic or essential genes [43, 44]. First characterized in the early 2000s, the human ROSA26 locus resides within a gene-poor region, minimizing risks of insertional mutagenesis or disruption of critical regulatory elements [43, 44]. Its promoter contains a CpG island, enabling robust and consistent expression of integrated transgenes without position-dependent silencing [43]. Over the past two decades, ROSA26 has been validated in preclinical studies as a reliable site for CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-ins, lentiviral integration, and AAV-directed gene targeting, particularly in stem cells and immune cells [43]. Its adoption in gene therapy reflects its compatibility with diverse editing platforms and its ability to sustain long-term transgene expression in vivo. These attributes position ROSA26 as a cornerstone GSH for engineering therapeutic cells, including CAR-T cells, where precise control of transgene expression and genomic safety are paramount. Building on its utility in mice, the human ROSA26 locus was confirmed as a versatile platform for genetic engineering in human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) [43]. In this regrad, Irion and colleagues successfully targeted a red fluorescent protein (tdRFP) cDNA into this locus via homologous recombination, demonstrating robust and ubiquitous expression across diverse hESC-derived lineages [43]. This study further highlighted the locus’s adaptability by employing RMCE to replace the tdRFP cassette with alternative transgenes, underscoring its suitability for iterative genetic modifications [43]. These findings validated ROSA26 as a transcriptionally permissive and functionally neutral site for stable transgene integration, aligning with its role as a GSH for applications requiring precise, reliable, and broadly active transgene expression in human cells [43].

Extending these principles to other species, recent work has established the rabbit ROSA26 homolog (rbRosa26) as a safe harbor for transgenic applications [45]. Identified through genomic homology to the murine Rosa26 locus, rbRosa26 encodes two ubiquitously expressed long noncoding RNA variants (rbRosaV1 and rbRosaV2) across tissues [45]. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated targeting of rbRosa26 yielded knock-in rabbits (rbRosa26-EGFP and rbRosa26-Cre-reporter), with founders and offspring exhibiting normal health and reproduction [45]. In F1 generations, EGFP and tdTomato reporters were stably and broadly expressed in all examined tissues, mirroring the locus’s transcriptional permissiveness [45]. Critically, disruption of rbRosa26 had no adverse phenotypic effects, confirming its functional neutrality [45]. This work validates rbRosa26 as a reliable platform for nuclease-mediated transgene integration, enabling gain-of-function, conditional knockout, and lineage-tracing studies in rabbits [45]. Its adoption expands the utility of Rosa26-derived safe harbors to non-rodent models, offering a standardized site for predictable transgene expression in translational research [45].

CLYBL

CLYBL has recently emerged as a promising GSH candidate, distinguished by its transcriptionally permissive chromatin architecture and genomic neutrality [46]. Initially characterized for its role in citrate and vitamin B12 metabolism, the CLYBL gene (chromosome 13q32.3) gained attention as a GSH due to its location in a gene-poor region devoid of oncogenes, essential coding sequences, or regulatory elements [47]. Recent studies demonstrate that TALEN-mediated integration of reporter genes into CLYBL achieves efficiencies of 38–58% in human iPSCs and neural stem cells (NSCs), outperforming AAVS1 in sustaining robust transgene expression during self-renewal and differentiation [47]. Notably, CLYBL-targeted integration exhibits stronger transgene expression and minimal disruption to neighboring genes compared to AAVS1, which is historically prioritized for single-locus engineering [47]. Furthermore, simultaneous editing at both CLYBL and AAVS1 loci enables stable, multiplexed transgene knock-ins, underscoring CLYBL’s versatility as a transcriptionally permissive, genomically neutral GSH for advanced stem cell applications [47]. While CLYBL remains less extensively validated than classical GSHs, its genomic insulation, transcriptional flexibility, and metabolic inertness position it as a compelling alternative for CAR-T cell engineering and other precision therapies requiring stringent control over transgene integration and expression.

TRAC

The TRAC locus (chromosome 14q11.2) has emerged as a pivotal GSH for CAR-T cell engineering, driven by its unique biological and functional attributes [48–51]. Historically, TRAC gained prominence in immunology as the site encoding the constant region of the TCR α-chain, a critical component of TCR complex assembly and antigen recognition [48–51]. With the advent of CRISPR/Cas9 and other precision genome-editing tools, TRAC was repurposed as a therapeutic target in CAR-T cell therapy to address challenges associated with random viral integration, such as insertional mutagenesis and variable transgene expression [48–50]. TRAC’s suitability as a GSH stems from two key features [49]. First, targeted integration of CAR constructs into TRAC disrupts endogenous TCR expression, eliminating the risk of GVHD in allogeneic CAR-T cell therapies [49]. Second, the locus resides in a transcriptionally active genomic environment, enabling robust and consistent CAR expression under the control of TRAC’s endogenous promoter [49]. Unlike traditional GSH candidates such as AAVS1 or CLYBL, which are prioritized for pluripotent or somatic stem cells, TRAC is uniquely tailored for T-cell applications [48–50]. Its integration site is devoid of oncogenic or regulatory elements, and its editing does not perturb critical cellular functions, ensuring genomic neutrality [49].

The seminal work by Eyquem et al. demonstrated that CRISPR/Cas9-mediated integration of a CD19-specific CAR into the TRAC locus enhances therapeutic efficacy by ensuring uniform CAR expression and optimizing T-cell functionality [49]. Unlike conventional γ-retroviral approaches, which risk insertional mutagenesis and variable transgene activity, TRAC-targeted CAR-T cells exhibit superior potency in preclinical models, such as ALL, outperforming randomly integrated counterparts [49]. This precision editing eliminates tonic CAR signaling—a driver of premature T-cell exhaustion—and enables dynamic CAR internalization and re-expression upon antigen encounter, preserving effector cell longevity [49]. Furthermore, TRAC-directed integration leverages the locus’s transcriptionally active environment, ensuring robust CAR expression under endogenous regulatory elements while simultaneously ablating TCR expression to prevent GVHD [49]. These findings highlight TRAC’s dual role as a GSH and a functional enhancer, offering a paradigm shift in CAR-T cell design by coupling safety with enhanced therapeutic performance [49].

Recent advancements in non-viral genome editing have further solidified TRAC’s utility as a GSH [48]. Kath et al. demonstrated the feasibility of virus-free CRISPR/Cas9-mediated CAR integration into the TRAC locus by HDR using dsDNA templates [48]. To overcome challenges such as dsDNA toxicity and low HDR efficiency, researchers leveraged pharmacological strategies: DNA-sensor inhibition to enhance cell viability and HDR-enhancing compounds to boost CAR knockin rates [48]. While residual dsDNA toxicity persisted, the combination approach significantly improved CAR+ T cell yields [48]. The resulting TRAC-replaced CD19-redirected CAR-T cells exhibited potent antigen-specific cytotoxicity and cytokine production in vitro and delayed leukemia progression in a xenograft mouse model [48]. By achieving TRAC-integrated CAR+ T cell frequencies exceeding 50%, this work demonstrates that pharmacological interventions can optimize non-viral editing efficiency without compromising genomic fidelity [48]. These findings align with TRAC’s role as a transcriptionally active, functionally neutral GSH, providing a scalable platform to generate allogeneic CAR-T cells with minimized GVHD risk and enhanced therapeutic consistency [48].

In a complementary approach, MacLeod and colleagues introduced a streamlined approach for developing allogeneic CAR-T cells by leveraging the TCR locus’s inherent properties [50]. These researchers utilized an engineered homing endonuclease and an AAV donor template to site-specifically integrate an anti-CD19 CAR transgene into the native TCR locus [50]. This strategy not only abolished endogenous TCR expression but also generated CAR-T cells with potent in vitro effector functions and robust in vivo antitumor activity, as evidenced by CD19+ tumor clearance in a mouse model [50]. Advantages of GSHs for CAR transgene integration

Targeted integration of CAR transgenes into GSHs addresses critical limitations inherent to conventional viral vector-based approaches, offering a paradigm shift in CAR-T cell therapy design. First, by circumventing proto-oncogenes, tumor suppressor loci, and genomic fragile sites, GSH-directed integration eliminates the risk of insertional mutagenesis—a longstanding concern associated with semi-random viral vector integration.

Second, GSHs ensure consistent transgene expression across CAR-T cell populations, resolving clonal variability and functional exhaustion linked to semi-random integration. Conventional viral vectors integrate CAR constructs into transcriptionally variable regions, leading to heterogeneous CAR expression levels that drive clonal dominance of high-expressing subsets. These clones often exhibit premature exhaustion due to tonic signaling, compromising therapeutic durability [52]. In contrast, GSHs such as ROSA26 and CLYBL reside in open chromatin environments with endogenous promoters that sustain uniform CAR expression. For example, TRAC-targeted CAR-T cells exhibit synchronized receptor internalization and re-expression dynamics upon antigen engagement, preserving stem-like memory phenotypes and prolonging in vivo persistence [48, 49]. This transcriptional stability eliminates “CAR silencing” observed in viral approaches, where epigenetic repression at integration sites diminishes efficacy over time [38]. Consequently, GSH-edited CAR-T cells demonstrate narrower CAR expression variance, enhancing dose predictability and reducing relapse risks from subtherapeutic clones [48, 49].

Third, GSH-directed integration facilitates the development of universal, “off-the-shelf” CAR-T cell products by standardizing transgene placement. Conventional manufacturing suffers from batch-to-batch variability due to positional effects—where integration sites influence CAR expression levels—complicating quality control and regulatory compliance [53]. By contrast, site-specific knock-in at validated GSHs ensures identical transgene positioning across donor cells, enabling scalable production of pre-engineered CAR-T cell inventories. Such standardization streamlines regulatory approval by demonstrating product consistency, a key requirement for advanced therapy medicinal product (ATMP) or FDA approval [54]. Moreover, GSH platforms enable multiplexed engineering (e.g., combining CAR integration with immune checkpoint knockouts) without compounding genotoxic risks, a feat unattainable with viral vectors due to cumulative integration hazards. As the field progresses, prioritizing GSHs in CAR-T cell design will be critical to unlocking the full therapeutic potential of cellular immunotherapy while safeguarding patient genomes.

Tools and strategies for site-directed CAR transgene integration

Genome editing platforms

The precision of site-directed CAR transgene integration relies on advanced genome-editing platforms, including CRISPR/Cas9, TALENs, and ZFNs. CRISPR/Cas9, the most widely adopted system, utilizes a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) to direct the Cas9 endonuclease to specific genomic loci, enabling DSBs with high efficiency and scalability [55]. Its modular design allows rapid retargeting to different sites, making it ideal for integrating CAR constructs into GSHs such as AAVS1 or TRAC [49, 55]. In contrast, TALENs and ZFNs employ protein-DNA recognition mechanisms: TALENs bind via repeat-variable di-residues (RVDs) that recognize specific nucleotide sequences, while ZFNs use zinc finger domains engineered to target DNA triplets [17, 56]. Although CRISPR/Cas9 dominates due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness, TALENs and ZFNs offer advantages in contexts requiring reduced off-target activity, as their larger binding sites (30–40 bp for TALENs vs. 20 bp for CRISPR) enhance specificity [56]. However, all platforms face challenges in primary T cells, including variable editing efficiencies and cytotoxicity from prolonged nuclease expression. Innovations such as high-fidelity Cas9 variants and transient mRNA delivery have mitigated these issues to some extent [57]. For instance, Vos and colleagues’ recent investigations in CRISPR/Cas9 engineering aimed to resolve the trade-off between editing precision and efficiency [57]. While high-fidelity Cas9 variants reduce off-target effects, their diminished activity limits therapeutic utility [57]. To address this, a hybrid enzyme, HyperDriveCas9, was developed by these researchers by merging the attributes of high-fidelity and hyperactive Cas9 variants [57]. This engineered nuclease maintained stringent specificity—minimizing unintended genomic alterations—while achieving robust editing efficiency in mammalian cells [57]. HyperDriveCas9 generated targeted insertions and deletions with a favorable off-target profile, offering an optimized balance of accuracy and potency for genome-editing applications in research and clinical settings, including CAR-T cell engineering [57]. Targeted integration via HDR

HDR, the primary mechanism for precise CAR transgene integration, requires the co-delivery of a donor template containing homology arms flanking the CAR construct [58]. This process is inherently limited to the S/G2 phases of the cell cycle, where HDR machinery is active, posing a challenge in quiescent or slowly dividing T cells [58]. To enhance HDR efficiency, researchers employ small molecules such as SCR7 (a DNA ligase IV inhibitor) or RS-1 (a RAD51 stimulator), which transiently suppress NHEJ and promote HDR. For instance, a study by Maruyama et al. showed that SCR7 suppresses NHEJ activity to bias DNA repair toward HDR [59]. This intervention improved HDR efficiency by up to threefold for large fragment insertions and ninefold for precise point mutations across diverse mammalian cell lines and in vivo mouse models [59]. In line with this approach, a recent study further explored strategies to optimize HDR-mediated knock-in efficiency [60]. The authors hypothesized that suppressing NHEJ or augmenting HDR activity could enhance nuclease-driven integration [60]. Their findings revealed that RS-1, a pharmacological enhancer of RAD51 (a key HDR mediator), robustly increased knock-in efficiency by twofold to fivefold across multiple genomic loci in vitro [60]. In contrast, the NHEJ inhibitor SCR7 showed negligible effects in their system [60]. Notably, RS-1’s efficacy extended to in vivo applications, where its use during animal model production significantly improved knock-in rates [60]. These results underscore RS-1’s utility as a potent tool for enhancing HDR in nuclease-mediated gene targeting, particularly in systems where SCR7 may exhibit limited activity [60]. Additionally, synchronizing T-cell cycles via cytokine stimulation (e.g., IL-7/IL-15) prior to editing has proven to be an effective strategy [61, 62]. Despite these advances, HDR efficiency remains variable across patient-derived T cells, necessitating continued optimization for clinical scalability.

Challenges

Despite these advances, GSH-based CAR-T cell manufacturing faces technical and logistical hurdles. HDR efficiencies in primary T cells remain suboptimal, even with cytokine pre-activation or small-molecule enhancers. Low HDR rates necessitate costly selection processes to isolate successfully edited cells, complicating scalability for large-scale production. Novel strategies, such as base editing or prime editing, promise to bypass HDR limitations by enabling precise integration without DSBs, but their application in T cells is still experimental [63, 64]. Scalability is further hindered by the reliance on viral vectors for donor template delivery, which introduces batch-to-batch variability and regulatory complexities. Long-term safety monitoring also presents challenges; while preclinical models show no acute genotoxicity, the latency of secondary malignancies (often emerging years post-therapy) demands extended follow-up in clinical trials. Additionally, immune responses to bacterial-derived Cas9 or residual editing components may limit therapeutic durability or trigger adverse events [65]. Addressing these challenges requires multidisciplinary collaboration to optimize editing protocols, standardize manufacturing workflows, and establish global registries for long-term outcome tracking.

Non-viral delivery systems

Beyond viral vectors, non-viral gene delivery methods provide a promising alternative for CAR transgene delivery with a potentially improved safety profile regarding SPM risk. These approaches avoid viral components and therefore eliminate the canonical mechanism of semi-random viral integration that underlies many insertional mutagenesis concerns. Key non-viral strategies include electroporation of donor DNA templates, for example linear dsDNA, plasmid donors, or AAV-derived CELiD molecules, often delivered together with CRISPR/Cas9 components to drive HDR-mediated targeted integration into validated GSHs [66, 67]. However, dsDNA donors can still integrate unintentionally at DSBs or by spontaneous end-joining, and certain end-modified linear constructs have been reported to show substantial spontaneous integration rates [66, 67]. Consequently, non-viral approaches reduce but do not abolish insertional- or editing-associated genomic risks and therefore require optimized donor chemistry and delivery conditions as well as prospective integration-site and clonal surveillance.

Electroporation of in vitro-transcribed mRNA produces cytoplasmic expression without entering the nucleus and therefore does not lead to vector integration into host chromosomal DNA under standard manufacturing conditions; expression is transient (typically lasting days) as the mRNA and encoded protein are degraded or diluted with cell division, so this approach substantially reduces insertional genotoxic risk compared with integrating viral vectors [68]. Clinical and translational reports of RNA-electroporated CAR-T cell products confirm rapid onset but short duration of CAR expression and the practical need for repeat dosing or engineered strategies to extend persistence when longer activity is required [69, 70]. There is a theoretical, mechanistic caveat from the retrotransposition literature showing that, in certain experimental settings, cellular reverse transcriptase activity can convert RNA to cDNA and insert sequences into the genome; however, such events have been observed mainly in virus-infected cells and have not been shown to occur at appreciable frequency following standard mRNA transfection/electroporation in T-cell manufacturing [71, 72].

Nanoparticle-based systems such as lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and polymeric nanoparticles are emerging as versatile vehicles for delivering mRNA, DNA, or ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes; LNPs, proven clinically for mRNA vaccine delivery, can be engineered for T-cell targeting and offer a scalable alternative to electroporation, though their innate-immune stimulant properties and the current challenges in efficient primary T-cell delivery mean their application in primary T-cell engineering remains an active area of development [73, 74].

DNA transposons such as Sleeping Beauty or PiggyBac are non-viral integrating systems with integration profiles that differ from viral vectors; Sleeping Beauty generally exhibits a more random distribution with relatively little transcription start site bias, PiggyBac shows enrichment in active genes and near transcriptional start sites, and both occupy an intermediate genotoxic risk profile between semi-random viral integration and the targeted precision of HDR-mediated knock-ins [75, 76]. Supporting this risk profile, clinical findings from a phase I trial (ACTRN12617001579381) utilizing the piggyBac transposon system for allogeneic CD19-redirected CAR-T cell manufacture reported the development of CAR+ T-cell lymphomas in two patients [77, 78]. Analysis of one tumor revealed a high transgene copy number and somatic structural variants, though no insertional mutagenesis of known oncogenes was identified. Transcriptional dysregulation was associated with these genomic alterations [77, 78]. Both cases exhibited progressive lymphoma, culminating in one fatality, underscoring that non-viral integrating systems retain oncogenic potential linked to high transgene burden and collateral genomic damage [77, 78].

Non-viral methods avoid viral integrases and can reduce some classes of insertional risk while easing certain manufacturing and anti-viral immunity concerns. These advantages are not absolute: non-viral integrating platforms and nuclease-driven edits can still cause insertional events or DSB-associated genomic damage, mRNA strategies are transient, and nanoparticle delivery faces efficiency and innate-immunity challenges in primary T cells. A pragmatic path forward is to pair precise GSH-targeting or DSB-minimizing editing with scalable non-viral delivery chemistries and prospective integration-site and clonal surveillance to balance durable efficacy with minimized genomic risk.

Comparative analysis of alternative risk-mitigation strategies

Suicide genes and safety switches

Suicide genes and safety switches represent a contingency strategy to mitigate CAR-T cell-related risks by enabling controlled elimination of engineered cells [79, 80]. The inducible caspase-9 (iCasp9) system, activated by a small-molecule dimerizer, triggers apoptosis in CAR-T cells upon administration, effectively halting severe toxicities like cytokine release syndrome (CRS) or unexpected clonal expansion [79, 80]. For instance, a phase 1/2 clinical trial (NCT03016377) demonstrated the clinical utility of the iCasp9 safety switch in managing severe, steroid-refractory immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) [81]. In a dose-expansion cohort, administration of the dimerizing agent rimiducid rapidly eliminated > 60% of circulating CAR-T cells within 4 h and > 90% within 24 h, correlating with abrupt resolution of ICANS symptoms [81]. This underscores the iCasp9 system’s capacity to swiftly deplete engineered cells and reverse life-threatening toxicities, validating its role as a fail-safe mechanism [81]. Similarly, herpes simplex virus thymidine kinase (HSV-TK), which renders CAR-T cells susceptible to ganciclovir, has been assessed in clinical settings [82]. However, these systems face limitations as immune responses against foreign proteins (e.g., HSV-TK) can limit CAR-T cell persistence, and incomplete elimination may permit residual cells to drive secondary malignancies [83]. Furthermore, suicide mechanisms do not address the root cause of genomic instability from semi-random integration, leaving patients vulnerable to insertional oncogenesis. While valuable as a safety net, these strategies are reactive rather than preventive, underscoring the need for proactive genomic safety measures.

mRNA-based CAR-T cells

RNA-based CAR delivery circumvents genomic integration risks by transiently expressing CAR constructs via mRNA electroporation or LNPs. This approach limits CAR-T cell activity to days or weeks, reducing opportunities for insertional mutagenesis or long-term clonal stress. A pilot phase I trial (NCT03060356) evaluated this strategy using intravenous RNA-electroporated cMET-targeting CAR-T cells in patients with metastatic melanoma (n = 3) or metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC; n = 4) [69]. Patients received up to six infusions without lymphodepletion [69]. The regimen proved safe, with no grade ≥ 3 toxicities, neurotoxicity, or treatment discontinuation; adverse events (grade 1–2) included anemia, fatigue, and CRS (1 patient) [69]. CAR mRNA was transiently detected in peripheral blood (all patients), but not in post-infusion tumor biopsies [69]. While no objective responses occurred, stable disease was observed in four patients [69]. These findings confirm the feasibility and safety of transient RNA-based CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors, with transient activity aligning with the technology’s pharmacokinetic profile [69]. This supports further exploration of RNA-electroporated CAR-T cell platforms to mitigate long-term risks while retaining therapeutic potential in aggressive malignancies. However, transient expression necessitates repeated infusions to sustain efficacy, increasing manufacturing costs and patient burden [84]. Additionally, RNA-based CAR-T cells exhibit reduced persistence in immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments, limiting utility in aggressive malignancies [84–86]. While RNA platforms eliminate genomic risks, their therapeutic window remains narrow compared to genomically integrated CAR-T cell products, highlighting a trade-off between safety and durability. Regulatory and clinical considerations

Safety assessments

Comprehensive safety assessments are critical for genome-edited CAR-T cell products to address potential genotoxic and immunogenic risks. Current protocols mandate rigorous off-target analysis using tools like GUIDE-seq or CIRCLE-seq, which identify unintended CRISPR/Cas9 activity across the genome [87, 88]. Long-term follow-up (LTFU) protocols, recommended by the US FDA, require monitoring patients for at least 15 years to detect delayed malignancies, leveraging droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) to track vector integration sites and clonal dynamics [89–91]. Additionally, immune responses to bacterial-derived Cas9 or residual editing components (e.g., sgRNA) are assessed via ELISA and T-cell activation assays [65, 92]. Functional assessments, such as in vitro transformation assays and xenotransplant models, can further validate the safety of edited CAR-T cells by simulating decades of replicative stress in compressed timelines. These multilayered approaches ensure that risks are quantified and mitigated prior to clinical deployment.

Regulatory frameworks

Regulatory agencies have established evolving frameworks to address the unique challenges of genome-edited CAR-T cell therapies. US FDA’s frameworks emphasize rigorous validation of assays used to map vector integration sites, requiring studies to demonstrate sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility using appropriate controls (1st recommendation). Current guidelines mandate integration site analysis if ≥ 1% of surrogate cells test positive for vector sequences via PCR, or if clonality assays reveal sufficient sensitivity (2nd recommendation). Longitudinal monitoring is advised, with PCR-based vector detection in surrogate samples every six months for five years, followed by annual testing for up to ten years (or until vector clearance) (3rd recommendation). If clonal dominance (e.g., oligoclonal/monoclonal patterns) or persistent clonal expansion is detected, integration site analysis must be prioritized, followed by repeat clonality assessments within three months (4th recommendation). Identified integration sites must be cross-referenced with genomic databases to evaluate proximity to oncogenes or cancer-associated loci (5th recommendation). While oligoclonality alone does not equate to malignancy, persistent monoclonality, clonal expansion, or integration near oncogenic regions necessitate intensified clinical surveillance for cancer risks, leveraging established screening protocols and expert clinical guidance (6th and 7th recommendations).

These frameworks underscore the necessity of proactive risk management in gene therapy, particularly given historical precedents where viral vector integration near proto-oncogenes led to insertional oncogenesis in some clinical trials. To address these risks, regulators recommend a two‑stage strategy that begins with rigorous in vitro and preclinical testing to map integration sites with high‑resolution techniques (such as next‑generation sequencing) and continues with vigilant post‑treatment surveillance to monitor clonal shifts that could signal early neoplastic changes. Emerging technologies, such as single-cell integration site analysis and liquid biopsy-based surveillance, are increasingly incorporated to enhance detection sensitivity and resolve clonal heterogeneity [93, 94]. Furthermore, agencies encourage sponsors to adopt harmonized standards, aligning with global initiatives like the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines, to streamline data comparability across trials [95]. The FDA also emphasizes transparency in risk–benefit communication, urging developers to disclose integration-related findings in patient consent forms and long-term follow-up plans. As genome-editing tools evolve, these frameworks are expected to adapt, potentially incorporating predictive algorithms to assess integration site oncogenic potential in silico preclinically. Ultimately, the goal is to ensure that CAR-T cell therapies not only achieve durable therapeutic effects but also maintain genomic integrity over decades.

Future clinical trial design and endpoints

To optimize both efficacy and safety in next-generation CAR-T cell trials, researchers are increasingly adopting adaptive clinical trial designs. These designs allow for real-time modifications based on interim safety and efficacy data, enhancing the trial's flexibility and responsiveness. A proposed, though not yet widely implemented, composite endpoint termed "durable complete remission without genomic toxicity" can integrate long-term disease control with genomic safety considerations. This concept reflects the evolving focus on not only achieving remission but also ensuring the absence of adverse genomic events post-therapy [96]. However, employing such composite endpoints presents challenges [96]. Balancing the goals of maximizing antitumor activity while minimizing off-target effects or insertional mutagenesis is complex [97]. Additionally, these endpoints may obscure nuanced safety-efficacy trade-offs, particularly in heterogeneous patient populations where individual responses can vary significantly [97]. For instance, patients with preexisting immunity to viral vectors or genetic predispositions to genomic instability may experience divergent outcomes that composite metrics fail to capture. To address these limitations, future trials may require tiered endpoints that separately evaluate efficacy (e.g., tumor regression rates) and safety (e.g., off-target editing frequency), alongside advanced biomarkers to stratify patients based on risk profiles [98, 99]. Furthermore, adaptive designs must incorporate sensitive tools, such as longitudinal genome-wide sequencing and immune monitoring, to detect rare or delayed adverse events [98, 99].

Future directions and conclusion

CAR-T cell therapy has revolutionized oncology, offering durable remissions for refractory malignancies, yet the emergence of therapy-related SPMs underscores the critical need to harmonize therapeutic efficacy with genomic safety. The semi-random integration of viral vectors during CAR-T cell manufacturing poses a persistent risk of insertional mutagenesis, particularly in patients with preexisting genomic instability. GSHs—such as AAVS1 (19q13.42), TRAC (14q11.2), CCR5 (3p21.31), and CLYBL (13q32.3)—can be introduced as platforms for site-directed CAR transgene integration. These loci, validated for transcriptional permissiveness, genomic neutrality, and distance from oncogenic regions, enable stable, high-level CAR expression while minimizing genotoxic disruption. Future advancements will hinge on optimizing genome-editing precision and scalability. While CRISPR/Cas9 remains the cornerstone for GSH integration, challenges such as low HDR efficiency in primary T cells and residual dsDNA toxicity in non-viral approaches must be addressed. Innovations like base/prime editing, which bypass DSBs, and hybrid nucleases (e.g., HyperDriveCas9) balancing fidelity and activity, hold promise for enhancing knock-in precision. Additionally, non-viral delivery systems, such as AAV-derived CELiD vectors, offer scalable alternatives to viral methods, though further refinements are needed to improve integration rates and reduce manufacturing costs. Multiplexed editing at GSHs, combining CAR integration with immune checkpoint disruption, may further enhance therapeutic efficacy without compounding genotoxicity.

Alternative and complementary cellular platforms, including invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells, CAR-engineered NK (CAR-NK) cells, and TCR-engineered T cells, are increasingly investigated as adjuncts or alternatives to conventional CAR-T cell and merit brief consideration here. Biologically, NK/NKT cells exhibit innate-like antitumor activity and typically show shorter in vivo persistence than conventional T cells, while TCR-engineered T cells restore antigen specificity through a transgenic α/β TCR and therefore retain HLA restriction [51, 100]; these differences influence both efficacy and safety. Importantly, the principal drivers of SPM risk outlined above, semi-random integrating vectors and nuclease-induced DSBs, apply across platforms. Any product that uses integrating lentiviral/retroviral vectors or permanent genome editing may carry insertional or editing-related oncogenic risks similar to CAR-T cell therapies, whereas approaches that employ transient mRNA or non-integrating donors (or that place transgenes into validated GSHs) are expected to reduce long-term genotoxic exposure at the cost of persistence and possibly efficacy. CAR-NK and NKT strategies may therefore offer a more favorable SPM profile when implemented with non-integrating delivery or short-lived effectors, but allogeneic off-the-shelf products that require gene disruption (to remove alloreactivity) reintroduce nuclease-associated DSB risk and mandate the same rigorous integration-site mapping and long-term clonal surveillance we advocate for CAR-T cell therapies. Given the limited longitudinal safety data for these newer modalities, we recommend that future trials prospectively include integration-site analysis, single-cell clonal tracking, and extended follow-up to directly compare SPM incidence and clonal behavior across cell types and delivery/editing platforms.

Regulatory frameworks must evolve to address the unique challenges of GSH-engineered CAR-T cell therapies. Current FDA guidelines emphasize rigorous validation of integration site assays, longitudinal monitoring for clonal dominance, and cross-referencing integration sites with oncogenic databases. Emerging technologies, including single-cell integration site analysis and liquid biopsy-based surveillance, will enhance detection of delayed genomic aberrations. Harmonizing global standards, such as ICH guidelines, will streamline clinical translation and ensure consistent safety evaluations across trials. Furthermore, adaptive trial designs incorporating composite endpoints could better balance efficacy and safety in heterogeneous patient populations. In conclusion, GSH-based CAR-T cell engineering represents a pivotal advance in reconciling therapeutic innovation with genomic integrity. By leveraging validated GSH loci, coupled with cutting-edge editing tools and proactive regulatory oversight, the field can mitigate SPM risks while preserving CAR-T cells’ curative potential. Collaborative efforts among researchers, clinicians, and regulators will be essential to overcome manufacturing hurdles, refine long-term safety monitoring, and democratize access to these precision therapies. As the next generation of CAR-T cell products enters clinical testing, prioritizing genomic safety will ensure that these life-saving treatments achieve enduring success without compromising patient genomes.
