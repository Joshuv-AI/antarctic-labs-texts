Neoadjuvant sintilimab plus chemotherapy in EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NEOTIDE/CTONG2104): phase II trial and correlative genomic analysis in China
Authors: Chao Zhang, Ben-Yuan Jiang, Li-Xu Yan, Li-Shan Peng, Jin-Hu Li, Zhi-Yong Chen, Yu-Xuan Sun, Jian Su, Ri-Qiang Liao, Song Dong, Hong-Hong Yan, Chong-Rui Xu, Qing Zhou, Xue-Ning Yang, Zheng Hu, Yi-Long Wu, Wen-Zhao Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant sintilimab, a programmed cell death 1 protein (PD-1) blockade combined with chemotherapy in patients with resectable stage II-IIIB epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsThis was a single-arm, open-label, phase II trial (CTONG2104) conducted in China. Patients with resectable stage II-IIIB EGFR (AJCC 8th edition) mutant NSCLC were enroled and received neoadjuvant sintilimab (200 mg) plus carboplatin (area under the curve 5) and nab-paclitaxel (260 mg/m2) for 3 cycles. Patients in stage II cohort received adjuvant osimertinib for up to 2 years while observation for stage I cohort. The primary endpoint was major pathological response (MPR) rate (per IASLC criteria) in patients who received at least one dose neoadjuvant sintilimab plus chemotherapy. Secondary endpoints included pathological complete response (pCR) rate (per IASLC criteria), objective response (ORR) rate (per RECIST), event-free survival (EFS), overall survival (OS) and safety profile in all enroled patients. This study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05244213.FindingsBetween May 10, 2022, and March 20, 2024, 35 eligible patients (median [range] age, 60 [48–73] years; 18 [51.4%] female) were enroled and received neoadjuvant treatment and 33 (94.3%) completed surgical resection. The median follow-up was 33.7 months. The primary endpoint was met, with a MPR following neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy of 34.3% (95% CI 19.1–52.2). The ORR and pCR was 60.0% and 11.4%, respectively. Patients with RB1 or RBM10 co-mutations respond well to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy while opposite for EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). The median EFS was not reached with 2-year EFS rate of 71.4% (95% CI 57.9–88.1%). The safety profile during neoadjuvant treatment was tolerable, with 29% of patients experiencing grade 3-4 adverse events. All patients underwent minimally-invasive surgery with 87.9% achieved R0 resection. 42.9% patients experienced local relapse and oligo-metastasis, respectively and 81.8% achieved objective response after receiving EGFR-TKIs.InterpretationNeoadjuvant sintilimab plus chemotherapy revealed encouraging clinical and pathological response with well tolerability in EGFR-mutant NSCLC. Upfront immunochemotherapy did not add sever immune toxicity or impact response rate to TKIs for adjuvant or first-line osimertinib. Further study with randomized controlled design is warranted to verified such treatment modality.FundingInnovent Biologics (Suzhou) Co., Ltd. provided PD-1 regimen (sintilimab), participant compensation and insurance. This work was supported by National Science Foundation of China, Noncommunicable Chronic Diseases-National Science and Technology Major Project, National Natural Science Foundation of China National High-Level Talents Special Support Program, National Natural Science Foundation of China Major Joint Project on Key scientific issues of lung Cancer, Guangdong Provincial Key Lab of Translational Medicine in Lung Cancer, Integrated Project of Major Research Plan of National Natural Science Foundation of China, National Health Commission Medical and Health Technology Development Research Centre Project.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We systematically searched PubMed for clinical trials published between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2024, with no language restrictions, using the terms: “neoadjuvant” AND “Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutation” AND “non-small cell lung cancer” AND (“immunotherapy” OR “PD-1” OR “PD-L1”). Before initiation of our study, EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) had become the standard of care for metastatic EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and the ADAURA trial had established adjuvant osimertinib after resection of early-stage disease. However, neoadjuvant EGFR-TKI trials including the recently reported phase 3 NeoADAURA study showed limited pathological responses, with major pathological response (MPR) rates below 15% and pathological complete response (pCR) rates below 10%, even with osimertinib plus chemotherapy. In contrast, neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy had shown substantial efficacy in EGFR wild-type NSCLC, yet patients with EGFR mutations were systematically excluded from most perioperative immunotherapy trials on the basis of advanced-stage data indicating resistance. Whether neoadjuvant programmed cell death 1 protein (PD-1) blockade plus chemotherapy could induce meaningful pathological regression in early-stage EGFR-mutant NSCLC, and whether this strategy would compromise subsequent TKI efficacy or increase toxicity remained unknown.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this phase 2 NEOTIDE trial is the first prospective study to show that neoadjuvant sintilimab plus carboplatin and nab-paclitaxel is both feasible and active in resectable stage II–IIIB EGFR-mutant NSCLC. The primary endpoint was met, with an MPR rate of 34.3% (95% CI 19.1–52.2) and a pCR rate of 11.4% (95% CI 3.2–26.7). Patients harbouring RB1 or RBM10 co-mutations which previously linked to poor TKI responses, showed favourable responses to immunochemotherapy, suggesting potential for biomarker-guided treatment selection. Comprehensive genomic profiling demonstrated that neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy significantly lowered tumour mutation burden and chromosomal instability without generating deleterious mutations. Quantitative circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) monitoring revealed that baseline ctDNA fraction and the degree of clearance correlated strongly with pathological response, outperforming qualitative ctDNA assessment alone. Importantly, subsequent osimertinib whether administered as adjuvant treatment or at relapse, retained high activity with manageable toxicity, alleviating concerns regarding safety for sequential treatment.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our findings challenge the prevailing notion that EGFR-mutant NSCLC is uniformly resistant to immunotherapy and suggest that neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy may offer a viable alternative for selected patients, particularly those with co-mutations predicting poor TKI response. The preserved efficacy of subsequent osimertinib supports the feasibility of this approach without compromising targeted therapy options. The identification of RB1 and RBM10 co-mutations as potential predictive biomarkers warrants prospective validation in larger cohorts. Quantitative ctDNA dynamics emerged as a promising tool for early response assessment, potentially enabling treatment adaptation. While these results are hypothesis-generating given the single-arm phase II design, they provide the rationale for an ongoing randomized trial comparing neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy vs. TKI-based approaches in molecularly defined EGFR-mutant NSCLC. Extended follow-up is required to establish long-term survival outcomes and confirm the safety of adjuvant osimertinib following preoperative immunochemotherapy.

Introduction

Epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) have long been established as the standard of care for metastatic non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) harbouring sensitive EGFR mutations.1,2 However, patients often experience disease progression within one to two years due to tumour heterogeneity.3 In the case of early-stage and locally advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC, chemotherapy, with or without radiotherapy, has been the standard treatment for decades.4, 5, 6 Although the ADAURA study has shifted the treatment paradigm for the adjuvant setting in early resected EGFR-mutated NSCLC,7 no phase 3 prospective clinical trials have yet demonstrated the superiority of upfront EGFR-TKIs as neoadjuvant therapy for resectable EGFR-mutated NSCLC.8,9 Recently reported phase 3 NeoADAURA failed to provide satisfactory outcome with pCR less than 10% even for osimertinib combined with chemotherapy and no mature survival outcome.10

Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting programmed death 1 (PD-1) in combination with chemotherapy have shown elevated pathological response rates and improved survival by restoring long-term immune responses.11, 12, 13 While data on immunotherapy in advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC revealed limited clinical efficacy,14, 15, 16 some studies evaluating combinations of chemotherapy and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors with anti-angiogenic agents exhibited treatment potentials for advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC.17,18 Although numerically improved progression-free survival (PFS) and duration of response (DOR) was found in IMpower151 trial for Chinese population, it failed to demonstrate additional PD-L1 inhibitor could significantly add survival benefit for EGFR-mutated NSCLC,19 suggesting a more complex response mechanism of immunotherapy for this subset. Moreover, its clinical feasibility in early-stage and locally advanced NSCLC remains uncertain. Potential reasons for this discrepancy may include high intratumoural heterogeneity and genomic instability in early-stage EGFR-mutated NSCLC compared with advanced EGFR-mutated NSCLC.20,21 Additionally, post-hoc analyses from several studies of adjuvant EGFR-TKIs indicate that a substantial subset of early-stage EGFR-mutated NSCLC patients may benefit more from chemotherapy than from EGFR-TKIs,22,23 underscoring the clinical need for precise stratification in this population.

The CTONG2104 (NEOTIDE) trial is a Simon's two-stage design phase 2 study assessing neoadjuvant sintilimab in combination with carboplatin and nab-paclitaxel (nab-PC) in patients with stage IIB-IIIB (N2) (AJCC 8th edition) EGFR-mutant NSCLC (ClinicalTrials.gov number NCT05244213).24 Here, we present the final prespecified analysis of the Simon's two-stage design phase 2 NEOTIDE trial, to evaluate the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant sintilimab combined with nab-PC, and to explore the predictive value of genomic characteristics and ctDNA on treatment outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Methods

Study populations and trial design

Eligibility criteria, as reported previously, included histologically or cytologically documented stage IIB-IIIB (N2) (AJCC 8th edition) NSCLC with confirmed EGFR mutations including non-sensitive subtypes. No prior anti-tumour treatment was allowed. All patients had Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0 or 1 with normal organ function and no contra-indication to surgery. Patients who had an active, known or suspected autoimmune disease, or any underlying disease that investigators consider it may affect patient's prognosis including sever cardiovascular, pulmonary disease or serious infections were excluded. Besides, patients with active prior malignancy within the previous 5 years except for locally curable cancers that have been apparently cured, such as basal or squamous cell skin cancer, superficial bladder cancer, or carcinoma in situ of the prostate, cervix, or breast were excluded. (Details of eligibility criteria are provided in the Supplementary Methods section as study protocol).

This Simon's two stage design, single-arm, open-label, prospective phase 2 trial (NCT05244213) was conducted at Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital. Between May 10, 2022, and March 20, 2024, patients were enroled and received up to 3 cycles of intravenous sintilimab 200 mg, nab-paclitaxel 260 mg/m2 and carboplatin AUC 5 at 3-week intervals. Surgery was scheduled within 3–6 weeks after last dose of neoadjuvant treatment if available. For adjuvant treatment, patients of stage I cohort were not suggested for additional adjuvant treatment while patients of stage II cohort were all recommended adjuvant osimertinib due to national medical insurance coverage and confirmed OS benefit in ADAURA trial. The duration of adjuvant osimertinib was recommended for at least 2 years. Additional details are provided in the study protocol.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice guidelines, and local applicable regulatory requirements. The study protocol, any amendments, and informed consent were approved by Guangdong Provincial People's Hospital's institutional review board (KY–H-2022-009). All participants provided written informed consent. The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05244213.

Randomisation and masking

Due to being an open-label and single-arm trial, no randomisation and masking were performed.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoint of the study was MPR (no more than 10% residual tumour within tumour bed) as assessed by the experienced thoracic pathologist per IASLC Multidisciplinary Recommendations for pathological assessment. Secondary endpoints were pCR (no residual tumour in both primary lung cancer and regional lymph nodes) as assessed by the experienced thoracic pathologist per IASLC Multidisciplinary Recommendations for pathological assessment, ORR (the proportion of patients achieving complete response [CR] or partial response [PR]) as assessed by investigators and IDMC per RECIST v1.1, EFS (time from initiation of neoadjuvant treatment to first PD, disease relapse or death), OS (time from initiation of neoadjuvant treatment to death) and safety profile of both neoadjuvant treatment and adjuvant treatment as assessed by the investigators per CTCAE v5.0. The exploratory endpoints included relationship between baseline genomic biomarkers and clinical efficacy, baseline and dynamic changes of informed-MRD, and immune microenvironment features.

PD-L1 and EGFR mutation assessments were mandatory before enrolment. For PD-L1 assessment, FFPE tumour tissues were applied to perform IHC analysis for PD-L1 with the use of Dako 22C3 immunohistochemical assay. PD-L1 expression was quantified as the proportion of PD-L1-positive tumour cells. Positive PD-L1 expression in a given specimen was defined as ≥1% for tumour cell and ≥50% for high expression. Cases with <100 total tumour cells for scoring were defined as not applicable (NA). For EGFR mutation assessment, either PCR or panel sequencing should be performed for EGFR mutation assessment during enrolment. Patients harboured insensitive EGFR mutations (eg. G719X, 20 insertion, etc) were allowed for enrolment.

Statistical analysis

We applied Simon's optimal two-stage design, which minimizes the expected sample size under the null hypothesis. The correct Simon's optimal two-stage design parameters were as follows: in stage I, 18 patients were enroled; if ≥3 MPRs were observed, the trial proceeded to stage II. With a total of 35 patients, if ≥6 MPRs were achieved, the study was considered positive. The thresholds differ from the initially submitted protocol due to a typographical error, which has now been corrected. If positive, it would be further evaluated in a randomized trial to verify the clinical superiority of this treatment modality in selected EGFR-mutant early-stage NSCLC. The sample size of the study is calculated based on the primary endpoint of MPR. The unacceptable (null hypothesis) MPR for the study was 10% and the desirable (alternative hypothesis) MPR was 30% following investigators' determinations and historical data. When the MPR rate is 10%, the probability of early termination is 73.4% with an average sample size of 22.5. The type I error (alpha) was set as 5% (one-sided) and type II error (beta) was set as 10%, generating 90% power.

Statistical analyses are to be carried out using SPSS version 25.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R 4.2.3 (http://www.r-project.org) (R packages used for this study included: (Survival analysis) survival v3.5-5, survminer v0.4.9; (Genomic analysis) maftools v2.16.0, deconstructSigs v1.9.0, MutationalPatterns v3.10.0; (Visualization) ggplot2 v3.4.2, ComplexHeatmap v2.16.0; (Statistical testing) rstatix v0.7.2) (Discrepancy from the protocol was due to software updates during the study period). For continuous variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney U test) was used for two-group comparisons when normality was not assumed, as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, the two-sample t-test was applied. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was used when data met normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions (tested by Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively) (Supplementary Table S1). Otherwise, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. Fisher's exact test was used to compare two categorical variables. All statistical tests are two-tailed, and p ≤ 0.05 is considered to indicate statistical significance, with a 95% confidence deployed interval. No multiplicity adjustments were made for secondary or exploratory analyses; thus, all findings beyond the primary endpoint should be interpreted as exploratory. p-values for these analyses are presented as nominal values without correction for multiple testing. For the pathological assessment endpoint including pCR and MPR, an experienced pathologist evaluates paired specimens of lung cancer and involved lymph nodes from each patient with at least 3 slides upon evaluation of tumour bed, necrosis, fibroblast and infiltrating immune cells. A histogram is applied to show pCR and MPR. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI was used to calculate the correlations between pathological response and clinical-relevant quantitative data including expression of PD-L1, EGFR mutation subtypes, gender and etc. For survival relevant endpoint, the Kaplan–Meier is applied for the EFS and OS curve with estimation for median and 95% CI value if available. Difference between survival is compared through log-rank test. For safety analysis, descriptive statistical analysis is applied to calculate the incidence of all-grade and grade 3–4 AEs according to CTCAE 5.0. To be noted, missing data were minimal in this study. For the primary efficacy analysis (MPR), all 35 patients had evaluable pathological specimens. For survival analyses, patients were censored at the last follow-up date if no event occurred. Two patients who did not undergo surgery were included in the intention-to-treat population for efficacy analyses. For genomic analyses, samples with insufficient quality (e.g., low tumour purity or failed sequencing) were excluded on a per-analysis basis, as indicated in the figure legends. No imputation was performed for missing data.

DNA extraction and quality control

Tumour tissue obtained from surgical procedures or biopsies was processed into formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections, with the tumour fraction of each sample determined through hematoxylin and eosin staining. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from tumour tissues with a pathological tumour cellularity of at least 30%, utilizing either the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit from Qiagen (Hilden, Germany) or the MagPure FFPE DNA Kit (High Pure) from Magen (Guangzhou, China). Matched gDNA was extracted from EDTA-anticoagulated peripheral whole blood or buffy coat samples using the MagPure Universal DNA Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. DNA concentrations were quantified using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay from Thermo Fisher (Carlsbad, CA, USA). For circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA), approximately 10 mL of peripheral blood was collected and preserved in Cell-Free DNA BCT tubes (Streck, La Vista, NE, USA). Within 72 h of collection, the sample was centrifuged at 2000 g and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was then transferred to a 15 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 16,000 g and 4 °C for an additional 10 min. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a new tube and stored at −80 °C until further use. cfDNA was extracted from 4 to 5 mL of plasma using either the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit or the QIAsymphony DSP Circulating DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Quantification of cfDNA was conducted with the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay (Thermo Fisher, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

Whole exome library preparation, sequencing and analysis platform

Library preparation for whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed using the Twist Human Core Exome Kit (Twist Bioscience, South San Francisco, CA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. FFPE DNA samples and matched white blood cell (WBC) gDNA samples were fragmented to a peak size of 200 bp using the M220 Focused-Ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA). The fragmented DNA underwent end repair, followed by ligation with Universal Adapters. After post-ligation purification, the ligated products were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with index primers, with the number of amplification cycles tailored according to the manufacturer's recommendations. Exome capture was achieved using the Twist Fast Hybridization and Wash Kit, employing a 33 Mb Human Core Exome panel along with a custom-designed supplementary panel. Up to 8 libraries were multiplexed in a single capture reaction, with each requiring 400 ng of input. The Qubit™ dsDNA HS assay (Thermo Fisher, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to quantify the final libraries. After determining library size distribution with the LabChip GX Touch System, the libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), generating 2 × 151 bp paired-end reads with unique dual indexing, achieving a mean target coverage of 500× for tumour samples and 150× for paired normal samples.

WES data analysis was conducted using the Illumina DRAGEN Bio-IT Platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) unless specified otherwise. FastQ files were generated from raw BCL data using the DRAGEN Bcl Convert pipeline v3.7.4. Adapters were trimmed using fastp version 0.23.0,25 discarding reads shorter than 50 bp. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (NCBI GRCh37; hg19).26 Initial sequencing and analysis marked PCR duplicates for downstream filtering. As part of a quality control (QC) process for all samples captured by the Human Core Exome panel, both tumour and paired-normal alignments were evaluated against multiple QC parameters using in-house software to assess capture efficiency, coverage uniformity, and library complexity. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (INDELs) were called with a minimum of 5 variant-supporting reads and a mutation variant allele frequency (VAF) of at least 3%. Variants present at equal to or greater than population frequencies in the ExAC, 1000 Genomes, dbSNP, and ESP6500SI-V2 databases were excluded. The ratio of tumour allele frequency (AF) to paired-normal AF was calculated for each variant to identify authentic somatic mutations, with variants having a ratio <3 or both tumour and paired-normal AFs >10% being excluded. All variants passing the applied filters were annotated using ANNOVAR27 and SnpEff version 3.6.28

Genomic metrics calculation and statistical analysis

Tumour purity and HRD score were calculated using Sequenza version 3.0.0 (R package [http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/biotools/sequenza/],) and scarHRD version 0.1.1 ([https://github.com/sztup/scarHRD]). Genomic instability metrics were determined using an in-house algorithm, Burning Rock Instability Detection of the Genome (BRIDGE),29 which incorporates the weighted genome instability index (WGII). This index combines two chromosomal instability (CIN) assessment metrics: the numerical complexity score (nCIN) and the structural complexity score (sCIN), which are based on chromosomal and large-scale structural aberrations, respectively. Additionally, Whole Genome Doubling (WGD) and aneuploidy were evaluated. GISTIC 2.030 was employed to identify somatic copy number alterations (sCNAs), either across the entire cohort or between MPR and non-MPR groups. Mutational signatures were calculated using the R package deconstructSigs31 and MutationalPattern.32 HLA prediction was conducted using the OptiType software,33 and HLA-LOH was identified by BRIDGE based on the whole-genome SNP-based genomic instability analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.2.3 (http://www.r-project.org). Statistical differences of specific continuous and categorical variables between two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Fisher's exact test, respectively. Changes in genomic metrics before and after neoadjuvant therapy were evaluated using the paired Wilcoxon test. Associations between two continuous variables were measured using Pearson's correlation coefficient. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) analysis

ctDNA analysis was performed using a personalized tumour-informed ctDNA panel based on whole-exome sequencing (PROPHET, Burning Rock Biotech, Guangzhou, China). The PROPHET algorithm for quantifying ctDNA was previously detailed in an earlier publication.34 Briefly, patient-specific somatic variants were identified through the analysis of WES data obtained from the primary tumour and matched normal white blood cells. From the identified variants, up to 50 with the highest Variant Allele Frequency (VAF) and VAF ≥3.0% were selected to construct the personalized panel. The biotinylated capture probe pool was developed in-house, tailored to each individual's personalized panel. Library preparation and enrichment followed the protocols using the Burning Rock HS unique molecular identifier (UMI) library preparation kit. Ultra-deep UMI-based sequencing was conducted on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), generating 2 × 151 bp paired-end reads, with a target raw depth of 100,000×. The ctDNA fraction in plasma samples was estimated using the maximum likelihood (ML) method, which monitored multiple loci as previously established. Initially, PROPHET assumed that the mutation rate at each site followed a Poisson distribution to assess the significance of each informed mutation. Sites with a p-value <0.05 were considered significant. A likelihood ratio test was then applied to determine whether the ctDNA fraction in the sample was significantly greater than zero, yielding a sample-level p-value. Ultimately, the assay sensitivity, defined as the ability to detect a single variant at 0.01% variant allele frequency with ≥95% confidence. ctDNA positivity was defined as detection of ≥2 significant variants with sample-level p < 0.005, with informative results requiring successful panel design from tumour sequencing.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between May 10, 2022, and March 20, 2024, 187 patients were screened for study eligibility, and 35 patients diagnosed with EGFR-mutant stage IIB-IIIB(N2) NSCLC, received at least one dose of neoadjuvant sintilimab in combination with carboplatin and nab-paclitaxel (stage I cohort of the trial, n = 18; stage II cohort of the trial, n = 17) (Fig. 1). All enroled patients were included in the clinical efficacy and safety analysis. Among the enroled patients, 68.6% had a history of smoking (current or former), and 74.3% carried sensitive EGFR mutations. The majority (77.1%) of the enroled patients were classified as stage IIIA-IIIB. Clinical efficacy and survival outcomes

Detailed clinicopathological features, along with radiological and pathological responses for individual enroled patients, are presented (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. S2A). For the primary endpoint analysis, 12 patients (34.3%, 95% CI 19.1%–52.2%) achieved major pathological response (MPR), including 4 patients (11.4%, 95% CI 3.2%–26.7%) who achieved pathological complete response (pCR). Among patients with sensitive EGFR mutations, 26.9% (7/26) and 15.4% (4/26) achieved MPR and pCR, respectively. Notably, patients with the 19del mutation had an MPR of 27.3% (3/11) and a pCR of 9.1% (1/11), while those with the L858R mutation had an MPR of 26.7% (4/15) and a pCR of 20.0% (3/15) (Fig. 2B). The confirmed objective response rate (ORR) was 21/35 (60%), with a disease control rate (DCR) of 97.1%. Patients with EGFR L858R mutations exhibited a tendency of higher radiological response rate compared to those with the 19del mutation (67% vs. 46%, Fisher's exact test p = 0.43) (Fig. 2C). One patient experienced PD during neoadjuvant treatment due to newly developed intrapulmonary metastasis. The overall N downstaging rate (lymph node metastasis stage reduced, including N2 to N1/N0 and N1 to N0) was 51.4% (18/35) (Fig. 2C). Upon stratifying MPR by clinicopathological features, we observed that patients without a smoking history had a significantly higher MPR rate compared to those with a smoking history (p = 0.0050), while baseline PD-L1 expression showed no correlation with MPR (p = 0.79) (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Perioperative safety profile and surgical outcome

As of the data-cutoff, the safety profiles of complete two stage cohort were consistent with those previously reported. All patients have experienced treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) and most of them were chemotherapy-related AEs including alopecia (29/35, 83%), nausea (23/35, 66%), paresthesia (21/35, 60%) and fatigue (21/35, 60%). Maximum-grade 3 or 4 treatment-related adverse events were 29% among which granulocytopenia was the most common adverse event (6/35, 17.1%). Immune-related adverse events occurred infrequently and were primarily mild, including thyroid dysfunction and skin rash. No patients experienced treatment discontinuation or treatment-related deaths during the neoadjuvant phase due to adverse events. Two patients did not complete the planned three cycles of neoadjuvant treatment due to personal refusal and PD, respectively. Pre- and post-treatment genomic correlation among different therapeutic modalities in localized EGFR-mutated NSCLC

To further illustrate the relationship between genomic features and different neoadjuvant treatment in localized EGFR-mutant NSCLC, we integrated 5 cohorts (N = 104) including real-world data of neoadjuvant EGFR-TKIs with mostly osimertinib (N = 13, in-house retrospective data), a phase 2 prospective cohort of neoadjuvant osimertinib (N = 27, NCT03433469)9 and a phase 2 randomized cohort of either neoadjuvant erlotinib (N = 15) or neoadjuvant GC (gemcitabine + cisplatin) regimen (N = 14) (NCT01407822).35 Most of the patients (85.6%) had performed either large panel targeted sequencing or whole-exome sequencing (WES) while others only had confirmed EGFR status through PCR. To make this genomic stratified analysis more accurate, we took the intersection genes from different panels for in-depth analysis. The most common co-mutations among four EGFR-mutated cohorts were TP53 (60.9%), RB1 (15.2%), RBM10 (9.5%), CDK4 (6.5%) and MDM2 (5.4%) (Fig. 4A). By comparing the radiological response among CTONG2104 cohort, neoadjuvant osimertinib and neoadjuvant erlotinib cohorts, patients with co-mutated RB1 (p = 0.29, 0% with RB1 vs. 74% without RB1), RBM10 (p = 0.060, 25% with RBM10 vs. 80% without RBM10) and MDM2 (p = 0.19, 33% with MDM2 vs. 76% without MDM2) revealed relatively inferior radiological response to neoadjuvant EGFR-TKIs while patients with TP53 (p = 0.13, 71% with TP53 vs. 40% without TP53), RB1 (p = 0.37, 83% with RB1 vs. 57% without RB1) and RBM10 (p = 0.51, 100% with RBM10 vs. 59% without RBM10) exhibited favourable radiological response to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (Fig. 4B). Quantified informed minimal residual disease (MRD) predict efficacy during perioperative treatment

A total of 84 blood samples were collected from 26 patients during the neoadjuvant phase for tumour-informed circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) analysis. At baseline, 92.3% of patients showed positive ctDNA for any mutations, while 72.0% had detectable EGFR mutations (Fig. 5B). Although baseline carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels did not correlate with ctDNA fraction (p = 0.50), tumour size exhibited a significant positive correlation with baseline ctDNA fraction (p < 0.0010) (Fig. 5C). During neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy, allele frequency of both all mutations and EGFR mutations captured through informed ctDNA abundance reduced remarkably, suggesting therapeutic potentials of immunochemotherapy in EGFR-mutant NSCLC (Fig. 5D). By qualitatively comparing the ctDNA clearance and MPR status, we found numerically higher positive predictive value (PPV) compared to negative predictive value (NPV) in both pretreatment (62.5% vs. 0%, p = 0.53) and posttreatment (83.3% vs. 42.1%, p = 0.16) informed ctDNA, respectively (Fig. 5E). Quantitative evaluation of baseline any mutations (p = 0.0029) or EGFR mutations (p = 0.0027) revealed superior predictive efficiency of MPR status while ctDNA of posttreatment did not (Fig. 5F). Moreover, the extent of decreased ctDNA fractions after neoadjuvant treatment was notably correlated with MPR status (p = 0.049) (Supplementary Table S2), suggesting potential predicting value of quantitively longitudinal ctDNA measurement in neoadjuvant treatment.

Patterns of disease recurrence and dynamic monitoring

14 out of 35 patients experienced disease relapse/progression, including 6 local relapse and 8 distant relapse (2 systemic relapse and 6 oligo-relapse). Most patients (9/14) with disease relapse were treated with first-line osimertinib and one patient with rare EGFR compound mutations (G719S/L861Q) was treated with afatinib. The ORR for these patients was 81.8% (9/11) (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). Due to concerns regarding severe toxicity from the sequential use of EGFR-TKIs followed by immunotherapy, we compared the safety profiles of patients treated with sequential and adjuvant osimertinib to data from the FLAURA and ADAURA Chinese cohorts, respectively. Overall, the most common TRAEs included diarrhoea (50%, 56%), mouth ulceration (50%, 44%), paronychia (25%, 38%) and pruritus (25%, 31%) for adjuvant and first-line osimertinib group, respectively. While the safety profile of osimertinib used after disease relapse was comparable to that of first-line osimertinib, patients treated with adjuvant osimertinib following immunochemotherapy exhibited numerically elevated adverse events (AEs) compared to the ADAURA Chinese cohort, specifically an 18% increase in paronychia and a 13% increase in ALT/AST elevation (Supplementary Fig. S3C). No severe immune-related toxicities were observed, which might suggest potential feasibility of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy followed by surgery and adjuvant osimertinib.

Discussion

In contrast to the established efficacy of immunotherapy in advanced wild-type NSCLC,36 EGFR-mutant NSCLC, accounting for up to 40–50% of NSCLC,37,38 have remained resistant to PD-1/PD-L1 based immunotherapy.39 The mechanism of resistance may be due to several factors, including low TMB,40,41 low immunogenicity42, 43, 44 and more suppressive immune microenvironment.45 For early-stage NSCLC, while a number of prospective studies have demonstrated the superiority of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy, most of the trials exclude EGFR-mutant NSCLC and the existing data remained conflict.11,13,46 Unlike advanced NSCLC, early-stage NSCLC exhibits distinct tumour immune microenvironment and genomic features, which might lead to diverse clinical outcome of immunotherapy in early-stage NSCLC.47,48 Besides, chemotherapy in addition to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade can efficiently improve the immune microenvironment and help induce more neoantigen released, which might enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy.49 Last but not least, the clinical goal of neoadjuvant setting for early-stage NSCLC is different from first-line setting for advanced NSCLC, of which pathological regression, downstaging and surgical impact would be the priority to consider.

Our Phase II prospective study provides data from one of the larger cohorts of early-stage EGFR-mutant NSCLC patients treated with neoadjuvant immuno-chemotherapy reported to date. We reported a major pathological response (MPR) rate of 34.3% (95% CI 19.1%–52.2%), a pathological complete response (pCR) rate of 11.4% (95% CI 3.2%–26.7%), and an objective response rate (ORR) of 60.0% (95% CI 42.1%–76.1%). These outcomes are significantly higher than those shown in earlier studies assessing osimertinib as a neoadjuvant therapy for early-stage NSCLC, which reported MPR rates ranging from 10% to 15% and pCR rates from 0% to 5%. This finding might suggest a potential advantage of integrating neoadjuvant immunotherapy into the treatment paradigm for early-stage EGFR-mutant NSCLC.

Patients with the EGFR 19del mutation showed a trend toward modestly improved clinical and survival outcomes compared with those harbouring the EGFR L858R mutation. Previous literatures suggest that while EGFR-TKIs showed lower efficacy in advanced NSCLC with the L858R mutation compared to 19del,1,50 the combination of TKIs with chemotherapy or angiogenesis inhibitors has improved outcomes for those with the L858R mutation.51,52 Though no direct comparisons exist regarding the efficacy of neoadjuvant EGFR-TKIs between these two mutations, differential co-mutation profile may partially explain the observed differences in treatment effectiveness.53,54 Several studies have indicated that co-mutations may adversely influence the clinical efficacy of EGFR-TKIs,54 and prior studies have found correlations between specific co-mutations, such as RB1 and RBM10, and reduced efficacy of adjuvant and neoadjuvant EGFR-TKIs, respectively. Interestingly, in our study, patients with either RB122 or RBM109 co-mutations exhibited significantly better outcomes when treated with neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy compared to those receiving neoadjuvant osimertinib alone.

An increasing number of studies have underscored the prognostic value of minimal residual disease (MRD) in resected early-stage NSCLC55,56; however, the ability to personalize perioperative treatment based on MRD remains uncertain. Post-hoc analyses of circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) from the Checkmate 81612 and AEGEAN studies57 have suggested that ctDNA clearance can predict MPR and pCR following neoadjuvant treatment. However, a considerable number of patients with ctDNA clearance after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy did not achieve MPR or pCR, suggesting limitations in relying solely on qualitative ctDNA status to predict clinical outcomes. In our study, we identified a high ctDNA positivity rate in stage IIB-IIIB NSCLC patients harbouring EGFR mutations, which previously deemed as relatively non-shedding disease.58 Unlike wild-type NSCLC treated with neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy,12,49 our cohort displayed a relatively higher positive predictive value (PPV) of ctDNA for identifying non-responders, contrasting with a negative predictive value (NPV) for responders. This discrepancy likely arises from the lower pathological response rates and the inherent biological traits of EGFR-mutant NSCLC. Furthermore, we found that higher baseline ctDNA fractions, coupled with greater clearance following neoadjuvant treatment, correlated significantly with improved clinical outcomes, suggesting that quantitative ctDNA assessments may provide more valuable prognostic information than qualitative analyses.

With adjuvant osimertinib now endorsed as the standard approach for resected early-stage EGFR-mutant NSCLC,59 it remains critical to evaluate the impact of upfront immunochemotherapy on the efficacy of subsequent TKI therapies, as well as potential severe toxicities associated with this strategy. Prior study has documented a relatively high incidence of severe immune-related adverse events among advanced NSCLC patients receiving osimertinib after prior immunotherapy.60 Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that the safety profile declines significantly when immunotherapy is added to TKIs.61,62 In our cohort, patients who relapsed after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy and surgery generally tolerated subsequent first-line osimertinib without unexpected toxicity,50 possibly owing to the time interval since the last immunotherapy dose. Even among those initiating adjuvant osimertinib approximately one-month post-surgery, although there was a higher incidence of liver function impairment, the overall safety profile remained comparable to that of the adjuvant osimertinib-only cohort.63 This finding may highlight the feasibility of administering neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy followed by surgery and adjuvant osimertinib. Nevertheless, the question of whether this treatment strategy can surpass the efficacy of adjuvant osimertinib alone warrants further extended follow-up and larger cohorts.

The present study is limited by its modest sample size, single-centre design, non-randomized nature, and restriction to an Asian population. These factors may introduce selection bias and preclude definitive conclusions regarding the superiority of immunochemotherapy over EGFR-TKIs in the neoadjuvant setting. To address these limitations, we have initiated a large-scale, multicentre randomized controlled trial to evaluate the potential benefit of neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy vs. TKIs in selected patients with resectable EGFR + NSCLC (ChiCTR2500106897, NCT 07038460). Although emerging data suggest that specific co-mutations may have predictive value across different treatment modalities, integrating data from multiple trials with heterogeneous enrolment criteria, sequencing platforms, and response evaluation methodologies in the cross-cohort genomic analysis might be confounding and larger cohorts will be needed to confirm their utility for individualized decision-making. Extended follow-up will also be required to assess long-term survival outcomes.

Taken together, these results suggest that neoadjuvant sintilimab plus nab-paclitaxel and carboplatin may be a reasonable treatment option for patients with stage IIB–IIIB (N2) resectable EGFR-mutant NSCLC, particularly in molecularly selected subgroups. Upfront immunochemotherapy does not appear to compromise the subsequent efficacy of TKI therapy or substantially worsen toxicity during TKI treatment. Future randomized controlled trials and longer-term follow-up studies will be important to confirm the potential advantages of this approach.
