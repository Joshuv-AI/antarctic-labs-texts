Chromosome-level genome assembly of American sweetgum (Liquidambar styraciflua, Altingiaceae)
Authors: Yazhen Ma, Shixiong Ding, Yingxiong Qiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe deciduous American sweetgum (Liquidambar styraciflua, Altingiaceae) is a popular ornamental and economically valuable tree renowned for its sweet-smelling bark resin, abundant volatile substances, and spectacular fall leaf color. However, the absence of a reference genome hinders thorough investigations into the mechanisms underlying phenotypic variation, secondary metabolite synthesis and adaptation, both in this species and other Liquidambar members. In this study, we sequenced and constructed a chromosome-level assembly of the L. styraciflua genome, covering 662.48 Mb with a scaffold N50 of 39.54 Mb, by integrating PacBio, Illumina and chromosome conformation capture data. We identified 58.83% of the genome sequences as repetitive elements and 25,713 protein-coding genes, 97.28% of which were functionally annotated. The genome sequencing reads, assembly and annotation data have been deposited in publicly available repositories. This high-quality genome assembly provides valuable resources for further evolutionary and functional genomic studies in American sweetgum and other Liquidambar species.Subject terms: Evolutionary genetics, Genome

Background & Summary

Liquidambar, the sole genus in Altingiaceae, comprises about 15 species disjunctively distributed in Southeast and East Asia, the eastern Mediterranean, eastern North America and Mesoamerica1. According to fossil records2,3, this genus was widespread across the Northern Hemisphere during the Cenozoic, with some extant species exhibiting morphological stasis4. Members of this genus are globally appreciated for their ornamental, aromatic, industrial and medicinal values5–7. L. styraciflua is a deciduous forest tree native to the eastern United States, eastern and central Mexico and the Central America8. It is commonly used as ornamental tree because of its colorful leaves and high resilience, and has been widely cultivated outside its natural habitat. In addition, this species is commonly called American sweetgum for its production of a sweet-smelling gum from its cracked trunk. The medicinal qualities of resins and essential oils extracted from the leaves have been traditionally utilized by native Americans to treat various ailments such as inflammation, stomachaches, wounds, coughs, bronchitis, liver enlargement, and amenorrhea9,10. Terpenes are the main chemical components of the leaf and stem oils10,11, that are proven to have important therapeutic potential with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and anticancer properties12,13. However, the terpenoids synthesis pathway in L. styraciflua remains uncharacterized due to the lack of a high-quality genome assembly for the species. Additionally, to date, there is only one reference genome sequence available for Liquidambar14. The limited genomic resources have hindered investigation of species relationships and their evolutionary history over time in the genus.

In this study, we utilized a combination of the PacBio HiFi data and Hi-C data to generate a high-quality chromosome-scale genome of American sweetgum. The assembled genome had a total length of 662.48 Mb, with a contig N50 of 18.73 Mb and a scaffold N50 of 39.54 Mb (Table 1). Approximately 94.67% (627.32 Mb) of the contig sequences were anchored to 16 pseudo-chromosomes, corresponding to the known haploid chromosome count of the species15 (Figs 1, 2). A total of 389.75 Mb of the sequences were identified as repetitive sequences, and 25,713 protein-coding genes were annotated across the genome (Table 1). Our results provide a valuable resource for exploring the evolutionary genomics and functional genomics studies of the genus Liquidambar, and for elucidating the genetic basis of volatile substance diversity in American sweetgum.

Methods

Sample collection and sequencing

For genome sequencing, fresh young leaves of L. styraciflua were collected from an adult individual at the Kunming Botanical Institute, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Yunnan Province, China. High-quality genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB method. Short-read libraries were constructed following Illumina’s standard protocol, and paired-end reads (150 bp) were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform, producing a total of 43.01 Gb (64.91×) of raw data. The data were used to perform genome survey analysis (Table 2). For long-read sequencing, the PacBio single molecule real-time (SMRT) library was prepared using the SMRT bell express template prep Kit and sequenced on the Pacific Bioscience Sequel IIe platform. The raw data were processed into high-fidelity HiFi reads using the CCS workflow, yielding approximately 21.23 Gb of HiFi data, equivalent to approximately 32.04× genome coverage (Table 2). Hi-C libraries were prepared following a modified library preparation protocol16 and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform with a 150-bp paired-end strategy. This process generated approximately 108.31 Gb (163.45×) of raw data for subsequent pseudochromosome assembly (Table 2).

Four different tissues (leaf, root, stem and fruit) from the same plant were collected for total RNA extraction. The RNA samples were divided into two aliquots: one for mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and one for full-length cDNA sequencing (Iso-Seq). The Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform was used for RNA-seq to generate on average of 8.3 Gb of reads per sample. Iso-Seq was performed on the PacBio Sequel IIe system using the Iso-Seq pipeline, producing 56.62 Gb of polymerase reads.

Genome assembly

Before assembling the genome, we estimated the genome size, heterozygosity rate, and repeat content of L. styraciflua by analyzing the 17-mer frequency distribution using Jellyfish v2.2.7. Raw reads were cleaned and filtered using fastp v0.19.47. Based on a total of 33,475,815,009 17-mers and a 17-mer depth peak of 46, the genome size of L. styraciflua was estimated to be 708.09 Mb. Meanwhile, the estimated heterozygous ratio and repeat content were estimated to be approximately 0.74% and 60.11%, respectively (Fig. 1a).

Next, the PacBio HiFi reads were assembled into initial contigs using Hifiasm v0.16.1, employing default parameters. The contig assembly encompassed a total size of 662.48 Mb, with a contig N50 of 18.73 Mb. For chromosome-level assembly, Hi-C data were utilized to group, order and orient the assembled contigs, and anchor them into pseudomolecules using ALLHiC v0.9.8. We manually inspected the Hi-C contact maps of the final assembly with Juicebox v2.13.07. Ultimately, the assembled genome size was 662.48 Mb, with 627.32 Mb anchored onto 16 pseudochromosomes (anchor rate: 94.67%) and a scaffold N50 of 39.54 Mb (Figs. 1b, 2, Table 1).

Genome annotation

Extensive de-novo TE Annotator (EDTA) v1.9.3, a benchmark tool that integrates homology-based and structure-based programs along with filtering scripts for comprehensive detection of transposable elements, were used for repeat annotation. A total of 656,484 repetitive sequences were annotated, spanning a cumulative length of 389,746,891 base pairs (bp) and accounting for 58.83% of the genome. Among these, long terminal repeat (LTR) elements accounted for the largest proportion (39.05%) of the assembly, containing 256,169 elements (258,710,621 bp; Tables 1, 3, Fig. 2).

For gene annotation, we adopted an integrated strategy that merged ab initio prediction, homology-based prediction, and transcriptome-based prediction to predict the protein-coding genes17. AUGUSTUS v3.2.318, GeneID v1.419, GENSCAN v1.020, GlimmerHMM v3.0.421 and SNAP v2013-11-29 were utilized for ab initio gene predication. For homology-based gene prediction, protein sequences of four published plant species, including Liquidambar formosana14, Cercidiphyllum japonicum22, Arabidopsis thaliana and Kalanchoë fedtschenkoi23 were used to aligned onto the repat-masked genome using TBLASTN with and E-value threshold of 1e−5. Subsequently, GeneWise v2.4.124 was used to align the matching proteins to homologous genome sequences for precise spliced alignments. For transcript-assisted annotation, raw Iso-Seq reads were processed using the IsoSeq pipeline (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/IsoSeq) to obtain polished consensus sequences. RNA-seq reads from four different tissues were aligned to the genome using HISAT v2.0.4, and the results were used for genome-based transcript assembly using StringTie v1.3.3. Additionally, Trinity v2.0.64225 was employed for de novo transcript assembly. Assembled transcripts and full-length transcript sequences were subsequently aligned to the genome to predict gene structure using PASA v2.4.1. All candidate genes models generated from these approaches were integrated into a final consensus set using EVidenceModeler v1.1.126. Finally, PASA was used to update and optimize the gene models by incorporating UTRs and alternative splicing information.

Putative gene functions were annotated by comparing sequences against the NCBI Non-Redundant database, TrEMBL, and SwissProt databases using BLASTP v2.12.0. Protein domains were identified using InterProScan v5.54-87.027 against the InterPro protein databases. Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathway information were assigned using Blast2GO v2.528 and the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS; https://www.genome.jp/kegg/kaas/), respectively. In total, the L. styraciflua genome was predicted to contain 25,713 protein-coding genes, with an average gene length of ca. 6,625 bp. Exons and introns had average lengths of ca. 240 bp and ca. 1,160 bp, respectively, with each gene containing an average of 5.16 exons (Table 1). Among these predicted genes, 25,014 (97.28%) were able to be annotated using at least one public database (Table 4).

Data Records

The raw sequencing data including the PacBio data, Illumina short reads, transcriptome data and Ios-seq data and the assembled genome have been deposited to National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) with accession numbers SRR29334813 to SRR2933482629–42 and JBEJUM00000000043 under BioProject PRJNA1121666. The final genome assembly and annotation data are available in the Figshare database44.

Technical Validation

The quality of the L. styraciflua genome assembly was evaluated based on three distinct methods. We first used BWA v0.7.1745 to align Illumina short reads to the L. styraciflua genome to determine the accuracy of the final assembly. In total, 98.3% of the filtered short reads were mapped to the sequences of L. styraciflua genome, with a mapping coverage of 99.9%, indicating a high level of accuracy in the assembly. To assess the integrity of the genome, we also performed benchmarking universal single-copy orthologs analysis using BUSCO 5.5.046 with database of embryophyte_odb10. The results showed 98.6% eudicot BUSCOs in the L. styraciflua genome and 93.9% eudicot BUSCOs in its predicted gene models (Table 1). In addition, the LTR Assembly Index (LAI) for the assembly was also calculated using the LAI module inside LTR_retriever v.2.9.067 to evaluate the contiguity of the assembly. The LAI score for the whole genome was estimated to be 14.1, surpassing the quality standard for reference genomes. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the L. styraciflua genome assembly possesses high accuracy, contiguity and completeness.
