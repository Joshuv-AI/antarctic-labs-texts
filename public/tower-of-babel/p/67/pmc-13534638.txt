Incorporating dataset-level semantic priors into large language models for environmental time-series forecasting: a soil moisture and temperature case study
Authors: Hang Yin, Lei Xu, Zeyu Wu, Gaoyang Song, Rana Muhammad Adnan Ikram, Xiaojiang Peng, Murtaza Hasan, Shahbaz Gul Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnvironmental time-series forecasting is challenged by non-stationarity, limited observability, and the absence of explicit representations of system-level context. While recent large language model (LLM)-based approaches have shown promise for sequential prediction, their ability to incorporate global environmental characteristics beyond local numerical observations remains insufficiently explored. This study introduces a semantic-prior-augmented LLM (SP-LLM) framework that integrates numerical soil moisture and soil temperature time series with dataset-level semantic representations describing recurring patterns, temporal resolution, and overall system behavior. These dataset-level semantic priors are constructed solely from training data and provide contextual information not directly encoded in local observation windows. Using high-frequency greenhouse soil data as a case study, we evaluate the proposed approach across short-, mid-, and long-term forecasting horizons and benchmark it against linear models, deep learning architectures, and foundation time-series models. Results show that incorporating semantic priors reduces mid-term forecasting error by 4–8% in mean absolute error (MAE) compared with numerical-only LLM models and improves forecast stability across 6–12-h prediction horizons for both soil moisture and soil temperature. At longer horizons (24–48 h), performance differences between models diminish, indicating intrinsic predictability limits of the system. These findings demonstrate how semantic representations can complement numerical data in environmental modeling and provide insight into the horizon-dependent strengths and limitations of LLM-based approaches for dynamic environmental systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57634-8.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Environmental time-series forecasting plays a central role in environmental modeling, supporting applications such as ecosystem monitoring, climate analysis, hydrological assessment, and agricultural decision support1–3. Accurate prediction of state variables, including soil moisture and soil temperature, is particularly important because they influence land–atmosphere interactions, plant growth, and water-energy exchange processes4–6. Despite extensive research, forecasting these variables remains challenging because soil systems exhibit nonlinear dynamics, non-stationarity, multi-scale temporal dependencies, and strong sensitivity to external disturbances such as irrigation and climate regulation7,8.

Traditional approaches to soil moisture and temperature forecasting have relied on physically based models that explicitly represent soil–plant-atmosphere processes9,10. While such models offer interpretability and physical consistency, their practical application is often constrained by high parameterization requirements and uncertainty under real-world operating conditions. As an alternative, data-driven methods, including machine learning and deep learning models, have been widely adopted for environmental time-series forecasting11–14. These approaches have demonstrated strong short-term predictive performance but typically depend exclusively on numerical time series inputs, implicitly assuming that all relevant system information is contained within local observation windows.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have introduced new possibilities for sequential modeling beyond natural language processing15–18. By leveraging autoregressive sequence learning and large-scale pretraining, LLM-based architectures have been adapted for time-series forecasting through techniques such as tokenization, patching, and prompt-based conditioning19–21. These methods have shown promising results across a range of domains; however, most existing LLM-based time-series models treat environmental observations purely as numerical sequences, without explicit representations of global system characteristics. Recent research has explored integrating semantic and contextual information into LLM-based time-series forecasting frameworks. For example, event-aware forecasting methods incorporate textual descriptions of external events or news signals to enhance predictive performance by providing contextual cues beyond numerical observations22. Similarly, studies investigating the capability of foundation language models to approximate complex physical processes suggest that large pre-trained models can capture latent structure in dynamic systems when properly conditioned23. These developments highlight the growing interest in combining textual knowledge representations with numerical time-series modeling. However, most existing approaches primarily rely on event-level or window-level contextual information. At the same time, the representation of dataset-level characteristics describing global environmental dynamics remains relatively unexplored.

Environmental systems often exhibit recurring operational patterns and dataset-level regularities that extend beyond local temporal windows. In greenhouse and managed soil systems, for example, irrigation schedules, control strategies, and sampling protocols introduce characteristic temporal structures that may not be fully inferred from short observation sequences alone. Conventional numerical techniques for capturing such structure, such as seasonal decomposition, trend extraction, or static covariates, encode quantitative patterns but do not represent qualitative system-level knowledge in a flexible or transferable manner24,25. Although large language models have recently been explored for time-series forecasting, most existing approaches treat numerical sequences primarily as tokenized inputs or incorporate short-window textual prompts that describe local temporal dynamics. In contrast, the central contribution of this study lies in introducing dataset-level semantic priors that encode global characteristics of the environmental system derived from statistical properties of the training dataset. These semantic priors describe attributes such as sampling resolution, recurring operational patterns, and overall temporal behavior of the monitored system. By integrating such dataset-level contextual descriptors with numerical time-series representations, the proposed framework enables forecasting models to incorporate system-level contextual knowledge that cannot be directly inferred from short observation windows while preserving strict temporal causality.

In this study, we posit that environmental time series contain latent semantic structure at the dataset level, reflecting global characteristics such as sampling resolution, recurring operational cycles, and overall system behavior. We propose to represent this information explicitly using dataset-level semantic priors, formulated as static textual descriptions derived solely from the training data. These semantic priors provide contextual information that complements numerical observations and can be integrated into LLM-based forecasting models through multimodal learning.

The objective of this work is not solely to improve predictive accuracy, but to investigate how semantic representations influence forecasting behavior across different temporal horizons. Using soil moisture and soil temperature dynamics as a case study, we evaluate whether dataset-level semantic priors enhance forecast stability and generalization at short- and mid-term horizons, and analyze their limitations in long-term prediction scenarios. By focusing on horizon-dependent model behavior, this study aims to provide modeling insight into the capabilities and constraints of semantic-prior-augmented LLMs for environmental time-series forecasting.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

Soil moisture and soil temperature data were collected from a commercial greenhouse cultivating Aglaonema commutatum (Big Apple) located in Yunfu, Guangdong Province, China (22°97′N, 111°82′E). The greenhouse is oriented north–south, measuring 50 m in length, 7 m in span, and 5 m in ridge height. To enable continuous environmental monitoring and automated regulation, an Internet-of-Things (IoT)-based sensing and control system was deployed (Fig. 1).

Environmental regulation and irrigation equipment are controlled via a programmable logic controller (PLC).

Soil temperature and soil moisture sensors were installed at approximately 15 cm below the soil surface, consistent with the crop’s shallow rooting characteristics. Environmental sensors were mounted at the geometric center of the greenhouse, 2.4 m above ground level, to reduce the effects of localized temperature variation. The soil moisture and soil temperature datasets were recorded at a temporal resolution of 10 min, enabling the capture of both short-term fluctuations induced by irrigation and climate control operations and longer-term environmental trends.

Data preprocessing

A standardized preprocessing pipeline was applied to ensure data quality and consistency. Raw sensor readings were first screened for abnormal spikes and sensor-induced noise, and obvious outliers were removed. Missing or blank values, primarily caused by temporary sensor communication failures, were imputed using linear interpolation between adjacent valid observations.

All variables were subsequently standardized using z-score normalization to mitigate scale differences and facilitate stable model training. For evaluation and interpretation, predicted values were transformed back to their original physical units using inverse normalization. The same preprocessing procedure was consistently applied across all models to ensure a fair comparison.

Dataset-level semantic priors

We define dataset-level semantic priors (DLSPs) as static semantic representations that encode global characteristics of an environmental time series using information from the training dataset alone. These characteristics describe system-level properties that extend beyond local temporal windows, including sampling resolution, recurring operational patterns, and qualitative aspects of overall system behavior. Unlike numerical representations such as trend components, seasonal decompositions, or static covariates, DLSPs are formulated in natural language. This representation enables the encoding of qualitative system knowledge that may be difficult to express using fixed numerical parameterizations. Importantly, DLSPs do not summarize future observations, thereby preserving temporal causality. In this study, DLSPs were constructed once prior to model training using descriptive statistics and qualitative inspection of the training data only. The resulting textual descriptions remained fixed throughout training and inference and were shared across all samples within a dataset. Separate semantic priors were defined for soil moisture and soil temperature to reflect their distinct physical behaviors and temporal characteristics.

To reduce subjectivity in the construction of dataset-level semantic priors, a structured procedure was followed. First, descriptive statistics of the training data were calculated, including the sampling frequency, mean, range, standard deviation, and dominant periodic patterns identified via autocorrelation analysis. Second, these statistics were mapped to a predefined textual template that converts numerical characteristics into standardized semantic descriptions (e.g., “sampled every 10 min”, “exhibits recurrent irrigation-driven spikes”, or “follows smooth diurnal variation”). Because the same statistical inputs and template rules are used, different researchers applying the procedure to the same training dataset would produce highly comparable semantic priors. Although minor wording variations may occur, the resulting semantic information remains consistent with the encoded dataset-level characteristics. By design, DLSPs provide contextual information that complements numerical observations rather than replacing them. Their role is to supply global system awareness to the forecasting model, enabling it to condition predictions on dataset-level regularities that may not be inferable from short observation sequences alone.

Unlike window-level semantic descriptors that summarize short-term dynamics within individual observation windows, the dataset-level semantic priors introduced in this study represent global contextual knowledge that remains constant across the entire dataset. These descriptors are constructed from statistical summaries of the training data and converted into structured natural-language descriptions using a predefined template. Once generated, the semantic priors remain fixed throughout training and inference, ensuring that they provide contextual conditioning without introducing additional time-dependent inputs. This design allows the forecasting model to incorporate global environmental characteristics while maintaining a clear separation between dynamic numerical observations and static contextual information.

Experimental setup

It is important to note that all forecasting experiments in the present study were conducted under a univariate setting. Specifically, each model used only the historical sequence of the target variable (soil moisture and soil temperature) to predict its future values. The monitoring system collected the additional sensor variables but were not used as model inputs or validation targets in the experiments.

All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel i9-13900 K CPU and an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. Models were implemented using PyTorch (version 2.5.1) and the HuggingFace Transformers library (version 4.40.1). The dataset contains 51,989 records collected from 2024-04-01 to 2025–04-01, and it was split chronologically into training, validation, and test sets using a 7:1:2 ratio, meaning 70% of the data was used for training, 10% for validation and model selection, and 20% for testing. This chronological split was adopted to avoid information leakage from future observations into model training and evaluation. The Adam optimizer was employed with a learning rate of and a weight decay of . Mean absolute error was used as the training loss function because it is widely used in environmental time-series forecasting and is robust to outliers and measurement noise commonly present in environmental sensor data. Model selection was based on validation performance, and the same experimental protocol was applied consistently across all baseline methods.

Results and discussion

Experimental settings and baseline models

To evaluate the impact of dataset-level semantic priors on environmental time-series forecasting, experiments were conducted across multiple forecasting horizons, representing short-, mid-, and long-term prediction scenarios. The soil moisture and soil temperature time series were sampled at a 10-min interval. Short-term forecasting corresponds to a 1-h prediction horizon, mid-term forecasting to 6-h and 12-h horizons, and long-term forecasting to 24-h and 48-h horizons (Figs. 2 and 3).

The proposed semantic-prior-augmented LLM model was compared against a set of representative baseline methods spanning different modeling paradigms. These baselines were selected to represent a range of model complexities and inductive biases commonly used in environmental modeling studies, which can be categorized into three groups. The first category was linear-based models, including the D-linear and N-linear models18, which are recent linear architectures designed to efficiently capture the seasonal and trend components of time series. The second category was deep neural network architectures, among which Informer was a transformer-based model, N-HiTs and N-Beats were neural forecasting models based on hierarchical and backward-residual decomposition. At the same time, TimesNet proposed a novel temporal convolutional operator that transformed time series into the frequency domain for more expressive representation learning. The last category was foundation and pre-trained language models, of which Timer34 and Sundial35 were large-scale generative pre-trained transformers for time series, while GPT4MTS36 represented the cutting edge of time-series large language models, designed to integrate both sequential dynamics and contextual semantics. An ablation variant without dataset-level semantic priors was additionally evaluated to isolate the contribution of global semantic conditioning.

All models were trained and evaluated using identical data splits, preprocessing procedures, and evaluation metrics to ensure fair comparison. Model hyperparameters were selected based on validation performance, and results were reported exclusively on held-out test data. Soil temperature dynamics are generally smoother and more periodic than soil moisture dynamics, reflecting stronger thermal inertia and diurnal regulation. Under these conditions, the inclusion of dataset-level semantic priors appears to help preserve longer-term temporal structure, enabling the model to maintain consistent phase and amplitude over extended horizons. In contrast, models relying solely on local numerical observations exhibited increased error accumulation and temporal drift at the 12-h horizon.

The numerical-only LLM baseline (GPT4MTS) already performed competitively in this setting, indicating that autoregressive sequence modeling is well-suited to soil temperature prediction. The additional improvement achieved by SP-LLM demonstrates that semantic priors provide complementary information beyond numerical dynamics, particularly in maintaining stability and reducing forecast degradation as the prediction horizon increases.

Overall, these results highlight the suitability of semantic-prior-augmented models for mid-term forecasting of thermally regulated environmental variables and reinforce the horizon-dependent value of dataset-level contextual information.

Role of dataset-level semantic priors in environmental forecasting

Across the conducted experiments, the comparison between the numerical-only LLM baseline and the semantic-prior-augmented model provides insight into the modeling role of dataset-level semantic priors. Rather than uniformly improving performance across all horizons, semantic priors primarily influence forecast stability and error growth as prediction horizons increase.

At short-term horizons, where local numerical observations strongly constrain system evolution, the contribution of dataset-level semantic priors is limited. In contrast, at mid-term horizons, semantic priors provide contextual information that complements local temporal dynamics, enabling the model to preserve recurring patterns and reduce forecast degradation. This behavior is consistent across both soil moisture and soil temperature tasks.

The diminishing impact of semantic priors at long-term horizons suggests that their effectiveness is bounded by the intrinsic predictability of the environmental system. When system dynamics become dominated by external interventions or stochastic variability, global contextual conditioning alone cannot overcome fundamental uncertainty.

From a modeling perspective, these findings indicate that dataset-level semantic priors act as stabilizing contextual constraints rather than sources of new predictive information. Their primary value lies in improving mid-term forecast consistency within the inherent predictability limits of environmental systems, rather than extending those limits. In this sense, dataset-level semantic priors function primarily as contextual augmentation, providing global descriptors of the environmental dataset that guide the model toward temporally consistent predictions.

To further isolate the contribution of dataset-level semantic priors from potential effects related to prompt length or prompt complexity, we conducted an additional ablation experiment comparing two configurations: (1) a model conditioned only on window-level semantic descriptors, and (2) the proposed model incorporating both window-level descriptors and dataset-level semantic priors. The comparison was performed on the soil temperature forecasting task at the 6-h prediction horizon, which represents a typical mid-term forecasting scenario where the influence of contextual information is expected to be most pronounced. The results are shown in Table 9, which indicate that including dataset-level semantic priors consistently improves predictive performance compared with the window–level–only configuration. Although this ablation experiment was conducted on a representative mid-term forecasting setting, the observed behavior is consistent with the broader experimental results reported in "Soil moisture forecasting performance" and "Soil temperature forecasting performance" sections, where the benefits of semantic priors are most evident at mid-term horizons. These findings further reinforce the interpretation that dataset-level semantic priors primarily contribute to forecast stabilization when local temporal information becomes less informative.

Discussion

Modeling implications of dataset-level semantic priors

The results across soil moisture and soil temperature forecasting tasks demonstrate that dataset-level semantic priors influence model behavior primarily through contextual stabilization rather than direct accuracy gains. By encoding global characteristics such as sampling resolution and recurring operational patterns, semantic priors provide a form of system-level conditioning that complements local numerical observations.

This conditioning is particularly effective at mid-term horizons, where numerical information from recent observations becomes insufficient to constrain model trajectories. In these settings, semantic priors help preserve recurring temporal structure and reduce error accumulation, resulting in more stable forecasts. Importantly, this effect is observed across variables with different physical properties, suggesting that semantic priors act as a general modeling mechanism rather than a task-specific heuristic.

From a modeling perspective, these findings support the view that semantic representations can serve as contextual constraints within data-driven environmental models. Rather than introducing new predictive signals, dataset-level semantic priors guide model behavior toward globally consistent dynamics, improving robustness without violating temporal causality. The construction of dataset-level semantic priors is computationally lightweight and scales naturally to larger or multi-site datasets. For heterogeneous environmental systems, semantic priors can be defined separately for each site or operational context using the same statistical summarization procedure. In such settings, the priors serve as compact metadata that describe site-specific characteristics, such as sampling resolution, dominant environmental cycles, or management practices. Because these descriptors are static and derived from training data statistics, their computational overhead remains negligible even for large datasets. Future work may explore automated generation of semantic priors directly from statistical summaries to further support large-scale environmental monitoring systems.

Horizon-dependent predictability and model limitations

A consistent pattern across all experiments is the strong dependence of model performance on forecasting horizon. At short-term horizons, recent numerical observations dominate prediction accuracy, and the contribution of semantic priors is limited. At long-term horizons, forecasting performance deteriorates across all modeling approaches, including those augmented with semantic priors.

This behavior reflects intrinsic predictability limits of the environmental system under study. Soil moisture and soil temperature dynamics in managed environments are influenced by external interventions, control actions, and stochastic variability that cannot be fully inferred from historical data alone. The convergence of model performance at extended horizons indicates that semantic conditioning cannot overcome these fundamental sources of uncertainty.

These results highlight the importance of horizon-aware modeling and evaluation in environmental forecasting. Rather than seeking universal performance improvements, modeling approaches should be assessed in relation to the temporal scales at which meaningful predictability exists.

Practical implications for environmental forecasting

The findings of this study have several implications for environmental modeling and decision support. First, the demonstrated benefits of semantic priors at mid-term horizons suggest that their integration may be most valuable in applications requiring short- to medium-range forecasts, where maintaining temporal consistency is critical. Second, the observed degradation at long-term horizons underscores the need for caution in interpreting extended forecasts generated by data-driven models. In such cases, forecasts may be more informative as qualitative trend indicators rather than precise predictions. Finally, the semantic-prior-augmented framework provides a flexible mechanism for incorporating contextual system knowledge into environmental forecasting models without requiring explicit physical parameterization. This approach may be particularly useful in settings where physical models are difficult to calibrate or where recurring operational patterns shape system behavior.

In contrast, long-term forecasting remains challenging for all evaluated approaches, including LLM-based models. This limitation reflects the inherently dynamic and context-dependent nature of agricultural environments, where external interventions and environmental variability constrain long-range predictability. From a practical perspective, short-term and mid-term forecasts are more relevant for greenhouse climate regulation and irrigation scheduling, suggesting that LLM-based approaches are well aligned with real-world agricultural decision-making needs.

Another important consideration concerns the role of exogenous drivers, such as irrigation schedules, greenhouse climate control, and other management interventions. In the present study, these operational variables were not available as structured numerical inputs. Consequently, some management-induced dynamics may be reflected only indirectly through dataset-level semantic priors that describe recurring environmental patterns. If explicit control signals were available, they could be incorporated directly as additional numerical covariates within the forecasting model. Integrating such exogenous variables with semantic priors may further improve predictive performance by jointly capturing both operational interventions and dataset-level environmental characteristics. Exploring hybrid representations that combine numerical control signals with semantic contextual information represents a promising direction for future research.

Limitations and future works

Although the proposed framework demonstrates promising results, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the empirical evaluation is based on data from a single greenhouse monitoring system and two environmental variables (soil moisture and soil temperature). Therefore, the present work should be interpreted as a case study rather than a general benchmark across environmental forecasting systems. Nevertheless, the proposed framework is conceptually applicable to other environmental systems because the dataset-level semantic priors are constructed from general statistical properties of the training data rather than domain-specific assumptions. Future work will extend the evaluation to multi-site environmental datasets and additional environmental variables further to assess the robustness and transferability of the approach.

Second, variability across repeated training runs was not explicitly evaluated due to computational constraints, as training LLM-based forecasting models is relatively resource-intensive. In this study, the reported results correspond to the best-performing configuration obtained during training. Importantly, the same experimental protocol was consistently applied across all baseline models to ensure a fair comparison.

More broadly, large-model–based methods represent an emerging trend in time-series forecasting due to their strong generalization capability. Although they typically involve slower inference speed, agricultural time series generally have modest data volumes. They are well-suited for centralized cloud-based processing. Our results suggest that LLM-based approaches are particularly effective for short-term and mid-term forecasting, which aligns with the practical requirements of agricultural decision-making. In future work, we aim to improve the inference efficiency of the framework for real-time deployment, expand the dataset with richer time-series–text pairs, and explore multimodal information fusion with additional data sources, such as images, to further enhance predictive performance and interpretability.
