Individual and combined effects of maternal per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances exposure on preterm birth: a nested case-control study in China
Authors: Yi Hu, Hao Chen, Sujie Sun, Lin Zhang, Dongjian Yang, Jiuru Zhao, Qianqian Zhang, Yitao Pan, Xiaorui Liu, Zhiwei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPer- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a large class of synthetic chemicals with ubiquitous human exposure. Maternal PFAS exposure has been linked with adverse birth outcomes, but their associations with preterm birth (PTB) remained ambiguous.MethodsTo assess the associations of individual and mixed PFAS exposure with preterm birth (PTB) and its subtypes (spontaneous and iatrogenic), a nested case-control study involving 177 PTB cases and 531 controls was conducted in Shanghai, China. Serum concentration of seven PFAS were measured in early pregnancy via ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). PTB, gestational weeks and birth weight were obtained from electronic medical record system. Conditional logistic regression and restricted cubic spline regression (RCS) were used for individual assessment. Weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression, quantile-based g-computation (QGC), and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) were conducted for PFAS mixture. Subtype analysis and sex stratified analysis were further examined.ResultsBoth individually and in mixtures, significant associations were observed between elevated PFAS concentrations and increased PTB risk, along with reduced gestational weeks and lower birth weight. The WQS, QGC, and BKMR identified perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), per-fluorononanoic acid (PFNA), and perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnDA) as the major contributors, with PFOA being particularly significant. Moreover, higher risks of PFAS-associated PTB were observed in the iatrogenic PTB subgroup and pregnant women with male infants.ConclusionMaternal exposure to PFAS, whether individually or in mixtures, was significantly associated with increased risk of PTB. These associations might be subtype-specific and sex-specific. Further research is needed to validate our results and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12940-025-01213-3.

Background

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), a large group of synthetic chemicals with over 4000 unique compounds, have been broadly applied in commercial and industrial products including firefighting foams, food packaging, nonstick cookware, and water- and stain-resistant fabrics since the 1940 s [1, 2]. Due to their resistance to degradation and biological accumulation in living organisms, PFAS are highly prevalent in various environmental media and result in ubiquitous exposure to humans [3, 4]. Humans are generally exposed to PFAS via dietary intake and drinking water [5]. Once absorbed, the elimination half-lives of PFAS in the human body are extremely long [6]. Taking three most common PFAS as examples, the eliminated half-lives of perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) and perfluorohexane sulfonate (PFHxS) are 2.7 years, 3.4 years, and 5.3 years, respectively [7]. Therefore, although the production and utilization of PFAS have been restricted or under review in many countries, high detection rates of PFAS compounds are still existed in human serum, urine, and breast milk samples around the globe, including pregnant women [8, 9]. Notably, increasing evidence has demonstrated that PFAS could cross the placental barrier, raising public concerns about the impacts of maternal PFAS exposure on not only adverse pregnancy outcomes but also the potential short-term and long-term health damage to offsprings [1, 10].

Preterm birth (PTB), defined as the delivery occurring before 37 completed weeks of gestation, is not only a primary cause of infant mortality and death among children under 5 years old but is also linked to a series of adverse developmental outcomes and long-term health consequences throughout the lifespan [11, 12]. The etiology of PTB is complex and multifactorial, with many factors still unexplained, leading to a lack of effective prevention and treatment options [13, 14]. While limited is known, increasing exposure to environmental contaminants during pregnancy may represent a potential contributor to PTB [15]. In recent years, numerous studies have investigated the association between maternal PFAS exposure and PTB, but current evidence remains equivocal and inconsistent [16]. For example, many studies reported significant associations between PFAS exposure and higher odds ratio (OR) of PTB or reduced gestational week [10, 15, 17– 20], while others studies indicated no relationship [13, 21–23]. Previous studies might have conflicting results because of the differences in study design, sample size, region, ethnicity, or sampling time point (Qu et al., 2024) [24]. Moreover, as a multifactorial disease with various clinical presentations, previous studies have primarily focused on overall PTB, which might conceal the specific influence of PFAS on PTB subtypes [4]. Furthermore, considering that humans are simultaneously exposed to multi-source PFAS in the real word, the combined effects of multiple PFAS exposures deserve more attention.

Therefore, in the present study, we performed a nested case-control study to investigate the individual and combined effects of maternal PFAS exposure in early pregnancy on PTB, including overall PTB and its subtypes (spontaneous and iatrogenic preterm birth), as well as the closely related gestational week and birth weight.

Methods

Study population

This was a nested case-control study grounded in Shanghai sub-cohort of the China National Birth Cohort, a substantial cohort study that completed recruitment and ongoing follow-up in Shanghai, eastern China. The detailed cohort description has been reported elsewhere [25]. Briefly, pregnant women in their first trimester of nature conceived were recruited if they were residents of Shanghai, able to read and write in Chinese, without severe history of mental illness, and plan to receive antenatal care and give birth at the International Peace Maternity& Child Health Hospital. Information on sociodemographic information, lifestyle characteristics and reproductive histories was collected via face-to-face baseline questionnaire administered by specially trained nurses, and maternal blood samples were acquired at recruitment. For the current study, 177 pregnant women having singleton delivery before 37 weeks completed gestation between October 2017 to September 2020 were selected as PTB cases, and controls (singleton delivery at or after 37 weeks completed gestation) were matched according to maternal age and infant sex at a ratio of 1:3 during the same period. Finally, 708 pregnant women were included.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the International Peace Maternity& Child Health Hospital [Grant NO. (GKLW)2016-21]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist (Supplemental Table 1).

Serum PFAS assessment

Maternal blood samples were collected around the gestational age 9 weeks with interquartile range 7.86 to 10 weeks. After collection, blood sample was separated and divided into aliquots by standard operating procedures, then immediately stored in −80 ℃ freezers until PFAS analysis. Serum concentrations of seven target PFAS were measured at State Environmental Protection Key Laboratory of Environmental Health Impact Assessment of Emerging Contaminants, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. Detailed procedure and quality assurance/quality control measures for serum PFASs quantification have been documented before [1, 26]. In brief, PFAS in serum were extracted by liquid-liquid extraction and quantified using ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) coupled with electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (ESI-MS/MS, QTRAP 6500 plus, AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA). Seven PFAS with detection rate above 99% were included as follows: PFOA, PFOS, perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA), perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnDA), 6:2 chlorinated polyfluorinated ether sulfonate (6:2 Cl-PFESA), and perfluoro-3,5,7,9,11-pentaoxadodecanoic acid (PFO5DoA). The limits of quantification (LOQs) value for seven PFAS was 0.02 ng/mL and concentration below the LOQ was replaced with value of LOQ/2 [27].

Outcomes

The dominant outcome of the present study was PTB, defined as singleton live birth occurring prior to 37 completed gestational weeks. Secondary outcomes included gestational week at delivery and neonatal birth weight. Furthermore, overall PTB was classified into spontaneous preterm birth (sPTB, delivery before 37 weeks completed gestation with labor and/or premature rupture of membranes) and iatrogenic preterm birth (iPTB, delivery before 37 weeks completed gestation by induction of labor or planned cesarean birth) [28]. All the outcomes were systematically extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR) system.

Covariates

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed to identify potential confounding factors based on our prior knowledge and previous literature [20, 29–33] (Supplemental Fig. 1), including maternal age (continuous), maternal BMI, maternal education (≤ 9 years, 10–12 years, 13–16 years, ≥ 17 years), annual household income (≤ 100000 CNY, 100000–200000 CNY, 200000–300000 CNY, >300000 CNY), smoking status in the first trimester (no, passive smoking, yes), parity (1, ≥ 2), season of birth (spring, summer, autumn, winter), and infant sex (male, female). Sociodemographic characteristics were obtained from the baseline questionnaire at recruitment. Additionally, information on the season of birth, infant sex, and maternal medical information during pregnancy [such as gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), gestational hypertension (GH), hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism] were recorded from hospital EMR system for further analyses.

Statistical analysis

The distributions of sociodemographic characteristics and birth outcomes were presented using mean ± standard deviation (SD), count (percentage), or percentiles, depending on the data characteristics. T-tests or rank-sum test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables was utilized to evaluate the differences between PTB cases and control groups. Spearman’s rank correlation was applied to assess the correlations among the seven target PFAS. Considering the skewed distributions, PFAS concentrations were normalized using natural logarithm transformation before being entered into any statistical models.

The associations between maternal individual PFAS exposure and PTB, gestational week and birthweight were examined via conditional logistic regression and restricted cubic spline regression (RCS) models. In the conditional logistic regression, both continuous variable (ln-transformed serum PFAS concentration) and categorical variable (divided into quartiles with the lowest quartile as reference) were used to assess the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of individual PFAS exposure and the risk of PTB. The P for trend was calculated by assigning the within-group median concentration of each quartile for every PFAS in conditional logistic regression. Furthermore, an RCS model with 4 knots was performed to explore the non-linear dose-response relationship between PFAS exposure (ln-transformed serum PFAS concentration) and gestational week as well as birthweight.

To explore the combined effects of maternal exposure to PFAS mixtures on PTB, gestational week and birthweight, three additional statistical models were performed, including weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression, quantile-based g-computation (QGC) and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR). Consistent with individual pollutant model, serum PFAS concentration was ln-transformed and same covariates were incorporated.

WQS regression is a highly valuable statistical method that has been widely applied in environmental epidemiology to estimate the combined effects of chemical mixtures on human health outcomes [34]. The comprehensive details of WQS regression have been well described elsewhere [35]. Briefly, WQS is a flexible regression in calculating the combined effects of chemical mixture while considering collinearity and determining the major compounds that accounted for overall effects. In this study, we examined the WQS index (PFAS mixtures) based on the quartiles of chemical levels, and estimated individual PFAS weight for their contributions to the combined effects of mixture. Given that WQS infers in a single direction, we performed the WQS regression in accordance with the cue from the individual pollutant model.

QGC is a novel method for exploring the combined impact of chemical mixtures that developed based on the generalization of WQS regression, which solves the limits of directional homogeneity in WQS and allows for non-linear relationships between chemical mixtures and health outcomes [36]. In this study, QGC was conducted to evaluate the combined effects of PFAS mixture exposure on PTB, gestational week and birth weight, and calculate the weight of each PFAS both in positive and negative directions.

BKMR model was subsequently utilized as a complementary approach to evaluate the individual and combined effects of PFAS mixtures by using a kernel function. Unlike WQS and QGC, BKMR is particularly effective in visualizing the individual exposure-response relationship, while estimating potential non-linear association and differential directions of effects among multiple exposures [37]. The BKMR model enables users to quantify not only the independent effects of individual chemical exposures but also the overall combined mixture effect concurrently. In the BKMR, posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) were calculated to determine the relative importance of a given PFAS exposure on the combined mixture effect. A threshold of 0.5 was used to identify important predictor [38].

Subtypes analysis, sex stratified analysis and sensitivity analysis were further performed. First, we repeated all the individual-pollutant and multi-pollutant models for PTB subtypes (sPTB and iPTB). Second, we conducted stratified analyses by infant sex to estimate the potential sex-specific effects of PFAS exposure on PTB, gestational week, and birthweight. Additionally, given the plausible associations of maternal PFAS exposure with GDM, thyroid disturbances, and GH [39–41] which were known as risk factors for PTB [42, 43], sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the robustness of our findings via excluding women with GDM (n = 55), hyperthyroidism (n = 23), and hypothyroidism (n = 34). Considering that only 8 women had GH in our study, sensitivity analysis by excluding GH case was not conducted.

All the statistical analyses were performed using R software (R version 4.3.2), and statistical significance defined as a two-sided P value less than 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study population and maternal PFAS exposure

The sociodemographic characteristics of 708 mother-infant pairs were shown in Table 1, including 177 PTB cases and 531 controls. The mean maternal age was 31.44 ± 3.91 years, and the mean BMI was 21.46 ± 3.47 kg/m2. Most mothers (93.93%) had a high-school diploma or above, 68.1% of the mothers had an annual household income > 200,000 CNY, and two-thirds (66.67%) were primiparous. During pregnancy, only 14 women smoked, while 193 women (accounting for 27.26%) were exposed to passive smoking. In our study, 55 (7.77%), 8 (1.13%), 23 (3.25%), and 34 (4.80%) of the pregnant women had GDM, GH, hyperthyroidism, and hypothyroidism, respectively. When comparing the PTB group with the control group, no significant difference was observed in most sociodemographic characteristics. However, the proportions of smokers (3.95% vs. 1.32%) and GDM (14.12% vs. 5.65%) were higher in the PTB group. The mean gestational week and birth weight in the PTB group were 35.12 ± 1.80 weeks and 2557.99 ± 537.07 g, which were significantly lower than those in the control group (39.18 ± 1.02 week and 3397.19 ± 387.37 g).

The distribution of maternal serum PFAS concentrations in study population were presented in Table 2. All seven PFAS were detected in over 99% of the study population. Among them, PFOA exhibited the highest median serum concentration, followed by PFOS, 6:2 Cl-PFESA, PFNA, PFDA, PFUnA, and PFO5DoA. The Spearman correlation coefficients of maternal PFAS concentrations were summarized (Supplemental Fig. 2), All seven PFAS were positively correlated with correlation coefficients ranging from 0.50 to 0.94, and strongest coefficient was observed between PFDA and PFUnDA (rs = 0.95).

Association of individual PFAS exposure with PTB, gestational week and birthweight

Conditional logistic regression was utilized to estimate the association between individual PFAS exposure and overall PTB as well as its subtypes (Table 3). Maternal exposure to seven target PFAS, namely PFOA, PFOS, 6:2 Cl-PFESA, PFNA, PFDA, PFUnDA and PFO5DOA, was significantly associated with a higher risk of PTB. When the population was divided into sPTB and iPTB subgroups, higher risks of individual PFAS exposure in relation to PTB were observed in the iPTB subgroup. Furthermore, the non-linear dose-response relationships of PFAS exposure with gestational week and birth weight were examined using RCS (Fig. 1). For gestational week, significant non-linear relationships were found for PFNA and PFUnDA in the overall population, PFOS and PFNA in the sPTB subgroup, and PFUnDA in the iPTB subgroup (P for non-linear < 0.05). For birth weight, significant non-linear relationships were observed for PFOS, PFNA, PFDA, PFUnDA, and PFO5DoA in the overall population, PFOS, PFNA, and PFUnDA in the sPTB subgroup, and PFO5DoA in the iPTB subgroup (P for non-linear < 0.05). Combined effects of PFAS mixture exposure on PTB, gestational week and birthweight

The combined effects of PFAS mixture exposure on PTB, gestational week, and birth weight were evaluated using WQS, QGC, and BKMR (Fig. 2). In the WQS analysis, the PFAS mixture was found to be positively associated with increased risk of PTB (OR = 1.941, 95%CI: 1.503, 2.505 for overall PTB; OR = 1.818, 95%CI: 1.355, 2.441 for sPTB; OR = 2.100, 95%CI: 1.408, 3.132 for iPTB) and negatively associated with gestational week (β = −0.408, 95%CI: −0.590, −0.226 in the overall population; β = −0.425, 95% CI: −0.673, −0.177 in the sPTB subgroup; β = −0.566, 95% CI: −0.872, −0.260 in the iPTB subgroup). Regarding birth weight, negative association was observed in the overall population (β = −104, 95%CI: −152.75, −55.25) and in the sPTB subgroup (β = −121, 95% CI: −183.99, −58.02), while no association was found in the iPTB subgroup (β = −59, 95%CI: −149.87, 31.87) (Fig. 2A). Similar results were observed in the QGC models, showing as significantly increasing risk for PTB and declining gestational week and birth weight except for PFAS mixture and birth weight in the iPTB subgroup (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the BKMR model further validated that PFAS mixture were positively associated with an increased risk of PTB and negatively correlated with gestational week and birth weight when seven target PFAS in given percentile comparing to their corresponding 50th percentile (Fig. 2C).

Stratified analysis and sensitivity analysis

Sex stratified analyses were conducted to explore the potential sex-specific effects of maternal PFAS exposure on PTB, gestational week, and birth weight. After stratification by infant sex, positive associations between individual PFAS exposure and the risk of PTB were mainly observed in male newborns. In contrast, no significant relationship was found in female newborns, with the exception of PFUnDA (Supplemental Table 2). Similarly, in RCS model, significant effect of PFAS exposure on gestational week was only observed in male newborns. As for birthweight, significant effects were observed for PFOS and PFUnDA in male newborns, while the significant effects of PFOA and PFNA were observed in female newborns (Supplemental Fig. 3). In the multi-pollutant models, although the inverse associations of PFAS mixture with gestational week and birth weight were observed in both sexes, the positive correlation between PFAS mixture and the risk of PTB primarily occurred in male newborns (Supplemental Fig. 4). The weights and PIP values of each PFAS in the corresponding WQS, QGC, and BKMR models in the sex stratified analyses were summarized in Supplemental Table 3. Among them, PFOA was considered as the most important contributor.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of our results after excluding participants with GDM (n = 54), hyperthyroidism (n = 23) and hypothyroidism in pregnancy (n = 34), respectively. The results regarding individual PFAS exposure and PFAS mixture exposure were showed in Supplemental Tables 4 and Supplemental Table 5 (Supplemental Fig. 5), respectively. Results of the sensitive analyses were consistent with the overall population findings, strengthening the robustness of our findings.

Discussion

In the current nested case-control study, we performed five statistical models to examine the associations of individual and multiple PFAS exposures with PTB, gestational week, and birth weight. Both individual and as a mixture, significant associations of elevated PFAS concentrations with increased risk for PTB, reduced gestational week and decreased birth weight were observed. When PTB was classified into iPTB and sPTB, the associations between PFAS and PTB risk appeared to be stronger in the iPTB subgroup, while the relationships of PFAS with decreased birth weight were more pronounced in the sPTB subgroup. Furthermore, sex stratified analyses indicated that the associations between maternal PFAS exposure and the risk of PTB were primarily occurred among male newborns. PFOA, PFNA, and PFUnDA were considered as the major contributing factors, especially PFOA.

Our results were in line with previous studies that have used individual or multiple pollutant models determining maternal PFAS exposure and PTB risk [4, 10, 15, 18, 29, 32]. For instance, Yu et al. reported higher odds of PTB in association with maternal PFAS exposure in a family-based cohort study, including PFOA (OR = 1.51, 95%CI: 1.27, 1.80), perfluorobutanoic acid (PFBA, OR = 1.16, 95%CI: 1.09, 1.25) and PFOS (OR = 2.07, 95%CI: 1.70, 2.52) [15]. In a nested case-control study within the LIFECODES cohort (comprising 128 PTB cases and 373 controls), Siwakoti et al. observed increased odds of placental PTB with interquartile-increase concentration in PFDA (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.00, 2.56), PFNA (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 1.06, 2.61) and PFUnDA (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.00, 3.12), with PFNA being the major contributor to placental PTB [4]. Nevertheless, weak or null association between maternal PFAS exposure and PTB also existed [13, 21, 22, 30, 44]. Besides the differences in study design, sample size, and geographic regions, recent studies have emphasized the importance of considering different types of PFAS congeners [45], the potential trimester-specific relationships [19], and the diverse PFAS exposure range with non-linear consideration when investigating the inconsistences among different results [46]. Especially, non-linear relationships of maternal PFAS exposure with birth outcome was examined in this study using RCS, and significant S-shape relationship for gestational week and inverted U-shape relationship for birth weight were observed (P for non-linear < 0.05). Our results suggested that maternal exposure to PFAS was non-monotonically associated with birth outcome, and their relationships might depend on the exposure range of PFAS. Future studies, particularly large cohort study with three trimesters detection of major PFAS congeners, are warranted to better elucidate the dose-related causal association between PFAS exposure and the PTB occurrence.

To our knowledge, only three studies have specifically explored the associations between maternal PFAS exposure and PTB subtypes, including one nested case-control study conducted in Shanxi, China [13], one prospective observational study based on Shanghai Birth Cohort [22], and one nested case-control study from the LIFECODES cohort in America [4]. Similar with our findings, Siwakoti et al. reported significanlty higher odds of placental PTB with prenatal exposure to PFNA, PFDA, and PFUnDA [4]. Whereas, in contrast to our results, two Chinese studies reported null association of PFAS with overall PTB, sPTB, or iPTB [13, 22]. Several factors may explain this phenomenon. First, the PFAS exposure levels in Shanxi nested case-control study were much lower than those in our study (0.87 vs. 7.50 ng/mL for PFOA, 1.73 vs. 5.80 ng/mL for PFOS, and 0.33 vs. 3.12 ng/mL for 6:2 Cl-PFESA), and the incomparable PFAS exposure levels may lead to the inconsistent results [13]. Second, although the PFAS exposure levels were comparable, the proportion of nulliparous women and the sex of newborns in the Shanghai Birth Cohort were 85.5% and 51.1% respectively, which clearly differed with the 66.7% and 66.1% in our study. The discrepancies in these critical demographic characteristics between the two studies may result in different findings [22]. To date, the existing evidence was too insufficient to draw any definite conclusion. Our findings should be interpreted with caution until confirmed by further research. Considering that the underlying etiologies, clinical risk factors, and maternal demographic patterns enormously differ between sPTB and iPTB [28], it is essential to distinguish the PTB subtypes when exploring the impacts of maternal PFAS exposure on PTB.

There are several potential mechanistic hypotheses that may explain our findings. As typical endocrine disrupting chemicals, numerous studies have proposed that PFAS can disrupt estrogens and thyroid hormones, which in turn could affect adverse birth outcomes [47, 48]. For example, Qin et al. found significant interactions between PFAS exposure and cord estradiol in relation to preterm birth in a nested case-control study involving 879 pregnant women in China, indicating that estrogen modified the association between PFAS exposure and PTB [10]. Wang et al. observed negative association of prenatal PFAS exposure with birth length, head circumference as well as gestational age, and maternal thyroid free triiodothyronine (FT3) and free thyroxine (FT4) mediated 30–36% of the effects, suggesting possible mediation roles of thyroid hormones in the relationships between PFAS exposure and birth outcomes [49]. As is well known, PTB is commonly associated with intrauterine infection or inflammation, and changes in intrauterine anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory cytokines play important roles in the pathogenesis of PTB [50]. Several studies have reported that prenatal PFAS exposure significantly increased the inflammatory biomarkers levels of interleukin 6 (IL-6), interleukin 10 (IL-10) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), encouraging further understanding of PFAS with inflammatory pathways on their clinical implications in PTB [51, 52]. Another potential mechanism was also mentioned that maternal exposure to PFAS may lead to PTB is via DNA methylation, since maternal PFAS exposures have been statistically linked with DNA methylation at cytosine-guanine dinucleotides (CpG) that are annotated to gene regions related with growth, inflammation, and immune activity [53–55]. Furthermore, recent studies have provided novel insights into placental arterial vasculature [56], placental structure [1], and plasma metabolomic signatures [57], which may also account for the impact of maternal PFAS exposure on PTB. Nevertheless, the mechanistic pathways by which maternal PFAS exposure increases the risk of PBT remain hypothesized. Further in-depth research is needed to explore the underlying mechanisms.

Moreover, our results suggest potential sex-specific effects of maternal PFAS exposure on PTB, with male infants demonstrating higher risk than female infants. In fact, previous studies have reported the sex-specific impacts of maternal PFAS exposure on PTB and related birth outcomes, although the sex-specific patterns have been inconsistent [4, 33, 58– 60]. The Shanghai Birth Cohort Study reported significant negative associations between maternal perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHpA) exposure and the infant length- for-age Z score, and these adverse association were more pronounced among male infants [33]. A Sweden cohort study found that increased maternal exposure to PFAS (PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, and PFDA) was associated with lower birth weight, while significant association was only observed in females [56]. Although the specific biological mechanisms were still uncertain, several factors, such as discrepancies in hormonal regulation [50, 51], differences in gene environments and epigenetic expression [55], or placental structure and efficiency [1], may partly account for the sexual dimorphism.

There were several notable strengths in the present study. First, given the nested case-control study design, sociodemographic information was prospectively recorded and the PTB outcomes (including PTB and its subtype) were systematically excerpted from EMR system, extremely minimizing the chance of recall bias and outcome misclassification. Second, we focused on seven common PFAS, including traditional long-chain PFAS of PFOA and PFOS and the major short-chain substitute of 6:2 Cl-PFESA, providing a more comprehensive understanding of PFAS exposure and its influence on PTB-related outcomes. Third, the association between mixed PFAS exposure and PTB was examined using QGC, WQS, as well as BKMR, and the consistent results ascertained the robustness and reliability of our findings. However, the following limitations need to be noticed when interpreting our findings. First, measuring serum PFAS concentrations at a single time point during pregnancy may not completely reflect the PFAS levels throughout the entire pregnancy. Although some PFASs have biological half-life spanning years (for example 2.7 years for PFOA, 3.4 years for PFOS [7], 15.3 years for 6:2 Cl-PFESA [61] and 3.5 years for PFNA [62]), concerns exist regarding potential variations in half-lives among other PFAS congeners. Repeated measurements of PFAS levels over time may better represent the prenatal exposure. Second, although potential confounders have been adjusted, some important factors for example dietary habits (especially nutrition intake) that may influence the exposure-outcome association were not included in this study [16]. Third, the relatively small sample size in PTB subtype analysis and sex stratification, particularly in the iPTB subgroup and among female newborns, might have contributed to the relatively wide confidence intervals and limited our statistical power [63]. Finally, since the study participants were from Shanghai and its surrounding areas, with relatively high education and family income, the generalizability of our results to other regions and socioeconomic groups may be inadequate.

In summary, the current study indicated that, both individual and as a mixture, maternal exposures to PFAS significantly increased the risk of PTB, and reduced gestational week and decreased birth weight, with PFOA, PFNA and PFUnDA being the major contributors in the combined effects. Especially, relatively higher risk of PTB associated with elevated PFAS concentrations were observed in the iPTB subtype and among pregnant women carrying male newborns. Our findings indicated that maternal PFAS exposure may have subtype-specific and sex-specific effects on PTB. Further studies are warranted to validate our results and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
