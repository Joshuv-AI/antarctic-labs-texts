FZD5 drives macrophage-mediated immunomodulation and predicts prognosis in glioma: evidence from single-cell sequencing
Authors: Jiongyuan Pei, Chenxiao Huang, Chantian Jiang, Qian Zhang, Lin Cai, Yuhang Guo, Yingyu Chen, Xin Ji, Xianghe Lu, Zhang’an Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGliomas are highly malignant brain tumors characterized by an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which limits therapeutic efficacy and contributes to poor clinical outcomes. The WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway is critically involved in tumor progression, and FZD5, a key receptor within this pathway, may participate in immune regulation. However, its specific role and underlying mechanisms in glioma remain unclear.MethodsRNA-seq and microarray datasets from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), together with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets from GEO, were comprehensively analyzed. The Seurat package was used to identify macrophage-related clusters and mitophagy-associated pathways. Cox and LASSO regression analyses, along with a prognostic nomogram, were applied to evaluate the prognostic significance of FZD5. Immune infiltration, functional enrichment, and immunotherapy response analyses were conducted, followed by validation using spatial transcriptomics, immunohistochemistry, and in vitro assays.ResultsIn bulk glioma transcriptomes, FZD5 emerged as an independent predictor of poor prognosis. Crucially, single-cell and spatial analyses revealed that the biologically significant FZD5 signal originated predominantly within tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), where it colocalized with the M2 marker CD163. Consistently, elevated FZD5 levels correlated with increased myeloid infiltration and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Functionally, macrophage-expressed FZD5 was associated with mitophagy-related programs and promoted an M2-skewed phenotype, thereby enhancing glioma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via macrophage–glioma crosstalk.ConclusionFZD5 is a TAM-enriched marker in glioma tissues and a potential regulator of macrophage-associated immunosuppressive programs, supporting its utility as a prognostic biomarker and a candidate target for microenvironment-oriented interventions in glioma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16332-4.

Introduction

Gliomas are the most common and aggressive primary tumors of the central nervous system [1]. Their highly heterogeneous and complex tumor microenvironment (TME) presents significant challenges for both clinical diagnosis and therapeutic development [2]. Epidemiological studies indicate an annual incidence of approximately 5–6 cases per 100,000 adults, with particularly poor prognosis for patients diagnosed with glioblastoma (GBM), who exhibit low median survival rates [3]. Glioma pathogenesis is influenced by multiple factors, including genetic variants, abnormal epigenetic regulation, and dynamic interactions among diverse cellular components within the TME. These complex mechanisms collectively contribute to persistent challenges in diagnosis and treatment. The standard therapeutic regimen, comprising surgical resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide chemotherapy, has demonstrated limited efficacy in substantially improving long-term survival outcomes [4]. Immunotherapy, as an emerging therapeutic approach, has garnered significant attention from researchers. Although immunotherapy has shown considerable promise in treating other solid tumors, its application in gliomas remains constrained by the highly immunosuppressive TME [5]. Emerging technologies such as molecular typing and imaging genomics provide promising avenues for early diagnosis and precise prognostic assessment. Key molecular markers, including IDH mutations, MGMT promoter methylation, and 1p/19q codeletion, have significantly advanced our understanding of glioma biology [6, 7]. However, the functional significance of these molecular markers within the TME remains limited. Furthermore, despite partial progress in understanding TME composition, the complexity of cell-cell interactions within the TME continues to challenge the translation of fundamental discoveries into practical advances in clinical diagnosis and immunotherapy [8, 9]. In parallel, recent cancer studies have increasingly demonstrated that tumor microenvironment-oriented molecular classification and prognostic signature construction can capture biologically meaningful immune heterogeneity and provide clinically informative risk stratification. For instance, disulfidptosis-related molecular subtypes and low-density lipoprotein-related prognostic signatures in lung adenocarcinoma have been linked to immune-cell infiltration, therapeutic-response features, and patient prognosis [10, 11]. Moreover, cloud-based genomic-analysis platforms and multi-omics resources are improving the efficiency, scalability, and reproducibility of large-scale omics data acquisition and interpretation [12]. These advances support the integration of bulk transcriptomics, single-cell/spatial transcriptomics, and immune-context analyses to identify functionally relevant prognostic biomarkers in glioma. Consequently, elucidating the interaction mechanisms among various TME cell populations during glioma progression, systematically integrating multimodal data, developing innovative combination immunotherapy strategies, and exploring the regulatory networks between metabolism and immunity have emerged as critical research frontiers [13–16]. These efforts provide a rationale for identifying immune-context-dependent biomarkers with both prognostic and functional relevance in glioma.

Macrophages are pivotal in gliomas and display notable plasticity within the tumor microenvironment [17]. Macrophages exert antitumor effects by releasing proinflammatory factors and cytotoxic substances but can also transform into tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) under tumor-derived signaling [18, 19]. TAMs facilitate tumor progression, angiogenesis, and immune evasion through the secretion of growth factors, immunosuppressive cytokines, and proteases [20]. Recent studies have indicated that a high presence of TAMs in gliomas is correlated with poor patient prognosis [21]. Emerging evidence points to targeted modulation of macrophage function as a promising therapeutic strategy [22]. Blocking macrophage polarization to the M2 phenotype can be achieved by inhibiting the IL-6/STAT3 signaling pathway [23]. By activating the cGAS-STING pathway, TAMs can be reprogrammed from the protumor M2 phenotype to the antitumor M1 phenotype [24]. Furthermore, monocytes and macrophages have been used as targeted drug delivery carriers. Drug-loaded macrophages can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and precisely target the tumor center [25, 26]. These innovative methods offer new ways to counteract the immunosuppressive microenvironment of gliomas. Therefore, defining the regulatory networks governing macrophage polarization in glioma is essential for understanding immune suppression and developing macrophage-oriented therapeutic strategies.

The frizzled (FZD) protein, a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), engages with WNT ligands via a cysteine-rich domain (CRD) at its extracellular N-terminus. Its intracellular C-terminus connects with the disheveled PDZ domain (Dvl) and interacts with G proteins [27]. In humans, 10 FZDs that mediate both the β-catenin pathway and various β-catenin-independent pathways depending on the cellular context have been identified. The WNT/β-catenin pathway is crucial for tumor development, whereas the β-catenin-independent WNT/PCP and WNT/Ca2+-PKC pathways also contribute to tumor progression [28–30]. Novel pathways, such as the WNT/Stat3 and WNT/Yap-Taz pathways, were recently identified, suggesting the complexity of FZD signaling [31]. FZD5 is a key receptor in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway that plays a significant role from embryonic development to oncogenesis and profoundly influences the tumor immune microenvironment [32–34]. The FZD5 gene, consisting of two exons, was mapped to the human chromosome 2q33.3-q34 region near the FZD7 gene and the FRA2I fragile site [35]. FZD5 plays a critical role in the progression of various cancers. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) with RNF43 mutations, FZD5 forms a crucial circuit with the Wnt pathway, indicating the vulnerability of tumor cells. Genome-wide CRISPR screening has confirmed the strong dependence of this tumor type on FZD5 [36]. In ovarian cancer, FZD5 upregulates ALDH1A1 in a β-catenin-dependent manner, activating the Akt pathway, promoting DNA damage repair, and conferring chemotherapy resistance [37]. Recent studies have shown that FZD5 can regulate mitochondrial autophagy-related pathways [38]. However, few studies have explored the compartment-specific role of FZD5 in glioma—specifically, whether the FZD5 signal observed in bulk tissues arises primarily from malignant glioma cells or from tumor-infiltrating macrophages, as well as how it shapes the immune microenvironment. To address this gap, we integrated large bulk cohorts with single-cell/spatial transcriptomics and macrophage-centered functional assays to define the cellular source and biological relevance of FZD5 during glioma progression.

Materials and methods

Data sources

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data utilized in this study were obtained from the GEO database, specifically dataset GSE162631. This dataset comprises 8 samples: 4 tumor-core specimens from glioblastoma patients (GSM4955731, GSM4955733, GSM4955735, and GSM4955737) and 4 peritumoral tissues used as non-tumor controls (GSM4955732, GSM4955734, GSM4955736, and GSM4955738), each providing detailed single-cell expression profiles [39]. In addition, bulk RNA sequencing data and related clinical information were sourced from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) databases. To obtain expression profiles for normal controls, we also collected RNA sequencing data from brain tissue of healthy donors via the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project.

Data filtering and standardization

Quality control, normalization, and cluster analysis of the single-cell RNA sequencing data were performed in the R environment (v4.5.1) using the Seurat [40] software package (v5.3.0). The quality control criteria included cells with 300 to 7,500 genes detected per cell (nFeature_RNA), more than 500 UMIs (nCount_RNA), less than 20% mitochondrial gene content, and less than 1% red lineage gene content. The data were normalized to identify the top 2000 highly variable genes for further analysis. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony package (v1.2.3), and downscaling was based on the first 30 principal components. Cell clustering and visualization were conducted using the UMAP method [41]. Cell type annotation was comprehensively performed according to the automatic annotation results in the SingleR package (v2.10.0), CellMarker database, and published cell marker genes [42].

Assessment of mitochondrial autophagy gene set activity and identification of differentially expressed genes

We applied the AUCell algorithm to the scRNA-seq data to evaluate the activity of mitochondrial autophagy-related gene sets in each cell. The gene set was sourced from GOBP_MITOPHAGY.v2025.1.Hs.gmt in the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). We systematically identified differentially expressed genes within each cell subpopulation using the “FindAllMarkers” function, which employs the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Ultimately, we intersected the upregulated differentially expressed genes with the mitochondrial autophagy gene set to identify significantly upregulated genes closely associated with mitochondrial autophagy function.

Analysis of differences in the cell subpopulations and functional enrichment analyses

We employed the clusterProfiler [43] software package (v4.16.0) to conduct Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. These analyses aimed to elucidate potential biological functions and signaling pathways associated with the differentially expressed genes involved in mitochondrial autophagy. An adjusted p value (FDR) of less than 0.05 was set as the significance threshold.

Construction and validation of the prognostic risk score model

To ensure the robustness of the identified biomarkers, we adopted a “Cross-Cohort Intersection” strategy. The CGGA and TCGA cohorts were utilized as independent datasets to identify consensus prognostic genes. Genes linked to overall survival were first identified through univariate Cox regression analysis. To refine the selected genes and prevent overfitting, LASSO regression was applied with 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ) that minimized the partial likelihood deviance. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was subsequently developed to calculate the Hazard Ratios (HR) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). With the rms package (v8.0-0) in R [44], a nomogram for key gene biomarkers was constructed. The predictive accuracy and robustness for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, the Concordance Index (C-index), and calibration plots comparing predicted versus observed survival probabilities.

Somatic mutation analysis and tumor mutation burden assessment

To explore the link between transcriptome-based prognostic risk models and genomic instability, we examined the tumor mutation burden and mutation gene profiles in both the high- and low-risk groups. We sourced somatic mutation data in ‘maf’ format and associated clinical information for glioma from the TCGA cohort. Because these variants were called from bulk tumor DNA, they predominantly reflect malignant-cell genomic alterations and were not used to infer somatic mutations in infiltrating immune cells (including macrophages). For each patient, we calculated the tumor mutation burden (TMB), defined as the number of nonsynonymous mutations per megabase of the sequenced region. We employed the Mann-Whitney U test to assess differences in the TMB between the high- and low-risk groups. Furthermore, we identified genes with mutation frequencies greater than 2% across the entire cohort and applied Fisher’s exact test to analyze differences in mutation frequency distribution between the high-risk and low-risk groups, thereby pinpointing genome-specific variants linked to poor prognosis.

Gene set enrichment analysis

We conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to thoroughly investigate the biological processes linked to genes associated with glioma prognosis and their phenotypes. Using the hallmark gene set in MSigDB as the reference, we assessed the significance of the enrichment results on the basis of a standardized enrichment score (|NES| > 1, FDR < 0.25).

Assessment of tumor-infiltrating immune cells

Algorithms such as CIBERSORT, ESTIMATE, EPIC, and MCP-counter were employed to quantitatively assess immune cell infiltration in tumor samples. CIBERSORT deconvolution analysis specifically estimated the relative proportions of 22 immune cell subtypes, examining their correlation with prognostic risk scores and key gene expression. Furthermore, Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) scores were calculated to evaluate potential responses to immunotherapy.

Macrophage subpopulation analysis

To investigate macrophage heterogeneity, macrophage populations were extracted from single-cell data for reclustering analysis. The 2000 most variable genes within this subpopulation were identified and analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA) with the Harmony method following batch effect correction. Dimensionality reduction and cell clustering were conducted using UMAP on the basis of the first 30 principal components. Macrophage subpopulations were annotated using established cell marker genes from the literature and the CellMarker database, which was used to determine the proportion of each subpopulation in the dataset.

Cellular communication

We analyzed intercellular communication between GBM cell subpopulations and microenvironmental cells using the CellChat [45] package in R (v1.6.1) and the CellChatDB.human ligand–receptor interaction database. Our evaluation of signaling pathways and receptor–ligand interactions revealed the synergistic signaling mechanisms among various cell types.

Analysis of spatial transcriptome data

Spatial transcriptome data were obtained from GEO dataset GSE194329, with a focus on sample GSM5833536. Mitochondrial autophagy pathway activity scores for each spatial location were calculated using the Poisson model in the GSVA [46] package in R (v2.2.0). To explore spatial relationships, colocalization analysis was performed to assess the expression of the gene FZD5, the distribution of macrophage markers, and mitochondrial autophagic activity within the tissue in situ.

Trajectory analysis

We analyzed cell differentiation trajectories in macrophage subpopulations using the Monocle2 [47] tool in R (v2.26.0). To reconstruct these trajectories, we employed the DDRTree algorithm. Additionally, we used minimum spanning trees (MSTs) to infer cell lineages and monitor developmental progress along the identified pathways.

Clinical samples

The glioma tissue samples used in this study were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. Histologically tumor-free peritumoral brain zone (PBZ) tissues were collected from the corresponding glioma patients when available and used as control tissues for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Western blot analyses. The histopathological diagnosis and WHO grade of each glioma specimen were confirmed by two experienced neuropathologists, and the corresponding PBZ tissues showed no histopathological evidence of tumor. None of the patients had received chemotherapy or radiotherapy before surgery. All tissue samples were collected with written informed consent. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. The clinical characteristics of the patients are detailed in Supplemental Table S1.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

In this study, immunohistochemical analysis was performed on paraffin-embedded sections from glioma tissues and corresponding PBZ tissues described above. Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then paraffin-embedded and serially sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were incubated with rabbit anti-FZD5 (1:250; Proteintech, USA; #21,519-1-AP) primary antibodies. After dehydration, the tissue sections were blocked with peroxidase. Primary antibodies were added for incubation overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking, followed by incubation for 1 h with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature. A Dako Cytomation EnVision + System-HRP (DAB) assay kit (Carpinteria, USA, #K406511) was used for processing. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and examined under a microscope. Two senior neuropathologists, who were blinded to the clinical parameters, evaluated the stained sections semiquantitatively. FZD5 expression levels were assessed in ten high-magnification fields (400×) according to staining intensity and the percentage of positively stained cells. The scoring criteria were previously reported [48].

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates from cultured cells, glioma tissues, and corresponding PBZ tissues were extracted using cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, China; #P0013J). The protein concentrations in the lysates were measured with an Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China; #P001). Protein samples, ranging from 30 to 50 µg of protein was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, USA; #IPVH00010). The membranes were then immunoblotted with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:2000; Proteintech, USA; #10,494-1-AP), rabbit anti-FZD5 (1:1000; Proteintech, USA; #21,519-1-AP), and rabbit anti-CD163 (1:1000; Proteintech, USA; #16,646-1-AP). Afterward, the membranes were treated with an enzyme-labeled secondary antibody (1:10,000; Proteintech, USA; #SA00001-2). Immunoreactive proteins were detected using an ECL assay kit (Advansta, USA; #K12045) and visualized with a Bio-Rad system (Bio-Rad, USA). The Western blot analysis was repeated three times to ensure the reproducibility of the results. Densitometric quantification of Western blot bands was performed using ImageJ software, and relative protein levels were normalized to GAPDH.

Cell culture and reagents

The human GBM cell lines U87 and U251, along with the human monocytic cell line THP-1 used in this study, were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, United States, #11875119) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, United States, #10099-141), while U87 and U251 cells were maintained in DMEM medium (Gibco, United States, #8121300) containing 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

Coculture model

THP-1 cells were treated with PMA (50 ng/mL) for 24 h to induce differentiation into M0-like macrophages. M0-like differentiation was assessed morphologically by microscopic observation of the transition from suspended round THP-1 cells to adherent macrophage-like cells, as commonly described in established PMA-induced THP-1 macrophage differentiation protocols. The resulting M0-like macrophages were then transduced with lentiviruses carrying either an FZD5 overexpression construct (FZD5-OE) or the corresponding control vector. For loss-of-function validation, a separate set of macrophages was transduced with lentiviral shRNA targeting FZD5 (shFZD5) or a matched control (shCtrl). After 48 h of transduction, culture supernatants were collected and applied to U87 and U251 cells for coculture. After 2 days of coculture, glioma cells were harvested for downstream functional assays, including CCK-8 proliferation assays, EdU incorporation assays, colony formation assays, scratch (wound-healing) assays, and Transwell invasion assays. In addition, macrophages were collected at days 3 and 7 post-transduction for immunoblotting to validate FZD5 modulation and associated polarization markers.

CCK-8, EdU, colony formation, scratch, and Transwell invasion assays

U87 and U251 cells were harvested after being cocultured with either M0-OE-Vector or M0-OE-FZD5 for 2 days. The cocultured cells were subsequently utilized in the following experiments.

For the CCK-8 cell proliferation assays, cells were seeded at 1,000 cells per well in 96-well plates containing 100 µL of complete medium and incubated at 37 °C. Following incubation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan; #CK04) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance (OD450 nm) was subsequently measured using a microplate reader. For the colony formation assays, cells were seeded at 1,000 cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 2 weeks. The cells were then fixed with formaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and examined under a microscope. For the EdU experiments, EdU kits were procured from Beyotime (C0075S, China). The EdU assay was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were captured using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, USA).

To investigate cell migration and invasion, scratch assays were performed by using a 1 mL pipette tip to create scratches on cells in a 6-well plate that had reached 80–90% confluence. Cell migration was monitored under an inverted phase contrast microscope (Olympus Corporation) at 48 h after scratching. In the Transwell invasion assay, a Matrigel solution was prepared by diluting Matrigel with serum-free cell culture medium at a ratio of 1:8. Prior to cell seeding, 24-well Transwell chambers (Corning Costar, USA, #3422) were coated with the Matrigel solution and incubated for 12 h at 37 °C.

Data analysis

We performed all computational analyses using R software version 4.5.1 and GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. For two-group comparisons, Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon test was used as appropriate. For paired comparisons between tumor tissues and corresponding PBZ tissues, a paired Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used according to data distribution. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc analysis was applied. For Western blot quantification involving treatment group and time point, two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple-comparisons test was used. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell transcriptome-based cellular mapping of the glioma microenvironment

Samples were filtered according to nFeature_RNA and nCount_RNA parameters (300 < nFeature_RNA < 7,500; nCount_RNA > 500; percent.mt < 20; percent.hb < 1) (Figure S1A). The ten genes with the highest standard deviations are displayed in Figure S1B. The data underwent a series of processing steps, including homogenization, standardization, PCA, and, subsequently, harmony analysis in the specified sequence (Figure S1C-D). Finally, 14 subgroups were identified by UMAP analysis (Fig. 1A and B, upper left). Cell types were identified on the basis of the CellMarker 2.0 database and established cell marker genes, as follows: macrophages (C1QB, C1QA, and C1QC), microglia (SPRY1 and P2RY12), monocytes (VCAN and EREG), neutrophils (FCGR3B, IL1R2, and S100A12), glia/neuroglia (CLU, MT3, and GPM6B), T cells (GZMA, NKG7, and CCL5), plasma cells (IGHG1, IGHG3, and JCHAIN), and fibroblasts (IGFBP7, SPARC, and COL4A1) (Fig. 1B, upper right). We further distinguished the cells as either normal or tumor cells (Fig. 1B, lower left). Cell cycle phase analysis revealed the distribution of S-phase, G1-phase, and G2M-phase cells (Fig. 1B, lower right). The expression levels of marker genes across these cell clusters are shown in Fig. 1C. The histogram in Fig. 1D shows the proportional distribution of cell types between the normal and tumor tissues in the eight samples to emphasize interpatient variability. This analysis provides a crucial foundation for understanding the complexity of the GBM tumor microenvironment.

Tumor-associated macrophages exhibit characteristic increased mitochondrial autophagic activity

To explore the role of mitochondrial autophagy in GBM development, we assessed mitochondrial autophagic activity across various cell types using the AUCell algorithm. Our findings revealed diverse mitochondrial autophagic activity among the cell types. In comparison with that in normal tissues, mitochondrial autophagy was significantly activated in macrophages, microglia, monocytes, and plasma cells but suppressed in neutrophils and glial/neuroglial cells (Fig. 1E). These results indicate that mitochondrial autophagy plays cell type-specific regulatory roles within the tumor microenvironment.

We focused on macrophages that demonstrate significant mitochondrial autophagy pathway activation and discovered 3,463 genes whose expression was differentially upregulated between normal and tumor-associated macrophages (Fig. 1F). By intersecting these genes with known mitochondrial autophagy genes, we identified 18 genes associated with mitochondrial autophagy that were significantly upregulated (Fig. 1G). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that these 18 genes were notably enriched in the KEGG mitochondrial autophagy pathway (Fig. 1H). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that these genes were primarily located in the mitochondrial outer membrane, played direct roles in mitochondrial autophagy and disassembly, and were regulated through mechanisms such as ubiquitination ligase binding (Fig. 1I).

Screening of core prognostic genes by multiple bioinformatics methods

We conducted a systematic multistage screening of two independent large glioma cohorts from the CGGA and TCGA to identify core regulators with clinical prognostic significance among the 18 genes. First, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis (P < 0.05, HR > 1) revealed that eight and four risk genes were significantly linked to poor prognosis in the CGGA and TCGA cohorts, respectively (Fig. 2A-B). To develop a more robust prognostic model, we employed LASSO regression analysis, which refined the selection to 9 and 7 key genes in the CGGA and TCGA cohorts, respectively (Fig. 2C-D). Ultimately, an intersection analysis of these gene sets (CGGA-Cox: 8, CGGA-LASSO: 9, TCGA-Cox: 4, TCGA-LASSO: 7) revealed three core genes with significant concordance: FZD5, TSPO, and SPATA33 (Fig. 2E).

FZD5 serves as an independent prognostic factor for glioma and is validated at multiple levels

To systematically assess the independent prognostic significance of FZD5, TSPO, and SPATA33, we incorporated these variables into a multifactorial Cox regression analysis alongside key clinical factors such as age, IDH1 mutation, and 1p19q codeletion. The analysis revealed FZD5 as a significant independent prognostic factor for glioma, with a hazard ratio (HR) greater than 1 and a P value less than 0.05 (Fig. 3A). Consequently, we developed a prognostic nomogram that integrates FZD5 expression levels with the aforementioned clinical parameters (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the calibration curve demonstrated strong concordance between the predicted and observed survival probabilities (Fig. 3C). The model also exhibited good performance for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year patient survival, as indicated by the area under the time-dependent ROC curve (AUC) of 0.727, 0.790, and 0.812, respectively (Fig. 3D), collectively supporting the robustness and prognostic value of the FZD5-based model. Recognizing the prognostic significance of FZD5, we conducted an in-depth characterization. Survival analysis revealed that patients with elevated FZD5 expression in both the CGGA cohort and the TCGA cohort had considerably shorter overall survival (log-rank P < 0.05; Figs. 3E-F). Additionally, FZD5 expression was positively associated with glioma malignancy: in the TCGA-GTEx dataset, FZD5 expression increased from normal brain tissue to low-grade glioma (LGG) and then to glioblastoma (GBM) (Fig. 3G), whereas in the CGGA cohort, FZD5 expression increased with WHO grade (Fig. 3H). To address the grade dependence, we performed within-stratum survival analyses in CGGA and added Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by WHO grade (LGG and GBM) and by key molecular features (IDH1, MGMT, and 1p/19q; Figure S4), supporting that the prognostic association of FZD5 is not solely driven by grade mixture. This expression pattern was validated at the protein level via immunohistochemical and Western blot analyses in our cohort (Fig. 3I-J). In the CGGA database, FZD5 was linked to prognostic markers in glioma patients (Figure S2A-D), with high FZD5 expression correlated with poor prognosis.

Mutation analysis

To characterize the molecular context associated with FZD5 expression, we analyzed tumor mutational burden (TMB) and driver-gene alteration patterns between FZD5-high and FZD5-low groups. The FZD5-high group exhibited significantly higher TMB (Fig. 3K), consistent with a more genomically unstable malignant-cell compartment. Furthermore, distinct driver alteration patterns emerged: TP53 alterations were more frequent in the FZD5-high group, while IDH1 mutations were enriched in the FZD5-low group (Fig. 3L-M). We present these mutation profiles to contextualize the FZD5-defined strata within established glioma molecular backgrounds and account for potential confounding by major tumor-intrinsic genotypes. Importantly, these profiles are derived from bulk tumor DNA and thus predominantly reflect malignant-cell alterations, not somatic mutations in infiltrating immune cells. Collectively, elevated FZD5 expression is associated with a molecular background characterized by higher TMB and increased TP53 alteration frequency, which may contribute to the adverse clinical outcomes observed in the FZD5-high group.

The FZD5 coexpression network is enriched in immune regulation and tumor microenvironment-related pathways

To explore the biological functions of FZD5, we performed a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis of its coexpressed genes. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that genes associated with FZD5 are significantly enriched in immunomodulatory functions. In terms of biological processes, these genes are involved mainly in “chemotaxis”, “taxis”, and “extracellular matrix organization”. Considering cellular components, the genes were found in the “collagen-containing extracellular matrix” and “external side of plasma membrane”. For molecular functions, the genes were enriched in “endopeptidase activity”, “extracellular matrix structural constituent”, and “immunoreceptor activity” (Fig. 4A). Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of FZD5-related genes in pathways such as “cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions” and the “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway”, which are closely associated with tumor immunoregulation (Fig. 4B). To further support these findings, we conducted gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). The results indicated that samples with high FZD5 expression were significantly enriched in pathways characteristic of tumor immune microenvironment remodeling, including the “epithelial-mesenchymal transition”, the “TNFA signaling via NFKB”, the “inflammatory response”, the “IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway”, and the “hypoxia” (Fig. 4C). Overall, these analyses demonstrate that FZD5 is involved not only in classical tumor progression pathways, such as extracellular matrix remodeling, but also in shaping the tumor microenvironment through associations with multiple immune-related pathways.

High FZD5 expression shapes the landscape of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment

We next examined immunotherapy-relevant immune checkpoint molecules. Compared with the FZD5-low group, the FZD5-high group exhibited significantly higher expression of CD274 (PD-L1), PDCD1LG2 (PD-L2), PDCD1 (PD-1), and LAG3 (Fig. 5E), indicating a checkpoint-enriched state commonly associated with T-cell dysfunction/exhaustion in immunosuppressive tumors. Concurrently, increased infiltration of M0 and M2 macrophages in the FZD5-high group further supports the presence of a myeloid-enriched, immunosuppressive microenvironment potentially linked to macrophage polarization. This is reinforced by a significant positive correlation between FZD5 expression and the M2 marker CD163 in both cohorts (CGGA: R = 0.41, P < 0.01; TCGA: R = 0.55, P < 0.01; Fig. 5F-G).

To further assess immunotherapy-relevant signatures, we applied the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) framework. The FZD5-low group showed significantly lower TIDE score, T cell Exclusion score, and indices of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) (Fig. 5H), suggesting a higher predicted likelihood of response to immune checkpoint blockade due to reduced immune dysfunction/exclusion (though this inference remains hypothesis-generating and requires prospective clinical validation in glioma).

Importantly, integrating these findings with EPIC-inferred increases in CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell abundance (Fig. 5D) suggests that FZD5-high tumors exhibit a paradoxical immune context characterized by the presence of T cells alongside functional restraint and/or exclusion, rather than effective cytotoxic immunity. Collectively, these data demonstrate that FZD5-high gliomas are characterized not only by myeloid enrichment but also by a broader immunosuppressive ecosystem marked by checkpoint upregulation and signatures of T cell dysfunction/exclusion.

FZD5+ macrophages are associated with immunoregulatory signaling networks in the glioma microenvironment

To clarify the cellular source of the bulk FZD5 signal, we quantified FZD5 expression across major scRNA-seq compartments. FZD5 was predominantly enriched in the myeloid lineage, whereas malignant and stromal compartments showed comparatively low expression (Figure S5). These data support interpreting bulk FZD5 as a myeloid-enriched microenvironmental marker. To clarify the role of FZD5 in cell communication at the single-cell level, we examined the differences in intercellular interactions between FZD5+ and FZD5− macrophages. Our analysis revealed notable variations in the interaction networks of these two macrophage populations (Fig. 6A-B). Specifically, FZD5+ macrophages demonstrated a more active signaling network linked to the M2 phenotype. Ligand–receptor interaction analysis revealed that FZD5+ macrophages exhibited enriched predicted communication through immunoregulatory pathways, including the MIF (CD74-CD44) signaling axis, the IL6 (IL6R-IL6ST) pathway, and the IL16-CD4 interaction (Fig. 6C-D). These signaling programs are widely implicated in macrophage immunoregulatory states; notably, the IL16–CD4 axis provides a direct communication route between FZD5 + macrophages and T cells, supporting a TAM-to-T cell crosstalk framework in the FZD5 + macrophage–dominant microenvironment. Together, these results suggest that FZD5 + macrophages constitute a signaling hub with enhanced immunoregulatory output (e.g., MIF/IL6- and IL16–CD4–associated communication), which is consistent with M2-skewed macrophage states and a downstream T cell–restrained microenvironment.

Spatial colocalization analysis of FZD5+ and M2 macrophages on the basis of single-cell and spatial transcriptome data

To verify the link between FZD5 expression and macrophage phenotypes at the single-cell level, we performed an in-depth subpopulation analysis of macrophages. Following stringent quality control, batch effect correction, and nonlinear dimensionality reduction clustering, we identified two major macrophage phenotypic subpopulations with distinct transcriptomic profiles (Fig. 7A). Using markers from the CellMarker 2.0 database, we identified the classic proinflammatory M1 subtype and the immunosuppressive M2 subtype. A gene expression bubble plot revealed that in the M2 subpopulation, FZD5 was significantly coenriched with the M2 macrophage marker CD163 (Fig. 7B). We further employed spatial transcriptomics to explore the spatial relationship between FZD5 and M2 macrophages within the tissue. Analysis revealed that regions with high FZD5 expression also presented increased CD163 expression and enhanced mitophagy pathway activity (Fig. 7C). The concordance between single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses supported a close spatial association between FZD5 expression and CD163-positive macrophage infiltration. These findings suggest that FZD5 expression is associated with M2-like macrophage features and enhanced mitophagy-related activity, potentially contributing to an immunosuppressive local microenvironment.

Pseudotemporal analysis reveals the dynamic expression pattern of FZD5 during M2 macrophage polarization

To elucidate the dynamic differentiation of macrophages during glioma progression, we conducted a time series analysis (Fig. 7D, top left and top right). The results revealed that macrophages exhibited obvious phenotypic evolution along the developmental trajectory. Initially, macrophages from normal tissues were predominant, whereas the end point was composed mainly of macrophages from tumor tissues (Fig. 7D, bottom left), supporting the biological plausibility of our trajectory model. Further analysis revealed that M1 macrophages appeared mainly in the early stage of development, whereas M2 macrophages were significantly enriched in the later stage (Fig. 7D, bottom right), suggesting that the trajectory is consistent with a macrophage phenotypic shift from M1-like to M2-like states. Notably, FZD5 expression significantly changed along this differentiation trajectory: its expression remained stable in macrophages from normal tissues but was significantly increased in macrophages from tumor tissues (Fig. 7E). Importantly, FZD5 expression did not significantly change in M1 macrophages but clearly tended to increase during the polarization of M2 macrophages (Fig. 7F). These findings indicate that high expression of FZD5 is closely associated with the polarization of macrophages toward the M2 phenotype, providing new evidence for exploring the mechanistic role of FZD5 in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment.

Functional experiments support a role for FZD5 in promoting M2-skewed macrophage polarization and glioma malignant phenotypes

To investigate the role of FZD5 in macrophage polarization and glioma progression, we performed functional assays using THP-1-derived macrophages. M0-like macrophages were generated by PMA induction and transduced with lentivirus to establish FZD5-overexpression (FZD5-OE) or vector controls. Immunoblotting confirmed sustained FZD5 protein upregulation at days 3 and 7 post-transduction. Concomitantly, levels of CD163, a canonical M2 macrophage marker, increased (Fig. 8A), suggesting that elevated FZD5 promotes an M2-skewed phenotype.

To establish directionality, we generated an FZD5 knockdown model (shFZD5), confirming efficient protein reduction at days 3 and 7. CD163 protein levels decreased concordantly with FZD5 depletion (Figure S6), supporting a requirement for FZD5 in maintaining the M2-like polarization program. Compared to controls, glioma cells treated with FZD5-OE macrophage-conditioned media exhibited significantly enhanced proliferation and invasive capacity. Discussion

Gliomas pose a significant challenge in neuro-oncology because of their aggressive nature, tendency to recur, and poor prognosis [49]. Although traditional treatments have improved, patient survival rates remain unsatisfactory, underscoring the need for innovative strategies. Recently, immunotherapy has offered new hope, prompting research into the intricate interactions between tumors and the immune microenvironment [50, 51]. However, the regulatory mechanisms between glioma cells and the immune microenvironment remain unclear, particularly concerning the role of immune cells in malignant progression. Evidence increasingly indicates that immune cells within the tumor microenvironment are crucial to glioma progression [52, 53], yet their specific immunoregulatory mechanisms need systematic clarification. A thorough investigation of the cellular composition and functional status of the glioma immune microenvironment is anticipated to reveal the intrinsic mechanisms of immune escape and lay the groundwork for the development of novel immune combination therapies.

In the glioma microenvironment, tumor-associated macrophages are the predominant immune cells, with metabolic reprogramming and functional polarization abilities being pivotal to tumor progression [54]. Studies have shown that macrophages can polarize into distinct subtypes, such as proinflammatory or regulatory subtypes, on the basis of microenvironmental signals. In glioblastoma, these macrophages generally exhibit M2-like phenotypic traits, promoting tumor growth by fostering an immunosuppressive environment, despite not strictly following the classical M1/M2 classification [55]. Notably, the complex signaling network between macrophages and glioma cells not only establishes this immunosuppressive microenvironment but also significantly enhances tumor angiogenesis, tumor invasion, and treatment resistance [56].

Numerous studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial quality control is essential for cellular homeostasis [57, 58]. Within the tumor microenvironment, heightened mitochondrial autophagy in M2 macrophages may satisfy their metabolic demands, thereby increasing their anti-inflammatory and protumorigenic effects [59]. These findings connect metabolic regulation with the immune phenotype, suggesting that mitochondrial autophagy might be a crucial link between macrophage metabolic reprogramming and immune regulatory functions. These insights provide a fresh perspective for understanding the regulatory network of the immune microenvironment in glioma.

In recent years, single-cell sequencing technology has become a powerful tool for exploring tumor heterogeneity [60, 61]. In this study, we used scRNA-seq to thoroughly characterize cellular heterogeneity within the GBM microenvironment. Our analysis revealed 14 distinct cell clusters, which we categorized into 8 major cell types: macrophages, microglia, monocytes, neutrophils, glia/neuroglia, T cells, plasma cells, and fibroblasts. We observed significant differences in the distribution of these cell types between normal and tumor tissues, as well as variations in their cell cycle characterization. Marker gene analysis provided insights into the distinct functional roles and heterogeneous distribution of these cell types in GBM tumors. Notably, macrophages exhibited a unique metabolic profile with significantly enhanced mitochondrial autophagic activity, suggesting that metabolic reprogramming might influence their functional regulation. Through multistage bioinformatics analysis, we identified 18 genes closely related to mitochondrial autophagy from 3463 differentially expressed genes.

Our survival analysis revealed that FZD5 overexpression is linked to poor prognosis across all glioma grades. Both univariate Cox regression combined with LASSO regression and multivariate Cox regression analyses confirmed that FZD5 is a significant independent prognostic factor for glioma patients. This analytical framework is consistent with recent TME-centered prognostic studies showing that functionally defined molecular signatures can stratify patient prognosis while reflecting immune microenvironmental states [10, 11]. Importantly, our study extends this concept to glioma by not only identifying FZD5 as an independent prognostic biomarker but also tracing the bulk FZD5 signal to tumor-associated macrophages at the single-cell and spatial levels. Furthermore, our macrophage-centered functional assays provide biological evidence that FZD5 contributes to M2-skewed macrophage polarization and glioma-promoting paracrine effects, thereby linking prognostic modeling with mechanistic immune regulation. We utilized nomograms, which often surpass traditional staging systems in prognostic predictions for various cancers [62], in our study. Our survival nomogram, which includes prognostic factors such as FZD5, demonstrated strong performance. Its predictive accuracy was validated using ROC curves, with AUCs ranging from 0.7 to 0.9 for both the discovery and validation cohorts, and through calibration curves. Our FZD5-based survival model showed superior predictive accuracy and discriminatory ability. FZD5 not only acts as an independent prognostic indicator but is also positively correlated with tumor malignancy. Notably, tumors with elevated FZD5 expression displayed unique genomic features, including a high mutational load and specific TP53 mutation patterns. Tumor-intrinsic drivers such as TP53 and IDH1 may contribute to differences in cytokine/chemokine and metabolic signaling programs that shape myeloid recruitment and polarization; accordingly, key molecular variables (including IDH1 status) were incorporated into our multivariable models, in which FZD5 remained independently prognostic. Our findings underscore the potential of FZD5 as a predictive biomarker in clinical settings, revealing a significant correlation between FZD5 expression and glioma grade in clinical samples. The upregulation of FZD5 in glioma tissues is linked to poor prognosis, supporting its clinical utility as a TAM-associated biomarker and highlighting a microenvironment-driven, pro-tumorigenic immunomodulatory role in glioma. Because bulk transcriptomic profiles capture both cellular composition and activation states, elevated bulk FZD5 most likely reflects enrichment and/or activation of infiltrating myeloid (monocyte/macrophage-lineage) cells within gliomas, rather than a uniform tumor-cell–intrinsic driver program. To further clarify the biological function of FZD5, we conducted GO, KEGG, and GSEA analyses. These analyses revealed that FZD5 significantly influences the tumor microenvironment by regulating various immune-related pathways and engaging in classical tumor progression processes such as extracellular matrix remodeling.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a crucial regulator in various human cancers and is typically activated by the binding of Wnt ligands to frizzled receptor proteins such as FZD5 [32]. Previous research has indicated that upregulation of FZD5 expression enhances the nuclear translocation of β-catenin in macrophages, shifting their phenotype from M1 to M2 and thus promoting skeletal muscle regeneration [63]. However, the role of FZD5 in the glioma tumor microenvironment, particularly its effect on macrophage polarization, is poorly understood. Therefore, we examined immune infiltration patterns in groups of glioma tissues with high and low FZD5 expression. Using multiple immune infiltration algorithms in conjunction with TIDE, we assessed their responses to immunotherapy, revealing distinct immune cell compositions and interactions within the glioma microenvironment. This analysis revealed a significant correlation between immune cell subtypes and macrophage subpopulation markers, suggesting a complex immunoregulatory role for FZD5+ macrophages in glioblastoma pathophysiology. These findings suggest that FZD5 may represent a TAM-associated candidate for future microenvironment-oriented therapeutic exploration, while lower FZD5 expression was associated with a more favorable predicted immunotherapy-response profile. Notably, infiltration of M0 and M2 macrophages was significantly higher in the FZD5-high group, and FZD5 expression was strongly correlated with the M2 macrophage marker CD163, suggesting that FZD5 may contribute to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by regulating M2 macrophage polarization. This observation is consistent with previous studies. To explore potential therapeutic strategies for glioma, we further identified twelve small-molecule compounds potentially effective for patients with high FZD5 expression (Figure S3).

In single-cell assays, FZD5+ macrophages exhibited a more active communication network. A detailed ligand-receptor interaction analysis indicated that FZD5 might play a crucial role in guiding macrophages toward the M2 phenotype by activating key signaling pathways such as MIF and IL6. A combined analysis of spatial transcriptomics and single-cell data revealed that FZD5 and M2 macrophage marker expression colocalized, along with markers of the mitochondrial autophagy pathway. These findings suggest that FZD5 may facilitate macrophage polarization to the M2 type by regulating mitochondrial autophagy, thereby promoting the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Furthermore, time series analysis revealed that FZD5 expression increased in tandem with tumor progression and M2 macrophage polarization. These findings suggest that FZD5 is not only involved in these processes but may also serve as a key regulatory hub linking them. Multiple studies have shown the crucial involvement of M2 macrophages in tumor evasion of the immune response [64, 65]. A potential novel approach for immunotherapy in the future could involve targeting FZD5 to hinder immune evasion orchestrated by M2 macrophages [66]. Functional experiments further showed that FZD5 overexpression increased the expression of the M2 macrophage marker CD163. Importantly, coculture experiments demonstrated that M2-like macrophages induced by FZD5 significantly enhanced the proliferation, migration, and invasion of glioma cells. Collectively, these findings highlight a potentially important role of FZD5 in orchestrating tumor-associated macrophage polarization and driving the malignant progression of gliomas.

Our study has several limitations. First, the lack of in vivo validation limits our understanding of the systemic effects of FZD5, especially its interaction with the blood–brain barrier and nonmyeloid cells. Second, although we propose that mitochondrial autophagy could be a crucial link between macrophage metabolic reprogramming and immunoregulatory functions, as well as a potential driver of M2 macrophage polarization, direct evidence, such as transmission electron microscopy analyses of mitochondria, is needed to establish causality. Third, while our investigation focused on key genes associated with M2 macrophages in the context of GBM biomarkers, other cell clusters may also be significant. Therefore, our future research will explore the specific roles of these cell clusters and their associated genes to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying GBM.

In conclusion, this study systematically revealed that increased FZD5 expression is linked to poor prognosis in glioma patients. FZD5 is associated with macrophage polarization and mitophagy-related programs, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment. These findings provide a new perspective on the immune escape mechanism of glioma and suggest potential strategies for future macrophage-oriented immunotherapy and personalized medicine.
