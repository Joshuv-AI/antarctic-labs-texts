Letter by İsa Ardahanlı Regarding Article, Association Between Nut Consumption and Metabolic Syndrome in Korean Adults
Authors: Murat Özmen, Faik Özel, Ramazan Aslan, İsa Ardahanlı
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest Shin’s article, “Association Between Nut Consumption and Metabolic Syndrome in Korean Adults”1 and appreciate the opportunity to provide comments. This well-designed study contributes valuable evidence on dietary patterns and metabolic health in Asian populations. Its strengths include the large, nationally representative sample and careful adjustment for confounding variables. The finding that moderate nut consumption (9–19 g per day) reduces the risk of metabolic syndrome in specific demographic groups is noteworthy, highlighting how simple dietary changes can improve health outcomes. The stratification of analyses by age and sex further illustrates how subgroups may respond differently to nut consumption.

While the original authors discussed the importance of age- and sex-stratified analyses, further exploration of potential mechanisms may yield additional insights. Hormonal influences may help explain sex-specific differences. In women, estrogen has been shown to enhance endothelial function, improve lipid profiles, and reduce inflammation, potentially augmenting the cardiometabolic benefits of nut-derived unsaturated fatty acids.2,3 In men, testosterone levels affect visceral adiposity and insulin sensitivity, possibly modifying the metabolic response to nut intake.4 Additionally, bioactive compounds in nuts—such as L-arginine, omega-3 fatty acids (especially from walnuts), and polyphenols—may interact differently with sex hormones to influence vascular function, inflammation, and oxidative stress.5,6

As the authors noted, repeated dietary assessments and nut-type stratification are resource-intensive in large-scale surveys such as Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES).7 A practical approach could be the integration of short-form, validated food frequency questionnaires specifically targeting nut intake in future KNHANES waves, which would capture habitual consumption with minimal added burden. In addition, collecting data on preparation methods (raw, roasted, salted) could increase the relevance of dietary recommendations, since previous studies suggest that salted nuts may exert distinct metabolic effects compared with unsalted varieties.8 Evidence also indicates that nut metabolism and associated health benefits may differ by sex, potentially reflecting hormonal regulation and variations in lipid metabolism.9,10

The relatively low levels of nut consumption in this population present both a challenge and an opportunity. Demonstrating benefits at modest consumption levels is encouraging from a public health standpoint. Feasible strategies in Korea could include incorporating small, affordable nut snack packs into workplace cafeterias, school lunch programs, and community health campaigns, coupled with public messaging on portion size and preparation methods. Such culturally tailored approaches may enhance accessibility and encourage broader adoption of dietary recommendations.

We commend the authors for their rigorous methodology and thorough analysis. Their study provides a solid foundation for future research, particularly intervention studies that can establish causal relationships. Incorporating broader dietary context (e.g., overall dietary quality) and potential biomarkers into future research would further strengthen the findings. This study makes an important contribution to our understanding of how simple dietary changes, such as nut consumption, can influence metabolic health. These findings are especially relevant given the increasing prevalence of metabolic syndrome in Asian populations.
