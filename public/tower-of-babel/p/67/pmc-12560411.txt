Understanding gestational diabetes mellitus: a qualitative study of awareness and prevention strategies among pregnant women at risk in Jimma town, Ethiopia
Authors: Samira Awel, Bekelu Teka, Remedan Jemal, Demuma Amdisa, Misra Abdulahi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRates of gestational diabetes (GDM) are increasing alarmingly, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Although reducing risk is possible through different prevention measures, there is a lack of exploratory evidence in the Ethiopian context.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore pregnant women’s awareness and prevention strategies on gestational diabetes mellitus.MethodA descriptive qualitative study was conducted in the Jimma, Oromia region, Southwest Ethiopia, from March 2 to April 10, 2024. A purposive sampling approach was used, and in-depth interviews were conducted by trained data collectors. The interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim by the research team on a daily basis.ResultThematic analysis was performed for the transcripts, and three broad themes emerged: understanding GDM, lifestyle changes (a healthy diet and exercise) and the effects of GDM on both mothers and babies. The findings indicate a range of gaps in women’s understanding of how lifestyle changes (diet and exercise) reduce the risk of GDM. Gaps included misconceptions regarding GDM, an incomplete understanding of lifestyle risk factors, limited awareness of the effect GDM has on mothers and babies, and inconsistent views of recommended physical activity during pregnancy.ConclusionThe findings identified the lack of awareness on GDM and its prevention strategies. Misconception on the adverse GDM outcomes and insufficient comprehension of lifestyle-related risk factors. The findings of this study may suggest that women need health information on GDM prevention diets and exercise during antenatal care.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) involves high plasma glucose levels first identified during pregnancy [1]. GDM is the most common global medical problem of pregnancy, affecting approximately 15–25% of pregnant women [2]. It is also increasing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Pooled data indicate that approximately 9% of pregnancies are affected by GDM in Sab-Saharan African countries [3]. In Ethiopia, 111 of the 420 (26.2%) pregnant women were affected by GDM [4].

GDM poses substantial risks to birth outcomes, maternal health, healthcare systems, and national economies. Common complications associated with GDM include cesarean delivery, fetal macrosomia, and increased healthcare costs. Beyond physical health implications, women with GDM often experience psychological distress, heightened self-care demands, stigma within their communities, and financial barriers to care [3, 5, 6]. The burden of GDM extends beyond the individual, impacting future maternal health, the quality and cost of health service delivery, and broader economic stability [7].

Owing to the lack of resources and trained personnel and other priorities related to maternal health, screening, diagnosing, and providing care to women with GDM and those who are at risk are not priorities in many LMICs, including Ethiopia [8, 9]. In Ethiopia, screening for GDM is only performed in the second trimester and for those who are subjected to risk factors [10]. However, identifying and counseling at-risk women is an input for the initiation of the motivation to prevent GDM in low- and LMICs where screening is difficult [11].

Evidence confirms that healthy lifestyle intervention during pregnancy has a positive effect on the probability of GDM among pregnant women who are at risk of it [12, 13]. In addition, identifying at-risk women could be an opportunity to target life-course lifestyle changes for better health in less developed countries, as women can obtain greater motivation to maximize their healthy life behavior and that of their newborns after delivery [7, 14].While clinical guidelines emphasize lifestyle modification as a key preventive measure, many pregnant women struggle to adopt and sustain healthy behaviors [15]. Moreover, the factors that influence their ability and opportunity to make such changes such as awareness, beliefs, support systems, and access to resources remain inadequately understood. This gap in knowledge limits the development of effective, context-specific interventions. Hence, this study aims to explore the views of pregnant women at risk for GDM during their current pregnancy, with a focus on their awareness of GDM and the lifestyle changes necessary for its prevention.

Method

Study area and period

The study was conducted in Jimma public health facilities, namely, Jimma Health Center, Higher Two Health Center, Mendera Kochi Health Center, and Bacho Bore Health Centers. Jimma town is located 353 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. The town has six public health facilities: one referral hospital, one general hospital, and four health centers. The study was conducted in four selected health centers from March 2-April 10,2024.

Study design

A descriptive qualitative study was employed to explore awareness, and prevention strategies on gestational diabetes mellitus among pregnant women at risk for GDM who were receiving antenatal care at Jimma health centers. An interpretive paradigm was employed for this study because it allows the understanding and elucidation of the subjective world of human experience, i.e., the view and knowledge of the women in this case [16]. Purposive sampling was employed to include seven pregnant women on the basis of the inclusion criteria and the sufficiency of the obtained information [17, 18]. First The risk-based assessment was performed by health care providers at each health center to identify pregnant women at higher risk of GDM; those with a family history of type 2 diabetes; those with a previous history of GDM, macrosomia, stillbirth and abortion; those who were overweight or obese; those who were over 35 years of age; and those who were hypertensive [3, 23]. The women were identified and recruited with the assistance of midwives who were trained on the objective of the study and criteria for the inclusion of pregnant women in this study. To the best of our knowledge, diversity in terms of age, educational level and sociocultural level was sought in the sample (purposive sampling).

Data collection

Participants were recruited on a voluntary basis. To ensure data quality, interviews were conducted by trained qualitative researchers with experience in maternal health research, including antenatal care, and fluent in local languages. Transcriptions were reviewed against audio recordings daily, and member checks were performed to confirm meaning. Peer debriefing and audit trails were also applied for credibility. The interview guide was adapted by a researcher after referring to relevant evidence sources [19–21]. It was prepared in English and translated into local languages by language experts. The interviews were conducted in Afan Oromo and Amharic in accordance with the preferences of the women.

In-depth interviews were employed to explore the awareness of pregnant women at higher risk of GDM and healthy lifestyle measures as a prevention measure. among women receiving antenatal care in public health centers in Jimma town to obtain several versions of reality from the perspective of the participants [22].

Prior to the main data collection, the interview guide was piloted with a small number of participants to ensure clarity and relevance, and minor adjustments were made accordingly. Each interview lasted approximately 25-30 minutes and included follow-up probes to thoroughly explore women’s views on the prevention of GDM and its adverse outcomes. Field notes were taken alongside audio recordings to capture non-verbal cues and contextual observations. Data collection continued until data saturation was achieved this was determined when no new themes or insights emerged across at least three consecutive interviews.

Trustworthiness

The study ensured its credibility, reliability, transferability, and confirmability through various methods. Data were collected from multiple viewpoints by engaging different pregnant women. The expanded authors participated in both the data collection and analysis. The authors remained present in the field throughout participant recruitment, data collection, and member validation. The analyzed data were cross-checked against audio recordings and field notes prior to finalizing the report. Furthermore, colleagues were invited to review the analyzed data. The study’s findings were audited and validated by a research assistant, peers, and other seasoned professionals in qualitative research.

Data management and analysis

This study was conducted among pregnant women at risk for GDM who were receiving antenatal care at Jimma health centers. There was no advanced change to the interview guide after the pilot study since the interview guide was in the local language, which was translated by experts who are experienced in the translation of the study tool and are from the community. Hence, the pilot-aged women were also included as part of the study sample. The interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim by the research team on a daily basis. The translated data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic content analysis approach [24]. Initial codes were developed through repeated reading of transcripts. Codes were grouped into categories and refined into themes and subthemes through constant comparison across participants. NVivo was not used; manual methods were employed.

The interview recordings were written for words and typed. The typed data were subsequently read thoroughly several times. We reviewed the audio records for consistency while reading the typed data. Finally, a series of primary codes were generated by arranging the data in order [25]. The participants’ terminologies and key words in their idea were used to create the initial codes. Similar codes are highlighted in the same colors throughout the typed data. A comparison of codes across transcripts revealed similarities and differences and consolidated them into themes and subthemes.

Results

Background information on the study participants

A total of seven different pregnant women participated in the in-depth interviews. The demographic data revealed that the mean age of the reproductive-age group of women was 33.4 years (range: 18–35). The majority of the women have no formal education and are multiparous in terms of parity. Study participants were identified on the basis of their risk of GDM. All respondents reported one or more risk factors for GDM. While family history of type 2 diabetes was frequently cited, some participants also reported multiple risk factors including obesity, family history of type 2 diabetes and advanced maternal age (age > 35). The transcripts were examined via thematic analysis, resulting in three main themes: understanding gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), lifestyle change and the effect of GDM on both mothers and infants.

Understanding the GDM

GDM occurs exclusively during pregnancy. One of these findings suggests that age or family history might also play a role and that GDM typically resolves after childbirth, although there is an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes later in life. The diagnosis of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) indicates potential health risks for both the mother and the infant. Women diagnosed with GDM are particularly susceptible to experiencing it again in future pregnancies. Thus, a GDM diagnosis can serve as an important warning sign of the likelihood of future type 2 diabetes and is considered an “excellent marker” for implementing targeted preventive measures during and after pregnancy [26].

In the first theme the study participants were asked, “What they know about GDM, how they believe the GDM affects pregnancy”.

Most participants did not recognize gestational diabetes mellitus, but they mentioned that they knew about diabetes mellitus. One of the 36-year-old study participants reported GDM and its prevention:It might be related to having severe nausea and headaches. I think it is something related to a lot of urine, but I am not sure about it. I have heard about diabetes, but not gestational diabetes.

It might be related to having severe nausea and headaches. I think it is something related to a lot of urine, but I am not sure about it. I have heard about diabetes, but not gestational diabetes.

We also asked for prevention measures that should be taken by the mothers at risk.

A 25-year-old participant replied, “Is it the same with diabetes? I have no idea about gestational diabetes mellitus. I guess having regular ante natal care follow-up may help the mother; otherwise, the one who protects from disease is only Allah/God. Lifestyle change is something luxury for us.”

Healthy lifestyle (diet and exercise)

Participants were also asked about the benefits of a healthy diet and exercise during pregnancy, and the majority of them replied that a healthy diet is a balanced diet. In this context, all the participants acknowledged the importance of diet as part of a healthy lifestyle. The participants had differing opinions on the amount and frequency of exercise during pregnancy, with some engaging in believing that regular tasks such as housework were adequate for maintaining fitness. Almost all of them replied that walking is good during pregnancy and that all other exercises should be avoided. Mothers understand that fruits and vegetables are essential components of a healthy diet. Effects of GDM on both mothers and babies

The majority of participants were unaware of their gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) status. However, they expressed the perception that any disease during pregnancy could affect both the mother and the infant. In addition to this general understanding, the participants did not provide detailed information regarding the specific effects of GDM.

Participants were asked, what do they know about the effects of GDM on their baby? How does it influence their actions during pregnancy?

Twenty-nine-year-old pregnant women reported GDM-related adverse outcomes as follows:I have never heard about it. One of my friends has delivered through a cesarian section. People were talking about how she had a cesarian section because of her diabetes status, but I don’t truly believe in it because now a days many women are having this cesarian section delivery.

Discussion

This research focused on women who are at high risk for gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). reveals significant gaps in understanding and awareness that are crucial for effective management of this condition. The thematic analysis identified three broad themes: understanding GDM, lifestyle changes (diet and exercise), and the impact of GDM on both mothers and babies. Each theme highlights different dimensions of the knowledge deficit among pregnant women and misconceptions regarding GDM and underscores the need for targeted educational interventions. Misunderstandings were particularly evident regarding the causes of GDM, lifestyle-related risk factors, the effects of GDM on maternal and neonatal health, the components of a healthy diet, and the appropriate level of physical activity.

Our study revealed that many women presented misconceptions about the nature of GDM, including its causes and implications. This aligns with previous research indicating that knowledge about GDM can be limited and often inaccurate [27]. These misconceptions might stem from inadequate educational resources or communication barriers between healthcare providers and patients [28]. This gap underscores the need for enhanced educational initiatives that clarify what GDM is, its risk factors, and its management.

Moreover, there was a notable lack of comprehensive knowledge among the participants. Misconceptions about the nature of GDM, such as its causes and implications, impede effective management and prevention. This finding aligns with research indicating that inadequate knowledge about GDM contributes to poor adherence to medical advice and a higher incidence of complications [29]. Improving understanding through clear, accessible information could empower women to take proactive steps in managing their condition.

The study further revealed that participants had an incomplete understanding of how lifestyle changes, particularly diet and exercise, can mitigate the risk of developing GDM. While some women recognized that diet and physical activity are important, there was a notable lack of detailed knowledge about what constitutes a healthy diet and the types of exercise that are beneficial during pregnancy. This finding is consistent with other studies that have shown that while women may be aware of general health recommendations, they often lack specific guidance on how to implement these changes effectively [30–32]. The gaps in knowledge about dietary guidelines and physical activity recommendations suggest a need for more targeted educational interventions that provide clear, actionable advice on managing GDM risk through lifestyle modifications.

The effect of GDM on both the mother and the baby was another area where participants demonstrated limited awareness. This finding supports previous research indicating that many women do not fully understand the potential short-term and long-term effects of GDM on maternal and fetal health [33]. Misconceptions and a lack of awareness about these effects can lead to insufficient management of GDM and, consequently, poorer health outcomes for both the mother and the infant [34, 35]. This gap highlights the importance of incorporating comprehensive education about the potential consequences of GDM into prenatal care and public health campaigns.

The participants’ inconsistent views on recommended physical activity during pregnancy further illustrate gaps in knowledge. While some women may be aware that exercise is beneficial, conflicting information about the type, intensity, and duration of exercise during pregnancy may lead to confusion and inconsistency in practice. This is supported by the literature indicating that there is often ambiguity in guidelines and advice regarding physical activity during pregnancy [36]. Providing clear, consistent, and evidence-based guidelines could help bridge this gap and support women in making informed decisions about their physical activity levels.

Addressing these gaps requires a multifaceted approach, including improved educational resources, clearer guidelines for dietary and physical activity recommendations, and enhanced communication between healthcare providers and patients [37]. Future research should continue to explore these gaps and evaluate the effectiveness of various educational interventions in improving knowledge and outcomes related to GDM.

The strengths of this study include using a qualitative approach that allows for an in-depth understanding of local perspectives the contextual richness of the data, and the ethical rigor applied throughout. However, the findings are limited by the study’s small, purposively selected sample and its restriction to a single geographic area, which affect generalizability. This study may be subject to social desirability bias, To mitigate this, interviews were conducted privately by trained interviewers who used neutral, non-judgmental language and assured participants of confidentiality.

Conclusions

This study contributes to a better understanding of the awareness gap among pregnant women at risk, regarding GDM and its prevention measures. The results highlighted a misconception regarding GDM, limited awareness of healthy lifestyles and adverse GDM outcomes. This study provides valuable insights that may inform healthcare practices and support systems for pregnant individuals at risk for or diagnosed with GDM. Moreover, raising awareness about healthy lifestyles among pregnant women is not only vital for GDM prevention but may also contribute to overall maternal health.
