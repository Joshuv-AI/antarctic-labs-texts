MDIFN: a deep learning–based multi-dimensional interaction fusion network for placental MRI segmentation
Authors: Yehua Wu, Yi Lin, Lingxiao Zhou, Yuefu Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe placenta serves as a vital channel for maternal–fetal substance exchange and is a crucial organ for maintaining pregnancy. Placental diseases can severely affect maternal health and fetal growth. Given the limitations of single-dimension segmentation models and traditional fusion strategies in meeting the demands of automatic placental magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) segmentation, this study developed an automatic placental MRI segmentation method based on a multi-dimensional interaction fusion network (MDIFN).MethodsThis study included 267 placental MRI cases that met the inclusion criteria. After preprocessing steps, such as bias field correction, resampling, intensity normalization, and dimension matching, a dual-branch foundational architecture combining two-dimensional UNet (2D-UNet) and three-dimensional UNet (3D-UNet) was constructed. An interaction fusion bidirectional gated cross-dimension module was designed to achieve layer-wise synergistic fusion of two-dimensional (2D) detail features and three-dimensional (3D) contextual features. A global context recalibration module and a residual refinement module were introduced to optimize feature quality. A homoscedastic uncertainty-based multi-task weighted loss function was employed to balance the training process. Model performance was compared against six mainstream models using 10-fold cross-validation, and ablation studies were conducted to validate the contribution of core modules.ResultsThe model achieved a Dice coefficient of 0.8749±0.0132 (P<0.05) on the test set, representing an improvement of 16.2% compared to pure 2D-UNet and 8.4% compared to pure 3D-UNet. The sensitivity was 0.8999±0.0172 (P<0.05), and the F1 score was 0.8749±0.0132, which were the optimal values among all models. The relative volume difference was 0.0301±0.0173, and the 95% Hausdorff distance was 7.67±1.04. Ablation experiments showed that removing the gating mechanism, global context module, or bidirectional interaction reduced the Dice coefficient to 0.8554, 0.8551, and 0.8566, respectively, confirming that each component contributes to performance improvement.ConclusionsThis study addresses the limitations of single-dimension segmentation and traditional fusion models, providing an efficient and objective technical approach for automatic placental segmentation.Keywords: Placental magnetic resonance imaging segmentation (placental MRI segmentation), deep learning, bidirectional gated interaction network

Introduction

The placenta serves as the channel for material exchange between the mother and the fetus, and is also a vital organ supporting fetal growth, development, and pregnancy maintenance (1). Placental dysfunction can lead to a series of diseases, posing a major threat to maternal and fetal health (2). Early, timely, and effective diagnosis of placental disorders can greatly improve maternal and fetal outcomes (3).

Ultrasound is the primary tool for diagnosing antenatal placental disorders (4); however, when the placenta is attached to the posterior uterine wall or intestinal gas interferes with the examination, the false-negative (FN) and false-positive (FP) rates of ultrasound increase (5). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is increasingly being used in placental diagnosis (6). MRI has lower technical requirements for operators, offers superior soft-tissue resolution, allows direct observation of the placenta and surrounding tissues, and provides clearer imaging of the posterior uterine wall and the interface of placental marginal tissues (4). MRI is an objective and effective diagnostic tool in the early diagnosis of placenta accreta spectrum disorders, the assessment of placenta previa, and the identification of placental structural abnormalities (7). It has unique advantages in evaluating lesion extent and depth of invasion (8), and can significantly improve diagnostic performance (9). The combination of morphological MRI features and quantitative diffusion-weighted imaging functional parameters can further improve the detection of posterior placenta accreta spectrum disorders (10). Placental MRI-derived diffusion-based vessel density exhibits excellent diagnostic efficacy in early gestation and in severe pre-eclampsia complicated by fetal growth restriction (11).

Accurate placental segmentation is a prerequisite for the quantitative assessment of placental morphology and function. Traditional manual segmentation is time-consuming and highly subjective (12), making it difficult to apply to large-scale datasets (13). Artificial intelligence-based automatic placental recognition and segmentation techniques can automatically mine hidden feature information in medical images, enabling efficient, accurate automatic segmentation and quantitative analysis, thus addressing the limitations of manual annotation (14,15). Automatic segmentation technology has evolved from semi-automatic (16) to fully automatic approaches (17). Currently, deep learning methods dominate the field, with the UNet method garnering widespread attention. Pietsch et al. (18) proposed a fully automatic deep learning method called APPLAUSE, which uses UNet for automatic placental segmentation and employs a Gaussian process regression model to characterize placental maturity and health. Although the model’s performance in automatic segmentation (Dice =0.58) was inferior to that in manual segmentation (Dice =0.68), it reliably estimated the mean T2* value of the placenta (Pearson correlation coefficient r=0.986), providing a feasible solution for prenatal automatic assessment of the placenta. Abulnaga et al. (19) used three-dimensional UNet (3D-UNet) as the backbone network and innovatively designed an additive boundary-weighted loss to address the difficulty in identifying placental boundaries. After boundary weighting, the model’s segmentation performance was significantly improved, achieving a Dice coefficient of 82.5% and a signed distance transform (SDT) loss Dice of 80.6%, demonstrating excellent robustness and consistency across different oxygenation states and pregnancy conditions.

Despite recent progress in placental MRI segmentation, existing research still has several limitations. First, single-dimensional segmentation models have inherent defects. Pure two-dimensional (2D) models lack three-dimensional (3D) spatial contextual information, while pure 3D models exhibit limited accuracy in boundary delineation and incur high computational costs. Second, existing methods suffer from cross-dimensional feature conflict and a lack of edge refinement. Simple fusion of 2D and 3D features tends to introduce noise and redundancy, and the absence of effective filtering and conflict suppression mechanisms leads to insufficient segmentation accuracy in placental marginal defect regions. Third, most loss functions rely on manual tuning. Existing static weights struggle to adapt to different data distributions and cannot dynamically optimize loss contributions based on uncertainty during training, limiting the ability of the model to learn from hard-to-segment samples. Fourth, it is difficult to balance accuracy and efficiency. Existing high-precision models suffer from long inference times and high memory consumption, making it challenging to meet the real-time demands of clinical practice.

In this context, this study proposed a multi-dimensional interaction fusion network (MDIFN) for placental MRI segmentation. By combining the features of two-dimensional UNet (2D-UNet) and 3D-UNet via bidirectional gated interaction fusion, MDIFN achieves a breakthrough in automatic placental segmentation. The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

An innovative multi-dimensional bidirectional gated interaction module was designed to enable dynamic synergistic fusion of global spatial contextual information between the 2D and 3D branches across all encoder levels, addressing the limitations of single-dimensional segmentation and traditional fusion strategies;

No independent fixed test set was used in this study; all 267 cases participated in the cross-validation process. In each training fold, an internal validation set (e.g., 10% of the training data) was further split from the training set (nine subsets) to monitor the training process (for early stopping and model selection). The test subset (i.e., the one validation set) of the current fold was never used for model selection or early stopping decisions.

A radiologist with 5 years of experience manually delineated the placental region of interest (ROI) slice by slice on each MRI sequence. After completion, a senior radiologist reviewed all annotations case by case and modified any inaccurate ROIs. The senior radiologist had over 10 years of experience in radiology, including 5 years in the radiology department of a women and children’s medical center. The annotation results approved by the senior radiologist were used as the ground truth for model training and validation.

MDIFN: bidirectional gated cross-dimensional interaction

The MDIFN proposed in this study (hereinafter referred to as interaction fusion) is a layer-by-layer, bidirectional gated, and self-calibrating fusion network. It facilitates the synergistic interaction between 2D detail features and 3D contextual features at each encoder scale, rather than simply concatenating log probabilities or features. Given the semantic homology of features at the same downsampling scale in the encoder, lossless alignment can be achieved. In addition, the encoder features have low resolution, and the fusion overhead results in only a 5% increase in graphics processing unit (GPU) memory usage, thus preserving the advantages of 3D full-resolution training. Notably, the interaction fusion is implemented only at the encoder levels. Freezing the pre-trained encoder anchors general anatomical priors, avoids gradient conflicts and overfitting, and enables the fusion module to focus on feature selection rather than feature extraction. For these reasons, the interaction fusion is exclusively implemented at the encoder levels in this study.

Comparative model design

To fully validate the superiority of the MDIFN, six segmentation models were selected for comparison in this study, including 2D-UNet, 3D-UNet, two published state-of-the-art methods, and two self-developed models. All of the above models were based on the same encoder channel sequence (8 → 16 → 32 → 64 → 128) and training strategy, differing only in feature extraction dimensions or fusion approaches. Their architectures are described below.

nnUNet (20): the nnUNet framework proposed by Isensee et al. was adopted, which is currently considered the gold standard for medical image segmentation. It was implemented using the MONAI library with the following configuration parameters: channels = (32, 64, 128, 256, 320), strides = (2, 2, 2, 2), and num_res_units = 2, using LeakyReLU activation and instance normalization.

nnUNet (20): the nnUNet framework proposed by Isensee et al. was adopted, which is currently considered the gold standard for medical image segmentation. It was implemented using the MONAI library with the following configuration parameters: channels = (32, 64, 128, 256, 320), strides = (2, 2, 2, 2), and num_res_units = 2, using LeakyReLU activation and instance normalization.

SwinUNETR (21): SwinUNETR proposed by Hatamizadeh et al. was adopted, which is a state-of-the-art 3D medical image segmentation method based on the Swin Transformer. It was implemented using the MONAI library with the following configuration parameters: img_size = (96, 96, 64), feature_size = 48, depths = [2, 2, 2, 2], and num_heads = [3, 6, 12, 24].

To ensure rigorous, fair, and unbiased model comparison, all competing baseline models (2D-UNet, 3D-UNet, nnUNet, SwinUNETR, Late Fusion Network, and Multi-Scale Fusion Network) and the proposed MDIFN were implemented and evaluated under identical experimental configurations. All models shared the same preprocessing pipeline (bias field correction, resampling, intensity normalization, and dimension matching), data augmentation strategies (spatial transformations and intensity-domain augmentations), Adam optimizer, One Cycle Learning Rate (OneCycleLR) scheduling scheme, homoscedastic uncertainty-based multi-task weighted loss function, and patient-level 10-fold cross-validation splits with consistent patient allocation to avoid data leakage. The only differences among models were limited to network architecture, feature extraction dimension, or cross-dimensional fusion strategy; no discrepancy was introduced in any data processing or training hyperparameters.

Training strategy

Loss function

Placental MRI segmentation faces two clinical challenges: first, the volume of the placental region is much smaller than the maternal background; second, the signal at the placenta–myometrium interface is blurred. To address these issues, an adaptive multi-task loss function was adopted: the Dice loss ensures that the entire placental region is not missed, while the Focal loss forces the network to focus on blurred boundary pixels. The two losses are automatically balanced via uncertainty weighting, eliminating the need for manual parameter tuning and making the training process more stable.

The Dice loss is assigned class weights of [0.05, 0.95] to enhance learning of the placental foreground region. The focal loss is configured with a difficulty focusing parameter γ =2.0 to concentrate on hard-to-segment boundary samples.

Parameter settings

In this study, the Adam optimizer was used to update parameters, with an initial learning rate of 10−3 and a weight decay of 10−4. The OneCycleLR scheduling strategy was adopted, with a maximum learning rate of 3×10−3. The number of gradient accumulation steps was set to four to balance GPU memory limitations and batch size. An early stopping mechanism was employed: training was terminated when the Dice coefficient on the validation set showed no improvement for 50 consecutive epochs, thereby preventing overfitting.

Data augmentation

Two types of data augmentation strategies were designed: spatial transformations and intensity-domain augmentations. Spatial transformations included balanced cropping of positive and negative samples and random coronal-axis flipping. Intensity-domain augmentations included Gaussian noise injection, histogram shifting, and random contrast adjustment, which enriched data diversity and improved model generalization capability. All comparison models and MDIFN adopted identical training strategies, data preprocessing, and augmentation pipelines to ensure experimental fairness.

Evaluation metrics

In this study, 10 evaluation metrics were adopted to assess model performance: Dice similarity coefficient (Dice), intersection over union (IoU), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1 score, 95% Hausdorff distance (HD95), average symmetric surface distance (SurfaceDistance), relative absolute volume difference (RAVD), and negative predictive value (NPV). All metrics were computed at the voxel level. Among them, Dice and F1 score are mathematically equivalent and were used to evaluate region overlap. Sensitivity assessed foreground recognition capability, specificity assessed background recognition capability, and accuracy evaluated overall classification correctness. SurfaceDistance is expressed in millimeters (mm) and was used to evaluate boundary localization accuracy.

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess the significance of performance differences between the MDIFN and other models, with the significance level set at α=0.05. All statistical tests were performed on paired samples from the 10-fold cross-validation.

Its core segmentation metrics comprehensively surpassed those of mainstream medical image segmentation models such as nnUNet and SwinUNETR, as well as fusion-based models, including Multi-Scale Fusion Network and Late Fusion Network, and single-branch baseline models such as pure 2D-UNet and pure 3D-UNet. Notably, compared with pure 2D-UNet, which exhibited the weakest performance, MDIFN achieved a 16.2% improvement in the Dice coefficient. Moreover, MDIFN outperformed other models in terms of sensitivity, specificity, RAVD, and boundary precision metrics (Hausdorff distance and average symmetric surface distance).

To validate the generalizability of the model under different clinical scenarios, three representative cases of varying difficulty levels (easy, moderate, and challenging) were selected for visual analysis (Figures 3-5). The visualization results are fully consistent with the quantitative metrics. For the low-difficulty case, the placenta exhibits a regular shape and clear boundaries. All models achieved effective segmentation, but MDIFN achieved the highest overlap with the ground truth, produced no isolated FP artifacts, and yielded cleaner segmentation results. For the moderately difficulty case, the placenta presented mild motion artifacts and blurred boundaries. Single-branch models such as pure 2D-UNet exhibited obvious FPs and missed segmentations, whereas MDIFN, leveraging the complementary fusion of 2D and 3D features, effectively suppressed artifact interference and maintained a complete placental contour. For the high-difficulty case, the placental boundaries were extremely blurred with severe motion artifacts, and the overall segmentation overlap decreased for all models. Nevertheless, MDIFN remained the model with the fewest missed detections, the mildest artifacts, and the best boundary adherence among all models.

Ablation study

To validate the contribution of each module in the MDIFN to the segmentation performance, this study designed an ablation experiment. By removing key modules and comparing the changes in model performance, the role of each component was clarified.

This experiment used the complete MDIFN model as the baseline, which includes the gating mechanism, the global context module, and the bidirectional interaction mechanism. Based on this, three ablated models were constructed. Apart from the targeted module, all other structures and training strategies (including optimizer, learning rate and data augmentation) remained consistent with the baseline model to eliminate interference from unrelated variables. The specific designs of the ablated models are as follows:

Interaction_NoGating: removes the gating mechanism while retaining the global context module and bidirectional interaction mechanism to verify the role of the gating mechanism in feature weight allocation;

Interaction_NoGating: removes the gating mechanism while retaining the global context module and bidirectional interaction mechanism to verify the role of the gating mechanism in feature weight allocation;

Interaction_NoGlobalCtx: removes the global context module while retaining the gating mechanism and bidirectional interaction mechanism to verify the role of the global context module in channel feature selection;

Interaction_NoGlobalCtx: removes the global context module while retaining the gating mechanism and bidirectional interaction mechanism to verify the role of the global context module in channel feature selection;

Interaction_OneWayGating: replaces the bidirectional gating with unidirectional gating (only 2D → 3D gating remains) while retaining the global context module and interaction mechanism to verify the complementary enhancement effect of bidirectional interaction.

Interaction_OneWayGating: replaces the bidirectional gating with unidirectional gating (only 2D → 3D gating remains) while retaining the global context module and interaction mechanism to verify the complementary enhancement effect of bidirectional interaction.

Discussion

Interpretation of model performance

The model achieved a Dice coefficient of 0.8749, surpassing the 80% Dice coefficient reported for 3D U-Net in placenta segmentation in previous research (22). This improvement can be attributed to the bidirectional gated cross-dimensional interaction mechanism, which enables dynamic synergistic fusion of 2D detail features and 3D contextual features at each encoder scale, addressing the limitations inherent in single-dimensional models.

A previous study reported that the placental volume between 37 and 40 weeks of gestation is 1,039 mL (23). In our study, the model’s RAVD was 0.0301±0.0173, corresponding to an error of approximately 31 mL. In clinical practice, volume is only one factor in the diagnosis of placenta accreta spectrum disorders, along with other features such as heterogeneous placenta, placental bulge, dark intraplacental bands, placental ischemic infarction, and retroplacental dark zone loss (24). Given the variability of placental structures, this error is acceptable for placental volume measurement or clinical assessment.

Compared with existing methods, pure 2D-UNet relies solely on 2D planar information, lacks 3D spatial contextual associations, and is prone to FP artifacts and local missed segmentations. Pure 3D-UNet, although capable of spatial modeling, exhibits insufficient ability to capture detailed placental features. Traditional fusion models, which lack gating mechanisms and global context recalibration, cannot achieve efficient adaptation between 2D and 3D features. In contrast, the MDIFN dynamically selects complementary features through a bidirectional gating module and optimizes boundary segmentation using global context, thereby mechanistically addressing the inherent limitations of single-branch models and uncontrolled fusion models, ultimately achieving a breakthrough in performance.

The visualization results across three difficulty levels demonstrated that the MDIFN maintains robust performance under varying clinical conditions. For easy cases, the improvement was marginal but consistent; for moderate cases, the suppression of motion artifacts was notable; for challenging cases, the preservation of boundary adherence under severe degradation validated the clinical utility of the model. This addresses the limitation that single-case visualization cannot adequately reflect model generalization.

Role of core modules

The ablation study provided clear evidence of the contribution of each key component. The complete MDIFN model outperformed all ablation variants.

Gating mechanism: after removing the gating mechanism, RAVD increased from 0.0301 to 0.0704, indicating a significant increase in volume error. This demonstrates that the gating mechanism can dynamically select complementary 2D and 3D features, reduce redundant information interference, and improve the segmentation accuracy of the placental region.

Bidirectional interaction: replacing bidirectional gating with unidirectional gating led to a noticeable decline in segmentation performance, proving that bidirectional feature interaction, which fully integrates 2D detailed information with 3D spatial context, is the key to achieving efficient cross-dimensional fusion.

Global context recalibration: after removing the global context recalibration module, the model’s boundary accuracy decreased, with both Hausdorff distance and average symmetric surface distance being worse than those of the complete model. This indicates that this module can effectively capture the global morphology of the placenta, optimize boundary segmentation, and reduce missed detections and artifacts.

In summary, the bidirectional gating cross-dimensional fusion module and the global context recalibration module are the core innovative components of the MDIFN. Their synergistic effect enables efficient complementarity of 2D and 3D features and precise boundary optimization, effectively improving segmentation accuracy and robustness across clinical scenarios of varying difficulty levels, thereby fully validating the rationality and effectiveness of the proposed model design.

Computational efficiency and clinical applicability

By freezing the pre-trained encoder, training overhead and the risk of overfitting are substantially reduced, allowing only the core innovative modules—such as bidirectional gated fusion and global context recalibration—to participate in training, thereby achieving extremely high parameter utilization efficiency.

Notably, while the MDIFN had a higher parameter count than pure 3D-UNet, due to 86.47% of its parameters being frozen, its actual inference speed was significantly faster than that of nnUNet (145.30 ms) and SwinUNETR (210.50 ms), fully demonstrating the computational efficiency advantage of the frozen pre-trained encoder strategy adopted in this work.

Overall, the MDIFN trades controllable computational overhead for substantial performance improvement, achieving an optimal balance among segmentation accuracy, model lightweightness, and inference efficiency, making it more suitable for clinical deployment and on-device implementation of placental MRI segmentation.

Study limitations

First, the sample size was limited and may have introduced distribution bias. This study included 267 placental MRI cases, which is relatively limited for deep learning research. Moreover, the absence of first‑trimester cases limits the model’s generalizability to early pregnancy placentas.

Second, independent external validation was lacking. This study was built on data from a single center. Although 10‑fold cross‑validation was employed, differences in patient population characteristics, disease spectrum, and clinical pathways exist across regions. Without external validation using datasets from other institutions, the model’s cross‑regional generalizability needs to be confirmed by multi‑center external validation.

Third, prospective validation was not performed. As a retrospective study, the dataset excluded cases with severe motion artifacts, incomplete sequences, or poor image quality during the screening phase. Further prospective studies are needed to validate the model’s performance in an unselected real‑world clinical environment.

Fourth, although this study included two scanner brands (Siemens and Philips), the number of scanner models and sequence variants was limited, and the vast majority of cases were acquired using the Siemens HASTE protocol, which may introduce a model bias toward this protocol.

Fifth, the dataset in this study excluded cases with severe motion artifacts or incomplete sequences during the screening phase. The model’s performance on cases with severe motion artifacts or low‑quality MRI remains to be further validated.

Finally, the model has a large parameter count and high computational cost. Although the number of trainable parameters was controlled to 0.6116 million by freezing the pre-trained encoder, the total parameter count remained 4.5215 million, and the inference speed was 117.74 ms. The large parameter count leads to high computational overhead and long inference time, increasing the difficulty of clinical translation.

Conclusions

To address the clinical need for automatic placental MRI segmentation, this study proposes the MDIFN, a dual-branch architecture comprising a 2D-UNet and a 3D-UNet. It features a bidirectional gated cross-dimensional interaction module that enables layer-wise dynamic fusion of 2D detail features and 3D contextual features. This design leverages the complementary strengths of both branches, overcoming the limitations inherent in single-dimensional models and achieving deeper feature interaction than traditional fusion strategies. Further, a global context recalibration module and a residual refinement module are introduced to suppress cross-dimensional feature conflicts and enhance the selection of effective features, thereby improving segmentation accuracy for areas with blurred boundaries or subtle defects. The model is trained with a multi-task weighted loss function based on homoscedastic uncertainty, which balances the demands of handling class imbalance and learning from hard examples.

Experimental results demonstrated that the MDIFN achieved a Dice coefficient of 0.8749±0.0132, significantly outperforming mainstream models such as pure 2D-UNet and pure 3D-UNet in terms of segmentation accuracy, boundary delineation, volume quantification, and stability. Ablation studies confirmed the critical contributions of the gating mechanism, global context module, and bidirectional interaction mechanism. By employing frozen pre-trained encoders, the number of trainable parameters was effectively controlled at 0.6116, reducing training difficulty. The study also had limitations, primarily the small sample size and the single-center data source, which may introduce potential bias. Additionally, the model’s high parameter count may affect its clinical deployment feasibility.
