Wireless light-gated photoelectrochemical microneedle wearables for zero-bias longitudinal monitoring in interstitial fluid
Authors: Huichuang Wang, Zepeng Huang, Xue Zhou, Jing Huang, Yibin Liu, Liangjian Lyu, Nan Shi, Shuming Pan, Guoyue Shi, Zhonghai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWearable chemical sensors could enable continuous health monitoring, but long-term measurements in interstitial fluid are limited by power consumption, electrochemical interference and signal drift during wear. Here we show a light-gated photoelectrochemical microneedle wearable for zero-bias chemical monitoring in subcutaneous interstitial fluid. The device combines a swelling-tolerant conductive hydrogel microneedle array, a plasmon-enhanced photoelectrode and a miniaturized module for light excitation, current recording and Bluetooth transmission. Visible light drives oxygen-mediated redox chemistry without an applied bias, reducing interfering electrochemical reactions and power demand. Using uric acid as a representative analyte, the wearable provides wireless readout under hydration and mechanical deformation. In vivo disease-model validation was performed in male rats and mice, where the device tracked uric acid dynamics in chronic kidney disease, diabetic hyperuricemia and bacterial treatment, with trends correlating with blood measurements. This light-gated microneedle architecture offers a modular route for longitudinal monitoring of interstitial-fluid chemistry.Subject terms: Kidney diseases, Biophysical chemistry, Sensors and probes, Diabetes

Introduction

Wearable chemical sensing promises to transform health management by moving molecular measurements from episodic laboratory tests to continuous, real-world monitoring1–3. Interstitial fluid (ISF) is particularly attractive for this vision because it can be accessed minimally invasively and carries rich metabolic information that reflects systemic physiology4,5. However, most wearable chemical sensors still struggle to translate short proof-of-concept demonstrations into reliable longitudinal readouts in vivo. In practice, long-term operation is constrained by the need for an applied bias that increases power consumption and accelerates fouling6,7, by electrochemical interference from coexisting redox-active species in complex biofluids8, and by cumulative signal drift arising from hydration, mechanical deformation, and evolving tissue–device interfaces9. Overcoming these system-level barriers requires wearable platforms that can sustain stable, interference-resilient chemical transduction under wearable constraints rather than relying on calibration-intensive, bias-driven operation.

Conventional electrochemical wearables typically address these challenges through incremental materials optimization or signal-processing strategies4,10,11, yet the underlying transduction paradigm still couples chemical readout to an applied bias. This coupling creates a fundamental trade-off: increasing bias can amplify signal, but it also raises power draw, broadens the electrochemical window for parasitic reactions, and accelerates surface contamination and baseline drift. Enzyme-based strategies can introduce molecular selectivity, but long-term deployment remains limited by enzyme instability, leaching, and susceptibility to inhibitors in vivo, often necessitating frequent recalibration12–14. Optical sensing approaches can mitigate some electrochemical interference, but they often face their own constraints in implantation depth, background scattering, and integration with compact wearable electronics15–17. Together, these limitations point to a need for a sensing architecture that is intrinsically low-power, selective in complex biofluids, and stable under the mechanical and hydration dynamics of long-term wear.

Photoelectrochemical (PEC) sensing offers a distinct route to break this trade-off by decoupling optical excitation from electrical readout18–21. Light gating provides a built-in modulation mechanism that enables differential measurements and suppresses drift, while photogenerated carriers can drive interfacial redox chemistry without imposing a sustained external bias. In principle, a zero-bias PEC wearable could simultaneously reduce power consumption and narrow the pathways for electrochemical interference22–24. However, translating PEC sensing into a practical ISF wearable is nontrivial. It requires a bio-integrated interface that can repeatedly access ISF with minimal irritation, maintain stable ionic contact under continuous hydration, tolerate bending and compression, and integrate with compact optics and wireless readout. Moreover, the PEC electrode must sustain selective interfacial chemistry in the presence of diverse redox-active interferents while avoiding progressive fouling and baseline drift25,26.

Here, we report light-gated PEC hydrogel microneedle wearables that enable wireless, enzyme-free, zero-bias longitudinal chemical monitoring in subcutaneous ISF. The platform integrates a swelling-tolerant conductive hydrogel microneedle array with an in situ constructed, plasmon-enhanced photoelectrode formed by silver nanowires (AgNWs) and urchin-like TiO2 (ULTiO2), together with a miniaturized module for illumination, current acquisition, and Bluetooth telemetry. This architecture addresses system-level constraints that impede long-term wearable sensing, including power demands from bias-driven operation, electrochemical interference in complex biofluids, and hydration- and deformation-induced signal drift. Mechanistically, the synergistic combination of a Schottky junction–induced built-in electric field and oxygen-mediated photochemistry promotes efficient charge separation and generates superoxide radicals, which drive target oxidation at 0 V applied bias and thereby suppress interference from common electroactive species while reducing power consumption. We demonstrate stable, real-time wireless readout from minutes to weeks under continuous hydration and mechanical deformation. Using uric acid (UA) as a representative analyte, the device faithfully tracks ISF dynamics across challenging metabolic disease models, including adenine-induced chronic kidney disease rats and diabetic mice, and resolves longitudinal trajectories that correlate with blood measurements even under severe physiological interference. Long-term monitoring during engineered bacterial therapy further shows that the wearable readout reflects therapeutic response over extended timescales. Ultimately, the fundamental advance of this work lies in the system-level synergy among the zero-bias PEC mechanism, the swelling-tolerant hydrogel microneedle architecture, and the integrated wireless module. This integrated design simultaneously addresses two key limitations of conventional wearable electrochemical sensors: the trade-off between high-bias operation and chemical selectivity, and the incompatibility between continuous wear and the mechanical fragility of rigid microneedles. Beyond UA monitoring, this light-gated microneedle–photoelectrode–electronics architecture offers a modular platform for minimally invasive ISF sensing, in which surface redox interfaces can be rationally tailored for different analytes while retaining the same wearable system framework. Such a strategy may enable longitudinal profiling of disease progression and therapeutic response in complex metabolic disorders.

Results

System-level design rationale for continuous ISF monitoring

Continuous chemical monitoring in complex metabolic diseases poses a system-level challenge that spans sampling strategy, signal transduction, and wearable integration. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, conventional blood-based measurements are inherently invasive and temporally sparse, while sweat-based sensing suffers from poor stability and weak correlation with systemic metabolism. In contrast, subcutaneous ISF provides a physiologically relevant and stable medium for continuous monitoring, yet requires minimally invasive and reliable access. Microneedle interfaces enable direct ISF sampling while avoiding pain and tissue damage, establishing a practical foundation for long-term wearable operation. Beyond sampling, the choice of sensing mechanism critically determines long-term stability in the complex ISF environment. As summarized in Fig. 1b, conventional electrochemical sensing typically relies on relatively high applied bias or enzyme catalysis, which can induce co-oxidation interference, enzyme inactivation, and signal drift during prolonged use. PEC sensing offers a fundamentally different transduction paradigm by decoupling optical excitation from electrical readout, enabling low- or zero-bias operation with reduced background noise and enhanced interference resistance.

a Comparison of conventional blood-based measurements, sweat-based sensing, and microneedle-enabled extraction of subcutaneous ISF for continuous monitoring; b schematic comparison between conventional electrochemical sensing and PEC sensing, which decouples optical excitation from electrical readout and enables low- or zero-bias operation with improved stability and reduced background noise in complex biological environments; c integrated wearable PEC microneedle system with the conductive hydrogel microneedle array for minimally invasive ISF access, the plasmon-enhanced AgNWs@ULTiO2 photocathode for zero-bias UA oxidation, and the wireless electronic module for real-time signal acquisition and transmission.

Based on these considerations, we designed an integrated wearable PEC hydrogel microneedle system for continuous UA monitoring (Fig. 1c). The platform combines a swelling-tolerant hydrogel microneedle array for efficient ISF extraction, a plasmon-enhanced AgNWs@ULTiO2 photoelectrode for zero-bias UA oxidation, and a wireless electronic module for real-time data acquisition and transmission. This system-level design establishes the foundation for stable, minimally invasive, and long-term monitoring of UA dynamics under physiologically relevant conditions, which is systematically validated in the following sections. Moreover, by decoupling sampling, signal transduction, and device integration, this platform provides a generalizable framework that can be readily adapted to other redox-active metabolites through rational tuning of the photoactive interface.

Swelling-tolerant conductive hydrogel microneedle for stable PEC operation

To enable stable PEC sensing under continuous ISF exposure, the microneedle interface must simultaneously satisfy mechanical robustness, swelling tolerance, and electrical conductivity. We therefore designed a conductive hydrogel microneedle architecture by integrating plasmon-enhanced AgNWs@ULTiO2 within a polyvinyl alcohol/polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVA/PVP) double-network hydrogel (Fig. 2a). The fabrication process yields uniform microneedle arrays with well-defined geometries, as confirmed by optical microscopy (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1) and scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2). The microneedles display a pyramidal morphology with a height of approximately 1200 μm, a base width of ~500 μm, and a sharp tip diameter of ~15 μm, arranged in a regular 10 × 10 array with an inter-tip spacing of ~1000 μm. Crucially, this specific 1000 μm interval was deliberately optimized to navigate the complex biomechanical and material constraints of hydrogel-based continuous sensing27. From a biomechanical perspective, it prevents the well-documented “bed-of-nails” effect, ensuring that the insertion force is not dispersed across mechanically coupled tips (which would cause global skin indentation), but rather concentrated at individual tips for reliable penetration. Furthermore, from a materials perspective, unlike rigid sensors, the PVA-PVP microneedles extract ISF through substantial volumetric expansion. With the initial base width of ~500 μm, the 1000 μm center-to-center spacing establishes an essential ~500 μm edge-to-edge structural safety buffer. This strategic layout perfectly accommodates the extensive in vivo swelling of the hydrogel network, preventing the subcutaneous coalescence of adjacent needles that would otherwise obstruct ISF transport pathways and induce tissue damage during patch removal.

The conductive and photoactive architecture is first established at the composite level through the hierarchical AgNWs@ULTiO2 structure. High-aspect-ratio AgNWs were synthesized via a modified polyol reduction method (Supplementary Fig. 3)28, followed by in situ hydrothermal growth of urchin-like TiO2 on their surfaces (Fig. 2d). The two components are intimately integrated, enabling plasmonic conductivity from AgNWs and photoactivity from TiO2 to coexist within a single hybrid building block. When incorporated into the hydrogel matrix, the AgNWs@ULTiO2 composite further assembles into a percolated conductive network throughout the microneedle structure. High-magnification SEM imaging reveals a dense and continuous distribution of AgNWs@ULTiO2 on the microneedle surface (Fig. 2e), establishing efficient electron transport pathways across the hydrogel. Crucially, the macroscopic integrity of the matrix during this hydrated state is sustained by dense physical crosslinking via microcrystalline domains formed during the cast-drying process, allowing the PVA-PVP hydrogel to swell without dissolving. This hierarchical organization preserves electrical continuity upon drying and subsequent swelling, while simultaneously creating extensive semiconductor–metal interfaces within the hydrogel. Under light illumination, photogenerated electrons in TiO2 can be rapidly extracted and transported through the interconnected AgNWs network, providing a structural basis for efficient PEC operation under wearable, fully hydrated conditions. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping further confirms the homogeneous distribution of Ag, Ti, and C within the microneedles (Fig. 2f). The linear Ag signal delineates the continuous AgNWs conductive network, while Ti signals coincide with the in situ grown urchin-like TiO2 structures. The uniform C background indicates effective encapsulation of the AgNWs@ULTiO2 composite by the PVA/PVP hydrogel matrix, collectively confirming the successful and well-dispersed integration of the photoactive heterostructure within the polymer network. Additional structural, compositional, and optical characterizations corroborate the formation of a plasmonic active AgNWs@ULTiO2 composites. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Supplementary Fig. 4) reveals the coexistence of anatase and rutile TiO2 phases together with diffraction peaks of metallic Ag, while high-resolution Ti 2p X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra display a characteristic Ti4+ and Ag0 doublets (Supplementary Fig. 5), confirming the crystalline phases and chemical stability of the composite. UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) further shows markedly enhanced visible-light absorption of AgNWs@ULTiO2 compared with pristine ULTiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 6) due to the plasmonic property of AgNWs. Furthermore, the UV-vis absorption spectra of four independently fabricated microneedle arrays exhibited nearly perfectly overlapping profiles (Supplementary Fig. 7), confirming the uniform nanomaterial dispersion and excellent batch-to-batch fabrication reproducibility. Collectively, these results support the favorable structural, chemical, and optical properties of the composite, along with the reliability of the standardized micromolding process, for visible-light-driven PEC operation.

We next examined how the AgNWs content governs the electrical performance of the hydrogel electrode. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) shows a monotonic decrease in charge-transfer resistance with increasing AgNWs loading (Fig. 2g), indicating the progressive formation of a percolated conductive network. Consistently, four-probe measurements reveal that electrodes containing 20 wt% AgNWs retain high electrical conductivity even after extensive swelling (Fig. 2h), demonstrating a swelling-tolerant conductive architecture capable of maintaining electrical continuity under hydrated conditions. The stability of signal transmission was further reflected in chronoamperometric measurements, where electrodes with insufficient AgNWs content exhibited unstable baselines and negligible photo-responses, whereas the optimized AgNWs loading enabled a stable baseline and reproducible light on/off photocurrent switching (Supplementary Fig. 8). Macroscopic resistance measurements further confirm the low overall resistance of the optimized microneedle patch, supporting efficient signal propagation across the device (Supplementary Fig. 9).

In parallel, the polymer composition of the hydrogel matrix was optimized to balance mechanical integrity, swelling behavior, and electrical stability. By tuning the PVP/PVA ratio at a fixed AgNWs content, we identify a compositional window in which rapid fluid uptake is achieved without compromising the structural support required for skin penetration or the continuity of the conductive network (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 10). Excessive swelling disrupts the percolated AgNWs pathways, whereas insufficient swelling limits ion transport. Based on this balance, a PVA:PVP ratio of 100:1.25 was selected, providing a mechanically robust yet highly conductive hydrogel electrode suitable for stable photoelectrochemical operation in interstitial fluid.

Zero-bias PEC mechanism enabling selective UA oxidation

Building upon the swelling-tolerant conductive microneedle architecture, we next examined whether the proposed PEC mechanism could enable selective UA detection under zero-bias conditions. As schematically illustrated in Fig. 3a, we propose a zero-bias PEC sensing mechanism driven by plasmon-enhanced charge transfer and reactive oxygen species (ROS) mediation. Upon visible-light excitation of the AgNWs@ULTiO2 composite promotes efficient charge separation through plasmonic coupling, enabling photogenerated electrons to transfer to dissolved oxygen and generate superoxide radicals (•O2-). These reactive intermediates subsequently oxidize UA to allantoin without requiring an external bias, thereby suppressing high-potential electrochemical interference. To verify the thermodynamic feasibility of this process, the energy band positions of AgNWs@ULTiO2 were determined by combining Tauc plots derived from UV–vis DRS (Supplementary Fig. 11) with XPS valence band spectra (Supplementary Fig. 12). The calculated conduction band potential is more negative than the O2/•O2- redox potential, confirming that photogenerated electrons can thermodynamically drive oxygen reduction to •O2−29. Together, these results establish the energetic basis underlying the photocathodic current generation.

As shown in Fig. 3b, the hydrogel-AgNWs@ULTiO2 photoelectrode exhibits a pronounced enhancement in photocurrent response in the presence of UA under visible-light illumination. Compared with the baseline signal recorded in the absence of UA, the steady-state photocurrent increases markedly during light on/off cycles, indicating an effective PEC response toward UA and its participation in the photoinduced redox process. To elucidate the role of molecular oxygen in this process, the generation of reactive oxygen species under illumination was examined. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using DMPO as a spin-trapping agent reveals clear signals attributable to •O2-, which emerge within 1 min of irradiation and intensify with prolonged illumination (Fig. 3c), indicating continuous accumulation of photogenerated reactive species. The oxygen dependence of the photo-response was further evaluated by oxygen-depletion experiments. As shown in Fig. 3d, the photocathodic current toward UA is pronounced in oxygenated electrolyte but is drastically attenuated upon removal of dissolved oxygen by nitrogen purging. This behavior demonstrates that the electron-transfer process is strongly dependent on molecular oxygen, supporting a pathway in which photogenerated electrons in TiO2 are transferred to O2 to form •O2-. Furthermore, scavenging the photogenerated •O2- with superoxide dismutase (SOD) completely abolished the subsequent photocurrent response to UA (Supplementary Fig. 13). This definitively confirms that UA sensing is exclusively mediated by •O2- rather than direct electro-oxidation.

Finally, mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of the reaction solution after prolonged photoirradiation reveals the formation of allantoin, a known oxidation product of UA, in addition to the original UA signal (Fig. 3e). The identification of this product provides direct chemical evidence for the photoinduced oxidation of UA, completing the mechanistic link between oxygen reduction, superoxide generation, and UA conversion under photoelectrochemical conditions.

In conclusion, the proposed zero-bias PEC sensing mechanism is established upon a rigorous combination of direct experimental proofs and theoretically supported inferences. Directly, EPR spectroscopy and LC-MS analysis definitively demonstrate the generation of •O2- intermediates and the final oxidized product (allantoin), respectively. Furthermore, the complete signal quenching observed in the SOD-scavenging control explicitly confirms that the UA oxidation is exclusively mediated by these photogenerated radicals. Inferred from these anchoring points, the upstream electron transfer pathway, specifically the plasmon-induced hot electron injection and the Schottky-junction-directed charge separation, is strongly corroborated by our empirical band structure calculations and Mott-Schottky analyses. Finally, the strict dependence of the photocurrent on dissolved oxygen levels further supports the inference that O2 serves as the indispensable electron acceptor, seamlessly bridging the solid-state photoelectrode dynamics with the liquid-phase UA oxidation.

On this basis, the quantitative PEC response of the hydrogel-AgNWs@ULTiO2 photoelectrode toward UA was evaluated. Under visible-light illumination, the photocathodic current increases systematically with increasing UA concentration over a physiologically relevant range (Fig. 3f), yielding a well-defined linear calibration (Fig. 3g). These characteristics ensure sufficient quantitative fidelity for resolving temporal UA fluctuations under continuous operation. Specificity is a critical requirement for biosensing in complex ISF environments. Common physiological interferents, including glucose, lactate, urea, and amino acids, generate negligible photocurrent responses compared with uric acid (Fig. 3h), indicating concentration-window-matched selectivity of the hydrogel-AgNWs@ULTiO2 photoelectrode. Although electroactive species such as dopamine and ascorbic acid produce larger photocurrents than UA at artificially elevated concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 14), their physiological levels in ISF are orders of magnitude lower than that of UA, rendering their contributions negligible under practical sensing conditions30. In contrast, conventional glassy carbon electrodes exhibit pronounced oxidation signals even at trace interferent concentrations, which severely obscure the UA signal (Supplementary Fig. 15). These results highlight that, by matching the PEC sensing mechanism to the physiological concentration window, the PEC hydrogel-AgNWs@ULTiO2 photoelectrode effectively suppresses interference from trace yet highly reactive species that commonly compromise traditional electrochemical detection.

Considering potential electrode fouling by proteins in biological fluids, the anti-biofouling performance of the hydrogel electrode was evaluated using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a model protein31. As shown in Fig. 3i, incubation in a BSA solution results in minimal attenuation of the photocurrent response, indicating effective resistance to nonspecific adsorption and protein-induced fouling. The operational stability of the PEC system was further assessed under continuous illumination, where the photocathodic current remains essentially unchanged over extended operation, demonstrating robust PEC stability (Fig. 3j). Crucially, extended I-t measurements confirm that the PVA-PVP hydrogel effectively resists structural degradation by photogenerated ROS (Supplementary Fig. 16). This macroscopic integrity is further corroborated by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) spectrometry, which shows no systemic accumulation of Ag or Ti in major organs post-application (Supplementary Fig. 17), ensuring secure nanomaterial encapsulation and exceptional in vivo biosafety. In addition, measurements obtained from independently fabricated electrodes show minimal variation, confirming good device-to-device reproducibility (Fig. 3k). Collectively, these results establish a zero-bias, oxygen-mediated PEC sensing mechanism that is selective, stable, and resistant to biofouling, providing a reliable mechanistic foundation for subsequent in vivo validation.

Skin–ISF interfacial performance and biocompatibility of the wearable PEC microneedle platform

Prior to in vivo application, the interfacial properties, mechanical robustness, and biosafety of the microneedle patch were systematically evaluated. As efficient ISF sampling relies critically on surface wettability32, the hydrogel microneedle surface was first characterized by contact angle measurements. As shown in Fig. 4a, the hydrogel exhibits a low contact angle of 36.1°, indicating high hydrophilicity that favors rapid ISF uptake upon skin insertion. Consistent with this property, when applied to a methylene blue–stained agarose skin model, the microneedle tips were selectively stained within minutes, visually confirming successful penetration of the simulated skin barrier and localized fluid extraction into the hydrogel matrix (Fig. 4b). Quantitative absorption kinetics further revealed a rapid increase in uptake volume that reached saturation within minutes, ensuring sufficient fluid availability for subsequent PEC reactions (Fig. 4c).

To evaluate sensing performance under tissue-mimicking conditions, an agarose gel model containing artificial ISF was employed (Fig. 4d). Within this semi-solid environment, the microneedle patch produced stable and concentration-dependent photocurrent responses toward UA, demonstrating that the PEC sensing mechanism remains effective even under diffusion-limited, skin-like conditions (Fig. 4e). Considering the mechanical deformation expected during daily wear, the flexibility and durability of the patch were assessed through repeated bending tests. Notably, the photocurrent response exhibits minimal attenuation even after 600 repeated bending cycles, confirming the mechanical durability and electrical integrity of the microneedle patch under deformation relevant to daily wear (Fig. 4f).

The mechanical strength and biosafety of the microneedles were further examined to assess their suitability for transdermal application. Compression testing confirmed that the microneedle array possesses sufficient mechanical strength to penetrate the stratum corneum without structural failure (Fig. 4g)33. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining histological examination of rat skin following microneedle insertion revealed well-defined microchannels extending through the epidermis, verifying effective skin penetration while avoiding deep tissue damage (Fig. 4h). Importantly, skin recovery studies demonstrated rapid self-healing following microneedle patch removal. As shown in Fig. 4i, the puncture marks progressively diminished over time and completely disappeared within 24 h, with no visible signs of erythema, inflammation, or tissue damage. These observations indicate that the microneedle insertion causes only transient and superficial microdisruptions to the skin, supporting the minimally invasive nature of the device and its suitability for repeated or long-term wear. Finally, the potential release of active components from the microneedle patch was evaluated to address biosafety concerns. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP–AES) analysis showed negligible leaching of Ag and Ti ions over extended incubation, indicating stable immobilization of the AgNWs@ULTiO2 composite within the hydrogel network (Fig. 4j). Collectively, these results establish that the microneedle patch combines favorable interfacial wettability, reliable skin-mimicking sensing performance, mechanical resilience, and biosafety, providing a solid foundation for subsequent in vivo continuous monitoring studies.

Continuous in vivo UA monitoring and therapeutic response evaluation in complex metabolic disease models

To realize a truly wearable and user-friendly PEC platform for dynamic monitoring, the hydrogel microneedle electrodes were integrated into a flexible, fully self-contained patch system (Fig. 5a). In this architecture, AgNWs@ULTiO2 hydrogel microneedles function as the working electrode, while Ag/AgCl-coated microneedles serve as the counter/reference electrode, both assembled on a transparent thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) substrate with integrated conductive interconnects and encapsulation for mechanical robustness and electrical insulation. A miniaturized micro-LED positioned beneath the sensing interface provides on-demand visible-light excitation (Supplementary Table 1). Crucially, the tight sandwich assembly of the opaque LED pad and TPU encapsulation functions as a “natural optical darkroom”, successfully eliminating ambient light interference to ensure high-fidelity signal acquisition (Supplementary Fig. 18). These components are coupled with a lightweight, battery-powered wireless electronic module that integrates light control (Supplementary Fig. 19), current acquisition (Supplementary Fig. 20), and Bluetooth data transmission, enabling real-time PEC sensing in a compact, flexible, and fully wearable system.

Photographs of the standalone microneedle patch (Supplementary Fig. 21) and the fully assembled wearable system (Supplementary Fig. 22) illustrated the high level of system integration. Owing to its lightweight design (2.57 g excluding the battery, Supplementary Fig. 23) and compact footprint (5 cm × 1 cm, Supplementary Fig. 24), the wireless module imposes minimal burden on the wearer. Notably, while this system- level footprint is currently governed by the physical dimensions of commercial batteries and FPCB routing, the intrinsic functional microneedle array requires a minimal active area of only 1 cm × 1 cm to maintain reliable in vivo signal-to-noise ratios. Future transition to custom integrated circuits (ASICs) could further shrink the overall device toward this 1 cm2 theoretical limit. When applied to the human arm and secured with a medical dressing, the flexible microneedle patch exhibits skin conformability and mechanical compliance, maintaining structural integrity under bending and movement (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 25). This wearable configuration effectively overcomes common limitations of conventional microneedle-based UA sensors, including inadequate mechanical robustness, poor operational stability, and limited wearing comfort. To explicitly validate its practical reliability against user motion, the microneedle patch was further subjected to dynamic operational tests. First, in vitro real-time sensing confirmed that the continuous photocurrent responses remained almost perfectly overlapped even when the patch was actively bent at severe angles (up to 90°, Supplementary Fig. 26). Second, in vivo continuous monitoring was performed on a fully awake, freely moving rat (Supplementary Fig. 27). Although the dynamic body movements inevitably introduced minor baseline fluctuations, the net steady-state photocurrent step (ΔI) generated by the light on/off modulation remained remarkably stable and highly consistent with the signals recorded in an anesthetized state. These dynamic stability validations firmly establish the capability of the wearable PEC platform to extract reliable analytical signals in the presence of motion-induced artifacts.

Given the close association between UA dysregulation and impaired renal function, we first evaluated the sensing performance of the wearable platform in an adenine-induced chronic kidney disease (CKD) rat model (Fig. 5c)34. Progressive renal dysfunction was confirmed by a sustained increase in plasma creatinine levels over the modeling period (Fig. 5d, inset), together with characteristic renal tubular atrophy and fibrotic lesions observed in histological analyses (Supplementary Fig. 28), validating the pathological relevance of the model. Despite the markedly elevated creatinine concentrations, in vitro interference tests showed that the photocurrent response to UA remained unaffected even under high creatinine levels, ensuring the operational reliability of the microneedle sensor in the CKD context (Supplementary Fig. 29). Longitudinal in vivo measurements further revealed that the photocurrent signals recorded by the microneedle patch closely tracked serum UA concentrations measured by a commercial analyzer throughout the 21-day disease progression (Fig. 5d). A strong positive correlation (Pearson’s r = 0.940, P < 0.01) was consistently observed between interstitial fluid UA dynamics captured by the wearable sensor and blood UA levels, demonstrating that the platform can reliably monitor UA fluctuations in vivo even under complex metabolic disturbances associated with renal impairment.

Because hyperuricemia frequently coexists with diabetes, where elevated glucose levels in ISF pose a major interference challenge for UA sensing35, we next evaluated the selectivity of the wearable platform under hyperglycemic conditions. In vitro interference tests showed that the photocurrent response to UA remained essentially unchanged even in the presence of high glucose concentrations (Fig. 5e), confirming the intrinsic selectivity of the PEC mechanism under extreme metabolic interference. Building on this result, real-time in vivo monitoring was performed in genetically diabetic (db/db) mice following the induction of acute hyperuricemia. Continuous photocurrent recording revealed dynamic UA fluctuations over time, and the temporal evolution of the sensor signal closely matched blood UA values measured in parallel using tail vein blood samples (Pearson’s r = 0.955, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5f). These results demonstrate that the wearable microneedle sensor can reliably track rapid UA changes in vivo even under hyperglycemic conditions, highlighting its potential for managing hyperuricemia in diabetic settings.

Finally, we examined whether continuous wearable monitoring could resolve therapeutic responses over extended timescales using a drug-induced hyperuricemia rat model (Fig. 5g)36. Representative on-body images confirm that the wireless microneedle patch can be stably worn during long-term monitoring without compromising conformal skin contact or signal acquisition (Fig. 5h). Longitudinal measurements obtained using the wearable microneedle patch clearly differentiated bacteria-treated, disease-control, and normal-control groups across multiple weeks (Fig. 5i). Following hypoxanthine administration, disease-control animals exhibited a sustained elevation of UA levels, whereas initiation of bacterial therapy led to a progressive reduction over time37. The temporal trends captured in interstitial fluid closely matched serum UA levels measured in parallel (Pearson’s r = 0.984, P < 0.001), validating the accuracy of the wearable readout during both disease progression and therapeutic intervention. Notably, upon entering the treatment phase, the photocurrent signals recorded from the bacteria-treated group decreased markedly in concert with serum UA concentrations, while untreated hyperuricemic animals maintained persistently high levels and normal controls remained stable. These results demonstrate that the wearable PEC microneedle platform not only enables minimally invasive and continuous in vivo UA monitoring, but also provides sufficient sensitivity and temporal resolution to track therapeutic efficacy over prolonged periods, elevating the system from point-of-care sensing to a wearable platform for longitudinal disease management and dynamic treatment evaluation.

Discussion

Continuous metabolic monitoring in vivo is fundamentally a system-level challenge rather than a sensor-level one. Achieving reliable operation over extended timescales requires that physiologically relevant ISF access, interference-resilient transduction, mechanical stability under skin deformation, and long-term biocompatibility be coordinated simultaneously within a single wearable system. In most existing approaches, these requirements are addressed in isolation, which can yield strong short-term analytical performance but poor fidelity once the device is placed under continuous skin contact. By integrating a light-gated PEC transduction paradigm with a swelling-tolerant conductive hydrogel microneedle interface and a wireless wearable architecture, the present platform resolves these competing constraints within a unified design space, enabling stable, minimally invasive monitoring under conditions that are representative of real-world wear. A defining capability of the platform is its ability to resolve longitudinal metabolic dynamics rather than isolated measurements. The wearable readout captures slow disease-associated trajectories, acute perturbation responses, and recovery processes across timescales spanning minutes to weeks. Such longitudinal resolution is difficult to achieve with bias-driven electrochemical wearables, where sustained operation can broaden parasitic reaction pathways, amplify interference, and accelerate fouling and baseline drift. In contrast, light-gated PEC readout decouples excitation from electrical sampling and provides intrinsic modulation, supporting interference-suppressed signal transduction at 0 V applied bias under continuous hydration and deformation. Conceptually, this shifts the dominant design question from maximizing instantaneous sensitivity to maintaining signal interpretability over time, which is the core requirement for wearable monitoring that aims to inform disease trajectories and clinical decision-making. It should be explicitly noted that while the sensor operates at a “zero-bias” electrochemical interface, eliminating the need for a power-consuming potentiostat and preventing the co-oxidation of electroactive interferents, the overall wearable system is not zero-power. A customized miniaturized battery is still required to provide the operational power for the LED light source, MCU signal processing, and wireless Bluetooth transmission. Nonetheless, the zero-bias PEC mechanism significantly simplifies the front-end circuit design and reduces the overall power overhead compared to conventional amperometric systems.

Beyond demonstrating prolonged monitoring, the platform enables in vivo therapy assessment as an operational sensing endpoint. Continuous tracking distinguishes untreated disease progression from treatment-induced recovery over extended periods, highlighting how ISF wearables can support dynamic evaluation of therapeutic efficacy rather than post hoc confirmation by sparse sampling. This distinction matters because metabolic disorders often evolve nonlinearly, with transient improvements or rebounds that are missed by episodic testing. By maintaining measurement continuity, the system provides a window into treatment kinetics and durability, establishing a practical pathway toward data-driven adjustment of therapy timing and intensity.

Although UA serves as the model analyte in this study, the broader implication is that wearable platforms integrating stable ISF access with interference-resistant PEC transduction may offer a generalizable route toward continuous monitoring of diverse metabolic biomarkers. However, adapting this modular platform for other redox-active species requires targeted strategies to ensure chemical selectivity and prevent signal overlap in complex biofluids. Future iterations could achieve this strict specificity through rational bandgap engineering to match the redox potentials of new targets, or by functionalizing the photoactive surface with specific recognition elements, such as enzymes or aptamers. In such hybrid designs, biochemical receptors would confer absolute target specificity while the zero-bias PEC interface provides a low-noise signal transduction background. With these functional expansions, alongside further optimization in power efficiency, personalization, and long-term safety, such systems have the potential to shift metabolic disease management from episodic testing toward continuous, data-driven assessment.

From a clinical translation perspective, this continuous PEC monitoring platform offers profound benefits to patients by eliminating the pain and poor compliance associated with frequent finger-prick blood sampling. More importantly, it provides longitudinal, dynamic metabolic trends that are critical for guiding personalized medication and preventing acute disease flares. However, successful commercialization and clinical translation must overcome several practical barriers, including individual variations in skin thickness (skin-to-skin variability) that affect insertion depth, inter-individual differences in physiological ISF-to-blood lag times (which highlight the persistent calibration needs), and the challenges of high-yield, large-scale manufacturing (to eliminate patch-to-patch variability). Additionally, future evaluations must systematically address the device’s oxygen dependence in vivo under extreme pathological conditions, its long-term optical and electronic stability, and possible tissue responses during repeated wear. To bridge these gaps in future iterations, ML-assisted single-point calibration algorithms can be integrated to individually compensate for anatomical and pharmacokinetic variations. Simultaneously, transitioning from lab-scale preparation to automated, wafer-scale micromolding processes will be imperative to ensure batch-to-batch uniformity, ultimately paving the way for the clinical deployment of next-generation wearable PEC diagnostics.

In summary, we report a wearable PEC microneedle platform that overcomes key system-level barriers to continuous metabolic monitoring. Crucially, the fundamental advance lies in the system-level synergy of the zero-bias PEC mechanism, the hydrogel microneedle architecture, and the integrated wireless system, resolving both the “high-bias vs. selectivity” and “continuous wear vs. mechanical breakage” contradictions. By integrating minimally invasive ISF access with a zero-bias, oxygen-mediated PEC transduction mechanism, the system achieves interference-resistant, mechanically robust, and biocompatible operation under continuous skin contact. The platform enables reliable in vivo tracking of UA dynamics across multiple disease models, including CKD, diabetic hyperuricemia, and long-term therapeutic intervention, capturing clinically meaningful temporal trends beyond single-point measurements. This work demonstrates that effective continuous monitoring arises from coherent integration of sensing chemistry, biointerface engineering, and wearable system design rather than isolated optimization of sensitivity. While UA serves as the model analyte, the modular platform architecture is readily adaptable to other metabolic biomarkers. While UA serves as the model analyte, the underlying platform architecture holds the potential to be adapted for other metabolic biomarkers in future studies. Overall, this study establishes a foundation for next-generation wearable biointerfaces that shift metabolic disease management from episodic testing toward continuous, data-driven assessment.

Methods

Ethical statement

All animal care and in vivo procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of East China Normal University and were approved by the committee under approval no. R20240306 (Shanghai, China). The non-invasive application of the device to a human arm was approved by the Ethics Committee of Putuo District Center Hospital, Shanghai, under approval no. PTEC-R-2025-76(Y)−1. Written informed consent was obtained from the volunteer before participation, including consent for the use of anonymized experimental data and non-identifiable images related to the non-invasive application of the device to the human arm for scientific publication and editorial review.

Synthesis of AgNWs

Long AgNWs were synthesized using a one-pot polyol method38. Briefly, 0.2 g of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, Mw 360,000) was dissolved in 25 mL of ethylene glycol (EG) under magnetic stirring at room temperature. Subsequently, 0.25 g of silver nitrate (AgNO3) was added and allowed to fully dissolve to obtain a transparent and homogeneous solution. Then, 3.5 g of FeCl3 solution (600 mM in EG) was rapidly introduced, followed by gentle stirring for 1–2 min. The reaction mixture was transferred immediately into a preheated reactor at 130 °C and maintained for 5 h to grow AgNWs. The resulting products were washed with acetone and ethanol and collected by centrifugation at 900 × g for 5 min. Finally, the AgNWs were re-dispersed in deionized (DI) water for further use.

Preparation of PVA–PVP prepolymer solution

To obtain PVA–PVP prepolymer solutions with defined mass ratios (100:5, 100:2.5, 100:1.25, 100:0), predetermined amounts of PVA and PVP powders were dispersed in DI water and heated to 97 °C under stirring until transparent and homogeneous. A 12 wt% PVA aqueous solution was prepared and stored for subsequent use.

Synthesis of AgNWs@ULTiO2 nanocomposites

ULTiO2 was synthesized through an EDTA-Na2–assisted hydrothermal approach39. First, 0.075 M EDTA-Na2 was dissolved into 50 mL aqueous dispersion containing 60 mg of AgNWs. After complete mixing, a precursor solution consisting of 1 mL titanium isopropoxide (TTIP) and 3 mL acetylacetone (ACAC) was added dropwise. The suspension was stirred for 3 h and then sealed in a Teflon-lined autoclave for hydrothermal treatment at 200 °C for 12 h. The obtained powder was washed thoroughly with methanol and DI water, and dried at 80 °C for 6 h.

Fabrication of hydrogel microneedle photoelectrodes

For preparing microneedle photoelectrodes, 0.45 g of PVA prepolymer solution was mixed with 11 mg of AgNWs@ULTiO2 and stirred for 1 h to form a uniform suspension. The mixture was transferred into PDMS microneedle molds, followed by vacuum-assisted filling to drive the prepolymer into the needle cavities. After drying for 12 h, the composite microneedles were demolded. For preparing counter/reference microneedle electrodes14, a 300 mg/mL solution of polystyrene (PS) in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) was prepared. The solution was cast into the mold and centrifuged at 1400 ×g for 5 min to ensure complete filling of the microcavities. Subsequently, the mold was placed on a hot plate and heated at 80 °C for 1 h to evaporate the solvent and solidify the structure. Subsequently, a chromium (Cr) adhesion layer and a gold (Au) conductive layer were sequentially deposited onto the polystyrene microneedles via ion sputtering. Finally, the microneedle surface was coated with Ag/AgCl paste.

Manufacturing and assembly of wearable microneedle patch

A 0.1-mm-thick transparent TPU film served as a flexible substrate. After laser cutting under optimized conditions (speed 100 mm s−1, power 30%), patterned PI films were laminated onto the trimmed TPU substrate (speed 100 mm s−1, power 35%). Silver paste was screen-printed to form conductive interconnects, followed by drying at 80 °C for 30 min. The working electrode (WE) and reference electrode/counter electrode (RE/CE) microneedle electrodes were mounted on the substrate and electrically connected with silver paste. The assembled wearable patch was encapsulated with a protective silicone layer (speed 100 mm s−1, power 35%), leaving the sensing MN tips exposed for electrical connection to a PCB or electrochemical workstation.

Flexible PCB and electronics design

A portable PCB was engineered for wireless PEC sensing. A low-power microcontroller unit was programmed for data acquisition, command processing, and task scheduling. The MCU communicated with an electrochemical analog front-end via SPI to perform chronoamperometry, equipped with a potentiostat circuit and a transimpedance amplifier. The WE-microneedle and RE/CE-microneedle were connected as WE and CE, respectively. A BLE module enabled wireless command and data transmission using a custom JSON-based protocol. A 300 mAh lithium coin cell with circuitry provided stable power for continuous chronoamperometric operation.

In Vitro PEC measurements

The artificial interstitial fluid (a-ISF) was prepared to mimic the ionic and molecular environment of human ISF40. The formulation was prepared by dissolving the following components in deionized water:2.5 mM CaCl2, 5.5 mM glucose, 10 mM HEPES (4-(2-hydroxyethyl)−1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid), 3.5 mM KCl, 0.7 mM MgCl2, 123 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM NaH2PO4, and 7.4 mM sucrose. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 7.4. To construct the skin-mimicking phantom41, 140 mg of agarose powder was added to 10 mL of the prepared a-ISF (resulting in a 1.4% w/v concentration). The mixture was heated to 120 °C in a glass bottle with continuous magnetic stirring until the agarose was completely dissolved and the solution became homogeneous. The hot solution was then poured into Petri dishes and allowed to cool and solidify at room temperature to form the hydrogel substrate. To simulate skins with different uric acid levels, uric acid solutions with varying concentrations were pipetted onto the surface of the respective agarose gels. The phantoms were then left undisturbed at room temperature for 60 min to ensure the uniform diffusion and equilibration of uric acid throughout the gel matrix before microneedle insertion and detection. The PEC performance of the microneedle patch was evaluated using the prepared agarose gel skin phantoms containing different concentrations of UA. A two-electrode configuration was employed for the measurements. The AgNWs@ULTiO2 hydrogel microneedle patch served as the working electrode, while a polystyrene microneedle array coated with a conductive silver layer and a subsequent Ag/AgCl layer was used as the counter/reference electrode to ensure stable potential reference in the semi-solid matrix. Both microneedle arrays were inserted into the agarose gel phantom to a depth ensuring complete penetration of the needle tips into the simulated interstitial fluid environment. The detection was performed at a bias of 0 V using an electrochemical workstation. A commercial visible LED was utilized as the light source to provide periodic irradiation (light on/off cycles). The photocurrent response was recorded as the difference between the current under illumination and the dark current. For interference tests, the patch was subjected to mechanical deformation (bending) or tested in gels containing interfering species (e.g., glucose, creatinine) following the same procedure to assess signal stability.

Mechanical characterization of microneedles

For compression tests, a 3 × 3 microneedle patch was compressed at 0.5 mm min−1 up to 10 N with an initial displacement of 2 mm. The force–displacement profiles were recorded.

Materials characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Sigma 300, ZEISS) was used to characterize MN morphology. SEM-EDS was employed to examine the elemental composition of the hydrogel MN composite. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo K-α) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were used to analyze the crystalline structures of the AgNWs@ULTiO2 composites.

Hardware operation and intermittent illumination protocol

During the in vivo continuous monitoring, the miniaturized wearable electronics module was programmed to operate under an intermittent pulsed illumination mode to optimize both power consumption and thermal stability. The integrated LED light source was driven by a custom microcontroller unit (MCU) using a 60-s measurement cycle, specifically set to 20 s ON and 40 s OFF. The 20-s excitation window was experimentally determined to be sufficient for the photoelectrochemical reaction to reach a steady-state plateau, allowing for the reliable extraction of the net photocurrent step (ΔI). The subsequent 40-s dark interval served a dual purpose: (1) it drastically reduced the average power consumption by operating at a ~ 33% duty cycle, thereby significantly extending the operational lifespan of the coin battery; and (2) it provided a critical thermal relaxation period to dissipate any localized heat generated by the LED. This intermittent sequence effectively prevented photothermal-induced baseline drift and avoided potential thermal damage to the skin tissue, ensuring the long-term robustness and safety of the continuous monitoring system.

In vivo demonstration for continuous and long-term monitoring

Male SD rats (n = 9, 5–7 weeks, 150–200 g) were housed under standard SPF conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 23 ± 2 °C, 60–70% humidity) with ad libitum food and water. For skin penetration evaluation, the dorsal hair was removed using electric clippers and depilatory cream (Veet), followed by cleaning with DI water and 75% ethanol. A single PS microneedle electrode was inserted into the exposed skin, harvested, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E staining.

Establishment and monitoring of the CKD rat model

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (200–250 g) were selected and randomly divided into two groups: the CKD group and the Control group. The rats were adaptively fed for one week before the experiment. To induce chronic kidney disease, the CKD group was administered an adenine suspension via gastric gavage at a daily dose of 300 mg/kg for 4 weeks34. In contrast, the Control group received an intragastric administration of an equal volume of physiological saline daily. The body weight of all rats was recorded daily. For in vivo monitoring, the rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the hair on their dorsal skin was removed using a depilatory cream. The wireless microneedle patch was applied to the hairless skin area and secured with medical Tegaderm™ film. It is important to note that the in vivo sensing timeline was initiated one week after the commencement of adenine administration to allow for the initial onset of renal stress. Therefore, the first day of the second week of adenine induction was designated as Day 0 for data recording. The wireless microneedle patch was applied to the dorsal skin of the anesthetized rats, and real-time photocurrent signals for both groups were recorded on days 0, 3, 7, 10, 14, 17, and 21 (relative to the defined Day 0). Simultaneously, blood samples were collected from the tail vein to measure serum uric acid (SUA) levels using a commercial uric acid meter for correlation analysis. To validate the model establishment, blood samples were also analyzed for serum creatinine (Scr) levels using a commercial creatinine assay kit. At the end of the experiment, the rats were euthanized, and kidney tissues were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining to evaluate renal pathological changes and fibrosis.

Real-time monitoring of acute hyperuricemia in diabetic (db/db) mice

Diabetic db/db mice (male, 6 weeks old) were used to evaluate the sensor’s performance under hyperglycemic conditions. Before the in-vivo test, the specificity of the sensor was verified in-vitro by measuring the current response to 200 μM UA in the presence of 18 mM glucose. For the in vivo experiment, the db/db mice were fasted for 12 h prior to the test. Acute hyperuricemia was induced by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of a mixture of hypoxanthine and potassium oxonate (400 mg/kg). The wireless microneedle patch was attached to the dorsal skin of the mice. The photocurrent response was continuously monitored for 180 min following the injection. Blood glucose levels were checked to confirm the hyperglycemic state ( > 16.7 mmol/L), and tail vein blood samples were collected at specific time intervals during the 180-minute period to measure blood uric acid concentrations using a commercial meter for comparison with the sensor data.

Evaluation of engineered bacterial therapy in hyperuricemia rat model

Male SD rats were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3 per group): Normal Control (Group C), Disease Control (Group B), and Bacteria-Treated (Group A). Hyperuricemia induction: Rats in Groups A and B received daily intraperitoneal injections of hypoxanthine (300 mg/kg) and potassium oxonate (300 mg/kg) for 4 weeks to establish a stable hyperuricemia model36. Group C received intraperitoneal injections of an equal volume of physiological saline. Bacterial Treatment: Starting from the 5th week, rats in the Treated Group (Group A) were administered the engineered E. coli strain via gastric gavage at a dose of 3 × 10¹⁰ CFU/rat daily. Rats in Groups B and C were gavaged with physiological saline as a control. Monitoring: Throughout the modeling and treatment phases (Total 7 weeks), the interstitial fluid (ISF) uric acid levels were monitored weekly using the wearable microneedle patch. The patch was applied to the dorsal skin as described previously, and stable photocurrent readings were recorded. Synchronous tail vein blood samples were collected to determine serum uric acid concentrations using a standard uric acid meter to validate the accuracy of the microneedle sensor in tracking the therapeutic efficacy. It should be noted that the longitudinal in vivo evaluation over the multi-week period was conducted using a session-by-session, intermittent monitoring protocol rather than the continuous wear of a single patch. Before each scheduled measurement session, the application site was re-treated with hair removal cream to ensure a bare surface, preventing any interference from rapid hair regrowth. A fresh microneedle patch was then applied, secured with a Tegaderm dressing, and used to record data for several hours before being completely removed at the end of the session.

To ensure data consistency and minimize user-induced artifacts during longitudinal studies, a strictly standardized application procedure was followed for all in vivo monitoring sessions. First, the identical anatomical location, the shaved upper dorsal skin (interscapular region), was selected for every rat to avoid variations in local skin thickness and ISF distribution. Second, the microneedle patch was applied manually by the same trained operator using a constant, firm vertical thumb pressure for exactly 10 s to ensure uniform stratum corneum penetration. Following insertion, the sensor and the attached FPCB module were immediately secured with a transparent medical adhesive dressing (3 M Tegaderm) to maintain a stable skin-sensor interface and prevent displacement. Because of the high batch-to-batch uniformity of the microneedle arrays and this highly reproducible insertion protocol, the sensor responses could be directly quantified using a universal pre-established calibration curve, without the need for additional session-by-session mathematical normalization or recalibration.

In vivo data acquisition, calibration, and time-lag handling

Signal Normalization: To eliminate baseline fluctuations across different individuals caused by variations in skin impedance or contact pressure, the net photocurrent step (ΔI) was employed as the characteristic analytical signal, defined as ΔI = Iphoto - Idark. In Vivo Calibration Protocol: The in vivo UA concentrations were determined using a single- or multi-point calibration method, wherein the measured ΔI was directly mapped to the reference uric acid levels acquired from tail-vein blood. Physiological Time-Lag Compensation: Considering the inherent physiological delay (typically 30 min) required for metabolites to diffuse from the systemic blood circulation into the dermal interstitial fluid (ISF), a staggered sampling strategy was adopted in the experimental protocol. Specifically, tail-vein blood was collected first, followed by PEC signal recording within the predefined delay time window. This temporal optimization ensures that the ISF chemical signal readouts are kinetically aligned with the reference blood concentrations, thereby significantly enhancing the accuracy of the in vivo monitoring.

ICP-MS elemental analysis of tissues and organs

To quantitatively evaluate the potential in vivo accumulation of functional nanomaterials, a systematic elemental analysis was performed following the 7-week monitoring study. Rats from both the experimental (microneedle-treated) and control groups were sacrificed, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) as well as the local skin tissue at the application site were harvested. All tissue samples were precisely weighed and transferred into clean porcelain crucibles. The samples were then subjected to thermal ashing in a muffle furnace at 550 °C for 6 h to completely decompose the organic matrix. The resulting ash was thoroughly digested with aqua regia (a 1:3 mixture of concentrated HNO3 and HCl, trace metal grade) under heating until the solution became clear. After cooling, the digested solutions were diluted with deionized water to a constant volume and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The total concentrations of Silver (Ag) and Titanium (Ti) were determined using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS, Agilent 7800). All measurements were performed in triplicate to ensure statistical accuracy.

Mass spectrometry characterization of reaction products

Qualitative identification of the PEC oxidation products of UA was performed using a quadrupole ultrahigh resolution liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry system (Thermo Fisher QE) equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in negative ion mode. The reaction solution was introduced into the system for the qualitative verification of the target product. Key mass spectrometry parameters included: negative ion spray voltage of 3.0 kV, ion transfer tube temperature of 300 °C, vaporizer temperature of 300 °C, sheath gas (N2) flow rate of 40.9 L min−1, auxiliary gas (N2) flow rate of 5.08 L min−1, and argon as collision gas. Data acquisition employed selective reaction monitoring mode to confirm the formation of the oxidation product (allantoin) based on its characteristic mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios.

Statistics and reproducibility

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.) unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses, curve fitting and graph generation were performed using Origin software. The number and nature of replicates, including technical replicates, independently fabricated sensors, independent microneedle patches, independent material batches and independent animals, are specified in the corresponding figure legends. Representative images or curves were selected from experiments that were independently repeated at least three times with similar results, unless otherwise stated. Sample sizes were chosen based on preliminary experiments, previous studies using microneedle or wearable biosensing systems, and practical and ethical considerations for animal experiments. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analysis unless explicitly stated. For in vitro sensor measurements, independently fabricated sensors or independently prepared samples were used as indicated in the figure legends. For in vivo experiments, animals were allocated to control, disease-model or treatment groups according to the predefined experimental design. Formal randomization was not applied where group identity was determined by genotype, disease-model induction or treatment schedule. Blinding was not performed during device application and real-time signal acquisition because the experimental procedures required the operator to know the device condition, animal model or treatment status. However, data processing and statistical analyses were performed using the same predefined procedures and criteria across all groups. The correlation between wearable PEC signals and blood UA levels measured by a commercial analyzer was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. The strength and significance of the linear relationship were quantified by Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) and the corresponding P value. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Significance levels are indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ns, not significant.
