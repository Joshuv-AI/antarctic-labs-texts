From fusion partner to clinical practice: A treatment-oriented framework for transcription factor E3 (TFE3)-rearranged renal carcinoma
Authors: Jiangwei Man, Kangyu Wang, Yalong Zhang, Hao Wang, Li Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranscription factor E3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma (TFE3-rRCC) is a rare, fusion-driven malignancy characterized by marked biological heterogeneity, diagnostic complexity, and suboptimal clinical outcomes under standard RCC therapies. Although several molecular studies have highlighted the importance of fusion partner diversity, these findings remain fragmented and incompletely integrated into clinical practice. In this review, We conducted a comprehensive review of genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and clinical studies on TFE3-rRCC. We synthesized evidence from retrospective cohorts, multi-omics analyses, and preclinical models to elucidate the molecular structure of recurrent diseases, protein-level signaling pathways, tumor microenvironment status, and emerging modalities of treatment response. We found that TFE3-rRCC is a fusion-defined disease family with suggest substantially more structural variations than point mutations. The coding features of fusion partners result in functionally distinct modules that reshape transcriptional regulation, RNA processing, protein homeostasis, and mitochondrial quality control, resulting in reproducible protein-level subtypes and immune-vascular “fingerprints”. These features support diagnostic and therapeutic hypotheses based on fusion partner regulation, including varying sensitivities to angiogenesis-targeting and immune-targeting strategies. Further, we suggest a combined diagnostic pathway with respect to pathology, molecular tests, computational techniques, and a partner-guided clinical trial framework for future validation. Taken together, this review formalizes a fusion partner-informed framework that links molecular mechanism with clinical investigation. By defining evidentiary boundaries and validation pathways, it provides a roadmap for translating mechanistic insight into rigorous evidence-based management of TFE3-rRCC.Keywords: Biomolecular condensates, Proteogenomics, Renal cell carcinoma, TFE3

Introduction

Translocation and transcription factor E3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma (TFE3-rRCC) and related microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MiT)/TFE-driven entities constitute a biologically distinct and clinically challenging disease spectrum defined by gene fusions that reprogram transcriptional control, cellular metabolism, and stress-response pathways.1 TFE3-rRCC was first recognized as Xp11 translocation RCC in 2004 and grouped with MiT family translocation RCCs (including TFE3, MiTF, TFEB, and TFEC) in the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) classification; it was further defined in the 2022 WHO system as an independent subtype under “molecularly defined renal cancers”.2, 3, 4, 5 This inclusion underscores the growing recognition of the molecular and clinical significance of TFE3-rRCC. These tumors span a broad histological spectrum, occur across a wide age range, and are disproportionately represented in pediatric and young adult populations. Clinically, accurate diagnosis and effective treatment of TFE3-rRCC remain challenging due to their morphological heterogeneity, overlapping immunophenotypes, and variable clinical manifestations.6

TFE3-rRCC and other subtypes of RCCs cannot be reliably differentiated diagnostically using routine histopathology or immunohistochemistry (IHC), and molecular confirmation is essential; however, diagnostic procedures vary greatly among institutions and the fusion partner gene is not always reported. Patient outcomes in TFE3-rRCC remain unsatisfactory compared to those of clear-cell RCC receiving standard systemic therapies, which underscores the importance of biologically informed treatment.7

Accumulating genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic studies indicate that TFE3-rRCC is a distinct fusion-driven disease family in which structural variation, rather than point mutation burden, is the dominant oncogenic driver.8 Importantly, emerging evidence suggests that the fusion partner may influence downstream signaling programmes, tumor microenvironment (TME) states, and therapeutic responsiveness.9,10 However, these observations are fragmented across studies and have not been systematically integrated into a coherent conceptual framework.

In this review, we define the term “fusion partner code” as a conceptual framework in which the identity of the TFE3 fusion partner determines recurrent pathway circuitry, TME states, and associated therapeutic outcomes, thereby providing an interpretable basis for biological stratification. This framework also offers an interpretable basis for clinical stratification in TFE3-rRCC by formalizing a “fusion partner code” framework that links fusion partners to pathway phenotype, microenvironmental state, and ultimately therapeutic implication.

A narrative synthesis rather than a formal systematic review or meta-analysis of PubMed and Web of Science databases was conducted, covering publications up to March 2025. Search terms included TFE3, MiT/TFE family, RCC, fusion partner, proteogenomics, and TME. Priority was given to studies with genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, or integrative multi-omics data, and mechanistic investigations with functional validation and clinically annotated cohorts. Case reports and small descriptive series that addressed rare fusion partners or diagnostic challenges were included. Given the rarity of TFE3-rRCC, evidence from retrospective cohorts and preclinical models was considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. This study does not constitute a formal meta-analysis but aims to synthesize mechanistic and translational insights into a coherent, partner-informed conceptual framework.

Epidemiology and population features

Incidence and age distribution

TFE3-rRCC is a rare type of kidney cancer that has a distinctive age distribution pattern compared to other RCC subtypes. In adult populations, TFE3-rRCC is very rare, accounting for <2% of all adult RCC cases.6 In contrast, these tumors constitute a substantially higher proportion of pediatric and adolescent RCCs, with a frequency ranging from 20% to > 40% in some series.11,12 This bimodal age distribution underscores fundamental biological differences between TFE3-rRCC and conventional RCC subtypes driven predominantly by point mutations.

Across age groups, TFE3-rRCC tends to present at a younger median age than clear-cell RCC and is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, with regional lymph node involvement or distant metastases. However, clinical behavior is heterogeneous, with both indolent and aggressive clinical courses observed in both pediatric and adult cohorts. Differences in stage distribution, treatment exposure, and follow-up duration across studies complicate direct outcome comparisons, highlighting the need for age-stratified and coordinated epidemiological analyses.6,13,14

Sex bias and X-chromosome biology

Female predominance has been reported across multiple cohorts of TFE3-rRCC, in contrast to the male bias observed in most RCC subtypes.6,12 This sex imbalance is evident in both pediatric and adult populations and suggests a biological contribution beyond referral or diagnostic bias.

Recent genomic studies provide a mechanistic basis for this observation by implicating X-chromosome architecture and regulation.15 TFE3 is located on the X chromosome, and fusion events frequently involve balanced X-autosome translocations rather than copy-number gains or losses. In female cells, X-chromosome inactivation creates a unique genomic context in which such balanced rearrangements may be more readily tolerated without deleterious dosage effects. Consequently, cells harboring TFE3 fusions may retain viability and proliferative capacity, facilitating tumor initiation.16

Furthermore, analyses of X-inactivation status indicate that TFE3 fusion events often occur on the active X chromosome, preserving transcriptional competence of the fusion gene. These observations suggest that sex-specific chromosomal biology contributes to disease susceptibility, and support the classification of TFE3-rRCC as a fusion-driven entity shaped by structural variation rather than conventional mutational processes.

Molecular architecture of Translocation and transcription factor E3-rearranged renal cell carcinoma (TFE3-rRCC)

Genomic landscape and structural variation

TFE3-rRCC is characterized by recurrent chromosomal rearrangements involving the TFE3 locus that places it in a fusion-driven oncogenic paradigm distinct from conventional RCC subtypes. In contrast to clear-cell RCC, these tumors typically exhibit a low somatic mutational burden and a relatively “quiet” landscape of small sequence variants. Instead, structural alterations constitute the dominant genomic drivers.8

Copy-number analyses across multiple cohorts have identified recurrent chromosomal imbalances, including losses of 3p and 6q and gains of 1q, which are variably associated with adverse clinical outcomes.7,17 However, these alterations cannot adequately explain the marked biological heterogeneity observed among cases. Fusion partner diversity now exceeds two dozen events (e.g., alveolar soft part sarcoma chromosome region, candidate 1 [ASPSCR1], splicing factor proline and glutamine rich [SFPQ], non-POU domain-containing octamer-binding protein [NONO], proline-rich mitotic checkpoint control [PRCC], mediator complex subunit 15 [MED15], RNA-binding motif protein 10 [RBM10]), and partner identity exhibits reproducible associations with histologic patterns, immunophenotypes, and clinical behaviors.18

Importantly, these rearrangements typically preserve the TFE3 DNA-binding domain, enabling aberrant transcriptional activity while appending partner-derived functional modules. This architectural configuration provides a unifying genomic principle while allowing substantial downstream variability.19

Transcriptomic programs and pathway preferences

Unsupervised transcriptomic analyses have resolved multiple gene expression signatures, including angiogenic, stromal/extracellular matrix (ECM), proliferative/cell-cycle, and metabolic states, highlighting intertumoral heterogeneity that mutation burden alone cannot capture. Several studies further point to lysosome-mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling, oxidative phosphorylation bias, and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)-linked stress-response modules as recurrent signaling axes of pathway activity, suggesting that partner-specific wiring converges on a limited set of actionable circuits while diverging in degree and context. These transcriptional states co-vary with elements of the TME, including vascular signatures and immune infiltration, suggesting that partner identity indirectly shapes immune-vascular phenotypes and, by extension, therapeutic responsiveness.13,20

Collectively, these lines of evidence establish TFE3-rRCC as a fusion-defined disease in which structural rearrangements, rather than point mutations, are the dominant genomic drivers, and in which partner-specific biology is clinically meaningful. Together, TFE3-rRCC is structurally driven with architectural genomic alterations giving rise to diverse transcriptional programs, facilitating downstream protein-level and mechanistic analyses [Table 1].

Protein-level subtypes and signaling circuits

Proteogenomic subtypes

Although genomic and transcriptomic methods define the organizational and transcriptional maps of TFE3-rRCC, protein-based profiling is a direct readout of pathway activity and cellular state. Integrated proteogenomic studies have demonstrated considerable discordance between messenger RNA (mRNA) abundance and protein expression in TFE3-rRCC, indicating extensive post-transcriptional and post-translational regulation.21 Therefore, protein-focused studies may offer complementary insights that are not always apparent from RNA-based studies.

Unsupervised clustering of tumor proteomes has identified multiple protein-level subtypes, including groups enriched for immune and platelet–complement components, translation and proteostasis machinery, and oxidative metabolism. These subtypes are associated with differences in genetic instability, stress-response action, and clinical results, indicating that tumors can be stratified based on proteomic states in functionally significant dimensions. Notably, protein-level profiling provides an independent, complementary layer of tumor stratification and is not limited by transcriptomic clusters and histological patterns.8 In recent years, several large-scale genomic, transcriptomic, and multi-omics datasets have further refined the molecular landscape of TFE3-rearranged renal tumors. These studies, published between 2023 and 2025, integrated exome sequencing, RNA sequencing, proteomics, and phosphoproteomics to delineate pathway activity, signaling circuitry, and microenvironmental states at increasing resolution. A curated overview of these publicly available datasets is provided in Supplementary Table 1, serving as an updated evidence base for the proteogenomic and signaling analyses discussed in this section.

Kinase circuitry and pathway activity

Phosphoproteomic studies sharpened this functional stratification by identifying unique kinase signaling pathways involved in TFE3-rRCC. The inference of kinase-substrate relationship indicate recurrent activation of ribosomal S6 kinase (S6K/RPS6KB1), cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5), and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (AKT) axis pathways. These signaling nodes are consistent with improved translational output, cytoskeletal dynamics, and adaptation to stress, and mechanistically explain the transcriptional and metabolic rewiring at the RNA level.21

Interestingly, the patterns of kinase activity do not track DNA-level changes directly, indicating the significance of direct phosphoprotein signals. Instead, pathway activation reflects integrated inputs from fusion-driven transcriptional programs, cellular stress responses, and microenvironmental cues. These findings suggest that kinase circuitry in TFE3-rRCC is emergent rather than mutation-driven, with implications for formulating therapeutic strategies.8

Diagnostic protein biomarkers

Beyond pathway analysis, proteomic studies have found candidate protein biomarkers with potential diagnostic utility. Proteins such as dystonin (DST) and TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) are overexpressed in TFE3-rRCC compared to other RCC subtypes, which has been validated using IHC assays in independent cohorts. More recently, TRIM63 has emerged as a sensitive screening marker capable of identifying cases with cryptic or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)-negative TFE3 rearrangements, further supporting a protein-level approach to diagnosis21, 22, 23.

However, the clinical use of these biomarkers remains constrained by the lack of standardized thresholds, prospective validation, and inter-laboratory harmonization. Consequently, protein biomarkers should currently be viewed as adjuncts to, rather than replacements for, molecular confirmation. Together, proteogenomic and phosphoproteomic analyses position TFE3-rRCC as a signaling-centric malignancy, in which protein-level profile and kinase circuitry provide critical insight beyond genomic architecture alone.24

Mechanistic foundations of the fusion partner code

Recurrent fusion-partner classes converge on a set of modular, testable processes that mechanistic explanations for circuit-level phenotypes and avenues for therapeutic exploration.

First, splicing-factor fusions (SFPQ-/NONO-/PRCC-TFE3) retain the TFE3 basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH)-leucine zipper while appending RNA-binding and scaffolding domains, promoting aberrant di- or oligomerization at active chromatin. These assemblies physically couple transcription with co-transcriptional splicing, broadening target engagement and accelerating cell-state transitions. Such coupling is predicted to generate characteristic splicing signatures, detectable by junction-centric RNA-seq, and suggests vulnerabilities at dimer interfaces or spliceosome-transcription factor contacts.7,8,25

Second, PRCC-TFE3 activates Parkin RBR E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (PRKN)-dependent mitophagy under oxidative stress by engaging the PRKN/PPARG coactivator 1 alpha (PGC-1α)-nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1) axis, thereby preserving mitochondrial quality and oxidative phosphorylation. While this program supports redox resilience, it also creates context-specific sensitivities to mitophagy disruption, mitochondrial biogenesis constraint, or respiratory stress.26,27

Third, ASPSCR1-TFE3 couples transcription to proteostasis through functional dependence on the AAA + ATPase valosin-containing protein (VCP)/p97, sustaining transcriptional output. Although VCP/p97 inhibition suppresses oncogenic transcription, therapeutic translation requires strategies that balance efficacy with tolerability.28,29

Fourth, multiple TFE3 oncofusions form RNA-mediated nuclear condensates that concentrate RNA polymerase II (Pol II) and co-activators at target loci, acting as biophysical amplifiers of transcription. These structures exhibit liquid-like properties and suggest condensate integrity as a druggable feature, with pharmacodynamic readouts including condensate dissolution and redistribution of transcriptional machinery.10

Finally, non-coding and epigenetic programs, including long non-coding RNA (lncRNA)-like nicotinamide riboside kinase 2 (NMRK2) transcripts and TRAF3IP2-AS1 promoter states, modulate metabolic and DNA-repair pathways, adding regulatory plasticity to fusion-driven circuits and informing biomarker-guided intervention.30

Beyond tumor-intrinsic circuitry, fusion partners impact distinct TME states, influencing angiogenesis, ECM organization, and immune composition. Together, mapping fusion partners to modular processes provides a coherent framework to align mechanism, biomarkers, and partner-informed therapeutic strategies.

Tumor microenvironment (TME) and immune-vascular fingerprints

The fusion partner-encoded mechanisms described above do not operate in isolation but propagate beyond tumor-intrinsic circuitry to shape the TME. Transcriptional rewiring, metabolic adaptation, and proteostasis or mitochondrial dependencies imposed by distinct TFE3 fusions influence angiogenesis, ECM organisation, and immune cell states. Together, these features form reproducible immune-vascular “fingerprints” that link intracellular mechanisms to clinically relevant phenotypes.

Immune contexture and checkpoint biology

Integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses indicate that TFE3-rRCC exhibits heterogeneous immune landscapes. Many cases display CD8+ T-cell infiltration accompanied by exhaustion-associated signatures, including upregulation of inhibitory receptors and stress-response pathways. Variable expression of immune checkpoint molecules, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), has been reported across tumor and myeloid compartments, suggesting context-dependent immune engagement rather than consistent immune evasion.31

Notably, activation of stress-adaptive programs, such as NRF2-linked responses and metabolic rewiring, may attenuate antitumor immunity by altering redox balance and nutrient availability within the TME. These immune features are consistent with the protein-level subtypes in Section 5, reinforcing the notion that immune states are emergent properties of fusion-driven signaling rather than independent variables.

Angiogenesis, extracellular matrix (ECM) topology, and stromal interactions

Angiogenic signaling represents a prominent microenvironmental axis in a subset of TFE3-rRCC. Transcriptomic enrichment of vascular growth factors and proteomic evidence of endothelial and stromal activation are accompanied by ECM remodeling, including altered collagen organization and increased vascular density. These features reflect downstream consequences of fusion-driven transcriptional amplification and stress adaptation, particularly under high metabolic demand.32

Proteome-defined subtypes enriched in platelet-complement and stromal components are linked to poorer clinical outcomes, implicating thrombo-inflammatory and matrix-related processes in disease progression. These stromal features also influence immune cell trafficking and drug delivery, highlighting the interplay of vascular and immune microenvironments.

Partner-specific microenvironmental states

Importantly, microenvironmental fingerprints vary based on fusion partner identity. Angiogenic and inflammatory programs are frequently co-enriched in ASPSCR1-TFE3 tumors, characterized by vascular proliferation, ECM remodeling, and immune infiltration. This provides a biological rationale for retrospective observations suggesting differential responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based combinations in this subgroup.31

In contrast, tumors harboring splicing-factor fusions, such as SFPQ- or NONO-TFE3, have less inflamed microenvironments and greater reliance on metabolic and stress-response pathways, consistent with the mechanistic dependencies outlined in Section 6. These distinctions suggest that immune-vascular fingerprints serve as an intermediate layer linking fusion partner-encoded mechanisms to therapeutic hypotheses.

Collectively, the TME in TFE3-rRCC is an amalgam of fusion-driven transcriptional, metabolic, and proteostatic programs. Recognition of partner-specific immune-vascular states conceptually connects molecular mechanism to clinical stratification, and informs subsequent discussion of diagnostic workflows and treatment responses.

Diagnostic workflow and partner-resolved classification

Pathology and immunohistochemical screening

Given the morphological heterogeneity of rRCC and the overlap of melanocytic and renal immunophenotypes, accurate diagnosis requires an integrated, stepwise pipeline that pairs morphology with orthogonal molecular confirmation.33 Although substantial overlap limits stand-alone diagnostic use, certain morphologic tendencies exist across common TFE3 fusion partners. ASPSCR1-TFE3 tumors often show alveolar or pseudopapillary architecture with large polygonal cells, abundant cytoplasm and frequent gritty calcifications. PRCC-TFE3 frequently displays compact chaotic nests composed of smaller cells with scant cytoplasm. SFPQ-TFE3 may show alveolar architecture with subnuclear vacuolation and occasional papillary elements. NONO-TFE3 frequently exhibits glandular/tubular or papillary patterns, sometimes biphasic, with round uniform nuclei and endometrioid-type subnuclear vacuolation. These partner-linked patterns are hypothesis-generating and warrant molecular confirmation (FISH/RNA-seq). Emerging retrospective data also suggest partner identity may modulate therapeutic behavior (such as angiogenic–inflamed programs in ASPSCR1-TFE3), although prospective validation remains lacking.34

IHC serves as a practical first-line screening tool for TFE3. TFE3-rRCC often shows reduced or focal epithelial marker staining (cytokeratin, epithelial membrane antigen [EMA]) with frequent tubular-lineage paired box gene 8 (PAX8) positivity.35 Melanocytic and other markers (HMB45, Melan-A, S100, CD117, MOC-31, P504S) are variably expressed and lack specificity for TFE3-rRCC.36 Cathepsin K is positive in approximately half of the cases (approximately 50%–70%) and its expression can be partner-associated (e.g., enriched in PRCC-TFE3), but is not entirely specific (positivity may occur in eosinophilic solid and cystic RCC and eosinophilic tumors with tuberous sclerosis complex [TSC]/mTOR alterations).25 Additional supportive markers include glycoprotein non-metastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB), tripartite motif- containing 63 (TRIM63), and NMRK2 transcripts.37,38 TRIM63 RNA in situ hybridization (RNA ISH) has emerged as a useful adjunct screening biomarker for MiT/TFE-altered renal tumors. In morphologically suspicious cases with negative or equivocal TFE3/TFEB break-apart FISH, TRIM63 positivity enriched for subsequent genomic confirmation of MiT/TFE aberrations, including cryptic inversions missed by FISH.22 Although these markers increase clinical suspicion, they are not specific and warrant further molecular confirmation. These IHC readouts can increase clinical suspicion but FISH is required to confirm rearrangement and RNA-seq is currently the most reliable approach to resolve fusion partners. However, performance is contingent on pre-analytic variables (fixation time, decalcification), epitope retrieval conditions (buffer pH, heat protocol), and clone-specific characteristics, all of which contribute to site-to-site variability in sensitivity and specificity.39 Accordingly, reports should specify clone, retrieval method, scoring rubric, and positivity thresholds, and TFE3 IHC should be considered a screening tool rather than a stand-alone determinant.

Molecular confirmation using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and RNA sequencing

In routine practice, FISH using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue is the most widely used confirmatory assay for TFE3-rRCC.40 Over the past decade, TFE3 break-apart FISH has become integral to diagnostic workflows because of its short turnaround time, cost-effectiveness and high analytical sensitivity/specificity. Standard break-apart FISH confirms rearrangement but does not identify the fusion partner; when a specific partner is suspected, targeted dual-fusion probes may help, while RNA sequencing most reliably resolves partner identity and cryptic/intrachromosomal events. Among nucleic-acid approaches, targeted or hybrid-capture RNA sequencing most reliably identifies the fusion partner and breakpoint and can uncover cryptic or rare configurations, thereby enabling biologically anchored reporting that supports clinical-trial matching and partner-stratified management.41 Best practice is to predefine RNA quality metrics (e.g., RNA integrity number [RIN]), junction-read support, and orthogonal confirmation rules for discordant cases. RNA-based next-generation sequencing (NGS) is used for partner resolution and cryptic events.42 Genetic testing provides comprehensive, clinically actionable information in TFE3-rRCC. For example, Zhao et al.43 reported protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type D (PTPRD) alterations with reduced expression, underscoring the value of integrated genomics. While break-apart FISH is the most widely used confirmatory assay for TFE3 rearrangement, it does not identify the fusion partner and may under-detect intrachromosomal inversions or complex events. By contrast, hybrid-capture or anchored multiplex PCR (RNA-seq NGS) can detect known and novel TFE3 fusions, provide breakpoint-level evidence and annotate co-occurring alterations, thereby identifying fusion partner and supporting treatment selection. These advantages complement, rather than replace, FISH; equivocal or negative FISH in high-suspicion cases should prompt reflex RNA-NGS testing.

Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted pathology and integrated diagnostic pipelines

Recent advances in computational pathology offer an additional layer of diagnostic support. Deep learning models trained on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained whole-slide images can enrich for suspected TFE3-rRCC, serving as an upstream triage tool that prompts reflex molecular testing. When combined with molecular confirmation, such approaches may enhance diagnostic consistency and quality control, particularly in high-throughput or multicenter settings.44,45

Together, these modalities form a partner-resolved diagnostic pipeline in which morphology, IHC, molecular assays, and AI-assisted tools are integrated to enable biologically anchored classification.

Complementing molecular tests, computational pathology models trained on H&E whole-slide images can enrich for suspected rRCC with externally validated area under the curve (AUC) of approximately 0.84–0.89, positioning AI as an upstream triage that triggers reflex molecular testing and provides quality-control signals for large workflows (domain shift monitoring, batch drift).46 Current consensus algorithms therefore proceed as follows: starting with morphology, with or without melanocytic or renal markers, followed by TFE3 IHC for screening, then fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for confirmation of rearrangement, and finally RNA sequencing for partner assignment, with explicit acknowledgment of each test's limitations in the final report [Figure 1].

Practical considerations for clinical implementation

While fusion partner-informed frameworks provide important biological insights, their application in routine clinical practice must account for real-world constraints. In centers without access to routine RNA sequencing, partner coding should be regarded as probabilistic rather than definitive. A stepwise diagnostic approach combining morphology, TFE3 IHC, and TFE3 break-apart FISH can reliably identify TFE3-rRCC, even when precise fusion partners cannot be resolved. In such settings, partner coding may primarily serve as a standardized reporting framework and a basis for referral to specialized centers or trial enrolment, rather than as a direct guide for treatment selection.47

Importantly, only a subset of biomarkers discussed in this review are currently suitable for routine clinical use. Established assays such as TFE3 IHC and break-apart FISH are widely available and clinically validated, whereas RNA sequencing, proteogenomic readouts, and functional indicators of condensates or proteostasis dependency remain largely confined to specialized or research settings. Clinically actionable biomarkers should be clearly distinguished from research-stage tools to avoid premature clinical extrapolation. With increasing sequencing capacity and prospective validation, partner-resolved classification may progressively transition from a conceptual framework to a more operational component of precision oncology workflows.48

Therapeutic responses and emerging targets

Management of localized resectable TFE3-rRCC is similar to that of conventional clear-cell RCC, surgery remains the standard of care; no validated partner-stratified adjuvant or neoadjuvant approaches are available. Accordingly, this section focuses on systemic therapy for advanced/metastatic TFE3-rRCC, emphasizing partner-modulated outcomes and mechanism-informed combinations.24

Current systemic treatment strategies

In recent years, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (VEGFR-TKIs) combined with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade have become a viable option for metastatic TFE3-rRCC. Retrospective series suggest a trend toward improved efficacy with ICI-TKI than with non-ICI regimens (e.g., sunitinib alone or historical immunotherapy), and pooled real-world analyses report median progression-free survival (PFS) of approximately 5.4–9.4 months and OS of approximately 12.4–24.4 months with conventional first-line TKIs, and longer PFS for ICI-TKI combinations in some cohorts.49 Although KEYNOTE-426 demonstrated superiority of pembrolizumab-axitinib over sunitinib in treatment-naïve advanced RCC overall, this was not specific to TFE3-rRCC and should be viewed as supportive/extrapolative evidence.50 Case-level signals (e.g., mesenchymal epithelial transition (MET)-directed TKI plus axitinib, or camrelizumab plus axitinib with durable complete response [CR]) indicate feasibility in Xp11.2/TFE3-fusion RCC.43 Mechanistically, VEGFR-TKIs may normalize tumor vasculature and reduce immunosuppressive cues, while ICIs reinvigorate antitumor immunity, providing a rationale for combination therapy. Prospective, partner-stratified trials are needed to confirm the beneficial effects and define optimal regimens.

Partner-modulated therapeutic signals

Retrospective and real-world studies suggest that fusion partner identity may modulate therapeutic responsiveness. In particular, ASPSCR1-TFE3 tumors frequently exhibit angiogenic and inflammatory immune-vascular fingerprints, providing a biological rationale for prioritizing ICI-TKI combinations in this subgroup. Several retrospective cohorts report improved PFS and response rates with combination therapy compared to non-ICI strategies, although these observations remain hypothesis-generating and require prospective confirmation.19

By contrast, tumors harboring splicing-factor fusions, such as SFPQ- or NONO-TFE3, tend to display less inflamed microenvironments and greater reliance on metabolic and stress-response pathways. In these contexts, conventional immunotherapy may be less effective, and alternative strategies incorporating targeted agents or metabolic modulation warrant exploration within clinical trials.

Translational barriers and emerging targets

Despite a strong mechanistic rationale, several new targets in TFE3-rRCC have significant translational constraints, as exemplified by VCP/p97 inhibitors. Although VCP/p97 is functionally required by ASPSCR1-TFE3 to maintain oncogenic transcription, an early clinical trial with CB-5083 discontinued treatment because of dose-limiting toxicity and limited therapeutic index. These results highlight that pathway dependency does not imply clinical tractability when the target is ubiquitously essential for cellular proteostasis, whereby VCP/p97 inhibition primarily serves as a mechanistic proof-of-concept.51

There are other challenges of targeting RNA-mediated transcriptional condensates. Targeting RNA-mediated transcriptional condensates presents additional challenges. Condensates are dynamic multivalent structures that resemble biophysically important nuclear structures, raising concerns regarding selectivity and systemic toxicity. Currently, condensate-disrupting interventions are very primitive, and have low pharmacological maturity.52

Collectively, these constraints support a shift toward rational combination strategies. Compensatory resistance to monotherapy occurs in TFE3-rRCC wherein metabolic adaptation, stress-response pathways, and immune regulation processes are inextricably connected. Metabolic or mitochondrial disruption in the TME can enhance immuno-power and may improve therapeutic efficacy. To test these strategies, fusion partner-stratified clinical trials should be performed, without any premature clinical extrapolation.

Partner-stratified clinical trial design framework

The significant biological heterogeneity of TFE3-rRCC limits the interpretability of unstratified clinical trials. To facilitate translational progress without premature clinical extrapolation, we propose a partner-stratified clinical trial design framework in which fusion partner identity is treated as a biological hypothesis guiding trial structure rather than a directive for routine therapy selection.

Within this framework, distinct fusion partners nominate different pathway-level dependencies and TME states that can be prospectively tested. ASPSCR1-TFE3 tumors consistently exhibit angiogenic and inflammatory programs across transcriptomic and proteogenomic studies, in accordance with retrospective signals, suggesting benefit from ICI-TKI combinations. Accordingly, ASPSCR1-defined trial arms may prioritize ICI-TKI-based hypotheses for formal testing. In contrast, splicing-factor fusions such as SFPQ- or NONO-TFE3 are associated with metabolic rewiring and the mTOR pathway, prompting exploration of mTOR-centered or metabolism-modulating strategies in biologically enriched cohorts. PRCC-TFE3 fusions further implicate PRKN-dependent mitophagy and mitochondrial quality control, suggesting the potential of metabolic or mitophagy-perturbing interventions under defined stress conditions.19

Multicenter umbrella trials represent an optimal design to test these hypotheses in rare tumors because they favor fusion partner-defined arms, equal control groups, adaptive randomization, and built-in pharmacodynamic endpoints, which are used to measure pathway activity. The approach to rare or heterogeneous fusion partners may be through either exploratory or registry-based arms, which offers a structured pathway of aligning molecular insight on evidence-based strict and informative clinical study [Table 3].

Current limitations and methodological challenges

Even with enhanced molecular understanding, reliable inference and clinical translation in TFE3-rRCC remain lacking. Small retrospective and single-center datasets are primarily used, reducing statistical power for fusion partner-specific analyses. In addition, representation is skewed by age, stage, and ancestry, while treatment exposure and outcome analyses are frequently confounded by selection bias and heterogenous therapeutic regimens.21

Second, the diagnostic variability remains a substantive issue. The TFE3 IHC is pre-analytically sensitive, sensitive to selection of antibody clones, and scoring threshold, leading to high inter-laboratory variation. Break-apart FISH reliably confirms rearrangement but may miss cryptic inversions or complex events and does not resolve fusion partner identity. Although RNA-based assays provide the most accurate partner identification, their availability, turnaround time, and reimbursement vary, and partner information is not yet uniformly incorporated into routine clinical reporting.53

Third, a gap persists between mechanistic discovery and clinical translation. Proposed partner-encoded liabilities, including proteostasis dependence, splice-transcription coupling, condensate integrity, and PRKN-dependent mitophagy, are largely supported by preclinical evidence. Pharmacologic selectivity, toxicity, on-target pharmacodynamics, and generalizability across tumors remain insufficiently characterized.54

Fourth, biological heterogeneity is incompletely resolved. Associations linking fusion partners to pathway activity, TME states, and therapeutic outcomes are predominantly hypothesis-generating and often derived from small subgroups, raising concerns regarding reproducibility. Protein-level subtypes and candidate biomarkers therefore require prospective validation using harmonized thresholds.

Finally, spatial and temporal dimensions remain underexplored. Few studies integrate spatial or single-cell profiling with proteogenomics and quantitative pathology, and longitudinal sampling across treatment courses and metastatic sites is rare. In addition, standardized quality control, reference materials, and minimum-information reporting are inconsistently implemented, limiting multicenter data aggregation and real-world evidence generation.

Importantly, the partner-informed framework proposed in this review is supported predominantly by retrospective cohorts and translational studies and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-defining. Rigorous validation will require partner-stratified prospective studies, including randomized or adaptive umbrella trial designs where feasible, with embedded pharmacodynamic and biomarker-driven endpoints. Given the rarity of TFE3-rRCC, international collaborative platforms, such as a dedicated TFE3-rRCC registry with standardized molecular and clinical data, will be essential to enable reproducible validation and translation into evidence-based clinical practice.

Future directions and research agenda

Future progress will require coordinated advances in cohort design, diagnostics, mechanistic validation, and clinical trial architecture. A key priority is to operationalize a fusion partner-resolved stratification framework that links partner identity to pathway phenotype, TME state, and testable therapeutic hypotheses. Routine partner-resolved reporting, with transparent annotation of assay type and evidence strength, should be adopted across registries, tumor boards, and clinical studies.

Prospective multicenter cohorts and platform trials are needed to validate partner-specific associations. Given the disease rarity, adaptive and Bayesian designs, shared control arms, and incorporation of pharmacodynamic endpoints are essential to maximize efficiency while preserving interpretability. Partner pre-specification should be embedded at enrolment rather than applied post hoc.32

Another pivotal direction is mechanism-led therapeutic development. Translational programs should prioritize improving selectivity and tolerability for proteostasis-axis modulation, developing tractable approaches to dimerization or splice-transcription interfaces, and establishing actionable pharmacology for condensate-associated vulnerabilities. Mechanism-faithful readouts of each strategy, e.g., chromatin occupancy, nascent transcription or mitochondrial quality-control measures, should be provided to make early go/no-go decisions.51,52

Translating proteogenomic knowledge into clinically feasible assays remains a critical need. These assays should be developed with standardized scoring rubrics, inter-laboratory calibration, and prospective validation, phospho-signature surrogates or targeted IHC with highly specific panels should be used to assess pathway state including DNA or RNA alone.55

Finally, the integration of spatial and single-cell multi-omics with quantitative digital pathology will be critical to map immune-vascular architecture, ECM topology, and drug-penetration barriers in a partner-aware manner. Longitudinal sampling before and after therapy, and across primary and metastatic sites, should be incorporated to define evolutionary trajectories and resistance mechanisms. Parallel efforts to establish consensus minimum-information standards, external quality assessment, and international partner-coded registries will provide the infrastructure required for scalable validation and guideline-level evidence generation.56,57

Conclusions

TFE3-rRCC is a fusion-driven disease family in which structural variation, rather than point mutation burden, defines biological behavior, microenvironmental context, and therapeutic response. Across genomic, proteogenomic, and mechanistic studies, fusion partner identity emerges as a unifying axis linking molecular circuitry to immune-vascular states and clinically relevant phenotypes.

In this review, we synthesize these diverse lines of evidence into a partner-informed framework that integrates molecular architecture, protein-level signaling, mechanistic liabilities, TME fingerprints, and therapeutic response patterns. By formalizing the concept of a fusion partner code, we provide an interpretable schema that connects upstream fusion biology to downstream clinical decision-making. This framework underlies the integrated diagnostic and treatment algorithm illustrated in Figure 1, in which partner-resolved classification informs therapy selection, response monitoring, and validation pathways.
