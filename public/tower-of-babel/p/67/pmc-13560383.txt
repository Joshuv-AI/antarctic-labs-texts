Distinct cytokine and chemokine alterations in bronchoalveolar fluid from patients with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis associated lung disease (SJIA-LD)
Authors: Ivanna Romankevych, Alyssa Sproles, Thuy Do, Lexi Auld, Taskin Sabit, Richard Chhaing, Elizabeth Baker, Ashish Kumar, Bruce Trapnell, Brenna Carey, John J Brewington, Christopher Towe, Alexei Grom, Grant Schulert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung disease associated with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (SJIA-LD) remains poorly understood. Evaluation of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) may better reflect disease pathogenesis. The objectives of our study were to measure levels of cytokines and chemokines in BALF and their associations with clinical features and treatment in patients with SJIA-LD.MethodsChildren with SJIA-LD undergoing clinically indicated diagnostic bronchoscopy were enrolled. Comparator BALF was collected from patients with other chronic inflammatory and non-inflammatory lung diseases. BALF was assayed for IL-6, 8 and 18, S100A8/9, S100A12, sCD25, CCL11, CCL17, CCL25, MMP7, and CXCL9.ResultsBALF was obtained from 21 patients with SJIA-LD and 54 comparator patients with other lung diseases. Compared to all controls, children with SJIA-LD had a significant elevation of IL-18 (median (IQR) 1406 (722.3–2812) vs 37.2 (25.3–70) pg/mL, p < 0.0001), S100A8/9 (4745.3 (3003–11506) vs. 1297.5 (260–7755) ng/mL, p = 0.02), sCD25 (31.6 (19–50.3) vs. 13.6 (8.6–37.3) pg/mL, p = 0.04), MMP-7 (18,466.7 (10,462.2–33,516.1) vs. 13.645 (8.6–37.3) pg/mL, p = 0.0384), and CCL17 (0 (0–63.1; p = 0.038) vs 0 (0–0) pg/mL. BALF IL-18 was significantly higher in children with SJIA-LD compared to all control subgroups (inflammatory and non-inflammatory airway disease, autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, and poorly controlled asthma), in patients who were actively treated with anti-cytokine biologics (N = 9), and in patients who ultimately underwent hematopoietic stem cell transfer (N = 8). Patients receiving anti-cytokine biologics also had significant elevations in several other cytokines compared to patients without such treatments, while profiles in those treated with JAKi (N = 14) were largely similar. Linear regression analysis showed an association between BALF level of IL-18 and the number of immunosuppressive medications utilized (p = 0.0167), but not with O2 requirement, dose of anakinra or prednisone, plasma IL-18, ferritin, CRP or ESR.ConclusionsPatients with SJIA-LD showed a distinct BALF cytokine profile, including significant IL-18, S100A8/9 protein, sCD25, and CCL-17 elevation. The level of IL-18 was higher in patients who required more immunosuppressive medications and were actively treated with anti-cytokine biologics. Introduction

Lung disease associated with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (SJIA-LD) is a rare but potentially fatal disease complication that occurs in approximately 7–8% of children with SJIA [1, 2]. Mortality from SJIA-LD ranges from 10 to 68%, with nearly half of patients progressing to require respiratory support or supplemental oxygen [3, 4]. SJIA-LD is considered a diffuse lung disease characterized by interstitial inflammation, development of endogenous lipoid pneumonia/pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, variable fibrosis, and pulmonary hypertension [1].

The recognition of SJIA-LD increased concurrently with the introduction of anti-cytokine biological agents, specifically anti-IL-1 and IL-6 biologics, raising concern for potential causative links [5]. However, the course of SJIA-LD and its response to treatment are highly variable and not clearly connected to ongoing use of biologics [4]. There are increasing reports of SJIA-LD responding well to treatment with JAK inhibitors, suggesting other cytokine pathways may be involved [6, 7]. In some highly refractory cases, patients have required an allogeneic bone marrow transplant [8].

Despite recent research, the pathogenesis of SJIA-LD remains unclear. Inflammatory cytokines IL-1, IL-6 and IL-18 play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of SJIA. However, use of anti-IL-1 and anti-IL-6 biologics does not provide full disease control in 20–30% patients [9, 10], and does not appear to prevent SJIA-LD. Prior work has supported a possible role for interferon-γ (IFN-γ) in patients with SJIA-LD [11], as well as elevations of other circulating biomarkers such as an intercellular adhesion molecule 5, matrix metalloproteinase 7 (MMP-7), and fibrotic or allergic chemokines [12]. However, the majority of studies in SJIA-LD have been blood-based, which, while reflecting systemic inflammation, may not reflect the inflammatory environment in the lungs. Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) collection allows obtaining a target organ-specific sample from the lower airways of the lung [13]. While BALF cytology analysis is widely used clinically to study pulmonary conditions, the measurement of cytokines in BALF can show important changes and different aspects of the inflammatory process in the lungs.

There is limited data examining BALF from children with SJIA-LD. Our prior work with a small cohort of SJIA-LD patients found significant elevations of IL-18, as well as elevated IFN-γ-induced chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10 in a subset of patients [1]. The presence of other inflammatory and non-inflammatory cytokines in SJIA-LD BALF has not been determined, and whether these may be specific compared to other inflammatory lung diseases is unclear.

The objective of this study was to determine levels of proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in SJIA-LD, their relationships with features of SJIA and SJIA-LD, and their specificity for other lung diseases. We also examined differences in inflammatory profiles based on specific medications utilized, such as anti-cytokine biologics and JAK inhibitors.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a single-center cohort study performed at the Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Center. This study was approved by the CCHMC Institutional Review Board (IRB) (2018–2408), and informed consent was obtained from all patients or parents/guardians. All SJIA patients were diagnosed based on the modified International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) diagnostic criteria [14], and all had a refractory course of disease based on suggested criteria [9].

The presence of SJIA-LD was confirmed based on characteristic findings on high-resolution chest computed tomography (HRCCT). We defined the course of SJIA-LD as progressive if worsening of respiratory symptoms (shortness of breath, tachypnea, increased oxygen demand, desaturation), decline in lung function confirmed by spirometry and/or 6-min walking test (if applicable), and radiological progression (new finding consistent with interstitial lung disease on HRCCT) were present.

As comparators, leftover BALF samples were also obtained from the Cincinnati Children’s Pulmonary Biorepository (CCHMC #2013–3309) and the Rare Lung Disease Repository (CCHMC# 2018–0852) from patients with airway disease, autoimmune pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (aPAP), and poorly controlled asthma. The airway disease group included children who underwent diagnostic or surveillance bronchoscopy for a tracheostomy tube, chronic cough, bronchiectasis, or aspiration. Based on the reasons for bronchoscopy, samples obtained from children with airway disease were conditionally divided into two groups: inflammatory controls (IC; children with chronic cough, aspiration, and bronchiectasis) and non-inflammatory controls (NIC; children with tracheostomy tubes and subglottic stenosis undergoing surveillance bronchoscopy).

BALF samples collection

All BALF were remnants procured from patients undergoing a clinically indicated bronchoscopy procedure (pre-BMT evaluation, ruling out underlying infection in cases of worsening of respiratory status, or therapeutic bronchoalveolar lavage). BALF was collected through a standardized protocol under general anesthesia. In brief, a flexible bronchoscope is introduced into a segmental bronchus until wedged, and normal saline is instilled in 10–20 ml aliquots based on age and weight. The BALF was then suctioned back through the scope into a sterile trap, decanted into a Falcon tube, and placed on ice. For processing, cell counts were performed, and BALF samples were centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min. Supernatant was aliquoted (500 μl) and banked with and without protease inhibitors (DMSO/PMSF) at −80 °C.

Measurement of cytokine and chemokine levels in blood and BALF

The measurement of cytokine and chemokine levels in BALF samples was performed by the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method for IL-18 (Invitrogen), S100A8/9 (R&D System) and S100A12 (MBL Life Science) proteins and human 8 plex Luminex (R&D Systems) for IL-6, IL-8, soluble CD25 (sCD25), CCL11, CCL17 and CXCL9 in duplicates according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytokine levels that were not detected by the plex system were reported as undetectable.

For most SJIA-LD patients, the IL-18, S100A8/9, S100A12, and CXCL9 were measured in the serum by ELISA as well. The blood samples were obtained for clinical reasons (disease activity assessment) on the same day as BALF collection or within 3 days before or after bronchoscopy.

Data analysis

We used parametric statistics (mean with standard error (SE)) for analysis of main clinical characteristics, such as age, disease, and lung disease durations, etc. Non-parametric statistics (median with interquartile range (IQR)) was used to analyze cytokine and chemokine levels in BALF due to non-normal data distribution, as confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. The mean ± SE was used for a better characterization of the mean, specifically its precision. Non-parametric Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied for multiple comparisons of data among the groups. P-value < 0.05 determined a statistically significant difference between groups. Linear regression analysis was applied to assess the possible connection between clinical variables and levels of cytokines and chemokines in BALF.

Results

Patients and comparison with chronic lung disease

We obtained BALF from 21 unique patients with SJIA-LD during the study period from 2019 to 2025 (Table 1). Full demographic and clinical data were available for 17 patients, while for one patient, only demographic data were available. Three additional patients were fully deidentified with no accessible clinical information and, therefore, could not be included in linear regression analysis and treatment group comparisons. The SJIA-LD patients were majority (67%) female and had a mean age of 6 ± 1.2 years (M ± SE), a mean SJIA duration of 4 ± 0.6 years, and a mean LD duration of 2.9 ± 0.6 years. All patients had a refractory course of SJIA, and 13 (76.5%) had progressive LD at the time of BALF collection. One patient (5.9%) had Trisomy 21. Eight (47%) of the 17 patients required oxygen (O2) supplementation at 0.5–2 L/min, including one patient who required bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) (Table 1). An underlying infection as a cause of worsening respiratory status in children with SJIA-LD was ruled out using cell count and differential in BALF, as well as viral, bacterial, and fungal cultures. Medication treatments at the time of BALF collection are summarized in Table 1; all patients except one (5.9%) received treatment with 2 or more immunosuppressive medications. All patients received daily prednisone, and 6 (35.3%) received intermittent IV pulse therapy with methylprednisolone. Fourteen (82%) patients were on Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi), including tofacitinib, ruxolitinib, and upadacitinib. Nine patients (53%) were receiving anakinra, and 2 (11.8%) were on tocilizumab. All children had prior exposure to anti-IL-1 biologics, and 9 (53%) had previously received treatment with Tocilizumab (Table 1). Eventually, 8 (47%) patients underwent allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant.

We also obtained BALF from 54 comparator patients with chronic lung diseases (14 of ICs, 15 of NICs, 10 with PAP, and 15 with asthma), 23 (43%) boys and 31 (57%) girls with a mean age of 15.2 ± 3.9 years.

Cytokine and chemokine profiles of BALF

Prior work has identified cytokines and chemokines that are systemically elevated in patients with SJIA-LD, but whether these are also increased in the lungs is largely unknown [1, 5, 12]. We utilized specific ELISA and a custom Luminex to quantify these biomarkers in BALF.

Compared to all controls, children with SJIA-LD showed a marked and significant elevation of IL-18 (median (IQR: Q1-Q3), 1406 (722.3–2812) pg/mL vs. 37.2 (25.3–70) pg/mL, p < 0.0017). In addition, patient with SJIA-LD showed significant elevations compared to all controls in S100A8/9 (4745.3 (3003–11506) ng/mL vs. 1297.5 (260–7755) ng/mL, p = 0.0032), sCD25 (31.6 (19–50.3) pg/mL vs. 13.6 (8.6–37.3) pg/mL, p = 0.0046), MMP-7 (18,466.7 (10,462.2–33,516.1 vs. 6691.4 (2043.1–25,728.1) pg/mL, p = 0.0384) pg/mL, and CCL17 (0 (0–63.1) vs 0 (0–0) pg/mL; p = 0.038) (Fig. 1A, Table 2).

In contrast, levels of S100A12 trended higher in the all-controls group compared to SJIA-LD (1975.9 (67.65–112,299) pg/mL vs. 440.66 (320.7–1506.3) pg/mL), but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.29). Levels of IL-6, IL-8, and CCL11 were overall similar between SJIA-LD patients and comparators. CXCL9 was numerically lower in SJIA-LD, but this was not statistically significant either. The CCL25 was undetectable in all patients with SJIA-LD and was only detected in 4 patients from the control group (Fig. 1A, Table 2).

We next compared BALF cytokine levels of children with SJIA-LD to those in each of the specific lung disease subgroups. Strikingly, the levels of IL-18 were significantly higher in children with SJIA-LD compared to all disease subgroups (Fig. 1B). Median IL-18 was 44.1 (28.9–66.5) pg/mL in NIC, 51.46 (27.8–73.1) pg/mL in IC, 37.9 (0–49.1) pg/mL in aPAP and 30.8 (1–265.7) pg/mL in asthma patients (Fig. 1B, Table 2). S100A8/A9 levels were numerically higher in SJIA-LD than in each subgroup; however, none of the differences were statistically significant. Soluble CD25 levels were significantly higher in SJIA-LD compared to NIC (31.6 (19–50.3) pg/mL vs 12.3 (9.5–43), p = 0.0007), but not compared to other comparator subgroups, with the highest median levels seen in aPAP patients (median 33 (14.1–58.7) pg/mL). MMP-7 was numerically highest in SJIA-LD compared to comparators, followed by the aPAP subgroup, but not significantly different. Similarly, CCL17 was mostly undetectable in the comparator groups aside from asthma, with no statistically significant differences as well.

In contrast, S100A12 was significant elevated in asthma (440.7 (320.7–1506.3) pg/mL) and NIC group (99,918 (6712–976,643) pg/mL, p = 0.0015), with levels higher than those seen in SJIA-LD (1928.9 (150.3–36,227) pg/mL). S100A12 was lower in each of the other comparator subgroups compared to that in SJIA-LD patients. The highest levels of CXCL9 were seen in aPAP patients, although not significantly different from SJIA-LD (p = 0.1916). Other cytokines and chemokines, including IL-6, IL-10, CCL11, and CCL25 showed no differences between subgroups (Fig. 1B, Table 2).

BALF IL-18 levels and SJIA-LD features

Given the striking elevation of IL-18, we then used linear regression analysis to examine the association between BALF level of IL-18 and other disease features (Fig. 2). We found no significant association between IL-18 and disease duration (p = 0.34), duration of LD (p = 0.59), or O2 requirement (p = 0.1758). We did find a significant association between BALF IL-18 levels and the number of immunosuppressive medications utilized (p = 0.0167). However, we did not find significant associations between the dose of anakinra (p = 0.7950) or daily prednisone dose (p = 0.7673). In the case of the number of immunosuppressive medications and O2 requirement, the linear relationship was positive, and in all other variables, it was inverse (Fig. 2). Finally, we found that SJIA-LD patients who later underwent hemopoietic stem cell transplant (bone marrow transplant, BMT) for disease control had a trend towards higher levels of IL-18 in BALF (1655.3 (1069.4–2944)) than patients who continued medication treatment only (987.8 (178.3–2261.4)) pg/mL (p = 0.18).

We also examined the association between BALF IL-18 and systemic markers of inflammation. Interestingly, there was no association between BALF IL-18 and plasma level of IL-18 (p = 0.56), nor with ferritin (p = 0.65), CRP (p = 0.96), or ESR (p = 0.52). However, BALF levels of S100A8/9 corresponded well with levels of S100A8/9 in blood (p = 0.0012) (Supplemental Fig. 1). BALF IL-18 levels strongly correlated with most other inflammatory mediators, except S100A8/9 (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Cytokine and chemokine profiles of BALF in children with SJIA-LD based on received treatment

Optimal treatment approaches for SJIA-LD are unknown, but given the increasing recognition of SJIA-LD concomitant with increased use of anti-cytokine biologics, it has been hypothesized that such treatments could contribute to the development of LD [15]. There are also increasing reports of improvement in SJIA-LD using JAKi [16, 17]. As such, we examined cytokine and chemokine levels in patients with SJIA-LD according to current treatment with anti-cytokine biologics and JAK inhibitors.

All SJIA-LD patients had a prior history of anti-IL-1 therapies, and 10 patients (59%) also had a history of anti-IL-6 biologic treatment. Alternatively, we analyzed BALF levels of cytokines and chemokines in patients receiving anti-IL-1 and/or anti-IL-6 biologics at the time of bronchoscopy (N = 9). These patients had significantly higher levels of most measured cytokines, compared to patients without such treatment (N = 8): IL-18 (2690.8 (1553.6–3683) pg/mL (p = 0.0005) vs 732.7 (140.3–1089.2) pg/mL, S100A8/9 (8000 (4007.9–21,670.9) pg/mL (p = 0.038) vs 3028.5(1865.3–4456.3) ng/mL), S100A12 (537.1 (431.3–607,785.3) pg/mL (p = 0.02) vs 333 (283.1–389.1) pg/mL), sCD25 (51.4 (39.9–70.1) pg/mL (p = 0.0079) vs 23.125 (17.7–30.3) pg/mL), CCL11 (18.3 (2.7–29.9) pg/mL (p = 0.044) vs 0 (0–9.1) pg/mL), CCL17 (78.1 (8.8–153) pg/mL (p = 0.01) vs 0 (0–0) pg/mL) and MMP-7 (34,786.6 (16,254.9–52,085.1) pg/mL (p = 0.09) vs 17,090.2 (11,343.5–20,510) pg/mL) (Fig. 3A, Supplemental Table 1).

Interestingly, patients treated with JAKi showed no significant differences in BALF cytokine and chemokine levels compared to patients not receiving such treatments (Fig. 3B, Supplemental Table 1). However, this comparison was limited as most patients were receiving such therapy at the time of bronchoscopy (14 patients vs 3 patients without JAKi). IL-18 levels trended higher for patients receiving JAKi (1609.3 (898.4–3254.3) pg/mL vs. 711.9 (37.7–1701) (p = 0.056). Interestingly, while not significantly different, CXCL9 levels were substantially lower in patients treated with JAKi (0 (0–0) pg/mL vs 30.6 (0–115.8) pg/mL, p = 0.24) (Fig. 3B, Supplemental Table 1). Together, these findings demonstrate similarities but also important differences in BALF inflammatory mediators in SJIA-LD associated with different treatment classes.

Discussion

In this work, we find a distinct BALF profile in patients with SJIA-LD compared to patients with other chronic pulmonary conditions, including inflammatory and non-inflammatory airway diseases, poorly controlled asthma, and aPAP. This profile was characterized by significant elevation of IL-18, as well as S100A8/9, CCL17, MMP-7, and sCD25 levels. BALF IL-18 was significantly associated with the number of medications being used, but not the dose of steroids or anakinra, other clinical or immunological disease features, or plasma IL-18. Finally, we found that cytokine and chemokine profiles varied based on treatment classes.

Our previous work with a small number of SJIA-LD patients reported elevation of IL-18 level in BALF when compared to aPAP, but the present work expands this with a substantially larger SJIA-LD cohort as well as a broader range of disease comparators [1]. IL-18 is increasingly felt to be one of the key proinflammatory cytokines in the pathogenesis of SJIA [18, 19], and is considered a marker of disease activity and severity [20, 21]. The elevation of IL-18 in BALF could further indicate its role in the pathogenesis of LD. Indeed, the level of IL-18 in BALF trended higher in patients with more severe clinical features of disease, such as the requirement of more immunosuppressive therapy, BMT and O2 supplementation. Interestingly, the BALF level of IL-18 did not show a significant association with serum levels of IL-18. This poor correlation between cytokine levels in BALF and serum has been seen in other studies, although this can vary by specific cytokine [22, 23]. Indeed, levels of S100A8/9 did correlate well with serum levels of the protein (Supplemental Fig. 1). Therefore, the question of whether BALF IL-18 elevation reflects systemic inflammation, local production in the lung, or both, remains open.

Our BALF analysis also showed a significant elevation of the proinflammatory, neutrophil-derived calcium-binding proteins S100A8/9 compared to all controls, as well as S100A12 compared to NIC. However, the highest levels of S100A12 were observed in patients with poorly controlled asthma and NIC group, supporting a role of neutrophils in a poor response to standard treatment and a more complicated course of asthma and changes in the airways after tracheostomy tube placement. Elevated S100A8/9 (or calprotectin) and S100A12 (or calgranulin C) in plasma are well known in patients with SJIA [24–26]. Although S100A8/9 and S100A12 belong to the same group of calcium-binding proteins, they have distinct mechanisms of activation, cellular sources, as well as different signal transduction pathways, but are both crucial in the regulation of neutrophil migration to inflammatory sites [27]. S100A8/9 is produced by both neutrophils and macrophages, while S100A12 is more specifically produced by neutrophils in the acute inflammatory response. A similar, more prominent elevation of S100A12 vs S100A8/9 was observed in BALF collected from patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome compared to patients with cystic fibrosis [28], which may reflect the abundance of neutrophils in ARDS. In contrast, histopathology from SJIA-LD lung tissue shows a more chronic inflammatory infiltrate with fewer neutrophils [1, 5].

The BALF of SJIA-LD patients showed higher levels of sCD25 compared to controls. Soluble CD25 is an alpha chain of the high-affinity interleukin-2 receptor (IL2RA), which plays an essential role in the differentiation and function of regulatory T-cells and is shed upon T cell activation [29]. More importantly, sCD25 is the marker of macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), including cases associated with SJIA [30–32]. Soluble CD25 is considered a marker of both overt MAS as well as subclinical or smoldering MAS in patients with SJIA, and isolated elevation of sCD25 may be seen in the early stage of the syndrome when T-cell activation dominates over interferon-γ production [33]. MAS, especially its recurrent form, is a known risk factor for SJIA-LD [1, 3, 34], which could explain its elevation in BALF. Conversely, given the lymphocyte predominant inflammation in the lungs in SJIA-LD, this could also reflect local T cell activation.

The elevation of BALF MMP-7 seen in SJIA-LD resonates with the MMP-7 elevation seen in blood proteome [12] as well as our prior cohort study [4]. MMP-7 is well known as a marker of lung fibrosis [35, 36], which occurs variably in SJIA-LD [1]. Notably, the levels of BALF MMP-7 seen in SJIA-LD were similar to those seen in aPAP, as well as in NIC, which can have airway fibrosis and remodeling. Therefore, elevation of MMP-7 may reflect a universal lung remodeling and fibrosis rather than a unique feature in SJIA-LD.

The other chemokine that was significantly elevated in the SJIA-LD group was CCL17, which has a key role in allergic processes by regulating the trafficking of Th2 lymphocytes [37–39] and regulatory T-cells [40, 41]. However, CCL17 also has critical roles in autoimmunity, chronic inflammation, and pulmonary fibrosis [42]. Belperio et al. demonstrated increased CCL17 expression in bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, and reduced fibrosis with specific chemokine neutralization [43]. Wang et al. showed the elevation of BALF CCL17 in patients with progressive pulmonary fibrosis [44], and serum level of CCl-17 can be a predictive marker of idiopathic interstitial lung disease [45]. Although the median level of CCL17 was significantly higher in children with SJIA-LD, it was undetectable in many patients (60%), suggesting heterogeneity within SJIA-LD.

As noted above, increased recognition of SJIA-LD is temporally associated with the widespread use of anti-cytokine biologics for SJIA [5]. While patients with SJIA-LD typically are refractory to such treatments, they also frequently exhibit clinically overt drug reactions or intolerance [4, 5], as well as other atypical disease features, including eosinophilia and fixed rashes. This has led to the hypothesis that anti-IL-1 and anti-IL-6 biologics may trigger a drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS)-like syndrome, which could relate to SJIA-LD pathogenesis [46]. Alternatively, such medications may not cause a drug reaction per se, but otherwise alter the inflammatory milieu, otherwise known as the “cytokine plasticity hypothesis” [47].

Here, we find that SJIA-LD patients treated with anti-cytokine biologics had higher levels of BALF IL-18, along with other mediators, including S100A8/A9, S100A12, CCL11, and CCL17. Elevated CCL11 and CCL17 in BALF in particular, given their association with Th2 responses, could support either a DRESS-like reaction in the lungs, or shift in T cell phenotypes reflecting pulmonary fibrosis [48–51]. However, this is only an association and may represent confounding by indication due to the higher clinical severity of these patients necessitating more aggressive and combined immune suppression. The interpretation is further confounded due to a majority of patients also receiving dual therapy with JAKi, as well as variable doses of daily corticosteroids. Indeed, it is also notable that JAKi have shown promising results in the treatment of patients with DRESS [52–54].

Notably, our prior measurement of BALF cytokines from a small group of SJIA-LD patients showed a subgroup with elevated CXCL9 and CXCL10, interferon-induced chemokines [1], which we were unable to replicate in the present study. However, our prior study was before the clinical introduction of JAKi in children with JIA. The majority of patients (82%) from this study were on JAKi, which broadly inhibit signaling via over 50 different cytokines and growth factors, including interferons [55–57]. Of note, CXCL9 levels were substantially higher in the small number of patients not treated with JAKi (Supplemental Table 1).

Our findings also raise the question of whether BALF cytokines merely reflect systemic inflammation or whether they are specifically produced by immune cells in the lungs and directly reflect lung-specific inflammation [58]. We found a strong correlation of plasma and BALF levels of S100A8/9 (Supplemental Fig. 1), but not other cytokines, including IL-18 (Fig. 2). In addition, other mediators such as CCL25, elevated in a blood proteome study in patients with SJIA-LD [12], were largely undetected in BALF.

Our study has several important limitations. It was relatively small and, therefore, underpowered to examine many associations with clinical disease features. BALF was collected using a standardized protocol, but there are variable levels of dilution during sampling. There is also no universally accepted method to normalize BALF for analysis, as both inflammation and airway injury lead to increased total protein content in the fluid. HLA typing, including the prevalence of the HLA-DRB1*15 haplotype, was not available. Another noteworthy limitation was the SJIA-LD cohort itself, which did not include treatment-naïve patients or those with less complicated LD. Indeed, many patients underwent bronchoscopy as part of a pre-BMT evaluation. Additionally, the analysis of levels of cytokines and chemokines in BALF compared to disease activity indexes such as sJADAS (Systemic Juvenile Arthritis Disease Activity Score) and PGALD (The Physician Global Assessment of Lung Disease) was not performed.

In conclusion, patients with SJIA-LD showed a distinct BALF cytokine profile, including significant elevation in IL-18, S100A8/9, sCD25, and CCL17, compared to children with other chronic lung diseases. The elevation of these cytokines may provide important insights into the pathophysiology of the SJIA-LD. Additionally, BALF cytokine profiles varied significantly by treatment, particularly the use of anti-cytokine biologics. The implications of this are unclear and could represent patients with more significant inflammation who remain dependent on such treatments. It also raises the possibility of a distinct pulmonary inflammatory response driven by such treatments, whether through drug hypersensitivity per se or otherwise skewing immune response to create an altered cytokine milieu. Further studies are urgently needed to define mechanisms of lung inflammation and SJIA-LD and answer this critical question.
