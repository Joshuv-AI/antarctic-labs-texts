Machine learning model development and validation using SHAP: predicting 28-day mortality risk in pulmonary fibrosis patients
Authors: Zijun Wu, Mingliang Li, Zhiliang Xu, Gang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly prediction of mortality risk within 28 days of admission is crucial for personalized treatment in patients with pulmonary fibrosis (PF). This study aims to develop a predictive model for 28-day mortality risk in PF patients using interpretable machine learning (ML) methods.MethodsData from patients with pulmonary fibrosis were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database. The study endpoint was mortality within 28 days of admission. Feature selection was performed using logistic regression and LASSO algorithms. Six machine learning algorithms—decision tree, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), LightGBM, single-hidden-layer neural network, support vector machine (SVM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)—were employed to construct risk prediction models. Additionally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were utilized to interpret the predictive models.ResultsAmong the six evaluated machine learning models, the LightGBM model demonstrated robust predictive performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.819. SHAP analysis revealed that length of ICU stay, respiratory rate, and white blood cell count were the three most important features for predicting 28-day mortality risk in PF patients, with ICU stay duration having the most significant impact.ConclusionThis study indicates that machine learning methods hold potential for early prediction of mortality risk within 28 days of admission in patients with pulmonary fibrosis. Moreover, SHAP analysis enhanced the interpretability of the LightGBM model, thereby guiding clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a progressive, irreversible chronic lung disease characterized by the scarring of lung tissue, leading to a continuous deterioration of pulmonary function. This condition not only severely diminishes the quality of life for patients but also poses a significant threat to their survival [1]. In Europe, between 2013 and 2018, the average mortality rate due to pulmonary fibrosis was 3.9 per 100,000 individuals annually, with a cumulative death toll exceeding 17,000 [2]. The median survival period for patients is a mere 2 to 5 years [3], and the in-hospital mortality rate remains alarmingly high, reaching an average of 10.9% in the United States between 2013 and 2017 [4]. The development of accurate in-hospital mortality prediction models is critical for optimizing patient management in pulmonary fibrosis. These models can assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients for timely interventions to reduce mortality while improving resource allocation and treatment efficiency.

Current prognostic assessment for pulmonary fibrosis primarily relies on clinical experience and conventional measures such as C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and GAP (Gender, Age, Physiology) scores. However, these traditional approaches have demonstrated limited predictive accuracy, highlighting the need for more sophisticated models to enhance clinical decision-making.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool in medical prediction, capable of capturing complex nonlinear relationships and identifying subtle risk factors often missed by conventional methods [5–7]. In pulmonary fibrosis research, ML applications are increasingly being adopted, with studies demonstrating their ability to predict disease progression and patient survival through clinical data analysis [8–11]. Notable examples include deep learning models that integrate high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) images with clinical parameters to assess disease severity [12].

Building on these advances, this study employs machine learning to systematically evaluate 28-day mortality risk in hospitalized pulmonary fibrosis patients. We aim to develop a clinically actionable prediction model to support early intervention decisions while comparing the performance of different ML algorithms to advance prognostic assessment in this field.

Methods

Database and study population

The data utilized in this study were sourced from the MIMIC-IV database, a multi-parameter intensive care database developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). The inclusion criteria for study subjects were as follows: (1) age between 18 and 100 years; (2) first admission to the ICU. We implemented a rigorous protocol for handling missing data to ensure analytical validity. Variables with missing rates exceeding 30% were systematically evaluated and excluded unless they represented clinically essential indicators for pulmonary fibrosis diagnosis or progression, resulting in a final set of variables all containing ≤ 30% missing values. The missing data mechanism was thoroughly investigated using Little’s MCAR test (χ²=2878, df = 2474, p < 0.001), which provided strong evidence against the missing completely at random (MCAR) assumption. Clinical evaluation further supported that missingness patterns were systematically associated with observable clinical factors - particularly disease severity and testing timing. For instance, critically ill patients frequently missed non-essential tests due to clinical instability, consistent with a missing at random (MAR) mechanism. To maintain data integrity, we standardized the treatment of outliers across all variables and employed predictive mean matching (PMM) for missing value imputation. This approach was selected for its ability to preserve original data distributions while accounting for the identified MAR patterns. The comprehensive methodology ensures both statistical robustness and clinical relevance in our analyses. Ultimately, 69 variables were included, and a total of 534 patients were selected. To ensure the randomness of the study, a random seed was set, and 70% of the samples were randomly assigned as the training set, with the remaining 30% serving as the internal validation set. Mingliang Ling, who holds the certification to access this database (Record ID 12102707), was responsible for data extraction.

Study variables

Outcome variable

The primary outcome measure of this study was defined as all-cause mortality within 28 days of admission among patients with pulmonary fibrosis. ​The data were derived from the MIMIC-IV database, encompassing patients admitted for the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis. Through the analysis of these patients’ clinical data, the study explored the associations between mortality within 28 days of admission and baseline characteristics as well as variables during the treatment process.

Predictor variables

The variables included in the analysis comprised 68 physiological indicators, laboratory tests, personal medical histories, and clinical interventions. Physiological indicators encompassed age, gender, weight, and a series of vital signs, such as heart rate (vitalhr), non-invasive systolic blood pressure (vitalnbps), non-invasive diastolic blood pressure (vitalnbpd), non-invasive mean blood pressure (vitalnbpm), respiratory rate (vitalrr), oxygen saturation (vitalspo2), and body temperature (vitaltemperature). Laboratory tests included basic blood parameters such as white blood cell count (labwbc), red blood cell count (labrbc), platelet count (labplateletcount), hemoglobin (labhemoglobin), red cell distribution width (labrdw), and hematocrit (labhematocrit), as well as electrolyte and biochemical indicators like sodium (labsodium), potassium (labpotassium), total calcium (labcalciumtotal), chloride (labchloride), glucose (labglucose), anion gap (labaniongap), and pH (labpt). Coagulation and renal function indicators included plasma prothrombin time (labptt), international normalized ratio (labinr), blood urea nitrogen (labureanitrogen), and creatinine (labcreatinine). Personal medical histories covered hypertension (diaht), type 2 diabetes mellitus (diadm2), type 1 diabetes mellitus (diadm1), heart failure (diahf), myocardial infarction (diami), malignancy (diamt), chronic kidney disease (diackd), acute renal failure (diaarf), cirrhosis (diacirrhosis), hepatitis (diahepatitis), tuberculosis (diatb), pneumonia (diapneumonia), stroke (diastroke), hyperlipidemia (diahlp), chronic bronchitis (diacopd), and obesity. Additionally, variables related to hospitalization included length of hospital stay (hosp_day), length of ICU stay (icu_day), and interventions after admission, such as hours of mechanical ventilation (ventilation_hour), whether mechanical ventilation was performed (ventilation), whether continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was administered (crrt), whether sepsis was diagnosed (sepsis3), whether acute kidney injury (AKI) occurred (aki), AKI stage (aki_stage), fluid input (input_amount), fluid output (output_amount), 28-day fluid balance (balance), antihypertensive medications (rxantihypertensive), total dose of antihypertensive medications (rxantihypertensivetotalval), glucocorticoids (rxglucocorticoids), nephrotoxic drugs (rxnephrotoxic), total dose of nephrotoxic drugs (rxnephrotoxictotalval), immunosuppressants (rximmunosuppressant), pirfenidone, and several commonly used clinical scores, including SOFA (sofa), APSIII (apsiii), SIRS (sirs), SAPSII (sapsii), OASIS (oasis), GCS (gcs), and Charlson Comorbidity Index (charlson).

Feature selection

To achieve both explanatory and predictive objectives, this study employed two complementary strategies: (1) Traditional statistical screening: We first performed univariate logistic regression analysis on baseline variables, followed by feature refinement using LASSO regression (R package ‘glmnet’) with 10-fold cross-validation to determine the optimal λ value (lambda.min). This process identified 11 non-zero coefficient variables, effectively addressing multicollinearity and high-dimensionality issues. Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression was applied to these selected variables, with a significance threshold of P < 0.1, ultimately retaining 6 statistically significant variables to optimize model parsimony and clinical interpretability. (2) Full-feature machine learning approach: We implemented an XGBoost model incorporating all baseline variables to capture potential nonlinear relationships and interaction effects, with feature importance assessed using SHAP values. To ensure rigorous validation, the dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and test (30%) sets, with area under the curve (AUC) calculated via the pROC package used to compare predictive performance between strategies while preventing data leakage.

Model construction

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2. The specific steps for model building and evaluation are as follows: Six different machine learning techniques were selected, including decision tree, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), LightGBM, single-hidden-layer neural network, support vector machine (SVM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), to construct the models. To ensure model stability, 10-fold cross-validation and grid search were employed for hyperparameter optimization. Model performance was compared based on multiple metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity (or recall), balanced accuracy, precision, F1 score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). The Youden index was used to select the optimal threshold, and ROC curves of the training and validation sets were compared to avoid overfitting. Additionally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed on the validation set to assess the practical value and relative advantages of each model.

Model interpretation

To delve into the contributions of each feature variable to the model’s final output, the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method was employed to interpret high-performance “black-box” models. SHAP values, framed within game theory, quantify the importance of each feature in the prediction outcome, treating each feature as an independent contributor. Through SHAP, we can fairly attribute predictive performance to individual features, clarifying their impact on specific observations. Moreover, SHAP values were plotted for each sample in the dataset to reveal overall patterns and the range of feature impacts. SHAP dependence plots were also utilized to explore the nonlinear relationships between features.

Comparative model evaluation

We compared our LightGBM model against five established clinical scores (SOFA, APS III, SIRS, SAPS II, OASIS) to assess predictive performance. All scores were calculated using ICU admission data (first 24 h) according to their original definitions. We evaluated each model’s discriminative ability by computing AUC values in both training and test sets.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2). Prior to formal analysis, descriptive statistics were computed for all baseline variables. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using Shapiro-Wilk tests. Normally distributed data were analyzed using Student’s t-tests and reported as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and presented as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were compared using χ² tests and expressed as counts (percentages). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Baseline clinical data

A total of 534 patients with pulmonary fibrosis were enrolled in this study according to the predefined inclusion/exclusion criteria, comprising 298 males (55.8%) and 236 females (44.2%), with a mean age of 75.5 years. The cohort demonstrated a 28-day all-cause mortality rate of 31.6% (n = 169). Using computer-generated randomization with a fixed seed, patients were allocated into training (n = 374, 70%) and test sets (n = 160, 30%) while maintaining balanced distributions of key prognostic factors. Factor selection for predictive models

The LASSO regression results are shown in Fig. 2A and B. The traditional statistical screening process ultimately retained six variables (labwbc, vitalrr, vitalspo2, icu_day, balance, sofa), all demonstrating statistical significance (p < 0.1) in multivariate logistic regression, indicating their independent association with clinical outcomes (Tables 2 and 3). SHAP value analysis of the full-feature XGBoost model confirmed the consistency of these core features, with all six traditionally selected variables ranking among the top 15 most important features. Notably, five additional variables (labureanitrogen, labpotassium, apsiii, sapsii, output_amount) excluded by traditional methods emerged in the top 10 most important features, suggesting their predictive value through nonlinear relationships or interaction effects (Table 4).

Construction and evaluation of machine learning models

We employed six machine learning algorithms—decision tree, k-nearest neighbors (KNN), LightGBM, multilayer perceptron (MLP), support vector machine (SVM), and XGBoost—to construct predictive models from the training set. During model construction, 10-fold cross-validation combined with grid search was utilized to optimize the hyperparameters of the decision tree. After performance evaluation across multiple metrics, the optimal hyperparameter combination was ultimately selected based on the ROC-AUC metric and used to train the final model.

To visually demonstrate the decision rules of the decision tree model, we conducted a visualization analysis. The root node’s splitting condition was “icu_day < 3.5,” indicating that the length of ICU stay is the most important splitting variable. The subsequent important variables were SOFA score and electrolyte balance (Fig. 3A). The structure of the neural network model is presented as an input layer, at least one hidden layer containing 24 neurons, and an output layer (Fig. 3B).

To better understand the relationship between model predictions and clinical variables, we applied SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis to our LightGBM model, which revealed key factors influencing 28-day mortality risk. Figure 5A displays six significant predictors ranked by their SHAP values, where bar length represents their relative contribution to model output. The color gradient (yellow to purple) indicates how feature values correlate with mortality risk—yellow dots (higher values) generally associate with increased risk, while purple dots (lower values) suggest reduced risk. Among these, ICU stay duration (icu_day) emerged as the most influential predictor (Fig. 5B).

Model comparison with clinical scores

Our LightGBM model demonstrated superior performance compared to five conventional clinical scores (SOFA, SIRS, APS III, SAPS II, OASIS). While all traditional scores showed moderate discrimination (AUC < 0.7) with notable performance drops in the test set (Table 6; Fig. 6), our model maintained stable performance (training AUC 0.844 → test AUC 0.819). The best-performing conventional score, OASIS, achieved only 0.697 AUC in the test set. Notably, APS III showed significant variability (training 0.700 → test 0.639). Using just six clinical variables, our model provided both better prediction of ICU outcomes and greater generalizability than conventional approaches.

Discussion

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF), a chronic and progressive pulmonary disease, often leads to a high in-hospital mortality risk for patients during the disease course. According to existing literature, the in-hospital mortality rate for patients with pulmonary fibrosis remains high, yet the medical community still faces significant challenges in identifying high-risk patients. Therefore, establishing an effective predictive model is of crucial importance for enhancing clinical diagnostic and therapeutic standards and achieving personalized treatment.

In this study, we employed 69 variables to develop six machine learning (ML) methods. After systematic comparison and evaluation, the LightGBM model was identified as the optimal predictive tool. This model is capable of identifying high-risk patients who are likely to experience mortality within 28 days of admission, thus aiding medical teams in implementing early interventions to improve patient outcomes. LightGBM is a highly efficient machine learning framework based on gradient boosting machines. Compared to traditional gradient boosting machines (GBM), LightGBM has significantly optimized computational speed and memory usage, making it particularly suitable for handling large-scale datasets. Currently, LightGBM has been widely applied in various aspects of the medical field, including disease prediction, diagnosis, and prognostic analysis [13–16].In the study of complex diseases such as PF, LightGBM has demonstrated great potential. For instance, previous research has constructed prognostic prediction models for pulmonary fibrosis by integrating CT radiomics data, with the LightGBM model showcasing remarkable predictive efficac [17]. However, to date, no studies have thoroughly explored the application of LightGBM in predicting the mortality risk within 28 days of admission for patients with pulmonary fibrosis based on clinical indicators.

The prognosis of pulmonary fibrosis is influenced by a variety of factors, including age, gender, pulmonary function indicators (e.g., FVC and DLCO), radiological manifestations (e.g., HRCT findings), and comorbidities [18]. Long-term studies have shown that the rate of disease progression and clinical manifestations (e.g., degree of dyspnea) significantly impact prognosis [19]. Additionally, research on prognostic factors in patients with COVID-19 has revealed that longer ICU stays are associated with poorer outcomes, particularly in patients with COVID-19-induced pulmonary fibrosis [20]. This may be related to prolonged mechanical ventilation, pulmonary infections, airway management, and other factors [21]. Specifically, various complications that occur during ICU stays, such as airway infections and thrombosis, can exacerbate patients’ conditions and thereby affect their prognosis. Similar observations were made in our study, where we found a significant correlation between the length of ICU stay and the risk of mortality within 28 days of admission in patients with pulmonary fibrosis. Moreover, we identified that respiratory rate is closely associated with mortality risk, second only to the duration of ICU stay in terms of significance. In certain pathological states, such as pulmonary fibrosis, an elevated respiratory rate may reflect increased dyspnea and declining pulmonary function, which in turn may impact survival rates. Anke Schertel et al. demonstrated through continuous 24-hour respiratory monitoring of patients with pulmonary fibrosis that an increased respiratory rate (RR) is associated with adverse outcomes, suggesting that respiratory rate could serve as an important indicator in the follow-up of patients with pulmonary fibrosis. In addition to these findings, our study also incorporated key clinical indicators such as white blood cell count, oxygen saturation, 28-day fluid balance, and SOFA score. White blood cell count and oxygen saturation have been proven in previous studies to be useful in predicting the prognosis of patients with pulmonary fibrosis [22, 23]. Fluid balance, which refers to the equilibrium between fluid intake and output in the body, can further exacerbate pulmonary dysfunction in patients with pulmonary fibrosis when disrupted. Yoshimasa Mizuno et al. found that intraoperative fluid balance plays a significant role in the prognosis of acute exacerbation of pulmonary fibrosis after pulmonary resection for primary lung cancer, with increased intraoperative fluid balance being an important prognostic predictor of postoperative pulmonary fibrosis [24]. The SOFA score, a widely used scoring system for assessing organ failure in critically ill patients, has increasingly garnered attention in the study of pulmonary fibrosis. Existing research indicates that the qSOFA score is an effective tool for predicting mortality in hospitalized patients with PF, especially in early assessments [25]. Additionally, studies have explored the combined application of the SOFA score with other clinical indicators (e.g., radiological examinations) to enhance the accuracy of predictive models and provide a more comprehensive assessment of the prognosis of patients with pulmonary fibrosis [26]. In summary, the six factors we included are all closely related to the occurrence of PF, indirectly verifying the reliability of the model we established.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) provides a robust framework for interpreting machine learning models by quantifying individual feature contributions to predictions. This method has gained prominence in medical research, particularly for prognostic prediction in cardiovascular, respiratory, and oncological conditions [27–30]. In our study, SHAP analysis of the LightGBM model identified ICU stay duration (icu_day), respiratory rate (vitalrr), and white blood cell count (labwbc) as the most influential predictors of 28-day mortality. The analysis revealed clinically significant patterns: prolonged ICU stays showed a positive association with mortality risk, leukocytosis (10–20 × 10⁹/L) indicated systemic inflammation, and elevated respiratory rates (20–30 breaths/min) signaled respiratory compromise. Partial dependence plots (Fig. 5E) confirmed these relationships, particularly the dose-dependent impact of ICU hospitalization duration. These findings highlight three key clinical indicators warranting close monitoring: extended ICU stays, white blood cell counts in the 10–20 × 10⁹/L range, and respiratory rates exceeding 30 breaths/minute. Such markers may reflect disease progression, infection severity, or organ dysfunction, suggesting the need for timely clinical intervention. The SHAP approach effectively bridges computational analysis with clinical decision-making by translating complex model outputs into actionable medical knowledge.

The LightGBM model demonstrated superior performance versus conventional clinical scores (SOFA, SIRS, APS III, SAPS II, OASIS) in our ICU cohort. In the test set, it achieved an AUC of 0.819, outperforming both the top conventional score (OASIS: 0.697) and SOFA (0.650), confirming its enhanced discriminative ability for high-risk patients.This supports machine learning’s potential to improve clinical risk stratification in critical care. However, external validation in diverse populations remains necessary to verify the model’s real-world applicability.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, case identification relied exclusively on administrative data without radiographic or pathological confirmation, which may impact diagnostic accuracy. Second, while the model demonstrated good performance in internal validation (70/30 split), its generalizability may be constrained by the single-center dataset that may not fully capture population heterogeneity. Furthermore, despite employing LASSO regression for variable selection and adjusting for multiple confounders, residual unmeasured confounding may persist.

To address these limitations, we propose several future research directions: (1) multicenter external validation using independent datasets from diverse institutions to assess model generalizability; (2) prospective studies to evaluate the model’s dynamic predictive performance in real-world clinical settings; (3) incorporation of multi-omics data (e.g., radiomics, genomics) to enhance model performance; and (4) optimization of clinical applicability through local calibration and workflow integration.

Moving forward, our research will focus on clinical translation, with particular emphasis on validating the predictive stability of the variable model across diverse populations through larger sample sizes and more rigorous study designs. These efforts will help establish the model’s clinical utility and robustness in different healthcare settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the LightGBM model exhibits high accuracy and efficiency in predicting the mortality risk of patients with pulmonary fibrosis, providing clinicians with a new tool to evaluate individual patient risks and support clinical decision-making. Prolonged ICU stays should raise high alert for adverse outcomes. However, further research is needed to validate the model’s effectiveness and integrate it into routine clinical practice.
