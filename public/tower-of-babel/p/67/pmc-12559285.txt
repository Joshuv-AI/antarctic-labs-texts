Nardilysin in adipocyte regulates insulin sensitivity via HIF1α and PPARγ
Authors: Shintaro Matsuda, Mikiko Ohno, Kiyoto Nishi, Shinya Ikeda, Hirotaka Iwasaki, Taiki Eifuku, Yoshinori Hiraoka, Takeshi Kimura, Koh Ono, Eiichiro Nishi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a critical global health issue and a major risk factor for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). However, molecular mechanism by which obesity leads to T2DM has not been fully elucidated. Here, we investigated the role of adipocyte nardilysin (NRDC) in insulin sensitivity using adipocyte-specific NRDC-knockout mice (Adipo-KO). Despite no difference in body weight, Adipo-KO demonstrated enhanced glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity exclusively under high fat diet (HFD) conditions. Epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) in Adipo-KO showed the decreased crown-like structures and F4/80-positive cells. Consistently, insulin-stimulated Akt phosphorylation was elevated in the eWAT of HFD-fed Adipo-KO and 3T3-L1 adipocytes with NRDC knockdown. In addition, NRDC knockdown led to a decrease in cellular oxygen consumption rate and hypoxia-induced HIF1α expression in 3T3-L1 adipocytes. The augmented Akt phosphorylation by NRDC knockdown was reversed by daprodustat, which stabilizes HIF1α, suggesting that the effect of NRDC on insulin sensitivity is mediated by HIF1α. Upstream analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in NRDC knockdown 3T3-L1 adipocytes revealed that PPARγ is enriched in the promoters of the DEGs. We also confirmed the complex formation of NRDC and PPARγ. In conclusion, NRDC in adipocyte regulates insulin sensitivity through HIF1α and PPARγ.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21276-z.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a global health issue, primarily driven by obesity due to overeating and lack of exercise1,2. Adipose tissue plays a central role in the causal relationship of obesity and T2DM, which, beyond serving as an energy storage depot, functions as an endocrine organ that secretes various adipokines. In individuals with obesity, excess adipose tissue undergoes pathological changes including hypertrophy, hypoxia, and macrophage and lymphocyte infiltration, leading to a state of chronic inflammation3,4. This inflammatory state certainly contributes to adipose tissue dysfunction and insulin resistance5,6, whereas detailed molecular mechanisms of the pathogenesis have not been fully clarified.

Numerous studies have explored how obesity leads to chronic inflammation and decreased insulin sensitivity in white adipose tissue, and one of the leading working hypotheses is that hypoxia in adipose tissue is responsible7–9. The expansion of adipose tissue in obesity is often coupled with hypoxic conditions due to the limitations in local oxygen supply relative to tissue demand10. Intriguingly, recent studies have also shown that obese adipocytes exhibit increased cellular oxygen consumption, which cause the relative hypoxia11. Hypoxia in adipose tissue triggers various cellular responses, including the upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1alpha (HIF1α), which mediates metabolic adaptations and inflammatory responses in adipocytes12–15. The transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) is another critical regulator in adipose tissue, playing a central role in adipocyte differentiation, lipid storage, and insulin sensitivity16. Importantly, PPARγ agonists, such as thiazolidinediones, have been used in diabetes treatment to improve insulin sensitivity. There is some evidence to suggest that HIF1α and PPARγ may functionally interplay and influence adipose tissue metabolism and other diseases15,17,18.

Nardilysin, also known as N-arginine dibasic convertase (NRDC), is a metalloendopeptidase of the M16 family that plays a complex role in energy and glucose metabolism. Mice globally lacking NRDC (Nrd1−/−) exhibited enhanced brown adipose tissue (BAT) thermogenesis and improved systemic insulin sensitivity despite of severe impairment in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS)19. Mice with β-cell specific deletion of NRDC showed severe diabetic phenotype with marked decrease in GSIS, indicating that NRDC in β cells are indispensable for the maintenance of GSIS. However, how NRDC regulates insulin sensitivity has not been elucidated. To determine which tissue-expressed NRDC is important in regulating insulin sensitivity, we first analyzed adipocyte-specific deficient (Adipo-KO) mice. Under normal chow diet (NCD), Adipo-KO mice showed enhanced BAT thermogenesis and hyperthermia through two mechanisms. First, NRDC and PPARγ co-activator-1α (PGC-1α) interact and co-localize at the UCP1 enhancer, where NRDC represses PGC-1α activity20. Second, NRDC negatively regulates the protein stability of UCP1 via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway21.

In the present study, we show that Adipo-KO mice exhibit enhanced glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity exclusively under HFD feeding. Interestingly, under HFD, Adipo-KO did not show hyperthermia due to BAT thermogenesis, but showed reduced chronic inflammation in the eWAT. Moreover, NRDC knockdown in 3T3-L1 adipocytes led to a decrease in cellular oxygen consumption rate and hypoxia-induced HIF1α expression. We also confirmed the association of NRDC and PPARγ, and that mRNA levels of several key targets of PPARγ are elevated in Adipo-KO eWAT. These results demonstrate that NRDC in adipocyte regulates insulin sensitivity through functional interaction with HIF1α and PPARγ.

Results

Improvement in glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity in adipocyte-specific NRDC knockout mice on a high-fat diet

To clarify the role of NRDC in adipocyte on glucose metabolism and insulin sensitivity, glucose and insulin tolerance tests were performed in adipocyte-specific NRDC knockout mice (Adipo-KO). Adipo-KO were generated by crossing Adipoq-Cre+/− with Nrd1flox/flox mice (Floxed) on a C57BL/6 background as previously described21, and Floxed mice without Adipoq-Cre expression were used as control mice. These mice were fed a normal chow diet (NCD) or a high-fat diet (HFD) from 6 weeks of age for 12 weeks. The general appearance and growth, including body weight, epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) and liver weight, remained similar in Floxed and Adipo-KO, either in NCD nor in HFD groups (Fig. 1A-C). In spite of no differences in glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity after NCD between Floxed and Adipo-KO mice, Adipo-KO showed significantly improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity after HFD for 12 weeks compared to Floxed mice (Fig. 1D-G). There were no significant differences in plasma insulin levels between Floxed and Adipo-KO mice during fasting or after glucose loading on NCD or HFD (Fig. 1H). Additionally, body temperature, VO2, locomotor activity, and the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) did not significantly differ between Floxed and Adipo-KO mice after HFD (Supplementary Fig. 1A-D). These findings suggest that NRDC deletion in adipocytes enhances systemic glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity specifically under HFD conditions.

Reduction of crown-like structures and F4/80-positive cells in adipo-KO on an HFD

To investigate the involvement of NRDC in HFD-induced chronic inflammation of eWAT, we performed H&E staining which revealed that Adipo-KO tissue showed significantly less inflammatory cell infiltration and crown-like structure (CLS) development compared to Floxed mice (Fig. 2AB). The size measurement of white adipocytes demonstrated that the composition of adipocytes in Adipo-KO WAT consisted of fewer small-sized cells and more large-sized cells compared to Floxed WAT (Fig. 2CD). This suggests a reduction in the number of adipocytes impacted by CLS. Consistently, F4/80 positive cells and the expression of F4/80 mRNA in eWAT, which are upregulated by HFD, were significantly decreased in the Adipo-KO group (Fig. 2EF). Residual expression of Nrd1 in Adipo-KO eWAT likely reflects NRDC expression in non-adipocyte cell types, including stromal or inflammatory cells.

Enhanced insulin-induced phosphorylation of AKT in eWAT of Adipo-KO mice and NRDC-knockdown 3T3-L1 adipocytes

We further investigated insulin sensitivity in adipose tissues of Floxed and Adipo-KO mice following NCD or HFD feeding. As shown in Fig. 3A-C, insulin-induced phosphorylation of AKT was elevated in Adipo-KO compared to that of Floxed mice in both NCD and HFD group. While the global level of AKT phosphorylation was lower in HFD group, the difference between Floxed and Adipo-KO eWAT was more obvious in HFD group compared to NCD group (Fig. 3A-C). Next, we examined 3T3-L1 adipocytes with NRDC knockdown to investigate whether NRDC directly influences insulin signaling in a cell-autonomous manner. Insulin stimulation of the differentiated 3T3-L1adipocytes with siRNA (siNRDC1 and 2) knockdown of NRDC showed higher levels of AKT phosphorylation than in cells treated with control siRNA (Fig. 3DE). These in vitro results suggest that NRDC plays a cell-autonomous role in modulating insulin signaling.

NRDC knockdown impacts cellular metabolism in 3T3-L1 adipocytes

NRDC knockdown did not visibly impact the differentiation of 3T3-L1 cells into mature adipocytes (Fig. 4A). However, despite similar cell densities, medium acidification occurred more slowly in NRDC-knockdown cells than in the control cells (Fig. 4B). These findings led us to propose that NRDC might affect cellular respiration in 3T3-L1 adipocytes. Subsequent analysis of the respiratory state of these adipocytes using an extracellular flux analyzer revealed that the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was reduced in NRDC-knockdown-adipocytes at baseline and during maximal respiration following oligomycin and FCCP addition, compared to control adipocytes (Fig. 4C, D). Notably, the reduction in OCR by NRDC-knockdown was not clearly observed in undifferentiated 3T3-L1 preadipocytes (Fig. 4E, F), suggesting that the effect of NRDC on oxygen consumption is dependent on the differentiation state of adipocytes.

NRDC in adipocytes regulates insulin signaling via HIF1α

NRDC knockdown reduced oxygen consumption in 3T3-L1 adipocytes, which likely alleviates relative intracellular hypoxia and thereby suppresses HIF1α stabilization under hypoxic conditions. Under this hypothesis, we assessed whether NRDC deletion could affect HIF1α expression induced by low oxygen environment (3% O2 condition). As expected, only control adipocytes exhibited an induction of HIF1α, whereas NRDC-knockdown adipocytes displayed no significant induction of HIF1-α expression (Figure 5A). We also examined 3T3-L1 preadipocytes, which did not show HIF1-α induction both in control and NRDC-knockdown cells (Figure 5A). This led us to hypothesize that mature 3T3-L1 adipocytes might experience relative hypoxia compared to preadipocytes and that NRDC knockdown could protect adipocytes from hypoxia. NRDC deficiency attenuates HIF1α induction and simultaneously enhances insulin sensitivity. To assess whether NRDC deficiency enhances insulin sensitivity via attenuation of HIF1α induction, we examined the effect of daprodustat, an inhibitor of HIF1α degradation. Daprodustat treatment markedly upregulated the expression of HIF1α, which reversed the increased phosphorylation of AKT by NRDC knockdown, suggesting that the effect of NRDC is mediated by HIF1α (Fig. 5BC).

NRDC associates with PPARγ and may regulate its target genes

We performed RNA-sequence to comprehensively analyze gene expression changes caused by NRDC knockdown in 3T3-L1 adipocytes. Upstream analysis of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs: Supple Table 1) using public database of ChIP-seq results in white adipose tissue revealed that PPARγ and Tle3 were enriched in the promoters of the DEGs (Fig. 6A). As we have previously reported that NRDC makes a complex with PPARγ co-activator-1α (ppargc1a and PGC-1α) and repress its transcriptional activity20, we examined the triad complex of NRDC, PPARγ and PGC-1α. Immunoprecipitation and Western blotting demonstrated that these three factors form a complex and pioglitazone, an activator of PPARγ, tended to reduce the association of NRDC with PPARγ (Fig. 6B). We next examined mRNA levels of several major PPARγ target genes in eWAT of HFD-fed mice, and found that Glut4 (Slc2a4) is significantly, while adiponectin (Adipoq) and lipoprotein lipase (Lpl) tend to be elevated in Adipo-KO eWAT. Notably, a major target of HIF1α, VEGF (Vegfa) also tended to be higher in Adipo-KO eWAT (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

We demonstrate here that the adipocyte-specific deletion of NRDC suppresses HFD-induced insulin resistance and chronic inflammation in adipose tissue. We also show that NRDC knockdown in 3T3-L1 adipocytes enhanced insulin-induced AKT phosphorylation, suggesting a cell-autonomous effect of NRDC on insulin sensitivity in adipocyte. Our previous study showed that global deletion of NRDC enhanced systemic insulin sensitivity, but adipose tissue-specific NRDC deficiency had an insulin sensitizing effect only under HFD, suggesting that insulin sensitivity, at least under NCD, is mediated by NRDC expressed in non-adipose tissue, possibly liver or skeletal muscle. On the other hand, glucose-stimulated insulin secretion is markedly suppressed by either global or β-cell-specific deletion of NRDC19, suggesting that NRDC expressed in each tissue regulates systemic insulin metabolism through different functions.

In the present study, adiponectin Cre was used for adipocyte-specific gene deletion, thus NRDC is deleted in both white and brown adipose tissue. We previously showed that NRDC expressed in BAT regulates adaptive thermogenesis via modulation of UCP1 expression and is involved in body temperature regulation under NCD21. Namely, on NCD, Adipo-KO mice exhibited hyperthermia and lower physical activity compared to control mice. Under HFD, however, there were no significant differences in body temperature and physical activity between Adipo-KO and control mice. Although it is not clear why HFD stress abolished the effect of NRDC deficiency on BAT activity, it is unlikely that NRDC in BAT had an effect on the insulin sensitivity observed in this study.

We show here that the oxygen consumption of NRDC-knockdown 3T3-L1 adipocytes is reduced compared to control cells. Furthermore, we show that HIF1α induction by hypoxia is suppressed in NRDC-knockdown cells and that enhancement of HIF1α expression by daprodustat, an inhibitor of HIF1α degradation, abolishes the insulin-sensitizing effect of NRDC knockdown. These results suggest that NRDC regulates insulin sensitivity via HIF1α. Evidence that HIF1α in adipocytes plays an important role in insulin sensitivity includes the fact that adipocyte-specific loss of HIF1α improves insulin sensitivity15,22 and inhibits atherosclerosis progression23. If the reduced oxygen consumption in adipocyte due to NRDC deficiency also occurs in vivo, it is possible that the hypoxic situation predicted in eWAT with obesity is less likely to develop, which may lead to the improved insulin sensitivity. Intriguingly, a recent study by Feng et al. showed that lactate binds directly to PHD2 and inhibits its hydroxylase activity24. Our results show that NRDC knockdown decreases lactate secretion into the medium, thus suggesting that the decrease in lactate may suppress HIF1α expression via enhanced PHD2 activity.

Since our data suggested that NRDC regulates HIF1α activity, the next question was whether HIF1α accumulated upstream of the gene that was altered by knockdown of NRDC. To this end, we analyzed the upstream regulators of DEGs using public databases of ChIP-seq analysis. Unfortunately, HIF1α ChIP-seq data in adipocyte were not publicly available. However, the analysis demonstrated that PPARγ was significantly accumulated in the upstream regions of the DEGs. Since we had previously reported that PPARγ coactivator, PGC-1α and NRDC regulate UCP1 expression in BAT via mutual interaction20, we examined the interaction between NRDC and PPARγ and confirmed the triad complex of NRDC, PPARγ and PGC-1α. NRDC inhibits the transcriptional activity of PGC-1α, and pioglitazone, the activating ligand of PPARγ, weakened the NRDC-PPARγ interaction. Given the several evidence showing mutual interplay of HIF1α and PPARγ17,18, our data suggest that NRDC may mediate the mutual repression of PPARγ and HIF1α.

One of the striking findings in this study is that inflammatory cell infiltration is markedly suppressed in visceral adipose tissue. We identified NRDC as a binding protein for HB-EGF25, and the subsequent studies showed that NRDC enhances ectodomain shedding of membrane precursors such as HB-EGF and TNFα26–28. Furthermore, we showed that NRDC deficiency leads to suppression of inflammatory diseases such as gastritis, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, and rheumatoid arthritis via attenuation of TNFα activation29–31. Although this study demonstrated that the nuclear actions of NRDC are important in adipocyte metabolism and that they contribute to the regulation of insulin sensitivity, it is possible that the extracellular functions of NRDC, especially in inflammatory cytokine regulation, are partially involved, which should be clarified in the future. Taken together, we demonstrate here that NRDC in adipocyte regulates insulin sensitivity possibly through the functional interaction with two critical transcription factors, HIF1α and PPARγ.

Methods

Animals

All animal experiments were performed according to procedures approved by the Institute of Laboratory Animals in Kyoto University, and Research Center for Animal Life Science in Shiga University of Medical Science under the Institutes’ guidelines for animal and recombinant DNA experiments. Animal protocols were approved by the Animal Research Committee, Graduate School of Medicine, Kyoto University and the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shiga University of Medical Science. Generation of Nrd1flox/flox mice (Floxed) were previously described (accession no. CDB1019K; http://www.clst.riken.jp/arg/mutant%20mice%20list.html)19. Adipocyte-specific Nrd1 knockout mice (Adipo-KO) were generated by crossing Nrd1flox/flox mice with Adipoq-Cre mice21. Adipoq-Cre mice were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. Male mice were used for all animal experiments. Floxed and Adipo-KO mice were fed a normal chow diet (NCD) or high-fat diet (HFD) for 12 weeks starting at 6 weeks of age, and then were subjected to each experiment. For HFD, high-fat and high-sucrose diet (F2HFHSD, Oriental Yeast CO.), in which 54.5% of the total caloric intake was derived from fat, was utilized. To obtain mouse tissue for the biochemical analysis, mice were deeply anesthetized by isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation, and epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) were then removed and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Respiratory gas analysis

Oxygen consumption (VO2), respiratory exchange ratio (RER), and ambulatory physical activity were measured using the Oxymax indirect calorimetry system (Comprehensive Lab Animal Monitoring System: Oxymax/CLAMS, Columbus Instruments, TX, US). The light period in the chamber was set for 12 h (8:00–20:00) and the dark period for 12 h (20:00–8:00). Room temperature was set at 23 °C. Mice were placed in the chamber and VO2, RER, and physical activity were measured continuously for 24 h at 10-minute intervals starting at 8:00. Food and water were taken freely during the measurement.

Telemetric body temperature monitoring was performed on conscious mice using a wireless ETA-F10 Mouse Implantable Transmitter System by Data Science International (New Brighton) as previously described21.

Glucose and insulin tolerance tests

For glucose tolerance tests, mice were fasted overnight and injected intraperitoneally with 10 ml/kg body weight of 20% D-glucose solution (2 g/kg body weight of D-glucose) (Otsuka Pharmaceutical). For insulin tolerance test, mice were fasted for 4 h, and then injected intraperitoneally with 1 (under NCD) or 1.5 (under HFD) unit/kg body weight of insulin (Humulin R, Eli Lilly). Blood samples were collected from the tails of mice before and at the indicated periods after the injection. A self-test glucometer was used to measure blood glucose levels. An ELISA kit (MS303; Morinaga Institute of Biological Science, Japan) was used to measure plasma insulin levels.

For analysis of insulin-induced AKT phosphorylation in eWAT, mice were fasted for overnight, and then injected intraperitoneally with 1 unit/kg body weight of insulin (under NCD and HFD). Epididymal WAT was harvested 30 min after insulin injection.

Histological procedures and immunohistochemistry

Mice were deeply anesthetized by isoflurane and transcardially perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde [volume %] in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline. Epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) was removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for paraffin block preparation. Paraffin sections of 4 μm thickness were obtained from four distinct layers, with each layer separated by more than 150 μm. A Keyence BZ-700 microscope was used for imaging. To calculate the number of crown-like structures (CLS), images of 3 × 3 consecutive fields at 40x magnification from three distinct layers were captured and jointed. The number of CLS was then normalized to the total adipose tissue area in the jointed image. Immunofluorescent staining for mouse tissues was conducted as previously described28, using an anti-F4/80 antibody (#70076, Cell Signaling Technology). to identify F4/80 positive cells. Images of 3 × 3 consecutive fields at 200x magnification from two distinct layers were captured and jointed, and the number of F4/80 positive cells was determined by identifying nuclei stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). LipiDyeII staining for the detection of lipid droplets in mature adipocytes was performed according to the manufacture’s protocol (Funakoshi), followed by observation using a Keyence BZ-700 microscope.

Cell culture

3T3-L1 cells obtained from ATCC were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with 4.5 g/L glucose (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% adult bovine serum (BS)(B9433) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (P/S) (Gibco) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For adipocyte differentiation, cells were initially cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (N4637) and 1% P/S. Differentiation was induced with a medium containing 1 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX) (I5879, Sigma-Aldrich), 1 µM dexamethasone (DEX) (D1756, Sigma-Aldrich), and 10 µg/ml insulin (I0516, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 days, followed by a medium with 10 µg/ml insulin alone for another 2 days, and finally, cells were cultured in insulin-free medium for 2 more days. For serum-free cultures, DMEM with 0.5% fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin was used (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical).

For analysis of insulin-induced AKT phosphorylation, differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes were treated with 17 nM of insulin for 15 min. To evaluate the expression of HIF1α in cells under hypoxic conditions, differentiated cells were incubated in a 3% O2-regulated incubator for 24 h, and then immediately lysed using the procedure described below. To assess the effect of Daprodustat on HIF1α expression and AKT phosphorylation, siRNA-transfected and differentiated 3T3-L1 adipocytes were treated with or without Daprodustat (50µM, 100µM) for 48 h.

SiRNA transfection

Sub-confluent 3T3-L1cells were transfected with Stealth RNAi™ siRNA targeting Nrd1 (MSS214013 (siNRDC1: F: 5’−3’ AAU CCU AGA AUU CCA GAU GUG UUC C, R:5’−3’: GGA ACA CAU CUG GAA UUC UAG GAU U) and MSS214014 (siNRDC2: F: 5’−3’ GGCUCACAGAGGACCUGGAAAGUAAA, R:5’−3’: UUUACUUCCAGGUCCUCUGUGAGCC), Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Differentiation of 3T3-L1 cells into adipocytes were then induced the day after the transfection, as described above.

Western blotting

Cultured cells or tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM sodium chloride, 0.5 w/v% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1 w/v% sodium dodecyl sulfate, 1.0 w/v% NP-40 substitute, protease inhibitor cOmplete™ (Roche), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 3 (P0044, sigma). The lysed samples were sonicated and centrifuged at 4 °C, 15,000 rpm for 5 min to remove impurities and fat, and then used for SDS-PAGE and western blotting. Anti-NRDC antibody (clone #1, #135, and #23) was raised in our laboratory. The clone #135 are commercially available at Millipore (MABS2057). Antibodies against anti phosphorylated (p)-AKT (T308), total AKT, GAPDH, HIF1α, and COX4 were obtained from Cell signaling technology. Antibody against PGC1α (#16) was developed in our laboratory as previously described20, and anti-PPARγ antibody was obtained from the Santa Cruz Biotechnology. Goat anti-mouse or anti rat-peroxidase conjugated IgG (1:5000, GE), or anti rabbit-peroxidase conjugated IgG (1:3000, CST) were used as secondary antibody. Intensity of signals on western blots was quantified using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html; version 1.54 g). Each signal intensity was normalized with that of GAPDH. For p-AKT, the intensity was normalized with that of total AKT. Full-length images of the Western blots shown in the main figures are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Real-time PCR

RNA from cultured cells or tissues was extracted using TriPure Isolation Reagent (Roche), and reverse transcribed to synthesize cDNA. Expression levels of individual genes were quantitated by real-time PCR as described previously19. The primers are listed in the Supplementary Table.

Measurement of oxygen consumption rate in 3T3-L1 adipocytes

The XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) was used to measure oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of 3T3-L1 adipocytes. The day before the assay, confluent cells were suspended in trypsin and re-seeded onto XF96-well microplates at 20% confluency. Measurements of OCR was performed according to the instruction manual of Agilent Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test (Part Number 103016-400). Briefly, OCR was measured at baseline and after sequential addition of (1) oligomycin (ATP synthase inhibitor), (2) carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation uncoupler; FCCP), and (3) rotenone and antimycin A (complex1 inhibitor). To normalize the OCR value by cell numbers, the number of cells per well was counted using ArrayScan VTI (ThermoFisher Scientific) after the staining with DAPI.

RNA sequence analysis

Total RNA from 3T3-L1 adipocytes was extracted using TriPure Isolation Reagent (Roche). RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 ((Agilent Technologies). Poly(A) + RNA was purified and libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) with multiplexed barcode adaptors, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Read quality was assessed by FastQC v0.11.9. Adapter sequences and reads shorter than 50 bp were trimmed using Cutadapt v4.5 with Python v3.10.13. Trimmed reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using STAR Aligner v2.7.8a, and transcript abundance was quantified using RSEM v1.3.3 against the Gencode M33 annotation. The DEGs were identified using edgeR v4.2.1 with the estimateGLMCommonDisp function (method = “deviance”, robust = TRUE, subset = NULL). Genes with a log₂ fold change > 1 or < − 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.1 were considered DEGs. To identify potential upstream transcriptional regulators of these DEGs, we conducted an in silico enrichment analysis using ChIP-Atlas32 (Threshold for Significance = 100), a public database that integrates published ChIP-seq datasets, including those from murine WAT.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean values ± the standard deviation (SD) or standard error (SE). Differences between treatments, groups, and genotypes were analyzed using an unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t-test for 2 groups or a one-way ANOVA (the post hoc Tukey–Kramer HSD test) for more than 3 groups.
