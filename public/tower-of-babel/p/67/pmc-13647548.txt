Optimizing stroke mortality prediction: a comparative study of sampling, cost-sensitive learning, and threshold adjustment
Authors: Rahim Nikbakht-Fard, Leili Tapak, Mojtaba Khazei, Elaheh Talebi-Ghane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke is a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide, creating a critical need for accurate prediction tools to support early clinical decision making. However, mortality prediction is often hindered by class imbalance, as death events represent a minority of cases in most clinical datasets. Various machine learning (ML) approaches and imbalance-handling techniques, including sampling methods, cost-sensitive learning, and threshold adjustment, have been proposed to address this challenge. This study aimed to compare these strategies and identify an optimal ML framework for predicting in-hospital mortality among patients with stroke.MethodsData from 2653 patients with stroke were analyzed. Five machine learning algorithms (XGBoost, RF, DNN, SVM, and LR) were evaluated using nested stratified 10-fold cross-validation. Class imbalance was addressed using threshold adjustment, cost-sensitive learning, and sampling methods (ENN, OSS, SMOTE, SMOTE-ENN, and SVM-SMOTE). Model performance was assessed using Accuracy, F1-score, G-mean, MCC, AUROC, and AUPRC. Statistical comparisons were performed using Friedman and post-hoc tests, and model interpretability was evaluated using SHAP.ResultsAmong the baseline models, XGBoost achieved the highest discrimination performance, with an AUROC of 0.898 ± 0.014 and an AUPRC of 0.656 ± 0.076. Support Vector Machine and Logistic Regression achieved the highest G-mean values, indicating a better balance between sensitivity and specificity. Threshold adjustment based on the G-mean criterion improved the G-mean of XGBoost from 0.661 to 0.804 and increased the F1-score from 0.551 to 0.624, while cost-sensitive learning achieved a comparable G-mean of 0.806. Among the sampling strategies, ENN produced the highest F1-score (0.615 ± 0.026), whereas SMOTE-ENN achieved the highest G-mean (0.799 ± 0.032). Although all sampling methods improved minority-class detection, statistical analysis showed no significant differences among sampling strategies (Friedman χ² = 9.44, p = 0.051). SHAP analysis identified consciousness status, awareness level, patient arrival condition, Glasgow Coma Scale category, respiratory complications, length of hospital stay, and age as the most influential predictors of in-hospital mortality.ConclusionXGBoost achieved the best overall performance for predicting in-hospital mortality among patients with stroke. Threshold adjustment and cost-sensitive learning substantially improved minority-class detection, whereas ENN and SMOTE-ENN provided the most favorable sampling results. Overall, algorithm selection and threshold optimization had a greater impact on predictive performance than the specific sampling strategy. These findings support the potential use of machine-learning models for early risk stratification in stroke care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03707-7.Keywords: Stroke, Machine learning, Imbalanced data, Sampling strategies, Cost-sensitive learning

Introduction

Stroke is a major global health concern and the second leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately 5.5 million deaths annually [1, 2]. Each year, nearly 13.7 million people experience a stroke, making it one of the leading causes of long-term disability. More than half of stroke survivors aged 65 years and older experience persistent impairments in physical mobility, resulting in substantial social and economic burdens on patients, families, and healthcare systems [3, 4]. Therefore, identifying factors associated with stroke mortality and enabling early risk stratification remain important clinical priorities. Stroke is broadly classified into ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes, both of which are associated with considerable morbidity and mortality.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising tool for clinical prediction because of its ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships and interactions among demographic and clinical variables [5–7].Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of ML techniques for predicting adverse clinical outcomes, including mortality, treatment-related complications, and disease progression across a wide range of medical conditions [8–10]. However, accurate mortality prediction remains challenging because of the heterogeneous nature of stroke and the complex interactions among patient characteristics, comorbidities, neurological status, and treatment factors.

One of the major challenges in clinical prediction modeling is class imbalance. In many healthcare datasets, adverse outcomes such as mortality occur relatively infrequently, resulting in substantially fewer observations in the minority class than in the majority class. Under such conditions, conventional ML algorithms often become biased toward the majority class. Consequently, they may achieve high overall accuracy failing to identify patients at high risk of death [8, 9, 11]. Because false negative predictions may delay timely clinical intervention and adversely affect patient outcomes, strategies specifically designed to address class imbalance are particularly important in medical prediction models [12, 13].

Several approaches have been proposed to mitigate the effects of class imbalance. These include sampling-based techniques, cost-sensitive learning, and threshold-adjustment methods [14]. Sampling techniques rebalance the class distributions through oversampling, undersampling, or hybrid approaches [11, 15]. Cost-sensitive learning assigns higher penalties to misclassification of minority-class instances without modifying the original data distribution [12, 15]. Threshold-adjustment strategies modify the classification threshold to improve the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, thereby enhancing the identification of high-risk patients.

Despite the growing use of machine learning in stroke research, limited evidence is available regarding the comparative effectiveness of different imbalance-handling strategies across multiple machine-learning algorithms. Therefore, the present study systematically compares five machine-learning algorithms, including Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Deep Neural Network (DNN), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), in combination with several imbalance-handling approaches. In addition, model interpretability was investigated using SHAP analysis, and statistical comparisons were performed to determine whether observed performance differences were statistically meaningful.

Methods and materials

Data description

A total of 2653 patients with stroke who were admitted to Sina Hospital, Hamadan, Iran, between 2013 and 2018 were included in this retrospective cohort study. The primary objective was to identify factors associated with in-hospital mortality and to develop ML models for mortality prediction. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hamadan University of Medical Sciences, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

Among the included patients, 2221 (83.72%) survived and 432 (16.28%) died during hospitalization. In-hospital mortality was defined as the target outcome variable and was coded as 0 for alive and 1 for death.

A comprehensive set of demographics, clinical, admission-related, and stroke-related variables was extracted from the electronic medical records. These variables encompassed demographic characteristics, pre-stroke comorbidities, neurological assessments, stroke severity indicators, treatment-related factors, post-stroke complications, and physiological measurements. As an initial screening step, variables showing very weak associations with the outcome (p > 0.40) and limited clinical relevance, including marital status, place of residence, smoking status, history of surgery, post-stroke vision problems, post-stroke weakness, and post-stroke immobility, were excluded from further analyses. Following this preliminary screening, 49 candidate predictors were retained for subsequent machine-learning modeling, comprising 43 categorical variables and 6 numerical variables (length of stay, age, respiratory rate, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, and diastolic blood pressure).

Data preprocessing

To ensure a robust and unbiased evaluation, all preprocessing, feature selection, hyperparameter optimization, and model development procedures were embedded within a nested stratified 10-fold cross-validation framework.

Outlier detection was performed using the Isolation Forest algorithm. This method is well suited for high-dimensional clinical datasets because it identifies anomalous observations without assuming any specific data distribution and has demonstrated good performance in medical data analysis [16, 17]. To prevent information leakage, the model was fitted exclusively on the training data within each outer cross-validation fold and applied only to the corresponding training samples. Samples identified as outliers were removed from the training subset, whereas validation data remained untouched. A conservative contamination rate of 5% was adopted to reduce multivariate noise while minimizing the risk of excluding clinically meaningful rare cases.

Missing values were handled using a multivariate iterative imputation approach based on the Extra Trees Regressor, which models missing values by iteratively predicting each variable from all remaining variables [8]. The imputation model was fitted exclusively on the training data within each cross-validation fold and subsequently applied to the corresponding validation fold. No information from the validation data was used during the imputation process.

Missing values in categorical variables were imputed using a constant Missing category and encoded using one-hot encoding, whereas missing values in numerical variables were imputed using an iterative imputation approach. Numerical variables were standardized using z-score normalization after imputation. All preprocessing procedures were learned exclusively from the training data and then applied to the corresponding validation data.

Feature selection was incorporated within the cross-validation framework using a Random Forest–based embedded method. For each training fold, feature importance scores were calculated and the 30 most informative features were retained. The selected feature subset was subsequently applied to the corresponding validation fold.

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using an inner five-fold stratified cross-validation procedure nested within each outer training fold. Machine learning algorithms

To compare the performance of different machine-learning approaches for predicting in-hospital mortality among patients with stroke, five algorithms were evaluated: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Deep Neural Network (DNN), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). These algorithms were selected because they represent a broad spectrum of statistical, kernel-based, ensemble-learning, and deep-learning approaches. These methods are widely used in clinical prediction modeling [10].

The primary objective of this comparison was to identify the algorithm that achieved the best predictive performance for stroke mortality. In addition, the effects of different class-imbalance handling strategies, including sampling methods, cost-sensitive learning, and threshold adjustment, were systematically evaluated across all algorithms. The characteristics and implementation details of each machine-learning algorithm are presented in the following sections.

Extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost)

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is an efficient and scalable implementation of the gradient boosting framework that constructs an ensemble of sequential decision trees to improve predictive performance. Classification and Regression Trees (CART) were used as the base learners, with each successive tree trained to minimize the residual errors generated by the previous ensemble. The final prediction is obtained by aggregating the weighted outputs of all trees, allowing the model to capture complex nonlinear relationships among predictors [18].

Figure 2 illustrates the general structure of the XGBoost model used in this study. During each boosting iteration, a new decision tree is added to reduce the current prediction error by optimizing a regularized objective function. The contribution of each tree is controlled by the learning rate, which improves model stability and generalization while reducing the risk of overfitting [19].

To further enhance robustness, XGBoost incorporates several regularization techniques, including L1 and L2 regularization, shrinkage (learning rate), row subsampling, and column subsampling. In addition, an efficient split-finding algorithm identifies near-optimal partitioning points with low computational cost. These characteristics make XGBoost particularly suitable for high-dimensional clinical datasets containing complex feature interactions and nonlinear relationships.

The output of the final boosted ensemble was converted into the probability of in-hospital mortality using the logistic (sigmoid) function (Fig. 2).

The Deep Neural Network (DNN) model was implemented using the MLPClassifier algorithm in Scikit-learn [20–22]. Hyperparameters were optimized within the nested cross-validation framework. The final architecture consisted of an input layer, two hidden layers with 64 and 32 neurons, respectively, and an output layer for binary classification. ReLU activation functions were used in both hidden layers. A sigmoid activation function was applied to the output layer to estimate the probability of in-hospital mortality.

Random forest (RF)

Random Forest (RF) is an ensemble learning algorithm that constructs multiple decision trees using bootstrap samples of the training data and random subsets of predictor variables at each split. Final predictions are obtained through majority voting across all trees. This approach reduces variance and improves generalization performance. By combining bootstrap aggregation (bagging) with random feature selection, RF increases model robustness and effectively captures complex nonlinear relationships in clinical data [8].

Support vector machine (SVM)

Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a supervised learning algorithm that constructs an optimal separating hyperplane in a high-dimensional feature space. By maximizing the margin between classes, it aims to improve generalization performance and reduce classification error [25]. In this study, an SVM with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel was implemented. Class probabilities were estimated using Platt scaling. This approach enabled the calculation of probability-based performance metrics such as AUROC and AUPRC.

Logistic regression (LR)

Logistic Regression (LR) is a widely used statistical classification method that estimates the probability of a binary outcome using the logistic function. It is widely recognized for its interpretability and simplicity. Owing to its widespread use in clinical prediction research, LR was included as a baseline comparator in this study [26].

Evaluation metrics

Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, F1-score, G-mean, Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve (AUPRC), and the confusion matrix. These metrics were selected to provide a comprehensive assessment of classifier performance in the presence of class imbalance. The F1-score combines precision and recall through their harmonic mean and is particularly informative when the minority class is of primary interest:

Although accuracy is widely used, it can be misleading for imbalanced datasets because high accuracy may be achieved by correctly classifying only the majority class. Therefore, imbalance-aware metrics were emphasized in this study.

The G-mean is defined as the geometric mean of sensitivity and specificity and evaluates the balance between correctly identifying positive and negative cases. A low sensitivity or specificity results in a low G-mean, indicating poor classification performance for one or both classes. MCC provides a balanced measure of classification performance by considering all elements of the confusion matrix and has been shown to be robust in the presence of class imbalance [27].

AUROC summarizes classifier discrimination across all possible classification thresholds by plotting sensitivity against (1 - specificity) [28]. However, for imbalanced datasets, the Precision–Recall (P–R) curve and its corresponding area under the curve (AUPRC) are often more informative because they focus on the trade-off between precision and recall for the minority class [26]. Given the imbalanced distribution of the mortality outcome, model comparison primarily relied on F1-score, G-mean, MCC, AUROC, and AUPRC rather than accuracy alone.

Hyperparameter optimization and feature selection

Hyperparameter optimization was performed within a nested cross-validation framework to obtain an unbiased estimate of model performance [8, 29]. For each outer training fold, a fivefold stratified cross-validation grid search was conducted to identify the optimal hyperparameter combination for each machine-learning algorithm. Candidate hyperparameter values were evaluated using only the training data, and the combination with the best average performance was selected. To prevent information leakage, all model-development procedures, including preprocessing, feature selection, and hyperparameter optimization, were performed exclusively within the training folds [29, 30]. The optimized hyperparameter settings for all algorithms are presented in Table 2.

Feature selection was performed within the nested cross-validation framework using a Random Forest–based embedded approach [29, 31]. Predictor importance scores were estimated from the fitted Random Forest model and used to identify the most informative variables. Within each training fold, the 30 highest-ranked predictors were retained and subsequently applied to the corresponding validation fold. Restricting feature selection to the training data minimized information leakage and ensured an unbiased evaluation of model performance.

To improve model interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to the final XGBoost model, which demonstrated the best overall predictive performance. SHAP is a game-theoretic framework that quantifies the contribution of each feature to individual predictions by estimating Shapley values. These values provide both global and local explanations of model behavior. They also facilitate the identification of predictors most strongly associated with in-hospital mortality [32].

Class imbalance handling strategies

Because in-hospital mortality represented the minority class in the study cohort, three complementary imbalance-handling strategies were evaluated: threshold adjustment, cost-sensitive learning, and data sampling. These approaches aim to improve the detection of death cases while maintaining overall classification performance.

Threshold adjustment

Most classification algorithms use a default decision threshold of 0.5 to assign class labels. However, this threshold may be suboptimal for imbalanced datasets. Therefore, alternative thresholds were identified using two commonly employed criteria: (i) maximization of the G-mean on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and (ii) maximization of the F1-score on the precision–recall (PR) curve. These approaches optimize the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. As a result, they improve the detection of the minority class [8].

Cost-sensitive learning

Cost-sensitive learning addresses class imbalance without modifying the original data distribution. In this approach, higher misclassification costs are assigned to minority-class observations during model training. Consequently, misclassifying death cases incurs greater penalties. This encourages the learning algorithm to place greater emphasis on correctly identifying patients at risk of in-hospital mortality [33].

Sampling methods

Five sampling techniques were evaluated in this study to address class imbalance: Edited Nearest Neighbors (ENN), One-Sided Selection (OSS), Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE), SVM-SMOTE, and SMOTE-ENN.

ENN is an undersampling method that removes observations whose class labels differ from those of their nearest neighbors. This process reduces noise and improves class separability [15, 34]. OSS is an undersampling technique that removes redundant and noisy majority-class observations while preserving informative minority-class samples. As a result, it produces a more balanced dataset [35].

SMOTE is a widely used oversampling method that generates synthetic minority-class samples by interpolating between existing minority observations and their nearest neighbors. This approach increases the representation of the minority class [15, 36]. SVM-SMOTE extends SMOTE by generating synthetic samples near the decision boundary identified by support vectors. This improves the representation of difficult-to-classify minority instances and enhances classifier performance in overlapping regions [15].

SMOTE-ENN is a hybrid sampling approach that combines the advantages of oversampling and data cleaning. Synthetic minority-class samples are first generated using SMOTE. ENN is then applied to remove noisy and potentially misclassified observations. This strategy improves class balance while reducing class overlap and noise [8, 36].

Results

Baseline characteristics

Tables 1–1 and 1–2 summarize the demographic, clinical, and physiological characteristics of the study population according to hospital outcome. These univariate analyses were performed for descriptive purposes only. Predictor selection was conducted using a Random Forest–based embedded feature-selection approach within the nested cross-validation framework. A total of 2653 stroke patients were included in the study. Of these 2221 (83.7%) survived and 432 (16.3%) died during hospitalization, indicating substantial class imbalance. Missing values were most frequent for GCS category (22.9%), previous recurrent stroke (22.4%), respiratory rate (19.9%), opiate use (14.7%), heart rate (14.2%), and education level (13.4%). Receiving blood (9.5%), patient arrival mode (8.3%), type of stroke (5.5%), and job status (4.7%) also exhibited moderate levels of missingness. All remaining variables contained less than 2% missing values. Variables with more than 30% missingness were excluded during data preparation. Consequently, no retained variable exceeded this threshold.

Several demographic and clinical variables showed notable differences between survivors and non-survivors in the descriptive analyses. These findings are presented only to characterize the study population. They should not be interpreted as the basis for predictor selection, which was performed using the embedded feature-selection procedure described above.

Discussion

This study aimed to develop and evaluate machine-learning models for predicting in-hospital mortality among patients with stroke. Among the evaluated algorithms, XGBoost demonstrated the strongest overall predictive performance, achieving the highest AUROC and AUPRC values. The superior performance of XGBoost may be attributed to its ability to capture complex nonlinear relationships and interactions among clinical predictors. It also controls overfitting through regularization and subsampling mechanisms. These properties are particularly advantageous in stroke datasets, where mortality is influenced by multiple interacting demographic, physiological, and neurological factors.

In recent years, machine-learning approaches have been increasingly applied to structured electronic health record data to improve the prediction of clinical outcomes, including mortality, complications, and disease progression [37]. Consistent with previous studies, our findings support the use of gradient-boosting methods for modeling complex clinical data and predicting stroke outcomes [38–40]. Support Vector Machine and Logistic Regression achieved a better balance between sensitivity and specificity. However, XGBoost and Random Forest demonstrated superior overall discriminative performance, as reflected by their higher AUROC and AUPRC values. These findings are in agreement with previous research indicating that gradient-boosting algorithms are particularly effective for prognostic modeling in patients with stroke and often outperform conventional statistical approaches and several alternative machine-learning methods [38–40].

After applying imbalance-handling strategies, substantial improvements were observed in G-mean, F1-score, and minority-class detection across all algorithms, although these gains were generally accompanied by a reduction in overall accuracy. Threshold adjustment based on the maximum G-mean or F1-score consistently enhanced the sensitivity of the models to the minority (death) class, confirming that the conventional decision threshold of 0.5 is often suboptimal for imbalanced clinical datasets [41]. Threshold adjustment does not require retraining the model. Therefore, the underlying probability estimates remain unchanged, and only the final classification rule is modified. Consequently, this approach can improve minority-class detection without increasing model complexity or training time [33].

As expected, AUROC and AUPRC remained largely unchanged following threshold adjustment because both metrics are threshold-independent and evaluate the ranking ability of the classifier across all possible thresholds [42]. The observed improvements therefore reflect a more favorable balance between sensitivity and specificity rather than changes in the intrinsic discriminative capacity of the model. These findings suggest that threshold optimization represents a simple yet effective strategy for improving the clinical utility of mortality prediction models in stroke patients.

Cost-sensitive learning also improved minority-class detection by assigning greater importance to death cases during model training. This approach increased sensitivity and G-mean and generally improved F1-score while preserving the original class distribution. Unlike sampling-based methods, cost-sensitive learning incorporates class imbalance directly into the learning process. Therefore, it avoids the potential information loss associated with undersampling or the generation of synthetic observations [42]. Taken together, these findings indicate that both threshold adjustment and cost-sensitive learning are effective approaches for mitigating class imbalance and enhancing the identification of high-risk stroke patients.

Sampling strategies

To address class imbalance, we evaluated five sampling strategies, including ENN, OSS, SMOTE, SMOTE-ENN, and SVM-SMOTE. Overall, all sampling methods improved minority-class detection compared with the original dataset, as reflected by higher F1-score and G-mean values. Among the evaluated approaches, ENN provided the most consistent improvement across performance metrics, achieving the highest F1-score while substantially increasing the G-mean. This finding suggests that removing noisy and potentially overlapping observations from the majority class may enhance class separability and facilitate the identification of high-risk patients. This result is consistent with previous studies demonstrating the benefits of data-cleaning and overlap-reduction strategies for imbalanced datasets [13, 15, 34].

SMOTE-ENN achieved the highest G-mean, indicating the best balance between sensitivity and specificity. However, this improvement was accompanied by a reduction in overall accuracy, highlighting the trade-off between minority-class detection and global classification performance. In contrast, OSS preserved the highest accuracy but yielded more modest gains in F1-score and G-mean. Although SMOTE and SVM-SMOTE improved minority-class performance relative to the original data, their gains were smaller than those observed with ENN and SMOTE-ENN. One possible explanation is that synthetic oversampling introduces observations near class boundaries. This may increase overlap between the death and survival classes and reduce the clarity of the decision boundary [13, 43]. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, where data-cleaning and hybrid sampling approaches often outperformed pure oversampling methods in imbalanced clinical datasets [15, 35, 43].

Overall, these findings indicate that no single sampling strategy demonstrated clear statistical superiority in terms of overall discrimination performance. Nevertheless, XGBoost combined with threshold adjustment and ENN-based resampling achieved the most favorable numerical balance between discrimination and minority-class detection in the present study. Future research should evaluate additional imbalance-handling techniques and validate these findings using independent multicenter datasets.

Beyond model performance, the interpretability analysis provided additional clinical insights into the factors associated with in-hospital mortality.

SHAP analysis identified consciousness status, awareness level, patient arrival condition, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), respiratory complications, length of hospital stay, and age as the most influential predictors. Consciousness status and awareness level showed the greatest contributions to the model predictions, followed by indicators of neurological severity (GCS), mode of arrival to the hospital, respiratory status, and patient age. These findings are clinically plausible because neurological impairment at admission is strongly associated with stroke severity and mortality risk. Similarly, advanced age, respiratory complications, and prolonged hospitalization have been consistently linked to poor outcomes among patients with stroke [1, 38, 39].

Beyond improving predictive performance, these findings provide clinically meaningful insights into mortality risk. Several of the most influential predictors are routinely available during the early stages of hospitalization, suggesting that machine-learning models may support clinical decision-making through early risk stratification. Early identification of high-risk patients could facilitate timely intervention, closer monitoring, and more efficient allocation of healthcare resources. Furthermore, interpretable machine-learning techniques such as SHAP improve model transparency. They provide clinically understandable explanations for individual predictions and may increase the acceptability of these tools in routine clinical practice.

From an implementation perspective, the proposed framework could be integrated into electronic health record–based clinical decision support systems. By automatically estimating mortality risk using routinely collected patient information, such systems may assist healthcare professionals in prioritizing patient management and identifying individuals who may benefit from more intensive care. However, prospective evaluation is required before routine clinical deployment can be recommended.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the study was based on data obtained from a single tertiary-care hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other populations and healthcare settings. Second, although nested cross-validation provided robust internal validation and reduced the risk of optimistic performance estimation, no external validation cohort was available. Therefore, the reported results should be interpreted as internally validated estimates rather than definitive evidence of model generalizability.

Third, the data were collected between 2013 and 2018. Temporal changes in stroke management, patient characteristics, and clinical practice may therefore influence model performance in more recent populations. In addition, Isolation Forest was applied conservatively and exclusively within the training folds. Nevertheless, some clinically meaningful rare cases may have been identified as outliers.

Finally, although XGBoost achieved the best overall performance in the present dataset, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of universal superiority over other machine-learning algorithms or imbalance-handling strategies. Model performance may vary across institutions, patient populations, and outcome prevalences. Future studies should therefore validate the proposed framework using independent multicenter cohorts and temporally distinct datasets to assess its robustness, transportability, and clinical applicability.

Conclusion

In conclusion, machine-learning models can effectively predict in-hospital mortality among patients with stroke, particularly when appropriate class-imbalance handling strategies are applied. Among the evaluated algorithms, XGBoost achieved the best overall predictive performance and the highest discrimination ability. Threshold adjustment and cost-sensitive learning substantially improved minority-class detection. In contrast, sampling methods produced only modest and statistically non-significant improvements in overall discrimination.

SHAP analysis identified neurological status, particularly consciousness level, awareness status, and Glasgow Coma Scale, as the most influential predictors of mortality. These findings suggest that algorithm selection and threshold optimization may have a greater impact on predictive performance than the choice of sampling strategy. However, external validation using independent multicenter cohorts is required before routine clinical implementation can be recommended.
