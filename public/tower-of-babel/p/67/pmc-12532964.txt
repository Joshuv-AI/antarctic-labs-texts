Exploring the pathogenesis of steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head co-regulated by mesenchymal stem cells and pyroptosis-related genes with the use of transcriptome
Authors: Xiang-Bin Rong, Wei-Wei Wang, Qiang Ding, Zhao Tian, Liang Guo, Hong-Cheng Tao, Chi-Cheng Niu, Xue-Shen Mo, Jin-Fu Liu, Ping Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and pyroptosis play an important part in steroid-induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SONFH). The present study aimed at the identification and analysis of MSC and pyroptosis-related biomarkers for the treatment of SONFH, as well as the elucidation of their biological mechanisms.MethodsThe present study first use the outliers in GSE123568 data set were removed by principal component analysis (PCA). Next, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in GSE123568 were identified using different expression analysis. Then, immune infiltration analysis and Wilcoxon test were performed. Later, acquires the DE_MSCs between DEGs and MSC, and DE_PRGs between DEGs and PRGs score. After, the top 20 intersected genes were selected by overlapping the two related genes. Afterward, select characteristic genes and diagnostic genes. Finally, the function enrichment analysis, drug prediction, molecular docking, molecular dynamic simulation. Then, reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT qPCR), Western blot analysis, and immunohistochemical staining were performed on the femoral head specimens separately. (The model group specimens are from necrotic femoral heads, while the control group specimens are from femoral heads of patients with femoral neck fractures).ResultsThe expression of FKBP8 and ANK1 was significantly lower in RT qPCR, western blot analysis, and immunohistochemical staining compared to the control samples. The expression of PTGS2 is actually higher.ConclusionsIn this study, FKBP8, PTGS2 and ANK1 were identified as the biomarkers of SONFH, providing a potential rationale for diagnosing and treating SONFH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-02920-w.

Introduction

Steroid induced osteonecrosis of the femoral head (SONFH) is a severe skeletal disorder, and is usually attributed to prolonged and/or high-dose use of steroid drugs [1, 2]. Steroids are a class of drugs that have been extensively used for many diseases, including but not limited to rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune diseases and transplant rejection [3, 4]. SONFH poses a serious threat to human health, particularly to the individuals aged 30–50 years. In the absence of timely and effective therapy, up to 80% of the patients diagnosed with SONFH will undergo the collapse of the femoral within 1–4 years, for which artificial joint replacement is required [5, 6]. SONFH usually presents as hip pain, which may spread to the thighs and knees as the disease progresses, resulting in symptoms, such as limited movement, abnormal gait, and muscle atrophy [7]. It has been identified that the majority of SONFH patients have a history of the use of hormones, including glucocorticoids [8], which may affect the vascular homeostasis and bone cell function of the femoral head in various ways. Regarding vascular homeostasis, it is recognized as a critical element in the pathogenesis of SONFH. Specifically, the use of hormones has been found to trigger vasoconstriction and microcirculation disorders, resulting in insufficient blood supply to the femoral head; moreover, the use of hormones causes the formation of blood clots, further aggravating the blood supply. All such vascular disturbances cause ischemia and hypoxia of the femoral head, ultimately resulting in osteonecrosis [3]. While, for the bone cell function, it may be directed affected by the use of hormones, and studies have shown that the use of hormones caused increased osteocyte apoptosis, leading to the destruction of bone tissue and osteonecrosis [9, 10]. Furthermore, hormones may inhibit the osteogenic function of osteocytes, leading to osteoporosis and damaged structure of the femoral head [11]. Other than the use of hormones, the development of SONFH may also related to genetic factors, such as vascular endothelial growth factor and nitric oxide, as well as behaviors, such as alcoholism and smoking and physical conditions like obesity [12, 13]. The treatment of SONFH includes both operative and nonoperative treatments. Nonoperative treatments include activity restriction, physical therapy, and medication, while operative treatment includes bone reconstruction, such as femoral head replacement surgery [14]. Although SONFH is a serious disease, its early diagnosis and treatment are conducive to the significant improvement of improve patient outcomes. Thus, it is of great value to identify the biomarkers in the early stage of SONFH.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are multipotent stem cells that able to self-renew and present multilineage differentiation. Under normal circumstances, MSCs are involved in bone repair and regeneration [15]. However, some studies have shown that dysplasia of adipogenesis and osteogenic differentiation of MSCs is a contributing factor to SONFH [16]. Pyroptosis is an inflammatory cell death caused by severe cell injury, ischemia and/or hypoxia [9]. Pyroptosis-related genes are associated with cell death by regulating apoptosis signaling pathway, inflammatory response and cell cycle, etc. It has been indicated an important part of pyroptosis in the formation and development of necrotic bone tissue during the occurrence of SONFH, and for which 10 pyroptosis-related genes have been identified as potential key genes of SONFH and thus are of great value for diagnosis [5]. Although studies have demonstrated that pyroptosis and MSCs work together in bone degenerative diseases, Huang Guanrui et al. protected the function of MSCs of intervertebral disc by inhibiting the pyroptosis of intervertebral disc cells, thereby improving the therapeutic effect of intervertebral disc degeneration [17]. However, there are no reports on the genes related to both MSCs and pyroptosis in SONFH. Therefore, understanding the association between SONFH and MSC-related genes (MSC-RGs) and pyroptosis-related genes (PRGs) has important clinical and biological significance.

Herein, the biomarkers involved in the interaction of SONFH with MSCs and pyroptosis were identified by bioinformatics means. Since understanding the correlation between SONFH and MSCS has important clinical and biological significance, the very study will help to reveal the molecular mechanism for the occurrence and development of SONFH, and thereby providing theoretical foundation for developing effective strategies to prevent and treat SONFH. Materials and methods

Data collection

In this study, data sets related to SONFH were extracted from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including GSE123568, GSE74089 data sets. Specifically, the data sets comprised 30 SONFH and 10 control blood samples based on the GPL15207 platform for GSE123568, 4 SONFH and 4 control blood samples based on GPL13497 platform for GSE74089, respectively. In addition, 42 PRGs were obtained from the existing literature [18].

Differential expression analysis

To identify outlier samples in GSE123568 data set, principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out using “FactoMineR” (v 2.8) [19]. Samples falling outside the 95% confidence interval were considered as outliers and then excluded for further analyses. Following this, differential expression analyses between SONFH and control samples from GSE123568 data set were conducted with the “limma” package (v 3.52.4) [20] and the screening criteria were |log2Fold Change (FC)|> 1 and adj. P < 0.05, using Bonferroni correction. In addition, volcano plots and heatmaps were obtained with “ggplot2” (v 3.3.6) [21] and “pheatmap” (v 1.0.12) [22], respectively, to visualize the DEGs. It was worth noting that the heatmaps displayed only the top 20 DEGs.

Immune infiltration analysis

In GSE123568 data set, xCell was utilized to determine the immunoscore of 64 types of immune cell, including MSCs, in both SONFH and control samples. xCell (https://xcell.ucsf.edu/) constructed a reference gene expression signature matrix and preprocessed the input gene expression data. It calculated enrichment scores based on ssGSEA, further converted the enrichment scores into cell type scores and performed compensation correction, and finally output the relative abundance estimates of immune cells and stromal cells in each sample. Subsequently, statistical analysis was carried out with the Wilcoxon test (P < 0.05) with respect to the differences in scores of cell infiltration from SONFH to control samples.

Correlation analysis

First, the Spearman’s rank correlation was carried out with the threshold for correlation coefficient of |r|> 0.3 and P < 0.05 to obtain the DE_MSCs between DEGs and MSCs. In addition, the PRGs score was derived with the use of single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) from “GSVA” (v 1.48.3) [23]. Then the DE_PRGs between DEGs and PRGs score was mined by Spearman's rank correlation (|r|> 0.3 and P < 0.05). Finally, the intersected genes were identified by overlapping the relate genes 1 and relate genes 2 by “ggvenn” (v 1.2.2) [24].

Identification and function analysis of candidate genes

To comprehend the protein interaction of the intersected genes, the genes without corresponding proteins were initially filtered, then the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was built based on STRING database (https://string-db.org/) with a confidence threshold of > 0.4. Meanwhile, this PPI network was visualized with the use of a network analysis software (Cytoscape 3.9.1) [25]. Afterward, candidate genes were identified using CytoHubba plugin by specifically overlapping the top 20 genes identified by Betweenness and BottleNeck algorithms, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2A–C.

Besides, the functions of derived candidate genes were analyzed by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)to investigate their biological processes and signaling pathways using clusterProfiler (v 4.7.1.001) [26], with a threshold level for statistical significance of P < 0.05 (Hypergeometric Test).

Machine learning

In light of the derived candidate genes, further feature selection was carried out by support vector machine-recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE) and Boruta using “e1071” (v 1.7.13) [27] and “Boruta”(v 8.0.0) [28], respectively; then by overlapping the genes selected from the two machine learning methods, the feature genes were determined.

Identification of biomarkers

For evaluating the value of determined feature genes for the diagnosis of SONFH in GSE123568, GSE74089 data sets, respectively, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted with the use of the “pROC” (v 1.18.4) [29], and then the genes for diagnosis purpose were screened with the criterion of an area under the curve (AUC) > 0.8.

Following this, gene expression analyses were performed for the determined genes for genetic diagnosis in both GSE123568 and GSE74089 via Wilcoxon test (SONFH vs control), then the genes with significant difference (P < 0.05) and consistent expression trend in both GSE123568 and GSE74089 were determined as the biomarkers for subsequent analyses.

Establishment and verification the nomogram

In view of the determined biomarkers, a nomogram was drawn using “rms” (v 6.7.1) [30]. Subsequently, a calibration curve of the nomogram was constructed, and closer the slope of which to 1, the greater accuracy of the model in predicting SONFH. Meanwhile, the accuracy of the nomogram was evaluated by plotting ROC curve with the use of “pROC” package and use permutation testing.

Subcellular distribution and function analysis of biomarkers

To figure out the role of the identified biomarkers in SONFH, Gene database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/) and mRNALocater database (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/hum-multi-2/#) were searched for the FASTA file and the distribution in 5 subcellulars (cytoplasm, endoplasmic reticulum, extracellular region, mitochondria, nucleus) of the biomarkers.

In addition, to further understand the biomarkers, the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted. First, the Spearman’s rank correlation was completed using “psych” (v 2.3.6) to investigate the relationship of the biomarkers with other genes [31], and the correlation coefficients were calculated and ranked. Then the background gene set “KEGG” was sourced from “msigdbr” (v 7.5.1). Finally, the GSEA was conducted via “clusterProfiler”, and the screening criterion was adj. P < 0.05.

Regulatory network analysis

To explore the underlying molecular regulatory network of the biomarkers, the miRNA targeting biomarkers and the lncRNA targeting miRNA were predicted by miRDB database (https://mirdb.org) and starBase (https://rnasysu.com/encori/index.php) with a screening criterion of clipExpNum > 30, respectively, and based on which, a lncRNA–miRNA–biomarker network was built and then visualized via Cytoscape.

Besides, the lncRNAs, which collectively targeted miRNAs through multiple biomarkers, were selected for subcellular localization analysis using lncLocator, database (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/lncLocator/index.html).

Drug prediction and molecular docking

To find drugs for treating SONFH, potential drugs targeting the biomarkers were searched on basis of the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, http://ctdbase.org/) and the biomarker–drug network was visualized with Cytoscape.

Subsequently, molecular docking analysis was conducted for the drugs targeting multiple biomarkers simultaneously. Initially, the 3D protein structures of the biomarkers were sourced from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Next, by retrieving the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), 2D structures of the drugs were obtained and then converted into 3D structures using Babel GUI software. Next, molecular docking simulations were performed with AutoDockTools. A drug–biomarker docking energy less than − 5 kcal/mol suggested a high binding affinity. The results were visualized using PyMOL system. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations were done with GROMACS 2024.4, following the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field. The TIP3P water model was used, and the simulation box was cubic. A 1 nm gap was kept between box edges and the protein surface; ions were added for electro-neutrality. Energy minimization was done via the steepest descent method. Then, the system was equilibrated in NVT and NPT ensembles. In NVT and NPT, temperature coupling used the V-rescale method (300 K, 2 fs time step, 100 ps). The production molecular dynamics simulation ran for 20 ns. RMSD of the protein–ligand complex, RMSF of the protein, total energy, and number of hydrogen bonds were calculated.

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Ten blood samples (5 normal and 5 SONFH) in total were acquired from the clinic in the Ruikang Hospital affiliated to Guangxi University of Chinese Medicine. The study had granted an approval with number of KY2024-157 from the Ethics Committee of the Ruikang Hospital, and a signed informed consent was provided by all subjects.

First, total RNA extraction was performed for the 10 samples with the TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Next, the extracted RNA was analyzed for its concentration using the NanoPhotometer® N50. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription with the SureScript™ First-strand cDNA synthesis kit, and the reverse transcription reaction was conducted with the S1000™ Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA). RT-qPCR assays were carried out by the CFX Connect Real-time Quantitative Fluorescence PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA), with the conditions as follows: 95 °C for 1 min (pre-denaturation), then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s (denaturation), 55 °C for 20 s (annealing), 72 °C for 30 s (extension). The relative quantification of mRNA levels was obtained with the use of the 2(− Delta Delta C(T)) method. The primer sequences refer to Supplementary Table S1. In the RT-qPCR experiment, GAPDH (glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase), as a key enzyme in cellular basal metabolism, is widely expressed in various cell types and tissues. Its expression remains stable under different experimental treatments and disease conditions, making it a commonly used reference gene. Therefore, GAPDH was selected as the housekeeping gene in this experiment.

Western blot detection of protein levels

After preparing the SDS-PAGE gel, extract the protein, use RIPA lysate to crush the cell sample, then take part of the protein and use the nucleic acid protein quantizer to quantify the protein, add 20 µg of the corresponding volume of total protein sample and 5 × protein gel electrophoresis loading buffer, gently mix, denature at 95 °C for 10 min, immediately insert into the ice for use, gently add the protein sample to the gel hole for electrophoresis, after the gel electrophoresis, transfer the protein bands separated from the gel to the solid phase supported by transfer electrophoresis, and then incubate and detect them with unmarked primary antibody and horseradish peroxidase labeled secondary antibody, respectively.

Immunohistochemical detection

Take paraffin sections of femoral head tissues from each group, perform routine dewaxing and hydration, and perform antigen repair. Dilute the secondary antibody appropriately according to the reagent instructions and incubate at 37 °C for 30 min; wash three times with PBS, each time for 3 min. DAB color development takes 5–10 min, sealed with neutral gum and observed under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Herein, all relevant statistical analyses were completed using R (v 4.2.3), and unless otherwise specified, the significance threshold was P < 0.05.

Identification of biomarkers FKBP8, PTGS2 and ANK1

For the 13 candidate genes, 7 and 12 genes were screened by SVM–RFE (errorlowest = 0.0551) and Boruta, respectively (Fig. 3A, B), and moreover, by overlapping the screened genes, 7 feature genes were selected (Fig. 3C). Subsequently, the ROC curves of both GSE123568 and GSE74089 data sets revealed that AUC values of 6 genes for diagnosis, including indicating that FKBP8, PTGS2, PECAM1, ANK1, GATA1, and FCGR3A, were greater than 0.8 Following that, gene expression analysis was conducted for the 6 genes, and the results revealed that in SONFH samples from both the GSE123568 and GSE74089 data sets, the expression of FKBP8 and ANK1 was low, while the expression of PTGS2 was high (Fig. 3F, G). Thus, FKBP8, PTGS2, and ANK1 were deemed as the biomarkers for subsequent analyses. For the three biomarkers, a nomogram was drawn, and for which the higher the point, the higher the risk of SONFH (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, the calibration curve and ROC curve (AUC = 1) also illustrated the reliability of the nomogram as a predictor (Fig. 4B, C). Subsequently, we added permutation tests to the ROC model, and the results is shown in Supplementary Figure S2. Drug–biomarker network analysis

By searching the CTD database, 65 drugs targeting the three biomarkers were found, and then a drug–biomarker network was constructed, covering the 3 biomarkers, the 65 drugs and 72 edges (Fig. 7A). Among the drugs, seven drugs were predicted to jointly target two of the three biomarkers (ANK1 and PTGS2), including ochratoxin A, bisphenol A, Aflatoxin B1, vehicle emissions, arsenic, benzo(a)pyrene, and CGP 5260. Subsequently, molecular docking analysis between ochratoxin A, bisphenol A, Aflatoxin B1, benzo(a)pyrene, CGP 52608, and the three biomarkers revealed excellent binding energies (< − 5 kcal/mol) between these drugs and the biomarkers (Table 1). Figure 7B depicts the molecular docking binding sites for ANK1 and Aflatoxin B1, demonstrating a docking binding energy of − 6.71 kcal/mol mediated by ARG-1437 and LEU-1417 residues. The other results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 3. Subsequently, molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to deeply understand the drug’s mechanism of action and evaluate the stability of the complex formed by the drug and the biomarker. In molecular dynamics simulations, smaller RMSD fluctuations indicated more stable binding between the small molecule and the protein, while smaller RMSF fluctuations suggested a more rigid region, and larger RMSF fluctuations implied a more flexible region. Typically, more rigid regions are likely to be binding sites. The analysis results revealed that both RMSD and RMSF fluctuations were minimal, demonstrating the stable binding of the drug to the biomarker (Supplementary Fig. 4 A, B). Furthermore, throughout all simulations, the total energy remained at a low level with a stable trend and minimal fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 4 C), and the minimum distance between the ligand and the protein also showed stable temporal changes (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Discussion

SONFH is a serious disabling condition that reduces the mechanical strength of bone, resulting in the collapse of the femoral head and arthritis of the hip [12]. In general, SONFH is caused by trauma, alcohol and the use of glucocorticoids [12, 32], and may also be related to excessive smoking, sickle cell disease, systemic lupus erythematosus, organ transplantation, HIV infection, coagulation disorders, decompression sickness, myeloproliferative disease (bone marrow accumulation disease) and radiation necrosis [33–35]. Over the years, the clinical incidence of SONFH is continuously increasing being dominated by non-traumatic SONFH [36]. Various mechanisms have been used to explain the occurrence of SONFH, such as abnormal intravascular coagulation, abnormal lipid metabolism, uncoupled bone remodeling, oxidative stress injury, abnormal differentiation of bone marrow MSCs, and pyroptosis [37].

Pyroptosis is an inflammatory cell death involved in the regulation of related genes [38]. Many scholars believe that the changes in osteocyte activity caused by the pyroptosis process play an important part in regulating the balance of bone metabolism and maintaining bone homeostasis [39], and may also be critical for the development of SONFH. So far, some studies have found that long-term application of glucocorticoids can cause pyroptosis of bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs), leading to the decline of osteogenic differentiation ability, which may contribute to the collapse of femoral head and the failure of osteonecrosis repair treatment for SONFH patients [40]. The regulation of pyroptosis by glucocorticoids is a complex dynamic pathological process with multiple targets, multiple pathways, and participation in various molecular biological responses [41, 42]. Through bioinformatics and animal experiments, it was found that 10 genes were involved in SONFH, and of great values for diagnosis purpose [5], but genes related to MSCs and pyroptosis in SONFH have not been reported. Herein, with focus on the variations in the expression level of the genes in relation to MSCs and pyroptosis co-regulated SONFH, the biomarkers were identified and their molecular biological characteristics and molecular mechanisms affecting the occurrence and development of SONFH were explored. The results obtained are of great significance both in theory and practice for SONFH in terms of understanding the pathological mechanism, early diagnosis and if appropriate, prognosis.

The nomogram was drawn according to the expression of the three biomarkers, and the slope of the calibration curve was close to 1, indicating a diagnostic power of the three biomarkers for SONFH. The AUC value of the ROC curve drawn was 1, demonstrating that the accuracy of prediction based on the nomogram model was excellent. According to the results of bioinformatics analysis for the GSE123568 data set of blood samples and the GSE74089 data set of hip cartilage samples, the top three intersected genes associated with MSCs and pyroptosis were further identified in terms of their expression levels. In comparison with the SONFH group, the mRNA levels of the FKBP8 and ANK13 genes were significantly up-regulated, while the mRNA levels of the PTGS2 gene were slightly reduced without significant difference, in the normal group. Although the increase in PTGS2 gene expression was not statistically significant, this may suggest its potential role in the pathogenesis of SONFH. PTGS2 (prostaglandin synthase 2) is closely related to inflammatory response and cell proliferation, and its upregulation may reflect the enhanced inflammatory response during the course of SONFH. However, this change may be limited by other regulatory factors, resulting in the upregulation of its expression level not reaching statistical significance. This observation was generally consistent in both the GSE123568 and GSE123568 data sets. It has been reported that FKBP8 and ANK13 genes are associated with osteogenesis, chondrogenic and angiogenesis of MSCs, and PTGS2 gene expression is associated with inflammation, autophagy and pyroptosis. Milton Osmar Aguilera et al. found that FKBP8 is involved as a new molecule in the regulation of autophagy pathway, and exists in mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum, overexpression of FKBP8 protein can trigger the autophagy cascade. Moreover, it is also involved in intracellular transport and protein folding [43, 44], apoptosis, cell growth and differentiation during multiple pathological processes, and plays a part in immune regulation [45]. A series of immune cascade reactions caused by pyroptosis in SONFH requires the participation of a variety of molecules, but FKBP8 has not been reported in existing studies on SONFH. However, bioinformatics analysis indicated that in SONFH pathology, and MSCs were positively correlated; specifically, the overexpression of FKBP8 can inhibit the programmed cell death, and can promote the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, thereby achieving the purpose of bone repair. The protein of ANK1 gene is an important component of the erythrocyte membrane structure, thus the gene is critical for the maintenance of both the structure and functional activities of erythrocyte membrane [46, 47]. As a candidate marker gene of SONFH, the mRNA levels of ANK1 was lower in the peripheral blood samples of the patients from three disease groups than in that of the non-SONFH group, and the expression in whole blood and influenced femoral head was lower for SONFH rats at 12 weeks (middle stage) than for that of normal controls, indicating a potential relationship between ANK1 and the development of SONFH blood stasis syndrome [48]. The above studies show that ANK1 and MSCs are positively correlated. Most studies on osteonecrosis of femoral head (ONFH) suggest that the normal repair function of bone tissue cells in ONFH patients is impaired, eventually leading to irreversible tissue destruction [49]. However, cartilage degeneration is also significantly related to ONFH with respect to its occurrence and development [50]. Since reactive oxygen species (ROS) plays a part in apoptosis of chondrocyte, activation of metalloproteinases and decreasing synthesis of matrix components, PTGS2 (also called COX-2), which can remarkably upregulates the matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP 1) in articular cartilage, can cooperate with and jointly participate in ROS in regulating oxidative stress, thus enhancing autophagy in cartilage [51, 52]. In inflamed joint tissues, the production of PGE2 is increased due to upregulated COX-2, while PGE2 is related to inflammation, apoptosis, and angiogenesis [53], and present a positive and negative correlation with pyroptosis in the process of SONFH pathology, and with MSCs, respectively. In light of the previous studies and reports, it is the first time that FKBP8 is found to play a part in the pathological process of SONFH. According to the study on FKBP8 in autophagy regulation, it is possible to also be involved in the process of MSCs, thereby influencing the pathological mechanism of SONFH. Therefore, it is believed that FKBP8, PTGS2 and ANK1 have good diagnostic performance for SONFH, contributing to clinical application. The traditional diagnosis of SONFH relies on imaging examinations (such as X-rays and MRI) and pain symptoms, but there are often no typical manifestations in the early stages, making diagnosis difficult. In addition, biomarkers can be detected before imaging shows abnormalities, making early diagnosis of SONFH possible. This study has validated the effectiveness of the biomarker through RT qPCR of blood samples. In the future, efforts can be made to develop serological detection kits to increase early diagnostic methods while avoiding the trauma of traditional biopsies [54, 55].

To clarify the underlying molecular mechanism of the reference MSCs and pyrogenesis co-regulated SONFH, a GSEA analysis was conducted to evaluate FKBP8, PTGS2 and ANK1 regarding their biological functions. According to the results of GSEA analysis, FKBP8 and ANK1 were positively correlated with olfactory transduction and neuroactive ligand receptor interactions, but negatively correlated with spliceosome, ribosome and oxidative phosphorylation. PTGS2 interaction, spliceosome, ribosome, and oxidative phosphorylation with the neuroactive ligand receptor were positively and negatively correlated with olfactory transduction, respectively. The role of olfactory transduction in SONFH has not been clarified and should be further investigated [56–59]. The spliceosome is a RNA–protein complex. It catalyzes pre-mRNA to form specific mRNA or different mRNAs by recognizing the splice site of pre-mRNA through AS-catalyzed splicing reaction, which is an important link in gene expression and regulation [60]. The formation of spliceosome and ribonucleoprotein complexes enables RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) to participate in the transcription, translation and degradation of RNA during the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs [61]. The decreased osteogenic activity and osteogenic differentiation ability of BMSCs in osteogenesis in patients with corticosteroid-induced inhibition of shear body function are involved [9]. Similarly, splicing disorder may lead to abnormal expression of certain important genes, triggering intracellular stress responses and activating inflammasomes, ultimately resulting in pyroptosis [62]. The spliceosome and olfactory transduction pathways may affect the function or apoptosis process of mesenchymal stem cells through these indirect pathways, and cause-related changes, providing potential therapeutic directions for SONFH.

Oxidative phosphorylation occurs in the pathological mechanism of SONFH, where macrophage-induced c-type lectin (Mincle) is activated by damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released by dead bone cells, and promotes the generation of osteoclasts through ITAM-based oxidative phosphorylation and calcium signaling, thus causing necrosis of bone cells in the femoral head [63]. It may also be related to glycolytic reprogramming and mitochondrial dysfunction, as the transition from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis can result in improper activation and function of cells, leading to the dysfunction of cell metabolism. It is mainly reflected in the impaired immune regulation of macrophages (Mj) and MSCs and the impaired bone regeneration in the case of inflammation [64].

In this study, the CTD database was employed for predicting the target therapeutic drugs for the three identified biomarkers. Among the predicted drugs, Bisphenol-A-diglycidyl ether (BADGE) bisphenol-diglycidyl ether is an antagonist of PPAR-c (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor). It can binds well with the biomarkers FKBP8 and ANK1 to reverse bone marrow adipogenesis and adipocyte hypertrophy, to enhance osteoblast proliferation, and to inhibit the generation of osteoclasts, thus can be employed for the prevention and treatment of SONFH at early stage [65]. CGP52608 is a retinoic acid receptor-related orphan receptor α (ROR α) activator that binds well with the biomarkers PTGS2 and ANK1 to enhance the activity of ROR α 1 and EPO luciferase reporter genes. It can enhance the expression of erythropoietin (EPO) mRNA and protein, as well as the levels of circulating EPO in vitro and in vivo, can promote the differentiation of endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) and neovascular formation via the ROR α-EPO–AMPK axis, enhancing tissue repair, which may affect MSCs in SONFH repair mechanism and thus promote femoral head bone repair-related [66]. The following drugs and compounds may have negative and negative effects on the treatment of SONFH. It has been shown that, at the cellular level, NRF 2 (nuclear factor erythroid-derived 2-like 2) plays a crucial part in resisting to oxidative stress. Nuclear import of NRF 2, which can binds well with biomarkers FKBP8 and PTGS2, leads to self-renewal of early passage MSCs and loss of osteogenic potential [67], which may be also related to the decreased osteogenic differentiation ability of osteoblasts in SONFH. Aflatoxin B1 (Aflatoxin B1 AFB1), which is a mycotoxin that can lead to serious damage of liver function and can binds with the biomarkers PTGS2, ANK1, can induce oxidative stress and destroy cell pathway to reduce the bone volume and tissue volume of animal femoral cortical bone, and can destroy vitamin D receptor and calcium–phosphorus homeostasis to damage bone mineralization, thus having a negative impact on bone health [68]. In addition, it severely inhibits the human and animal immune systems to induce oxidative stress of macrophages by influencing the respiratory chain, which results in the activation of inflammatory response-related signaling pathways, so as to enhance the formation of ROS, thus leading to oxidative damage [69]. These characteristics are in relation to the occurrence and development of SONFH due to oxidative stress. Benzo [a] pyrene is a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon that generally exposed as a hazardous component from cigarettes and it can binds well to the biomarkers PTGS2 and ANK1. As an agonist of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), it can regulate the BMP/Smad pathway of bone morphogen receptor 2 (BMPRII) and Hey1 through armorphogen receptor (AhR), thus inhibiting bone osteogenic protein 2 (Bmp 2) [70].

Although high-throughput data on SONFH can be obtained from GEO database, the number of relevant data sets and samples are limited, and more homologous data sets can be enriched in the future. Moreover, the molecular mechanism underlying DEGs in the pathogenesis and progression of SONFH remains unknown, and further basic experimental validations, such as gene knockout, related animal models, may still be lacking for in-depth study on molecular mechanisms of SONFH. In this regard, future studies may focus on experimental validations to more fully understand the molecular mechanism underlying SONFH, laying the foundation for development of new approaches for the prevention and treatment of the disease.

Conclusion

In summary, three biomarkers of FKBP8, PTGS2 and ANK1, which co-regulate SONFH with pyroptosis and MSCs, were identified in this study through a series of bioinformatics methods. The finding and results obtained therefrom may contribute to future research on how to influence SONFH with respect to the occurrence and development by regulating MSCs and pyroptosis, and provide theoretical supports and new ideas for the specific diagnosis and treatment of SONFH.
