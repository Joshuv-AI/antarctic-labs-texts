Health Communication Patterns in Breast Implant Illness Content on TikTok: A DISCERN-based Analysis by Creator Type
Authors: Madhulika R. Kastury, Fatima Z. Khan, Amber L. Edson, Abigail M. Royfman, Noelle T. Thompson, Kirollos Tadrousse, Haripriya S. Ayyala, Richard Simman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Breast implant illness (BII) describes a constellation of patient-reported symptoms attributed to implants. Despite lacking diagnostic consensus, BII remains prominent on TikTok, where health-related content often lacks expert oversight. This study evaluates the presentation, quality, and reliability of BII-related TikTok videos using the DISCERN framework, comparing healthcare professional (HCP) and non-HCP content.Methods:This observational study analyzed the top 30 videos from 5 BII-related hashtags. Videos were categorized by creator type and independently assessed by 4 raters using the DISCERN tool. Statistical methods included the Mann-Whitney U test, intraclass correlation coefficients, analysis of variance, and multivariable linear regression with log-transformed engagement metrics.Results:Of 150 videos screened, 36 met inclusion criteria. HCP videos (41.7%, n = 15) demonstrated significantly higher DISCERN reliability (20.9 versus 16.4, P < 0.001) and total scores (31.6 versus 27.8, P = 0.03). Treatment quality scores did not differ (P = 0.22). Among HCPs, follower count was positively associated with DISCERN score (P = 0.032; interaction P = 0.031); like count trended negatively without reaching significance (P = 0.055). The final model explained 73.5% of the variance in DISCERN scores (R2 = 0.735; adjusted R2 = 0.694; F (5, 30) = 17.71, P < 0.001).Conclusions:The reliability and quality of BII content on TikTok varied by creator type. HCP videos scored higher across DISCERN domains, especially when paired with higher follower counts. These findings signify the need for platform-specific strategies to promote evidence-based voices in digital health communication.

Takeaways

Question: Is there a difference in reliability and quality of TikTok video content discussing breast implant illness created by healthcare professionals versus nonhealthcare professionals?

Findings: TikTok videos created by healthcare professionals scored significantly higher in reliability and overall video ratings than those made by nonhealthcare professionals. However, no significant difference in quality scores was observed between the two groups.

Meaning: Healthcare professionals’ videos provide more accurate and reliable information than nonprofessionals. This study underscores the need for active engagement by healthcare professionals on social media to provide evidence-based information and combat misinformation.

INTRODUCTION

Breast implant illness (BII) refers to a constellation of patient-reported symptoms attributed to breast implants, including fatigue, joint pain, memory disturbances, and autoimmune-like manifestations.1–3 Despite growing public awareness, BII lacks a clearly defined diagnostic standard or validated biomarkers. Regulatory organizations, such as the US Food and Drug Administration and the American Society of Plastic Surgeons, have acknowledged these symptoms and encourage shared decision-making between patients and providers, but a formal clinical definition has yet to be established.4,5 Without a unified diagnostic framework, clinical responses and treatment strategies remain inconsistent and often contradictory.6,7

As a result, many patients have turned to social media platforms for health information. TikTok, a short-form, algorithm-driven platform, has emerged as a prominent venue for medical discourse, especially among younger users.8,9 Although the platform enables rapid content dissemination, it often blurs personal testimony with evidence-based medical advice, allowing anecdotal claims to circulate widely without proper vetting. In the context of BII, emotionally charged narratives may attract significant engagement, potentially shaping public understanding and influencing medical decisions that diverge from clinical evidence.10,11

Healthcare professionals (HCPs) have begun engaging on TikTok to counter misinformation. However, the presentation quality and sourcing of their content, particularly in comparison to that of nonprofessionals, is underexamined. Prior research indicates that the reliability and structure of online health information are greatly influenced by the creator’s background.12,13 To explore how this distinction manifests in the BII discourse, this study applies the DISCERN tool to evaluate the transparency and organization of consumer health videos independent of scientific accuracy.14,15 By comparing TikTok videos produced by HCPs and non-HCPs, this analysis examines how professional identity affects online medical content, its presentation, and perceived credibility within a high-visibility, low-regulation digital ecosystem.

METHODS

This observational, qualitative study utilized the DISCERN tool to assess the structure, clarity, and reliability of TikTok videos related to BII, comparing content produced by HCPs with that of non-HCPs.14,15 Videos were identified using 5 BII-related hashtags: #breastimplantillness, #bii, #explant, #breastimplantillnesssymptoms, and #breastimplantillnessawareness. Hashtags were chosen based on relevance and volume following exploratory, iterative searches. For each, the top 30 English-language videos (≥1000 views) were collected during a 1-week window (January 12–19, 2024) to minimize algorithmic variability introduced by prolonged sampling. Of 150 total videos screened, 36 met inclusion criteria. The hashtag #explant was excluded from comparative statistical analyses due to a lack of HCP-generated videos, but eligible posts were retained for descriptive analysis. Eligible videos addressed BII-related symptoms, treatment, or etiology, were 10 seconds to 10 minutes long, and featured original, nonpromotional content. Creators were classified as HCPs if they were licensed providers (MD, DO, NP, PA, and RN), verified through profile data and public registries. Non-HCPs included patients and wellness influencers who described at least two symptoms and noted changes after explantation. No videos featured creators who identified as both licensed HCPs and patients; all accounts were screened for overlapping roles using profile claims and external sources. Videos were excluded if, at the time of data collection, they were non-English, promotional, reposted, or unrelated to BII.

Four medical student reviewers evaluated videos using the DISCERN instrument, which scores content across 3 domains: (1) reliability, (2) quality of treatment information, and (3) overall score (1 = not met, 5 = fully met). Raters underwent structured DISCERN training through guided consensus meetings, with oversight from 2 board-certified plastic surgeons who remained available to clarify scoring criteria. Each video was evaluated independently, and disagreements were resolved through group discussion. Evaluations focused on how clearly and transparently information was presented, consistent with the DISCERN tool’s purpose of assessing informational structure rather than clinical accuracy.

Statistical analyses included intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) to assess interrater reliability. DISCERN scores were compared between HCP and non-HCP groups using two-sample t tests and Mann-Whitney U tests to account for nonnormal distributions. One-way analysis of variance evaluated score differences within HCP subgroups (eg, plastic versus non–plastic surgeons). Multivariable linear regression was used to examine the relationships between DISCERN scores and video characteristics (eg, creator type, engagement metrics). The Fisher exact test was applied to categorical comparisons given small subgroup sizes (HCP n = 15; non-HCP n = 21). Due to the modest sample size and multiple comparisons, both type I and II error risks were elevated, and results should be interpreted accordingly.

This study did not involve human subjects, identifiable data, or direct interactions, and was therefore exempt from institutional review board approval. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (versions 4.2.2 and later; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A completed STROBE checklist is included in Supplemental Digital Content 2 to enhance methodological transparency. RESULTS

A total of 36 videos were included of the 150 TikTok videos screened across 5 hashtags. Fifteen videos (41.7%) were created by HCPs, most of whom were plastic surgeons. Two videos were posted by verified MDs from other specialties: 1 by a board-certified OB/GYN with additional training in integrative and functional medicine, and the other by a physician whose specialty could not be confirmed through public records. The remaining 21 (58.3%) videos were created by non-HCPs, including patients and wellness influencers. Each creator type emphasized distinct aspects of BII in their content. HCP videos tended to focus on implant-specific diagnoses such as capsular contracture and rupture, often using clinical terminology. In contrast, non-HCPs more commonly highlighted systemic symptoms, including fatigue, brain fog, and joint pain (Fig. 1). These differences in thematic focus were mirrored in DISCERN evaluations of structure and reliability.

Most common symptoms reported in TikTok videos on BII. This figure depicts the most frequently mentioned BII symptoms in TikTok videos, showing that non-HCPs emphasized systemic symptoms while HCPs used more clinical terms, reflecting stylistic rather than diagnostic differences.

Observed DISCERN scores ranged from 3 to 47, with a mean of 30.89 ± 7.91. HCP videos scored significantly higher in reliability (20.9 ± 2.3 versus 16.4 ± 3.1, P < 0.001) and overall quality (31.6 ± 3.4 versus 27.8 ± 3.7, P = 0.03). Treatment quality scores showed no significant difference between groups (10.1 ± 1.8 versus 9.6 ± 2.2, P = 0.22), suggesting HCPs and non-HCPs discussed management at similar rates (Table 2). Interrater agreement was strong, with ICCs above 0.74 across all domains. The highest agreement was observed in the treatment quality domain (ICC = 0.808), reinforcing the reliability of the scoring protocol.

Additional nonparametric analyses expanded on these trends by examining score differences across specific hashtags. Mann-Whitney U tests revealed significantly higher reliability scores in HCP videos tagged with #breastimplantillnessawareness (P = 0.022) and #breastimplantillnesssymptoms (P = 0.024). A trend toward significance was observed for #bii (P = 0.085). (Table 3). Effect sizes were substantial: reliability and total DISCERN scores yielded large effects (r = –0.79 and –0.65, respectively), and treatment quality showed a moderate effect (r = –0.49). These patterns indicate that HCP-produced videos were not only more consistent but also more structurally coherent. An exploratory analysis of variance within HCP subgroups suggested a nominally significant difference in treatment quality scores under #breastimplantillnesssymptoms (P = 0.043). However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously due to the small number of non–plastic HCPs (n = 2).

A multivariable linear regression model incorporating log-transformed follower, view, and like counts provided further insight into predictors of DISCERN score. Follower count (P = 0.032) and view count (P = 0.025) were both positively associated with higher DISCERN scores. Like count was inversely associated with DISCERN score, but it was not statistically significant (P = 0.055). An interaction term between creator type and follower count indicated that the positive effect between audience size and DISCERN score was stronger among HCPs (P = 0.031). These results indicate that structural quality improves with broader reach only when paired with clinical qualifications; however, popularity metrics alone are insufficient indicators of informational reliability. The final model accounted for approximately 74% of the variance in DISCERN scores (R2 = 0.735; adjusted R2 = 0.694), with a statistically significant overall fit (F(5,30) = 17.71, P < 0.001) (Table 4). However, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the modest sample size and limited generalizability.

DISCUSSION

Videos created by HCPs consistently received higher DISCERN scores for both reliability and overall structure. This outcome likely reflects how clinical training improves presentation clarity, source transparency, and clinical judgment regarding treatments.8,9 Several HCPs in the sample referenced peer-reviewed studies, acknowledged limitations, and used precise medical terminology when describing complications. One video cited emerging evidence for breast implant-associated immune dysregulation, referencing studies that implicate chronic inflammation, cytokine imbalance, and immune cell infiltration.16 These practices align with the DISCERN criteria, which assess how clearly and logically information is conveyed, regardless of its scientific accuracy. In clinically ambiguous domains like BII, well-structured content may still present unverified or disputed claims.5,6 The literature continues to highlight the shortage of high-quality longitudinal research on explantation, reinforcing the need for more rigorous clinical data.17 By contrast, non-HCP videos were often grounded in experiential storytelling. These creators typically chronicled symptom trajectory, the choice to explant, and the road to recovery using informal, emotionally charged language. Although these accounts offered insight into patient experience and resonated with large audiences, they rarely included citations, risk framing, or comparative treatment discussions. Because DISCERN prioritizes structured delivery, this absence likely contributed to the lower reliability scores observed among nonprofessionals.

Notably, treatment-related scores did not differ significantly between groups. What distinguished the groups was not how often they mentioned treatment, but how treatment was contextualized. HCPs generally discussed procedures using clinical reasoning and expected results. Non-HCPs focused on symptom relief, dissatisfaction with care, and emotional recovery. This contrast reflects a broader phenomenon in online health communication, where narrative-driven content can gain greater traction than technically accurate explanations.12,13,18

Regression analysis offered further insight into how content quality is shaped by digital reach. Among HCPs, larger audiences, as indicated by follower and view counts, were significantly associated with higher DISCERN scores, implying that clinical expertise combined with digital reach may enhance the clarity and structure of health-related communication. On the other hand, no such relationship was found among non-HCPs. Like count showed a negative association with DISCERN score, though this did not reach statistical significance. These findings challenge the assumption that popularity metrics reflect informational quality.19,20 TikTok’s algorithm favors emotionally engaging content, which may amplify anecdotal material while limiting exposure to more rigorously structured videos.18,21

These statistical trends reflect a deeper epistemological divide. HCPs often organize their messaging around diagnostic reasoning, whereas non-HCPs foreground lived experience. Although not inherently opposed, these styles diverge in ways that may shape how BII is perceived by the public. Bridging this gap may require hybrid approaches, where professionals collaborate with trusted influencers to reach broader audiences without compromising the integrity of the information. Evidence from chronic illness communities suggests that such partnerships can increase transparency, strengthen trust, and improve interpretability across diverse viewer groups.10,11 Figure 3 outlines several strategies to facilitate this alignment, including citation tagging, metadata structuring, and improved creator verification. Plastic surgeons and other clinicians may benefit from collaborating with high-reach non-HCP influencers to co-create content that is both evidence-based and digitally resonant.

This study has several limitations. The dataset included only 36 English-language TikTok videos drawn from 5 BII-related hashtags, which limited subgroup comparisons and reduced statistical power for interaction terms. Although DISCERN is validated for evaluating consumer health content, it does not measure factual correctness. Well-organized but speculative material may still receive high scores under this system. Evaluations were conducted by four medical students trained in DISCERN methodology and supervised by two board-certified plastic surgeons. Although students may have had difficulty interpreting highly technical content, their familiarity with TikTok’s platform logic, visual conventions, and narrative pacing likely enhanced their ability to assess how information was framed and received. Although interrater agreement was strong (ICC > 0.75), the absence of clinically practicing raters may have limited interpretive depth in evaluating complex medical content. The study did not systematically assess financial incentives, such as affiliate marketing or branded explantation services. Finally, TikTok’s algorithmic structure may disproportionately surface emotionally provocative material, suppressing exposure to more evidence-based posts.8,12

CONCLUSIONS

This study identified meaningful structural differences in how BII is presented on TikTok, with HCP-generated content more consistently aligning with DISCERN criteria for transparency and clarity. These findings highlight the importance of HCP participation in digital spaces, particularly when public understanding is shaped by anecdote and uncertainty. Efforts to improve the reliability of online health content may benefit from clearer content guidelines, stronger media literacy initiatives in medical education, and structured partnerships between clinicians and digital creators.8 Although DISCERN does not assess scientific validity, its focus on organization and source transparency offers a valuable lens for evaluating health messaging in public forums.14

Study limitations, including modest sample size, single-platform focus, and the inability to assess clinical accuracy, reflect the broader obstacles in digital health research. These challenges do not diminish the study’s relevance but instead underscore the urgent need for more robust methodologies to analyze medical content in algorithm-driven environments. Future research should involve more social media platforms, test interventions like citation tagging and creator verification, and investigate how algorithmic design impacts information exposure.9 As patients increasingly turn to social media for medical advice, distinguishing between structured communication and anecdotal narrative is essential to safeguarding informed decision-making. Trustworthy health communication online requires delivery models that integrate clinical rigor with platform-specific strategies informed by digital engagement trends.
