The health effects of nursing home specialization in post-acute care
Authors: Zachary S. Templeton, Nate C. Apathy, R. Tamara Konetzka, Meghan M. Skira, Rachel M. Werner
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Nursing homes serve both long-term care and post-acute care (PAC) patients, two groups with distinct financing mechanisms and requirements for care. We examine empirically the effect of nursing home specialization in PAC using 2011–2018 data for Medicare patients admitted to nursing homes following a hospital stay. To address patient selection into specialized nursing homes, we use an instrumental variables approach that exploits variation over time in the distance from the patient’s residential ZIP code to the closest nursing home with different levels of PAC specialization. We find that patients admitted to nursing homes more specialized in PAC have lower hospital readmissions and mortality, longer nursing home stays, and higher Medicare spending for the episode of care, suggesting that specialization improves patient outcomes but at higher costs.

Introduction

The role of specialization in health care has been widely discussed by economists and policymakers, and is often proposed as a way to improve health care quality, decrease health care spending, and improve the value of care (Detsky et al., 2012). The potential benefits of specialization underpin the increasing prevalence of specialized physicians and the recent growth of specialty hospitals, which primarily or exclusively treat patients with specific conditions or undergoing specific procedures. Whether policymakers should encourage specialization is an open question. Much of the empirical literature that examines the purported benefits of specialization does so in the hospital context, and the results are mixed. Furthermore, the impacts of specialization on costs have been relatively unexplored, and little is known about the causal effects of specialization in other health care settings, especially nursing homes.

The nursing home sector is an important and appropriate setting to study specialization, as nursing homes serve two distinct populations that require different types of care. Most nursing homes are certified as skilled nursing facilities (SNFs) and provide post-acute care (PAC), which includes short-term skilled nursing and rehabilitation services (e.g., physical, speech, and occupational therapy) to patients after a stay in an acute-care hospital. Medicare is the dominant payer for this care. Nursing homes also provide less intensive, long-term care services for individuals who can no longer reside independently in the community. Medicaid finances most of these long-term care services and does so at a much lower rate relative to Medicare per diem reimbursements for PAC. Specialization is a key consideration for nursing homes, as they must decide the degree to which they will concentrate their care among these two distinct populations.

In this paper, we examine the effects of nursing home specialization in PAC on a host of outcomes among Medicare patients admitted to nursing homes following a hospital stay, including hospital readmission and mortality, nursing home length of stay, and Medicare spending. We define specialization based on the fraction of patients receiving PAC in a nursing home, which captures the degree to which a given nursing home focuses its operations on PAC. Specialization in PAC likely improves patient outcomes and PAC quality through organizational and process improvements linked to an increase in the relative concentration of efforts toward PAC. However, its impacts on length of stay and Medicare spending are ambiguous and therefore an empirical question. On one hand, specialization could lead to efficiencies that result in quicker and smoother patient recoveries. On the other hand, the larger profit margins associated with Medicare-financed PAC relative to Medicaid-financed long-term care, as well as the variable and unpredictable demand for PAC, provide incentives for specialized nursing homes to lengthen PAC stays.

Identifying the causal effects of nursing home specialization in PAC is challenging due to potential patient selection into more versus less specialized facilities. That is, there may be unobservables correlated with both admission to a highly specialized nursing home and patient outcomes. To overcome this challenge, we employ an instrumental variables (IV) strategy that exploits changes over time in the distance from the patient’s residential ZIP code to the closest nursing home in different quartiles of the PAC specialization distribution. Using data on over 12 million SNF Medicare admissions from 2011 to 2018, we find that admission to a facility that is more specialized in PAC (measured as the proportion of residents whose stay is covered by Medicare), leads to lower hospital readmission and mortality within 30 days following hospital discharge, longer SNF stays, and higher Medicare spending during the PAC episode. Our main analyses include residential ZIP code fixed effects to account for time-invariant heterogeneity in local patient characteristics and market factors that may correlate with specialization and our outcomes of interest. Our results are also robust to the inclusion of nursing home fixed effects, which control for time-invariant nursing home heterogeneity that may be correlated with PAC specialization and our outcomes. Furthermore, we find that the increase in patient length of stay and spending among more specialized nursing homes is smaller among facilities that face less uncertainty in the demand for their PAC services, namely those owned by or located near hospitals.

Our analysis contributes to several strands of literature. One strand examines the relationship between PAC volume in nursing homes and patient outcomes and generally finds that nursing homes caring for more PAC patients exhibit higher quality. Li et al. (2012) use data from 2008 and document a descriptive association between nursing homes with greater volumes of PAC patients and lower hospital readmissions. Gozalo et al. (2015) instrument for volume and find that among patients discharged from the hospital after hip fracture, those going to nursing homes providing a high volume of PAC have higher rates of successful discharge to the community. Both studies are limited in scope, as they consider the relationship between volume and a single outcome, and Gozalo et al. (2015) focus only on hip-fracture patients. More importantly, they focus on a different construct—PAC volume—while we are interested in the effects of PAC specialization. Specialization (i.e., the share of patients in a nursing home receiving PAC) and volume (i. e., the number of patients receiving PAC) are related but distinct concepts. Increased volume may lead to quality improvements via the experience gained from repetition. In contrast, increased specialization may improve performance by increasing the relative focus of the nursing home’s operations toward PAC. That is, a “focused factory” approach may allow specialized nursing homes to focus on a narrower set of care-related activities with greater effectiveness (Skinner 1974).

We are aware of only one study that considers the impacts of specialization (distinct from volume) in the nursing home setting. Rahman et al. (2013) find that after the closure of hospital-based nursing homes, which are the nursing homes that are most specialized in PAC, patients were admitted to less specialized nursing homes (i.e., freestanding nursing homes not owned by a hospital) and experienced more hospital readmissions. We extend this work by examining the effect of PAC specialization on a wider range of outcomes and defining specialization as a continuous measure across all nursing homes, not a binary measure of whether the nursing home is hospital-owned. In doing so, we provide a more comprehensive and generalizable estimate of the effects of PAC specialization.

Our work also contributes to the broader literature on specialization in health care, which has largely focused on the hospital context. In studies that do not account for patient selection, support for the purported benefits of specialization has been mixed in the specialty hospital setting (Cram et al., 2005; Greenwald et al., 2006; Nallamothu et al., 2007). Using a difference-in-differences strategy, Barro et al. (2006) find markets that experience the entry of a cardiac specialty hospital see declines in spending for cardiac care, while the effects on mortality are mixed and sensitive to modeling assumptions. Swanson (2021) uses differential distance to physician-owned specialty hospitals as an instrumental variable and finds no statistically significant mortality improvements for cardiac patients treated at specialty hospitals.1 Some studies have analyzed the effects of condition-specific patient volume or condition-specific specialization in general acute-care hospitals. Patients undergoing treatment for hip fracture experience lower mortality rates at hospitals caring for a higher volume of such patients (Hentschker and Mennicken 2018), and increased specialization in cardiovascular care (i.e., share of hospital patients with a primary diagnosis in the area of cardiovascular disease) is associated with lower patient mortality rates (Clark and Huckman 2012).

On the methodological front, our work is most similar to Cornell et al. (2019). They estimate the effect of being admitted to a nursing home with a higher quality rating on patient outcomes by instrumenting the quality rating of the admitting nursing home with distance from the patient’s residential ZIP code to the closest nursing home of various rating values. We study a fundamentally different and unexplored potential determinant of patient outcomes—specialization. More generally, our approach is related to studies that estimate the effect of a PAC provider characteristic on patient outcomes using distance-related instruments.

Within the broader context of these literatures, our paper makes several important contributions. First, we provide new causal evidence that specialization improves health care quality—a central question in health economics—even after controlling for the volume of admissions at nursing homes. To date, the literature on the effects of specialization on quality finds mixed results, often conflates specialization and volume, and is limited to the hospital setting. Second, we provide novel evidence on the effects of specialization on health care utilization, a proxy for health care costs. Prior work has rarely considered the cost implications of specialization. Third, we focus on the nursing home sector, a setting where a large number of vulnerable and ill adults receive care at a large cost burden for Medicare. In addition to its policy relevance, the nursing home setting is well-suited to examine the effects of specialization, as the vast majority of nursing homes provide care for both PAC and long-term care patients. Finally, we carefully address endogeneity of specialization that arises from two sources—patient selection into specialized nursing homes and nursing home selection into degrees of specialization (Konetzka et al., 2019)—by instrumenting for patient-level selection using distance-related instruments and demonstrating robustness of our results to including nursing home fixed effects.

Background on nursing homes and post-acute care

Nursing homes provide a unique and important setting in which to study the effects of specialization. Nursing homes provide care for two distinct patient populations: short-stay PAC patients admitted for skilled care after discharge from a hospital (e.g., rehabilitation, intravenous treatments, or wound care), and long-term care residents who can no longer live independently in the community and need help with activities of daily living. Medicare is the most common payer for PAC and pays nursing homes generously at a per diem rate for these stays, which often require short but intense periods of rehabilitation, therapy, and other skilled services. Long-term care—typically covered by Medicaid at substantially lower reimbursement rates than Medicare-funded PAC—requires low-intensity, ongoing residential care for people with functional and/or cognitive impairment.

Because of Medicare’s relatively generous payment for PAC, along with declining lengths of hospital stays, spending on PAC has grown tremendously in recent decades. PAC spending increased from less than 1 % of Medicare’s expenditures in 1983 (Schieber et al., 1986) to more than 8 % in 2018, totaling $28.5 billion across 2.2 million SNF stays (Medicare Payment Advisory Commission 2020). More than 40 % of Medicare beneficiaries received PAC after hospital discharge in 2013 (Tian 2016). Most PAC takes place in a SNF (Werner and Konetzka 2018), and the average number of PAC admissions per nursing home bed increased from 1.2 in 2001 to 1.6 in 2017, a relative increase of 41 % (Werner et al., 2021).

The tradeoffs of treating PAC and long-term care patients within the same nursing home have long been scrutinized (Konetzka et al., 2016; Mor et al., 2004). There are well-described financial benefits to nursing homes from providing PAC, as Medicare margins for PAC are substantially higher than margins for Medicaid-financed long-term care. Although PAC is a relatively small proportion of overall nursing home care as measured by bed-days, it is an important source of revenue. In 2018, the average profit margins for Medicare and non-Medicare nursing home patients were 10.3 % and −3.0 %, respectively (Medicare Payment Advisory Commission 2020). Because of this disparity, Medicare payments for PAC patients cross-subsidize the care for long-stay residents (Troyer 2002; Medicare Payment Advisory Commission 2020). In turn, the financial reliance on PAC may generate negative spillovers for long-term care patients in those same nursing homes (Corredor-Waldron 2021; Konetzka et al., 2006). For example, Konetzka et al. (2006) find that the shift from cost-based reimbursement for PAC to Medicare prospective payment, which decreased the average Medicare reimbursement received, led to worse outcomes for long-term care patients. In addition, the structure of Medicare and Medicaid reimbursements creates conflicting incentives, particularly for dually-eligible patients, resulting in fragmentation of care and indirect adverse consequences for patients (Grabowski 2007). For example, it has been well-documented that nursing homes have an incentive to hospitalize their long-stay Medicaid patients because Medicare will then pay for the hospitalization and potentially a higher-margin PAC stay when the patient returns to the nursing home. The disparities in financing between PAC and long-term care have again been criticized in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, with nearly one-third of all COVID-related deaths in the US occurring in nursing homes (Werner and Konetzka 2022). In this paper, we study a different aspect of this tradeoff, namely, the effects of PAC specialization on Medicare patient outcomes, SNF utilization, and Medicare costs.

Conceptual framework

We assume nursing homes maximize profits, where profits depend on the number of admissions, the length of each patient’s nursing home stay, and the profit margin associated with each stay.2 The number of admissions—particularly PAC admissions—depends in part on perceived nursing home quality, typically learned via public report cards (Perraillon et al., 2019; Werner et al., 2012; Werner et al., 2016) or through prior hospital or patient experience with referrals to a nursing home. Given that reimbursements and margins for PAC patients are significantly higher than those for long-term care patients, profits are generally increasing in the number of new PAC admissions and the length of each PAC stay. Along with the relatively higher reimbursement, PAC admissions also increase profits because most nursing homes operate below capacity and have unoccupied beds.

Importantly, we treat specialization as distinct from volume. Volume is throughput, or in our setting, the number of patients receiving PAC in a nursing home. The effect of volume on quality of care is directly related to the experience gained from repetition, often referred to as learning-by-doing (Gaynor et al., 2005). By contrast, specialization is the fraction of a nursing home’s patients who receive PAC. Nursing homes may be specialized in PAC despite a low absolute volume of PAC admissions.

Nursing home PAC specialization may affect patient outcomes, length of stay, and Medicare spending. By increasing the share of patients receiving PAC, nursing homes increase the relative focus of their operations and mission toward PAC. This may result in improved production of PAC that comes from better developed organizational processes, coordination, and communication. While specialization-induced organizational and process improvements predict improved patient outcomes, the implications for patient length of stay and Medicare spending are ambiguous. If longer stays are an important input for patient outcomes and recovery, more focused and increased PAC expertise may lead to increased length of stay and spending for PAC patients. Indeed, studies find that longer nursing home stays for PAC result in lower hospital readmission rates (Werner et al., 2019, 2022). On the other hand, as nursing homes and individual providers specialize in PAC, they may improve their ability to deliver treatment that matches a given patient’s needs, leading to faster recovery, shorter length of stay, and decreased Medicare spending.

Through more efficient and narrow use of labor and technology, specialized nursing homes may be better able to produce high-quality PAC at lower costs. In our setting of Traditional Medicare (TM) with administratively set per diem reimbursements, lower costs achieved through these efficiencies translate to higher nursing home margins rather than lower Medicare spending. All else equal and in the setting of per diem payments, these higher margins create an incentive for nursing homes to increase length of stay for PAC patients. Further amplifying this incentive is the fact that nursing homes’ average costs associated with providing therapy and non-therapy services to PAC patients decrease over the length of stay. Despite the fact that nursing homes regularly reassess patient needs and their associated case-mix group (which determines their prospective per diem rate), prior work has demonstrated that profit margins increase as stays lengthen (Acumen 2016), giving nursing homes, especially those specialized in PAC, additional incentive to maximize length of stay.

Importantly, the demand for PAC in nursing homes is limited, variable, and unpredictable. Nursing homes not only compete with one another for a limited number of PAC admissions in each market, but also with other non-nursing-home PAC providers, such as home health agencies. In part because of the constrained and unpredictable nature of PAC demand, specialized nursing homes face a risk of unfilled beds. As a result, it is rare for nursing homes to completely specialize in PAC. Instead, they care for a mix of high-margin PAC patients and low-margin long-stay residents, who help pay fixed costs and compensate for the variability in PAC demand (Hackmann et al., 2020). Nursing homes that do specialize may compensate for this risk by increasing length of stay for PAC patients to keep their beds occupied. This incentive to increase length of stay may be dampened in nursing homes with a more reliable supply of PAC patients, such as those owned by a hospital or located geographically close to a hospital.

Overall, we expect nursing homes that are more specialized in PAC to provide higher quality PAC, which will result in decreased hospital readmissions and mortality rates. While specialization could decrease patient length of stay, other forces incentivize longer stays for PAC patients in specialized nursing homes. Thus, the effect of specialization on length of stay and Medicare spending is theoretically ambiguous. We expect incentives to maximize length of stay to be dampened among nursing homes that face lower variability in PAC demand.

Data and sample

Data sources

We use three main data sources. First, we use Medicare Provider Analysis and Review (MedPAR) files to identify all acute-care hospitalizations (and rehospitalizations) for Medicare beneficiaries.3 Second, to identify PAC stays in nursing homes, we link hospital discharges with nursing home admissions in the Minimum Data Set (MDS),4 in combination with the claim for the SNF admission in MedPAR. We define nursing home admissions within three days of hospital discharge as PAC. Third, we use annual nursing home-level LTCFocus data to obtain nursing home characteristics, including specialization in PAC (described in more detail below).5 We supplement these data with the Medicare Beneficiary Summary File, which includes information on beneficiaries’ enrollment status, demographics, and mortality. All data are available from 2011 to 2018.

Study sample

Our main sample includes all beneficiaries 65 years and older enrolled in TM who are admitted to nursing homes within three days of hospital discharge between 2011 and 2018. We do not include Medicare Advantage beneficiaries in our main analysis sample because many of our outcomes (e.g., Medicare payment and SNF length of stay) cannot be measured for them in the data. We exclude hospital discharges of individuals who leave against medical advice and nursing home admissions where the individual was comatose on admission or enrolled in hospice, as these patients are not typically admitted to nursing homes for PAC. We also exclude nursing home admissions without a corresponding SNF claim in MedPAR (6.9 % of nursing home admissions), as these admissions are unlikely to be for PAC, and admissions that occur in a nursing home not included in LTCFocus (2.5 % of nursing home admissions). Our final sample consists of 12,105,116 nursing home admissions across 15,257 nursing homes.

Outcome variables

We measure the effect of PAC specialization in nursing homes on three categories of outcomes: patient outcomes, nursing home length of stay, and Medicare payments. For patient outcomes, we create separate indicators for unplanned, all-cause hospital readmission and mortality, both measured within 30 days of hospital discharge.6 Nursing home length of stay was measured using MedPAR’s utilization day count, or the number of covered days for the admission that were paid by Medicare. We calculate four Medicare payments within 90 days of hospital discharge: payment for the index SNF stay, payment for subsequent PAC (including SNF, home health, and inpatient rehabilitation) after the index SNF stay, payment for rehospitalizations, and total Part A payment. Payment outcomes are prorated for episodes of care that exceed the 90-day hospital discharge window.

Nursing home specialization in post-acute care

Our main explanatory variable is a continuous measure of a nursing home’s specialization in PAC, defined as the proportion of residents whose primary payer for their stay is Medicare, the dominant payer for PAC. This proportion, obtained from LTCFocus, is measured at the time of the nursing home’s inspection survey, which takes place approximately yearly. In the remainder of the paper, we use “PAC specialization” and “percent Medicare” interchangeably to refer to our explanatory variable of interest. This share-based specialization measure is similar to those used in studies of specialization in other health care settings (e.g., Clark and Huckman 2012; Lee et al., 2015) and captures the construct we are interested in, namely the degree to which nursing homes focus on treating PAC patients. We prefer this share-based measure to a measure based on volume of PAC patients as volume-based measures may simply reflect differences in nursing home size or total number of admissions and length of stay. Furthermore, while volume-based measures proxy well for experience gained via repetition and learning-by-doing, they do not capture the degree of focus of the nursing home’s operations, which is our main interest.

We also use the percent Medicare measure to categorize nursing home-years into specialization quartiles, which we use for calculating our distance instruments (as described in our empirical strategy) and presenting descriptive statistics. Nursing home-years in the first quartile are the least specialized and have the lowest percent Medicare; those in the top quartile are the most specialized and have the highest percent Medicare. We calculate the specialization quartile cut points by pooling all SNF admissions across our sample period, including both TM and Medicare Advantage admissions. Because specialization quartiles are based on all nursing home admissions (including TM and Medicare Advantage), not nursing homes, and our analysis sample includes only TM admissions, neither the number of nursing homes nor the number of nursing home admissions are evenly distributed across specialization quartiles. In addition, since more specialized nursing homes (conditional on size) have more PAC admissions, there are mechanically fewer nursing homes in higher, more specialized quartiles.

We present descriptive statistics of the nursing homes and patients in our sample by specialization quartile in Table 1.7 The most notable differences are that the most specialized nursing homes are more likely to be hospital-owned, smaller in size (as measured by number of beds), and less likely to admit dual-eligible patients.

In Fig. 1, Panel A we plot mean facility-level PAC specialization over time. Average PAC specialization decreased from 16.7 % in 2011 to 13.7 % in 2018. In addition, there is significant variation in PAC specialization across nursing home-years; one standard deviation is 15.0 %. There is also significant variation in PAC within nursing homes over time (Fig. 1, Panel B); one standard deviation of within-nursing home changes in specialization is 7.9 %. As we discuss in more detail in our empirical strategy, within-facility changes in specialization are the main source of variation in our distance instruments.

Table 2 shows that there is also significant variation in our outcomes of interest across quartiles of PAC specialization. Patients admitted to more specialized nursing homes, on average, have lower mortality, lower hospital readmissions, and shorter nursing home stays. While these patterns may be the result of specialization, they could also be driven by healthier patients selecting into more specialized nursing homes and/or more specialized nursing homes selectively admitting healthier patients. In our empirical strategy, we describe our instrumental variables approach that addresses these selection concerns.

Covariates

In our analyses, we control for observable confounding variables at the patient and nursing-home level. Patient covariates include age at admission, sex, race, and dual eligibility for Medicare and Medicaid. We also include the length of the hospital stay preceding the nursing home admission and fixed effects for both the diagnosis-related group (DRG) code associated with the hospitalization and the year of the admission. To proxy for disease severity, we include 29 indicators for patient comorbidities that comprise the Elixhauser comorbidity index (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality 2017; Elixhauser et al., 1998). We account for censoring due to patient death when analyzing time-bound outcomes. For 30-day readmissions, we include the number of days after hospital discharge that a patient was alive, top-coded to 29 days.8 For length of stay, we include an indicator for whether a patient died during her SNF stay. For 90-day payment outcomes, we include the number of days after hospital discharge that a patient was alive, top-coded to 89 days. As we discuss in further detail in the next section, we include fixed effects for patients’ residential ZIP code in all models to control for time-invariant local patient or market factors that may be correlated with both nursing home specialization and patient outcomes. Nursing home covariates include size (bed count), indicators for-profit status, whether a nursing home was part of a chain, and whether a nursing home was owned by a hospital. In robustness checks, we separately add nursing home fixed effects and discharging hospital fixed effects. Nursing home fixed effects account for time-invariant, unobserved nursing home characteristics that may be correlated with that nursing home’s specialization in PAC and our outcomes of interest. Hospital fixed effects account for selection into nursing homes based on pre-existing hospital-nursing home relationships or other time-invariant hospital heterogeneity.

Empirical strategy

Empirical model

We are interested in the causal effect of nursing home PAC specialization on patient outcomes, SNF length of stay, and Medicare spending. We write the relationship as: (1) Yi,z,n,t = β0 + β1Si,n,t + Xiγ + Cn,tδ + θt + ζz + εi,z,n,t where Y indicates the outcome for patient i residing in ZIP code z admitted to nursing home n in year t. Si,n,t denotes nursing home n’s PAC specialization in year t. Patient-level observable controls are given by vector Xi, while nursing-home controls are given by vector Cn,t. θt denotes year fixed effects, ζz denotes patient residential ZIP code fixed effects, and εi,z,n,t is the error term. In our main specification, standard errors are clustered at the ZIP-code level.9

Inference problem: patient selection

We first estimate EQ1 using OLS, exploiting within-ZIP (and within-nursing home) variation in specialization over time as well as across-nursing home variation within ZIP codes. However, these estimates are likely biased due to both demand- and supply-side patient selection. That is, nursing homes that specialize in PAC may (selectively) admit patients whose health is different from patients admitted to less specialized nursing homes. In addition, patients may choose more specialized nursing homes due to unobservable differences that are also correlated with their outcomes, such as unobservable dimensions of health or their ability to navigate complex health care decisions. While we include a rich set of controls for patient demographics and comorbidities, there are likely unobservable patient-level factors that are correlated with a nursing home’s PAC specialization and our outcomes of interest. To obtain causal estimates of the effect of PAC specialization, we use four distance-based instruments for PAC specialization and estimate EQ1 using two-stage least squares (2SLS). We explain our instruments in detail in the next section.

Proximity to nursing homes of varying PAC specialization as instruments

To account for endogeneity arising from patient selection into specialized nursing homes, we create four instruments that measure distances between the patient’s residential ZIP code and nursing homes of varying degrees of specialization. Our endogenous measure of specialization is the proportion of a nursing home’s residents each year whose primary payer is Medicare. We rely on variation in distance to nursing homes in different parts of the specialization distribution given the large body of work showing proximity to a nursing home is one of the main determinants of admission (Gandhi 2020; Hackmann 2019; Hirth et al., 2013; Rahman et al., 2014; Rahman et al., 2016). To construct our instruments, we first categorize each nursing home-year into specialization quartiles, using the above-described quartile cut points calculated from the pooled nursing home-admission-level data for our sample period.10 The cut points (inclusive) are 10.8 % for quartile 1 nursing homes, 17.2 % for quartile 2 nursing homes, and 28.3 % for quartile 3 nursing homes. While the cutoff values remain constant over time, nursing homes may change quartile designations if their percent Medicare changes from year to year. In specification checks, we test whether our results are sensitive to these quartile cut points by using a binary definition of PAC specialization. Note that the classification of nursing home-years into quartiles of specialization is used only for calculating our distance instruments. We rely on this categorical classification of nursing homes for constructing our distance instruments because the concept of differential distance cannot be applied to a continuous measure of specialization.

With specialization quartiles assigned, we then calculate straight-line, Euclidean distances between each patient’s residential ZIP code (using the ZIP code centroid) and the nearest nursing home in each quartile of specialization in a given year (using latitude and longitude of the nursing home address from Google Maps (Google Maps Application Programming Interface, n.d.). We use the Stata command geonear to calculate these distances (Picard, 2010).

Our instruments are similar in design to those in Cornell et al. (2019). They examine whether being admitted to a nursing home with a higher Nursing Home Compare star rating leads to better health outcomes for patients. To overcome the endogenous selection of patients into nursing homes of different quality ratings, they exploit variation over time in the logged distance from a patient’s residential ZIP code to the closest nursing home of each star rating. Like in Cornell et al. (2019), two sources of variation underly changes in our instruments over time. First, the specialization quartile of a given nursing home can change year to year. Out of 15,301 unique nursing homes in our data,11 11,824 change specialization quartile at least once. Importantly, as the quartile cutoffs are fixed over time, this variation is due to actual changes in the nursing home’s percent Medicare (Fig. 1, Panel B). Second, nursing homes can enter or exit the market. However, this second source of variation is negligible as only 400 nursing homes enter and 872 exit during our sample period.

Our first-stage regression uses these four logged distances to nursing homes in each specialization quartile as instruments for the PAC specialization (or percent Medicare) of the admitting nursing home: (2) Si,n,t = α0 + ∑q=14αqDz,tq + Xiη + Cn,tμ + θt + ζz + νi,z,n,t where Dz,tq is the natural logarithm of the distance from patient ZIP code z to the nearest nursing home of quartile q in year t. Intuitively, with the inclusion of ZIP code fixed effects, our instrumental variable strategy compares patients who reside in the same ZIP code but are discharged from a hospital in different years and choose different nursing homes because nursing home specialization and distances to facilities in different parts of the specialization distribution change over time. The validity of these instruments depends on the instruments being strong predictors of the PAC specialization of the admitting nursing home (i.e., relevance) and being uncorrelated with unobservables that may affect outcomes, conditional on patient residential ZIP code and other covariates (i.e., exclusion). We provide empirical support for these assumptions in the next section.

We next analyze the predictive strength of our instruments. Table 4 presents results from the first-stage regression, which regresses PAC specialization (percent Medicare) on our four logged distance instruments (EQ2). Our instruments strongly predict the PAC specialization of the admitting nursing home. The coefficients on all four instruments are statistically significant at the 1 % level, with an associated partial F-statistic of 868. As expected, the coefficients are monotonically decreasing with nursing home specialization quartiles. When patients live farther from less specialized nursing homes, their admitting nursing home is more likely to be specialized in PAC (has higher percent Medicare). Conversely, when patients live farther from specialized nursing homes, the admitting nursing home is on average less specialized (has lower percent Medicare).

The figure includes three lines that denote patients whose distance to the nearest highly specialized nursing home is less than, equal to, or greater than the ZIP code’s median distance over the entire study period.13 At each point along the horizontal axis, we show a comparison of the share of patients admitted to a quartile 4 (highly specialized) nursing home in years when distance to the nearest highly specialized nursing home is above their ZIP code level median, the share admitted during years when the distance was below their ZIP code level median, and the share admitted during years when the distance equaled their ZIP code level median.

We highlight two findings from Fig. 2.14 First, the downward sloping lines provide visual evidence of the strength of our instruments to predict nursing home specialization, showing that as median distance to a highly specialized nursing home increases, the share of patients admitted to a highly specialized nursing home decreases. Second, within-ZIP-code variation in distances is important. When an individual’s distance to the nearest highly specialized nursing home is below the ZIP code’s median distance to a highly specialized nursing home over the study period, patients are admitted to highly specialized nursing homes more often (top, solid line). Conversely, when an individual’s distance to the nearest highly specialized nursing home is above the ZIP code’s median distance, patients are admitted to highly specialized nursing homes less often (bottom, dotted line). Thus, because our identifying variation is from within-ZIP changes in distance over time, this figure demonstrates that when the distance to a highly specialized nursing home increases for a given ZIP code, the probability of admission (from that ZIP code) to a highly specialized nursing home decreases. We address concerns highlighted by Mogstad et al. (2021) regarding the use of multiple instruments later in specification checks.

In our setting, the exclusion restriction is that within-ZIP code variation in distance to nursing homes of different degrees of specialization only affects outcomes through PAC specialization of the admitting nursing home. To explore the validity of this assumption, we examine the balance of observable patient and nursing home characteristics across values of the instruments in Table 5. As in the previous discussion, we divide patients into three groups based on their distance to the nearest highly specialized (quartile 4) nursing home relative to the within-ZIP code median distance to a highly specialized nursing home over the study period. Means and standard deviations are nearly identical for all covariates across these three groups, illustrating that our distance instruments are not systematically correlated with patient or nursing home observables. We also examine balance across quality measures reported in Nursing Home Compare from 2013 to 2018. Although we do not include these quality measures in our main specification, we are reassured that there are no meaningful differences across groups.

As a final check, we conduct a falsification test in which we estimate our first-stage regression on a subsample of patients who were discharged from a hospital 100 or more miles from their residential ZIP code. Because these patients were discharged from a hospital far from home, we expect our instruments based on distance from residential ZIP code to nursing homes not to be predictive of PAC specialization of the admitting nursing home. In Appendix Table A1, column 1, we report first-stage coefficient estimates that are much smaller in magnitude compared to those from our overall sample. In addition, the associated partial F-statistic decreases substantially from 868 to 17. We also provide estimates from a reduced-form regression of mortality on our instruments in this subsample in Appendix Table A1, column 2. None of these estimates are statistically significant, and the signs of these estimates are not all consistent with increases in specialization leading to decreases in mortality.15 Taken together, these results support our assumption that distance to nursing homes of varying degrees of specialization predicts the PAC specialization of the admitting nursing home, exogenous to patient characteristics and local (residential) market factors.

Results

Primary results

We report our primary results of the effect of PAC specialization in Table 6. Using OLS to estimate EQ1, we find that an increase in nursing home PAC specialization is associated with lower 30-day mortality, shorter length of stay, and lower 90-day index SNF payment, although the effects are small in magnitude. Because patients admitted to more specialized nursing homes for PAC tend to be healthier (Table 2), these estimates may be biased downward due to positive selection on patient health.

With our preferred 2SLS specification, we find that admission to a specialized nursing home results in better patient outcomes and longer length of stay. Patients admitted to nursing homes with a one standard deviation higher PAC specialization (or 19.5 percentage point higher percent Medicare)16 have a 0.43 percentage point lower probability of 30-day readmission on a base readmission rate of 20.5 percent (a relative decrease of 2.1 %) and a 0.57 percentage point lower probability of 30-day mortality on a base mortality rate of 8.0 percent (a relative decrease of 7.1 %). Their stays are also 2.7 days longer relative to a mean length of stay of 26.7 days (a relative increase of 10.1 %). Nursing homes with a one standard deviation higher PAC specialization receive $1041 more in 90-day payments for the index SNF stay, on a base of $11,038 (a relative increase of 9.4 %). A one standard deviation increase in PAC specialization also results in Medicare paying an additional $88 for subsequent PAC and $1208 for total care over a 90-day period.

Heterogeneous effects

We test whether our 2SLS results vary based on certain nursing home characteristics by reestimating EQ1 on samples stratified by our characteristic of interest (Table 7). First, we examine heterogeneity by whether the distance of the admitting nursing home to the nearest hospital is below or above the median. The median and interquartile range of distance to the nearest hospital are 2.4 and 4.0 miles, respectively. We hypothesize that specialized nursing homes located closer to hospitals face less variability in PAC demand and thus have weaker incentives to increase length of stay. Our estimates support this hypothesis: specialized nursing homes that are located closer to a hospital have smaller increases in length of stay and generally smaller increases in Medicare spending compared to more specialized nursing homes where the nearest hospital is relatively far, although these differences are not statistically significant (Table 7, columns 2 and 3). We also find that PAC specialization in nursing homes located closer to hospitals leads to larger declines in rehospitalization rates, which is consistent with prior work demonstrating that hospitals form informal partnerships with nursing homes in their market, resulting in lower rehospitalizations (McHugh et al., 2017).

Next, we stratify our sample by whether nursing homes are owned by hospitals or are freestanding. Hospital-owned nursing homes tend to be more specialized in PAC, with a mean of 33.1 % Medicare patients, compared to freestanding nursing homes with a mean of 14.7 %. Because hospital-owned nursing homes predominantly accept patients from the associated hospital, we expect our distance instruments to be less predictive of admission to these nursing homes. Indeed, in this sample our first stage is much weaker, with an F-statistic of 44 (Table 7, column 5). We find that the effects of PAC specialization on length of stay and 90-day index SNF payment are significantly smaller for hospital-owned nursing homes compared to freestanding nursing homes. However, we also find that more specialized, hospital-owned nursing homes have significantly higher 30-day readmission, 90-day subsequent PAC payment, and 90-day rehospitalization payment. We hypothesize that hospital-owned nursing homes face different incentives for allocating tasks across the hospital stay and SNF stay. Hospitals that own a nursing home may be more willing (or able) to discharge patients quickly and to readmit the marginal patient because the transaction costs of doing so are small compared to those of freestanding nursing homes. More specialized nursing homes (i.e., with narrower expertise in post-acute care) may recognize signs of clinical deterioration faster, and for a hospital-owned nursing home, it is relatively easier to transition the patient to the hospital setting, resulting in shorter SNF stays and higher rehospitalization rates.

Third, we examine heterogeneous effects by nursing home PAC volume. We define volume as the number of PAC admissions in the previous year (including both TM and MA admissions) and consider heterogeneity by whether those admissions are below or above the median. While we hypothesize that the effects of specialization arise independent of volume, improvements in patient outcomes due to specialization may be amplified at nursing homes that treat high volumes of Medicare patients if the benefits of focused operations associated with specialization interact with learning-by-doing and repetition that arises from higher volumes. Our results support this hypothesis, as high-volume nursing homes experience a statistically significant reduction in 30-day readmissions with more specialization, while at low-volume nursing homes, there is no statistically significant effect of specialization on readmission rates (Table 7, columns 6 and 7). The difference in the effects of specialization on readmissions by volume is significant at the 5 % level. The results for other outcomes are more variable across volume strata. Compared to more specialized, high-volume nursing homes, more specialized, low-volume nursing homes exhibit a similar reduction in 30-day mortality, a statistically significant larger increase in length of stay, and similar increases in Medicare spending.

We lastly examine heterogeneity by area-level characteristics of patients (Appendix Table A2), stratifying by above versus below median Medicare Advantage penetration and percent living in poverty; median values are calculated at the ZIP code-year level. Patients who are admitted to more specialized nursing homes exhibit similar reductions in readmissions and mortality, regardless of whether they reside in a low- or high-MA-penetration ZIP code. We find larger increases in 90-day subsequent PAC and total Part A payments for patients residing in high-MA-penetration ZIP codes compared to those in low-MA-penetration ZIP codes, and these differences are statistically significant. Those living in below-median-poverty ZIP codes experience lower readmission rates from increased specialization, while above-median-poverty ZIP codes do not. However, patients from both below- and above-median-poverty ZIP codes have similar decreases in mortality from being admitted to more specialized nursing homes.

Specification checks

To examine the robustness of our main results, we consider a series of alternative specifications. Column 2 of Table 8 shows results from 2SLS estimation of EQ1 with the addition of nursing home fixed effects, along with (patient) ZIP code fixed effects. In this specification, we cluster standard errors at the nursing-home level. Variation in our endogenous regressor, percent Medicare, now comes from within-nursing home changes in PAC specialization over time. Our results do not change qualitatively with the inclusion of nursing home fixed effects, and some estimates increase in magnitude. This strengthens our confidence in the main findings since nursing home-level fixed effects control for time-invariant nursing home heterogeneity that may be correlated with PAC specialization and our outcomes, and they account for facility-level selection into specialization based on time-invariant unobservables (Konetzka et al., 2019). In column 3 of Table 8, we show results with the inclusion of hospital fixed effects (in place of nursing home fixed effects) and standard errors clustered at the hospital level. The estimates are nearly identical to the main results, suggesting that hospital-specific, time-invariant factors, including nursing home referral patterns, do not drive the results.

The inclusion of nursing home fixed effects may inadequately control for time-varying factors that impact nursing home production of PAC. A key time-varying factor is staffing levels. In Table 8, column 4, we include contemporaneous, unadjusted staffing ratios (specifically, RN, LPN, and CNA hours per resident day) from LTCFocus as additional controls in our main 2SLS specification. The results are qualitatively unchanged from those of our main specification.

One threat to identification is reverse causality: higher quality nursing homes have better patient outcomes, which may increase PAC demand for those nursing homes and lead them to become more specialized. To address this concern, we use publicly available nursing home-year-level data on quality from Nursing Home Compare from 2013 to 2018, including the overall five-star rating; health inspection rating; quality measure rating; staffing rating; total weighted health inspection score; and adjusted staffing hours per resident per day for nurse aides, licensed practical nurses, and registered nurses. Appendix Table A3 provides OLS estimates from a regression of these quality variables on percent Medicare. In all specifications, we include nursing home fixed effects, and in one model, we also include controls for whether the nursing home was part of a chain, for-profit, and based in a hospital; number of beds; and year fixed effects (Appendix Table A3, column 2). We find no evidence of significant correlations between these quality measures and specialization except for the overall star rating and the health inspection rating and score. Even for these quality measures, the magnitude of the correlation is very small.17 We also estimate a specification check in which we lag these quality variables and control for them in our 2SLS regressions (Table 8, column 5). Point estimates are similar to those from our main specification. The effect on 30-day readmissions is no longer statistically significant; however, our standard errors are larger because we can only estimate these regressions using 2014–2018 data. While we are reassured by the lack of correlation between quality and specialization, and the robustness of our results to the inclusion of lagged quality variables, the quality measures may be poor predictors of health outcomes, at least in the short run. To address this concern and more directly test for reverse causality, we adopt the approach used in Clark and Huckman (2012) and regress percent Medicare in year t + 1 on 30-day mortality in year t. We also include our usual set of controls, measured in year t, as well as nursing home fixed effects. The coefficient estimate (standard error) on mortality is −0.0003 (0.0001). The interpretation is that an additional patient death18 is associated with a −0.03 percentage point decrease in the following year’s percent Medicare, which we consider to be small. Taken together, we do not find evidence that reverse causality drives our main results.

As discussed previously, specialization and volume are distinct concepts. Given the substantive literature documenting improved outcomes with higher patient volumes, we investigate whether our results are driven by nursing homes with higher numbers of PAC admissions, irrespective of specialization. Because (contemporaneous) PAC admissions suffer from similar endogeneity concerns as percent Medicare, and are correlated with percent Medicare, we control for lagged number of PAC admissions in Table 8, column 6. Estimates from this specification are similar to those from our main specification and provide evidence that specialization affects outcomes independently of volume.

Another potential concern is that our percent Medicare variable may not accurately reflect specialization at the time of admission to a given nursing home. As noted above, PAC specialization is calculated as the proportion of a facility’s residents whose primary payer was Medicare at the time of the nursing home’s annual survey. Because we do not observe the survey date, it is possible that this measure was recorded months before or, more problematically, after a given admission. To test whether our results are sensitive to specialization measurement timing, we estimate a specification using a one-year lagged measure of PAC specialization as our endogenous variable. We find qualitatively similar results to our main specification, except for 90-day rehospitalization payment, which decreases for more specialized nursing homes (Table 8, column 7). The magnitudes of the coefficients using lagged PAC specialization are smaller, except for 30-day readmission and 90-day rehospitalization payment. We attribute the smaller effects to the introduction of (more) noise into the PAC specialization measure.

It is also possible that quartile categories do not capture meaningful differences in specialization across nursing homes. We test the sensitivity of our results to this modeling decision by creating a dummy variable equal to one if a nursing home-year’s PAC specialization is in the 75th percentile or above (i.e., the top quartile across all Medicare admissions), which corresponds to greater than 28.3% percent Medicare. Analogous to our main specification, we create two distance instruments—distance to the nearest specialized and non-specialized nursing homes—to predict the PAC specialization of the admitting nursing home. Estimates from this specification are similar to our main results, except that the coefficient for 30-day readmissions is no longer statistically different from zero (Table 8, column 8).

Mogstad et al. (2021) argue that the canonical monotonicity assumption given by Imbens and Angrist (1994) in the setting of multiple instruments is implausibly restrictive in that it assumes all individuals are moved by the instruments in a similar fashion (e.g., in the case of two instruments, all individuals must be moved into treatment more by one instrument compared to the other). Mogstad et al. show that under a weaker condition called partial monotonicity, the interpretation of the 2SLS estimand as a positively weighted average of causal effects for complier groups is preserved. In our context, partial monotonicity requires that each instrument encourages all patients toward or away from a more specialized facility. We follow Mogstad et al. and show that our instruments satisfy this partial monotonicity condition by estimating four separate models in which we use a single instrument to predict specialization, while conditioning on the remaining instruments (Appendix Table A4). There is some variability in the magnitudes and statistical significance of the effects of specialization across these four models. However, this is consistent with a loss of statistical precision due to relying on a single instrument and the fact that the single IV models identify treatment effects for different mixtures of complier groups. Moreover, the direction of the estimates remains unchanged from the main specification.

Finally, to test for potential nonlinear treatment effects, we add a second endogenous variable in both the first and second stages, percent Medicare squared (Appendix Table A5).19 The coefficient on this squared term is statistically significant, positive for 30-day mortality, and negative for length of stay, 90-day index SNF payment, and 90-day total Part A payment. This implies that the decrease in mortality and increase in length of stay and Medicare payments diminish at higher levels of specialization. There is no statistically significant effect of percent Medicare squared on 30-day readmission.

Discussion

We estimate the effects of nursing home specialization in PAC on patient outcomes, nursing home length of stay, and various measures of Medicare spending. Our 2SLS results imply that increased specialization in PAC leads to improvements in quality of care of PAC patients, as measured by hospital readmissions and mortality 30 days after hospital discharge. An increase in specialization also leads to longer SNF stays and increased Medicare spending, but these increases are dampened among specialized nursing homes owned by or located near hospitals. Longer SNF stays and higher Medicare spending are consistent with longer stays being an important input to PAC quality and rational responses by specialized nursing homes to the variability and uncertainty around the supply of PAC admissions.

We find that accounting for selection on unobservables into specialized nursing homes is critical, especially in terms of spending and length of stay. Whereas the OLS results imply that more specialized nursing homes have both better outcomes and lower costs, a net welfare improvement, the 2SLS results indicate a tradeoff between outcomes and spending. Specifically, more specialized nursing homes have lower readmissions and mortality, even after accounting for selection of healthier patients, but keep their patients longer and incur greater Medicare spending. Thus, the overall welfare effects of nursing home specialization are ambiguous based on our results.

While our study provides new and comprehensive evidence on the consequences of specialization in the nursing home setting for PAC patients, it has some limitations. In particular, we only consider the effects of specialization in PAC on patients receiving Medicare-financed PAC. We do not provide insights into the effects of PAC specialization on the outcomes of long-stay patients, which we believe is a fruitful area for future research, especially because the effects are theoretically ambiguous. On one hand, specializing in one type of care may come at the expense of expertise and provision of effective care in the other area, resulting in negative spillovers. On the other hand, the increased margins enjoyed by PAC-specialized facilities may provide them with more financial resources which they could invest in general quality improvements that benefit patients receiving long-term care services. Consistent with the latter, Lepore and Leland (2015) exploit within-nursing home variation in the proportion of Medicare-covered patient days from 2007 to 2010 and find evidence of quality improvements for long-stay residents in homes with a higher Medicare proportion, which they attribute to nursing homes having greater financial resources.

A second limitation is that we are unable to determine the exact mechanisms by which more specialized nursing homes improve patient outcomes and increase utilization. Using more granular data, future research could examine the adjustments facilities make as they become more specialized. For example, more specialized facilities may invest in capital (e.g., improvements in building conditions); train staff differently, hire staff with different skill levels, or hire additional staff;20 or improve internal processes. Understanding these mechanisms will help policymakers improve delivery of PAC in nursing homes more broadly.

Finally, our results contribute to the current debate around reimagining the financing and delivery of long-term care in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2022).21 Our results suggest that increased specialization in PAC leads to quality improvements for Medicare patients receiving care after a hospital discharge but also to longer SNF stays and increased Medicare spending. In other words, specialization leads to higher quality at higher cost. Specialization, therefore, is accompanied by a complex array of costs and benefits that policymakers must consider as they weigh alternatives to the current nursing home model, such as providing PAC and residential long-term care in separate settings.
