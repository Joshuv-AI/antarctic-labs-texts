Authors' Response: Ethical use of artificial intelligence in academic writing
Authors: Himel Mondal, Shaikat Mondal
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

We thank the authors[1] for taking the time to read our article, “ChatGPT in academic writing: Maximizing its benefits and minimizing the risks,”[2] and sharing their thoughtful concerns regarding the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in academic or scientific writing.

We acknowledge the valid points they raised regarding potential challenges and risks associated with AI-generated content from large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT, Google Bard, Microsoft Bing Chat, Perplexity, and Claude. The potential emergence of biased content in the generated output may come from biased training data. Hence, authors should exercise caution and refrain from unquestioningly relying on AI-generated content, recognizing that LLMs function as a tool and not as the primary author.[3]

For example, a typist was instructed by an officer to type a letter to a friend to convey birthday wishes. Due to the habit of drafting official letters, the typist addressed the letter with the salutation “Respected friend.” However, when the officer checked it, the typist was asked to modify it to “Dear friend.” The amended letter was signed by the officer and was dispatched to the friend. The recipient experienced joy upon receiving the heartfelt wish as if the letter was written by her friend. In the contemporary era, AI takes on a role akin to the typist, allowing users to command its functions for content creation and editing in accordance with their preferences.

In this context, we would like to share why the AI is not an author. According to the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors criteria, an author should satisfy at least one criterion from all four categories of tasks as shown in Table 1. While AI can satisfy the first and second criteria, it cannot satisfy the third and fourth criteria.[4]

The concern raised about overreliance on AI is an interesting and futuristic thought. When a new technology comes, we start using it and gradually it evolves. For example, the internet is such a technology. Can we think now about writing a research protocol without searching literature on the internet? However, think about the difficulty researchers faced before the introduction of PubMed in 1997.[5] Generative AI is evolving, and the future will tell us how much we will be dependent on that. In this context, we would like to share that leading publishers are not against the use of AI in medical writing. However, they suggest cautious use of the technology. In response to the suggestion for more concrete examples or case studies, we appreciate the feedback. We recognize the importance of providing practical guidance, and we will consider incorporating relevant examples and case studies in future publications to enhance the clarity of our recommendations.

The insights regarding the vulnerability of residents under training and postgraduate practitioners to potential misuse of AI tools are particularly valuable. Any technology comes with its own set of advantages and disadvantages. As the technology is with us and comes with free access, any resident can access it for any purpose. Hence, we presume that a widespread training program on the ethical use of AI technology for such vulnerable users is the need of the hour.
