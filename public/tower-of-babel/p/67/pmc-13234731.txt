Cordycepin alleviates nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety via AMPK/CREB/BDNF pathway activation in the hippocampus of male mice
Authors: Tianqi Wang, Shu Tian, Mengru He, Siyu Tang, Xinyu Ji, Qixin Deng, Wenjuan Zhang, Ge Jin, Jian Mao, Haifeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety is a key contributor to the return to cigarette smoking. Cordycepin, an adenosine analogue, is hypothesized to counteract the withdrawal-induced decline in hippocampal AMP/ATP ratios and consequent AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) dephosphorylation, as its monophosphate derivative structurally mimics AMP and directly activates AMPK. In this study, a mouse model of chronic nicotine exposure followed by withdrawal was used to investigate whether cordycepin mitigates nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety and its underlying mechanisms. Our results revealed that chronic nicotine exposure significantly elevated hippocampal AMPK phosphorylation, whereas nicotine withdrawal led to a sharp decline in p-AMPK levels (p-AMPK/t-AMPK: F = 23.11, P < 0.001), concomitant with pronounced anxiety-like behaviors in the elevated plus maze (open-arm time: F = 16.18, P < 0.01) and open field tests (center time: F = 6.63, P < 0.01). Pretreatment with cordycepin (10 mg/kg) effectively alleviated nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety-like behaviors (open-arm time: F = 11.74, P < 0.01; center time: F = 5.79, P < 0.01), an effect that was counteracted by the AMPK inhibitor Compound C. Further mechanistic studies via Western blot and immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated that cordycepin activated the hippocampal AMPK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway, restoring withdrawal-induced reductions in p-AMPK, p-CREB, and BDNF expression. This effect was further validated in PC12 cell experiments. In conclusion, cordycepin alleviates nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety by activating the AMPK/CREB/BDNF pathway, offering preliminary experimental evidence for its potential as an adjunctive therapy for smoking cessation.

Introduction

Cigarette smoking is a leading cause of preventable death and disease worldwide, and quitting smoking significantly reduces the risk of smoking-related illnesses and extends life expectancy among those who smoke (Qin et al., 2025). However, smoking cessation success rates remains low, largely due to nicotine withdrawal symptoms—such as physical discomfort, emotional disturbances, and cognitive impairments—that hinder long-term abstinence (Chellian et al., 2021). Despite the availability of cessation treatments such as nicotine replacement therapy, varenicline, and bupropion, their overall efficacy remains limited (Smith and George, 2020). Therefore, advancing research into the molecular mechanisms underlying nicotine withdrawal symptoms and developing novel therapeutic strategies are crucial for improving smoking cessation outcomes and public health.

AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a core regulator of cellular energy metabolism, composed of three subunits: α, β, and γ. Under conditions of energy deficiency, AMP binds to the γ subunit, triggering phosphorylation of the α subunit at Thr172 and subsequent AMPK activation. As a key cellular energy sensor, AMPK plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of metabolic disorders, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and central nervous system disorders (Ashraf and Van Nostrand, 2024). Emerging evidence suggests that AMPK is also involved in mood regulation. In a mouse model of chronic unpredictable stress (CUMS)-induced anxiety and depression, AMPK phosphorylation at Ser172 was reduced in the brain (Zhu et al., 2014), indicating its potential role in stress-induced mood disturbances. Similarly, in an OVX/D-Gal-induced Alzheimer's disease model, diminished AMPK activity in the amygdala led to disrupted energy metabolism, impaired autophagy, and neuronal damage, contributing to anxiety and depression-like behaviors (Kamel et al., 2022). Furthermore, in a repeated social defeat model, decreased AMPK activity in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) was associated with increased anxiety-like behaviors, and conditional AMPK knockout in the mPFC exacerbated these effects (Zhang et al., 2023). Notably, metformin, an AMPK activator, reduced nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety-like behaviors by enhancing AMPK signaling in the hippocampus (Brynildsen et al., 2018). These findings highlight AMPK as a pivotal regulator of anxiety and depression, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target. Pharmacological activation of the AMPK pathway may offer a novel approach for treating mood disorders.

Cordycepin, the primary bioactive component of Cordyceps sinensis and Cordyceps militaris, exhibits diverse pharmacological activities such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-tumor effects (Wang et al., 2024). Recent studies have increasingly explored its potential therapeutic applications in neurological diseases. For instance, cordycepin enhances cognitive function in Alzheimer's disease (AD) mice by regulating microglial HKII and PDK2, improving mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming, and promoting M2 polarization (Zhong et al., 2024). Additionally, cordycepin demonstrates metabolic regulatory effects and alleviates stress-induced anxiety and depression by modulating the gut-brain-fat axis (Jing et al., 2023). Critically, as an adenosine analog, cordycepin undergoes intracellular phosphorylation to form cordycepin monophosphate (CoMP), which structurally mimics AMP. CoMP can directly bind to the γ subunit of AMPK, thereby activating the AMPK signaling pathway (Hawley et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2014). Given that nicotine withdrawal reduces cellular energy status and suppresses AMPK activity (Brynildsen et al., 2018), we reasoned that cordycepin, through its AMP-mimetic property, might restore AMPK activity and thereby alleviate withdrawal symptoms. Although AMPK activity is suppressed following nicotine withdrawal, whether cordycepin mitigates nicotine withdrawal symptoms via AMPK pathway activation remains unexplored.

The involvement of AMPK in neuroplasticity and mood regulation may be mediated, at least in part, through its regulation of downstream signaling molecules, particularly brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). As a critical neurotrophin, BDNF plays essential roles in neuronal growth, survival, and plasticity, with profound implications for cognitive function, mood regulation, and the pathogenesis of neuropsychiatric disorders (Balaratnasingam and Janca, 2012, Boulle et al., 2012, Song et al., 2017). BDNF expression is primarily regulated by the transcription factor cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), which, upon phosphorylation, promotes BDNF transcription and thereby modulates its expression across different brain regions (Esvald et al., 2020). AMPK activation increases intracellular cAMP levels, which in turn activates protein kinase A to phosphorylate CREB and upregulate BDNF expression (Fang et al., 2020). Moreover, AMPK can regulate CREB activity directly or indirectly, further affecting the expression of BDNF and related neuroplasticity factors (Huang et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2022).

Based on the above findings, we hypothesized that cordycepin has the potential to alleviate nicotine withdrawal symptoms, and this effect may involve activation of the AMPK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway and subsequent enhancement of neuroplasticity. To test this hypothesis, we adopted a preventive design—administering cordycepin before nicotine exposure—which allows us to establish a clear cause-and-effect chain at the molecular level in a controlled system, avoiding confounders associated with established prolonged nicotine exposure and withdrawal-induced neuroadaptations. This study therefore examined the effect of cordycepin on nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety-like behaviors and the involvement of the AMPK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway in mediating this effect. By elucidating cordycepin’s mechanism of action, this study aimed to provide experimental evidence supporting its potential as a novel therapeutic intervention for smoking cessation.

Materials and methods

Animals

Specific pathogen-free-grade male C57BL/6 J mice (6 weeks old, 18 ± 2 g) were purchased from Zhejiang Weitong Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. The mice were group-housed (5–6 per cage) in individually ventilated cages under standard conditions: 22 ± 2 °C ambient temperature, 12-hour light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to water. All experimental protocols adhered to ethical guidelines for animal research and were approved by the Life Science Ethics Review Committee of Zhengzhou University (Approval No.: ZZUIRB2024–167).

Reagents

Nicotine (N412450) was purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals. Cordycepin (S31387) was obtained from Shanghai YuanYe Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. The AMPK inhibitor Compound C (BD237195) was acquired from Shanghai Bide Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor antagonist (Mecamylamine hydrochloride, M136896) was sourced from Aladdin. Nicotine, cordycepin, and Compound C were all dissolved in physiological saline for administration.

Animal experiment design

After a 1-week adaptation period, the mice were randomly divided into groups based on experimental objectives. The experiment design comprised three distinct components:

Nicotine-conditioned place preference (CPP) model

A nicotine-CPP model was established to evaluate nicotine-associated reward-related behavior. Mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10 per group) and subcutaneously (s.c.) administered either saline (control group) or different doses of nicotine (0.2 mg/kg, 0.5 mg/kg, 1.0 mg/kg) daily for 14 days (Walters et al., 2006). Following the conditioning period, CPP testing was performed to assess nicotine-associated reward learning. Based on the results, the intermediate dose (0.5 mg/kg) was selected for subsequent experiments.

Chronic nicotine exposure and withdrawal model

To examine the effects of nicotine withdrawal on mouse behavior and neurobiology, a chronic nicotine exposure model was established using 0.5 mg/kg nicotine (s.c.), and withdrawal was induced by discontinuing nicotine injections after 14 days. Mice were divided into three groups (n = 15 per group): Control group (normal saline s.c. for 16 days), nicotine exposure group (0.5 mg/kg nicotine s.c. for 16 days), and nicotine withdrawal group (0.5 mg/kg nicotine s.c. for 14 days, followed by 24 h of withdrawal prior to behavioral testing over 2 days). Following behavioral assessments, hippocampal tissues were collected for protein analyses.

Cordycepin and AMPK inhibitor treatment

To investigate the therapeutic potential of cordycepin on nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety-like behaviors and its dependence on AMPK signaling, we established additional treatment groups involving cordycepin and the AMPK inhibitor Compound C (Fig. 3A). Mice were randomly divided into seven groups (n = 10–15 per group): Control group (normal saline injection for 19 days), nicotine exposure group (0.5 mg/kg nicotine injection for 16 days), nicotine withdrawal group (0.5 mg/kg nicotine injection for 14 days, followed by 24 h of withdrawal prior to behavioral testing over 2 days), Cordycepin 10 mg/kg group (Cor 1), Cordycepin 10 mg/kg pre-treatment + withdrawal group (Cor 1 + WD), Cordycepin and AMPK inhibitor co-treatment + withdrawal group (CC + Cor 1 + WD), and Cordycepin 20 mg/kg pre-treatment + withdrawal group (Cor 2 + WD). For the Cor 1 + WD and Cor 2 + WD groups, cordycepin (10 or 20 mg/kg, intraperitoneally [i.p.]) was administered daily starting 3 days prior to nicotine exposure and continued throughout the experiment period. The CC + Cor 1 + WD group received concurrent treatment with the AMPK inhibitor Compound C (10 mg/kg, i.p.), initiated 3 days before nicotine withdrawal and maintained until experiment completion. Following all pharmacological interventions, mice underwent behavioral testing, followed by hippocampal tissue collection for molecular analyses.

Behavioral experiments

All behavioral experiments were digitally recorded using a video tracking system. Behavioral parameters were automatically quantified using the ANY-maze software package. Between experimental sessions, all apparatuses were thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol to eliminate odor interference.

CPP test

The CPP test was employed to quantitatively assess nicotine-associated reward-related behavior in mice. The standardized three-phase protocol was conducted as follows:

Habituation phase (days 1–3): Prior to conditioning, mice were allowed to freely explore the CPP apparatus for 15 min each day with the connecting door open. Time spent in each chamber was recorded to establish baseline values.

Conditioning phase (days 4–17): In the morning sessions, the control group received daily injections of normal saline (s.c.), while the nicotine groups received different doses of nicotine (0.2, 0.5, or 1.0 mg/kg, s.c.). All mice were immediately confined to the drug-paired chamber (white) for 15 min. In the afternoon sessions, all mice received normal saline (s.c.) and were confined to the opposite (black) chamber to balance associations. Training lasted for 14 days.

Testing phase (day 18): Following the final conditioning session, CPP testing was conducted, with time spent in each chamber during a 15-minute period recorded and CPP score calculated.

Open field test (OFT)

A 15-minute test session was conducted. Mice were placed in the center of an open field arena, and their movement trajectories were recorded. The arena was divided into corner zones (4 corners) and a center zone (3 × 3 grid in the center). Time spent in each zone was recorded. Anxiety level was assessed by the ratio of time spent in the corner zones versus center zone.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The EPM test was performed to assess anxiety levels in mice. The total test duration was 5 min, during which the time spent in the open arms and closed arms was recorded. The frequency of entering the open arms (less frequent entries to the open arms indicate higher anxiety levels) was also recorded.

Anxiety behavior indicators

Mice were placed in the OFT chamber, and the number of face-washing, exploration, and standing on forepaws events were recorded during a 5-minute period. Full body vertical standing was counted as standing. The experiment used a double-blind random assignment method.

Marble burying test (MBT)

The MBT capitalizes on the fact that mice tend to bury objects when in an anxious state. Fifteen glass marbles were placed equidistantly in a chamber, and mice were allowed to freely explore for 5 min. The number of buried marbles (more than 50% buried counted as "buried") was recorded.

Behavioral test sequence

Withdrawal- and anxiety-related behavioral assessments were conducted over two consecutive days following the completion of nicotine treatment. On day 1, mice underwent the MBT. On day 2, the OFT was conducted first, followed by the EPM. The behavioral assays were arranged in ascending order of anxiogenic intensity to minimize carryover stress effects between paradigms. This two-day design was implemented to reduce cumulative stress and fatigue associated with multiple behavioral tests performed within a single day.

Tissue collection

Following completion of the behavioral tests, mice were returned to their home cages for 30 min to allow acute stress hormone levels to normalize. Mice were then anesthetized with 0.5% sodium pentobarbital (i.p.) and transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline. The brain was rapidly removed, and the hippocampus was dissected on ice. Hippocampal tissues for Western blot analysis were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until use. Tissues for immunohistochemistry (IHC) were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by paraffin embedding and sectioning.

Western blot analysis

Mouse hippocampal tissues or PC12 cells were lysed on ice using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm for 20 min), the supernatant was collected as total protein, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. For protein denaturation, the lysate was mixed with 5 × loading buffer (4:1 ratio), heated at 95 °C for 5–10 min, and cooled before performing sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (120 V for 90 min). After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (300 mA for 2.5 h in ice water). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk or bovine serum albumin for 2 h, followed by three washes with TBST. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-AMPKα (5831S), anti-p-AMPKα (50081S), anti-AMPKβ1/2 (4150S), anti-p-AMPKβ1 (4186S), anti-ACC (3676S), anti-p-ACC (11818S), anti-CREB (9197S), and anti-p-CREB (9198S) (all from Cell Signaling Technology); anti-BDNF (ab108319; Abcam); anti-activity-regulated cytoskeleton-associated protein (ARC) (sc-17839; Santa Cruz); and anti-β-actin (66009–1-Ig) and anti-β-Tubulin (10094–1-AP) (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The next day, the membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with goat anti-rabbit (SA00001–2) or goat anti-mouse (SA00001–1) secondary antibody (Proteintech) for 1–2 h at room temperature. After washing, chemiluminescence detection was performed using ECL reagents, and images were captured. Band intensities were quantified using Image J software.

IHC

IHC was performed as previously described (Qin et al., 2025). Briefly, paraformaldehyde-fixed hippocampal tissues underwent paraffin embedding, sectioning, dewaxing, and dehydration using a gradient ethanol series. After antigen retrieval, slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-BDNF antibody (ab108319, Abcam). After rinsing with TBST, slides were treated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody polymer (SA00001–2, Proteintech), followed by application of the DAB solution (ZLI-9018, Zsbio, China) and counterstaining with hematoxylin.

Cell culture

PC12 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Once the cells reached an appropriate density, they were treated with 10 μmol/L nicotine for various durations (8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h) to assess AMPK activation levels. In the cordycepin treatment experiment, cells were treated with 20 μmol/L or 40 μmol/L cordycepin for 6 h, and AMPK activation was detected. To simulate nicotine withdrawal, cells were initially treated with 10 μmol/L nicotine for 48 h, followed by 6 h of 20 μmol/L cordycepin treatment, and changes in AMPK activation levels were analyzed. After all treatments, cells were collected for Western blot analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 21.0 software. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used. Paired and unpaired comparisons were performed using paired t-tests and independent-samples t-tests, respectively. Continuous data are presented as the mean ± SD. Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

AMPK phosphorylation in the hippocampus is elevated during nicotine exposure but diminishes after withdrawal

We first established a chronic nicotine exposure and withdrawal model in mice using gradient doses of nicotine. After one week of treatment, both 0.5 mg/kg and 1.0 mg/kg nicotine significantly increased the CPP scores in mice (Fig. 1A), indicating the induction of nicotine-associated reward-related behavior at both doses. Because nicotine was paired with the initially non-preferred white compartment in this biased CPP design, the increased CPP score reflects a reduction of innate aversion to the drug-paired chamber rather than the formation of an absolute place preference de novo. Based on these results, we selected the lower dose (0.5 mg/kg) for subsequent exposure and withdrawal studies. Further CPP testing demonstrated that both 7-day and 14-day treatments with 0.5 mg/kg nicotine effectively induced stable nicotine-CPP (Fig. 1B and C). Following chronic nicotine exposure and CPP conditioning, nicotine administration was discontinued to induce withdrawal. After 24 h of abstinence, the withdrawal group exhibited significantly reduced time spent in the open arms but increased time in the closed arms in the EPM test (Fig. 1D and E). Similarly, in the OFT, withdrawal mice spent significantly more time in the corner zones and less time in the center area (Fig. 1F and G). Furthermore, nicotine withdrawal significantly increased the frequency of anxiety-related behaviors, such as face washing (Fig. 1H), one-legged standing (Fig. 1I), and exploratory activity (Fig. 1J). Together, these findings demonstrate that nicotine withdrawal induces pronounced anxiety-like behaviors in mice.

Next, we explored whether chronic nicotine exposure and withdrawal modulate AMPK activation in the hippocampus of mice. Western blot analysis revealed that nicotine-exposed mice exhibited significantly elevated phosphorylation levels of AMPKα (p-AMPKα/t-AMPKα), ACC (p-ACC/t-ACC), and AMPKβ1 (p-AMPKβ1/t-AMPKβ1/2) in hippocampal tissue compared to controls. However, these increases were significantly attenuated following nicotine withdrawal (Fig. 1K-1N). These findings suggest that nicotine exposure activates AMPK in the hippocampus, while withdrawal reverses this effect.

Cordycepin pretreatment alleviates nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety

Given that AMPK activation fluctuates during chronic nicotine exposure and withdrawal and that cordycepin is a known activator of AMPK with neuroprotective properties, we investigated whether cordycepin could ameliorate nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety and its underlying mechanism through comprehensive behavioral assessments.

In the EPM test, mice pretreated with cordycepin at 10 mg/kg exhibited significantly increased time spent in the open arms and decreased time in the closed arms during withdrawal, demonstrating its efficacy in alleviating nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety (Fig. 2A and B). However, the higher dose (20 mg/kg) showed no significant anxiolytic effect. Notably, when co-administered with the AMPK inhibitor Compound C (10 mg/kg), the anti-anxiety effects of 10 mg/kg cordycepin were abolished, as evidenced by the lack of significant changes in open/closed arm exploration time. The OFT results corroborated the EPM findings. Mice pretreated with 10 mg/kg cordycepin spent significantly less time in the corner zones and more time in the center zone compared with withdrawal controls (Fig. 2C and D). In contrast, the higher cordycepin dose (20 mg/kg) did not produce significant anxiolytic effects. Importantly, the anti-anxiety effects of 10 mg/kg cordycepin were completely blocked by co-administration of Compound C (10 mg/kg), as evidenced by the lack of significant changes center/corner time distribution.

The MBT and body behavior indicators demonstrated that nicotine withdrawal significantly increased anxiety-like behaviors compared to the nicotine exposure group: Nicotine-withdrawn mice exhibited significantly increased marble-burying behavior and elevated frequencies of face washing, standing, and exploratory behaviors (Fig. 2E-H). Notably, pretreatment with 10 mg/kg cordycepin significantly attenuated these anxiety-related behaviors. However, this anxiolytic effect was completely abolished when cordycepin was co-administered with the AMPK inhibitor Compound C. Furthermore, the higher dose of cordycepin (20 mg/kg) showed no significant anti-anxiety effects. Collectively, these results demonstrate that cordycepin's anxiolytic action during nicotine withdrawal is dose-dependent and mediated through AMPK signaling.

Cordycepin activates AMPK in the hippocampus of mice

Western blot results (Fig. 3B-D) showed that both nicotine (0.5 mg/kg) and cordycepin (10 mg/kg) significantly increased AMPK activation in the hippocampus of mice, whereas nicotine withdrawal markedly reduced AMPK activation. Pretreatment with 10 mg/kg cordycepin prevented the withdrawal-induced decline in AMPK activation, restoring phophorylation levels comparable to those in nicotine-exposed mice. This reversal effect was completely abolished by co-treatment with the AMPK inhibitor Compound C, confirming the specificity of cordycepin's action. In contrast, the higher cordycepin dose (20 mg/kg) was not observed to modulate AMPK activation. Interestingly, cordycepin did not significantly alter p-AMPKβ1 levels (Fig. 3E). Cordycepin modulates neuronal plasticity by activating CREB/BDNF signaling to alleviate nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety symptoms

Given that AMPK activation regulates CREB and BDNF expression, we next investigated whether cordycepin influences hippocampal CREB phosphorylation and the expression of neuronal plasticity markers (BDNF and ARC).

Western blot analysis demonstrated that both nicotine administration and 10 mg/kg cordycepin treatment significantly elevated the ratios of p-CREB/t-CREB and m-BDNF/pro-BDNF in the hippocampus, along with ARC expression levels, compared to controls (Fig. 4A-D). Conversely, nicotine withdrawal significantly reduced these molecular levels relative to the nicotine exposure group. Importantly, pretreatment with 10 mg/kg cordycepin prior to withdrawal completely restored these molecular changes to levels comparable to the nicotine exposure group. IHC analysis revealed region-specific BDNF expression patterns in the hippocampus, with changes in the CA1 region mirroring the Western blot results (Fig. 4E). No significant alterations were observed in the CA3 or DG region, suggesting regional selectivity in these plasticity modifications.

These findings collectively indicate that: (1) Nicotine enhances hippocampal neuronal plasticity via the AMPK/CREB/BDNF pathway; (2) the activity of this pathway declines during withdrawal; and (3) cordycepin exerts its anxiolytic effects by reactivating this plasticity pathway in a regionally selective manner.

Cordycepin activates the AMPK/CREB/BDNF pathway in PC12 cells

To validate cordycepin’s modulation of the AMPK/CREB/BDNF pathway, we established an in vitro model of chronic nicotine exposure and withdrawal in PC12 cells. Time-course experiments revealed that 10 μmol/L nicotine treatment induced transient AMPK activation, peaking at 36 h and returning to baseline by 48 h (Fig. 5A-C). Pharmacological inhibition experiments confirmed the specificity of this response, as both the AMPK inhibitor Compound C and the nicotinic receptor antagonist Methamine attenuated nicotine-induced AMPK activation (Fig. 5D-E). In the nicotine withdrawal state (characterized by diminished AMPK activity at 48 h post-nicotine exposure), treatment with 20 μmol/L cordycepin for 6 h effectively restored AMPK activation (Fig. 5I-J), recapitulating our in vivo observations in mice.

Furthermore, both nicotine (10 μM) and cordycepin (20 μM) treatment for 12 h significantly enhanced CREB phosphorylation (p-CREB/CREB ratio) and promoted BDNF maturation (m-BDNF/pro-BDNF ratio) in PC12 cells (Fig. 5K-M), indicating that both compounds can activate CREB and modulate neuronal plasticity in vitro.

Discussion

This study establishes a chronic nicotine exposure-withdrawal mouse model to elucidate the anxiolytic mechanism of cordycepin during nicotine withdrawal. Our findings demonstrate that cordycepin ameliorates nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety by activating the hippocampal AMPK/CREB/BDNF neuroplasticity pathway. These results not only offer a potential therapeutic strategy for managing nicotine withdrawal symptoms but also highlight cordycepin's broader applicability in treating neurological disorders involving AMPK-mediated neuroplasticity.

Nicotine withdrawal often induces a range of aversive somatic and emotional symptoms that frequently precipitate an early return to smoking (Lindson et al., 2023). While the neurobiological mechanisms remain incompletely characterized. AMPK, a key intracellular energy sensor and metabolic regulatory hub (Radhi et al., 2021), appears critically involved in nicotine's physiological effects. Studies have shown that nicotine selectively activates the catalytic AMPKα2 subunit in adipocytes, stimulating lipolysis and contributing to nicotine-induced weight loss (Wu et al., 2015). In the rat hippocampus, nicotine activates AMPK to modulate transcriptional coactivators and ameliorate lipopolysaccharide-induced fear memory deficits (Shu et al., 2020). Notably, Yin et al. (Brynildsen et al., 2018) discovered that chronic nicotine exposure enhances hippocampal AMPKα activity to promote neuroplasticity, whereas withdrawal-induced AMPK suppression correlates with anxiety/depression-like behaviors. Our findings showed that nicotine withdrawal reduced the phosphorylation levels of both AMPKα and AMPKβ in the hippocampus. Notably, cordycepin selectively restored AMPKα phosphorylation, whereas AMPKβ phosphorylation remained unchanged. These results suggest that cordycepin may exert its protective effects primarily through AMPKα-associated signaling rather than through uniform modulation of all AMPK subunits.

Cordycepin, a bioactive natural compound, exhibits robust neuroprotective properties across multiple neurological disease models. In ischemic animal models, cordycepin significantly ameliorates learning and memory deficits (Cai et al., 2013, Cao et al., 2018, Robertson, 1988). The compound also alleviates stress and improves depression-like behaviors in CUMS animal models (Li et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2025). Notably, in neurodegenerative diseases, cordycepin demonstrates therapeutic potential for both Parkinson's disease and AD, improving cognitive function and reducing neuronal damage in preclinical models (Huang et al., 2023, Jiao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024).

As an adenosine analog, cordycepin has been extensively investigated for its ability to activate AMPK signaling, with most studies focusing on its therapeutic potential in metabolic (Lan et al., 2021, Tian et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2014), cardiovascular (Xu et al., 2022, Yu et al., 2021), and oncological disorders (Khan and Tania, 2023, Yoon et al., 2022). However, studies examining cordycepin's neuroprotective effects via AMPK activation in the central nervous system remain scarce. To our knowledge, only one study has demonstrated that cordycepin protects dopaminergic neurons in a Parkinson's disease model by activating substantia nigra AMPK signaling to suppress Drp1-mediated pathological mitochondrial fission (Zhang et al., 2021). This notable gap in research highlights the need for further investigation into cordycepin's neuromodulatory mechanisms via AMPK pathways.

Our results demonstrate that cordycepin (10 mg/kg) effectively ameliorates nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety behaviors, an effect that was completely abolished by co-administration of the AMPK inhibitor Compound C. This pharmacological evidence establishes AMPK activation as the crucial mechanism underlying cordycepin's anxiolytic action. At the molecular level, cordycepin treatment restored hippocampal AMPK phosphorylation in withdrawn mice to levels comparable to nicotine-exposed animals, correlating with behavioral improvements. Interestingly, the higher cordycepin dose (20 mg/kg) did not produce comparable anti-anxiety effects, mirroring the previous observation that the anxiolytic and antidepressant effects of cordycepin followed a non-monotonic dose-response pattern, with 12.5 mg/kg demonstrating superior efficacy to the 25 mg/kg dose (Jing et al., 2023). These findings not only confirm the therapeutic window for cordycepin's anxiolytic efficacy but also emphasize the critical importance of dose optimization in developing cordycepin-based treatments for nicotine withdrawal.

Neuronal plasticity represents the dynamic ability of neurons to undergo structural and functional adaptations in response to external stimuli or experience. BDNF serves as a critical modulator of neuronal plasticity (Fiedler et al., 2021). Previous evidence reveals markedly reduced BDNF levels in patients with anxiety and depression (Wang et al., 2023), with such deficits contributing to impaired synaptic transmission, diminished neurogenesis, and hippocampal atrophy—pathological changes strongly associated with affective symptom manifestation (Autry and Monteggia, 2012, Colucci-D′Amato et al., 2020, Wang et al., 2023). Notably, antidepressant drugs like fluoxetine demonstrate therapeutic efficacy by restoring BDNF expression in the hippocampus (Chen et al., 2006, Jin et al., 2017), underscoring the central role of neurotrophic signaling in emotional regulation. Clinical observations reveal significantly elevated plasma BDNF levels in individuals who smoke compared to those who do not (Abdelkhalek et al., 2022). Animal studies further demonstrate that nicotine exposure and withdrawal dynamically regulate BDNF expression across multiple brain regions (Huang et al., 2020). Specifically, hippocampal BDNF levels show characteristic increases during chronic nicotine exposure but marked decreases during withdrawal periods (Carboni et al., 2021, Keyworth et al., 2018, Valvassori et al., 2019). Our Western blot results show that nicotine significantly elevated the expression of BDNF and ARC, two neuronal plasticity markers, in the hippocampus, while nicotine withdrawal reduced these molecules to control levels. These findings implicate BDNF-dependent hippocampal neuronal plasticity as a potential mechanistic substrate for nicotine-associated reward adaptation and withdrawal symptoms. It should also be noted that the present study employed a biased CPP paradigm, in which nicotine was paired with the initially non-preferred chamber. Therefore, the increased CPP scores observed here likely reflect attenuation of innate aversion rather than the generation of a novel contextual preference, which is nonetheless widely accepted as an indicator of nicotine-associated reward learning.

The BDNF gene is primarily regulated by the transcription factor CREB, whose transcriptional activity depends on phosphorylation at serine 133. This post-translational modification enables CREB to bind the cAMP response element in the BDNF gene, initiating transcription (Guo et al., 2017, Wang et al., 2018). Importantly, AMPK, as a key cellular energy sensor, enhances BDNF expression by activating CREB (Huang et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2022). For example, the anti-diabetic drug metformin upregulates BDNF via AMPK/CREB pathway activation, conferring neuroprotection (Liu et al., 2022). These collective findings establish the AMPK/CREB/BDNF axis as a critical regulator of both neuronal plasticity and neuroprotective mechanisms. Specifically, we observed that both nicotine and cordycepin (10 mg/kg) significantly enhanced hippocampal phosphorylation of p-CREB in the hippocampus, while nicotine withdrawal suppressed p-CREB phosphorylation. Notably, cordycepin pretreatment (10 mg/kg) prevented the withdrawal-induced downregulation of p-CREB and BDNF expression, an effect that was abolished by AMPK inhibition. Immunohistochemical staining further demonstrated that changes in BDNF expression in the hippocampal CA1 region mirrored the trend of AMPK activation. The region-specific BDNF response to cordycepin—significant in CA1 but not in CA3 or DG—may reflect functional and molecular heterogeneity across hippocampal subfields. Previous studies have shown that the CA1 region is particularly sensitive to stress-related neuroplasticity and activity-dependent BDNF signaling, which may increase the detectability of cordycepin-induced changes in this region (Chen et al., 2007, Hall et al., 2000). In contrast, although CA3 and DG also contribute to emotional regulation, their distinct connectivity, cellular composition, and molecular response patterns may render them less responsive under the present experimental conditions. In addition, subtle alterations in CA3 or DG may not have been detectable using the current experimental approach. Future studies employing region-specific interventions and more sensitive analytical methods are warranted to further clarify the differential responsiveness of hippocampal subfields to cordycepin treatment. Collectively, these findings suggest that cordycepin enhances neuronal plasticity through AMPK-mediated activation of CREB/BDNF signaling, with a preferential effect observed in the CA1 subfield. To further validate this mechanism, we established an in vitro nicotine addiction-withdrawal model using PC12 cells. Time-course experiments showed that nicotine induced AMPK activation, peaking at 36 h and returning to baseline by 48 h. Cordycepin treatment effectively restored AMPK activity during the withdrawal phase and upregulated p-CREB and BDNF expression, recapitulating our in vivo findings.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the in vitro experiments utilized PC12 cells, a rat adrenal pheochromocytoma-derived cell line, rather than primary hippocampal neurons. Although PC12 cells cannot fully replicate the physiological properties of central nervous system neurons, they have been widely used in neurobiology and nicotine-related mechanistic studies because they provide a stable and well-characterized model for investigating intracellular signaling pathways. Previous studies have demonstrated that PC12 cells express functional nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), including the α4β2 and α7 subtypes, as well as AMPK, CREB, and BDNF, which are the core signaling components investigated in the present study (Takahashi et al., 1999, Di et al., 2025). Importantly, the molecular changes observed in PC12 cells were consistent with those detected in mouse hippocampal tissues, supporting the biological relevance of the in vitro findings. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that PC12 cells cannot fully reproduce the cellular complexity, neuronal connectivity, and signaling dynamics of hippocampal neurons in vivo. Future studies using primary hippocampal cultures or neuron-derived cell lines will therefore be necessary to further validate and extend the present findings. Second, the study was conducted exclusively in male mice. This decision was guided by two factors: (1) It reflects the primary demographic of people who smoke in China (overwhelmingly male); and (2) it was essential to minimize the significant variability introduced by the hormonal fluctuations of the female estrous cycle, which is known to influence anxiety, nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, and withdrawal responses. Using males allowed us to establish a clear, interpretable baseline for the neurobiological mechanism under controlled conditions. However, this choice constrains the translational value of our findings, as substantial evidence indicates sex differences in nicotine susceptibility and withdrawal. The lack of female subjects is a significant shortcoming given the global prevalence of smoking among women, and it limits the generalizability of the results. Third, a preventive design was deliberately chosen for this mechanistic proof-of-concept study to minimize confounding variables and allows for a clearer interpretation of the causal relationship between cordycepin, the AMPK/CREB/BDNF pathway, and the subsequent reduction of withdrawal-induced anxiety. In contrast, medicinal use in the clinic would likely be initiated among patients who are already physically dependent on tobacco. Having established this causal chain, a critical next step for future research will be to validate whether cordycepin retains its efficacy when administered in a therapeutic schedule—that is, given after the establishment of nicotine dependence and during the withdrawal phase—to directly mirror a potential clinical use case.

In conclusion, this study elucidates the critical role of AMPK/CREB/BDNF pathway dynamics in chronic nicotine exposure and withdrawal. We demonstrate that cordycepin effectively ameliorates nicotine withdrawal-induced anxiety by restoring AMPK-mediated neuronal plasticity. These results provide mechanistic insights into nicotine withdrawal pathophysiology, preclinical validation for targeting AMPK signaling in withdrawal management, and compelling evidence for cordycepin's therapeutic potential in substance withdrawal disorders. This work significantly expands the potential clinical applications of cordycepin beyond its traditional uses.
