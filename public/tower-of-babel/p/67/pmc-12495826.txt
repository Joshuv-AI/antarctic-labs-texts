High return to sports and favorable long-term clinical outcomes in professional elite skiers following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction utilizing hamstring tendon autografts
Authors: Qingfa Song, Bao Zhang, Aofei Gao, Zhenxing Shao, Xiaofeng Pang, Qiang Liu, Guoqing Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury are commonly observed in athletes; however, there is a scarcity of research examining the long-term outcome of the use of hamstring tendon autografts for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) in professional elite skiers.PurposesTo report the clinical outcome and associated factors of ACLR in a professional elite skiing population in China.Patients and methodsThe research encompassed a cohort of elite skiers in China who underwent ACLR utilizing hamstring tendon autografts. The selection criteria were limited to professional elite skiers, specifically those affiliated with the national ski team who had attained top three rankings in international or domestic competitions prior to their injury occurrence. The data on the levels of return to sports, graft rupture occurrences, long-term functional outcomes, and the influencing factors were comprehensively examined.ResultsA cohort of 29 elite skiers (33 knees) were included for follow-up at an average of 8.6 ± 4.1 years (range, 5.0–18.0 years). Following ACLR, only one elite skier was unable to resume participation in training, while 84.8% (28/33) of elite skiers successfully returned to compete in official competitions. Furthermore, 66.7% (22/33) of elite skiers achieved top three placements in international or domestic competitions, indicating a successful return to elite-level competition. Athletes who return to formal competition exhibited a significantly shorter delay between injury and surgery compared to those who did not (3.1 months and 13.7 months, respectively, P = 0.039). Graft rupture after ACLR was observed in six knees (18.1%). Several associated factors for graft rupture were identified, including a high Beighton score, a longer delay between injury and ACLR, a graft diameter less than 8 mm, and partial meniscectomy.ConclusionThe majority of professional elite skiers were capable of returning to sport after ACL injuries with ACLR utilizing hamstring tendon autografts. Moreover, elite skiers who resumed participation in formal competitions exhibited a shorter delay between injury and surgery, in contrast to those who did not.Levels of evidenceLevel IV. Keywords: Anterior cruciate ligament, Skiing, Snowboarding, Return to sport, Hamstring tendon autografts

Introduction

Skiing and snowboarding have become popular recreational and competitive sports in recent years. The most common injury in professional skiers is to the knee, and the most frequent specific diagnosis is injury of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) [3, 22, 34]. Knee joint injuries account for 29.9% of professional skiers’ sports injuries, among which ACL injuries account for 41.5% [34]. The treatment of young athletes with ACL injury is complex because they have a higher demand for return to sports (RTS) after surgery and are at a high risk of subsequent graft rupture [19]. Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is frequently performed to treat ACL injury in this population, with high success rate [15, 24, 35]. By systematic review, Mohtadi et al. [27] reported a 63–97% RTS rate for highly competitive athletes after ACLR. However, following an ACLR, it is important to note that not all athletes who achieve optimal functional and clinical outcomes will be able to return to formal competition [2]. Multiple factors are associated with RTS after ACLR, including preinjury activity level, the surgical technique used, and psychological factors [2, 14, 32].

Despite the extensive literature available on the prevalence of injuries among skiers and snowboarders, there is a scarcity of research regarding the postoperative outcomes following ACLR in this specific population [3, 20, 22, 34]. Among the limited number of studies focusing on skiers and snowboarders, Erickson et al. [16] reported an 87% rate of RTS after ACLR in skiers participating in the winter X-Games, while snowboarders had a slightly lower rate of 70%. Guy et al. [18] found graft rupture rates were 34.0% in the isolated ACLR group and 6.5% in the ACLR with a lateral extra-articular procedure group for elite alpine skiers. However, there is a lack of extensive investigation into the impact of ACLR on the career of professional elite skiers or snowboarders, particularly in terms of their ability to return to formal competition and the associated risk factors for RTS and graft rupture. Furthermore, the existing literature lacks comprehensive investigations on elite Chinese skiers. China’s resource-intensive training model for elite athletes are different from the predominantly club-based pathways common in Western countries [34]. The utilization of diverse training methodologies may result in variations in athletic performance levels.

This study was designed to analyze the level of RTS, the long-term functional outcomes, and graft rupture in professional elite Chinese skiers who have undergone ACLR utilizing hamstring tendon autografts. Additionally, the study aimed to investigate the potential factors associated with the level of RTS and graft rupture. We hypothesized that the professional elite skiers who undergo ACLR utilizing hamstring tendons will have a low graft rupture rate and will be able to return to high levels of sports.

Methods

Study design

A retrospective, single-center study was performed on professional elite skiers who underwent ACLR according to the STROBE guidelines. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the authors’ hospital (No. M2022783).

Potential participants for this study were identified as professional elite skiers from China who were members of the national ski team and had achieved top three times in major domestic or international competitions before sustaining ACL injury. These skiers had undergone ACLR surgery using the hamstring tendon autografts between the years 2004 and 2018. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) multiple ligament injuries, (2) patients with a previous surgery history on the same side of the knee, (3) ACLR with a double-bundle procedure, and (4) Incomplete data or less than 5 years of follow-up (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart

Surgical techniques and rehabilitation

The surgical procedures were conducted by highly experienced arthroscopic specialists at the author’s hospital. All the ACLR for patients enrolled in the study were performed with autogenously hamstring single bundle reconstruction. The graft preparation, tunnel techniques, femoral and tibial fixation methods, and devices were used as previously published [25].

All patients underwent a uniform rehabilitation protocol. On the first day after the operation, exercises such as quadriceps sets, straight-leg raises, and prone hangs were initiated. Postoperative day 3 marked the initiation of range of motion exercises. Range of motion was gradually increased to 90° after 1 week and to 120° after 1 month. Crutches were used for 3 to 4 weeks, but patients were allowed to bear weight as tolerated. At 4 to 6 months, patients advanced to running without the need for a brace. Approximately 6 months after the surgical procedure, individuals were permitted to engage in sports activities. After undergoing surgery, patients were permitted to resume their preinjury levels of physical activity within a timeframe of 9 to 12 months.

Evaluation

The basic data and surgical information of the patients were obtained through the hospital medical record system or questionnaires. All athletes were clinically assessed by a sports medicine specialist at final follow-up (at least 5 years after the surgery). The postoperative data of the patients was obtained through the network questionnaire, including the level of RTS, occurrence of graft rupture, clinical scores such as the International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) scores, Tegner Activity Scale scores, and Lysholm knee scoring scale scores at final follow-up. The level of RTS was further validated through consultation with the national team doctor and coach at final follow-up.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS Statistics software package (IBM, version 20.0), and a significance level of p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Quantitative data are expressed as mean and standard deviation or median and range, and qualitative data are described as numbers and percentages. Comparisons between quantitative variables were made using either a Student t test or a nonparametric test, depending on whether the data were normally distributed. Qualitative variables were compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact test.

Results

Patient demographics

A total of 29 professional elite skiers (33 knees) met the study criteria and were available for follow-up at an average of 8.6 ± 4.1 years (range, 5.0–18.0 years). The percentage of freestyle skiers, alpine skiers, and snowboarders were 65.5% (19/29), 17.2% (5/29), and 17.2% (5/29) respectively. The average age during the surgery was 20.6 years, including 27.6% (8/29) of male. Table 1 summarizes some basic characteristics of our study sample. Before these elite skiers were injured, 20.7% (6/29) of athletes placed in the top three in the World Cup or Winter Olympics Games and 79.3% (23/29) placed in the top three in domestic competitions.

During the follow-up period of at least 5 years after the surgery, the athletes had a mean Lysholm score of 80.5 ± 14.1, a mean IKDC score of 82.2 ± 11.0 and a median Tegner Activity Scale score of 8 (Table 2).

In this series, four elite skiers sustained bilateral ACL knee injuries. After ACLR surgery, they were able to achieve comparable results to the athletes with unilateral ACL injury. All of them were able to resume to formal competition. Among them, three elite skiers (75%) were able to return to the elite level.

Graft rupture and associated factors

Graft rupture after ACLR occurred in six knees (18.1%). Revision surgery was performed with BPTB autograft or the Ligament Augmentation and Reconstruction System (LARS) ligament. High Beighton score (P = 0.015), longer delay between injury and ACLR (p = 0.011), a graft diameter less than 8 mm (p = 0.005), and partial meniscectomy (p = 0.027) were the associated factors for graft rupture after ACLR (Table 4). The cut off value of the Beighton score is 4.5 and of the duration between injury and surgery is six weeks for graft rupture after ACLR through ROC curve.

Discussion

The majority of professional elite skiers were capable of returning to sport after ACL injuries with ACLR utilizing hamstring tendon autografts. Furthermore, those who resumed formal competition had a shorter delay between injury and surgery compared to those who did not. Moreover, through comparative analysis, graft rupture elite skiers after ACLR had a High Beighton score, longer duration between injury and surgery, a higher proportion of graft diameter less than 8 mm, and a higher proportion of partial meniscectomy. The results from this study may help surgeons and athletes to understand the RTS levels of elite skiers after ACLR.

The treatment of young athletes with ACL injury is complex because they have a higher demand for RTS after surgery and are at a high risk of subsequent ACL injury [19]. For athletes, RTS at their pre-injury level with minimal time is an important metric indicative of successful recovery. ACLR is widely regarded as the gold standard for the treatment of ACL injury to restore knee joint function and prevent further tissue damage [32]. In a systematic review of 3744 patients, Xiao et al. found that 61.8% of athletes returned to sport (66.8% of male patients; 55.4% of female patients) [33].The RTS rate of elite athletes after ACLR is reported to be 63–97%, but these studies mainly focused on football or basketball players [8, 15, 26, 27]. Among the limited number of studies focusing on skiers, Erickson et al. [16] reported an 87% rate of RTS after ACLR in skiers participating in the winter X-Games, while snowboarders had a slightly lower rate of 70%. Guy et al. [18] found graft rupture rates were 34.0% in the isolated ACLR group and 6.5% in the ACLR with a lateral extra-articular procedure group for elite alpine skiers. In our study, we observed that 84.8% of elite skiers returned to participate in official competitions, while 66.7% resumed their engagement in elite-level competition, a rate comparable to that reported for elite athletes in other competitive sports. Skiing requires high-impact tolerance, complex proprioceptive control, and environmental adaptability (e.g., uneven terrain, variable snow conditions), which may present unique neuromuscular challenges compared to field sports like basketball or football. While team sports emphasize sprinting, jumping, and multidirectional agility, skiing demands full-body coordination under kinetic chain loading during high-velocity turns and landings.

Multiple factors are associated with RTS after ACLR, including preinjury activity level, the surgical technique used, reestablishment of knee laxity and patient physical functioning and psychological factors [14, 19, 24, 32, 33]. In our study, we identified those who resumed formal competition had a shorter delay between injury and surgery compared to those who did not. Opinions still differ on the effect of delayed surgery for ACLR [1, 13, 17, 28]. A systematic review [28] reported early ACLR was not associated with improved functional outcomes nor fewer complications, regardless of the follow-up duration. However, it is important to note that this particular study did not specifically focus on elite athletes. For professional skiers or snowboarders, we have observed that delayed surgery has a significant impact on the postoperative return to official competition following ACLR. On one hand, delayed surgery increases the risk of cartilage and meniscus injury [11, 31]. On the other hand, excessive waiting time for surgical intervention may lead athletes to develop psychological barriers hindering their RTS, such as diminished confidence or fear of re-injury [17, 33]. Previous studies have shown that the fear of re-injury and a lack of confidence are important factors affecting the level of RTS after ACLR [24, 32, 33]. More importantly, we found that the delay surgery might be related to graft rupture after ACLR, which is consistent with the findings reported in previous literature [1, 13, 17, 23]. Through a comparative study, Ji et al. [1] found surgical delay of more than 12 weeks from injury were significant risk factors for postoperative knee instability after ACLR. Based on these findings, early surgical intervention is recommended for ACL injury in elite skiers; however, further research is warranted to determine the optimal timing.

In contrast to recreational skiing, elite skiers requires extreme skiing skills, experience and fitness. The mechanism of ACL injury are markedly different from the mechanisms described among recreational skiers, and elite skiers are more prone to ACL injury [5–7]. ACL graft rupture is a career-threatening event for elite athletes. Limited data exist on the ACL graft rupture in professional elite skiers. The rate of graft rupture for skiers after ACLR was 4%−34% [16, 18, 20]. Previous studies on skiers have primarily focused on alpine skiers, whereas our study included 20 freestyle skiers (71.4%). Despite this difference, the rate of graft rupture we observed (18.1%) was consistent with previous findings.

For ACLR, Kartuset et al. [21] reported that patients who underwent a resection of minimum one-third of menisci had more pain, swelling and laxity than those with intact menisci. We observed a significant impact of concurrent partial meniscectomy surgery on graft rupture. However, meniscus repair had no significant effect on graft rupture. In addition, we observed a significantly higher Beighton score (6.5 vs.) in elite skiers who experienced graft rupture following ACLR. This discovery is similar to previous studies [4, 36]. Batty et al. [4] demonstrated the Beighton score as a predictor of a high-grade pivot shift. Ziegler et al. [36] found Beighton score was significantly different between primary and revision ACLR. These findings suggest that patients with a high Beighton score are more prone to graft rupture following ACLR.

Multiple studies [29, 30] have demonstrated a negative correlation between graft diameter and the failure rate of ACLR. For professional elite skiers, we observed the group with graft rupture had a higher proportion of graft diameters less than 8 mm. However, in the case of a limited source of autograft to reduce ACLR failure rate, the suitable diameter of implants still needs further research. Therefore, further research is required to determine the optimal diameter of implants in cases where autograft availability is limited, aiming to reduce the failure rate of ACLR [10]. Additionally, apart from single-bundle or double-bundle ACLR techniques, a lateral extra-articular procedure can be added to effectively decrease postoperative failure rates [9, 18]. This approach proves particularly beneficial for patients at a high risk of postoperative re-injury due to factors such as high Beighton score or ACL revision surgery [12].

The study has several limitations. Although all operations were performed by senior experts, there may be some slight differences in the surgical techniques used by each expert that could not be identified. The large time span (2004–2018) may have caused the evaluation to be insufficiently objective, but the number of elite skiers meeting the criteria for this study was relatively small; thus, we could not perform time-focused research. The surgical techniques were also being optimized in this period, which may have caused deviations in the results. Nevertheless, the findings of this study demonstrate that favorable outcomes can still be achieved in elite skiers following ACLR with hamstring tendons; ultimately, the long-term clinical results are deemed satisfactory given the heightened intensity of training and competition among these exceptional athletes.

Conclusion

The majority of professional elite skiers were capable of returning to sport after ACL injuries with ACLR utilizing hamstring tendon autografts. Moreover, individuals who resumed participation in formal competitions exhibited a shorter delay between the injury and the surgery, in contrast to those who did not.
