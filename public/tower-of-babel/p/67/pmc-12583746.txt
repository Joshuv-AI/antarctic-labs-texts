OSU-ERβ-12: a promising pre-clinical candidate selective estrogen receptor beta agonist
Authors: Gregory M Young, Timothy H Helms, Samuel K Kulp, Adeoluwa A Adeluola, Hanna S Radomska, Tyler A Wilson, Reena Shakya, Alyssa Kabat, Gillian Mulcahy, Abigail K Mayo, Raphael A Malbrue, William C Kisseberth, Chad E Bennett, Christopher C Coss
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstrogen receptor beta (ERβ) is a favorable therapeutic target for mediating inflammation, attenuating fibrosis, and treating cancer. However, selectively targeting ERβ over estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) has been a longstanding challenge. Recently, we developed OSU-ERβ-12, a novel carborane-based ERβ agonist that has a greater than 100-fold selectivity for ERβ over ERα. In this study, we compare the pharmacokinetics and functional activity of OSU-ERβ-12 against the clinical comparator ERβ agonist erteberel (LY500307) in multiple model systems. Pharmacokinetic profiling revealed OSU-ERβ-12 to have superior pharmacokinetics in pre-clinical models compared to LY500307 while maintaining a similar ERβ selectivity. Additionally, OSU-ERβ-12 displayed high human liver microsome stability and negligible CYP, hERG, and off-target interactions. Overall, OSU-ERβ-12 is a potent, selective, pharmacokinetically superior ERβ agonist that warrants additional study.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22258-x.Subject terms: Pharmaceutics, Pharmacology, Hepatitis, Liver fibrosis

Introduction

Estrogen signaling plays a critical role in numerous physiological processes beyond the well-recognized development of sexual characteristics1, including the regulation of metabolism2 and immune function3. Endogenous estrogens primarily regulate these systems by binding to estrogen receptors (ERs). The two predominant ERs are ERα and ERβ4 and belong to a large family of nuclear hormone receptors. When estrogen binds to these nuclear receptors, they can dimerize and bind directly to estrogen response elements (EREs) within the DNA to exert transcriptional changes. Alternatively, they may instead tether to transcription factors and promote binding to those factors’ response elements5. While ERα and ERβ have homologous DNA binding domains and structurally similar ligand binding domains, activation of these receptors exerts different, sometimes even antagonistic, effects5–7. This is thought to be due in part to differences in the N-terminal domain that influence which transcription factors associate with the ligand-bound receptor8. Moreover, ERα and ERβ have distinct tissue distributions throughout the body, further governing the systems each receptor can influence9.

ERα has been extensively studied and is primarily associated with reproductive tissue development10,11 as well as inducing tumor growth and metastasis in certain cancers12. ERα stimulation is also linked to the increased risk of pulmonary thromboembolism when given a high dose of exogenous estrogen13,14. Meanwhile, ERβ has emerged as a promising therapeutic target due to its involvement in regulating inflammation15, fibrosis16, and hindering cancer growth and invasion17,18. Therefore, selective ERβ agonists offer significant therapeutic potential with reduced risk for undesirable side effects typically associated with ERα activation.

Achieving selective agonism of ERβ has traditionally been challenging because of the high structural similarity of the ERα and ERβ ligand-binding domains. Erteberel (LY500307, Fig. 1A), is a clinically evaluated synthetic ERβ agonist which displayed a 32-fold functional selectivity for ERβ over ERα19. LY500307 was initially developed for the treatment of benign prostatic hypertrophy (BPH)20. Its clinical development transitioned to cognitive impairment associated with schizophrenia (NCT01874756, completed in 2017) and then ultimately perimenopause related depression (NCT03689543, completed in 2024), a female only patient population. Although LY500307 is a potent and selective ERβ agonist, its clinical development has not been successful. It remains unclear whether these failures are primarily due to lack of efficacy in the tested indications, as has been shown for BPH, or inadequate safety possibly secondary to insufficient selectivity for ERβ. Clinical pharmacokinetic data from LY500307’s BPH development reported low and highly variable oral bioavailability which could contribute to difficulty in clinical development21. Nevertheless, given the promising pre-clinical data using ERβ agonists as anti-cancer22,23 and anti-fibrotic therapeutics15,24, there remains interest in developing an ERβ agonist with improved pharmacokinetic properties for clinical use.

We recently developed a series of para-carboranes featuring a unique carbon-boron cluster linked to an alkyl side chain that reveal surprising ERβ selectivity25. We found that the addition and extension of the alkyl side chain reduced the potency of ER activation overall, but dramatically increased ERβ selectivity over ERα. From this series, we derived OSU-ERβ-12 which was found to have more than 100-fold selectivity for ERβ (Fig. 1A). Although carboranes represent an expanding area of synthetic medicinal chemistry26, very few reports of carborane pharmacology exist27.

Here, we present the results of detailed in vitro and in vivo studies that establish OSU-ERβ-12 as a promising ERβ agonist candidate when benchmarked against LY500307. Using competitive binding and transcriptional activation assays, we characterize its receptor selectivity, functional activity, and off-target effects. Additionally, we explore its pharmacokinetic properties across multiple species and assess its anti-fibrotic efficacy in a carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)-induced liver fibrosis model. The results highlight OSU-ERβ-12’s superior murine pharmacokinetics, predicted metabolic stability in human, and selective ERβ activity, supporting its further development as a potential therapeutic.

Results

In vitro Pharmacology

To directly test the binding affinity and selectivity of OSU-ERβ-12 compared to the clinically available LY500307 (Erteberel), we employed a cell-free radioligand competitive binding assay using full length recombinant human ERα and ERβ. We observed LY500307 exhibits 10-fold greater receptor binding affinity when compared to OSU-ERβ-12 with a Ki of 0.19 nM versus 2.02 nM, respectively (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, LY500307 had greater selectivity for hERβ over hERα binding relative to OSU-ERβ-12 with an hERβ/hERα affinity ratio of 6.3 versus 3.3 respectively. To further investigate the ER specificity of OSU-ERβ-12, an expanded series of assays were performed against 87 known drug safety targets. This serum free screen confirmed extensive hERα binding at 1µM as well as inhibition of cannabinoid type 2 receptor and activation of the 5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 2B (Fig S1A-B). Overall, these data indicate OSU-ERβ-12 binds potently and selectively to hERβ, albeit less so than LY500307, with limited potential for off-target binding.

We next sought to explore the functional selectivity of OSU-ERβ-12 compared to LY500307 in a transactivation assay. HEK-293 cells were transiently transfected with either human ERα or ERβ as well as an estrogen response element (ERE) driven luciferase reporter construct. OSU-ERβ-12 demonstrated greater than 100-fold selectivity for hERβ over hERα (78.3 nM versus > 10,000 nM, respectively, Fig. 1C). LY500307 exhibited a higher potency than OSU-ERβ-12 (EC50 of 3.2 nM versus 78.3 nM) and hERβ selectivity of about 270-fold. In a similar transiently transfected HEK-293 assay, cells were treated with an ERβ selective concentration of 1 µM OSU-ERβ-12 with or without the well-characterized ER antagonist fulvestrant. Near complete reversal of hERβ-mediated transactivation by fulvestrant provided confirmation that luciferase activity was the result of ER activation through the canonical 17β-estradiol binding pocket (Fig. 1D).

We also investigated the ability of OSU-ERβ-12 to drive transcription through modulation of other nuclear receptors including androgen (AR), progesterone (PGR), glucocorticoid (GR), mineralocorticoid (MR), liver X (LXRα/β), farnesoid X (FXR), pregnane X (PXR) and estrogen related receptors (ERRα, ERRβ, and ERRy, Table S1- S3). We found that OSU-ERβ-12 failed to activate any receptors up to concentrations of 10 µM (Table S1). Substantial inhibition was only found at the 10 µM concentration against AR (47%) and LXR α/β (50, 36%, respectively) (Table S3). These data show OSU-ERβ-12 is at least 130-fold more selective for ERβ than other closely related nuclear receptors. Taken together, OSU-ERβ-12 has similar ERβ selectivity to LY500307 with very limited off-target activity detectable from in vitro assays.

Determination of ERβ selective dose levels

To determine an ERβ-selective dose in mouse, mice were treated by oral gavage with escalating doses of OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307 and assessed for uterotrophic stimulation. Uterine hypertrophy is completely ascribable to ERα activation28 and is pronounced when estrogen-naive (i.e., pre-pubescent) mice are presented with exogenous pan ER agonists, such as 17β-estradiol (E2). We found that doses less than 30 mg/kg of OSU-ERβ-12 are devoid of overt ERα-mediated effects (Fig. 2A). LY500307 showed ERα activation effects only at the 100 mg/kg dose (Fig. 2B).

To further confirm that the doses we carried forward for our efficacy studies were ERβ selective, we utilized 8-week-old ERα and ERβ global, germline knockout male mice and WT littermate controls to test graded doses of OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307. The mass of the urogenital tract (prostate, coagulating gland, seminal vesicles, and urinary bladder, [UGT]) is highly androgen dependent such that the presence of exogenous estrogen will cause UGT atrophy due to the suppression of steroidogenesis through the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonad axis via ERα activation29. We found no significant change in UGT mass in the ERβ-KO or ERβ-WT mice when orally dosed with 10 mg/kg OSU-ERβ-12, confirming this dose to be devoid of overt ERα activity (Fig. 2C). By contrast, 100 mg/kg of OSU-ERβ-12 suppressed UGT masses, irrespective of ERβ genotype, consistent with its uterotrophic effects. Interestingly, while 5 mg/kg of LY500307 exerted no obvious uterotrophic effects, we observed a reduction in UGT mass following LY500307 treatment suggesting 5 mg/kg may have some ERα activation in male mice. To confirm effects on UGT mass were ERα-mediated, we repeated the study in ERα-KO mice and controls, which showed no ligand-mediated effects on UGT mass, reinforcing ERα to be the primary driver of UGT atrophy with these treatments (Fig. 2D).

In vivo pharmacology

To understand the pharmacokinetics associated with an ERβ-selective dose of OSU-ERβ-12, mice were administered 10 mg/kg OSU-ERβ-12 by oral gavage (PO), subcutaneous injection (SC) or intravenously (IV) in a 20% 2-hydroxypropyl-beta-cyclodextrin (HPBCD) formulation (Fig. 3A). OSU-ERβ-12 exhibited a relatively large systemic exposure of 73 µM*hr (AUClast) following a 10 mg/kg PO dose, which represented 54% oral bioavailability (Fig. 3B). OSU-ERβ-12 had a low systemic CL of 5 mL/hr following IV dose, representing roughly 5% of mouse liver blood flow30, with an elimination half-life of approximately 5.4 h.

We compared the murine pharmacokinetics of OSU-ERβ-12 to LY500307 in an established formulation19. (Fig. 3C). Compared to OSU-ERβ-12, LY500307 had a reduced elimination half-life of approximately 1.8 h and a notable increase in systemic clearance observed to be 100 mL/hr following an IV dose, representing nearly 97% of mouse liver blood flow (Fig. 3D). At a matching dose of 10 mg/kg PO, LY500307 exhibited a systemic exposure of 1.17 µM*hr (AUClast) which represented 14% oral bioavailability. The systemic exposure of LY500307 following a 10 mg/kg PO dose was approximately 60-fold lower than that achieved by OSU-ERβ-12. Notably, both OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307 readily penetrated brain, with brain to plasma ratios of approximately 0.3 at 6 h following a 10 mg/kg oral dose (Fig. 3E).

We further investigated OSU-ERβ-12 pharmacokinetics in research hounds (Fig S2A, B) and mini pigs (Fig S2C, S2D). Hounds weighing 30 to 35 kg and given an IV dose of OSU-ERβ-12 exhibited a prolonged elimination half-life of 42 h and a systemic CL of 350 ml/h/kg representing 18% of liver blood flow31. Mini-pigs weighing 10 to 18 kg receiving IV OSU-ERβ-12 exhibited an elimination half-life of 7.6 h and a systemic CL of 14.9 L/hr representing 48% of liver blood flow30. Compared to mice, we observed lower oral bioavailabilities in research hounds and minipigs of 16.5% and 10.8%, respectively.

To estimate hepatic clearance in humans, we employed a human liver microsome stability assay with OSU-ERβ-12 and three standard controls: testosterone, diclofenac, and propafenone (Fig. 3F). In human liver microsomes, we observed a half-life of > 145 min, suggesting OSU-ERβ-12 was stable under these assay conditions. Overall, this data suggests OSU-ERβ-12 is likely to have low systemic clearance in humans and may be suitable for once daily oral dosing.

OSU-ERβ-12 metabolism, safety profile and plasma protein binding

To understand how OSU-ERβ-12 is metabolized, OSU-ERβ-12 was incubated with mouse, canine, rat, monkey, and human hepatocytes for 120 min and the resulting metabolite milieu analyzed by LC-MS/MS. In primary mouse hepatocytes, 35% of parent compound remained and the primary metabolite (M1, 46%) could not be structurally resolved. The second most abundant metabolite was M12 (9.6%) which represented oxygenation and dehydrogenation on the alkyl tail combined with glucuronidation in an unresolved location (Table S4). In canine hepatocytes, only 5.6% of the parent compound remained with the most predominant metabolites M14 (36.1%) and M11 (34.6%) likely representing direct glucuronidation of both -OH groups. A matched experiment using primary human hepatocytes 23.6% of OSU-ERβ-12 remained with the primary metabolite (M10, 28.7%) representing oxygenation and glucuronidation in an unresolved location. In summary, we found the metabolite profiles were most similar between mouse and human while rat, dog, and monkey had distinct metabolites.

To better understand the potential drug-drug interaction risk of OSU-ERβ-12, we investigated CYP450 enzyme inhibition. Consistent with our prior reports using different assay systems25, OSU-ERβ-12 did not potently inhibit the metabolic capacity of any of the CYPs tested (Table S5). At concentrations beyond what is required for full ERβ activation, we found 3 CYP450 enzymes were inhibited: CYP3A4 (IC50: 18.6 µM), CYP2B6 (16.3 µM) and CYP2C19 (13.1 µM).

The OSU-ERβ-12 safety profile was further assessed by measuring its inhibitory effect on hERG potassium channels, a commonly used technique to measure potential cardiac toxicity. Chinese hamster ovary cells were transfected to express hERG potassium channels. OSU-ERβ-12 and an amitriptyline positive control were incubated with these cells for no less than 5 min, with three voltage phases applied (−80 mV, −50 mV and 20 mV) every 15 s. OSU-ERβ-12 saw no hERG inhibition at the highest concentration tested (30 µM) (Table S6).

An equilibrium dialysis assay was used to further understand OSU-ERβ-12 plasma protein binding in mice, rat, monkey and human plasma (Table S7). Two µM of OSU-ERβ-12 was incubated with plasma for 6 h. Unbound drug is able to freely move through a membrane and be quantified by LC-MS/MS. For OSU-ERβ-12, however, no measurable concentration of drug was found in the dialysate side (i.e., no drug diffused through). Therefore, in all species, OSU-ERβ-12 was calculated to have greater than 99.9% plasma protein binding under these conditions.

Anti-fibrotic efficacy of OSU-ERβ-12 in CCl4 mouse model

The efficacy of OSU-ERβ-12 was compared to LY500307 in an established CCl4-mediated liver fibrosis model32. Escalating doses of CCl4 were orally administered three times a week for 6 weeks (Fig. 4A). Starting at week 4, OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307 were given daily by oral gavage and E2 was given daily via subcutaneous injection. The CCl4 challenge ceased at week 6 and mice were kept on continuous treatment until they were sacrificed at week 8. In agreement with previous reports33, spontaneous partial resolution of hepatic fibrosis was observed in the vehicle-treated groups two weeks after cessation of CCl4 treatment (Fig S3). Nonetheless, both ERβ agonists were able to significantly reduce the levels of liver fibrosis as determined by picrosirius red staining of collagen fibers and subsequent quantification. Specifically, LY500307 treatment reduced staining by 17% whereas OSU-ERβ-12 reduced staining by 26% and 20% at 10 and 100 mg/kg dose levels, respectively. (Fig. 4B, C). By contrast, treatment with the pan-agonist, E2, did not reduce fibrotic burden.

To identify transcriptional changes potentially underlying the observed anti-fibrotic effects of ERβ agonism, we measured whole liver tissue gene expression using the NanoString nCounter platform in the OSU-ERβ-12 treatment groups (Fig. 4D). Using a fibrosis-focused microarray, we measured the differential gene expression of over 760 relevant transcripts and found the most prominent effect of OSU-ERβ-12 treatment was a broad downregulation of the TGFβ signaling pathway. TGFβ plays a significant role during the induction and progression of liver fibrosis34–36. TGFβ mRNA reduction induced by OSU-ERβ-12 was compounded by a decrease in its cognate receptor TGFβR1. Thrombospondin-2 (THBS2) is a soluble factor expressed in most fibroblasts including hepatic stellate cells. THBS2 upregulation has been shown to be critical in the generation of fibrosis in NASH37. Notch1, another member of the TGFβ superfamily, was also downregulated with OSU-ERβ-12 treatment. Notch1 signaling drives inflammation and fibrosis in the liver36. Reelin (Reln) is expressed largely in hepatic stellate cells and its inhibition has been shown to reduce fibrosis in a CCL4 model. TIMP2 expression is known to lead to fibrosis as it inhibits the metalloproteinases which spare collagen from degradation38. We also observed a trend of dose dependent reduction in several collagen genes following OSU-ERβ-12 treatment. Overall, these data suggest a potential mechanism by which OSU-ERβ-12 attenuates fibrotic development in the CCl4 model.

ERα Off-target effects following CCl4 study

To confirm the absence of ERα-mediated side effects associated with the estrogen regimens displaying anti-fibrotic effects, we evaluated several markers of ERα activation following sacrifice. Consistent with previous reports39,40, E2 reduced testis mass, but none of the ERβ agonists induced significant change (Fig. 5A). Estrogens can inhibit bile salt export pumps41 which can then lead to an accumulation of serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP). This effect is mediated through ERα and is an additional means to measure ERα activation outside of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonad axis. We found 10 mg/kg OSU-ERβ-12 did not significantly increase ALP levels, while E2, 5 mg/kg LY500307 and the non-selective 100 mg/kg dose of OSU-ERβ-12 significantly increased serum ALP (Fig. 5B). Moreover, a similar pattern of ERα activation was observed in body weight-normalized urogenital tract (UGT) weights, which showed that only the 10 mg/kg OSU-ERβ-12 spared UGT weight (Fig. 5C) while the other estrogen regimens caused significant reductions in UGT mass.

UGT mass is dependent on circulating androgens, whose homeostatic production is supported by serum levels of luteinizing hormone (LH). ERα activation reduces circulating LH and therefore represents a sensitive circulating biomarker for ERα activity39. We used 8-week-old, castrated-male mice to analyze changes in LH with increasing doses of OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307 administered for 28 days (Fig. 5D). As expected, E2 significantly reduced LH by around 75% compared to vehicle control. Additionally, all doses of OSU-ERβ-12 tested reduced LH with the greatest magnitude of suppression at 30 and 100 mg/kg dose levels. Similar to OSU-ERβ-12, LY50037 suppressed LH at 30 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg but had more mixed effects at 3 and 10 mg/kg. Unlike OSU-ERβ-12, which suppressed LH at all doses tested, the 1 mg/kg dose of LY500307 did not have significant effects on circulating LH.

Discussion

This report extends previously published descriptions of OSU-ERβ-12 by directly comparing it to LY50030725,27 arguably the most clinically advanced selective ERβ agonist to date. In doing so, we provide the first in-depth description of LY500307 murine pharmacology, including pharmacokinetics, uterotrophic stimulation and UGT suppression. Consistent with LY500307’s development as a clinical candidate ERβ agonist, it demonstrated ~ 6-fold selectivity in receptor binding, resulting in potent ERβ activity and > 250-fold functional selectivity over ERα in transcriptional activation (Fig. 1). Though OSU-ERβ-12 was less potent and selective in cell free receptor binding compared to LY500307, it still exhibited functional selectivity of > 100-fold for ERβ over ERα in transcriptional activation, albeit at ~ 20-fold reduced potency. Our characterization of OSU-ERβ-12 in vitro pharmacology extended to evaluating its agonist or antagonist behavior against other nuclear receptors using robust transcriptional assays distinct in methodology from our prior reports but in agreement in their results25. When taken together these data show OSU-ERβ-12 demonstrates 100-fold selectivity, or greater, for ERβ over other nuclear receptors and low risk for modulating other receptors known to impact drug safety according to our cell free CEREP screen (Fig S1, Tables S1-3).

LY500307 exhibited high systemic clearance following an IV dose and with low and variable oral bioavailability in mice (Fig. 3), consistent with similar limitations reported in other preclinical species43 and human21. Notably, our choice in oral vehicle for LY500307 of carboxymethyl cellulose and Tween 80 is the diluent for this agent reported by the original manufacturer Eli Lilly19, for its evaluation in a bioassay of ERβ selectivity, suggesting our data represent at least a partially optimized oral formulation. By contrast, OSU-ERβ-12 demonstrated very low systemic CL following an IV dose and high exposure following oral dosing in mice, resulting in more than 50-fold higher levels of circulating drug at matched 10 mg/kg oral doses when compared to LY500307. Though both agents were readily brain penetrant, OSU-ERβ-12 demonstrated greater variability in brain: plasma ratios at 6 h following a single 10 mg/kg oral dose (0.07 to 0.5, Fig. 3E). As this measurement was made at a single time point, it is unclear how the differential absorption kinetics of OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307 may have contributed to this snapshot of selective ERβ agonist brain levels. Follow-up, full time course studies are required to completely characterize OSU-ERβ-12 brain pharmacokinetics. Our interrogation of the in vitro stability of OSU-ERβ-12 using liver microsome and hepatocyte preparations (Fig. 3F, Table S4) revealed higher predicted systemic CL in canine, relative to mouse, that we confirmed with a pharmacokinetic study (Fig S2). Despite poorer canine pharmacokinetics, our in vitro drug metabolism studies showed OSU-ERβ-12 to have stability more consistent with mouse than dog in human hepatocytes and to be completely stable in human liver microsomes. Based on the mouse, pig and dog IV pharmacokinetics, we performed a rudimentary prediction of a first-in-human dose equivalent to the efficacious doses of 1 mg/kg44 to 10 mg/kg in mouse resulting in estimated doses of 333 to 3331 mg (Fig S4). Furthermore, using orthogonal assay systems to those we previously reported25, OSU-ERβ-12 again demonstrated low risk for drug-drug interactions mediated by CYP450s and no detectable hERG inhibition under our assay conditions (Table S5, S6).

Both OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307 demonstrated anti-fibrotic efficacy in male mice utilizing a well-characterized model of liver fibrosis whereas E2 did not (Fig. 4). These effects are consistent with prior reports of selective ERβ agonists demonstrating the ability to reduce both fibrotic24 and metabolic-based45–47 liver injury in animal models of disease. E2’s lack of effects are surprising given prior reports of concomitant E2 administration mitigating CCL4-mediated fibrosis48,49. A possible explanation is that when exogenous estrogen treatment is initiated in the presence of established fibrotic burden, as was the case in our design, ERα signaling counteracts therapeutic ERβ activation. Additional studies would be required to fully explore this possibility. Our limited analyses of fibrotic signaling pathways suggest OSU-ERβ-12 treatment can impact pro-fibrotic hepatic TGFβ signaling (Fig. 4D). ERβ modulation of TGFβ-mediated fibrosis has been previously reported in cardiac16 and intestinal fibroblasts50 as well as proximal tubular epithelial cells51. Determining the specific means by which OSU-ERβ−12 regulates hepatic TGFβ signaling will require additional in-depth study.

Notably, we demonstrated anti-fibrotic effects with each ERβ agonist at dose levels that did not elicit uterotrophic effects in female mice (10 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg for OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307, respectively, Fig. 2A-B). As this is a common means of determining ERα activation, these were considered ERβ selective doses. Closer examination of ERα sensitive tissues in male mice, namely UGT mass, revealed differential effects between OSU-ERβ-12 and LY500307. Across multiple studies, 5 mg/kg doses of LY500307 reduced UGT mass (Figs. 2C and 5C). By contrast, only 100 mg/kg doses of OSU-ERβ-12 exhibited significant effects on these endpoints whereas the antifibrotic 10 mg/kg dose of OSU-ERβ-12 did not. To better understand what might underlie this differential ERα sensitivity, we evaluated direct effects on serum LH by dose response in orchiectomized male mice (Fig. 5D). OSU-ERβ-12 demonstrated robust LH suppression at dose levels associated with ERα-mediated effects in both males and females (30 to 100 mg/kg) whereas LY500307 exhibited only minimal LH suppression at dose levels impacting ERα-sensitive tissues in males (3 to 10 mg/kg) with more robust suppression at dose levels impacting uterotrophism in female mice (30 to 100 mg/kg). These data suggest male mice may be more sensitive to off-target ERα activation associated with LY500307 as compared to OSU-ERβ-12 administration. It is possible that the large differences in mouse pharmacokinetics between LY500307 and OSU-ERβ-12 (Fig. 3) can explain the muted effects of LY500307 on serum LH in males. However, a reasonable alternative explanation is potential direct effects of LY500307 on male sex tissues such that reductions in UGT weight are not mediated exclusively through the HPG-axis. Consistent with our findings in mice, extensive rat reproductive toxicities studies for LY500307 report clear ERα-mediated endocrine effects at 3 mg/kg daily doses and higher in both males and females43. Additional extended dose response studies in mice are required to identify anti-fibrotic doses of LY500307 devoid of unwanted endocrine side effects.

Taken together, OSU-ERβ-12 represents a promising selective ERβ agonist preclinical candidate with a potentially improved endocrine safety profile in males when compared to LY500307. Consistent with the agreed upon best practices of major pharmaceutical companies52 and FDA53, we performed extensive surveys of other nuclear and safety receptors that support OSU-ERβ-12 as a highly selective ERβ agonist. Robust analyses of potential CYP450-mediated drug-drug interactions also showed low safety risk. OSU-ERβ-12 demonstrated improved murine pharmacokinetics when compared to LY500307 and predicted human metabolic stability consistent with once daily oral dosing. The improved pharmacokinetic profile of OSU-ERβ-12 may overcome the low and variable oral bioavailability reported for LY500307 which likely contributed to its unsuccessful clinical development for benign prostatic hypertrophy20,21. OSU-ERβ-12 has demonstrated efficacy in fibrotic liver disease, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis54,55, chronic heart failure56, neuroinflammation57, ER + breast cancer58, ovarian cancer59, prostate cancer60, and glioblastoma61 and improving response to immune checkpoint inhibitors22,23. Additional studies of this promising selective ERβ agonist preclinical candidate will be the subject of future reports.

Methods

Estrogen receptor beta agonist synthesis

OSU-ERβ-12 was synthesized by the Drug Discovery Shared Resource at The Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center as described previously25. LY500307 was also obtained from the Drug Development Institute at The Ohio State University.

ER radioligand binding assay

The radioligand binding study was conducted by Eurofins Panlabs Discovery Services (New Taipei City, Taiwan). Purified human ERα or ERβ was incubated with 0.5nM tritium-labelled estradiol in Tris-BSA buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 10% Glycerol, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% BSA) for 2 h at 25 °C. Depending on the treatment, varying concentrations of diethylstilbestrol, LY500307 or OSU-ERβ-12 was co-incubated with the labelled estradiol. The Ki was calculated as described previously62 using the observed IC50, the estradiol concentration, and the KD values of estradiol against the ER subtypes63.

CEREP safety receptor screen

OSU-ERβ-12 was tested by CEREP (Eurofins) at 30 µM, 10 µM, and 1 µM to determine its ability to inhibit or activate physiologically relevant enzymes and transporters. Compound binding was calculated as a percent inhibition of the binding of a radioactively labeled ligand specific for the target protein. Temperature and time of incubation was modulated based on the target to induce radioligand binding. Compound enzyme inhibition effect was calculated as a percent inhibition of the control enzyme activity. Inhibition greater than 50% shows a significant effect of target modulation by OSU-ERβ-12. Inhibition of 25% − 50% indicates a weak modulation and inhibition of less than 25% has no significant effect.

Human estrogen receptor transactivation assays

Human ERα (pcDNA3-hERα), ERβ (pcDNA3-hERβ), and luciferase reporter vectors (pGL4.26-3xERE) were provided by Dr. David Sedlák (CZ-OPENSCREEN; Prague, Czech Republic) and have been used for previous steroid compound screening64. HEK-293 cells (ATCC CRL-1573) were grown in DMEM/10% FBS to ~ 80% confluence and then medium was replaced by DMEM with charcoal stripped FBS. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 and transfection was conducted according to the manufacturer instructions. Following transfection, cells were trypsinized and dispensed into 96 well plates with either OSU-ERβ-12, LY500307, or estradiol at concentrations indicated. For the fulvestrant study, 10µM fulvestrant was added at the time of stimulation with estradiol or OSU-ERβ-12. Following 18 h of incubation, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using Luciferase Assay System.

NHR assay

Study performed by Indigo Biosciences (State College, Pennsylvania, USA). Reporter cells express either the native receptor or a receptor hybrid which contains Gal4 DNA Binding Domain and a firefly luciferase reporter gene. For agonist and inverse-agonist assays, Reporter Cells were dispensed into wells without treatment while antagonist assays were first supplemented with 2-fold EC80 concentration of the reference agonist. Reporter cells were incubated for 24 h in a cell culture incubator. Cells were subsequently lysed, and Luciferase Detection Reagent was added. After a 5-minute incubation, Relative Luminescence Units (RLU) values were quantified.

Animal studies

These animal studies are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Unless otherwise noted, the use of mice in this study was approved by and performed in accordance with the guidelines of The Ohio State University (OSU) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol#2015A00000079, approval dates: 01/2019–12/2027). Animals were housed in a barrier facility on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Wildtype C57BL/6N mice were supplied by Envigo (Indianapolis, IN) unless otherwise stated. ERα and ERβ knockout mice (#02617665 and #02617766, respectively) and corresponding wild-type littermates were obtained from in-house breeding colonies generated from breeders obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Prior to beginning studies, mice were randomized into treatment groups. Mice were provided ad libitum access to a purified, defined AIN-93G diet (Dyets, Inc., Bethlehem, PA). OSU-ERβ-12 was prepared in 5% DMSO and 5% Tween 80 in water, unless otherwise stated. LY500307 was formulated in 1% carboxymethyl cellulose and 0.25% Tween 80 in water. Estradiol formulations used 5% DMSO in corn oil. Mice received all compounds by oral gavage needle unless otherwise stated. Mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death.

Juvenile uterotrophic assays

For determining ERβ-selective doses of LY500307 or OSU-ERβ-12, 20-day-old estrogen-naive female (9.0 ± 0.8 g) mice were treated with escalating doses of either compound, 50 µg/kg estradiol by SC injection, or respective vehicle. Mice were administered the indicated compound once daily for 4 consecutive days and then sacrificed the day after the last dose. The uterus was removed, incised and blotted to remove intraluminal fluid, and then weighed. Weights are reported as a percentage of body mass.

Estrogen receptor beta selectivity urogenital tract assays

LY500307 or OSU-ERβ-12 was given to 8-week-old male mice (25.3 ± 1.8 g) with global genetic ablation of ERα or ERβ. Mice were treated for 28 days by oral gavage and then sacrificed. The urogenital tract (prostate, coagulating gland, seminal vesicles, and urinary bladder) was harvested, bladders incised and blotted to remove urine, and then weighed. Weights were reported as a percentage of body mass.

Luteinizing hormone suppression assay

Two weeks after orchiectomy at the supplier (Envigo), 8 to 9-week-old male C57BL/6 N mice (23.9 ± 1.2 g) were treated orally with OSU-ERβ-12, LY500307, or vehicle once daily for 7 consecutive days. Positive control mice were treated with 50 µg/kg estradiol once daily by SC injection. At sacrifice, serum was obtained from blood collected by cardiac puncture and then stored at −80 °C until measurement of LH concentrations (University of Virginia Center for Research in Reproduction, Ligand Assay and Analysis Core, Charlottesville, VA).

Pharmacokinetic studies

Mouse pharmacokinetic studies were carried out by Charles River Laboratories (Worcester, MA). Eight-week-old C57BL/6 male mice (25.1 ± 2.0 g) received a single dose of either OSU-ERβ-12 or LY500307 by the indicated routes of administration with three mice per route and treatment. Serial blood samples were taken for each mouse prior to drug administration and at 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h and 24 h after agent administration. To determine drug movement into the brain, mice were dosed with 10 mg/kg OSU-ERβ-12 or LY500307 and sacrificed after 6 h. The brain was carefully excised and subsequently homogenized. Drug concentrations in plasma and brain were determined by LC-MS/MS for OSU-ERβ-12 as previously reported27 and LY500307 as outlined below.

Dog pharmacokinetic studies were performed at OSU (Protocol#: 2015A00000114-R1, approval dates: 09/2018–09/2021). Three male hounds (Marshall Farms, North Rose, NY) weighing 30–35 kg were administered OSU-ERβ-12. Serial blood samples were collected prior to agent administration and at 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 168 h after administration. After the end of study and an appropriate washout period, the dogs were transferred to another protocol and/or used for unrelated pharmacokinetic studies.

Pig pharmacokinetic studies were performed by WuxiApptec (Jiangsu, China) in accordance with AAALAC International guidelines as reported in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, National Research Council (2011) and The People’s Republic of China, Ministry of Science & Technology, “Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals,” (2017). Six minipigs (Wujiang Tian Yu Biological Tech. Co., Ltd), four months of age between 10 and 18 kg were assigned to two groups and received a single dose of OSU-ERβ-12 at the indicated doses and routes. Blood was collected at 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 24 h and 48 h. At the end of study, pigs were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium and then euthanized by exsanguination. For each study, blood was centrifuged, and the resulting serum was collected. For both dog and pig, drug concentrations were measured using an established LC-MS/MS assay at Charles River Labs27 and WuxiApptec, respectively.

In each study, pharmacokinetic parameters for each animal were determined by a non-compartmental model using Pheonix WinNonlin (Certara, Princeton, NJ) with geometric mean values for the group reported.

LY500307 LC-MS/MS assay

LY500307 concentrations were determined by Charles River Labs using a level 2 research grade assay. Briefly, a Waters UPLC (Milford, MA Column: BEH C18, 1.7 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm) using 100% water as mobile phase A and 100% acetonitrile as Mobile Phase B was used in conjunction with SCIEX API-6500 + Triple Quad MS/MS system with electrospray ionization in negative mode (Danaher Corporation, Washinton DC). LY500307 281.1/175.0 m/z and Dexamethasone internal control 391.1/361.1 m/z. Assay range 0.1 to 5,000 ng/mL.

Microsome stability assay

This study was performed by Wuxi AppTec. Human liver microsomes (BD Biosciences) were incubated with 1 nM OSU-ERβ-12 or equivalent concentrations of control compounds testosterone, diclofenac, and propafenone. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 0, 10, 20, 30 and 60 min before stopping with cold acetonitrile. Samples were shaken for 10 min then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. Remaining concentrations were determined by LCMS. Half-life and clearance were determined by first order kinetics.

CYP Inhibition assay

This study was performed by Wuxi AppTec. Human liver microsomes were thawed and incubated with a NADPH cofactor solution. After, the liver microsomes were incubated with a CYP-specific substrate, and either no inhibitor, 3 µM of a known inhibitor or with increasing concentrations of OSU-ERβ-12 (0.1370 µM to 100 µM). Reactions were terminated by adding cold stop solution (200 ng/mL tolbutamide and labetalol in acetonitrile). Samples were then analyzed by LC-MS/MS to quantify metabolite formation and assess enzyme inhibition.

hERG inhibition assay

This study was performed by Wuxi AppTec. CHO cells stably expressing human hERG potassium channels (Aviva Biosciences) were used. Electrophysiological recordings were performed at room temperature using the QPatchHTX automated patch-clamp system (Sophion Bioscience). Cells were voltage-clamped at a holding potential of − 80 mV, and a predefined voltage protocol was applied to evoke tail currents following depolarization to + 20 mV. Amitriptyline and OSU-ERβ-12 were tested at six concentrations (0.12 to 30 µM), with at least two replicates per concentration. Data analysis was conducted using QPatch Assay Software. For each well, percent inhibition relative to the vehicle control was calculated based on peak current measurements. Dose–response curves and IC50 values were generated by fitting the data to the Hill equation.

Plasma binding assay

This study was performed by Wuxi AppTec. Mouse, rat, monkey, and human plasma matrices were mixed with OSU-ERβ-12. A semipermeable membrane was used to separate free and bound drug fractions by selectively allowing unbound drug molecules to pass through, while protein-bound drug was retained within the plasma compartment.

CCl4-induced fibrosis model

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6N mice (22.2 ± 1.3 g) were orally gavaged with carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) three times a week for six total weeks to induce liver fibrosis32. Starting at week 4, mice were treated with OSU-ERβ-12, LY500307, estradiol or respective vehicle once daily for 4 weeks. At week 6, CCl4 treatment was ceased and estrogen regimens continued to week 8 (n = 10/group initiated treatment). The two weeks of combined CCl4 and 100 mg/kg OSU-ERβ-12 were poorly tolerated resulting in the early removal of 3 mice in this group during the treatment period. One additional mouse in this group was removed due to inadvertent gavage injury. Fighting among group-housed mice across several groups occurred such that 1 animal in the LY500307 vehicle, 1 animal in the LY500307 treatment, and 1 animal in the OSU-ERβ-12 vehicle group, were prematurely removed from the study. A total of n = 6–10 animals per group were evaluated at completion of the study. At sacrifice, UGTs were removed and weighed, serum was obtained from whole blood collected by cardiac puncture and stored at −80 °C until used for analysis, and liver sections were fixed in 10% NBF and stained using Picrosirius Red stain. Images of sections were captured and red pixels were counted using Leica ImageScope software’s Positive Pixel package.

Nanostring

RNA was extracted from liver using TRIzol Reagent (ThermoFisher) isolation following the manufacturers protocol. RNA was submitted to The James Comprehensive Cancer Center’s Genomics Core. RNA integrity number met or exceeded the quality control standards. Fibrotic gene expression was evaluated using the nCounter Murine V2 Fibrosis Panel (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA). nSolver software was used to generate normalized reads. Limma software was used to identify genes with significant expression differences (significance defined by an FDR-corrected p-value threshold of 0.2).

OSU-ERβ-12 metabolites

This study was performed by Wuxi AppTec. Ten µM OSU-ERβ-12 was incubated with mouse, rat, dog, monkey and human hepatocytes at 37 °C for 120 min. The positive control 7-ethoxycoumarin (7-EC) at 30 µM was run simultaneously to determine Phase I and Phase II metabolism in hepatocytes. Following incubation, the structures of the metabolites were determined by LC-MS.
