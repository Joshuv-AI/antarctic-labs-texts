Rocahepevirus ratti: molecular evolution, zoonotic potential and public health impact
Authors: Antonio Rivero-Juarez, Reimar Johne, Siddharth Sridhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRocahepevirus ratti (ratHEV) is now recognised as an emerging zoonotic cause of hepatitis E, entrenched in urban rodent populations and already implicated in more than 50 confirmed human cases across Asia and Europe. Spillover occurs even in the absence of reported rodent contact, pointing to indirect environmental routes that remain poorly defined. Most infections are self-limiting, but acute hepatitis can be severe or fatal, and in immunocompromised patients ratHEV can persist and progress to chronic hepatitis. The absence of specific serological assays and the suboptimal performance of current molecular protocols, compounded by the high genetic diversity of the virus, limit the sensitivity of case detection. These challenges, together with limited awareness among health professionals, mean that the true magnitude of ratHEV globally remains unknown. This Review synthesises current knowledge on ratHEV ecology, host range, molecular virology and clinical impact, and outlines priorities for improving diagnostics, surveillance and prevention strategies.Subject terms: Viral epidemiology, Viral hepatitis

Introduction

Hepatitis E is a well-recognised cause of acute viral hepatitis worldwide1, accounting for an estimated 19.5 million cases annually, and contributing to 5.4% of the global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to acute hepatitis2. The clinical entity of hepatitis E was considered synonymous with infection by the hepatitis E virus (HEV)3, now classified as Paslahepevirus balayani within the Hepeviridae family4. HEV exhibits a dichotomous epidemiology5: large waterborne outbreaks occur in low- and middle-income countries6, caused exclusively by human-restricted genotypes 1 and 27, whereas sporadic zoonotic infections, linked to the consumption of undercooked pork or game meat carrying genotypes 3 or 48, are globally distributed, and infections by camelid-associated genotype 7 have been reported in specific settings in Middle Eastern countries9. Until recently, other members of Hepeviridae were long regarded as strictly host-restricted: Avihepevirus in poultry, Chirohepevirus in bats, and Rocahepevirus in rodents, shrews and mustelids, with no recognised role in human disease4. This long-standing assumption was fundamentally challenged in 2018, when a member of the Rocahepevirus genus was confirmed to infect humans.

Rocahepevirus ratti (rat hepatitis E virus, ratHEV) was first identified in wild Rattus norvegicus in Germany in 201010, revealing a genetically distinct lineage within the Hepeviridae family11. Initially regarded as a rodent-restricted virus with no relevance to human health12, it remained largely overlooked until the first confirmed human infection was reported in Hong Kong in a liver transplant recipient with chronic hepatitis of unknown origin13. Subsequent case report in Canada revealed that ratHEV infection is not confined to immunocompromised hosts, indicating that zoonotic spillover can also occur in immunocompetent individuals14. Since then, retrospective analyses and active surveillance have identified dozens of additional cases across Asia and Europe15–19, confirming that ratHEV is not a sporadic spillover but a zoonotic virus with established human transmission. This evidence reveals a new zoonotic threat, forcing a rethinking of the hepatitis E landscape and raising urgent questions about its true distribution, sources and public health impact.

This review synthesises current knowledge on ratHEV, highlighting the diagnostic blind spots, hidden epidemiology and unresolved transmission ecology that make it an underrecognized zoonotic threat. By integrating advances in molecular virology, animal ecology, clinical medicine and environmental surveillance, it provides a framework to guide clinicians, researchers and public health authorities in recognising, diagnosing and mitigating the public health impact of ratHEV. Addressing the knowledge gaps surrounding ratHEV is essential for accurate diagnosis and prevention, and a crucial step toward a comprehensive One Health framework for hepatitis E that explicitly integrates both classical HEV and the emerging ratHEV.

Taxonomy and virological features

Within the family Hepeviridae, subfamily Orthohepevirinae, the genus Rocahepevirus currently includes two recognised species4: Rocahepevirus ratti (ratHEV) and Rocahepevirus eothenomi, detected into voles. In addition, putative or unassigned Rocahepevirus-like viruses have been reported in Asian and South-American rodents, broadening the ecological niche of the genus20,21. RatHEV is organised in five genotypes (C1–C5): genotype C1 circulates in rats and shrews22, whereas C2 is restricted to mustelids (ferrets and minks)23,24, and three additional rodent-associated lineages (C3–C5) have been identified through environmental and wildlife surveillance25,26. RatHEV is a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus of approximately 6.6–7.0 kb, structurally similar to other Hepeviridae yet highly divergent from HEV4 (Fig. 1). In analogy with HEV, the ratHEV particle can occur in two forms: as a non-enveloped particle or as a quasi-enveloped particle containing a lipid membrane derived from the host cell27. Its genome contains three canonical open reading frames (ORFs): ORF1 encoding the non-structural polyprotein with methyltransferase, helicase and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase domains; ORF2 encoding the capsid protein; and ORF3 encoding a small multifunctional phosphoprotein involved in virion egress. Additionally, a putative fourth open reading frame (ORF4), overlapping ORF1, has been reported in some ratHEV strains11, but seems not consistently found across all rodent-associated hepeviruses20, suggesting lineage-specific retention or loss. Experimental mutagenesis of infectious cDNA clones indicates that ORF4 encodes a protein dispensable for viral replication in vitro but potentially modulating replication efficiency under certain conditions, hinting at a role in fine-tuning the viral life cycle28. Its distribution across ratHEV lineages remains poorly mapped.

Comparative genomic analyses show that ratHEV diverges substantially from HEV across all three canonical ORFs. In ORF1, amino acid identity is generally below 50%, with marked divergence in the hypervariable region and the N-terminal portion of the methyltransferase domain, suggesting potential differences in host–protein interactions and replication dynamics20. Unlike HEV, in which insertions and recombination events have been described, no such insertions have yet been identified in ratHEV, indicating a more stable evolutionary pattern29,30. Other domains of ORF1, such as the helicase and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, remain comparatively conserved29,30. ORF2, which encodes the capsid protein, is the most divergent, sharing less than 60 % identity with HEV, a level of divergence that underlies partial serological cross-reactivity and frequent diagnostic escape31. ORF3 also shows high sequence divergence (48–55% identity) yet retains conserved late-domain motifs such as PSAP32, indicating preservation of key functional roles despite extensive variation20. Comparative evolutionary analyses indicate that ratHEV represents an ancient lineage that diverged millions of years before the diversification of human and animal HEV20. This deep divergence, supported by recent phylogenomic reconstructions20–22, underscores the long-standing circulation of ratHEV in rodent reservoirs. The absence of recombination or major insertions to date may reflect ecological stability within commensal rat populations.

Phylogenetic analyses reveal that ratHEV genotype C1 is structured into two major clades (I and II) and six subtypes (a–f) (Fig. 2), reflecting long-standing circulation and local adaptation in rat populations22,33. Most genetic diversity described to date originates from Asia, where multiple subtypes have been defined, whereas subtype Ia predominates in Europe, the Americas and Africa. Clade I encompasses most European and North American strains, including all documented human cases from these regions, whereas Clade II is dominated by sequences from Hong Kong and southern China, with human infections clustering in the II.b lineage34. These subtype designations, however, are based on a still limited number of genomes from a few geographic regions, and a universally accepted subtyping framework for ratHEV remains to be established. Molecular clock estimates indicate that the II.b subcluster emerged several decades before the first recognised human case, suggesting that human exposure likely predated its formal recognition by years or even decades33. Codon usage analyses show that C1 strains, regardless of host origin, are highly compatible with the human translational machinery, supporting a genotype-wide zoonotic potential33. In contrast, the ORF2 capsid of human-derived strains exhibits signs of relaxed purifying selection31, a pattern consistent with early adaptive steps toward sustained human-to-human compatibility33. At the molecular level, this continuum translates into specific host–cell interactions that define its zoonotic potential. RatHEV genotype C1 can attach to and enter human hepatocyte-derived cells, relying on heparan sulfate proteoglycans for initial binding35, similar to HEV, with additional, as-yet undefined cofactors likely required for productive entry. Once internalised, the virus can complete its replication cycle and produce infectious particles, although replication efficiency is mostly lower than that of genotype 3 HEV36. By contrast, ratHEV genotype C2 and the divergent rodent lineages C3–C5 fail to attach to or replicate in human hepatocytes, indicating negligible zoonotic potential34. Experimental infection data further support this restriction, showing no productive infection in relevant in vivo mammalian models37. This molecular and evolutionary pattern helps to explain why only genotype C1 has acquired zoonotic capacity, whereas other lineages remain host-restricted.

Epidemiology

Animal and environmental epidemiology

RatHEV is entrenched in synanthropic rat populations worldwide, with detection in Norway rats (Rattus norvegicus), black rats (Rattus rattus) and other Rattus species across Europe, Asia, and North America (Fig. 3). Surveys report ratHEV-RNA prevalence in trapped rats typically ranging from 10–30%10–12,26,38–56, with detection rates varying with host species, geography and habitat. Prevalence tends to be higher in southern Europe and subtropical Asia than in northern temperate regions26,34,53, and in urban habitats than in rural areas26,38,52,54,57, likely reflecting the influence of climate, colony stability, food availability and contact networks on transmission dynamics. In some settings, Norway rats exhibit lower infection prevalence than other rat species40,53, although this pattern is not consistent across regions26,56. Age or sexual maturity has also been suggested as a risk factor50,57, although evidence is conflicting26,46,53. Phylogenetic analyses consistently demonstrate clustering of ratHEV strains by geographical region, from intra-city to continental scales33,38,58, supporting long-term circulation and continued viral evolution within local rat populations, as expected for a true reservoir. Detections of ratHEV in other rodent species are rare and almost invariably incidental, with most involving genotypes C3–C520–22,25,32,59. Occasional genotype C1 infection has been documented with sequences virtually identical to those from cohabiting Rattus populations39,45,53,60, indicating spillover infection rather than sustained transmission. Such events occur primarily in urban or peri-urban habitats where interspecific contact is frequent, whereas truly sylvatic or farm-associated rodents rarely carry the virus26,43,60.

Beyond rodents, occasional ratHEV genotype C1 infections have been reported in carnivores61, almost certainly reflecting incidental exposure or environmental contamination rather than true host status. More substantive evidence of exposure comes from serological surveys in free-roaming cats and dogs from both Asia and southern Europe62,63, which have detected ratHEV-specific antibodies, albeit without concurrent RNA detection, suggesting exposure without sustained infection. In contrast, domestic pigs are the only non-rodents mammal species in which active replication of ratHEV has been demonstrated. Viral RNA has been detected in faeces and blood from pigs raised on farms64,65, and experimental studies confirm replication in liver tissue and shedding66. These findings raise the possibility that pigs could act as transient amplifier hosts and contribute to indirect human exposure, although their role as a stable reservoir remains unproven. Extending beyond mammals, ratHEV genotype C1 has been detected in birds of prey67, and experimental infection studies in chickens have demonstrated avian susceptibility68. Infected birds developed viremia, faecal shedding and bird-to-bird transmission, although replication was limited, indicating a potential role as occasional spillover hosts that has yet to be substantiated in natural settings.

Faecal shedding is the main route of ratHEV excretion69, with urinary shedding occurring less frequently and at lower viral loads70. Experimental infections in non-human primates and pigs show that viral RNA appears in faeces early post-infection and persists for approximately two to three weeks66,69, creating a critical window for dissemination. Population-based studies indicate that nearly half of infected rats are actively shedding virus at any given time53, implying a substantial contribution to environmental viral load. Given the high prevalence of rats in urban sewer systems71, viral RNA has been detected in raw wastewater in all locations investigated to date72–82, including Africa where no rodents survey has been conducted81, typically with very high detection frequencies. Such dissemination bridges rat maintenance cycles and human cases, supporting the hypothesis that indirect contact with contaminated urban settings is the dominant transmission route15. Detection in treated effluents72, in downstream surface waters63,74, and in bivalve molluscs harvested from these areas highlights a plausible pathway for indirect transmission73. Despite these frequent detections, no experimental data are currently available on the environmental stability or half‑life of ratHEV in water, soils or on fomites, which represents a major gap for quantitative risk assessment of environmental transmission.

Human epidemiology

Serological studies demonstrate circulation of ratHEV in human populations in both Asia and Europe, with exposure documented in the general population18,56,83, immunocompromised individuals83,84 and vulnerable or occupationally exposed groups84–87. In Asia, seroprevalence estimates range from < 1% in urban settings to 22% in rural China18,88, with higher values consistently associated with older age, rural residence and reported rodent contact18,56,88, reflecting pronounced geographical heterogeneity. In Europe, elevated seroprevalence has been reported among forestry workers and people experiencing homelessness85,86, although direct comparisons with the general population are lacking, and potential risk factors have not been systematically evaluated. In parallel with serological evidence, confirmed human infections have been reported in multiple countries across three continents, with the highest numbers in China18,56,88 and Spain17,19,76,83,85, and additional sporadic cases in France89,90, Germany91, Canada14 and Thailand92. Cases have also been documented in children93,94. Although the total number of documented cases remains limited, the widespread circulation of ratHEV in rodent reservoirs worldwide, combined with the lack of routine diagnostic screening, suggests that additional human infections may be going undetected. Molecular detection studies have attributed up to 0.7%–3% of acute hepatitis cases of unknown origin in Asia to ratHEV15,16,18,56. In Europe, available data suggest a possible geographical gradient: studies from northern countries have so far reported negligible detection rates (0–0.08%)91,95,96, whereas investigations from southern regions have identified ratHEV RNA in 1.1–2.5% of cases17,19. These apparent differences are based on a small number of centres and should be interpreted cautiously, although they are consistent with patterns described in local rodent reservoirs and with potential variation in human exposure and environmental conditions.

Diagnosis

Molecular detection and sequencing

Diagnosis of ratHEV infection remains challenging owing to the absence of commercial assays and the limited validation of existing protocols. Detection of viral RNA in serum is currently the only reliable marker of active infection, as the kinetics of the human antibody response have not been characterised. Molecular testing is therefore essential in patients with acute or chronic hepatitis of unknown aetiology, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. Assays developed for HEV are unsuitable for ratHEV detection due to substantial sequence divergence, even when commercial kits target conserved hepevirus regions97. Among protocols specifically designed for ratHEV13,29,30,37,94–96, analytical sensitivity varies widely, with detection rates from 6% to 72% depending on the assay19. Because none of the available primer sets aligns completely with all ratHEV clusters, and given the need for broad genomic coverage32, current evidence supports the use of multiple qPCR protocols as the most reliable screening approach19. Sequencing is not required for clinical diagnosis, as genotype and subtype does not currently guide patient management, but remains critical for confirming zoonotic origin and elucidating transmission pathways. Most confirmed human cases include sequence data linking them to local rodent strains15,18,19,76,88,93. Sensitivity varies substantially between protocols, with Sanger-based approaches, although widely used, often yielding only partial genomes and their success depending on the predominant local cluster19. Although still applied in relatively few studies, high-throughput approaches such as next-generation sequencing (NGS) offer the potential to recover complete or near-complete genomes, particularly from samples with high viral loads. In clinical cases, complete or near-complete genomes have been recovered from serum in only about one quarter of PCR-positive samples, almost exclusively those with high viral RNA concentrations (Ct < 30)18,33,56. By contrast, in animal surveillance and experimental settings, high-load liver specimens yield full or near-full genomes in up to ~ 80% of PCR-positive cases20,31,34,54, although recovery drops sharply when RNA levels are low. This disparity largely reflects the analytical matrix, with clinical investigations relying on serum, which typically contains lower viral loads than the liver or faecal samples used in animal studies. To overcome these constraints, a tiled amplicon strategy with degenerate primers (varVAMP) has been developed, enhancing genome recovery from clinical samples across diverse ratHEV lineages and viral load ranges98. Metagenomic and target-enrichment strategies have proven effective in environmental monitoring72,80, and rodent surveillance34,54, but remain constrained by technical complexity, cost, and the absence of standardised protocols.

Serological evaluation

Antibody-based assays currently serve an epidemiological role only. Partial antigenic overlap between ratHEV and HEV leads to variable cross-reactivity depending on assay format and antigen source. Using HEV-specific assays, cross-reactivity has been reported in 20–40% of samples for IgM and in 10–70% for IgG31. Detection of ratHEV-specific IgM remains anecdotal, with only three cases reported, and all showed partial cross-reactivity with HEV antigens84. Consequently, IgG detection currently provides the most robust evidence of prior exposure. Several in-house methods have sought to improve specificity, including dot blot and Western blot assays targeting conserved ORF2 epitopes31,83–87, but these are technically demanding and unsuitable for large-scale use. ELISA-based formats are more scalable and have been widely used in seroprevalence studies, yet cross-reactivity persists. Virus-like particle (VLP)-based ELISAs show up to 27% reactivity in individuals previously exposed to HEV84. A more recent in-house sandwich ELISA targeting ratHEV-specific IgG via the p241 recombinant epitope has shown improved performance and has been validated in multiple Asian populations56,83, although residual cross-reactivity of 10–25% with HEV remains. To enhance discrimination, a differential antigen recognition enzyme immunoassay (DARE) has been developed, combining p241 reactivity with broader HEV antigen signals; in early validation, cross-reactivity was reduced to < 5%18, making DARE a promising serological tool.

Clinical management and prevention

The clinical course of ratHEV infection closely mirrors that of HEV (Fig. 4), ranging from subclinical infections to acute hepatitis and, in some cases, fulminant liver failure with a fatal outcome15–19,84,86. A complete list of all confirmed human ratHEV infections (n = 51) reported to date, including demographics, immune status, clinical presentation and outcome, is provided in Supplementary Table 1. In immunosuppressed patients, predominantly transplant recipients and people living with HIV, ratHEV can persist and lead to chronic hepatitis13,15,16,89–91. Such cases have been documented in both Asian and European settings, suggesting that viral lineage does not appear to constrain the risk of progression, although their true frequency remains uncertain due to limited case numbers and diagnostic under-ascertainment. In addition to hepatic disease, individual reports of glomerulonephritis and encephalitis in immunosuppressed patients with confirmed ratHEV infection raise the possibility of extrahepatic involvement15,16,19, mirroring the systemic complications described for HEV99. However, no systematic investigations have evaluated whether ratHEV contributes to neurological, renal or haematological syndromes, given that molecular screening to date has been limited to individuals with liver-related disease. Data on pregnancy are similarly scarce. In vitro experiments indicate that ratHEV replicates inefficiently in placental tissue compared with HEV, with limited cytopathic effect100. Consistently, only three ratHEV infections have been documented in pregnant women to date, and none developed clinically overt hepatitis18. These sparse but reassuring observations are insufficient to define obstetric risk, which remains an important gap for future surveillance and clinical studies.

Clinical manifestations of ratHEV infection.

The spectrum ranges from subclinical infection to acute hepatitis, fulminant hepatitis/liver failure and chronic hepatitis in the liver, with extrahepatic involvement including meningoencephalitis affecting the central nervous system and renal dysfunction with glomerulonephritis in the kidney.

Management is currently guided by clinical experience with HEV, as no ratHEV-specific guidelines exist. The European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) recommendations for hepatitis E therefore provide a pragmatic framework for clinical decision-making101. In immunocompetent individuals, infection is usually self-limiting with supportive care16, although severe hepatitis, including cases without underlying liver disease, has been reported14,16,19. In immunocompromised patients, particularly transplant recipients, reduction of immunosuppressive therapy may be attempted, but sustained viral clearance with this approach alone has not been documented13,15,16. Evidence regarding the efficacy, optimal dosing and duration of therapy for chronic ratHEV infection is extremely limited. Nevertheless, ribavirin is generally considered the mainstay of treatment, with several cases descriptions suggesting favourable outcomes88,90. Alternative antivirals show in vitro activity against ratHEV102,103, but have not been evaluated in human infection.

Although not developed for ratHEV, growing experimental evidence suggests that the licensed HEV genotype 1 vaccine (Hecolin) confers partial cross-protection. Monoclonal antibodies derived from B-cells of vaccine recipients recognise ratHEV antigens and neutralise ratHEV-like particles in vitro104, and in a rat challenge model, immunisation significantly reduced viral loads and attenuated hepatic lesions, although sterilising immunity was not achieved31. Consistently, experimental data in Mongolian gerbils showed that prior HEV infection does not prevent subsequent ratHEV infection, reinforcing that cross-immunity between HEV and ratHEV is incomplete105. However, there are no clinical efficacy data in humans. Pending a specific ratHEV vaccine, prevention should focus on reducing contact of humans to rodents and their excretions through hygiene, environmental sanitation and targeted rodent control15.

Conclusions

The 21st century has brought increasing recognition of HEV as a zoonotic pathogen, primarily associated with swine and related animal reservoirs. The identification of ratHEV marks a new chapter, highlighting zoonotic risk from a genetically divergent variant of HEV with completely differing animal reservoirs and transmission pathways (Box 1). Our understanding of its impact on human health remains nascent. Although current data suggest that ratHEV is an uncommon cause of hepatitis in developed regions, systematic surveillance has thus far been limited to Western Europe and parts of China. Expanding surveillance efforts to underrepresented regions, particularly the Global South, is a priority given the high seroprevalence reported in some settings. The inclusion of ratHEV in HEV international guidelines may facilitate increased diagnostic uptake and targeted research investment.

Rodents are the primary reservoir of ratHEV and are presumed to be the main source of human infection. However, the precise transmission routes remain poorly defined. Direct contact with rodents is rarely reported by infected individuals, suggesting that indirect exposure, via contaminated environments or food, may be more common. The high detection frequency of ratHEV RNA in urban wastewater, treated effluents and bivalve molluscs underscores the potential for environmental persistence to drive exposure. Evidence of infection in other species, including domestic pigs and experimentally infected birds, raises questions about possible secondary transmission pathways that warrant further study. While human-to-human transmission is well documented for other enterically transmitted viruses such as hepatitis A virus, there is currently no evidence that this applies to ratHEV. Nonetheless, the occurrence of infections in people living with HIV, men who have sex with men, and children indicates that the epidemiological spectrum is broader than initially appreciated. Blood-borne and transplant-related transmission cannot be ruled out.

A major obstacle to defining the true burden of disease is the absence of standardised, sensitive diagnostic tools. No commercial molecular assays or ratHEV-specific IgM kits are available, limiting acute-phase diagnosis. Current qPCR protocols vary widely in sensitivity and genomic coverage, necessitating the combined use of multiple assays. Expanding NGS protocols capable of recovering complete genomes, even from low-RNA clinical samples, would strengthen molecular epidemiology, improve cluster detection and enable monitoring of antiviral resistance. As more cases are identified, the clinical spectrum of ratHEV will become clearer. Reports of extrahepatic manifestations, including renal and neurological syndromes, highlight the need for systematic evaluation of outcomes beyond hepatic disease. Animal models are essential to explore pathogenesis and test therapeutics in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised settings. Importantly, ratHEV is more readily adapted to small animal models than HEV106, offering an accessible platform for preclinical evaluation of antivirals107. These models, together with improved in vitro systems, should also be leveraged in the development of broad-spectrum, pan-hepevirus vaccines capable of protecting against both HEV and ratHEV.

To address these gaps, international coordination will be essential to develop validated diagnostics, harmonise surveillance protocols and strengthen early detection of emerging zoonotic threats. Centres with experience in clinical diagnosis and molecular confirmation of ratHEV should lead efforts to develop reference materials for public health laboratories and academic institutions seeking to investigate local circulation. However, the challenge posed by ratHEV goes beyond technical preparedness. It exposes deeper structural gaps in our ability to identify and respond to pathogens at the interface of human, rodent and environmental health108. As highlighted by the Lancet One Health Commission109, One Health must evolve from a conceptual aspiration to an operational and institutionalised framework, underpinned by sustained investment and governance. RatHEV should serve as a sentinel example for the importance of such transdisciplinary integration, not only to contain this emerging pathogen, but to build resilience against future zoonotic threats.
