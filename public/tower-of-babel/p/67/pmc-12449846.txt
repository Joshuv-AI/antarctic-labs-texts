Caregivers' Experiences Taking Care of Children and Teenagers With Autism Spectrum Disorders During the COVID‐19 Pandemic in Thailand: A Qualitative Approach
Authors: Chalisa Rujilekpanya, Wilai Napa, Tusana Thaweekoon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDuring the COVID‐19 pandemic, children and teenagers with autism exhibited behavioral changes, which contributed to increased parenting stress. This study focused on caregivers' experiences in Thailand, as they often lacked information on how to address these issues, and it may serve as a valuable source of essential information for further interventions for parents.PurposeTo explore caregivers' experiences while caring for children and teenagers with autism.MethodologyThe researcher conducted a qualitative study to explore caregivers' experiences regarding caregiving for children and teenagers with autism during the COVID‐19 pandemic. A total of 15 participants, who were caregivers, were interviewed using a semi‐structured questionnaire until data saturation was achieved. The data set was transcribed verbatim by professionals and analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsThe participants' experiences were reflected in four themes: (a) facing difficulties with behavioral changes; the caregivers faced the behaviors changing such as decreasing connecting behaviors, children's self‐stimulating behaviors, losing control of their eating, and time spent watching their screens (b) caring adaptation: caregivers adapted their caregiving strategies to manage child‐developmental training and infection prevention; (c) confronting emotions: caregivers experienced anxiety and stress while caring for their children; and (d) assistance: caregivers received help from local volunteers who delivered food and medicine.ConclusionThe caregivers' experiences included dealing with changing behaviors, adapting to care, and facing negative emotions while caring for their loved ones. Health professionals should provide parents with information about managing changing behaviors and reducing parenting stress. Additionally, they should deliver information via alternative platforms, such as telemedicine phone calls, when needed.Keywords: caregivers, children and teenagers with autism, COVID‐19, qualitative research, Thailand

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that includes but is not limited to persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction, and restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (American Psychiatric Association 2022). Children and teenagers with autism present varying capabilities depending on their development. Some live independently and have a job or education, but some rely on their caregivers, especially their parents (World Health Organization 2023). The main consequences for the caregivers from providing care for their children and teenagers with autism are exhaustion, fatigue, and sleep deprivation (Department of Mental Health 2018). Likewise, parents of children with autism experience higher levels of stress compared to parents of children without autism, as the behaviors of children with autism contribute significantly to parental stress (DesChamps et al. 2020). In addition, Kunlaka (2018) found that parents' needs included assistance from medical professionals, empowerment, collaboration with school personnel, and skills training for caring for children and teenagers with autism.

The COVID‐19 pandemic began in 2019 and lasted 4 years until it was officially declared an endemic disease in 2022 (Ministry of Public Health 2022). During the pandemic, families with children and teenagers with autism found that their children presented behavioral changes, such as easily becoming angry, confused, and even sad, which disturbed their daily living routines and delayed their development (Parenteau et al. 2020; Tokatly Latzer et al. 2021). Such parents had difficulty teaching their loved ones to perform and comply with preventative behaviors, such as washing their hands, wearing masks, and keeping their distance; additionally, they subsequently experienced severe tension and stress, and the lockdown regulations impacted the parents' feelings of isolation (Lim et al. 2020). Parents showed increased anxiety, heightened stress levels, and decreased emotional well‐being compared to normal conditions (Alhuzimi 2021; Gangopadhyaya and Garrett 2020). Moreover, during the COVID‐19 outbreak, children with autism could not access treatment as usual, subsequently delaying the evaluation of developmental diagnoses and their usual appointments (Bellomo et al. 2020).

Research on parents' experiences with autism revealed that they had to deal with developmental delays and difficulties in behavior management, including feeding (Tokatly Latzer et al. 2021). In the Thai context, before the pandemic, the researchers revealed the parents' experiences with having children with autism, including feeling discriminated against because of having children with autism, facing negative emotions, and having to fully assist their children with autism (Chinchai et al. 2023). There is limited information on caregivers' experiences taking care of children and teenagers with autism during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Additionally, the caregivers' perspectives may provide beneficial information for further implementation to enhance their handling of their children's behaviors in the event of a future outbreak. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the experiences of parents caring for children and teenagers with autism during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Materials and Methods

This qualitative study explored the experiences of caregivers of children and teenagers with autism in the northern part of Thailand who brought their children to the outpatient clinic to follow up on their development. The data collection was performed from August 2023 to August 2024. During this period, the World Health Organization (WHO) and Thailand's public health authorities continued to monitor and report COVID‐19 transmission data.

Study Design

The researchers conducted a descriptive qualitative study (Turale 2020) to explore caregivers' experiences with children and teenagers with autism during the COVID‐19 pandemic. This approach enabled the researchers to reveal the caregivers' perspectives, fill gaps in mental health professionals' understanding of their experiences, and inform the development of specific targeted interventions as needed.

The participants were recruited from the outpatient department of a hospital in northern Thailand using purposive sampling and met the following criteria:

Data Collection

After the researcher received permission from the IRB board at the hospital, she asked the gatekeeper at the outpatient department to inform the target caregivers and request that they participate in the research. She then explained the study procedures to them and asked them to sign the consent form. Regarding the bracketing of ideas, the interviewer cleared their bias before conducting the interviews and listened to the participants' stories with active listening. The interviewer obtained the participants' permission to tape‐record the session and closely observed their nonverbal responses. The interview with each participant lasted approximately 45 min to 1 h and followed a semi‐structured interview format. The questions explored their perspectives on dealing with the pandemic outbreak. Examples of the interview guide are shown in Table 1. The interview questions were reviewed by three content experts, including a mental health and psychiatric nurse, a nurse lecturer, and a qualitative researcher, who approved the questions before the researcher conducted the interviews. The interviewer, who had previously worked as a mental health nurse, interviewed three sets of caregivers of children and teenagers with autism. She was trained by her advisor as a qualitative interviewer to refine the interview technique and structure. In total, 15 participants were available for individual interviews at the outpatient department of a hospital, conducted in a separate room to minimize noise and disturbances.

The interviewer started with a grand tour question to build rapport with the participants. Thereafter, she asked the participants specific questions to explore their perspectives on caring for children and teenagers with autism during the COVID‐19 pandemic and follow‐up questions to probe more deeply into their experiences, until she gained insight into the caregivers' experiences. She used open‐ended questions to allow participants to describe their experiences freely and observed their nonverbal responses without judgment or obstruction during the interviews. She also summarized the vital descriptions to the participants and asked them to correct any inaccuracies before ending the interview. While performing data collection, the researcher analyzed the data set from the caregivers' experiences prior to an interview with a new participant. The researcher used the same interview procedure for each participant until her analysis revealed no new concepts, indicating data saturation had been achieved.

Data Analysis

The researcher transcribed the participants' descriptions from the tape recordings verbatim after the interviews and ensured each transcript was correct. Thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2006) was conducted by reading and rereading each transcript until she understood the participants' descriptions before starting the analysis. The data set of each participant's descriptions was analyzed step by step, as shown in Table 2, and then the results were presented to the researcher's advisory team to ensure they accurately represented the participants' experiences.

Trustworthiness

The researcher followed Lincoln and Guba's (1985) methodological guidelines to enhance trustworthiness in qualitative research participation. To establish credibility, three participants were randomly selected to confirm the accuracy of the findings and whether or not they explained their experiences. The findings were presented at a peer debriefing, including the advisory committee. In addition, the researcher conducted an external audit of the research, performed by a qualitative researcher who evaluated the accuracy of the research process and findings to determine whether the findings accurately reflected the participants' descriptions and whether they were biased. Subsequently, the researcher presented all feedback to her research committee for review and approval. Additionally, each participant was interviewed with specific questions and follow‐ups to ensure detailed descriptions of their experiences caring for children and teenagers with autism were obtained.

Findings

This study included a total of 15 participants, aged between 24 and 61 years. Their relationships to the children and teenagers with autism were seven mothers, two fathers, one aunt, one uncle, three grandmothers, and one older sister. Ten participants were married, four were single, and one was divorced. The educational levels ranged from fourth grade (elementary school) to a bachelor's degree, and the duration of caregiving experience ranged from 6 to 15 years. The children and teenagers with autism in the study were between 8 and 15 years old, and all had been diagnosed with autism since they were between 2 and 5 years old. None of the children had severe physical comorbidities, and all were receiving regular developmental follow‐up care. While specific autism severity levels were not systematically assessed, based on caregiver reports during interviews, the children demonstrated varying levels of independence in daily activities and communication abilities. Most children were able to communicate their basic needs verbally, though with varying degrees of clarity and complexity. The caregivers described their experiences in caring during the COVID‐19 pandemic, while dealing with the difficulties, including adjusting their teaching to incorporate new protective skills, managing emotional distress, and seeking support. The researcher identified four main themes from the thematic analysis of the caregivers' experiences: facing behavioral changes, adapting to care, confronting emotions, and receiving local assistance, as described below:

Discussion

The COVID‐19 pandemic posed significant challenges for caregivers of children and teenagers with autism. These challenges became even more complex due to disruptions in daily life and limitations in accessing essential services, which directly impacted caregiving. Parents navigated these difficulties by managing changes in their children's behavior, adjusting caregiving approaches, coping with their emotions, and seeking crucial support during the outbreak.

In the first theme, having difficulties with behavioral changes, caregivers of children and teenagers with autism faced regression in development, including social withdrawal, restlessness, picky eating, and excessive screen time. Likewise, the pandemic caused disruptions in daily life, such as the loss of familiar routines and the sudden closure of schools, which affected the development and learning of children and teenagers with autism (Baweja et al. 2022). During the COVID‐19 outbreak, many children did not receive developmental training as usual, and school closures led to these children displaying signs of mental stress, resulting in behavioral issues, such as increased stereotypies, aggression, hypersensitivity, and changes in eating habits (Tokatly Latzer et al. 2021). Therefore, when these children and teenagers stayed home for an extended period, their development did not progress; instead, it regressed. This led to them exhibiting disruptive behaviors, as mentioned earlier.

In the second theme, caring adaptations, the COVID‐19 pandemic created a crisis that compelled people to adjust their lifestyles, resulting in a new normal. These parents have to assist with new daily routines for children and teenagers with autism, such as hygiene practices, handwashing, mask‐wearing, and maintaining social distancing (Lim et al. 2020). Therefore, the parents were also required to frequently repeat the instructions to their children and teenagers and closely monitor their hygiene behaviors rather than only taking responsibility for developmental issues. The numerous resulting challenges encouraged parents to learn and adopt new strategies for caring for children and teenagers with autism. These adaptive strategies align with Taiwanese research on autism families, which found parents employed similar problem‐focused coping approaches, including communication, support, and management strategies (Chin et al. 2023). However, the current study highlights explicitly how pandemic constraints intensified these processes, as parents simultaneously managed new protective behaviors, home‐based developmental training, and infection prevention. While some caregivers noted positive aspects such as enhanced teaching opportunities, these benefits were overshadowed by increased responsibilities. These findings may extend the views presented in previous studies.

In the third theme, confronting emotions, the COVID‐19 pandemic forced caregivers to take on a more significant role in caring for their children with autism. These emotional challenges are consistent with research on parenting stress in autism families. Chin et al. (2023) found that Taiwanese parents of children with autism experienced similar anxiety and stress, using both problem‐focused strategies (communication, support, management) and emotion‐focused strategies (acceptance, adaptation) to cope with parenting challenges. Notably, Taiwanese parents favored problem‐focused coping, which aligns with our participants' emphasis on adapting caregiving approaches and learning protective behaviors during the pandemic. These emotional difficulties appear to be part of a broader pattern that extends beyond pandemic‐related stress. Chao et al. (2018) found that Taiwanese parents experienced similar emotional challenges during the diagnostic process itself, moving through phases of uncertainty, anxiety, and stress before reaching acceptance and adjustment. This suggests that emotional vulnerability may begin early in the autism caregiving journey and can be intensified during crises like COVID‐19. Our findings extend this study by demonstrating how pandemic‐specific stressors—such as lockdowns, health concerns, and service disruptions—intensified the typical emotional challenges faced by autism caregivers. This aligns with findings by Parenteau et al. (2020), who reported that parents had to adapt significantly to provide routine care for their children at home, particularly during periods when schools were closed and their regular access to services was limited. Consequently, the parents mainly had greater levels of anxiety and stress, reflecting the challenges of caring for children with autism under the constraints of the pandemic. Additionally, Lim et al. (2020) stated that parents had to face the challenge of managing the changed behaviors of children with autism due to changes in daily routines and familiar environments, which directly impacted parents' emotions. This pattern of pandemic‐related stress among autism caregivers was further confirmed by Wang (2021), who found that parents of children with autism highlighted experiencing significant anxiety and stress during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

The fourth theme concerns the need for assistance among caregivers of children and teenagers with autism. The caregivers expressed that they had difficulty taking their loved ones to receive services, especially developmental training, as the city lockdown resulted in postponing or canceling appointments. Parents had to deal with stereotyped behaviors or train their loved ones in self‐care skills on their own, and they asked for more assistance to support them. Likewise, parents of children with autism faced difficulties in daily living training, which led them to have mental health problems. They needed to create a new normal by increasing their ability to use telehealth or remote services (Lee et al. 2021). The parents also reported lacking individualized services from specialists during the city lockdowns (Tokatly Latzer et al. 2021).

Conclusions

This study explored the experiences of caregivers of children and teenagers with autism during the COVID‐19 pandemic in Thailand. Four main themes emerged from the analysis: (a) behavioral changes in children, including social withdrawal and increased screen time; (b) caregiving adaptations to pandemic restrictions; (c) emotional challenges faced by caregivers; and (d) community support received during lockdowns. These highlight that the pandemic significantly impacted both children and teenagers with autism and their caregivers, leading to behavioral regression and increased caregiver stress. However, caregivers demonstrated resilience by adapting their approaches and utilizing available community support.

Implications for Practice

Healthcare professionals should provide targeted support for managing behavioral changes in children and teenagers with autism, for example, an education program to address these behavioral changes and help relieve the caregiver's stress. Hybrid care models combining telehealth and in‐person services should be developed to ensure continuity of care during future pandemic cycles.

Study Limitations

The data collection occurred during the unique circumstances of the COVID‐19 pandemic, which limited the transferability of the findings to normal conditions. An additional limitation of this study is the lack of detailed clinical information about the children's level of functioning, verbal abilities, and specific autism severity, which would have provided a better context for understanding the caregivers' experiences and the applicability of findings to different autism populations. However, healthcare providers should carefully interpret it when implementing it in different contexts. This study presents the caregivers' experiences during the COVID‐19 pandemic; however, it is now an endemic time, and consequently, healthcare providers may need to establish sustainable support systems that can withstand recurring disruptions while maintaining quality care for individuals with autism and their families.
