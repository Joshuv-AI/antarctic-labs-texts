Evaluation of soil quality frameworks in rice-wheat systems under integrated nutrient management in Indo-Gangetic plains
Authors: Tridiv Ghosh, Bappa Das, Debashis Chakraborty, Vinod Kumar Singh, Debarup Das, Pramila Aggarwal, Abhijit Sarkar, Prakash Kumar Jha, P. V. Vara Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sustaining soil quality under intensive land use systems to feed the growing population is a major challenge in the developing world. A prerequisite for effective soil quality analysis is the selection of a minimum data set (MDS) which effectively represents a given production system. As soil is a complex and dynamic system, there is no universal consensus on the ideal set of soil indicators for assessing soil quality. To address this challenge, this study evaluated multiple machine learning-based feature selection techniques in conjunction with conventional principal component analysis (PCA) for selection of the MDS, with the hypothesis that non-linear machine learning-based feature selection algorithms could better capture the complexity of soil quality dynamics. Weights for the selected indicators were computed using both PCA and entropy methods, aiming to develop a novel framework for soil quality analysis in relation to crop yield. Integrated nutrient management practices had a significant influence on soil physical, chemical and biological properties, all of which are essential for maintaining soil health and sustaining crop productivity. MDS selection and weight assignment through PCA provided strong correlations with crop yield (R2 = 0.91, p < 0.01 for rice; R2 = 0.93 p < 0.001 for wheat), comparable to the performance of MDS selection using PCA with entropy-based weighting (R2 = 0.88, p < 0.01 for rice; R2 = 0.90, p < 0.01, for wheat). When the goal is to maximize crop yields, the use of MDS selected through PCA and PCA-based weights for soil quality index (SQI) calculation appears to be more effective for a better representation of the production system. The proposed framework holds promise for application across diverse cropping systems and management practices, offering valuable insights for enhancing sustainable crop production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21630-1.

Introduction

Soil is an important natural resource, and its quality depends on the management of its physical, chemical, and biological properties which influence crop productivity and sustainability. Healthy soils boost higher crop productivity while maintaining environmental quality and sustainability, ultimately supporting the health of humans, animals, plants, and ecosystems. Understanding soil quality (SQ) is vital for identifying problem areas, assessing sustainable agricultural practices, and detecting early signs of adverse trends1,2. A reliable and accurate SQ evaluation system, which integrates various soil physical, chemical, and biological properties is an important decision-making tool. However, its effectiveness depends on the selection of right indicators and combining them into a single index tailored to specific soil function of interest3. However, there is no common consent on the set of indicators for SQ assessment as soil is a complex system. The key to efficient SQ assessment lies in the careful selection of representative soil characteristics that are crucial for ecological functions4–6.

The rice–wheat cropping system, covering approximately 13 million hectares (Mha) in the Indo-Gangetic Plain (IGP), accounts for about 10 Mha in the Indian segment alone. Although the Green Revolution initially led to substantial yield gains in this region, recent evidence suggests that these gains have reached a plateau or are in decline7. Farmers in the IGP generally apply greater than recommended rates of fertilizer N and P while other nutrients are often ignored8. A growing body of literature highlights the adverse effects of continuous and imbalanced fertilizer application on soil health within this intensive cropping system, contributing to the gradual degradation of soil quality across the IGP9. The application of inorganic sources in conjunction with organic, known as integrated nutrient management (INM), optimizes nutrient availability to plants while ensuring a safe and sustainable environment10,11. The INM practices not only sustain soil fertility and soil health12 but also increase agronomic efficiency13 and maximizing yields14,15. Several studies have found that INM practices have a significant impact on SQ. For example, Das et al. (2014)16 reported INM practice as a viable option for improving soil physicochemical and mechanical properties, therefore sustaining system productivity. Abid et al. (2020)17 showed that the addition of organic manure has a significant impact on preserving soil sustainability and quality under intensive nutrient exhaustive cropping systems. Thirty-one years of long-term INM practices improved soil physical properties, carbon sequestration, and available macro- and micro-nutrients in the rice–wheat system18.

Long-term experiments are critical for studying the impact of management practices on SQ and related indicators. Evaluating SQ based on a range of soil physical, chemical or biological properties can be challenging due to limitations in labour, time and money. However, this can be addressed by identifying a minimum data set (MDS) that captures key indicators efficiently. In addition to reducing time and cost, MDS also eliminates co-linearity and data redundancy3,19,20. A MDS minimises the requirement for numerous indicators, while still representing a full SQ data set21,22. Principal component analysis (PCA) is a popular and widely used technique to identify the MDS23,24. For example, Mishra et al. (2017)25 used PCA to assess the impact of shifting cultivation on SQ in Nagaland, using a weighted soil quality index (SQI). The SQI was calculated using an MDS selected by PCA, with parameters normalised by using linear scoring function. Similarly, Yu et al. (2018)26 developed SQI based on PCA to assess the impacts of various land uses on SQ in semi-arid, alkaline grassland of northeastern China, identifying 13 out of 22 properties as key indicators. Rezaee et al. (2020)27 used PCA for MDS selection and weight computation to evaluate the SQ of paddy fields using linear and non-linear scoring functions. In Northern Iran, Hemmati et al. (2022)28 conducted a similar evaluation of paddy fields. Ghorai et al. (2023)29 reported a stronger agreement between equivalent rice yield and expert opinion-based indexing compared to PCA, identifying the nonlinear scoring function as the most effective tool for soil quality assessment in the lower IGP. Similarly, Das et al. (2021)30 developed a unified SQI using PCA under conservation and conventional tillage practices in a rice–mustard rotation in the north-western IGP. Basak et al. (2022)7 assessed soil quality in a rice–wheat system using PCA and concluded that the conjunctive application of NPK fertilizers with organic manures was more effective in restoring overall soil quality than other nutrient management practices in IGP. A more recent study by Qian et al. (2023)31 extended PCA-based topsoil quality assessment in China, selecting an MDS composed of six indicators. Additionally, Nehrani et al. (2020)32 used discriminant analysis (DA) and PCA for MDS selection, while Liu et al. (2018)26 employed factor analysis (FA) and DA to select MDS for SQI development.

Studies have shown that both linear and nonlinear scoring functions yielded similar results when compared to artificial neural networks (ANN) estimated values33, and PCA with multiple correlation analysis is an effective statistical technique for selecting key SQ indicators34. PCA and FA are the most used methods for reducing variables in SQ assessments35,36. However, these methods often select only a few SQ parameters37, excluding critical soil properties38, leading to incomplete or inaccurate results34–36. Moreover, PCA assumes linear relationships between variables, which may limit its ability to capture nonlinear interactions39.

In contrast, machine learning-based feature selection offers a more flexible approach, capable of identifying important variables and capturing nonlinear relationships among them. These techniques can enhance computation efficiency, improve accuracy, and provide deeper insights into the data40. Several studies reported the use of feature selection techniques in different aspects, including mapping soil organic matter41,42, mapping soil particle-size fractions43, detecting mango ripening stages44, and assessing crop suitability45, developing a composite soil degradation assessment index46, and retrieving surface soil moisture47. Recently, feature selection algorithms have been used to predict the spatial variation of SQI48–50. Despite these advancements, there remains a gap in research specifically targeting feature selection for MDS selection in soil quality evaluation, highlighting an important area for further exploration.

Most of the previous studies used PCA to select the MDS and compute weights. However, alternate methods, such as Shannon’s entropy have also been proposed for determining weights51. Entropy quantifies the uncertainty of variables and assesses how controlling factors influence the output52. A comparative study by Wu et al. (2022)53 between PCA and entropy method revealed that neither approach could be universally accepted as superior for developing the indexing method in all contexts. Consequently, a clear research gap emerges, highlighting the absence of conclusive evidence on which method consistently yields the best results. Furthermore, the use of entropy-based approach in developing a SQI for soil quality assessment and its comparison with PCA methods, remain unexplored.

We hypothesize that selecting the MDS using various machine learning-based feature selection techniques will provide a more accurate representation of soil quality and the entropy-based weighting method will exhibit a stronger correlation with crop yield compared to the SQI derived from PCA-based weights. The current study aims to (1) select the MDS using multiple feature selection techniques and PCA, (2) compute weights by using both PCA and entropy method to evaluate SQ changes under different INM practices, and (3) evaluate the performance of these methods based on the agreement between SQI and crop yield. The results provide valuable insights into the comparative strengths of different feature selection and weighting methods, offering a more robust framework for soil quality assessment. Ultimately, this could help improving sustainable land management practices and optimise crop productivity through precise soil quality monitoring tools in the long run.

Materials and method

Climate and soils of the experimental site

The experiment was carried out on the research farm of the Indian Council of Agricultural Research-Indian Institute of Farming Systems Research, Modipuram (29.08°N, 77.69°E, and 237 m above sea level), Uttar Pradesh, India (Fig. 1). It has a semi-arid, subtropical climate with mean annual rainfall of 747 mm, most of which is received due to south-west monsoon during July–September. The hottest month is May, with minimum and maximum temperatures of 24.2 °C and 39.8 °C, respectively, whereas January is the coldest month, with corresponding temperatures of 7.8 °C and 20.1 °C, respectively. The soils are alluvium-derived and belong to Typic Ustochrept, characterised by sandy loam in texture, well-drained, and flat terrain (1% slope). Soil had a bulk density of 1.55 Mg m−3 with 42% (v/v) water holding capacity, pH of 7.9, EC of 0.4 dS m−1, and Walkley–Black C of 4.1 g kg−1. The Olsen phosphorus (P), available potassium (K), and sulphur (S) were found at 16.4, 96 and 14.5 kg ha−1, respectively.Fig. 1Location map of the study area (29.08°N, 77.69°E).

The FYM referred to the decomposed mixture of dung and urine of farm animals along with crop residues and left over feed or fodder. Treatment details.

For both rice and wheat, fertiliser application rates were 120, 26, and 33 kg ha−1 N, P2O5, and K2O, respectively as urea (46.4% N), diammonium phosphate (18% N, 20% P), and muriate of potash (49.8% K), respectively. In the S treatment, single super phosphate (7% P, 12% S) was applied as a source of P (40 kg ha−1). Zinc was applied to rice in INORG1 and INORG2 @ 5 kg ha−1, through zinc sulphate (21% Zn). During transplanting/sowing, one-third of N was applied along with full P2O5, K2O, S, and Zn, and the remaining N was top-dressed in two equal splits at maximum tillering and panicle/ear emergence. The details of the crop management practices have been described in Das et al. (2014)16. The 25% of the recommended dose applied through rice or wheat straw was calculated by assuming N content in straw was 0.5%54. Crop residues were incorporated 15–20 days before transplanting/sowing of the crops as per treatments.

Soil sampling

For soil analysis, ~ 1000 g bulk soil sample was taken in triplicate from each plot using a shovel from 0 to 7.5, and 7.5 to15 cm layers. Sampling was conducted following the harvest of the wheat crop, after 18 years of continuous application of the same fertilizers and manures to the same plots. Each sample was split into two portions: the first was refrigerated to determine the microbial biomass carbon (MBC) and mineral-N, and the second was processed for other soil properties (Table 2). Selection of minimum dataset (MDS)

A total of 34 SQ indicators were used, out of which the MDS was selected. In case of feature selection, eight treatment classes, namely CTRL, INORG1, INORG2, ORG1, ORG2, ORG3, DORG1 and DORG2 recoded as CTRL, INORG, ORG and DORG, were used as the dependent variable while SQ indicators were the independent variables. The details of the methodology for selecting the MDS are described in the following sections:

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA is a multivariate technique utilised to reduce dimensionality. For MDS, only highly weighted factors with absolute values within 10% of the highest factor loading inside each PC were selected. If more than one variable was retained under a single PC, multivariate correlation coefficients were utilised to check the redundancy and subsequent removal from the MDS56. From a set of indicators with a high degree of correlation (r > 0.70), only one was chosen for the MDS57.

Feature selection

The feature selection process involves choosing a subset of the original features based on predetermined criteria. It is a widely applied dimensionality reduction data mining process58. There are three categories of feature selection techniques, namely wrapper method, filter methods, and embedded methods41. The wrapper model technique (e.g., genetic algorithm, simulated annealing) uses the classification method to estimate the importance of features set. So, the selected features depend on the classifier model used. Filter-based techniques (e.g., consistency-based filter, stepwise linear discriminant analysis) are frequently employed as a pre-processing step and are independent of any learning algorithms, allowing them to assess classification accuracy using the chosen subset of data. Embedded methods (Boruta, RF) for selecting variables are based on machine learning models. Feature selection improves prediction performance, avoids data redundancy, reduces processing time, reduces cost, mitigates the effects of the “curse of dimensionality” and helps with data comprehension59.

Following are the twelve feature selection techniques used in the study.

Correlation-based feature selection (CFS)

The CFS is a filter-based algorithm that selects features using a heuristic (correlation-based) function. This is done to identify characteristics having a strong association with the target class. The individual predictive power of each feature, as well as the degree of interdependence between them, are taken into account by CFS when determining the subset of attributes60. The best feature subset is selected using CFS, typically combined with other search techniques such as forward selection, backward elimination, best-first search, bi-directional search, and genetic search.

Consistency-based filter

Consistency-based filter is a multivariate strategy that chooses subsets of features based on how consistent they are with the class. It utilises an inconsistency criterion to decide an appropriate data reduction rate61.

Best-first search

It is one of the heuristic algorithms which operates by choosing the created node with the lowest cost at each stage until the goal node is reached. It uses an evaluation function to distribute the score to each candidate node. There are two node lists. One list contains the nodes that will be explored (open nodes), while the other list contains the nodes that have already been visited (close nodes). The search can begin by starting a backward search with a full attribute set, a forward search with an empty attribute set, or a backward and forward search from any node. Open nodes are placed in the priority queue when they are near the goal. Instead of employing a small group of neighbour nodes, the best first algorithm explores unvisited nodes to select the best of all nodes. Even though the algorithm has reached a dead end, the search will continue. As a result, it is looking for the best node among the others62.

Greedy-search

A computationally effective method for locating the ideal feature subset is greedy search, which incorporates backward and forward elimination techniques. With the forward selection method, a set that is initially empty is gradually filled in with features based on some goodness measure. In contrast, backward elimination starts with the entire collection of features and gradually eliminates the less promising aspects from this set63.

Hill climbing search

Hill climbing is an optimisation strategy based on local search. It begins with the initial solution and progressively generates its neighbouring successors. The neighbouring state becomes the current state if it generates results that are superior to those of its predecessor. A feature subset is expanded using hill-climbing, which then directs attention to the subset with the highest accuracy. When no subset is better than the present subset, the hill-climbing stops. Hill climbing is a straightforward method, yet it is a crucial building component for many complex search methods, including simulated annealing, tabu search, beam search, and to some extent genetic algorithm64.

Stepwise linear discriminant analysis (SWLDA)

The SWLDA is a reliable feature selection method for choosing the standout features. It is simple to understand, has strong predictability, and is more computationally efficient65. SWLDA employs two methods, namely parallel forward and backward algorithms, to choose the best features. The backward procedure is used to eliminate unnecessary features (i.e., those with a p-value > 0.25) after the new features have been added using the forward technique66.

Stepwise quadratic discriminant analysis (SWQDA)

The SWQDA is closely related to SWLDA and is used to classify observations into two or more classes based on a set of predictor variables. It is assumed that predictors are normally distributed within each class and that covariance matrices differ between classes. In comparison to Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA), the Quadratic Discriminant Analysis (QDA) does not require the mean and covariance of each class to be identical.

Classification and regression tree (CART)

CART is a nonparametric statistical approach67 utilised to select the optimal subset of variables for categorical dependent variable from a large continuous dataset of independent variables68,69. The CART method assumes that the decision tree is a binary tree. The CART model can be used for both classification and regression problems. In regression problems, a continuous dependent variable is predicted based on a set of continuous and/or categorical predictors while for classification problems, it is used to predict a categorical variable. In the present study, CART was used for classification purposes.

Random forest

The collection of unpruned classification or regression trees makes up a random forest (RF). Each tree is built using a random extraction of the observations from the dataset with randomly selected features70. According to its capacity to raise the level of leaf purity, each tree in the random forest can estimate the importance of a feature. The first ten important features were selected for SQ analysis.

Boruta algorithm

The Boruta algorithm is a feature selection technique that was developed as a wrapper around the RF algorithm. The features are selected by comparing the importance of the original feature to the importance that is obtained randomly. The strategy can be broken down into (i) duplicating each independent variable and rearranging added features to remove any correlations with the output variable (shadow features are synthetic attributes that are generated by randomly rearranging the values of the actual features. These shadow features function as a standard against which the significance of the actual features is assessed, (ii) using the enlarged dataset to train the RF model and calculate the Z score, and (iii) finding variables with Z scores greater than the maximum Z score (MZSA) among shadow features47,71.

Genetic algorithm

The most popular evolutionary algorithmic technique is called genetic algorithm (GA). It enables us to identify potential optimisation solutions in a reasonable amount of time, particularly when the search space is very wide72. GA mimics the Darwinian process of natural selection through search73. This algorithm creates a new generation that gradually grows to be included in or get close to the ideal solution through a series of genetic operations, including selection and crossover, which are mutations. The main objective of this procedure is to recombine the two candidate subsets to obtain a new subset which is better than the previous one. The subset with the best fitness is selected in the assessment evolution step, where the fitness function is used to evaluate the quality of the solutions. The details of the methodology are available in Katoch et al. (2021)74. In this study, the GA procedure was carried out with ten-fold cross-validation and hundred iterations. The three key parameters for GA, namely population size, crossover rate, and mutation rate, were set to 50, 0.8, and 0.1, respectively75.

Simulated annealing

Simulated annealing is an optimisation technique that draws inspiration from the metallurgical annealing process by combining probabilistic theory and statistical mechanics. This approach makes finding the global optimum of a given function utilised in feature selection easier76. If the search space is discrete, the simulated annealing algorithm is typically utilised. It is an optimisation algorithm that iteratively compares the results of the objective functions running with the current and adjacent points in the domain; if the latter produces a better result than the former, the base solution for the following iteration is saved. Otherwise, the algorithm terminates the procedure without searching for better results in a larger domain77.

Two sets of MDS were developed, one following PCA and the other following the feature selection. All 12 methods (section "Selection of minimum dataset (MDS)") were employed for feature selection to identify the most important variables to include in the MDS. The frequency of indicator selection by various feature selection techniques was computed. Features with more than four cumulative frequency of selection were identified for developing a soil quality index (SQI). Calculation of weights

The weights (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{W}}_{\text{i}}$$\end{document}) for indicator i in the MDS were calculated based on PCA and entropy.

PCA based weights

For each observation, the PCA results were utilised to weigh the indicators. Each principal component (PC) explained a proportion (%) of the variance in the data set. The weighted factors for indicator variables chosen under a PC was calculated as the ratio of the percentage variance explained by the PC to the total percentage of variance explained by all PCs with eigenvalues greater than one78. The weight was taken from the PC that held the selected feature derived from multiple feature selection techniques.

We generated six SQI for six combinations of methods: (1) all the indicators with weights calculated by using PCA (All-PCA), (2) selection of MDS (MDS1) and computation of weights by using PCA (PCA-PCA), (3) selection of MDS through feature selection, FS (MDS2) and weights computed by using PCA (FS-PCA), (4) all the indicators and weights computed through entropy method (All-Entropy), (5) selection of MDS selection through FS and weights through entropy method (FS-Entropy), and (6) selection of MDS through PCA and weights through entropy method (PCA-Entropy). Statistical analysis

Mean separation with Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) for SQI among the INM treatments under a specific PCA and feature selection combination was performed in R package “agricolae”79. The correlation matrix of PCA and feature selection selected soil quality indicators were performed by “metan” package of R80. The CFS, consistency-based filter, best-first search, greedy-search, hill climbing search were executed using “FSelector” package81 while SWLDA, SWLDA, CART, RF, genetic algorithm and simulated annealing-based feature selection was performed using “caret” package75. Boruta was implemented using “Boruta” package71 in R software version 4.3.182. The polynomial relationships between crop yield and SQI were established by “tidyverse”83 package in R.

Results

Descriptive statistics of soil characteristics and yield

The descriptive statistics of the soil parameters are presented in Supplementary Table 1. The results suggested that the TOC was ranging between 1.53 and 0.98%. The mean and SD of WBC were 0.49% and 0.09%, respectively. The PmOC varied between 0.95 and 0.47%, and the MBC had a mean of 213.6 g kg−1, ranging between 317.0 and 126.5 g kg−1. The mean value of very labile carbon (VLC), labile carbon (LC), less labile carbon (LLC) and non-labile carbon (NLC) was 1.57, 2.47, 0.87, and 7.86 g kg−1, respectively, however, only LC was negatively skewed. The mean NH4+ and NO3− concentrations were 23.5 and 30.1 mg kg−1. The mean values of P, S, K, Fe, Mn, Zn, and Cu were 9.9, 8.2, 121.9, 18.0, 9.2, 1.5, and 1.6 mg kg−1, respectively and out of these P and Cu were negatively skewed. Out of all physical parameters, the maximum standard error and range were observed in silt + clay aggregate (SCA) (1.4 and 21.4 g 100 g−1 soil). The mean value of dry and Yoder MWD was 5.4 and 1.4 mm, respectively. The mean FC and PWP moisture content for both 5–8 mm and 2–5 mm size aggregates were 0.21% and 0.09%, respectively. The saturation water content varied from 0.41 to 0.48%, with a mean and median of 0.44%. So, there was sufficient variability within all variables to perform multiple machine learning algorithms for feature selection.

PCA-based minimum dataset selection (MDS1)

The details of the PCA results for indicator selection are given in Table 3, according to the criteria established by Brejda et al. (2000)84, the PC with eigenvalue 1 which explained at least 5% of the variation in the data85 were considered in the current study. The variables with the highest factor loading under each selected PC were deemed to be the most representative of system attributes. PC1, PC2, PC3, PC4, PC5, and PC6 explained 26.5, 21.2, 16.1, 9, 7.6, and 6.5% of the variance, respectively. The cumulative variance explained by all these PCs was 86.89%. The highly weighted factors (absolute values within 10% of the highest factor loading) were retained for MDS within each PC. When more than one factor was retained under a single PC, multivariate correlation coefficients were used to determine whether the variables were redundant and thus removed from the MDS56. Only one variable was chosen for the MDS from the group of variables that were highly correlated. However, depending on the importance of the situation, flexibility criteria were also applied in rare cases. In this study, six SQ indicators were chosen in PC1: WBC, PmOC, MBC, VLC, LC, and NH4 (Table 3). The correlation matrix, on the other hand, showed that WBC, PmOC, VLC, and LC were highly correlated (Fig. 3). Hence, WBC was only chosen as it was easy to measure. In PC2, five SQ indicators were selected, namely dry MWD, aggregate density, TS, Yoder MWD, and LM, but because they were significantly correlated (r > 0.80), only Yoder MWD was chosen. In PC3, both non-labile carbon (NLC) and K were chosen, despite strong correlated (r = 0.84) (Fig. 3), because of their distinctly different properties. So, WBC, MBC, NH4, Yoder MWD, NLC, K, MiC, SM, Cu were retained from PC1 to PC6 as the final MDS1.

Selecting MDS using feature selection algorithms (MDS2)

Different feature selection techniques were also used to select the SQ indicators. Fe content was selected by almost all the feature selection techniques (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Labile carbon (LC) was selected by consistency-based filters (CFS), hill-climbing search, stepwise LDA, Boruta, RF, GA, and CART. CFS only selected PmOC, PWP (2–5 mm) and labile carbon (LC). In addition to the stepwise LDA, Boruta, RF, and GA also selected saturation water content. The best first search selected only Fe and MBC. In association with the best first search, MBC was also selected by hill-climbing search, Boruta, RF, CART and simulated annealing. Fe, NLC, Cu, LM, and SCA were selected by greedy search. Stepwise LDA selected three SQ indicators viz. LC, saturation water, and dry MWD, whereas stepwise QDA only gave importance to Fe. The GA selected almost all indicators except MBC, P, SM, M and SCA. The hill climbing and GA both selected small macro aggregate carbon (Supplementary Table 2).

The cumulative frequency of SQ indicator selection by various feature selection techniques varied between 1 and 7 (Fig. 4, Supplementary Table 2). However, we have fixed a threshold value of 4 to select the SQ indicators to develop SQI. Accordingly, Fe, LC, MBC, K, PWP (2–5 mm), TOC, PmOC, NLC, WBC, NO3, Mn, Cu, saturation water, and silt + clay carbon (SCC) were selected. The Pearson correlation matrix showed that there was a significant correlation among soil organic carbon-related parameters namely WBC, LC, PmOC, NLC, SCC and TOC (Fig. 3). Consequently, only WBC was chosen to develop the SQI because it is relatively straightforward to measure. Furthermore, Fe and Mn concentrations had significantly positive correlation. As Fe had a higher frequency of selection through feature selection algorithms, Fe was considered as the MDS. Finally, Fe, MBC, K, PWP (2–5 mm), WBC, NO3, saturation water, and Cu were chosen as MDS2 for SQI development. The weight was taken from that specific PC in which the selected indicator had maximum value during PCA (FS-PCA). Additionally, the entropy method was also used to compute the weights for the development of the SQI (FS-Entropy).Fig. 4Frequency of soil quality indicators selection through different feature selection techniques.

Discussion

In constructing a soil quality index, the first step is selecting the minimum dataset. Our study utilised two techniques for MDS selection: PCA and feature selection. Among a set of highly correlated variables, only one was retained for the MDS, although flexible criteria were applied in certain situations. For example, despite the correlation between MBC and WBC, we retained MBC as a key biological soil quality indicator57. MBC assesses soil microbial abundance and activity, which significantly increases with the application of organic amendments, either alone or in combination with inorganic inputs. This enhances microbial growth, C sequestration and overall soil quality86,87. Both methodologies selected soil organic carbon-related parameters, highlighting their critical role in soil quality assessment. Soil organic C is a key indicator of soil health88, influencing essential functional processes such as nutrient cycling, aggregate stability89 and increases microbial activity. Organic amendments have been shown to improve soil C sequestration90–92 which is consistent with our findings. Numerous studies further emphasise the significance of organic C and its fractions in soil quality assessment, including work by Raiesi (2017)20, Nabiollahi et al. (2018)93, Nehrani et al. (2020)32, Yu et al. (2018)26, and Samaei et al. (2022)94.

The dry mean weight diameter, aggregate density, tensile strength of aggregates, Yoder MWD, and large macroaggregates were all strongly correlated (r > 0.80). As a result, only Yoder MWD was chosen because of its ease of measurement and wide availability. The MWD increases with higher weight proportions of large macroaggregates (R > 250 m) indicating that the two variables are highly correlated95. The observed strong and positive correlation among dry MWD, aggregate density, aggregate tensile strength may be attributed to reduced aggregate porosity associated with lower organic matter content, resulting in increased contact points between soil particles, making them less friable16. Soil aggregation or MWD is a key indicator of soil quality, influenced by various soil factors96,97. Higher MWD values are associated with better aggregate stability, which enhances soil resistance to erosion, and supports improved root growth and increased microbial activity98. Our findings support the importance of MWD as an important measure for evaluating soil quality, consistent with studies by Gu et al. (2019)99 and Zhou et al. (2020)96.

Nitrogen is an essential nutrient that plays a key role in the growth and productivity of plants. Its availability is influenced by fertilisation and management practices, making it a key indicator of soil quality100. In this study, ammonium (NH4) and nitrate (NO3) were selected for minimum datasets 1 (MDS1) and 2 (MDS2), respectively, emphasizing the significance of N in soil quality assessment. This aligns with the findings reported by Rezaee et al. (2020)27, who highlighted the importance of total N, and Yu et al. (2018)101, who focused on available N. Copper (Cu) is an essential micronutrient, is critical for plant disease resistance and the regulation of key physiological processes, including photosynthesis, respiration, and enzyme activation102. Its inclusion in both MDS is therefore justified. Similar findings by Qian et al. (2023)31, highlighted role of copper in enhancing plant health and soil quality.

The soil quality index quantifies soil quality and sustainability as influenced by management practices4. This study found that the average SQI in organic plots was higher than in inorganic plots (Table 4), implying that INM practices may have a positive impact on soil quality, potentially enhancing soil health and productivity. The increase in organic matter content, improved soil structure, and increased microbial activity associated with INM methods may all contribute to higher SQI values observed in organic plots. Our findings support the idea that chemical fertilisation combined with organic inputs could be a sustainable option for improving SQ103–105. The study also identified varying impacts of different organic amendments on soil quality. Specifically, the application of FYM produced more significant benefits compared to other organic amendments such as crop residues, green manure, or sulphitation pressmud (SPM) (Fig. 5). Both ORG1 and DORG1 showed significantly higher SQI values and crop yields compared to other organic amendments. This research highlights the greater efficacy of FYM in improving soil quality and increasing crop productivity. Rice and wheat crop residues with their wider C:N ratio as compared to FYM, SPM or green gram residues decompose more slowly and release nutrients at a lower rate, resulting in less pronounced improvements in soil quality55. Furthermore, the addition of sulphur (ORG2) significantly increased the grain yield of rice and positively impacted subsequent wheat crop (Fig. 5). However, it was less effective in enhancing soil C content, indicating a decrease in SQI (Das et al., 2014). Since there is no well-defined method for directly validating the SQI, this study established a relationship between SQI and crop yield as a proxy for assessing efficacy of soil quality assessment techniques (Table 5). The results showed a strong correlation between rice and wheat yields and SQIs derived from various techniques, with the exception of FS-Entropy. Our findings align with Liu et al. (2017)101, who found a significant relationship between SQI and rice yield under a variety of agricultural management practices, and with Li et al. (2019)106, who also reported a significant relationship between SQI and wheat yield in a wheat–maize cropping system in China. Moreover, the strength of the relationship between SQI and rice or wheat yield was the strongest when the MDS selected through PCA were integrated with PCA-based weight (PCA-PCA), making SQI analysis cost and labour-effective. The performance of entropy-based weighting was also found to be superior when the MDS was selected through PCA106. While incorporating all features and weight calculated through either PCA (All-PCA) or entropy (All-entropy) demonstrated a strong relationship with crop yield, but this approach may not be the best choice due to concerns about cost and labour efficiency. The MDS selected through various feature selection techniques with entropy-based weight performed poorly. Wu et al. (2022)53 noticed that the weights of a higher-dimension dataset could be calculated using the entropy method. Weighting through PCA seems to be a more effective method than entropy with minimum dataset. The entropy measures variable uncertainty and assesses how governing factors affect the result107.

To our knowledge, this is the first study in which entropy-based weighting technique was applied in soil quality analysis. A key limitation of the study is the small sample size used to fit the feature selection models. Machine learning-based techniques like Boruta, RF, genetic algorithm and simulated annealing typically require larger datasets for optimal performance, which may explain the reason for poor performance of some feature selection techniques. Stochastic models like genetic algorithms and simulated annealing rely on randomness within the dataset to explore the feature space but the limited data can lead to suboptimal solutions due to insufficient sampling of the search space108,109. On the other hand, methods like Random Forest (RF) and Boruta, which use bootstrapped data subsets to assess feature importance, are more prone to overfitting and high variance when working with small datasets110,111. Future studies should explore feature selection with larger datasets to potentially improve their performance.

Limitations and applicability

While the proposed framework demonstrates strong potential for soil quality assessment under integrated nutrient management, its broader applicability is limited by the single-location dataset and cropping system specificity. Machine learning-based feature selection techniques used in the study may require larger, more diverse datasets for reliable generalization. Future studies should validate the framework across varied agro-ecological zones and cropping systems to ensure scalability and robustness of the proposed methodology.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of soil quality indexing approaches within rice–wheat systems under integrated nutrient management (INM) in the Indo-Gangetic Plains. By integrating a wide range of soil physical, chemical, and biological properties, the research emphasizes the significance of robust soil quality assessment and the identification of minimum datasets (MDS) through both principal component analysis (PCA) and machine learning-based feature selection methods. A structured framework was developed to construct Soil Quality Indices (SQIs) using PCA- and entropy-based weighting schemes, effectively capturing the relationship between soil indicators and crop yield.

The findings demonstrate that INM practices substantially enhance key soil quality attributes, contributing to improved crop productivity. The study highlights the utility of PCA not only for MDS identification but also for weighting in SQI construction—particularly beneficial when working with limited datasets. These results provide practical guidance for soil health monitoring and informed nutrient management strategies. To strengthen the applicability of this framework, future research should aim to validate these methods across varied agro-ecological regions and cropping systems using larger and more diverse datasets.
