The role of anterior segment biomarkers in the development and progression of myopia
Authors: Haoran Cheng, Zhen Ling Teo, Mark Yu Zheng Wong, Joey Chung, Jia Ling Lee, Daniel Shu Wei Ting, Marcus Ang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myopia is a major global health concern, projected to affect nearly half of the world’s population by 2050. While axial elongation is known to be the underlying pathology in myopia, the pathophysiology of factors that drive axial elongation is not fully understood. Factors driving axial elongation include genetic factors and environmental factors such as outdoor activity time and the amount of near work. With advances in ocular imaging technology, there is interest in the role of anterior segment biomarkers in the onset and progression of axial elongation in myopia. Imaging modalities such as corneal tomography, anterior segment optical coherence tomography, optical biometry, and corneal biomechanical analyzers have enabled the precise measurement of corneal shape, anterior chamber depth, lens parameters, and biomechanical properties. These biomarkers have been implicated in myopia development, progression risk prediction, and response to interventions such as orthokeratology. In this review, we explore contemporary anterior segment imaging modalities and seek to better understand the complex relationship between anterior segment biomarkers and axial elongation in myopia. By understanding the role of these biomarkers, we may be better equipped in predicting myopia onset and progression and pave the way for potential treatments in myopia.

Introduction

Myopia is a global public health concern due to its rising prevalence and associated vision-threatening complications. Recent estimates indicate that about 28% of the world’s population is myopic, with especially high rates among children and young adults in East and Southeast Asia.[1] By 2050, it is projected that half the global population will suffer from myopia, and close to 10% having high myopia.[2] In China, a nationwide analysis of over 5 million children and adolescents estimates myopia prevalence to be an alarming 80.8% in urban and 76.0% in rural students aged 16–18, with high myopia projected to affect 22.1% of this group by 2050.[3] Early-onset and progressive myopia not only increases dependency on refractive correction but also heightens the lifetime risk of vision-threatening pathologic sequelae such as retinal detachment, myopic maculopathy, glaucoma, and cataract.[1] These complications arise primarily in eyes with excessive axial elongation, typically high myopia, underscoring the importance of understanding and managing myopia in childhood.[1]

While myopia develops mainly through axial elongation of the eyeball, the exact pathophysiology behind factors that drive this axial elongation is not fully understood. Genetics is a known risk factor for myopia with studies demonstrating that myopia risk increased with the number of myopic parents. Children with one parent with myopia and both parents with myopia have 2.9 and 7.8 times, respectively, higher risk of developing myopia than children without myopic parents.[45] Environmental factors have also been associated with myopia incidence and prevalence. Near work activity, such as reading, has been strongly associated with myopia.[67] On the contrary, myopic discordant twin studies showed that increased outdoor activity time is associated with lower myopia prevalence and progression.[8] Optical factors have also been studied in the control of refractive error development. Early animal studies manipulating retinal defocus demonstrated its impact on anatomical eye growth.[91011] The additional manipulation of peripheral retinal defocus (such as in peripheral defocus lenses or orthokeratology [ortho-k] contact lenses) has also been associated with axial elongation control.[12]

With better anterior segment imaging modalities, there is a rising interest in anterior segment biomarkers, which significantly affect the optics of the eye, and the hypothesis that these biomarkers may have an important role in myopia.[13] The anterior segment provides a variety of biomarkers – providing objective measurement of anatomical or biomechanical features. In this review, we summarized evidence on several anterior segment biomarkers made available by modern imaging modalities and their relationship with the onset, progression of myopia, and response to myopia treatment modalities. Literature was identified primarily through PubMed and Web of Science searches using terms such as “myopia,” “anterior segment,” “ocular imaging,” and “corneal biomechanics,” supplemented by manual review of reference lists from relevant articles. Our focus was on human studies, although mechanistic insights from animal and in vitro studies were occasionally cited where directly relevant. Articles published up to September 2025 were considered. Anterior Segment Biomarkers associated with myopia onset and progression

Anterior Segment Biomarkers and Myopia

Corneal keratometric power

The cornea contributes approximately two-thirds of the eye’s total refractive power by focusing incoming light onto the retina.[32] There exists a myriad of modalities to obtain corneal power measurements. Placido-Disk Corneal Topography is an established method for mapping the corneal surface.[3334] The technique projects concentric black-and-white rings onto the tear film, and the reflected pattern is used to reconstruct the anterior corneal curvature. This allows clinicians to generate detailed topographic maps, keratometry (K) readings, and astigmatism profiles over the central 3–6 mm of the cornea. Beyond Placido disk-based technology, advances such as Scheimpflug corneal tomography made the measurement of both anterior and posterior corneal curvatures possible. These geometric radius values are converted to give the corneal refractive power. In Scheimpflug corneal tomography, a rotating Scheimpflug camera captures slit images through the anterior segment, reconstructing a three-dimensional model of the cornea and chamber,[35] generating comprehensive corneal curvature maps, pachymetry, and various anterior chamber parameters.[35]

The ability to better measure corneal curvature has led to the question if corneal curvature is simply an association with axial length (AL) or is corneal curvature a factor driving AL change. Studies have shown that a lower corneal curvature is associated with increased AL.[1436] A study by Jiang et al. suggested that unusually large corneal diameters or very flat K values can act as early warning markers of axial myopia, complementing AL-based screening.[15] In addition, changes in corneal curvature with the extent of central corneal flattening achieved are, in themselves, a useful early indicator of treatment response in ortho-k lenses for myopia progression control. Research has shown that greater changes in corneal curvature within the first several days or weeks of ortho-k wear correlate with better refractive outcomes and possibly more myopia control, as the induced peripheral defocus is linked to slowed eye growth.[161718] In fact, even very short-term changes – corneal curvature flattening and thinning measurable after just minutes or one night of wear – have been proposed as indicators of the cornea’s “malleability” and likely response to long-term ortho-k.[161718]

However, despite these encouraging observations, the current evidence base warrants cautious interpretation. Much of the existing literature is derived from cross-sectional studies, which limits the ability to disentangle whether corneal curvature changes actively drive axial elongation or merely reflect underlying ocular growth patterns.[161718] Furthermore, the majority of these studies are derived from East Asian populations, in whom baseline corneal geometry, myopia prevalence, and progression rates differ substantially from those of Caucasian or African populations.[37] This population-specific evidence base may therefore constrain the external validity and generalizability of corneal curvature-based risk stratification or treatment-response metrics across diverse ethnic groups.

Axial length-corneal radius ratio

Axial elongation directly reflects the structural growth of the eye. Devices such as the IOLMaster (Zeiss) and Lenstar (Haag-Streit), and newer swept-source biometers, use interference of light to obtain ocular measurements with high precision. Optical biometers are non-contact and highly reproducible, making them preferable to ultrasound AL measurement for monitoring myopia progression.[19]

AL is considered the gold-standard metric for tracking myopia progression in children undergoing treatment, as it provides a more reliable measure of progression than refractive error change which can be influenced by accommodation or corneal curvature changes.[1920] Hence, most myopia studies use annual AL elongation as the primary outcome. In practice, if a child’s AL is not increasing after starting treatment, it indicates a good response, even if the refraction has changed. Conversely, continued rapid AL growth would signal inadequate control, prompting a change or intensification of therapy.[20] AL is also invaluable in ortho-k patients: since ortho-k causes corneal flattening that can temporarily reduce myopia, refractive error alone is misleading – only by measuring AL can we discern if the underlying myopic progression is slowed.[20]

Researchers have begun to explore integrated indices that capture the interplay between corneal shape and axial elongation, and the AL-Corneal Radius (AL/CR) Ratio is an emerging composite measure. The AL/CR ratio reflects the balance between axial elongation and corneal optical power. A ratio approaching or exceeding 3.0 often indicates insufficient hyperopic reserve and imminent myopia onset.[21] Data from over 500,000 Chinese children demonstrated superior performance using AL/CR for myopia detection, outperforming AL alone.[38] Although the landmark study provides substantial statistical power, a universal threshold may oversimplify the dynamic refractive changes occurring during early emmetropization, particularly in younger children whose axial growth and corneal remodeling are still physiologically adaptive. Moreover, variability in CR estimation across different optical biometers – driven by proprietary algorithms and measurement assumptions – introduces the potential for systematic bias, challenging the application of a universal AL/CR cutoff in routine clinical practice.[39]

Central corneal thickness

Central corneal thickness (CCT) is defined as the distance between the anterior epithelial surface and the posterior endothelial surface at the center of the cornea.[40] CCT is most commonly measured using ultrasound pachymetry as well as newer non-contact methods such as Anterior Segment Optical Coherence Tomography (AS-OCT) and Scheimpflug imaging. Traditional ultrasound provides a quick single-point central reading, whereas modern AS-OCT and Scheimpflug tomographers can generate high-resolution pachymetry maps across the cornea.

Several studies have examined CCT in relation to myopia onset and progression. While cross-sectional analyses in adults found no significant correlation between CCT and refractive error magnitude, recent longitudinal evidence in children indicates that a thinner central cornea may predispose to earlier and faster myopic eye growth. In a 12-month study, baseline CCT showed a positive correlation with the age of myopia onset and a negative correlation with myopia progression rate – eyes with thinner corneas became myopic at younger ages and underwent faster axial elongation.[22] One proposed explanation is biomechanical: a thin cornea often accompanies lower corneal hysteresis (CH) and possibly a thinner, more deformable sclera. Since progressive myopia involves scleral thinning and remodeling, facilitating axial elongation, CCT might serve as an accessible proxy for overall ocular wall rigidity. Further details on corneal biomechanics are discussed in Section 2.5.

Beyond risk assessment, changes in CCT themselves can act as an early indicator of treatment response in myopia control. A meta-analysis found that most of the CCT reduction with ortho-k occurs within the 1st week of treatment, with the cornea remaining thinner thereafter.[23] In clinical practice, larger early changes in central thickness under ortho-k tend to correspond with greater myopia reduction and potentially more effective slowing of axial elongation over time. Thus, both the static value of CCT and its dynamic changes with interventions have emerged as useful biomarkers in understanding and managing myopia progression.

Nevertheless, the current evidence base warrants cautious interpretation. Associations between thinner CCT and earlier myopia onset or faster progression are largely derived from pediatric studies with relatively short follow-up periods and small sample sizes, limiting inferences about long-term predictive value. Similarly, meta-analytic data on ortho-k–induced corneal thinning often lack follow-up beyond 1 year, making it difficult to determine whether observed reductions in CCT represent a sustained biomechanical adaptation or a transient epithelial redistribution. This distinction is clinically important, as short-term thickness changes may overestimate the role of CCT as a surrogate for deeper stromal or scleral remodeling relevant to long-term myopia control.

Anterior chamber depths

Anterior chamber depth (ACD) is defined as the distance from the posterior surface of the cornea to the anterior surface of the lens. Modern optical biometers using low-coherence interferometry are able to measure ACD with greater precision than traditional ultrasound biometry.[2141] AS-OCT is another technology that can image the chamber angle and depth. These advances in imaging have made ACD assessment quick, repeatable, and highly accurate in both research and clinical settings.[42]

Myopic eyes tend to have deeper anterior chambers than emmetropic or hyperopic eyes.[24] As the eye elongates in myopia, the lens thins and moves posteriorly, expanding the anterior chamber. Improved biometric tracking has shown that a greater ACD often accompanies longer AL in children. A prospective study reported that children with faster AL growth over 6 months had a significantly deeper baseline ACD compared to those with minimal growth.[25] However, the relationship between ACD and AL is by far more complex. It has been reported that amongst East Asian patients, it is not uncommon for eyes with myopia and long ALs to be associated with shallow ACDs.[4344] The disproportionate elongation between the anterior segment and posterior segments of the eye during myopia development remains poorly understood with lens mechanism or ciliary body structural factors postulated to be involved.[45]

ACD changes have also been noted after myopia control interventions. Pharmacological interventions like atropine eye drops produce small but noticeable changes in ACD and lens thickness.[26] Within a few months of moderate-or high-concentration atropine treatment, children’s lenses flatten and thin slightly, leading to a deeper anterior chamber than baseline. One study found a significant increase in ACD accompanied by a decrease in lens thickness at 3 and 6 months of 0.3% atropine therapy, consistent with the expected effect of ciliary relaxation on lens shape.[26] These optical biometric changes are reversible and reflect an immediate biomechanical response to atropine-induced ciliary muscle relaxation. However, they are more likely to represent transient physiological effects on accommodation and lens configuration rather than durable structural remodeling of the anterior segment with direct relevance to long-term myopia control.

Corneal biomechanics

Corneal biomechanical evaluation has emerged as a functional biomarker of the cornea. Two notable devices are the Ocular Response Analyzer (ORA) and the Corneal Visualization Scheimpflug Technology (CorVis ST). The ORA uses a collimated air puff and an optical sensor to measure the cornea’s deformation and rebound, providing metrics like CH and Corneal Resistance Factor (CRF).[46] CH reflects cornea viscoelasticity, while CRF is weighted towards elastic resistance. The CorVis ST uses a high-speed Scheimpflug camera to capture the cornea’s dynamic deformation in response to an air puff.[4748] These biomechanical metrics are emerging as potential biomarkers for ocular rigidity.

Ocular biomechanical biomarkers offer complementary insights into myopia risk by capturing the mechanical properties of the cornea, which reflect broader scleral and ocular tissue characteristics. ORA-based measures, including CH and CRF, are consistently lower in myopic eyes, with the steepest declines observed in high myopia. A recent systematic review reported that CH and CRF were significantly reduced in highly myopic compared with emmetropic eyes.[27] Mendelian randomization analyses suggest that lower CH and CRF values may not simply be secondary to elongation, but could represent intrinsic biomechanical susceptibility to myopia development.[4950] Decreases in CH and CRF can accompany periods of rapid axial growth, potentially serving as early biomechanical warning signs.[51] It has even been proposed that scleral elasticity is a driver of axial elongation.[52] Supporting this, interventions that increase scleral stiffness (e.g. experimental collagen cross-linking treatments) have been shown to slow eye growth in animal models.[52]

A recent meta-analysis synthesizing Corvis ST data found that corneas from high myopes tend to have longer first applanation times, greater deformation amplitudes, and lower stiffness parameters than nonmyopic eyes, indicative of a more compliant cornea.[28] These biomechanical differences may reflect generalized ocular wall susceptibility to progressive axial elongation. Biomechanical biomarkers may contribute to risk profiling for myopia onset and progression.

Corneal biomechanical properties are increasingly recognized as potential predictors of treatment response in myopia control and refractive interventions. Recent research suggests that corneal biomechanical properties may predict ortho-k response: eyes with a more deformable (“softer”) cornea might undergo shape changes more readily but could also be prone to faster axial elongation.[53] Corneal biomechanical metrics also change with ortho-k treatment. Studies have documented decreases in CH and CRF after beginning ortho-k, implying the cornea becomes slightly less resistant due to lens-induced shape changes.[293031] Concurrently, the Corvis Biomechanical Index (CBI) tends to increase, as noted in a 1-year prospective study of children on ortho-k.[54] Importantly, that study found CBI rose the most in the “good responders” who had rapid initial myopia reduction, suggesting that an early increase in CBI might predict favorable treatment outcomes.[54] However, it also noted that an excellent initial flattening response does not always guarantee long-term slowing of axial elongation, underscoring the need for ongoing monitoring of AL despite corneal changes.

Surgical Implications of Anterior Segment Biomarkers in Myopia

Beyond its role in myopia onset and progression in childhood, anterior segment biomarkers play a huge role in determining myopia treatment options in adulthood. For adults seeking permanent myopia correction, the anterior segment anatomy determines candidacy for laser refractive surgery and phakic intraocular lenses.

Refractive surgeries such as laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK), photorefractive keratectomy, or keratorefractive lenticule extraction remove corneal tissue to treat refractive error. Pachymetry is important to ensure sufficient residual stroma thickness post laser refractive surgery, as a residual stromal thickness of less than ~ 250 μm has a higher risk of post-LASIK ectasia.[55] Flatter corneal curvatures can also lead to complications such as free flaps in LASIK, larger ablation zones, and more induced aberrations.[56] Corneal biomechanics also plays an important role in preoperative assessment, whereby eyes with low CH and thin corneas are at higher risk of postlaser vision correction ectasia and may change surgical management decision-making. There is also emerging interest in scleral cross-linking to stiffen the sclera and slow axial elongation.[57]

In patients with moderate to high myopia, implantation of phakic intraocular lenses such as the ICL requires a minimum ACD of around 2.8–3.0 mm.[55] Accurate ICL sizing requires not only white-to-white (WTW) measurements but also sulcus-to-sulcus or angle-to-angle metrics obtained from AS-OCT, as these better predict postoperative vault than WTW alone.[5859] After implantation, AS-OCT plays a key role in postoperative monitoring: an optimal central vault of ~ 250–750 μm minimizes the risk of cataract from ICL–crystalline lens contact at low vaults, and angle closure or elevated intraocular pressure from excessive vault at the other extreme.[6061]

Current Limitations and Future Prospects

Despite these advances, the application of anterior segment biomarkers in myopia prediction and management is still limited by several technical and methodological barriers. Technical differences between imaging platforms result in substantial measurement variability. WTW, LT, corneal refractive power, and astigmatism show systematic discrepancies between biometry devices, reducing comparability between studies and hindering the establishment of universal reference standards.[62] Similarly, ACD and other parameters also differ depending on the imaging modality used.[63] These discrepancies arise from differences in resolution, illumination, and calibration. Compounding this issue is the lack of standardized imaging protocols, as acquisition settings and environmental factors such as lighting can influence results.

In addition, there is an unclear predictive value of many proposed anterior segment biomarkers and their limited adoption in routine clinical practice. While these modern anterior segment biomarkers in isolation demonstrate high accuracy and potential impact on myopia management, further work is required for successful integration of all these biomarkers into clinical myopia management pathways acceptable to patients and providers.[64] There is also a lack of large-scale longitudinal datasets tracking anterior segment changes over the course of myopia development, especially in pediatric populations, where physiological emmetropization and rapid ocular remodeling may confound associations observed at single time points. Long-term studies are needed to determine whether these anterior metrics can predict future myopic shift or are merely concurrent phenomena. In addition, health economic analyses will be required to evaluate the utility of multi-modal imaging in myopia management.

While these limitations have slowed clinical adoption, they also define clear opportunities for innovation – particularly through artificial intelligence. AI has shown substantial promise in predicting myopia onset and progression, with several studies demonstrating high accuracy when models are trained on retinal fundus images and basic biometric parameters such as AL and spherical equivalence.[6566] The integration of anterior segment biomarkers into AI myopia models could improve long-term risk stratification of myopia patients. Beyond risk prediction, AI could support personalized treatment selection by forecasting individual response to interventions such as low-dose atropine, ortho-k, or myopia control spectacles, thereby optimizing therapeutic outcomes in both pediatric and adult populations.[676869] Generative AI and federated learning may also enable larger datasets for model development in situations where data access may be challening such as in the pediatric myopia population.

Conclusion

Myopia development is a multifactorial process, but technological advances in anterior segment imaging provide a wealth of measurable indicators that may improve our understanding and management of this condition. Further work is required to integrate these anterior segment biomarker findings into current myopia clinical pathways. Ultimately, the goal is to use all available biomarkers for personalized predictive medicine. In doing so, we can hope to mitigate the personal and public health burden of myopia in East Asia and around the world, for generations to come.
