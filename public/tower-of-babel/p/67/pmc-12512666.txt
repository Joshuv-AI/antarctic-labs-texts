Work engagement and its associated factors among nurses in hospitals of western Ethiopia
Authors: Dereje Chala Diriba, Temesgen Tilahun, Adugna Olani Akuma, Amsalu Takele, Tesfaye Shibiru, Tesfaye Abera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNurses’ work engagement is vital for enhancing the quality of care in healthcare settings. As a significant part of the healthcare workforce, nurses require motivation, self-identification, full concentration, and deep engagement to perform effectively. Factors such as the meaning of work, nursing leadership, and available resources have been identified as influencing nurses’ work engagement. Despite previous studies addressing work engagement, few have assessed the associations between evidence-based practise, sociodemographic factors, and work-related characteristics. This study examines the level of work engagement and its associated factors among professional nurses in hospitals in western Ethiopia.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted between 21 September and 31 December 2023, involving 418 nurses working in seven hospitals. The modified Job Demands-Resources and the Nursing Job Demands-Resources models guided the research. Work engagement was assessed using the 9-item Utrecht Work Engagement Scale, independent variables such as organisational justice, social support, job satisfaction, and employee involvement, were measured using the Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire, and evidence-based practise was measured using a short Evidence-based Practise Implementation Scale. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS version 25, with simple and multiple linear regression analyses identifying relationships between independent and dependent variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe mean work engagement score among nurses was 4.02 (SD \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document} 1.29). Being female (β = 0.301, 95% CI: 0.067, 0.535), employed at a teaching hospital (β = 0.697, 95% CI: 0.395, 1.00), the meaning of work (β = 0.124, 95% CI: 0.057, 0.190), and engagement in evidence-based practise (β = 0.184, 95% CI: 0.065, 0.303) were associated with work engagement. The model explained 20.1% of the variability in work engagement.ConclusionThe level of work engagement among nurses in western Ethiopia aligns with findings from other studies. Factors like being female, working in the teaching hospitals, the meaning of work, and engagement in evidence-based practise were found to positively influence work engagement. Future research should explore predictors of work engagement at the national level.

Introduction

Nurses constitute a substantial portion of the healthcare workforce, and their engagement is essential for enhancing the quality of patient care [1, 2]. Work engagement is defined as a positive, fulfilling work-related state of mind that is characterised by vigour (high activation), dedication (high identification), and absorption (being fully concentrated and deeply engrossed in one’s work) [3]. Engaged nurses possess the energy and commitment needed to meet job demands effectively, which benefits not only the nurses themselves but also the nursing profession and the broader community [4].

The job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model serves as a framework for understanding factors that drive work engagement [5, 6]. According to this model, job demands–physical, psychological, social, or organisational aspects of work – can lead to fatigue if not balanced by job resources, which are factors that help to meet job challenges. Job resources are defined as “those physical, psychological, social, or organisational aspects of the job” [5]. Both job demands and resources are critical in influencing work-related stress and overall well-being [5, 6]. This model involves the manager’s leadership and resources (job, professional and personal). Job resources can be divided into two categories: organisational justice and support from peers (supervisor support and colleague support); professional resources include employment involvement, and personal resources refer to how employees give meaning to work [4–6]. Job satisfaction is one motivational factor in work engagement and better job performance [6–8] (Fig. 1).

A systematic review conducted in 2016 with most studies from North America suggested that the nursing job demands-resources (NJD-R) model should guide nursing practise, research and policy in work engagement [4]. Given that the JD-R model is not directly applicable to nursing [4] and the NJD-R model is complex, we developed a model that is suitable for professional nurses’ work engagement. Two domains of the JD-R and NJD-R models, including job demands and organisational outcomes, were excluded from this modified model. Motivation, evidence-based practise (EBP), sociodemographic characteristics and work-related characteristics were incorporated in this model. Despite EBP being a recent initiative in decision-making and improving patient care [9], the association with work engagement has not been studied. Moreover, sociodemographic and work-related factors were not assessed for their associations with work engagement.

The mean work engagement of nurses ranged from 2.33 [10] to 4.94 [11]. Although several factors impact nurses’ work engagement, nurses’ engagement has received considerable attention owing to their positive impressions of patient outcomes, including patient safety and quality of care in healthcare [12, 13]. Previous studies have indicated that gender, years of experience [11, 14] and supervisory support [10, 15, 16] positively associate with work engagement. A review of the literature further revealed that social support, meaning of work, organisational justice [17], peer support [18] and job satisfaction [15] also contribute positively to work engagement. Inversely, workload has been identified as a negative factor influencing nurses’ work engagement [15]. Employee involvement does not predict work engagement [18]. Engaging in overtime work has a mixed impact on work engagement. When overtime is involuntary or coerced, it tends to produce negative outcomes; however, when it is perceived as enjoyable, it can enhance work engagement [15, 19, 20]. A review of 38 studies indicated that holding a higher job position is positively and consistently associated with work engagement [15]. Another recent study showed that employment in a general hospital setting is linked to increased work engagement and lower levels of frustration [11]. Collaboration among nurse practitioners was shown to reduce competency-related frustration and enhance work engagement. Furthermore, a qualitative study reported that managerial roles positively influence work engagement. However, no significant association was found between salary and work engagement [11].

Despite growing global emphasis on EBP for patient care, few studies have addressed the associations between work engagement and EBP, as well as sociodemographic and work-related characteristics in nursing. Therefore, this study aims to assess the level of work engagement among nurses working in hospitals in Western Ethiopia and identify the associated factors. By exploring these dimensions, the findings may inform nursing practise and policy in the region.

Methods and materials

Study setting and design

A multicenter hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted between 21 September and 31 December 2023, involving nurses from seven hospitals in Western Ethiopia: two teaching university hospitals, one comprehensive specialised hospital, one general hospital, and three primary hospitals.

Participants

The participants were nurses employed at the hospitals included in the study, which are located in western Ethiopia. Nurses were eligible for inclusion if they (a) had completed at least a diploma in nursing and (b) had more than six months of work experience at the respective hospital. Nurses who declined to participate were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

A sample size of 384 nurses was determined using a single population formula, assuming a 95% confidence level and a margin of error of 0.05. Due to a lack of previous studies on the target population, an estimated 50% prevalence of work engagement was used. To account for a 10% nonresponse rate, the total sample size was rounded to 422 nurses. The sample was proportionally allocated based on the number of nurses in each hospital.

Sampling method

A list of nurses was obtained from the hospitals’ matron or coordinator offices, and participants were selected using simple random sampling method.

Data collection tools

Data were collected using a structured self-administered questionnaire that included socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, educational level, role of nurses, and ethnicity), work-related variables (level of hospital where the nurse works, work experience, the number of nurses assigned to ward, department or unit, weekly working hours on business time, salary, and engagement in paid duty times (night/weekend/holiday duties), and nurses’ work engagement.

The Utrecht work engagement scale (UWES-9)

The UWES-9 is a 9-item, 7-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 0 = never to 6 = always) designed to measure work engagement [7]. Scores range from 0 to 6, with higher scores indicating greater work engagement. The scale demonstrates good internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.85.

The Copenhagen psychosocial questionnaire (COPSOQ III)

The COPSOQ III has 30 scales used to measure the psychosocial work environment. Based on the study objectives, organisational justice, supervisor support, colleague support, meaning of work, and job satisfaction were assessed using varying scale items. Organisational justice – employees’ perceptions of the fairness of management’s decisions and actions – was measured using four 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = very large extent to 5 = very small extent) items, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.77. The items are reverse-coded, and the score ranges from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater organisational justice. Supervisor support was measured using three items, and colleague support was measured using three items, with all items on a 6-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = always to 6 = I do not have a supervisor/colleague) and were reverse-scored. The social support scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81. The meaning of work was measured using two items with a 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = very large extent to 5 = very small extent) and were reverse-scored. The scale reveals acceptable internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78. Job satisfaction was measured using five items with a 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = very satisfied to 5 = very unsatisfied) and was reverse-scored, and the Cronbach’s alpha was 0.75 [21–23].

Employee involvement measure

Employee involvement measure is a 3-point Likert-type item ranging from 1 = sometimes to 3 = always, and the other one is dichotomised to Yes or No responses, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.76 [24] measure the extent to which workers participate in work-related decisions. The highest score demonstrates the highest workers’ participation in work-related decisions.

Evidence-Based practise implementation (EBPI) scale

The EBPI scale is a 3-item, 5-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) developed to measure the level of evidence-based practise (17). Scores range from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater evidence-based practise. The scale has established face, content and construct validity with internal consistency reliabilities of 0.85.

Data quality assurance

Before data collection, the data collectors received orientation on the tools. Completed questionnaires were collected immediately to ensure data integrity. Investigators closely supervised the data collection process. The internal consistency of the instruments was evaluated using Cronbach’s α, with values ranging from α = 0.70 to α = 0.90 considered reliable [25]. Cronbach’s α scores for the UWES-9 (α = 0.85), organisational justice (α = 0.70), employee involvement scale (α = 072), meaning of work (α = 0.71), job satisfaction (α = 0.70), social support (α = 0.72), and EBPI (α = 0.91), confirming their reliability.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analysed using IBM SPSS version 25. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarise socio-demographic characteristics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SDs). Additionally, we assessed assumptions for parametric testing, such as linearity, normality, homoscedasticity, multicollinearity, and undue influence of outliers.

Simple and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine associations between nurses’ work engagement and independent variables. Variables that were significantly associated with work engagement in the simple linear regression were included in the multiple linear regression analysis. Beta coefficients, confidence intervals, and p-values were reported. Variables with a p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study examined the level of work engagement among nurses and the factors influencing it in hospitals located in western Ethiopia. The modified JD-R and NJD-R models were employed to examine organisational environment, resources, motivation, EBP, sociodemographic characteristics, and work-related variables in relation to work engagement. These models also served as the basis for developing the study variables. Being female, employed at a teaching hospital, the meaning of work, and engagement in evidence-based practise were associated with work engagement.

The mean work engagement score of 4.02 (2.73–5.31) suggests a level of engagement comparable to that reported among nurses in Ghana (4.03 ± 1.32) [26], China (3.17 ± 1.09) [27], the Netherlands (4.55 ± 0.8) [11] and the United States (3.68 ± 1.05) [28]. These findings suggest that, despite significant variations in working conditions, nurses’ work engagement levels may be relatively comparable across countries. However, the findings differ slightly from those of a study conducted in Japan (2.33 ± 1.00) [10]. This discrepancy may be attributed to a higher proportion of female participants in the Ghanaian study, as women tend to report higher work engagement than men [28]. This finding highlights the potential influence of gender on engagement levels, suggesting that female nurses may play a key role in sustaining clinical workforce engagement.

Female nurses reported higher work engagement than male nurses. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in China [14] and the United States [28]. This similarity may be attributed to female nurses exhibiting higher in vigour, dedication, and absorption than males [28]. These findings suggest that gender may influence the dynamics of work engagement through differential approaches to caregiving and emotional involvement in patient care.

There was a positive association between employment in teaching hospitals and nurses’ work engagement. This finding is consistent with the studies conducted in Oman [29] and the Netherlands [11]. The level of the hospital plays a significant role in influencing nurses’ work engagement. Higher-level hospitals, such as tertiary or teaching institutions, often offer more opportunities for professional development, greater access to advanced medical technologies, closer patient care, and more structured support systems. These factors can contribute positively to nurses’ psychological empowerment, job satisfaction, and ultimately their work engagement. Conversely, nurses working in lower-level hospitals may face resource constraints, heavier workloads, and limited career progression, which can hinder their engagement. Nonetheless, smaller institutions might offer stronger interpersonal relationships and a greater sense of community, which could foster engagement under certain conditions. Thus, the impact of hospital level on nurses’ work engagement is multifaceted, and understanding this relationship is crucial for developing targeted interventions to enhance workforce performance and the quality of patient care.

Meaning of work was positively associated with nurses’ work engagement: each unit increase in nurses’ perceived meaning of work predicted an increase in nurses’ work engagement. This may reflect that nurses with clear work roles, high professional self-efficacy, resilience [15, 18, 30] and nurses’ affective commitment [31] have improved work engagement.

Evidence-based practise was positively associated with nurses’ work engagement. Nurses who engaged more in EBP scored 0.184 times higher on work engagement compared to those engaged less. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Korea [32]. A study applied a path analysis reported that work engagement mediates the relationship between EBP and perceived professional practise [33], demonstrating work engagement enhances the implementation of EBP and fosters professional practise. These findings imply that the implementation of EBP nurtures nurses’ work engagement. Therefore, cultivating a work environment in which nurses have a voice in decision-making and can perceive the tangible impact of their care on patient outcomes is essential. To enhance nurses’ work engagement, hospital administrators are encouraged to promote their involvement in EBP, develop strategies that enhance the meaningfulness of their work, and incorporate them into broader professional responsibilities. Furthermore, given the demonstrated positive influence of evidence-based practise on work engagement, the integration of these factors within the modified JD-R and NJD-R models is imperative.

Strengths and limitations

The NJD-R and JD-R models, which are particular to nursing and can guide nursing practise, research and policy in work engagement, were used to guide the study. Despite these insights, this study has limitations. Its cross-sectional design cannot establish causality, and the sample was drawn exclusively from hospitals, which may limit the generalisability of the findings. Additionally, self-reported data could be subject to bias. Future studies should consider longitudinal designs and include diverse healthcare settings for a better understanding of the factors influencing nurse engagement across different contexts. In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of multifaceted approaches to enhance nurses’ work engagement in western Ethiopia, focusing on personal, organisational, and meaningful aspects of the nursing profession. By addressing the identified factors, hospitals can improve engagement levels, leading to better patient outcomes and a more stratified nursing workforce.

Conclusions

The nurses’ work engagement in hospitals in western Ethiopia is comparable to that observed in other studies, indicating a significant level of engagement among this workforce. Being female, employed at a teaching hospital, the perceived meaning of work, and engagement in evidence-based practise were associated with work engagement.

Nurses need to give existential meaning to their jobs and address nurse-related issues. These findings underline the necessity for hospital administrators to foster an environment that promotes nurses’ sense of purpose and engagement. Strategies should include enhancing support systems and integrating evidence-based practises into daily operations. Further research is warranted to explore these dynamics on a broader scale, incorporating a variety of healthcare settings beyond hospitals in Ethiopia. Such studies could help establish a more comprehensive understanding of the work engagement level and its associated factors among nurses. Ultimately, by focusing on the factors that drive engagement, health care institutions can enhance not only the well-being of their nursing staff but also the quality of care provided to patients.
