Cereblon upregulation overcomes thalidomide resistance in multiple myeloma through mitochondrial functional reprogramming
Authors: Jubert Marquez, Nammi Park, Jae Hyeog Choi, Maria Victoria Faith Garcia, Jessa Flores, Bayalagmaa Nyamaa, Jung Eun Seol, Hyoung Kyu Kim, Myung Geun Shin, Sae Gwang Park, Jin Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patients with multiple myeloma develop resistance to thalidomide during therapy, and the mechanisms to counteract thalidomide resistance remain elusive. Here, we explored the interaction between cereblon and mitochondrial function to mitigate thalidomide resistance in multiple myeloma. Measurements of cell viability, ATP production, mitochondrial membrane potential, mitochondrial ROS, and protein expression via western blotting were conducted in vitro using KSM20 and KMS26 cells to assess the impact of thalidomide on multiple myeloma. An in vivo analysis using xenografted multiple myeloma cells in BALB/c nude mice revealed that KMS20 cells were resistant to thalidomide, whereas KMS26 cells were sensitive. Overexpression of CRBN in a KMS20 xenograft model reversed its resistance to thalidomide, reduced tumor growth, and significantly extended the survival rate of the mice. Overexpression of CRBN in thalidomide-resistant KMS20 cells during thalidomide treatment led to effective cell death through the modulation of mitochondrial function and protein expression, mediated by AMPKα1 signaling. Conversely, both genetic and pharmacological knockdowns of CRBN rendered KMS26 cells resistant to thalidomide, indicating that CRBN level modulation directly influences mitochondrial functions. These findings propose that targeting cereblon offers a promising strategy in overcoming thalidomide resistance in multiple myeloma through mitochondrial reprogramming.

INTRODUCTION

Multiple myeloma, a hematological malignancy, arises from the neoplastic transformation of terminally differentiated B lymphocytes. The survival of multiple myeloma patients has significantly improved over the past decades owing to the introduction of immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs) such as thalidomide. It has been shown that thalidomide binds to cereblon (CRBN) at specific sites, thereby inhibiting E3 ubiquitin ligase (Cul4) complex activity (1).

CRBN, a thalidomide-binding protein, is localized in the cytosol, plasma membrane, and nucleus, and is ubiquitously expressed in various tissues including skeletal muscle, liver, brain, and heart (2-4). CRBN interacts with large-conductance calcium-activated potassium channels (BKCa) in the brain, as well as with the calcium channel voltage-dependent L type alpha 1C subunit (Cav1.2α) and transient receptor potential channel 1 (TRPC1) in the heart (5-7). In cancer biology, CRBN is a primary target, mediating the teratogenicity of thalidomide, linked to cytotoxicity and highly efficient anti-myeloma activity (8, 9). Thalidomide’s inhibitory effects have also been observed in prostate cancer cells (10). Recently, the role of CRBN in cancer diagnosis has expanded; pan-cancer analysis indicates that CRBN expression may act as a prognostic biomarker across various cancer types and a target for CRBN-related immunotherapy (11).

Clinically, lower levels of CRBN are observed in patients resistant to lenalidomide, whereas higher levels of CRBN are noted in multiple myeloma patients responding to treatment and those achieving complete remission (12, 13). Similarly, CRBN association with extended progression-free survival (PFS) is documented in patients receiving thalidomide therapy (14). Additionally, thalidomide-based induction regimens prove as effective as bortezomib-based regimens in elderly multiple myeloma patients with expressed CRBN (15). Consequently, CRBN has become a critical therapeutic target for personalized treatment of multiple myeloma, serving as a potential biomarker to predict patient response to IMiDs. However, most patients eventually develop drug resistance and relapse. Initially, CRBN depletion is cytotoxic to myeloma cells, which subsequently evolve to exhibit heightened resistance to IMiDs. This suggests that the drug resistance of multiple myeloma cells to sustained drug exposure might arise from various mechanisms, including metabolic reprogramming (16).

Alterations in the metabolic profile can activate signaling pathways, inducing cell phenotypes that are resistant to apoptosis and drug treatments (17). This resistance is partly attributed to the dependence of cancer cells on evolutionary mechanisms aimed at preserving mitochondrial function, which further exacerbates acquired drug resistance. Previously, we demonstrated that modulating mitochondrial function reduces bortezomib resistance in multiple myeloma cells (18). Nonetheless, the specific regulators of mitochondrial dysfunction that lead to drug resistance in multiple myeloma are not well understood. Herein, we explore how targeting CRBN may regulate mitochondrial function and counteract thalidomide resistance in multiple myeloma.

RESULTS

Thalidomide resistance in multiple myeloma cells is attributed to CRBN-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction

We initially characterized the CRBN profile and mitochondria-related genes in healthy individuals and multiple myeloma patients. Utilizing publicly accessible data from the Gene Expression Omnibus, high CRBN expression was observed in patients with multiple myeloma. Among the analyzed mitochondria-related genes, NRF1 exhibited significantly reduced expression in these patients (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, we measured CRBN and mitochondria-related protein expression in drug-resistant KMS20 and drug-sensitive KMS26 cells. KMS26 cells showed high CRBN and lower mitochondrial biogenesis marker expression. In contrast, KMS20 cells had low CRBN expression and elevated mitochondrial biogenesis markers (Fig. 1B). In vitro analysis at the basal level using multiple myeloma cell lines revealed that KMS20 cells exhibited higher ATP levels, increased mitochondrial membrane potential (∆Ψm), and reduced mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) levels compared to KMS26 cells (Fig. 1C).

We then assessed the efficacy of thalidomide in modulating the viability of multiple myeloma cells. Treatment with thalidomide led to a time-dependent decrease in cell viability in KMS26 cells (Fig. 1D). Even after a 24-hour treatment period, there was a significant reduction in KMS26 cell viability in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1E). However, neither the duration nor dose of thalidomide treatment affected the viability of KMS20 cells (Fig. 1D, E). Thalidomide treatment significantly reduced ATP levels in KMS26 cells, whereas KMS20 cells were unaffected (Fig. 1F). Moreover, thalidomide treatment decreased ∆Ψm, while no significant depolarization of ∆Ψm was observed in KMS20 cells (Fig. 1G). Both KMS20 and KMS26 cells exhibited increased mtROS levels during thalidomide treatment in a dose-dependent manner. However, this increase was significant only in KMS26 cells (Fig. 1H).

Next, we evaluated the effectiveness of thalidomide in multiple myeloma cell lines KMS20 and KMS26 by intraperitoneally administering thalidomide in xenograft models with KMS20 and KMS26 cells in vivo (Fig. 1I). Thalidomide did not impact tumor growth or survival in KMS20 tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 1J). In contrast, tumor growth was decreased and survival was significantly extended in KMS26 tumor-bearing mice treated with thalidomide (Fig. 1K). These findings suggest that KMS26 cells resemble newly diagnosed thalidomide-sensitive multiple myeloma patients, whereas KMS20 cells resemble those who develop thalidomide resistance during their treatment.

Combined CRBN overexpression and thalidomide treatment overcome resistance in multiple myeloma through mitochondrial reprogramming

Given this characterization, and given that high CRBN expression is associated with enhanced survival in patients with newly diagnosed multiple myeloma undergoing thalidomide maintenance therapy, we investigated how in vivo overexpression of CRBN could effectively target thalidomide-resistant multiple myeloma cells (14) (Fig. 2A). When combined with thalidomide treatment, CRBN overexpression in the xenograft KMS20 model increased susceptibility to thalidomide, effectively inhibited tumor growth, and substantially prolonged survival (Fig. 2B-D, Supplementary 1). Meanwhile, in vitro overexpression of CRBN alone reduced the viability of thalidomide-resistant KMS20 cells. Additionally, the combination of thalidomide and CRBN overexpression using the Ad5-CMV-CRBN-IRES-EGFP adenovirus vector (Ad-CRBN) further decreased the viability of thalidomide-resistant KMS20 cells relative to the Ad-vehicle (Ad5-CMV-IRES-EGFP control vector, Fig. 2E). The decrease in viability can be attributed to significantly reduced ATP production (Fig. 2F), a consequence of the marked decrease in ∆Ψm and increase in ROS induced by CRBN overexpression in combination with thalidomide treatment (Fig. 2G, H). The observed changes in mitochondrial function can be ascribed to the regulation of mitochondrial proteins. Notably, the expression of mitochondrial proteins such as PGC-1α, NRF1, TFAM, and ERR-α in KMS20 cells treated with thalidomide alone remained unchanged, while their expression significantly decreased during the combined CRBN overexpression and thalidomide treatment (Fig. 2I).

AMPK is an essential regulator of mitochondrial function and biogenesis, and CRBN was previously identified as a negative regulator of AMPK signaling (19). We subsequently explored how AMPK contributes to regulating thalidomide resistance and sensitivity. At the basal level, CRBN overexpression in KMS20 cells reduced the expression of p-AMPKα1 (Supplementary Fig. 2A). The expression of p-AMPKα1 in KMS20 cells treated solely with thalidomide remained unaltered, whereas its suppression occurred in KMS20 cells when CRBN overexpression and thalidomide treatment were combined (Fig. 2I). These findings suggest that enhancing CRBN expression in thalidomide-resistant models boosts treatment efficacy by modulating mitochondrial function and protein expression, which are influenced by AMPKα1.

CRBN ablation reverses thalidomide sensitivity in multiple myeloma in vitro

To determine whether CRBN knockdown could reverse thalidomide sensitivity, we introduced sh-CRBN into thalidomide-sensitive KMS26 cells (Fig. 3A). The introduction of sh-CRBN decreased sensitivity as evidenced by the resistance developed by KMS26 cells to thalidomide (Fig. 3B).

This resistance can be attributed to stable ATP levels, where ATP levels during CRBN knockdown in all thalidomide treatment doses remained similar to those of the control (Fig. 3C). ∆Ψm also remained unchanged, indicating normal membrane depolarization function (Fig. 3D). Decreased mtROS was observed in KMS26 cells treated only with thalidomide, whereas thalidomide treatment during CRBN knockdown did not affect mtROS levels (Fig. 3E). At the basal level, CRBN knockdown in KMS26 cells elevated the expression of p-AMPKα1 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Moreover, dose-dependent thalidomide treatment during CRBN knockdown in KMS26 cells increased the protein expression of p-AMPKα1 (Supplementary Fig. 3). This suggests that CRBN directly regulates AMPKα1, and its expression modulates not only thalidomide resistance but also sensitivity in multiple myeloma.

We then conducted a pharmacological knockdown of CRBN using TD-165, a PROTAC-based CRBN degrader derived from aminobenzotriazino glutarimides (Fig. 3F). 1 μM TD-165 degraded CRBN and inhibited mitochondrial function in thalidomide-sensitive KMS26 cells without inducing cytotoxicity (Fig. 3G). TD-165pre-treated KMS26 cells exhibited reduced sensitivity to thalidomide, accompanied by desensitization of ATP production, ∆Ψm, and mtROS responses to thalidomide (Fig. 3H-K). The findings from the genetic and pharmacologic knockdown of CRBN indicate the inverse regulatory role of CRBN in mitochondrial function in thalidomide-sensitive cells, leading to desensitization and ultimate resistance to thalidomide. Enhancing CRBN expression in thalidomide-resistant models augments treatment efficacy by regulating mitochondrial function and protein expression.

DISCUSSION

The results of our study demonstrate the varying susceptibilities of multiple myeloma cells to thalidomide, which hinge on their intrinsic CRBN expression levels. These findings further substantiate clinical reports that associate low CRBN expression with resistance to thalidomide monotherapy, suggesting that CRBN protein expression enhances cellular sensitivity to thalidomide (13, 20). Modulation of CRBN expression in human myeloma cell lines governs IMiD resistance, aligning with the documented downregulation of CRBN in patients resistant to lenalidomide (1). CRBN knockdown diminishes lenalidomide sensitivity in MM, and the absence of CRBN correlates with clinical resistance to both lenalidomide and pomalidomide, which are analogs of thalidomide (1, 21). Moreover, 5-30% of cells from various multiple myeloma lines remain viable following CRBN inhibition, indicating that survival of these cells is contingent upon CRBN downstream signaling (8). This downstream signaling may involve the mitochondria-dependent signaling pathway (18).

Mitochondria synthesize numerous essential metabolites vital for cancer growth, proliferation, and metastasis; their biogenesis is a notable aspect of cancer metabolic reprogramming. Mitochondrial proteins, located in close proximity to ROS sources, are consequently more prone to oxidative damage, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction caused by excessive ROS production, which accumulates oxidized mitochondrial proteins that may exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction and induce cell death (22, 23). More recently, irreversible mitochondrial dysfunction has been attributed to excessive mtROS production resulting from long-term PROTAC-induced CRBN KO (24).

Furthermore, AMPK is a vital regulator of mitochondrial function and biogenesis and plays an essential role in energy metabolism in response to acute energy crises (25). Numerous studies have investigated the role of CRBN in modulating AMPK as a substrate recognition component of the CRL4CRBN E3 protein ligase. Co-immunoprecipitation experiments have demonstrated that CRBN directly interacts with the α1 subunit of AMPK (AMPKα1) (19). This interaction induces the polyubiquitination and resulting degradation of AMPKα, consequently inhibiting its phosphorylation and activity (26). Additionally, the study by Yang et al. reveals that CRL4CRBN E3 protein ligase also contributes to the degradation of the AMPKγ subunit (27, 28). AMPKγ, a regulatory subunit of the holoenzyme, acts as a sensor for the intracellular AMP/ATP ratio, and its interaction with AMP facilitates the phosphorylation of the α subunit (26). The level of CRBN increased following thalidomide treatment, leading to reduced AMPKα1 and mitochondrial proteins, corroborating previous findings that depict CRBN as a negative regulator of AMPK signaling (19).

Ideally, a combined modality of thalidomide and CRBN overexpression could significantly benefit individuals suffering from multiple myeloma. However, substantial translational and clinical challenges remain to be addressed. Firstly, a safe and effective human CRBN overexpression delivery system must be developed to confirm its potential therapeutic applications in multiple myeloma patients. Secondly, it is necessary to explore whether CRBN overexpression combined with thalidomide analogs used in the treatment of multiple myeloma, such as lenalidomide or pomalidomide, will achieve the same efficacy as reported in the current study. Thirdly, further studies are required to enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms that underlie the role of CRBN in the drug resistance of multiple myeloma cells.

Nevertheless, we have demonstrated that CRBN overexpression enables multiple myeloma cells to surmount their resistance to thalidomide by markedly enhancing the direct binding partner of thalidomide, which subsequently modulates mitochondrial function and downstream proteins through AMPKα1 signaling (Fig. 3L). A combined therapeutic strategy comprising thalidomide treatment and CRBN overexpression offers a novel approach that could assist researchers and clinicians in tackling thalidomide resistance in multiple myeloma patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

Thalidomide was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). TD-165, a PROTAC-based CRBN degrader, was synthesized by the Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology (Daejeon, South Korea) according to a previous protocol (29).

Adenovirus infection

The adenovirus was produced by KOMABIOTECH (Seoul, South Korea) with the full vector name Ad5-CMV-CRBN-IRES-EGFP adenovirus vector (Ad-CRBN) and Ad5-CMV-IRES-EGFP control vector (Ad-Vehicle). Following the release of the recombinant viral DNA, HEK-293 cells plated the day before on six-well plates were transfected with the adenoviral DNA and incubated for three days at 37°C. Cells were then harvested and subjected to a freeze/thaw treatment. Fresh HEK-293 cells plated on 6-well plates the day before were then infected with the resulting cell lysate and incubated at 37°C until cells showed completed cytopathic effects (CPE). Cells were harvested, and viral particles were released by freeze/thaw treatment. 1E＋07 HEK 293 cells were infected at MOI 2. Cells were incubated until they showed CPE. Release of viral particles from the infected cells via Freeze/Thaw treatment. Viral particles were purified using an AdenoONE TM purification kit (SIRON Biotech). Infectious units (IU) were determined via immunochemical detection of the adenoviral hexon protein in infected KMS cells.

Mice tumor models

Female BALB/c nude (nu/nu) mice (5-6 weeks of age) were purchased from Orient Bio (Sungnam, Korea) and used for experiments when the mice reached a body weight of 17-20 g (6-8 weeks of age). The mice were allowed to acclimate before experiments under specific pathogen-free conditions at the animal care facility of the College of Medicine, Inje University. All experimental procedures were examined and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Inje University (Protocol no. 2017-017). Thalidomide-resistant KMS20 human multiple myeloma cells (1 × 106) or thalidomide-sensitive KMS26 human multiple myeloma cells (7.5 × 106) were injected subcutaneously (s.c.) into the backs of female BALB/c nude (nu/nu) mice anesthetized with a mixture of ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Tumor volume was calculated as (a x b x c)/2, where a-c are the measurements along three orthogonal axes. When the tumor volume reached about 150 mm3 to 200 mm3, mice were treated with thalidomide or vehicle (10% [v/v] DMSO). Thalidomide was intraperitoneally (i.p.) administered at 50 mg/kg doses on indicated days. 50 mg/kg i.p. administration of thalidomide in mice is approximately 200 mg, the starting therapeutic dose used in patients (30, 31). The recurrence of thalidomide administration is based on previous studies that also used thalidomide and its analogs in mice models (32).

To test whether CRBN expression can recover the IMiDs susceptibility in IMiDs-resistant KMS20 tumor in vivo, Ad-CRBN or Ad-vehicle (total 8 × 1010 PFU/mice) were injected intratumorally (i.t.) on days −1 to 2 of thalidomide treatment. Mice were sacrificed upon achieving a tumor volume >2000 mm3, which was used to define survival. Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg body weight). Images were taken 7 days after thalidomide treatment (day 18).

Cell culture

KMS20 and KMS26 cells were initially obtained from the Kawasaki Medical School (Kurashiki, Japan) and were maintained in RPMI 1640 (Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA). Cells were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, PAA, ThermoFisher, Rockford, IL, USA) and penicillin/streptomycin (100 units/ml and 100 μg/ml, respectively). Cells were counted and checked for viability with trypan blue. Cell stock with more than 98% cell viability is used for in vitro cytotoxicity. The cell density was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/ml with the addition of a complete growth medium and was grown in a cell incubator at 37°C, with 5% CO2.

Cell proliferation test (CCK-8)

Cell death was determined using a CCK-8 assay kit (Dojindo, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, thalidomide-treated KMS20 cells were inoculated into a 96-well microplate, and CCK-8 solution (10 μl/100 μl medium) was added to each well of the plate. After incubation of the plate for 2-3 h in a CO2 incubator at 37°C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm in each well using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) with a reference wavelength of 650 nm.

Measurement of mitochondrial ROS

Mitochondrial ROS generation was assessed using MitoSox Red (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). KMS20 cells were seeded onto 30-mm culture dishes at a density of 3 × 105 cells and incubated with 1 μM Mito-Sox for 20 minutes at 37°C. The intensity of fluorescence from 10,000 events was measured. For quantitative analysis of ROS generation, Mito-Sox-treated cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using a FACSCantoII (BD Biosciences) instrument.

Mitochondrial membrane potential analysis

Mitochondrial membrane potential (∆Ψm) was assessed in KMS20 cells using the ∆Ψm -specific fluorescent dye, TMRE (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Cells (1 × 106) from each group were incubated with 100 nM TMRE for 30 minutes at 37°C. The intensity of fluorescence from 10,000 events was analyzed using a FACS CantoII flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Measurement of Mitochondrial ATP level

Mitochondrial ATP level was measured by Mitochondrial ToxGloTM assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, KMS20 cells were plated 2 × 106 cells/well in a 60 mm tissue culture plate treated with thalidomide for 24 h. Treated cells were harvested and resuspended by pipetting, and cells were plated 2 × 104 cells/well in a 96-well white with clear bottom culture plate in 50 μl of glucose-free fresh medium and supplemented with 10 mM galactose. The plate was incubated at 37°C in a humidified and CO2-supplemented incubator for 90 min. Assay solution (100 μl) was added to the plate and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Luminescence was measured using a luminometer (Molecular Device, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Western blot analysis

Following thalidomide treatment, floating cells were collected, centrifuged, and resuspended in lysis buffer A 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM EGTA, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 1% Triton X-100 and protease cocktail I/II (Sigma-Aldrich). The tissue samples were homogenized using tissue homogenizer (FastPrep-24 5G, MP Biomedicals, at the Cardiovascular and Metabolic Disease Core Research Support Center. Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in 0.01M Tris-buffered saline (pH 7.5) containing 0.5% Tween 20 and incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies CRBN (HPA045910, Sigma-Aldrich); PGC-1α (sc-517380, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA); NRF1 (46743S, Cell Signalling, Danvers, MA, USA); ERRα (13826S, Cell Signalling); TFAM (sc-376672, Santa Cruz Biotechnology); p-AMPKα1 (2535, Cell Signalling); AMPKα1 (2795, Cell Signalling); α-Tubulin (2144, Cell Signalling). Immunodetection was performed using PierceTM ECL Western Blotting Substrate for western blot analysis (ThermoFisher, Rockford, IL, USA).

In silico analysis

Publicly available data on CRBN expression in healthy and multiple myeloma patients (accession code: GDS4620) were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Student’s t-test, One-way and Two-way ANOVA, and log-rank test where applicable using GraphPad Prism 8 software; P values as indicated above figures were derived to assess statistical significance. Data for all figures are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and P-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant. No statistical methods were used to determine the sample size. The investigators were blinded to the genotypes of the individual animals during the experiments and outcome assessment.
