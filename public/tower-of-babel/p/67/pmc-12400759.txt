Associations between dietary index for gut microbiota with COPD prevalence and all-cause mortality: insights from the 1999–2018 NHANES data
Authors: Xiaopeng Liu, Zhixiong Hu, Qi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGut microbiota dysbiosis plays a vital role the pathogenesis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). This study aimed to: (1) examine the cross-sectional association between dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM), a novel biomarker reflecting gut microbiota composition and function, and COPD prevalence; and (2) assess the prognostic significance of DI-GM score for all-cause mortality in COPD patients.MethodsWe analyzed data from the 1999–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. DI-GM score was calculated from 24-hour dietary recall. Primary outcomes were COPD prevalence and all-cause mortality risk in COPD patients. Multivariable logistic regression assessed the association between DI-GM and COPD prevalence, while Cox proportional hazards models evaluated all-cause mortality risk in COPD patients.ResultsThe prevalence of COPD was 6.87% among the 22,859 participants included. Compared to participants with DI-GM score of 0–3, the odds ratio (95% confidence interval) for DI-GM score of 4, 5, and ≥ 6 were 0.88 (0.70–1.11), 0.78 (0.64–0.97), and 0.75 (0.62–0.90), respectively. During a median follow-up time of 84 months, a total of 570 (28.15%) participants died among the 1,580 COPD participants. Compared to DI-GM score of 0–3, the hazard ratios (95% confidence intervals) for DI-GM score of 4, 5, and ≥ 6 were 0.78 (0.60–1.01), 0.63 (0.47–0.83), and 0.69 (0.56–0.85), respectively.ConclusionHigher DI-GM scores are significantly associated with both reduced COPD prevalence and improved survival in COPD patients. Our results suggest dietary modifications targeting gut microbiota may represent a novel strategy for COPD prevention and management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03908-2.Keywords: Dietary index for gut microbiota, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Mortality, NHANES, Prevalence

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a globally prevalent yet potentially preventable respiratory disorder, clinically characterized by wheezing, chronic cough and exertional dyspnea resulting from progressive airflow limitation and parenchymal lung tissue destruction. Data from the Global Burden of Disease Study reveal that although the prevalence of COPD has declined, it continues to impose a substantial public health burden, particularly in countries with a low sociodemographic index [1]. While COPD is currently recognized as a heterogeneous disease with multifactorial and incompletely understood pathophysiology, its development and progression may involve both genetic susceptibility and environmental exposures [2].

Emerging evidence points to the involvement of gut microbiota dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of COPD [3]. The gut microbiota, which is a complex ecological community comprising bacteria, fungi, archaea, and protists, plays a pivotal role in food digestion, metabolism, innate immunity, gut barrier integrity, and systemic homeostasis [4]. In experimental models, active smoking, a major etiological factor for COPD, has been shown to deplete potentially beneficial genera while enriching pathobionts [5]. Furthermore, Li et al. reported significant microbial dysbiosis in COPD patients relative to healthy controls, with distinct compositional shifts across disease severity stages [6]. Most compellingly, fecal microbiota transplantation in cigarette smoke-induced COPD mice ameliorated COPD hallmarks, whereas transfer of smoke-modified microbiota following antibiotic depletion exacerbated lung inflammation and impaired colonic immunity [7].

Although 16 S rRNA gene sequencing remains the gold standard technique for characterizing microbial changes in stool samples, its high cost and technical demands limit its practicality for large-scale epidemiologic studies [8]. By contrast, the dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) is an emerging, literature-derived metric that quantifies gut microbial diversity and function based on dietary patterns [9]. Given that diet represents the most accessible and potent modulator of gut microbiota, we hypothesized that higher DI-GM scores, indicating more favorable gut microbiota profiles, would be associated with lower COPD prevalence and mortality risk. To test this hypothesis, we first conducted a cross-sectional analysis using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data to examine associations between DI-GM scores and COPD prevalence in US adults. Subsequently, we performed a mortality follow-up study to evaluate whether higher DI-GM scores predicted improved survival in COPD patients.

Methods

Study population

The NHANES, conducted biennially by the National Center for Health Statistics, provides nationally representative health and nutritional data on the non-institutionalized US population. The survey protocol of NHANES was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent.

This study comprised two components: (1) a cross-sectional analysis assessing the association between DI-GM and COPD prevalence; and (2) a mortality follow-up study in COPD patients. Among the 36,252 participants aged over 40 years from 1999 to 2018 NHANES, we excluded: (1) individuals with pregnancy at the time of survey; (2) participants with missing dietary data or COPD status; (3) participants with implausibly high or low total energy intake (< 800 kcal/d or > 4200 kcal/d for men, and < 600 kcal/d or > 3500 kcal/d for women) [10]; (4) participants with incomplete covariate data. Finally, 22,859 participants (including 1,582 with COPD) were included for cross-sectional analysis. The number of participants and those with COPD in each individual cycle of NHANES were 1871 and 71 in 1999–2000 cycle, 2398 and 92 in 2001–2002 cycle, 2280 and 112 in 2003–2004 cycle, 2171 and 106 in 2005–2006 cycle, 2593 and 280 in 2007–2008 cycle, 2714 and 252 in 2009–2010 cycle, 2278 and 170 in 2011–2012 cycle, 2496 and 177 in 2013–2014 cycle, 2314 and 193 in 2015–2016 cycle, and 1744 and 129 in 2017–2018 cycle, respectively. For the mortality follow-up study, we focused exclusively on COPD patients and further excluded participants lacking follow-up data (n = 2). Figure 1 illustrates the participant selection process.

Participant selection flowchart for the cross-sectional analysis (n = 22,859) and mortality follow-up study (n = 1,580) using 1999–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data

DI-GM assessment

The DI-GM was constructed using the 24-hour dietary recall data from NHANES. According to previous reports [9, 11], we retrieved dietary data on 13 food items, including 9 beneficial components (avocado, coffee, cranberries, fermented dairy, broccoli, chickpeas, fibre, soybean, and whole grains) and 4 unfavorable components (whereas processed meat, refined grains, red meat, and high-fat diets). The sex-specific median of each food item was determined. For beneficial components, participants with dietary consumption above the sex-specific median were assigned a score of 1, whereas lower consumption were assigned a score of 0. Conversely, consumption above the sex-specific median for unfavorable items scored 0, while below-median consumption scored 1. The total score for DI-GM ranged from 0 to 13, with a higher score denoting more favorable gut microbial profiles.

COPD assessment

Similar to earlier studies in the NHANES population [12], COPD case definition was based on a composite of clinical and spirometric criteria that required fulfillment of at least one of the following criteria: (1) self-reported history of physician-diagnosed COPD, emphysema or chronic bronchitis; (2) a pre-bronchodilation forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity ratio < 0.70 on pulmonary function test; or (3) those aged over 40 years, with a history of smoking or chronic bronchitis, and are using COPD-related medications, including selective phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, mast cell stabilizers, leukotriene modulators, or inhaled corticosteroids.

Assessment of all-cause mortality

Mortality data were obtained from the National Death Index public-use linked mortality files, which provide vital status and cause of death information for NHANES participants. The follow-up time was calculated as the time interval between the physical examination date and the data of death or December 31, 2019, whichever came first [13].

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on established literature and clinical relevance, encompassing five domains: (1) demographics, including self-reported age, sex and race/ethnicity; (2) socioeconomic status, including poverty-income ratio, marital status (single defined as widowed, divorced, separated or never married), and education attainment; (3) lifestyle factors, including smoking, drinking, physical activity, and body mass index; (4) medical comorbidities, including hypertension (self-report, or use of anti-hypertensive medications, or measured blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg at the Mobile Examination Center), diabetes (self-report, or fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or 2-hour post-load glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL, or use of anti-diabetic medications) [14], cancer, dyslipidemia (total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL, or triglycerides ≥ 200 mg/dL, or low-density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥ 160 mg/dL, or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women) [15], and cardiovascular disease (self-reported congestive heart failure, stroke, angina, heart attack, and coronary artery disease); and (5) total energy intake. Definitions for smoking, drinking, and physical activity were the same as previously reported [16].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared across DI-GM score categories (0–3, 4, 5, ≥ 6) using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables or chi-squared test for categorical variables. For the cross-sectional analysis, we evaluated the association between DI-GM score and COPD prevalence using multivariable logistic regression. Results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) across three progressively adjusted models: an unadjusted crude model; Model 1 adjusted for demographics and socioeconomic status; and Model 2 additionally incorporating lifestyle factors, medical comorbidities, and total energy intake. For mortality analysis, we employed Cox proportional hazards models with identical adjustment strategies to generate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. Restricted cubic spline curves were generated to characterize the dose-response relationship between DI-GM score and COPD prevalence or mortality by adjusting for all covariates in Model 2. Subgroup analyses were done for stratification by age (< 60 vs. ≥ 60 years), sex (men vs. women), poverty-income ratio (< 1 vs. 1–3 vs. > 3), body mass index (< 25 vs. 25-29.99 vs. ≥ 30 kg/m2), diabetes (yes vs. no), cardiovascular disease (yes vs. no), and smoking status (never vs. former vs. current). We also conducted two sensitivity analyses to increase study robustness: (1) multiple imputation for missing values; (2) additional adjustment for synbiotics, probiotics, and prebiotics. A two-sided P value < 0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Results

Cross-sectional analysis

Comparison of baseline characteristics

There were 1,582 (6.87%) participants with COPD among the 22,859 individuals included. The overall and DI-GM-stratified participant characteristics were presented in Table 1. Compared to participants with DI-GM scores of 0–3, those with DI-GM scores of ≥ 6 were significantly older, more likely to be women and non-Hispanic white, more educated and economically advantaged, had lower body mass index and total dietary energy intake, were less likely to be current smokers and heavy drinkers, and had significantly lower comorbidities of hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hyperlipidemia, but increased prevalence of cancer.

Associations between DI-GM and COPD prevalence

Both continuous and categorical analyses demonstrated significant inverse associations between DI-GM scores and COPD prevalence (Table 2). In the fully adjusted Model 2, each 1-point increase in DI-GM score was associated with an 8% reduction in COPD risk. Compared to the reference group (DI-GM score of 0–3), participants with DI-GM scores of 4, 5, and ≥ 6 showed progressively lower risks of COPD, with adjusted ORs (95%CIs) of 0.88 (0.70–1.11), 0.78 (0.64–0.97), and 0.75 (0.62–0.90), respectively (P for trend = 0.002). The restricted cubic spline analysis (Fig. 2) confirmed a significant linear inverse relationship (P for non-linearity = 0.37).

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis by multiple imputation (Supplementary Table 1) consistently demonstrated that higher DI-GM scores remained significantly associated with reduced COPD prevalence risk. In another sensitivity analysis that further adjusted for probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics, the ORs (95% CIs) for the DI-GM scores of 0–3, 4, 5, and ≥ 6 were 1, 0.80 (0.58–1.10), 0.71 (0.53–0.95), and 0.75 (0.58–0.96), respectively (P for trend = 0.02).

Mortality follow-up study

Baseline characteristics

The comparison of baseline characteristics of the 1,580 COPD participants with available follow-up information stratified by the DI-GM score were presented in Supplementary Table 2. Compared with those with a DI-GM score of 0–3, those with a DI-GM score of ≥ 6 were less likely to be non-Hispanic blacks, more educated, economically advantaged, were less likely to be current smokers and heavy drinkers, and had less comorbid cardiovascular disease.

Associations between DI-GM score and all-cause mortality

Over a median follow-up time of 84 (interquartile range 43–128) months, a total of 570 (28.15%) deaths occurred. The mortality rate was highest in the DI-GM scores of 0–3 group (38.84%, 202/446), followed by 27.43% (133/363) for DI-GM scores of 4, 24.56% (113/375) for DI-GM scores of 5, and lowest in the DI-GM scores ≥ 6 group (21.43%, 122/396). As summarized in Table 3, the fully-adjusted Model 2 indicated that compared to DI-GM of 0–3, the HRs (95% CIs) for those with a DI-GM of 4, 5, and ≥ 6 were 0.78 (0.60–1.01), 0.63 (0.47–0.83), and 0.69 (0.56–0.85), respectively. The restricted cubic spline curves (Fig. 4) demonstrated an inversely linear relationship between DI-GM score and risk of all-cause mortality.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation yielded consistent results (Supplementary Table 3). After further adjustment for probiotic, prebiotic, and synbiotic use in Model 2, the HRs (95% CIs) for all-cause mortality were 0.87 (0.60–1.25) for DI-GM score 4, 0.82 (0.54–1.25) for score 5, and 0.69 (0.49–0.96) for score ≥ 6 (P for trend = 0.03).

Discussion

In this comprehensive investigation combining cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, we have demonstrated that adherence to a gut microbiota-friendly dietary pattern, as quantified by the DI-GM score, is significantly associated with both reduced prevalence of COPD and lower all-cause mortality among COPD patients in the United States. Our findings revealed a consistent linear dose-response relationship between higher DI-GM scores and COPD prevalence and mortality, with particularly robust protective effects observed in individuals without pre-existing CVD. Collectively, these findings underscored DI-GM score as a valuable tool for the individualized dietary management for the prevention of COPD and mitigation of its mortality risk.

The observed associations between DI-GM and COPD prevalence and mortality risk align with and extend previous research in several important ways. The DI-GM, first conceptualized by Kase and colleagues in 2024, represents an innovative approach to quantifying dietary patterns based on their potential to influence gut microbial composition [9]. Our results complement growing evidence that DI-GM may serve as a valuable predictor for various chronic conditions, including metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease [17], chronic kidney disease [18], constipation [19], stroke [20], diabetes [21], depression [22], kidney stone [23], female infertility [24], and sarcopenia [25]. The protective association we observed is biologically plausible given the established role of individual DI-GM components - such as coffee, dietary fiber, and whole grains - in promoting respiratory health through their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [26, 27]. It has been demonstrated that higher adherence to a healthy dietary pattern rich in fruits, nuts, whole grains and vegetables may help to prevent COPD or reduce mortality, while unhealthy dietary patterns characterized by sweets, saturated fats and processed met may increase the risk of COPD or negatively impact its long-term survival [28]. Consumption of avocado, dairy products, and fiber has also been associated with reduced risk of mortality in the general population [29–31].

The connection between the gut microbiome and COPD prevalence can be potentially explained by the complex gut-lung axis interactions. First, the beneficial microbial communities fostered by a higher DI-GM diet may contribute to maintaining intestinal barrier integrity [32], thus preventing translocation of bacterial products that could trigger pulmonary inflammation. Second, microbial-derived metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids produced from dietary fiber fermentation, have been shown to mitigate airway inflammation and improve COPD symptoms in animal models [33]. Third, gut microbiota composition significantly influences systemic immune regulation, particularly the balance between pro-inflammatory Th17 cells and anti-inflammatory Treg cells [34], a critical factor implicated in the pathogenesis of COPD [35].

Of note, our mortality findings represent a particular novel contribution to the current literature that is predominantly cross-sectional in design and no prior studies have examined DI-GM in relation to survival outcomes among COPD patients. The observed 31% reduction in mortality risk between the DI-GM score ≥ 6 and 0–3 suggests that dietary patterns supporting gut microbial health may have profound implications for COPD prognosis. Mechanistically, DI-GM could influence COPD prognosis through multiple pathways. First, higher adherence to DI-GM is associated with improved cardiometabolic profiles, including better glycemic control and lipid metabolism. This is consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that a higher DI-GM score correlates with a reduced risk of CVD [36]. Given that CVD is a leading comorbidity and a major contributor to mortality in COPD, the cardioprotective effects may partly explain the observed survival advantage for a higher DI-GM. A lower DI-GM score may also relate to low-grade systemic inflammation, a well-established predictor of mortality in COPD [37]. Importantly, inflammation could also serve as a mediator in the relationship between DI-GM and frailty, a critical geriatric syndrome in COPD. A meta-analysis of 25 studies found that COPD patients with frailty face a 3.21-fold higher mortality risk than non-frail individuals [38]. Thus, the inverse relationship between DI-GM and systemic inflammation may mitigate frailty progression and improve survival. Third, emerging evidence has linked a lower DI-GM to insulin resistance [39] and accelerated cognitive decline [40], both of which are independent risk factors for mortality in chronic diseases.

The differential effect between DI-GM score and COPD prevalence observed in CVD subgroups warrants careful consideration. The attenuated benefit among participants with existing CVD may reflect more severe underlying gut dysbiosis or metabolic disturbances. For instance, studies have demonstrated that microbiota metabolites short-chain fatty acids have positive effects on the cardiovascular system [41]. Conversely, chronic CVD is associated with persistent microbiota-derived metabolite imbalances, such as reduced short-chain fatty acids and elevated trimethylamine N-oxid, making dietary modification of gut microbiota difficult [42]. This result highlights the critical window for dietary intervention in COPD management and underscores the need for integrated, multi-modal therapies in this patient population.

Our study findings carry several clinical implications: (1) The DI-GM’s practicality enables feasible risk assessment in routine primary care visits, potentially allowing earlier identification of high-risk patients before significant lung function decline occurs. (2) Our results suggested for incorporating gut microbiota-targeted nutritional therapies into COPD management paradigms, either through personalized dietary modifications, probiotic supplementation, or combined interventions.

Several methodological strengths and advantages enhance the validity of our findings, including the use of a nationally representative data, long-term mortality follow-up time, and comprehensive adjustments for known potential confounders. Most importantly, we also adjusted for probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics from the dietary supplements, given that previous reports have suggested probiotics and prebiotics are beneficial for preventing COPD [43]. The consistency of results across multiple sensitivity analyses further supports the robustness of our conclusions.

Certain limitations of this study, however, should also be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of prevalence analysis precludes causal inference, and residual confounding may persist as the DI-GM score likely reflects broader patterns of health-conscious behaviors beyond diet alone (e.g., preventive healthcare use, sleep quality, or unmeasured aspects of dietary quality). Second, reliance on self-reported dietary and COPD status data may introduce measurement error. Prospective studies incorporating objective measures of lung function and microbiome analysis in the future are needed to verify our findings and generalize the conclusion. Third, while we collected cause-of-death information, the number of events for specific mortality causes remained insufficient for meaningful stratified analysis. This limitation precludes definitive conclusions about whether the observed mortality benefits reflect specific pathway effects (e.g., cardiovascular protection) versus global health benefits. Larger prospective studies with adequate power for cause-specific mortality analysis are needed to address this important question. Finally, the DI-GM’s focus on 13 dietary components may not capture the full spectrum of foods influencing gut microbiota composition.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this nationally representative study provides preliminary evidence that adherence to a gut microbiota-supportive diet, as measured by the DI-GM score, is significantly associated with both reduced COPD prevalence and lower all-cause mortality among US adults. Our findings demonstrate a particularly strong protective effect of higher DI-GM scores against COPD development in individuals without pre-existing CVD, suggesting this population may derive special benefit from dietary modifications targeting gut microbial health. Future research should prioritize prospective cohort studies with objective dietary assessments to establish the temporal relationships between DI-GM and COPD risk and mortality. It is also interesting to assess whether DI-GM-based dietary interventions in high-risk populations would decrease COPD risk and mortality and the underlying mechanisms.
