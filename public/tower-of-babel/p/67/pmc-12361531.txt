High-precision transdermal drug delivery enabled by a dual-pump parallel valveless piezoelectric micropump integrated with hollow microneedles
Authors: Lei Zhang, Wei Sha, Yi Li, Xin Chen, Lulu Liu, Hong Hu, Jian Song, Jie Liang, Jianhua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransdermal drug delivery (TDD) system based on piezoelectric micropumps is a novel route of drug delivery that is wearable and highly patient-compliant. The advantage of valveless micropumps, such as their simple structure, high reliability and low cost, have been investigated in comparison to valve-based micropumps. However, the flow pulsation and backflow effect of valveless pump can reduce the controllability and precision of the system. Here, a TDD wearable device integrated with a dual-pump parallel valveless piezoelectric micropump and hollow microneedle array is presented, which is fabricated by means of 3D printing technology. The simulation analysis and experimental results indicate that the dual-pump parallel micropump with asynchronous driving can achieve a full forward flow and reduces the influence of flow pulsation on the system’s stability, showing the excellent controllability and high precision of the device. The output flow rate and pressure of the micropump can be controlled by the driving signal, which are about 1.61 mL/min and 700 Pa with the sine driving signal of 40 V, 600 Hz. Through animal experiments, we demonstrate that the device can successfully deliver insulin to mice and control blood glucose. In conclusion, this TDD device which potentially provides low-cost and high-precision results than existing technologies, shows considerable promise in wearable drug delivery applications, especially in managing chronic diseases that require long-term treatment. Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering; Environmental, health and safety issues

Introduction

The increasing number of patients with chronic diseases, such as diabetes, require regular self-administration of therapeutics over a long period of time. However, the pain and inconvenience caused by the traditional self-injection regimens can lead to high patient non-adherence1,2. The Transdermal drug delivery (TDD) system is an attractive novel method of releasing drugs due to the advantages of painless, easy administration and no first-pass metabolism in the liver3.

The micropump, as an option for driving elements in TDD system, can accurately transport microfluids, such as liquid drugs, over a wide range of backpressure3. Compared to the driving mechanisms of traditional TDD systems, micropumps demonstrate significant advantages. They not only efficiently, accurately, and reliably deliver drugs from reservoirs to target sites but also possess the capability to deliver high molecular weight and high-viscosity drugs. Coupled with the precise dose control capability of micropumps, the TDD system brings tremendous convenience to self-treatment for patients with chronic conditions4, particularly in the precise delivery of insulin for daily diabetes management5.

Various micropumps based on different actuation and pumping mechanisms have been reported, such as piezoelectric (PZT), electrothermal, electro-osmotic, and shape memory alloy6–9. In particular, PZT micropumps have garnered extensive attention in the field of drug delivery due to their simple structure, good stability, high control accuracy, and low power consumption10–12. The valveless micropump, without complex valve structures, has good durability and high reliability13,14 for drug delivery, have been widely reported6,15,16. Since the directional flow of a valveless micropump is generated by the flow resistance difference, there will be the backflow effect which can reduce the stability of the system. The methods to control the backflow effect have studied17,18. Chen et al. presented a valveless PZT micropump and highlighted the methods to control the pumping performance and backflow effect, by controlling excitation and optimizing the structure sizes19. As far as we know, the valveless PZT micropump with a full forward flow is yet to be reported.

Due to the barrier function of skin, it is difficult to deliver the high molecular weight drugs20. The hollow microneedles can deliver the relatively large doses of high molecular weight drugs directly to the dermis through hollow flow passages21,22 and the release of the drugs to be very precisely controlled over time23,24. The operational mechanism of hollow microneedles makes them highly suitable for integration into micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS), particularly in drug delivery systems where micropumps serve as the driving components. Several studies have reported the combination of micropumps and hollow microneedles25–28, which is easy to self-administer and carry, permits real-time, controllable, and high-precision modulation of flow rate and pressure.

Here, we propose a parallel valveless PZT micropump achieving full forward flow, integrated with hollow microneedles as a controllable, high-precision TDD device. The 3D finite element method models of the micropumps and the hollow microneedle array are established using COMSOL Multiphysics software. On this basis, the pump body and a hollow microneedle array are successfully fabricated using 3D printing and biocompatible materials. To realize such effects, a novel dual-pump parallel structure with asynchronous driving is adopted. A test platform is set up to investigate the output flow rate of the prototype micropumps and its relation to the backpressure. The high-speed camera captures the full forward flow driven by the proposed structure, which is highly consistent with the simulation analysis. In addition, we perform animal experiments of insulin delivery in normal mice and diabetes mice to verify the feasibility of the device in controllable, high-precision TDD system.

Results and discussion

Micropump structure and working principle

Figure 1a shows the overall structure of proposed valveless PZT micropump integrated with hollow microneedles. The device consists of two PZT actuators, a pump body with nozzle/diffuser structures as two inlets and an outlet, and a 7 × 7 hollow microneedle array. The PZT actuator, made by a PZT ceramic (lead zirconate titanate PZT-5H) bonded to a copper substrate using conducting adhesive, is the driving element of the PZT pump. A significant feature of the device is the dual-pump parallel structure. The inlets of two pump chambers are independent, but outlets are connected in parallel through internal flow channels and lead to the hollow microneedle array.

.

Overall structure and working principle. a Schematic of the valveless PZT micropump integrated with hollow microneedles. b The operation mode of parallel micropump

The nozzle/diffuser structure is rectifying structure of valveless pumps. The flow resistance in nozzle direction is higher, while the flow resistance in diffuser direction is relatively low29. Under the driving of a sine signal, when the PZT actuator moves downward, micropump works in the pump mode, causing the fluid within the chamber to be expelled through both the inlet and outlet. Owing to the disparity in flow resistance imparted by the nozzle/diffuser structures, the outflow volume at the outlet exceeds that at the inlet. Similarly, when the PZT actuator moves upward, the micropump works in the supply mode. At this time, the inflow volume from the inlet into the pump chamber is greater than that from the outlet. The micropump cycles between the pump mode and the supply mode to achieve net flow volume from the inlet to the outlet.

The proposed TDD device is driven by two valveless PZT micropumps in asynchronous mode. As shown in Fig. 1b, the two micropumps are driven by a set of sine signals with a phase difference of 180° (Fig. S1) and are in pump mode and supply mode, respectively. At this point, the output flow rate of micropump B exceeds the backflow rate of micropump A. When the two micropumps circulate in two modes, the output flow rate of one micropump is always greater than the backflow rate of the other micropump. For the whole device, the combined action of the two micropumps reduces the backflow effect at the outlet.

Flow characteristics of dual-pump parallel structure

Based on the above analysis and the working principle shown in Fig. 1b, a model of the dual-pump parallel valveless PZT micropump is established as shown in Fig. S4. In the case, the phase difference of the driving signals for the two micropumps is set to four groups of 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°. Figure 3a and b show the fluid flow streamlines field inside the parallel micropumps and the respective working modes when the phase difference is 180°. Micropump A and micropump B are in the pump mode and the supply mode, respectively. The fluid flow streamlines field shows that the output flow volume at the main outlet is always positive. The real-time output flow volumes of the parallel micropumps under four different conditions are calculated. It can be seen that when the phase differences are 0°, 90°, and 270° (Fig. 3c), the real-time output flow volumes of the parallel micropumps are a fluctuation wave. When the phase difference is set to 180° (Fig. 3c), that is, two inverted sine signals drive asynchronously. At this point, the real-time output flow volume increases monotonically. Figure 3d shows that the input flow volumes of the two micropumps exhibit an anti-phase characteristic during each driving cycle, meaning that when one reaches its peak, the other attains its trough. Furthermore, the fluctuation amplitudes of the two curves are approximately identical, yet they are staggered along the time axis, demonstrating the existence of a 180° phase difference. This phase difference results in a monotonically increasing output flow rate, while the phase differences in other groups do not exhibit such a characteristic (Fig. S5). The dual-pump parallel structure with asynchronous driving achieves efficient full forward flow at the main outlet. Here, full forward flow is defined as the output fluid of a micropump flowing only in the forward direction. The flow velocity may change, but the flow direction remains positive.

Devices fabricating and experimental results

The designed 7 × 7 hollow microneedle array is made of a biocompatible resin material using 3D printing technology. The microscope images reveal that sharp and uniform microneedle tips are successfully fabricated and the microneedles are completely hollow as shown in Fig. 4a, b. The diameter of the hollow microneedle’s channel is 100 μm, and the wall thickness is 80 μm. The microneedle is conical in shape with a bottom diameter of 370 μm, and the length is 800 μm. The spacing between microneedles is approximately 1 mm, which is large enough to prevent the ‘bed of nails’ effect30. A simulation model is established to test fluid flow within the microneedle array. The properties of the resin material are assigned to the structure of the microneedle array, and the fluid channel is set to laminar flow. The inlet pressure of the array is set to 700 Pa and the outlet pressure is set to 0 Pa. The streamline fluid distribution inside the 7 × 7 hollow microneedle array is shown in Fig. 4c. The simulation results show that the fluid flows uniformly towards the hollow channels of all microneedles in the array. In addition, the mechanical analysis of microneedle is also simulated for inserting into the skin (Fig. 4d). A pressure of 3.183 MPa is applied to the tip of the microneedle, which theoretically allows the microneedle to penetrate the skin31. The simulation results show that the Von Mises stress inside the microneedle array is mainly found at the needle tip and microneedle base, and the maximum stress is 31.6 MPa. Referring to the properties of used resin materials, there is no risk of damage or breakage when microneedles insert into the skin. And the deformation of needle tip is very small, with a maximum deformation of about 0.5 μm. This will reduce the patient’s pain and skin damage.

An experiment has been performed using Parafilm® film as a skin model for microneedle insertion into skin32. The hollow microneedles should be able to provide access to at least 200 μm below the surface of the skin, so that the drug can diffuse towards the systemic circulation33. In this work, 8 layers films, with thickness of 127 μm per layer, are used to simulate insertion into skin. The films, which the hollow microneedle array is manually inserted into, are examined as shown in Figs. 4e and S6. It can be seen that the first layer of the film was completely punctured. The fourth layer of the film shows signs of pressure being applied, but it was not completely punctured, indicating a maximum insertion depth of about 400 μm. After this test, the hollow microneedle array has no obvious damage, as shown in Fig. S7. This verifies that the hollow microneedle array can be applied in practical transdermal drug delivery.

Based on simulation analysis, a prototype of the dual-pump parallel valveless PZT micropump is fabricated. The pump body is also fabricated using 3D printing technology, as shown in Fig. 5a. The pump body with the pump chamber and nozzle/diffuser structure is directly fabricated without further processing. The PZT actuator and pump body are bonded to each other using epoxy resin glue to fabricate the prototype micropump. A prototype micropump designed for testing flow performance is shown in Fig. 5b, with the overall size of 28 × 21 × 3 mm.

The electric field strength applied to the PZT ceramic is a key parameter for studying the relationship between the driving signal and the performance of the micropump. And the electric field strength should not exceed a range of threshold value (400-500 V/mm) to avoid a decrease of the polarization intensity of the PZT ceramic over time (depolarization). Considering the thickness of the PZT layer to be 200 μm, the voltage of the driving signal should not exceed 60 V. According to Fig. 5c, the output flow rate of the micropump increases and decreases with the increase of frequency. The maximum flow rate occurs in the range of 600–700 Hz, and the flow rate decreases after exceeding 700 Hz. When the frequency is set to 600 Hz, different voltages (20–100 V) of the driving signal are applied. The results show that the maximum output flow rate increases with the increase of voltage (Fig. 5d). Based on the above analysis and considering the safe voltage of TDD systems, a sine driving signal of 40 V and 600 Hz was selected. Under this condition, the output flow rate of the micropump can reach 1.61 mL/min. Figure 5e shows the relationship between the driving voltage and the output pressure. As the driving voltage increases, the output pressure shows a linear growth trend. Under the set driving signal, the output pressure can reach about 705 Pa. The output flow rate of the micropump under different backpressure is shown in Fig. 5f. The output flow rate decreases with the increase of backpressure. When the backpressure is about 705 Pa, the output flow rate tends to zero. The TDD system should be able to drive flow under a backpressure of at least 400 Pa generated by skin resistance34. According to Fig. 5f, the output flow rate of the micropump under 400 Pa backpressure is about 0.76 mL/min. Therefore, the operation of TDD devices can be controlled based on the test results. When the driving signal is fixed, different administration durations are set based on the output flow rate of the micropump to achieve control over the drug dosage that TDD device transdermal delivers.

As shown in Fig. S9, when the driving voltage is low, the output flow rate of a single micropump differs significantly from that of a dual-pump parallel micropump due to the flow resistance generated by the pump chamber and tube. When the driving voltage is high, the output flow rate of the dual-pump parallel micropump cannot reach twice that of a single micropump, indicating that the output flow rate of the dual-pump parallel structure with asynchronous drive is lost compared to the theoretical flow rate.

Animal experiments

In order to verify the feasibility of the device in actual TDD application, based on the analysis of the above experimental part, in vivo experiments are carried out using mice. In the experiment, the ability of the device to administer insulin in vivo is studied using mice whose blood glucose concentration disturbed by glucose14 and the diabetic mouse models36. The blood glucose value of normal mice is about 6.5 mmol/L. The model mice are cultured on a high-fat and high-sugar diet to induce diabetes (hyperglycemia). And the blood glucose value of the diabetic mice is about 14 mmol/L. The experimental mice are divided into untreated group and test group. The test group is given insulin by the device, and the untreated group is given the same amount of normal saline. First, the hair on the back of a mouse is shaved to expose the blank application site for the device (Fig. S12). The device is then manually pressed on the surface of the mouse skin, and the microneedles are ensured to be inserted into the skin (Fig. 7c). And the device is delivering insulin to mice. The drug dose is set to 0.5 IU/kg according to the weight of the mice. After the treatment removing the device, as shown in Fig. 7d, the pinholes on the mouse skin can be observed, but there is no obvious bleeding.

Figure 7e shows the experimental results of mice whose blood glucose concentration disturbed. In the experiment, blood glucose values are measured every 10 min. At the 10th minute, mice in the test group and the untreated group are all injected with 2 g/kg glucose to disturb the blood glucose concentration. At the same time, the devices of the test group are started to administer insulin. It can be observed that the blood glucose values of the two groups increase rapidly after the injection of glucose and then decrease gradually. The decrease of blood glucose in the untreated group is due to the normal secretion of insulin. After transdermal injection of mice in the test group, the insulin forms a drug chamber in viable epidermis and then passively diffuses to the systemic circulation. This accelerates the process of blood glucose value recovery. Therefore, the blood glucose value of mice in the test group returns to the normal level in 80 min, which is less than that in the untreated group in 120 min. Figure 7f shows the experimental results of diabetic mouse model. The devices started to inject normal saline and insulin into the two groups of mice, respectively. Blood glucose values are measured every 20 min. The blood glucose values of mice in the untreated group do not change significantly. The blood glucose values of the mice in the test group rapidly decrease and reach the lowest level after 1 h, that is, the normal blood glucose concentration. After that, the blood glucose value is measured every 1 h, and it is observed that the blood glucose values of mice in the test group increase slowly. Because the insulin in the drug chamber is gradually absorbed, and less and less. The above experimental results show that the TDD device successfully delivers insulin to mice and controls blood glucose.

Conclusion

We have presented a valveless PZT micropump integrated with hollow microneedles that can be used in TDD system. A 3D simulation model of valveless PZT micropumps has been established. The flow characteristics, especially the backflow characteristics, of a valveless piezoelectric micropump are studied. The parameters of 3D nozzle/diffuser structure are analyzed to select parameters with better output performance and lower backflow rate. Moreover, the results support that the proposed dual-pump parallel structure with asynchronous driving can achieve a full forward flow. And a 3D simulation model of a hollow microneedle array has been established to verified the uniformity of fluid flow inside the array and the mechanical properties of microneedles. The pump body and microneedle array are fabricated using 3D printing technology and biocompatible resin material. The test results of the device show that the output flow rate of the micropump is about 1.61 mL/min with a sine driving signal of 40 V, 600 Hz. Under a back pressure of 400 Pa, the micropump can still drive fluid flow, with an output flow rate of about 0.8 mL/min. Using of a high-speed camera module, it has been verified that the parallel valveless PZT micropumps with asynchronous driving can achieve full forward flow. In animal experiments, the device successfully delivers insulin to mice and controls blood glucose, verifying the feasibility of applying the device for TDD system. In summary, it provides a reference for the application of the device in wearable drug delivery systems. Compared with other reported piezoelectric micropumps integrated with microneedles, this device has the advantages of well controlled, low driving voltage, high-precision, and high stability. Before further practical application, we will continue to reduce the size of the device. Combining more compact power and control chips, as well as flexible PCB technology, to enhance the application potential of the device in flexible wearable TDD system.

Materials and methods

Simulation model establishment and calculation

The simplified 3D model includes a PZT actuator structure and a fluid domain. The fluid phase is set to water with the material properties defined by the software. The PZT actuator structure consists of a copper substrate and a PZT layer (PZT-5H). The bonding layers between the PZT layer and the copper substrate, as well as between the PZT actuator and the pump body, are ignored in the model. In addition, the pump chamber has also been simplified to a cuboid shape. When setting up the model, the substrate surface of the PZT actuator is set as a fixed constraint. One side of the PZT layer is applied with a driving signal, and the other side is set to ground. The contact surface of the PZT actuator and the fluid domain is the pair of fluid-structure interaction. The inlet and outlet channels of the micropump are designed in the shape of the nozzle/diffuser structure to provide a rectification function. The nozzle/diffuser structure as the inlet is consistent with the parameters as the outlet. The pressure conditions of the inlet and outlet are set to zero, and the inhibit reflux is unchecked to reduce interference with the functionality of the nozzle/diffuser structure. In order to conserve the computing resource for the simulation, considering the symmetry of the model structure, the simulation model is simplified by 3D XZ axis symmetry. The simplified model has approximately 360,000 meshes with an average element quality of about 0.68, and the relative tolerance is set as 0.001. When the step size is set as 0.025/Frequency, it takes approximately 25 min to calculate the steady state for 6 cycles (2 × Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8358 CPU @ 2.60 GHz, 512 GB RAM).

Device fabrication and test

The designed 7 × 7 hollow microneedle array is made of a biocompatible resin material using 3D printing technology (microArch® S130, Boston Micro Fabrication). Properties of the biocompatible resin material

The pump body of the micropump is also fabricated using biocompatible resin through 3D printing technology. The piezoelectric disc is made of lead zirconate titanate (PZT-5H) ceramic material, with silver electrodes plated on both sides. After polarization and aging treatment, it is attached to a copper substrate using conductive adhesive, forming the PZT actuator (Keyisheng, China). The diameter of the piezoelectric disc is 10 mm, and its thickness is 0.2 mm. The diameter of the copper substrate is 12 mm, and its thickness is 0.13 mm. Finally, apply resin glue to the mounting area for the PZT actuator on the pump body, and wait for the glue to cure for 48 hours after installing the PZT actuator. The same process applies to the installation of the hollow microneedle array.

The tests include the relationship between output flow rate, back pressure, and sine driving signal. The experiment is carried out at room temperature, and delivery medium is pure water. The experimental platform is shown in Fig. S8. The driving signal is generated by a signal generator (UTG2025A, UNI-T, China) and amplified by a power amplifier (ATA-2161, Aigtek, China). The instruments used in the experiment also include a microscope, electronic balance, etc. There are two methods for measuring the output flow rate of a micropump. It can be calculated by weighing the mass of fluid output by the micropump per unit time, or by measuring the distance of fluid movement in the outlet tube per unit time and then calculating the output flow rate based on the tube diameter. The measurement method for the output pressure of a micropump is to fix a sufficiently long outlet tube in the vertical direction and calculate the output pressure based on the equation P=ρgh, where h is the difference between the maximum height of the output fluid in the vertical outlet tube and the pump chamber.

In order to verify the full forward flow illustrated by the simulation results, experiments are carried out using the prototype micropump. The drive signal is set to 100 V, 600 Hz. The module is composed of high-speed camera (SH6, SSZN, China), microscope lens (NAVITAR, USA), LED lamp (EF-220, JIN, China), and PC. The camera has a shutter speed of 20 μs and a frame rate of 10,000 FPS.

Mice experiment

To carry out the mice experiments, male C57 mice (Chengdu GemPharmatech Biotechnology, China) with weighs of ~40 g and aged six weeks were used. To establish the diabetic mouse models, the mice were first allowed to acclimate for one week before being subjected to a high-sugar, high-fat diet with ad libitum access to food. On the 10th day, the fasting blood glucose was measured through the tail vein blood to determine whether the mice reached the state of diabetes. Mice with stable blood glucose levels above 11.1 mmol/L were considered successfully modeled for diabetes. Unsuitable mice were excluded from the experiment. Prior to conducting experiments with the device, both normal and diabetic mice were fasted and anesthetized. Before attaching the device to the mice, the hair on the back of a mouse was shaved, and the blank application site was cleaned. During the experiments, the insulin dosage was set at 0.5 IU/kg based on the mice’s weight, while the glucose dosage used to disrupt blood glucose levels in normal mice was 2 g/kg.
