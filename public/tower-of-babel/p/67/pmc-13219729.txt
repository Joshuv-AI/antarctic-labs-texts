Polarized dislocation covalent organic frameworks ignite hot electrons to propel photocatalytic hydrogen generation
Authors: Can Huang, Yingyin Mo, Ruiqi Gao, Rongchen Shen, Guijie Liang, Peng Zhang, Xin Li, Qihua Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Covalent organic frameworks are promising photocatalysts for H2 generation; however, their efficiency is limited by energy dissipation arising from hot electron loss and carrier recombination. In this work, we introduce interlayer dislocation and polarization in covalent organic frameworks to extend hot electron lifetime to 37.1 ± 2.0 ps, activating a photocatalytic H2 generation rate of 429.4 ± 3.7 mmol g−1 h−1 and apparent quantum yield of 85 ± 1% at 420 nm. Ultrafast spectroscopy and theoretical calculation elucidate the underlying mechanisms of hot electron production: interlayer dislocations induce energy-band anti-crossing at the K point in the Brillouin zone, suppressing acoustic-phonon scattering to contribute 8.4 times extension of both hot and cooled electrons; polarization effects reduce exciton dissociation and carrier recombination, further increasing carrier and optical-phonon concentration to achieve hot-phonon bottleneck and 180 times of hot electron duration. This study validates hot-electron-enhanced photocatalytic hydrogen generation and offers a general strategy for manipulating hot electrons in two-dimensional photocatalysts.

Introduction

Photocatalytic hydrogen (H2) production provides a clean energy solution by utilizing water and solar energy to ensure the sustainable development1. Various photocatalysts, such as CdS, ZnInS4, carbon nitride, hydrogen-bonded organic frameworks (HOFs), and covalent organic frameworks (COFs), have been widely explored for efficient H2 generation1–5. Compared to inorganic photocatalysts, organic photocatalysts exhibit narrow bandgaps with remarkable visible-light absorbance, but their relatively low crystallinity limits operational durability. COFs, orderly assembled through covalent bonds between adjustable building blocks, feature high crystalline, diverse porous structures, and efficient photon collection, emerging as competitive photocatalysts for H2 generation6,7. Typically, COFs convert incident photons into electrons (e−) and holes (h+), which subsequently migrate through the framework to catalyze interfacial redox reactions. However, the strong Coulomb interaction between e− and h+ in COFs results in a high exciton binding energy (Eb) which hinders free carrier production and utilization8–10. Previous studies on COF-based photocatalysts have primarily focused on topological connectivity, donor-acceptor architectures, and heterojunction construction to enhance light absorption and internal electric fields, thereby improving photon utilization and decreasing Eb to indirectly promote carrier production6,7,11–13. Nevertheless, direct manipulating the energy-band structure and elucidating its relationship to carrier behavior in COFs remain scarce.

Tuning strategies for two-dimensional topological materials have been experimentally reported to introduce energy band anti-crossing, particularly in few-layer magic-angle graphene system14–16. Theoretical studies indicate that symmetry breaking can eliminate symmetry-protected energy-band crossing by opening Dirac point or Weyl point to form a bandgap. Additionally, constructing interlayer heterogeneous components can spatially redistribute electron-hole populations near conduction band minimum (CBM) and valence band maximum (VBM) to regulate their excitation state and carrier properties17,18. Therefore, two-dimensional COFs, with constrained intralayer carrier transport due to their in-plane conjugated structure and flat band feature along vertical momentum path, are promising platform photocatalysts for regulating energy-band structure and investigating photo-generated carrier dynamics19.

Notably, high-energy photons from the continuous solar spectrum can directly stimulate valence band (VB) electrons into high-energy conduction band (CB), producing non-equilibrium hot carriers with high energy and mobility, which offers a potential route for efficient H2 generation20–22. However, non-equilibrium hot carriers rapidly lose their excess energy through multiple carrier-phonon interactions (i.e., cooling), relaxing to the band edge to reach thermal equilibrium state, which causes intrinsic energy dissipation often overlooked in photocatalytic process20,23. Since every hot carrier cooling step involving phonon emission must obey energy conservation, each carrier transition to a lower energy band requires a coupled phonon with energy matching the interband gap. Therefore, designing discrete energy band structures can suppress rapid inter-band relaxation of hot carriers by blocking low-energy acoustic phonon coupling20,24. However, the high symmetry in two-dimensional COFs typically results in multiple energy-band degenerate and crossing, thereby accelerating hot carrier cooling25. Therefore, precisely regulating energy-band configuration in COFs can justifiably activate photo-generated hot carriersto decrease energy dissipation during photocatalysis process, further identifying the rationale behind structure-dependent hot carrier production, relaxation, and utilization.

Herein, we design two types dislocated COFs (dlCOF) on a substrate of well-stacked cyano-based COF (CN-COF) via two-step template method, both the single-component dlCOF (CN-dlCOF) and methoxy-substituted bi-component dlCOF (CN/OCH3-dlCOF) exhibit obvious hot electron (HE) production and photocatalytic performance improvement. Theoretical and experimental research prove that interlayer dislocation breaks the intrinsic COF symmetry to produce optical-phonon and reduce acoustic-phonon coupling; while polarization field and orbital reorganization enhances carrier separation and transport efficiency to achieve enough carrier and optical-phonon concentration. Eventually, this synergistic effect triggers the hot-phonon bottleneck and produces long-lived hot electron in CN/OCH3-dlCOF. The symmetry and orbital regulation offers an effiective approach for hot electron utilization and thus propels the efficient photocatalytic H2 generation.

Results

Synthesis and structural characterization

Two-dimensional cyano-group-based COFs have been reported for efficient photocatalytic hydrogen generation, benefiting from strong in-plane donor-acceptor effect; however, their out-plane interactions between adjacent layers remain unclear26. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, we propose a template method based on well-stacked cyano-substituted COFs (CN-COF) synthesized via a Schiff-base condensation of 2,4,6-triformylphloroglucinol and 4,4’-diamino-[1,1’-biphenyl]-3,3’-dicarbonitrile (see details in Methods). This method employs the well-stacked CN-COF as a template (67 mol.% in this work, unless otherwise specified), utilizing higher acetic acid concentration and reaction temperature to control the secondary condensation for building dislocated layer structures. Notably, formation energy calculated by density functional theory (DFT) proves that CN-COF in AA stacking with larger interlayer π-π overlapping is energistically favorable than that in AB stacking (ΔE = +27.2 kcal mol−1) and ABC stacking (ΔE = +28.9 kcal mol−1), indicating that thermodynamically unfavorable dislocation structures are synthesized under kinetics control (Figure S1). Depending on the monomers used in secondary condensation, this process yields dislocated layers from the same component as CN-COF (CN-dlCOF) or from the component including methoxyl-group (CN/OCH3-dlCOF). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy evidences the β-ketoenamine linkages and cyano-groups in CN-COF, CN-dlCOF, and CN/OCH3-dlCOF (Figure S2a). 13C cross-polarization magic-angle-spinning solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance confirms the specific chemical structures of CN-COF, CN-dlCOF, and CN/OCH3-dlCOF (Figure S2b). Powder X-ray diffraction pattern of CN-COF is consistent with the AA stacking model, as simulated by Pawley refinement (Rwp = 2.74%, Rp = 2.13%), confirming the layered and ordered-stacking template (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Data 1). In CN-dlCOF and CN/OCH3-dlCOF, peaks at 3.5° and 6.0° correspond to (100) and (110) planes matched to the AA stacking of CN-COF template. The intensified peak at 6.0°corresponds to the (110) plane from AB stacking, while the additional peaks at 5.3° and 9.2° are indexed to the (110) and (101) planes from ABC stacking, as identified by reference samples prepared by a grinding method (Figure S3). The crystalline structure evolution proves that bottom-up approach can introduce various stacking mode on well-stacked template and create the interlayer misalignment. N2 adsorption/desorption measurements exhibit high Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) specific surface areas of CN-COF (1172.8 m2 g−1), CN-dlCOF (718.2 m2 g−1), and CN/OCH3-dlCOF (721.4 m2 g−1), indicating the successful synthesis of porous framework structures (Fig. 1c). Pore size distributions (PSD) are analysized by N2 and CO2 adsorption/desorption and theoretical simulation (Figure S4). CN-COF primarily show the ordered mesopore structure with PSD from 2.0 to 2.5 nm matched to the theoretical simulation of AA-stacking mode (2.3 nm), while PSD from 0.7 to 1.0 nm can be attributed to AB (0.9 nm) and ABC (0.7 nm) stacking mode. CN-dlCOF and CN/OCH3-dlCOF exhibit large proportion of disordered micropore structure with PSD around 1.0 and 1.4 nm and below 0.7 nm, which can be assigned to partially fragmentized pores and interlayer glide. Performance and rate-determining step in photocatalytic H2 generation

Photocatalytic H2 generation tests are implemented using 0.1 M ascorbic acid solution as a hole quencher and 5 mg of photocatalyst with Pt as a cocatalyst (H2PtCl6 precursor, 2.0 wt.% loading) under visible-light irradiation (Figure S6). Optimal samples of CN-dlCOF (141.1 ± 2.8 mmol g−1 h−1) and CN/OCH3-dlCOF (429.4 ± 3.7 mmol g−1 h−1) show a 2.2 and 6.8-fold improvement over pristine CN-COF (63.5 ± 1.5 mmol g−1 h−1) (Figs. 2a, S7–S12). The inferior photocatalytic activity of OCH3-COF and physically mixed samples demonstrate the critical regulation effect in dlCOFs synthesized through template method (Figures S13-S15). Specifically, CN/OCH3-dlCOF exhibits a wavelength-dependent apparent quantum yield (AQY) and achieves the AQY of 85.1 ± 1.0% at 420 nm, indicating the higher energy conversion efficiency compared to CN-COF and CN-dlCOF (Figs. 2b, S16 and Tables S1–S3). After six sequential cyclic stability test (4 hours per cycle), CN/OCH3-dlCOF retains 93% of its initial photocatalytic efficiency for H2 generation over a total of 24 hours (Fig. 2c). The retained crystallinity and Moiré patterns of CN/OCH3-dlCOF after cyclic operation demonstrate its stable interlayer structures (Figures S17, 18). High-angle annular dark field and energy dispersive spectroscopy element mapping images show a uniform Pt loading on COF after stability test, which resulted in 11% of surface area loss and partial micropore filling, contributed to the slight performance degradation (Figures S19, 20). The additional long-term stability test using 20 mg of CN/OCH3-dlCOF photocatalyst in 150 mL 0.1 M ascorbic acid solution shows 81% of activity retention after 50 hours (Figure S21 and Supplementary Movie 1). To understand the role of Pt cocatalyst, one of the best electrocatalyst for hydrogen evolution reaction, photocatalytic performance of COFs are investigated using various Pt loadings. A linear growth is observed from 0 to 2.0 wt.%, followed by saturation up to 3.0 wt.% (Fig. 2d). Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using DMPO as spin trapping agent shows characteristic nine-line signal, confirming that photogenerated electron can efficiently generate hydrogen radical \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$({{{{\rm{H}}}}}^{\bullet })$$\end{document}(H∙) intermediate in CN/OCH3-dlCOF system with Pt as cocatalyst and ascorbic acid as hole scavenger (Fig. 2e)11. To evaluate the concentration of reactive sites, we conduct poisoning experiments by absorbing H2S onto the Pt-sites with a 2.0 wt.% Pt loading (Fig. 2f)35. As the amount of Na2S increases, the photocatalytic rate of the COFs gradually decreases until a critical threshold of 6 mmol is reached, corresponding to complete deactivation of the Pt-sites. Even with a large proportion of Pt-sites covered by H2S, residual Pt-sites continue to efficiently extract electrons and catalyze hydrogen generation. Linear sweep voltammetry curves and corresponding Tafel plots confirm the favorable overpotential at 1 mA cm−2 (η1) and Tafel slope of Pt foil (259 mV and 90.1 mV dec−1) compared to CN-COF (725 mV and 428 mV dec−1), CN-dlCOF (705 mV and 535 mV dec−1), and CN/OCH3-dlCOF (716 mV and 482 mV dec−1), indicating Pt-sites are high active for the hydrogen evolution reaction (Figure S22). Transient photocurrent measurements in 0.1 M Na2SO4 solution without sacrifice agent show a distinct spike due to instantaneous carrier recombination upon light illumination (Fig. 2g); however, this recombination is significantly inhibited by the hole quencher in 0.1 M ascorbic acid solution, as also confirmed by the reduced photoluminescence in 0.1 M ascorbic acid solution (Figure S23). Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy of Bode plots in both solutions show dual time-constant assigned to electron transfer process at high frequency region and hole transfer process at low frequency region (Figure S24a, b). In 0.1 M ascorbic acid solution, both electron and hole transfer process exhibits phase angle closing to 0° and plateau in amplitude frequency diagram corresponded to the typical faradic process. In 0.1 M Na2SO4 solution, electron transfer process also exhibits faradic feature, but phase angle closing to 90° and negative linear relation between logarithmic frequency and impedance demonstrate the large resistance and capacitance-dominant behavior at low frequency region. Two semicircles in Nyquist plots are corresponded to electron transfer resistance (Ret) and hole transfer resistance (Rht), and the similar Rht of COFs in 0.1 M ascorbic acid solution demonstrates that ascorbic acid can efficiently consume excess holes, compared to the invalid hole transfer with tremendous Rht in 0.1 M Na2SO4 solution (Figure S24c, d). Therefore, photo-generated hole consumption and proton reduction are not the dominant factors determining the overall catalytic rate; these processes are in-situ quenched by ascorbic acid and efficiently catalyzed at the Pt-sites, respectively. The flat band potential, fitted from the positive slope in Mott-Schottky plots, suggests that the n-type semiconductor of CN-COF ( − 0.79 V vs. RHE), CN-dlCOF (−1.07 V vs. RHE), and CN/OCH3-dlCOF (−1.05 V vs. RHE) can sufficiently drive a hydrogen evolution reaction (Figure S25). The CBM potentials, as evaluated by VB-XPS and Tauc plots calculated according to a direct optical transition, further proves that photo-generated electrons in CN-COF ( − 1.2 V vs. RHE), CN-dlCOF (−1.3 V vs. RHE), and CN/OCH3-dlCOF (−1.6 V vs. RHE) are enough for proton reduction (Figs. 2h, S26, 27). Consequently, the rate-determining step (RDS) of the photocatalytic process in this system is the effective free electron production and transport to the Pt sites.

Ultrafast spectroscopy studies for hot electron dynamics

Femtosecond-transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) at 298 K with a 400 nm pump laser in water tracks the photo-injection carrier dynamics, primarily assigned as electrons dynamics due to their majority carrier status (Figure S28). Sharp peaks around 450-470 nm appear within a few hundred femtoseconds, forming a strong negative ground state bleaching (GSB) signal of exciton (GSB-Exciton) from photo-excitation (Figure S29)36,37. Afterwards, GSB-Exciton signals rapidly vanish to form the broader GSB signals of hot electron (GSB-HE), representing HE thermalization to the Fermi-Dirac statistics with electron temperature larger than the lattice temperature38. The high-energy tail of normalized fs-TAS spectra gradually disappear within a few picoseconds in CN-COF, hundreds of picoseconds in CN-dlCOF and over a nanosecond in CN/OCH3-dlCOF, revealing a typical characteristic of hot carrier cooling (Fig. 3a, b)21,22,39. Dynamics fitting of GSB-HE centered at 470 nm in CN-COF reveals HE cooling processes via fast acoustic-phonon scattering (τ1) and slow multiple phonon emission (τ3) to reach the thermal equilibrium state with an averaged lifetime (τave) of 0.206 ± 0.020 ps (Fig. 3c upper panel and Table S5)20,40. When interlayer dislocations are introduced, CN-dlCOF exhibits a tri-exponent dynamics fitting of GSB-HE with an extra process of τ2 (3.60 ± 0.37 ps) attributed to optical-phonon scattering from intrinsic phonon bottleneck effect, that further suppresses acoustic-phonon scattering (τ1: 0.248 ± 0.014 ps) and multiple phonon emission (τ3: 43.8 ± 2.0 ps) to achieve a longer τave (1.74 ± 0.23 ps) of HE20,41,42. Consequently, HE can directly trigger a distinctive wavelength-dependent photocatalytic efficiency with highest AQY of 85.1 ± 1.0% at 420 nm, and indirectly supply energetic optical-phonon to re-excite trapped electrons, thereby improving the overall energy utilization.

Interlayer structure regulates hot electron cooling

DFT calculations using the CP2K software package on a periodic multilayer model reveal the electronic energy-band structures of the two-dimensional COFs (Figure S30 and Supplementary Data 2–4). Well-stacked CN-COF in the P6 space group shows electronic energy-band crossing at high-symmetry point of K in the Brillouin zone due to symmetry protected degeneracy, facilitating high-energy exciton and carrier cooling through coupling with acoustic-phonons. (Fig. 4a left panel)46–48. The ultrafast phonon-assisted intervalley/band scattering hinders hot carrier utilization and results in a critical energy dissipation44. Introducing interlayer dislocations breaks the structural symmetry, removes energy-band degeneracy, opens a CB gap at K point, and suppresses acoustic-phonon scattering to hinder exciton and carrier relaxing to the adjacent electronic band (Fig. 4b, c left panel)14. Larger gaps are created by introducing different methoxyl-group in CN/OCH3-dlCOF (48.0 and 64.4 meV) and breaking more structural symmetry, compared to CN-dlCOF (26.5 and 51.8 meV). The increased inter-CB gap result in the demand for high-energy optical-phonon and inefficient multiple phonon emission, namely the intrinsic phonon bottleneck42. On the other hand, hot electrons can also migrate through higher energy bands with lower effective mass (m*) in CN-dlCOF (CB2: 0.32 me, CB3: 0.14 me) and CN/OCH3-dlCOF (CB2: 0.11 me, CB3: 0.25 me) than in CN-COF (CB1: 0.56 me) (Table S6)9,19,49. Despite inter-CB gaps permit the production of high-energy optical-phonon, high carrier concentration is required for effective Auger reheating to reach the hot-phonon bottleneck20,43. Projected density of states (PDOS) analysis in CN-dlCOF reveals that molecular orbitals contributed from the dislocated layers are primarily located near the CBM, whereas those from template layers are distribute over both the CBM and VBM, leading to carrier recombination (Fig. 4b right panel)24. In contrast, the orbital reorganization driven by incorporation of the methoxyl-group in CN/OCH3-dlCOF creates a separated distribution of the molecular orbitals associated with the CBM at the CN-COF layers and those associated with the VBM at the OCH3-dlCOF layers, improving non-equilibrium carrier concentration (Fig. 4c right panel)25. Transition dipole moments calculated by time-dependent DFT describe the transition direction and strength for various excited states. The transition dipole moment anisotropy, derived from the lowest 50 excited states, demonstrates that interlayer polarization in CN/OCH3-dlCOF can redirect the in-plane excitation of single-component CN-COF and CN-dlCOF to out-of-plane excitation (Fig. 4b). Benefited from separated orbital distribution and vertical charge-transfer excitation, experimental Eb of CN/OCH3-dlCOF (43.4 ± 2.3 meV) is evident smaller than that of single-component CN-COF (71.0 ± 3.7 meV) and CN-dlCOF (66.2 ± 4.8 meV) derived from temperature-dependent photoluminescence spectra from 80 K to 290 K (Figs. 4e, S33)12. Besides, the characteristic HE peaks is weak in CN-dlCOF and strong in CN/OCH3-dlCOF, consistent with the signals from fs-TAS spectra, further supporting the efficient HE production in CN/OCH3-dlCOF and the hot-phonon bottleneck mechanism (Fig. 3a, b)43,45,52. The larger dipole moment in CN/OCH3-dlCOF (6.59 Debye) demonstrates the higher polarization degree to promote carrier transport and prevent carrier recombination, in accord with the intensive transient photo-current and smaller electron-transfer resistance from electrochemical measurements (Figs. 2g, 4f, S24). Therefore, the interlayer polarization further reduces Eb and carrier recombination to significantly increase carrier concentration for enhancing the Auger reheating and hot-phonon bottleneck effect, thereby achieving the efficient hot electron utilization in CN/OCH3-dlCOF.

Consequently, Pt cocatalysts extract high-energy, high-mobility hot electrons to efficiently power hydrogen generation with the assistance of an ascorbic acid hole-quencher. The production and utilization of hot electrons are predominant factors for photocatalytic hydrogen generation in this system, which is optimizable by interlayer dislocation-induced symmetry breaking and polarization-regulated orbital reorganization. Symmetry breaking in high-symmetry COFs opens inter-CB gaps to permit the production of energetic optical-phonon. Orbital reorganization and polarization field decrease Eb and carrier recombination to reach the high carrier concentration, synergistically igniting the efficient hot electron production.

Discussion

In summary, effective hot electron production and utilization are achieved through the stepwise introduction of interlayer dislocation and polarization in CN/OCH3-dlCOF for efficient photocatalytic H2 generation. The interlayer dislocation in the COFs eliminate energy-band degeneracy, creating inter-CB gaps in both dlCOFs, which surpress hot electron cooling and enable hot electron extraction to propel hydrogen generation. Furthermore, the incorporation of methoxyl-group evidently reduces Eb and carrier recombination to attain enough carrier concentration for hot-phonon bottleneck effect. Long-lived hot electrons activated by high-energy photons leads to a wavelength-dependent AQY for photocatalytic hydrogen generation. This energy-band engineering of two-dimensional COFs provides direct insights into edge state tuning in periodic structures for advancing hot electron photocatalysis.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals and solvents are directly used, and their purity and manufacturer are as follows: The 1,2-dichlorobenzene (o-DCB, 99%), anhydrous n-Butanol (n-BuOH, 99.4%), acetic acid (99.0%), 4,4’-diamino-[1,1’-biphenyl]-3,3’-dicarbonitrile (98%) and 3,3’-dimethoxy-benziden (95%) are acquired from Macklin Chemicals. Tetrahydrofuran (THF, 99%) and anhydrous acetone (200 mL) is supplied by Guangzhou Chemical Reagent Factory. 2,4,6-triformylphloroglucinol (98%) is purchased from Jilin Chinese Academy of Sciences-Yanshen Technology Co. Ltd.

Synthesis of photocatalysts

Synthesis of CN-COF

4,4’-diamino-[1,1’-biphenyl]-3,3’-dicarbonitrile (28.1 mg, 0.12 mmol) and 2,4,6-triformylphloroglucinol (16.8 mg, 0.08 mmol) are firstly charged in a Pyrex tube, then o-DCB (0.5 mL) and n-BuOH (0.5 mL) are carefully injected. The mixture is homogenized by sonication for 5 minutes, and then 0.1 mL 6 M acetic acid is added. Three times of degassing procedure are performed via freeze-pump-thaw under 77 K in liquid N2 bath, then the flame-sealed Pyrex tube is placed in an oven under 393 K for 3 days. The precipitate is centrifugated and washed 3 times by THF (100 mL) and anhydrous acetone (200 mL), then dried under vacuum at 333 K for 24 hours to obtain a dark red solid with a yield of 89%.

Synthesis of CN-dlCOF

A Pyrex tube is charged with CN-COF (30.4 mg, 0.67 equivalent), 4,4’-diamino-[1,1’-biphenyl]-3,3’-dicarbonitrile (9.4 mg, 0.04 mmol, 1.00 equivalent), 2,4,6-triformylphloroglucinol (5.6 mg, 0.027 mmol, 0. 67 equivalent), o-1,2-dichlorobenzene (0.5 mL), n-butanol (0.5 mL), and acetic acid (0.1 mL). The as-synthesized CN-COF is dispersed along with monomers by sonication for 30 minutes to pre-condensate on CN-COF template. Three times of degassing procedure are performed via freeze-pump-thaw under 77 K in liquid N2 bath, then the flame-sealed Pyrex tube is placed in an oven under 413 K for 3 days. The precipitate is centrifugated and washed 3 times by THF (100 mL) and anhydrous acetone (200 mL), then dried under vacuum at 333 K for 24 h to obtain a reddish-brown solid with a yield of 82%. The different ratios for template and dislocation layer are similarly synthesized to the method discussed above except alter the equivalent of CN-COF template (0.50 to 1.00 equivalent) and 4,4’-diamino-[1,1’-biphenyl]-3,3’-dicarbonitrile (1.50 to 0 equivalent), and 2,4,6-triformylphloroglucinol (1.00 to 0 equivalent).

Synthesis of CN/OCH3-dlCOF

A Pyrex tube is charged with CN-COF (30.4 mg, 0.67 equivalent), 3,3’-dimethoxy-benziden (9.8 mg, 0.04 mmol, 1.00 equivalent), 2,4,6-triformylphloroglucinol (5.6 mg, 0.026 mmol, 0. 67 equivalent), o-1,2-dichlorobenzene (0.5 mL), n-butanol (0.5 mL), and acetic acid (0.1 mL). The as-synthesized CN-COF is dispersed along with monomers by sonication for 30 min to pre-condensate on CN-COF template. Three times of degassing procedure are performed via freeze-pump-thaw under 77 K in liquid N2 bath, then the flame-sealed Pyrex tube is placed in an oven under 413 K for 3 days. The precipitate is centrifugated and washed 3 times by 100 mL THF and 200 mL anhydrous acetone, then dried under vacuum at 333 K for 24 h to obtain a reddish-brown solid with a yield of 87%. The different ratios for template and dislocation layer are similarly synthesized to the method discussed above except alter the equivalent of CN-COF template (0.50 to 1.00 equivalent) and 3,3’-dimethoxy-benziden (1.50 to 0 equivalent), and 2,4,6-triformylphloroglucinol (1.00 to 0 equivalent).

Characterization of the materials

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) is implemented on a Rigaku Smartlab diffractometer (Cu target, Kα = 1.54178 Å) and a Si sample holder, collecting data from 3 to 30° with a 0.02° step size. Fourier-transformed infrared (FTIR) spectra are measured on a Nicolet Avatar 6700 FT-IR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, America). 13C cross-polarization magic-angle-spinning solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (CP-MAS ss-NMR) are carried out on a Bruker Avance 400 MHz spectrometer operating at 100.6 MHz. XPS measurements are carried out on AXIS SUPRA UltraDLD. The absorbance spectra are calculated by Kubelka-Munk function based on diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) acquired from Shimadzu UV-2550 UV-vis-NIR spectrophotometer. N2 and CO2 adsorption/desorption measurements are performed on Quantachrome Autosorb Station 1 with 12 hours of vacuum degassing procedure under 393 K. The surface areas are calculated within a pressure range of P/P0 = 0.005–0.05 of N2 adsorption isotherm using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is implemented on JEOL JEM-2010 electron microscope.

Temperature-dependent photoluminescence spectroscopy measurement

Temperature-dependent photoluminescence spectroscopy measurements are measured through FLS980 with an Oxford Optistat attemperator which can control temperature from 77–400 K with liquid nitrogen cooling. Samples are pumped by 340 nm light source, and measured at 80–290 K with a gradient of 30 K.

Femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS) measurements

The femtosecond pump-probe transient absorption measurements are performed using a regenerative amplified Ti:sapphire laser system (Coherent; 800 nm, 85 fs, 7 mJ/pulse, and 1 kHz repetition rate) as the laser source, a TOPAS optical parametric amplifier (OPA) for wavelength-tunable pump beam generation, and a Helios spectrometer (Ultrafast Systems LLC) for probe beam detection. A series of neutral-density filters are used to adjust the power of the pump beam focused on the sample with a waist of ~0.3 mm. The pump and probe delay is tuned by optical path length. The absorbance change (ΔA) is calculated by subtracting pump-unblocked probe pulse from adjacent pump-blocked probe pulse. All samples are homogeneously dispersed in water with a concentration of 0.1 mg mL−1. The quartz cell is sealed and washed by N2 bubbling for 10 min before measurement.

Theoretical calculation detail

The CP2K quantum chemistry and solid-state physics software package is employed to perform periodic density functional theory calculations. The PBE function is used with Grimme’s DFT-D3 dispersion correction in the BJ-damping term. MOLOPT basis with appropriate diffusion functional basis is implemented by the Gaussian and plane waves (GPW) method. DZVP-MOLOPT-GTH basis is employed for cell optimization, geometry optimization and energy calculation. Convergence tests of cutoff (600 eV) and rel_cutoff (60 eV) are performed within a threshold of 10−7 Hartree before the calculation. The PERIODIC Poisson_solver is select for three-dimensional periodic boundary condition calculation with vacuum slab over 15 Å, and SCF convergence threshold is 10−7. The GEO_OPT and CELL_OPT module are used for geometry and cell optimization of ground state, respectively, until achieve a displacement below 0.0003 Bohr and force gradient below 0.0003 Hartree. The optimized structures are visualized by VESTA53, and surficial electrostatic potential distributions are visualized by VMD54.

The high local exchange HLE-17 function is used for time-dependent density function theory (TDDFT), energy band structure and density of state (DOS) calculation to achieve more accurate bandgap value compared to PBE function which severely underestimate the bandgap in conjugate organic system. The transition dipole moment of excited state for singlet transition is calculated by TDDFPT module with a convergence criterion of all excitation energy below 0.1 meV. The energy band diagram and DOS are calculated with a smear temperature at 298 K in Fermi-Dirac statistics to obtain the accurate Fermi energy.
