Ambulatory Surgery Centers for Coronary Angiography and Percutaneous Coronary Intervention: Modeled Savings for Medicare and Beneficiaries
Authors: Pranav Puri, Charles D. Miks, Vikram Sharma
Published: 2025

Introduction

Over the past decade, cardiovascular catheterization procedures have shifted from inpatient to outpatient settings, including ambulatory surgery centers (ASC). In 2020, Medicare expanded ASC reimbursement to include percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). The Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) subsequently published a position paper providing guidance on starting PCI programs in ASC, and the number of cardiology-focused ASC have increased from 55 in 2018 to 221 in 2023.1,2 These changes have generated interest in the potential of ASC to improve patient convenience and reduce costs.

Limited data are available describing the adoption of ASC for diagnostic coronary angiography and PCI. Similarly, the potential cost savings from performing these procedures in the ASC setting have yet to be quantified, both from the perspective of Medicare and patient cost sharing. This study aims to quantify cost differences and estimate potential savings from increased migration of procedures from hospital outpatient departments (HOPD) to ASC.

Materials and methods

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 2023 data using the 100% Medicare Carrier Files to identify claims for the diagnostic coronary angiography and PCI Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes approved by Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) for ASC use. Clinical setting (HOPD vs ASC) was determined using claim place of service codes, and national average costs were obtained from the Medicare procedure price lookup tool. These included PCI (CPT 92920, 92928) and diagnostic coronary catheterization procedures (CPT 93454-93461). For each procedure, we extracted the physician fee, facility fee, total Medicare payment, patient copayment, and overall cost in both ASC and HOPD settings.

We then modeled cost savings under varying levels of ASC utilization (current observed share, 10%, 20%, and 40%) while holding total procedural volume constant. Aggregate savings were calculated by applying observed payment differentials across the full set of approved interventional CPT codes.

Results

The most frequently performed procedures in ASC were CPT 92928 (PCI with stent placement, 3.8% ASC utilization, n = 3687) and CPT 93458 (diagnostic coronary angiography with left heart catheterization, 2.8% ASC utilization, n = 2203). In contrast, procedures such as CPT 93456 (combined coronary and left heart catheterization, n = 61, 0.6% ASC utilization) and CPT 93461 (combined right and left heart catheterization with imaging, n = 27, 0.3% ASC utilization) had minimal ASC adoption. Discussion

This analysis demonstrates that shifting diagnostic coronary angiography and PCI from HOPD to ASC could generate substantial cost savings for Medicare and patients. The savings stem almost entirely from lower facility fees in ASC, as physician reimbursement is site-neutral. This aligns with CMS’ prior estimate that even modest migration (5% of PCI volume) could yield $20 million in savings.2

Despite these potential cost savings, ASC utilization remains low. Contributing barriers likely include infrastructure limitations, regulatory hurdles, and uncertainty regarding safety in nonhospital settings. Professional guidance emphasizes strict case selection and program standards for ASC PCI (eg, screening for high-risk clinical/anatomic features, explicit transfer criteria, mock drills, and early follow-up), which can mitigate risk as adoption grows.2,3 However, a growing body of evidence supports the clinical safety of cardiac catheterization in ASC. Li et al4 conducted a large propensity-matched analysis of PCI for stable ischemic heart disease in both ASC and HOPD. Their analysis found no significant differences in a composite outcome of 30-day myocardial infarction, bleeding complications, and hospital admission.4 These findings mirror earlier transitions in electrophysiology (EP), where early evidence suggests comparable safety profiles for a wide range of catheter-based EP procedures in ASC as compared to HOPD.5 Current federal quality reporting for ASC offers limited cardiovascular-specific metrics, underscoring the need for specialty-relevant outcomes tracking as site-of-service shifts accelerate.2

This study is limited by its reliance on Medicare Part B claims data. As such, we were unable to account for patient-level clinical characteristics or other categories of health care utilization, such as postprocedural care. Crucially, our model does not include downstream costs associated with procedural complications requiring hospital transfer and admission, which could offset some of the modeled cost savings. Additionally, our cost sharing estimates assume Medicare cost structures; patients with supplemental insurance would not necessarily experience the modeled out-of-pocket savings. Despite these limitations, this analysis focuses specifically on estimating cost savings at the programmatic level for CMS and potential implications for procedural site-of-service decisions.

Conclusion

Increasing the proportion of diagnostic coronary angiography and PCI performed in ASC represents a high-value opportunity to reduce health care spending and patient financial burden. Realizing these savings at scale will require adherence to case selection/program standards and more targeted cardiovascular metrics in ASC quality reporting.
