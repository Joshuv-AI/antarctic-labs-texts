Neonatal Seizures in Tuberous Sclerosis Complex: A Case Series
Authors: Kristina Jülich, Kristen Arredondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEpilepsy affects up to 90% of patients with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC); earlier seizure onset is associated with worse neurocognitive outcomes. They have not developed epilepsy and have had milder neurodevelopmental impairment.In conclusion, data from our center suggest that neonatal seizures may be common in TSC, especially in high‐risk infants with TSC2 with highly epileptogenic lesions. With recent preliminary data about safety and efficacy of mTOR inhibitor treatment in infants with TSC, early EEG monitoring should be considered.Keywords: cortical dysplasia, early onset epilepsy, electroencephalogram, electrographic, TSC1, TSC2

Introduction

Epilepsy affects 75%–90% of patients with tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC) [1, 2, 3] and is intractable in over half such patients [4]. Earlier seizure onset and refractory epilepsy are associated with more severe neurocognitive outcomes [1, 2, 5]. In a cohort of 32 infants with TSC, the average age of first epileptiform electroencephalogram (EEG) abnormalities on EEG recordings was 4.5 months. Half of the patients developed seizures, with an average interval of 3.6 months between spike onset on EEG and clinical seizure onset [6]. The positive predictive value of the presence of interictal epileptiform discharges (IEDs) on EEG for the development of epilepsy was 77%. Large cohorts of European infants with TSC have been described by the EPISTOP investigators. Within a cohort of 366 infants with TSC and epilepsy [7], 21 (5.7%) developed epilepsy within the first 28 days of life. Identified risk factors for neonatal onset seizures were the presence of large focal cortical dysplasias, congenitally diagnosed subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs), and perinatal complications such as prematurity, hypoxia, or infection.

The EPISTOP clinical intervention trial was a combined randomized and open‐label multi‐center study that evaluated the time to first clinical seizure in infants with TSC in groups treated with vigabatrin at the time of initial identification of IEDs on EEG compared with those started on vigabatrin at the time of clinical seizure onset [8]. Among the 94 patients screened for the trial, 22 infants were excluded because of clinical or electrographic seizures prior to randomization [9]. Infants were enrolled at a median age of 28 days (IQR 14–64 days). The median age at the time of first epileptiform EEG findings was 77 days (IQR 23–111 days), or corrected age of 49 5/7 weeks' gestation. And in 41% of the cohort, the first recorded EEG already contained IEDs, a number that increased to 78% by 4 months of age. Patients with TSC2 had a median age of 68 days at the time of initial IEDs on EEG, significantly younger than the 132 days for patients with TSC1. Importantly, a younger age at the time of IED identification was associated with lower neurodevelopmental scores when tested at 2 years of age.

The PREVeNT clinical trial was a randomized and placebo‐controlled trial involving the use of vigabatrin as preventive therapy for infants enrolled from 13 TSC centers in the United States [10]. This study enrolled infants less than 6 months of age with an average enrollment age of around 2 months. Twelve of 84 infants screened were excluded from the study, primarily due to the presence of seizures on the baseline EEG. The primary outcome, an assessment for a difference in scores on neurodevelopmental Bayley assessments at 2 years of age in children treated with vigabatrin at initial onset of IEDs on EEG versus those who were not treated until the time of clinical seizure onset, was negative. There was no difference in the timing of seizure onset or later diagnosis of intractable epilepsy.

A recent case report described an infant with TSC and neonatal status epilepticus; the authors speculated whether early initiation of antiseizure medications, especially vigabatrin, may have improved the child's developmental outcome [11]. Trials are ongoing to determine whether early intervention using mTOR inhibitors can reduce the devastating effects of drug‐resistant infantile epilepsy in TSC.

The time to TSC diagnosis for infants is decreasing, in part due to a rise of fetal diagnoses based on findings such as cardiac rhabdomyoma on prenatal ultrasound [12, 13] or early confirmatory genetic testing in utero for familial cases. EEG is considered a biomarker for the subsequent development of epilepsy in these patients. However, current clinical management guidelines for TSC do not specify the timing of the first EEG [14].

At our institution, infants with a prenatal TSC diagnosis are admitted postnatally for a complete evaluation, including brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and prolonged EEG. We aimed to determine the rate of neonatal onset epilepsy in our cohort of infants with a prenatal or neonatal diagnosis of TSC.

Methods

We performed a single‐center medical records analysis of patients seen in our TSC clinic between the years 2020 and 2025. Inclusion criteria were a prenatal or neonatal diagnosis of TSC and EEGs recorded at our center. We reviewed age of seizure onset, treatment, and diagnostic evaluation including EEG and brain MRI data.

Patient Descriptions

Of the 66 individuals with TSC who are being followed at our center, six had a prenatal or neonatal diagnosis of TSC and sufficient data available for analysis. Four of these patients had neonatal seizures, including two infants with EEG‐confirmed seizures and focal IEDs in the first days of life. Another two began having events suspicious for clinical seizures at 3 and 4 weeks of life, although they were not formally diagnosed with epilepsy until 4 and 5 weeks of age, respectively. The two patients with neonatal onset EEG‐confirmed seizures were born at our center, allowing the most robust data availability for the prenatal and early neonatal clinical findings. These two patients are described in detail below.

Patient 1

Patient 1 is a 5‐year‐old boy who was diagnosed with TSC in utero due to several large cardiac rhabdomyomas. Fetal MRI and prenatal genetic testing were not performed. He was born at term and admitted to our neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) on the first day of life due to concerns for cardiac outflow obstruction. MRI brain showed numerous tubers and several subependymal nodules, including a 9 mm enhancing nodule at the foramen of Monroe, and confirmed the clinical diagnosis of TSC. Genetic testing showed a pathogenic de novo variant in TSC2, c.5238_5255del (p.His1746_Arg1751del). A routine EEG recorded on day of life four showed a normal background and runs of multifocal brief rhythmic discharges. Subsequent long‐term EEG monitoring showed frequent subclinical seizures, meeting criteria for neonatal status epilepticus. Seizures were refractory to multiple antiseizure medications, including vigabatrin and everolimus, which had been started in the first week of life to reduce rhabdomyoma growth. Despite brief periods of clinical seizure freedom in his first year of life, he developed hypsarrhythmia and underwent combined corpus callosotomy and resection of a large right frontal tuber at 14 months of age. This led to marked improvement in both his seizure burden and his development. Currently, he has been seizure‐free for almost 2 years. A developmental evaluation at age 3 years showed severe global developmental delay.

Patient 2

Patient 2 is a 2‐year‐old girl, diagnosed in utero with TSC2 (de novo pathogenic variant, c.1565_1566dup, p.His523Thrfs*13). She was born at term and admitted to the NICU for further evaluation and monitoring. Postnatal brain MRI showed numerous tubers, some with calcifications, and several subependymal nodules. Electrographic neonatal seizures were detected on the first day of life on long‐term EEG monitoring, with focal IEDs and normal background organization. Seizures subsided after treatment with phenobarbital, which was transitioned to levetiracetam and vigabatrin. She remained seizure‐free until age 3 months, when she developed infantile spasms that were refractory to treatment with steroids and vigabatrin but improved significantly after adding everolimus. Her spasms subsided completely, and focal impaired awareness seizures appeared that resolved with adding lacosamide. Her EEG background is currently almost normal with occasional focal epileptiform discharges. Her developmental evaluation at 18 months showed moderate global developmental delay.

A total of four of six infants with an in utero or neonatal TSC diagnosis (67%) in our cohort had neonatal seizures.

All children with neonatal seizures at our center had TSC2, high‐risk lesions on brain MRI (large dysplastic areas), and rapidly developed drug‐resistant epilepsy despite extensive medical and surgical management, including initiation of vigabatrin, and in one patient everolimus, in the first weeks of life. Three of four also had large subependymal nodules at the foramen of Monroe that were read as SEGA on initial MRI, although they did not increase in size on follow‐up studies. Two out four developed infantile spasms, and three of four underwent epilepsy surgery between the ages of 4 and 14 months, with marked improvement in both seizures and developmental trajectories (Table 1).

Of the other two children, one had signs of dysmaturity on neonatal long‐term EEG monitoring but no focal epileptiform discharges or seizures, and the other had a normal neonatal EEG in the first week of life. Both these children have remained seizure‐free at current ages of 1 and 3 years, respectively. The two children without neonatal seizures had TSC1 and mosaic TSC2. They have not developed epilepsy and have in comparison had better developmental outcomes with normal development (child with TSC1), and predominantly motor delay (child with mosaic TSC2). The child with mosaic TSC2 also received everolimus in the neonatal period due to large cardiac rhabdomyomas.

Five of six infants in our cohort (80%) had an abnormal EEG, although focal or multifocal epileptiform discharges only occurred in the children with neonatal seizures.

Discussion

We aimed to determine the occurrence of seizures in neonates with TSC at our center. In our cohort of six infants, four had neonatal seizures (67%). There is a paucity of data analyzing and characterizing neonatal seizures in TSC, and current guidelines do not provide detailed recommendations about when to obtain the initial EEG. Clinical trials such as EPISTOP and PREVeNT report excluding between 7% and 23% of infants prior to randomization because of seizures, with enrollment at a mean age of 2 months (PREVeNT) and 1 month (EPISTOP). Infants with known clinical seizures were excluded from screening, so rates of seizures were likely higher in those populations.

Only electrographic seizures were detected on EEGs in the first week of life in our study; clinical seizures were not seen until several weeks later. This is concordant with previously published literature, where most neonatal seizures are electrographic and the detection rate of seizures in neonatal units without the use of EEG was below 10% [15]. Even in older infants, clinical seizures in TSC may be subtle and identification by parents unreliable [16].

In our study, all children with neonatal seizures had focal or multifocal IEDs on their initial EEG. All children with neonatal seizures had TSC2 and high‐risk epileptogenic lesions on brain MRI, such as a high tuber burden, focal cortical dysplasias, or SEGAs. Conversely, the children without neonatal seizures had TSC1 and mosaic TSC2. In concordance with the published literature, TSC2 has higher rates of drug‐resistant epilepsy and a higher risk of worse neurocognitive outcomes [17, 18]. Focal cortical dysplasias and SEGAs have been identified as risk factors for early onset seizures in TSC [19].

Based on this data, we suspect that the risk of neonatal seizures, especially in infants with TSC2 and highly epileptogenic lesions, may be high, but clinical seizures may not become obvious until a few weeks later.

Limitations of our dataset include the small number of patients, which precludes generalizability and statistical analyses, and ascertainment bias since only infants with known TSC that were either born at our center or referred in the first weeks of life were included. The latter may have prevented enrolling children with milder TSC, diagnosed later in life, into our analysis.

Current TSC management guidelines recommend obtaining a baseline routine EEG, capturing wakefulness and sleep, every 6 weeks in the first year of life. If abnormalities are present, a longer EEG study should be done [14]. The timing of the first EEG is not specified.

Despite our limited dataset, we hypothesize that neonatal seizures may be more common in individuals with TSC than previously reported. Recording a routine EEG at several weeks or months of age may fail to identify early onset seizures and the opportunity to initiate early treatment, especially considering promising preliminary data of early mTOR inhibitor administration. At our center, we switched our practice from obtaining routine EEGs to obtaining long‐term EEGs in every infant with a prenatal diagnosis of TSC.

Although further investigations are needed to further address this question, we believe that postnatal long‐term EEG monitoring should be considered in infants with a prenatal or neonatal diagnosis of TSC, especially TSC2, and/or other risk factors such as highly epileptogenic lesions.
