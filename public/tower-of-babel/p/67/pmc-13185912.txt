Living Donor Kidney Transplantation in High-Recurrence Kidney Diseases: Precision Risk Stratification and Management
Authors: Catherine Verhellen, Arnaud Devresse, Valentine Gillion, Elliott Van Regemorter, Tom Darius, Antoine Buemi, Eric Goffin, Nada Kanaan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Living-donor kidney transplantation (KT) offers the best outcomes for patients with kidney failure (KF). However, recurrence of primary kidney disease in the allograft remains a major concern and represents 1 of the leading causes of graft loss, significantly influencing donor and recipient selection. The recurrence risk varies substantially among kidney diseases and must be carefully assessed during pretransplant evaluation. For diseases associated with low-to-intermediate recurrence risk, such as lupus nephritis, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies-associated vasculitis, or IgA nephropathy, living-donor KT is generally safe, although appropriate counseling remains essential. In contrast, diseases such as membranous nephropathy (MN), primary focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), C3 glomerulopathy (C3G), primary hyperoxaluria (PH), and amyloid light-chain (AL) amyloidosis are associated with higher recurrence rates and often require complex, individualized assessment. Recent advances have significantly transformed this field. Biomarkers such as anti-phospholipase A2 receptor (anti-PLA2R) antibodies in MN and antinephrin antibodies in FSGS enable refined risk stratification and tailored pre- and post-transplant strategies. Complement gene analysis and targeted therapies, including eculizumab, have markedly improved outcomes in aHUS, whereas novel complement inhibitors show promise for recurrent C3G. RNA interference therapy has expanded kidney-alone transplantation options in PH, and modern hematologic therapies have improved post-transplant prognosis in AL amyloidosis. These developments are reshaping the decision-making landscape for living-donor KT in diseases with a high risk of recurrence, highlighting the need for multidisciplinary evaluation, individualized strategies, and transparent donor-recipient communication to optimize transplant outcomes.

Living-donor KT is the best therapeutic option for patients with KF, because it significantly shortens the waiting period for those on dialysis, and facilitates pre-emptive transplantation. Moreover, it is associated with better renal outcomes compared with deceased-donor KT.1 Recurrence of native kidney disease on the kidney allograft is a concerning issue. Indeed, primary kidney disease can recur in up to 20% in transplanted allograft2, 3, 4 and represents the third most common cause of graft loss.3

International guidelines underscore the importance of evaluating the risk of primary renal disease recurrence after KT.2,5 Although some kidney diseases present a small risk of recurrence with a low likelihood of graft loss, others carry a significant risk of recurrence leading to potential graft failure. In such cases, nephrologists and transplant teams may sometimes consider these conditions as contraindications for transplantation, especially from living donors. Additionally, genetic kidney diseases with a high risk of recurrence present a unique challenge, as a related living donor may carry the same genetic mutation, exposing him or her to the risk of ultimately developing kidney disease.6, 7, 8, 9

As our comprehension of the mechanisms underlying kidney diseases at risk of recurrence continues to evolve, providing accurate and up-to-date information on this risk is challenging in daily practice. New biomarkers have emerged, enabling a more accurate assessment of disease activity linked to recurrence risk. Furthermore, new drugs have recently been developed, or are being tested in clinical trials, that aim to prevent disease recurrence after KT or effectively treat recurrent disease in the kidney allograft. These advances have transformed the landscape for certain kidney diseases previously deemed contraindications for living donor transplantation. They must be taken into consideration and communicated to potential living donors and their recipients, when making decisions regarding living donation eligibility.

In this review, we explore recent advances in assessing the risk of recurrence, and the availability of prophylactic measures and treatments for kidney diseases with a high risk of recurrence. We consider how these advances can inform the challenging decision of approving living donation.

Kidney Diseases at Low or Intermediate Risk of Recurrence

Kidney diseases with low/intermediate risk of recurrence will not be discussed here. Briefly, the risk of recurrent disease in patients with systemic lupus nephritis, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies-associated systemic vasculitis, and antiglomerular basement membrane disease is low when they are transplanted whereas in remission.10, 11, 12 Histologic recurrence of IgA nephropathy in the kidney allograft is frequent (50%–60%), but studies relying on for-cause biopsies report lower recurrence rates of 10%–30%,13 with an estimated 10-year risk of graft loss because of a recurrence rate of 9.7%.14 Recurrence rate of Henoch-Schoenlein purpura is 11% at 10 years with a risk of graft loss from recurrence around 7.5%.15 For all of these diseases, living donor transplantation is considered safe, but recipients and donors should always be informed of the risk of recurrence (Table 1).Table 1Kidney diseases at low/intermediate risk of recurrenceDiseaseRisk evaluationPre-transplant work upPost transplant monitoring/managementTreatment of recurrenceRecent advancesIgA nephropathy10–35% recurrence. MN

For this section, the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) and UK guidelines were used as reference standards.

MN is the leading cause of nephrotic syndrome in adults with a reported recurrence rate following transplantation ranging from 10% to 50%, with 50% of graft losses in recurrent cases attributable to recurrence.16 The cumulative incidence of recurrent MN was 31% at 10 years post-transplant in a recent multicenter, international retrospective study of 194 adults with biopsy-proven native MN,16 and 26% after a median follow-up of 23.5 months in a prospective multicenter French cohort study.17

Several risk factors for MN recurrence have been suggested,18, 19, 20, 21 but the Post-Transplant Glomerular Disease Consortium found no risk factors other than rapid progression of native disease towards KF (within 3 years) and elevated pre-transplant level of anti-PLA2R antibodies which were strongly associated with recurrence (hazard ratio [HR]: 18.58; 95% CI: 5.37–64.27).16

A decade ago, a major advance in the understanding of the pathology of MN was achieved with the detection and characterization of PLA2R as the major target antigen in primary MN. Since then, the majority of studies have reported a significant association between the presence of anti-PLA2R antibodies before transplantation and the risk of disease recurrence.17 Taking anti-PLA2R antibodies into account, a recurrence risk of up to 70% was reported in case of positive pre-transplant anti-PLA2R, the higher risk being observed for patients with high titers. Patients with negative anti-PLA2R antibodies but a history of positive staining for PLA2R antigen in a native kidney biopsy are at low risk (10%), whereas those with both negative markers are at moderate risk (30% recurrence).18,22, 23, 24, 25

A better risk assessment and prevention of recurrence can be made for the risk of MN recurrence most likely driven by the PLA2R status at transplantation (Table 3). Ideally no detectable antibodies should be present on the day of transplantation, as a prospective study showed that the presence of antibodies regardless of their level, was predictive of recurrence.17 The pretransplant cut-off value of anti-PLA2R antibody associated with a low risk of post-transplant recurrence is debatable. Quintana et al.24 suggested in a retrospective cohort study of 21 patients that anti-PLA2R antibody value < 45 RU/ml on ELISA was associated with a negative predictive value of 92% in regard to recurrence risk, whereas Gupta et al.26 proposed a cut-off value of 29 RU/ml to predict MN recurrence with a sensitivity and specificity of 85% and 92% respectively. However, these studies are limited by the small number of included patients and have to be confirmed in larger prospective studies. Recognizing MN as an antibody-mediated autoimmune disease has led to the use of immunosuppressive therapy in patients considered at risk of progressive disease.23 Rituximab, a B-cell depleting agent, has shown its efficacy in inducing complete or partial remission in about two-thirds of treated patients.28 In that context, monitoring the anti-PLA2R antibody level of patients on the waiting list followed by a personalized management strategy to reduce anti-PLA2R antibody titers to below laboratory cut-off, should be considered for kidney transplant candidates, particularly for living donor transplantation (Table 3). However, this statement is mostly derived from observational cohort studies and expert consensus rather than randomized controlled trials.16,18 The KDIGO 2020 transplant guidelines suggest (grade 2C) measuring anti-PLA2R antibodies pretransplant to inform recurrence risk, but suggest against (grade 2D) systematic use of rituximab or alkylating agents for prevention. The evidence supporting pretransplant treatment to reduce antibody titers comes from extrapolation of native kidney disease trials showing rituximab efficacy in achieving immunologic remission.23 For transplant candidates with elevated anti-PLA2R antibodies, KDIGO 2021 guidelines suggest the use of rituximab (1–2 infusions of 1g each, 2 weeks apart) or cyclophosphamide plus alternate-month glucocorticoids, or calcineurin inhibitor-based therapy (grade 1B).23

Management of patients after KT can be stratified according to the pretransplant positivity of anti-PLA2R antibodies. For those with PLA2R-negative MN, KDIGO guidelines advise to evaluate proteinuria every month for 6 to 12 months and to consider allograft biopsy if proteinuria exceeds 1 g/day. In cases of PLA2R-associated MN, regular monitoring (every 1–3 months, depending on risk status) of anti-PLA2R antibodies and proteinuria for the first 6 to 12 months is recommended, with biopsy consideration for patients with proteinuria between 0.3 to 1.0 g/day and persistently high or increasing antibody titers. Primary FSGS

For this section, the KDIGO and UK guidelines were used as reference standards.

Primary FSGS is 1 of the primary causes of nephrotic syndrome in adults and pediatric patients. Assessing the characteristics of native FSGS is important, as recurrence is significantly low in genetic and secondary forms.31 In case of suspected genetic FSGS, genetic testing is recommended both in the recipient, to provide prognostic information on transplant outcomes, and in the living related donor candidates to preclude a risk of chronic kidney disease and confirm eligibility23 (Table 5).

Recurrence of primary FSGS after transplantation is common, estimated to range between 10% and 60%.32,33 The Post-Transplant Glomerular Disease study found a recurrence rate of 32% in 176 kidney transplant recipients (KTRs) with a biopsy-proven primary FSGS, with most recurrences occurring early after transplantation (median time to recurrence 1.5 months).32 Recurrence is associated with a decrease in graft survival, with a 5-year graft survival rate of 53% for patients with recurrence compared with 83% for those without.33

A recent meta-analysis found that progression to KF within 3 years of the diagnosis of primary FSGS, as well as recurrent FSGS leading to the loss of a previous allograft, were associated with disease recurrence.33 In case of recurrent FSGS leading to the loss of a previous allograft, the reported risk of recurrence is > 80%34,35 (Table 3).

The pathogenesis of primary FSGS appears to be linked to the presence of a circulating factor. Possible culprits include the serum soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor, the anti-CD40 autoantibody and the apolipoprotein A-Ib.36 More recently, circulating antinephrin antibodies were identified in 9% of patients with primary FSGS37 and are potentially involved in the pathogenesis of post-transplant recurrent FSGS. Shirai et al.38 performed a multiinstitutional study of 22 Japanese KTRs with FSGS—14 with primary FSGS and 8 with genetic FSGS. Among patients with primary FSGS, 11 experienced a recurrence of the disease, and median plasma levels of antinephrin antibodies were markedly high in those patients, either before transplantation or during recurrence. Graft biopsies in recurrent patients showed punctate IgG deposition colocalized with nephrin and nephrin tyrosine phosphorylation. These depositions and the nephrin pattern disappeared in graft biopsies performed after remission. Patients with primary FSGS without recurrence and those with genetic FSGS did not show these modifications on the graft biopsy and had antinephrin antibody levels comparable to those of 30 control individuals.38 Furthermore, Batal et al.39 conducted a retrospective multicenter study of 39 North American and European KTRs with minimal change disease or FSGS as their initial nephropathy. They showed that positive pretransplant antibodies were associated with a higher risk of recurrent disease (HR: 4.9; P < 0.001) and had 100% specificity and 38% sensitivity for predicting recurrence. If validated in larger cohorts, antinephrin antibody screening in pretransplant patients may be useful in the future to identify patients at risk of recurrent FSGS.39 Recently, Habbig et al.40 described a pediatric patient with FSGS and evidence of antinephrin antibodies before undergoing a living-donor KT. Treatment with rituximab and plasma exchange lowered antibody levels before transplantation. No signs of disease recurrence in the transplanted kidney were noted.40

Another interesting observation aimed at assessing disease activity and anticipating recurrence before KT was reported by Gupta et al.41 who proposed using kidney organoids generated from pluripotent stem cells for detecting permeability factors in FSGS. Applying diseased plasma to the organoids revealed podocytopathy, extracellular matrix protein deposition, fibrosis, and apoptosis. Furthermore, organoids treated with plasma obtained from the final round of therapeutic plasma exchange exhibited the least injury. Therefore, by providing a 3-dimensional model that mimics human glomerular architecture, this approach offers a promising platform for future research.41

After transplantation, the 2009 KDIGO guidelines suggest screening KTRs with primary kidney disease caused by FSGS for proteinuria (grade 2C) at least daily for 1 week, weekly for 4 weeks, every 3 months for the first year, and then annually (grade 2D)42 (Table 4).

There is no standardized treatment for the recurrence of FSGS, but the 2009 KDIGO guidelines suggest plasma exchanges.42 International cohort studies reveal that the majority of patients receive treatment with therapeutic plasma exchange, often combined with rituximab32 (Table 4). Given the absence of sufficient evidence, a preemptive use of rituximab or plasmapheresis is not recommended. A systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that preemptive rituximab, with or without plasmapheresis, as well as plasmapheresis alone, did not show an association with a reduced risk of FSGS recurrence following KT.43 High-dose or intravenous calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine or tacrolimus) are used as adjunctive therapy, though evidence is primarily from case series.44 In the future, newer B cell therapies could help in developing targeted therapeutic strategies44). Recent case reports suggest obinutuzumab and daratumumab combination therapy may be effective for rituximab-refractory recurrent FSGS. Randone et al.45 reported 2 cases where combined B-cell/plasma cell targeting with obinutuzumab (1000 mg/1.73 m2, 1 dose) plus daratumumab (18 mg/kg, 8 doses) achieved sustained proteinuria reduction in patients who failed rituximab and plasmapheresis. This dual approach targets both B cells (via obinutuzumab) and plasma cells producing circulating permeability factors (via daratumumab), addressing a key limitation of anti-CD20 monotherapy. Although experience remains limited to case reports, this represents a promising salvage strategy for severe, treatment-resistant recurrent FSGS, particularly in plasmapheresis-dependent patients.45

Another emerging therapy relies on lipid apheresis. Lipid apheresis has US Food and Drug Administration humanitarian device exemption approval for treatment of resistant FSGS with glomerular filtration rate ≥ 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2. In published series totaling approximately 141 patients, 58% achieved complete remission, 15% partial benefit, and 27% had little benefit. Predictors of response include younger age, concurrent glucocorticoid use, shorter disease duration, and better baseline renal function. However, data are limited by lack of randomized trials and its application in the kidney transplant setting is still to be explored.46

Because of the high risk of FSGS recurrence, transplant teams can be reluctant to provide living donor KT for these patients. However, if the risk of recurrence is weighed up and discussed, it remains a reasonable option. For patients who have lost a previous graft because of disease recurrence and are at high risk of recurrence in a subsequent allograft, careful consideration is mandatory.47 In these cases, UK guidelines recommend considering living donor KT only in exceptional circumstances, following a thorough evaluation by a multidisciplinary team and discussion of the risks of recurrence, graft loss, and treatment options with both the recipient and the donor.29 Progression to kidney failure within 3 years is another risk factor for recurrence; living donation remains reasonable but requires thorough counseling.23

Atypical Hemolytic and Uremic Syndrome

For this section, the KDIGO and UK guidelines were used as reference standards.

Atypical hemolytic and uremic syndrome (aHUS) (MIM #235400) is triggered by genetic (accounting for 50 % of cases)48 or acquired abnormalities of the complement alternative pathway and clinically manifests with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) associated with significantly poor renal outcomes in the absence of treatment.49 In view of the therapeutic implications in KT, it is recommended to screen all KT candidates with a history of demonstrated (biologic or histologic) TMA for a possible genetic abnormality using a complement pathway panel50 (Table 5). However, recognizing a genetic form of aHUS remains challenging. Initial screening for aHUS patients involves testing for anticomplement factor H(CFH) antibodies and for variants in complement genes that may have therapeutic implications in KT, namely CFH, C3, CFI, CFB, MCP, C5, and CFD.7 In view of the importance of genetic findings on transplant decision-making and post-transplant management, it is recommended to screen all kidney transplant candidates with a history of biologic or histologic TMA using a comprehensive complement pathway gene panel.6,51 To date, more than 500 variants in these 5 complement genes have been identified in patients with aHUS.52 Despite this, identifying a constitutional complement dysregulation fails in 40% to 60% of patients with aHUS.48,52,53 Other biochemical markers of complement system dysregulation – as measurement of sC5b-9 in plasma, C5b-9 depositions in kidney biopsies, and ex-vivo test for C5b-9 endothelial deposition—52,53 are also available, or are in clinical development to help in the diagnosis of aHUS, but their clinical relevance remains to be demonstrated. Serum complement measurements might also be helpful by providing important functional assessment. In aHUS, C3 levels are typically reduced (mean 72.1 mg/dl vs. reference 75–175 mg/dl), whereas C4 levels are usually normal (mean 20.4 mg/dl vs. reference 14–40 mg/dl), reflecting alternative pathway activation. CH50 is reduced (mean 28.3 U/ml vs. reference 30–75 U/ml) and AH50 (alternative pathway hemolytic activity) is markedly decreased (mean 27.6% vs. reference ≥ 46%). Factor H levels may be normal or reduced depending on the specific defect. Complement activation markers including C5a, C5b-9 (sC5b-9), and Bb are typically elevated, reflecting ongoing complement activation.54 However, normal complement levels do not exclude aHUS, as functional defects may exist despite normal protein concentrations.55 Exclusion of causes of secondary TMA (severe hypertension, eclampsia, systemic sclerosis, stem cell transplantation, or chemotherapy) is critical for definitive diagnosis of aHUS. Whether systemic screening of complement genetic abnormalities is required in secondary HUS is uncertain. Emerging data suggest that some patients with secondary HUS actually have genetic defects in complement.56, 57, 58, 59, 60 The most illustrative report57 included 26 patients with hypertensive emergency and TMA confirmed by kidney biopsy. Rare complement gene variants were present in 8 patients (31%). Sixty-one percent of patients progressed to kidney failure within 3 months of diagnosis, 5 of 7 patients responded at least partially to eculizumab, and 4 KTRs experienced TMA recurrence in 7 kidney grafts.

The risk stratification for aHUS recurrence after KT is mostly based on genetic considerations.61, 62, 63, 64, 65 High-risk refers to the transplantations performed in patients who had experienced a recurrence with a previous allograft and/or harbor a pathogenic variant of the CFH/C3/CFB genes. aHUS patients who have either a negative complement screening result or a pathogenic variant in the CFI gene or detectable circulating anti-CFH antibody are considered at moderate risk. Low-risk transplantations include isolated mutations in the MCP and DGKE genes, or anti-CFH antibodies no longer detected at the time of transplantation in patients in whom positive anti-CFH antibody testing had been previously identified (Table 3).

Before the introduction of eculizumab, a C5 inhibitor, the majority of patients with aHUS developed KF, with post-KT outcomes characterized by poor prognosis because of a high recurrence rate of 60% to 80%, which was strongly correlated with allograft failure.48 Eculizumab has shown to be effective in inducing remission during acute episodes of aHUS and maintaining long-term remission in both native and transplanted kidneys.52,62,66, 67, 68, 69 Therefore, prophylactic use of eculizumab to prevent aHUS recurrence after KT in genetically high and moderate-risk patients is recommended61 and has been applied by most centers since 201367 (Table 3). Eculizumab is recommended to be administrated immediately before surgery or intraoperatively, with the first dose at 900–1200 mg (depending on weight). Standard weight-based dosing is 900 mg weekly for 4 doses, then 1200 mg at week 5, followed by 1200 mg every 2 weeks maintenance.70 Some centers use individualized dosing based on complement activity monitoring (follow up of CH50).71 During treatment, it is recommended to regularly assess complement activity (CH50 and AH50), lactate dehydrogenase, haptoglobin, platelet count, and renal function. Some centers also monitor eculizumab concentrations.71

The KDIGO consensus advice62 for lifelong prophylaxis after KT, especially in case of high-risk mutations, is not supported by randomized data. Some studies have suggested that eculizumab could be stopped in patients with aHUS in native kidneys, as relapses appear to respond effectively to the prompt reinitiation of anti-C5 treatment.71, 72, 73, 74 These considerations are important given the cost of the drug and the constraints on healthcare systems.75

Ravulizumab, another anti-C5 monoclonal antibody with a longer half-life, allowing for a more extended interval between infusions, exhibits similar efficacy and safety to eculizumab in the treatment of patients with aHUS. However, experience in kidney transplant patients is limited.76 Compared with eculizumab, ravuzilumab has a longer half-life allowing dosing (depending on weight) every 8 weeks. Recommendations regarding biologic surveillance during treatment are similar to those with eculizumab.

When considering a living KT in patients with aHUS, the aspects of the genetic disease in a related donor and the risk of recurrence must be carefully weighed. The first consideration is to rule out the same genetic disease in a related donor candidate. The UK guidelines state that though living-unrelated transplantation may be considered, living-related KT should be avoided in cases of aHUS unless the etiology of the disease in the recipient has been clearly identified and ruled out in the donor. Even under these circumstances, related donors may carry an elevated risk of developing aHUS and should be appropriately informed of this potential risk.29 The development of HUS in the donor was reported in the following 4 cases: 1 case involved a CFH mutation that was discovered retrospectively, whereas the other 3 had no identified mutations in CFH, MCP, CFB, or CFI. Nephrectomy could act as a trigger in these patients, who may have an undetected genetic susceptibility.77 The KDIGO guidelines are consistent with previous recommendations, highlighting that current genetic testing cannot reliably exclude aHUS in donor candidates, even when the recipient’s mutation is known.78

C3G

For this section, the KDIGO and UK guidelines were used as reference standards.

C3G (MIM #614809) is a rare disease resulting from abnormal regulation of the alternative complement pathway with C3-dominant glomerular deposits on immunofluorescence, and absent or minimal Ig staining. It encompasses 2 subtypes as follows: dense deposit disease and C3 glomerulonephritis).79

The dysregulation of the complement alternative pathway in C3G is most commonly induced by autoantibodies that stabilize C3 convertase or are directed against other complement regulatory proteins and less commonly by an inherited defect. Pathogenic variants in the alternative complement pathway, the same genes implicated in aHUS, are detected in about a third of patients. Screening for genetic or acquired causes of complement alternative pathway to guide treatment and inform risk of recurrence is suggested before transplantation (level of evidence: 2C).80

The treatment of native C3G is based on disease severity, and involves mycophenolate mofetil plus corticosteroids in patients with moderate-to-severe disease, even though there is no established optimal treatment strategy. If this initial approach fails, eculizumab is considered (expert opinion), but has shown limited benefits.23,80,81 Recently, data on oral complement inhibitors have been published. In the APPEAR- C3G study, iptacopan, a Factor B inhibitor, showed a statistically significant reduction of 35.1% in 24-hour urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio at 6 months (P = 0.0014, 95% CI: 13.8%–51.1%) versus placebo.82

Despite the use of mycophenolate mofetil, corticosteroids, and calcineurin inhibitors after KT, recurrence of the disease is frequent in patients with C3G, ranging from 67% to 84%, with a median time to recurrence of 14 to 28 months83,84 (Table 3).

Recurrence is often aggressive with a significant impact on graft survival.22,85 In contrast to aHUS, there is currently no evidence to suggest a direct link between genetic defects and the recurrence of disease following transplantation. Treatment of recurrence follow KDIGO 2021 guidelines that suggest maximal conservative, antiproteinuric therapy, and rituximab if proteinuria exceeds 1g/d23 (Table 4).

The role of new complement inhibitors in the treatment of recurrent C3 glomerulonephritis after transplantation has been evaluated on limited number of patients in clinical trials.85 In the Novel of Blocking Lectin-pathway in End-stage renal disease trial, 8 transplanted patients with recurrent C3G received pegcetacoplan, a C3 inhibitor, in addition to the standard of care and were compared with 2 patients who received standard of care alone. At week 12, the intensity of C3c staining in kidney biopsies significantly decreased from baseline in the pegcetacoplan group (P = 0.0094). A median reduction of 54% in urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio was observed at week 12 in the pegcetacoplan group, compared with 1.3% in the standard of care group. Kidney function remained stable over 12 weeks in both groups.86 The VALIANT trial is a randomized, multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 study evaluating pegcetacoplan in 124 patients with native or post-transplant recurrent glomerulopathy or primary immune complex membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis. A 68.3% reduction in the urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio was observed in the pegcetacoplan arm compared with the placebo arm at week 26 (95% CI: −76.3 to −57.7; P < 0.0001) (Table 4).87 The extension phase of the VALIANT trial, known as the VALE study, will evaluate the long-term safety and efficacy of pegcetacoplan.

Iptacopan has been evaluated in a phase 2 study. Eleven adult KTRs with biopsy-proven C3G recurrence received iptacopan 200 mg twice daily for 12 weeks.88 These patients were at least 3 months post-transplant, on stable immunosuppression, and had no evidence of rejection on biopsy. Repeat kidney biopsies showed a statistically significant decrease in the median C3 deposit score on Day 84 compared with baseline. Serum C3 levels also improved whereas eGFR and urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio remained stable. Iptacopan was well tolerated. An open-label extension study (NCT03955445) is now ongoing to evaluate the longer-term efficacy and safety of iptacopan. Iptacopan is now the first and only US Food and Drug Administration-approved treatment for C3G after the phase 3 APPEAR-C3G trial showed a 35% sustained proteinuria reduction at 12 months.89 Longer-term studies will be important in determining their value in real life clinical scenarios. A recent report of 2 cases of kidney transplant patients with recurrent GNC3 treated with iptacopan showed a favorable clinical evolution with a good tolerance.90

Narsoplimab (OMS721) is a monoclonal antibody targeting mannan-binding lectin-associated serine protease-2, a key enzyme in the lectin complement pathway, and is currently being tested in a phase II trial (NCT02682407) for patients with native C3G. Danicopan, an orally administered Factor D inhibitor, was evaluated in patients with native kidney C3G but failed to achieve the optimal concentration needed for effective ACP inhibition, resulting in clinical inefficacy. Similarly, BCX9930, another Factor D inhibitor in native C3G patients in the phase II open label RENEW trial (NCT05162066), has also been terminated.91

Given the high risk of disease recurrence, transplantation from a living donor, although not an absolute contraindication, warrants careful risk assessment, and discussion involving a multidisciplinary team, the recipient and the donor.29 (Table 5) Particular caution should be exercised when considering living-related donors of candidates with genetic abnormalities in complement proteins or in cases where the cause of C3G is unknown, as these individuals may carry similar genetic mutations and could therefore be at risk of developing C3G in the future.92 Counseling can become even more challenging when both the donor and the recipient carry variants of undetermined significance. Patients who have previously lost a graft because of recurrent C3G are at such a high risk of recurrence upon subsequent transplantation that this factor must be a key consideration when evaluating candidacy.79

PH

For this section, the UK guidelines were used as reference standards.

PH refers to a group of rare autosomal recessive diseases, characterized by a hepatic overproduction of oxalate. The persistently high oxalate results in recurrent kidney stones, nephrocalcinosis, oxalate nephropathy, chronic kidney disease, and ESKD. As kidney function declines < 30 to 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2, systemic accumulation of oxalate occurs.93, 94, 95 Three types of PH have been described, depending on the hepatic enzymatic defect involved. PH1 (MIM #259900) is the most frequent and severe (approximately 80% of cases),94 and is caused by a deficiency or loss of activity of peroxisomal alanine glyoxylate aminotransferase, an enzyme involved in the final step of glyoxylate metabolism. The kidney function outcome for patients with PH1 is very poor, with most patients reaching KF within the first 2 to 3 decades of life and nearly all by the age of 50.96

The risk of recurrence of oxalate nephropathy in the kidney graft is high, driven by systemic oxalosis, which can precipitate in the newly transplanted kidney and lead to irreversible damage. It has been extensively reported in PH193, 94, 95,97 and, more recently, in PH2 (MIM #260000).98, 99, 100 The risk of recurrence of PH is over 90% if KT is performed without correction of the underlying hepatic metabolic defect,42 and the risk of graft loss because of recurrent disease is high, with 5-year graft survival rates as low as 14% to 45%.99 Combined liver-KT significantly improves graft survival with 5-year rates of 48% to 89%.99 Long term data with Lumasiran are too limited to determine a recurrence rate.

Diagnosing the disease before KT is crucial. Consequently, all KT candidates with a history of kidney stones and/or nephrocalcinosis should be genetically tested for PH before transplantation, using a genetic panel for kidney stones. KT alone, without correcting the hepatic metabolic defect, is not a good option for patients with confirmed PH1, and possibly PH2, except in a few highly selected cases (e.g., patients responsive to vitamin B6). Liver transplantation has proven efficient in treating the metabolic defect,100 but faces 2 major challenges—organ availability and the morbimortality associated with the procedure.100,101 In all cases, achieving low plasma oxalate level before KT is required.

Recent advances have been made in the field. Lumasiran, an RNA interference drug that targets the liver enzyme glycolate oxidase (GO), has proven safe and efficient to decrease the liver production of oxalate in the Illuminate trials, including patients with severe kidney dysfunction, who are potential candidates for KT.102 It has opened the door to a transplant strategy considering kidney alone transplantation in PH1 patients and thus avoiding liver transplantation. The first French case series103 included 5 patients treated with Lumasiran for 5 to 17 months who successfully underwent isolated KT. In the Illuminate C trial, 5 patients treated with lumasiran from 6.7 to 32.5 months also received a successful isolated transplantation (unpublished data). In both studies, no patient experienced early massive oxalate nephropathy recurrence (Table 3).

For a living donor candidate, this recent medical advance has implications. Indeed, the risk of donating a single kidney is far lower for the donor than donating a combined or sequential liver-kidney. It also enables an earlier access to transplantation reducing the time spent on chronic dialysis, a well-known risk factor for systemic oxalosis associated with an increased risk of oxalate recurrence after KT.95 Furthermore, living donor KT is associated with a reduced risk of delayed graft function compared with deceased-donor KT. Though not extensively investigated, delayed graft function has been associated with oxalate recurrence. Despite these encouraging advances, the experience is still limited and the follow-up of these patients after transplantation is still relatively short. In that context, it remains important to discuss with the living donor candidate the risk of oxalate release from bone despite mRNA interference treatment and the probability that oxalate production might not be completely normalized in some patients, leading oxalate to be added to the amount already stored in bones.104

In the near future, gene editing therapy might modify this strategy. The redePhine study is currently recruiting (NCT06839235) to test the ABO-101 which is a potential 1-time CRISPR gene editing therapy targeting the HAO1-gene encoding for glycolate oxidase (Table 4).

AL Amyloidosis

For this section, the UK guidelines were used as reference standards.

AL amyloidosis is an uncommon disorder where systemic amyloid deposition is caused by fibrils composed of fragments of monoclonal light chains. Patients with this condition may have amyloidosis alone or in association with other plasma cell dyscrasias, such as multiple myeloma or Waldenström macroglobulinemia.

AL amyloidosis was previously associated with a poor prognosis, but survival rates have improved over the past 20 years with the emergence of new treatments, such as high-dose melphalan with autologous stem cell transplantation, protease inhibitors, and monoclonal antibodies. This has led to an increased incidence of chronic kidney disease progressing to KF.105 Historically, KT was limited by the occurrence of early graft loss and shorter overall survival because of disease recurrence. Recent studies have demonstrated favorable KT outcomes in patients who respond well to hematologic treatment. Indeed, the risk of recurrence is estimated overall at 16% to 37% but the risk of graft loss because of recurrence is < 15% in patients with sustained hematologic response.106 In 2019, Angel-Korman et al.107 reported a series of 49 patients with AL amyloidosis who underwent KT in the United States between 1987 and 2017. Over a median follow-up period of 7.2 years, the median overall survival from diagnosis and from KT were 15.4 and 10.5 years, respectively, comparable to other high-risk populations, including patients >65 years or those with diabetes. Graft survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were 94%, 89%, and 81%, respectively. Patients who achieved a hematologic complete response or a very good partial response before KT had significantly better survival rates compared with those with only a partial response or no response, with a median time to graft loss of 10.4 years for the complete response/ very good partial response group and 5.5 years for the others.107 Fourteen patients received treatment for recurrent amyloidosis in the graft, with a median time from KT to recurrence of 3.5 years. Graft loss because of amyloidosis was reported in only 4 patients, indicating that the second or third lines of treatment can prevent graft loss. In this study, the recommendation was to wait 6 to 12 months after achieving a hematologic response to document its durability before proceeding with transplantation. Most of the patients (65%) in this study had a living donor.107 Havasi et al.108 also reported a multicenter, observational study including 237 patients with AL amyloidosis who underwent KT between 1987 and 2020. In their cohort, patients with complete response and very good partial response had the most favorable patient and graft survival. Twenty-nine percent of the cohort experienced hematological relapse that was treated effectively preventing graft loss in the majority (87%).108 Future studies will need to determine whether KT can be a valid option for those patients who achieve a durable hematologic response accompanied by a cardiac response.109

Data from the US organ procurement and transplantation network database supports this improved trend of patient and graft survival. Yamauchi et al.106 analyzed transplant outcomes of 643 kidney recipients with amyloidosis compared with those with diabetic and nondiabetic causes of KF between 2002 and 2021. They found no significant differences in graft and patient survival between kidney recipients with amyloidosis and those with diabetes. Outcomes were also not significantly different when comparing amyloidosis to nondiabetic causes of KF, except for patient survival among living donor KTRs, where patients with amyloidosis had lower survival rates (P = 0.04),106 a finding probably related to confounding factors.

For living donor transplantation, the UK guidelines state that it is a reasonable treatment option once adequate control of the underlying disease has been achieved and recommend that patients with amyloidosis be discussed with the National Amyloidosis Centre before proceeding with living donor transplantation.29

Conclusion

Medical advances are constantly changing our attitude towards eligibility for transplantation for both recipients and living donors. Kidney diseases that were once considered to carry a very high risk of recurrence and consequent graft loss are being better understood through the elucidation of pathogenesis, the description of new antibodies, and the availability of innovative treatments. This leads to specific considerations in the decision-making process for living donation. There is no doubt that this landscape will continue to evolve as the field advances.
