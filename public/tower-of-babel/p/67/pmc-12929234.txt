Deep learning–based automated positioning system for maxillary skeletal expander: development and clinical validation
Authors: Jie Pan, Zhenpeng Liu, Yixin Liu, Yu Zhang, Mengqi Xu, Xiaozhe Li, Xianming Chen, Guangshun Wei, Dongxu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning-based artificial intelligence (AI) system capable of automatically generating accurate MSE placement plans to enhance clinical efficiency, safety, and consistency.MethodsA total of 120 patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion and transverse maxillary deficiency (ages 10–39) were included. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) and intraoral scan data were used to construct individualized three-dimensional anatomical coordinate systems. A deep learning model was trained to automatically segment key anatomical structures (e.g., the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture) and identify essential craniofacial landmarks. The MSE was then automatically positioned within this coordinate system, while a collision detection algorithm ensured appropriate spacing from the palatal mucosa and avoided vital anatomical structures. Model performance was compared with manual placement using metrics such as mean Intersection over Union (mIoU), Avoidance Success Rate (ASR), Mean Radial Error (MRE), Mean Angular Error (MAE), and Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC).ResultsThe average mIoU of the segmentation network was 0.75. The ASR exceeded 90%, demonstrating effective anatomical avoidance. The automated system achieved an axial MRE of 0.32 ± 0.32 mm, a three-dimensional (3D) Euclidean distance error of 0.69 ± 0.36 mm, and an MAE of 1.84 ± 2.13°, with CCC values above 0.90 for linear and translational directions and between 0.87 and 0.93 for angular directions. These results showed no statistically significant differences from manual placement (P > 0.05).Bland–Altman analysis further showed minimal mean bias between AI and manual planning (translation bias ≤ 0.047 mm; rotation bias within − 0.26° to 1.52°), with 95% limits of agreement for all parameters remaining within clinically acceptable ranges; a mild proportional bias was observed only for yaw (P = 0.013).The automated process required an average of 3 min, significantly faster than the 45–60 minutes typically required for manual planning.ConclusionThe proposed deep learning–based automated MSE positioning system enables accurate and efficient MSE digital planning in a retrospective setting, shows promising clinical potential, and may contribute to more standardized and intelligent orthodontic expansion workflows.Clinical relevanceThis deep learning–driven tool may facilitate standardized MSE placement, shorten treatment planning time, and support clinicians with limited experience, thereby enhancing the safety and efficiency of maxillary skeletal expansion in clinical practice.Keywords: Maxillary skeletal expander, Deep learning, CBCT, Automated positioning, Artificial intelligence, Intraoral scan

Introduction

Maxillary transverse deficiency (MTD) is a prevalent skeletal malocclusion that compromises not only facial harmony but also periodontal health and essential oral functions, such as mastication and speech [1–3]. Epidemiological studies report a prevalence of 13% – 23% in children and up to 30% in adults [4, 5], underscoring its significant clinical burden. Early diagnosis and intervention are therefore critical to improving patients’ oral function, aesthetics, and quality of life.

For the treatment of MTD, children are primarily treated with Rapid Palatal Expansion (RPE), which uses growth potential to separate the palatal suture [6, 7]. However, as individuals age, the palatal suture gradually ossifies, limiting the effectiveness of traditional RPE. Adults require more effective and less invasive treatment options [8, 9]. In recent years, the micro-implant-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE) technique has emerged as an important solution for adult MTD. The maxillary skeletal expander (MSE), introduced by Won Moon and colleagues, utilizes micro-implants placed in the posterior maxilla to directly counteract expansion resistance. This approach markedly improves the success rate of skeletal expansion, minimizes side effects such as tooth tipping and bone resorption—common complications of traditional expansion methods—and has seen widespread clinical adoption [10–12].

In clinical applications of the MSE, the accuracy of its positioning design directly impacts both the safety and effectiveness of the treatment. Incorrect placement of the MSE may lead to inadequate or asymmetric expansion, and in severe cases, it can even damage teeth, bone tissue, and oral mucosal health [13, 14]. In the past, MSE positioning typically relied on plaster models, which, while providing physical dimensions and anatomical features of the patient’s oral cavity, lacked 3D visualization of the bony structures. This approach is complex, less safe, and limited in accuracy [15]. With the advancement of digital technology, digital-assisted design based on CBCT images and intraoral scan models has gradually become mainstream, significantly improving design efficiency and accuracy [16–18]. However, the current process still requires extensive manual intervention by the clinician, including anatomical structure identification, bone volume assessment, and implant positioning planning. This process is cumbersome, heavily reliant on the clinician’s experience, and difficult to standardize or automate.

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) technology, centered on deep learning and computer vision, has made significant strides in medical imaging diagnosis, orthodontic design, tooth positioning, and implant planning [19–22]. For instance, Dai et al. [19] used a deep learning model to automatically measure the maxillary width to assist in diagnosing MTD; Woodsend et al. [21] employed AI and machine learning techniques to fully automate tooth landmark identification. The advantages of AI in big data analysis and feature recognition provide novel perspectives and solutions to overcome the challenges faced in traditional MSE positioning design.

This study aimed to develop and evaluate an AI-assisted workflow for automatic MSE positioning based on CBCT and intraoral scan data. By automating key steps (landmark identification, critical-structure segmentation, and pose optimization with avoidance constraints), the system is designed to reduce manual workload and improve planning consistency.

Materials and methods

Overall workflow

Figure 1 provides an overview of the proposed AI-assisted workflow for automatic MSE positioning. CBCT, intraoral scan (IOS), and the digital MSE model were used as inputs. The workflow includes five sequential steps: (1) landmark detection, (2) construction of the maxillary and MSE 3D coordinate systems, (3) segmentation of critical structures (incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture), (4) CBCT–IOS registration, and (5) constrained pose search with collision avoidance. The output is the final MSE pose (position and orientation parameters).The detailed implementation of each step is described in the following sections.

Dataset description

In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Stomatology, Shandong University (Approval No. 20250715). All data were collected from the Department of Orthodontics, School of Stomatology, Shandong University (Jinan, Shandong, China). Because MSE is primarily indicated for patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion and transverse maxillary deficiency (MTD), we restricted the cohort to this population to best reflect real-world clinical scenarios for model development. Inclusion criteria were a confirmed diagnosis of skeletal Class III with MTD, defined as: (a) ANB angle < 0°, and (b) Yonsei index < 2.26 mm. The Yonsei index was defined as the distance between the centers of resistance (CR) of the bilateral maxillary first molars minus the distance between the CRs of the bilateral mandibular first molars. Exclusion criteria were: (a) poor CBCT image quality (e.g., blurring, excessive noise, or low resolution) that could compromise identification of osseous landmarks; (b) incomplete maxillary coverage or insufficient scan range preventing clear visualization of key bony landmarks; (c) maxillary fractures or other pathological changes (e.g., osteoporosis, hyperostosis, or maxillary asymmetry) that could interfere with normal anatomical landmarking; and (d) history of maxillary expansion or orthognathic surgery, which may alter native maxillary anatomy and is unsuitable for training an AI model based on normal anatomy.

Between 2021 and 2025, 147 patients were initially screened; 27 were excluded due to poor image quality, incomplete maxillary coverage, or a history of maxillary surgery. Finally, 120 patients with skeletal Class III malocclusion and MTD were included (70 females and 50 males; mean age 17.5 years, range 10–39 years). All patients underwent CBCT scanning using a NewTom 5G system (Verona, Italy) with the following parameters: voxel size 0.3 mm, scan time 4.8 s, field of view 18 × 16 cm, 110 kV, and 7.33 mA. Scans were acquired according to the manufacturer’s recommended protocol and saved in DICOM format; the mean CBCT matrix size was 610 × 610 pixels with 538 slices. Intraoral scans were obtained using an iTero Element 2 scanner (Align Technology, San Jose, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol, with full coverage of the maxillary palatal region to accurately capture palatal mucosal morphology. IOS data were saved in STL format and used for subsequent model training and validation.

Model Preparation

To ensure the robustness of the evaluation, the dataset was randomly divided into three parts: the training set, validation set, and testing set, before training the deep learning model. The training set (n = 80) was used to train the CBCT keypoint detection network and segmentation network; the validation set (n = 10) was used to monitor network performance during training; and the testing set (n = 30) was used to evaluate network accuracy and the final performance metrics of the entire process. These three subsets are independent of each other, with no data leakage.

Data annotation and segmentation

Anatomical landmark annotation and coordinate system establishment

CBCT images were manually annotated using Mimics software (version 21.0, Materialise, Belgium). The annotation process was performed by two experienced orthodontists. Both clinicians underwent standardized training on using Mimics for landmark identification and marking to minimize observer variability. Additionally, a double-checking strategy was employed: after the first clinician completed the initial annotation, the second clinician independently reviewed the marked points. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, ensuring the accuracy and consistency of the annotations. Finally, the annotated results were saved in XML format, including the coordinates of each landmark. Specifically, multiple bony landmarks in the mid-facial region were identified in the 3D CBCT images of the patients, which were then used to establish reference planes and create the X, Y, Z coordinate system for the maxilla. The first reference plane is the Median Sagittal Plane (MSP), passing through the ANS, PNS, and N points (Fig. 2C). The reference points are defined as follows: ANS (anterior nasal spine, the anterior point of the hard palate), PNS (posterior nasal spine, the posterior point of the hard palate), and N (nasal root, the most anterior point of the nasofrontal suture). The second reference plane is the Horizontal Palatal Plane (HPP), defined as perpendicular to the MSP and passing through the MS5 and MS7 points (Fig. 2D). The reference points are defined as: MS5, the point on the palatal midline at the level of the second maxillary premolar, and MS7, the point on the palatal midline at the level of the second maxillary molar. The final reference plane is the Coronal Maxillary Plane (CMP), defined as perpendicular to both the MSP and HPP and passing through the Center of Maxilla (CM) point (Fig. 2E). The CM point is determined by orthogonally projecting the line connecting the ZR and ZL points onto the HPP, and identifying the intersection of this projection with the MSP. ZR and ZL are the outermost points of the zygomatic process on both sides of the maxilla. The CM point serves as the origin of the maxillary coordinate. The MSP, HPP, and CMP planes together define the three-dimensional coordinate system of the maxilla [17] (Fig. 2F).

Segmentation and identification of the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture

The segmentation of the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture was also performed using Mimics software (version 21.0, Materialise, Belgium). The entire process was carried out collaboratively by two experienced clinicians who had undergone standardized training, ensuring consistency and accuracy in the segmentation results. The incisive foramen is located at the anterior end of the palatal suture of the maxillary palatal process, near the palatal side of the upper central incisors; the transverse palatine suture is located at the posterior part of the palatal bone, connecting the maxillary palatal process and the horizontal plate of the palatine bone. The clinicians adjusted the tomographic views to accurately determine the position and orientation of the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture (Fig. 3A-B). Once the locations were identified, the segmentation tool in Mimics was used to manually trace the structures layer by layer, precisely outlining the contours based on the grayscale variation at the edges of the structures in each layer. The process was performed continuously layer by layer to ensure complete extraction of the target structures in three-dimensional space (Figs. 3C-D).After segmentation, the extracted structures were reconstructed in 3D using Mimics and exported in DICOM format, providing a standardized data foundation for subsequent model training and automatic localization algorithms (Fig. 3E).

Network training

Landmark detection

This study references the SA-LSTM 3D landmark detection framework proposed by Chen et al. [23] and constructs a deep learning network to predict landmarks, with a new loss function designed according to the task requirements. The pipeline of the landmark detection framework is shown in Fig. 4. The network divides the landmark detection process into two stages: coarse localization and fine localization. In the coarse stage, 3D U-Net is used to learn global features from down sampled CBCT data and predict 3D heatmaps for each landmark. The 3D U-Net consists of three conv layers and one bottom layer, with 32, 64, 128 and 256 channels. Each conv layer containing two 3*3*3 convolution with Batch Normalization and ReLU. The approximate coordinates of each landmark are then obtained by regressing the heatmap, which guides the cropping of the CBCT image for the fine localization stage. The fine stage utilizes a three-layer LSTM network, which is essentially the same as the core structure of SA-LSTM. The size of the input CBCT cropped blocks decreases layer by layer. Except for the first layer, which is guided by the results from the coarse localization, the cropping area for the other layers is determined by the predictions from the previous layer. The sizes of the cropping blocks for each layer are cubes with edge lengths of 32, 16, and 8 voxels, respectively. During training, we improved the loss function for the fine localization stage by adding an orientation loss (Lori) to the existing position error loss (Lc). The orientation loss is defined as the total angle between the normal vectors of the reference planes determined by the model’s predicted results and the annotated ground truth (Lori = dMP + dPP + dCP).The improved fine localization loss function is expressed as Lc = (1 – α)Lc + αLori, where α = 0.1 is the weight of the orientation loss, determined through experiments.

Pipeline of the landmark detection framework. Fα is a 3D U-Net used for coarse landmark detection. Fβ is the visual feature encoder, and Fγ is the graph attention module. The coarse stage uses low-resolution data as input. The fine stage uses cropped data as Fβ‘s input, which is guided by the prediction result of the previous layer. We use three attention-gated modules to build our fine prediction network, and use the third layer’s output as the final landmark prediction result

Segmentation

Due to the large size of the original CBCT and the low detail requirements in the segmentation area for collision detection, in order to balance the cost and accuracy, this study uses a 2D U-Net to perform layer-by-layer segmentation prediction on the annotated CBCT data. The pipeline of the segmentation network is shown in Fig. 5. We used a four-layer U-Net network structure, with a loss function that combines Dice loss and cross-entropy loss, and the segmentation accuracy was evaluated using the mean Intersection over Union (mIoU). Before training, several preprocessing steps were applied to the images, including: automatic cropping to remove image regions without the target area (ensuring a minimum of 30 pixels between the target area and the image border in 256 × 256 pixel images), normalization of the image grayscale values, random rotation and translation for data augmentation, and extraction of the labeled target area as an independent binary mask.

U-Net convolutional neural network pipeline for automated segmentation of the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture

After segmentation of each slice, the results needed to be combined and refined. Specifically, for continuous slice segmentation results of the same anatomical structure, slices with a segmentation area larger than a given threshold (50 pixels in a 256 × 256 image) were selected, and only the group of consecutive slices with the largest span was retained. Next, based on the segmented regions of this group of slices, region growing was performed according to the original grayscale CBCT image: for each 2D slice, the segmented region was expanded into neighboring regions with similar grayscale values, and the results from all layers were combined to obtain the final segmentation of the target anatomical structure. Since both the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture are cavities within bony tissue with low and stable grayscale values, the region growing threshold was set within 25 grayscale units above and below the average grayscale value of the segmented region.

Automatic positioning of the expander

Coordinate system initialization and initial registration

The MSE (BioMaterials Korea, Seoul, Korea) has a regular geometric shape, and its own coordinate system can be spatially aligned with the maxillary anatomical coordinate system using a preset algorithm. In this study, we first constructed the MSP, HPP, and CMP reference planes using the output of the landmark detection network based on the previously defined reference plane method, and established a three-dimensional reference coordinate system for the maxilla using the normal vectors of these planes. Next, a coordinate mapping function was used to align the predefined MSE local coordinate system with the above maxillary reference coordinate system, automatically setting the initial position of the expander (Fig. 6B).The MSE local coordinate system is defined as follows: the center point of the mucosal side of the appliance (CA point) is set as the origin, the X-axis is defined along the short side of the expander, the Y-axis is defined along the long side, and the positive direction of the Z-axis is defined along the direction of the micro-implant (Fig. 6B). In the initial setup, the MSE’s X (left-right), Y (front-back), and Z (up-down) axes are strictly aligned with the corresponding axes of the maxillary coordinate system, serving as the reference posture for subsequent pose optimization.

Mucosal clearance and safety margin screening

After the coordinate system alignment and initial position setting, the system automatically adjusts the MSE’s posture to prevent compression of the palatal mucosa and to find a reasonable posture that meets the palatal mucosa gap requirements. This process is achieved by controlling the vertical displacement and rotation angle of the MSE in a coordinated manner. The specific process is as follows: the MSE is moved along the maxillary Z-axis until any implant hole makes initial contact with the mucosa, and then, based on this posture, the MSE is moved stepwise within a 0.5–2.5 mm range with a 0.1 mm step size. At each height, the MSE is rotated around the X-axis within a − 15° to + 15° range with a 1° step (Fig. 6C). For each combination of height and rotation angle, the system automatically calculates the minimum distance from the center point of each micro-implant slot of the expander to the palatal mucosal surface (Fig. 6D). The 3D shape of the palatal mucosa is obtained by rigidly registering the intraoral scan (IOS) data with the CBCT images (Fig. 6A). When the minimum distance falls within the 1–2 mm range, the corresponding posture is defined as a valid posture and retained as a candidate result for further analysis.

Anatomical avoidance and final pose determination

After obtaining the valid posture, the system further checks whether the MSE in each posture makes contact with key anatomical structures identified by the segmentation network, such as the incisive foramen or transverse palatine suture. If the candidate’s posture avoids these structures entirely, it is determined as the final positioning (Fig. 6E). If the candidate posture makes contact with any key structure, the system begins from the initial position of the expander, moving the MSE along the Y-axis (front-back direction) in 0.3 mm increments, and repeats the vertical movement and rotation search process at each new position. This process continues until the posture closest to the initial position is found, simultaneously meeting both the palatal mucosa gap requirements and the anatomical structure avoidance criteria, which is considered the final positioning result (Fig. 6F). The final positioning result is saved in the form of 3D coordinates and posture parameters, which can be used for subsequent personalized stent design and surgical guide preparation.

Details of collision detection

The screening of the mucosa–mucosa gap and the anatomical avoidance described above are both collision-detection processes. The key structures (incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture) were obtained through CBCT segmentation and expanded outward by 1.0 mm to form a safety buffer zone. During the search process, for each candidate MSE pose, the expander is represented as a pre-defined key element (four micro-implant slot centers and their corresponding implant trajectories). In the stage of calculating the palatal mucosal gap, we calculate the minimum distance between the center of the micro-implant slot and the palatal mucosal grid generated from intraoral scanning data. Only when this distance meets the preset mucosal safety gap threshold (1–2 mm), the corresponding posture is defined as a valid posture. In the anatomical avoidance phase, if any key element intersects with the expanded key structure buffer, the pose is determined as invalid, and the MSE is moved along the Y-axis and returned to the palatal mucosal gap calculation stage. It is worth noting that in order to meet the requirement of MSE closest to the initial pose, collision detection always starts from values closer to 0 offset and 0 rotation angle, and the rotation angle and offset range of each new position are calculated based on the ‘valid posture’ of the previous adjacent position.

Manual MSE placement and expert reference standard

Manual MSE placement was independently performed by two experienced orthodontists (each with more than 5 years of clinical experience in skeletal expansion planning). The manual planning followed the same anatomical and biomechanical criteria as those encoded in the proposed automated workflow. For each case, a three-dimensional maxillary coordinate system was constructed based on predefined CBCT anatomical landmarks. The MSE pose was then manually adjusted in 3D to meet the following clinical requirements: (i) symmetric placement on both sides of the mid-palatal suture in the transverse direction; (ii) sagittal positioning at the level of zygomatic support; and (iii) vertical positioning as close as possible to the palatal bone while maintaining sufficient clearance to avoid palatal mucosal compression. The two orthodontists completed the planning independently under mutual blinding and reported the CA-point coordinates (X, Y, Z) and pose angles (pitch, yaw, roll). Any disagreements were resolved through consensus discussion, and the finalized manual plan was regarded as the expert reference standard for subsequent quantitative comparisons.

Given the lack of established task-specific clinically acceptable error thresholds, clinically acceptable error ranges for each parameter were derived from inter-rater agreement. Specifically, Bland–Altman analysis was used to estimate the mean bias and the 95% limits of agreement (LoA) of the differences between the two experts’ independent plans, and the half-width of the LoA (1.96 × standard deviation of the differences) was used as the clinically acceptable error threshold for each parameter.

Model evaluation metrics

This study evaluates the effectiveness of the model from three aspects: (i) anatomical structure segmentation accuracy; (ii) collision detection performance; and (iii) positioning precision and accuracy.

First, in terms of anatomical structure segmentation accuracy, we use the mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) as the evaluation metric. mIoU measures the overlap between the segmentation results generated by the AI model and the ground truth annotations manually provided by the clinicians, with values ranging from 0 to 1, where a value closer to 1 indicates higher segmentation accuracy of the model.

Second, for collision detection performance, we evaluated the model using the Avoidance Success Rate (ASR) and the Average Minimum Safe Distance (AMSD). ASR refers to the proportion of cases in which the expander does not collide with the incisive foramen, transverse palatine suture, or palatal mucosa after placement; a higher value indicates better avoidance performance. AMSD measures the average minimum distance between the expander and the anatomical structures.

Finally, to assess the precision and accuracy of the positioning results, we evaluated six degrees of freedom: three translational directions (vertical, horizontal, sagittal) and three rotational directions (pitch, yaw, roll). The evaluation metrics included Mean Radial Error (MRE), Mean Angular Error (MAE), and Successful Detection Rate (SDR) [24]. MRE was calculated based on the CA point to quantify the linear distance between the manually placed and model-predicted center points. MAE was used to quantify the angular difference between the manual and predicted orientations. SDR measured the proportion of model predictions falling within defined error thresholds(The threshold settings are shown in Tables 3 and 4). SDR reflects the positional accuracy between the manually defined ground truth and the automatically predicted results. This metric has been proven effective for evaluating localization quality and is widely adopted in related studies [24–26].

Additionally, we calculated the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for both linear and angular measurements between the model-predicted and manually placed positions to further assess the localization error [27].

Overall, we defined the mean radial error (MRE) and mean angular error (MAE) as the primary clinical accuracy metrics. The success detection rate (SDR) under clinically acceptable thresholds was used as an intuitive summary indicator of success rate. The anatomical success rate (ASR) and average minimum safe distance (AMSD) were specifically used to summarize performance in avoiding collisions with critical structures.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in this study were conducted using MedCalc software (version 22.032; MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium) and Python (version 3.10.9; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P< 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We first used the Shapiro–Wilk test (n < 50) to assess whether the paired data followed a normal distribution. If the data were normally distributed, a paired t-test was used; otherwise, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. These tests were conducted to determine whether there were significant differences between the model-predicted and manually placed expander positions in terms of angular and linear measurements. In addition, the concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) [28] and its 95% confidence interval were calculated to evaluate the agreement between the model predictions and the manual reference.

Building on the overall consistency assessment using the CCC, Bland–Altman analysis was further performed to quantify whether there was any systematic bias (mean difference) between the AI and expert manual planning, as well as the range of individual differences (limits of agreement, LoA). In addition, proportional bias was tested by linear regression of the differences on the means; when the regression slope was not statistically significant (P > 0.05), this was interpreted as evidence of no proportional bias.

Segmentation accuracy and avoidance performance

Overall, the system achieved satisfactory segmentation accuracy and high avoidance performance for critical anatomical structures in the independent test set (Table 2).In the test set of 30 cases, the mean segmentation mIoU for the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture was 0.7546, with 0.7210 for the transverse palatine suture and 0.7882 for the incisive foramen. In the same test set, the ASR for the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture were 100% and 96.7%, respectively. The AMSDs between the posterior micro-implants and the transverse palatine suture were 2.26 mm and 2.09 mm, while the AMSDs between the anterior micro-implants and the incisive foramen were 5.60 mm and 5.36 mm. The overall ASR between the centers of the four micro-implant slots and the palatal mucosal surface was 86.7%. The minimum average distances for the left anterior, right anterior, left posterior, and right posterior implants were 1.40 mm, 1.48 mm, 1.51 mm, and 1.56 mm, respectively (Table 2).

Linear positioning performance

Overall, the AI system achieved clinically acceptable linear positioning accuracy: the mean linear error was 0.32 ± 0.32 mm (< 1.13 mm), and the success rate within the 1.2 mm threshold was 93.33% (Table 3). Table 3 summarizes the results of the model’s linear positioning accuracy. We calculated the axial error between the manual reference positions of the expander center point and the model predictions, as well as the SDR along the X, Y, and Z axes at error thresholds of 0.3 mm, 0.6 mm, 0.9 mm, and 1.2 mm. The SDRs for the predicted center point of the expander within these thresholds, compared to manual placement, were 16.67%, 40.00%, 73.33%, and 93.33%, respectively. Accuracy along the X-axis indicates whether the expander is symmetrically positioned across the mid-palatal suture, while the Y- and Z-axis SDRs reflect the model’s positioning performance in the sagittal (anterior-posterior) and vertical directions, respectively. The combined mean SDRs across the three axes at the four thresholds were 65.56%, 82.22%, 92.22%, and 98.90%, respectively. Additionally, the radial error along each axis ranged from 0.01 mm to 1.37 mm, with a 3D Euclidean distance error(3D-EDE)of 0.69 ± 0.36 mm.

Angular positioning performance

For angular alignment, the AI system achieved a mean angular error of 1.84 ± 2.13° (<3.84°), and an SDR of 85.56% within the 4° threshold (Table 4), indicating that the overall angular positioning performance reached a clinically acceptable level.Table 4 presents the evaluation results of the model’s angular positioning accuracy. We calculated the SDRs for pitch, yaw, and roll angles at angular error thresholds of 1.0°, 2.0°, 3.0°, and 4.0°. The pitch angle reflects rotation around the X-axis, indicating whether the anterior or posterior end of the expander tilts upward or downward. The yaw angle indicates rotation around the Z-axis, corresponding to lateral deviation of the expander. The roll angle represents rotation around the Y-axis, reflecting whether the left or right side of the expander is raised or lowered. Across the four thresholds, the overall mean SDRs were 48.89%, 72.22%, 82.22%, and 85.56%, respectively. The model’s MAE ranged from 0.01° to 8.12°.

Discussion

In this retrospective digital planning study, we proposed and validated a deep learning–based workflow for automatic positioning of a maxillary skeletal expander (MSE). Overall, the system demonstrated good feasibility in terms of positioning accuracy, safety-related avoidance, and agreement with expert planning. For linear positioning, the 3D-EDE error was 0.69 ± 0.36 mm, the axial MRE was 0.32 ± 0.32 mm, and the SDR reached 93.33% within a 1.2 mm threshold. For angular positioning, the MAE was 1.84 ± 2.13°, with an SDR of 85.56% within a 4° threshold. To further characterize agreement across the full measurement range and to examine potential systematic bias, we combined CCC and Bland–Altman analyses. The results indicated good overall agreement (CCC > 0.87, P > 0.05), with only a mild proportional bias observed for yaw (P = 0.013). This finding may be attributable to the relatively fewer constraints on the yaw degree of freedom and its higher sensitivity to subtle registration discrepancies; importantly, the magnitude of this bias was small and remained within clinically acceptable limits. Collectively, the proposed system may serve as an AI-assisted digital planning tool to shorten planning time and improve planning consistency.

Although artificial intelligence has been widely applied to orthodontic imaging tasks-such as tooth segmentation and landmark identification [29, 30]—its use for automated 3D placement of orthodontic appliances—particularly automatic MSE positioning—remains limited. Prior studies have shown that deep learning can achieve automatic segmentation of multiple structures on CBCT with acceptable overall accuracy, for example, Fontenele et al. reported Dice similarity coefficients (DSC) ranging from 78.9% to 95.4% [29, 31–33].However, to date, no AI segmentation models have been specifically trained or validated for the incisive foramen or transverse palatine suture. In our study, segmentation accuracy for the transverse palatine suture (mIoU = 0.7210) was slightly lower than previously reported metrics. This potentially due to its limited grayscale contrast on CBCT, indistinct boundaries in highly ossified or nearly fused cases, and its tortuous, asymmetric morphology, all of which increase recognition difficulty. Importantly, our goal was not “perfect” suture segmentation, but clinically usable “reliable avoidance.” Therefore, we expanded the segmented regions of critical structures by 1 mm to construct a safety buffer, improving robustness to segmentation uncertainty and enhancing placement safety. This strategy performed well in the test set, yielding avoidance success rates of 100% for the incisive foramen and 96.7% for the transverse palatine suture.

Regarding landmark detection, Tanikawa et al. developed and validated a deep learning algorithm for fully automated craniofacial landmark detection on CBCT, reporting localization errors of 0.8–1.54 mm, which were considered clinically acceptable [30, 34, 35]. Consistent with these findings, the automatic positioning results in our study also achieved clinically acceptable spatial accuracy, with a 3D-EDE of 0.69 ± 0.36 mm and an angular error of 1.84 ± 2.13°, together with high SDR values under the 1.2-mm and 4.0° thresholds.

We incorporated a standardized 3D maxillary coordinate system into the AI framework to improve pose comparability and reproducibility across individuals [23]. The zygomatic support level was chosen as the sagittal reference because the pterygopalatine and zygomaticomaxillary sutures represent major resistance regions, with the center of resistance typically located in the posterior maxilla near the zygomatic process [10, 36–41]. Positioning the expander at this level may allow forces to act more directly on the resistance center, facilitating more parallel posterior expansion [12]. Constraints based on the mid-sagittal and horizontal palatal planes further promote symmetric placement and palatal conformity, reducing directional deviations and excessive lever-arm effects, thereby improving initial stability and consistency [23].

Beyond biomechanical plausibility, the pose search incorporated soft-tissue and critical-structure constraints by maintaining a 1–2 mm mucosal clearance and avoiding contact with the incisive foramen and transverse palatine suture to reduce iatrogenic risk and improve feasibility. Bone availability also influences mini-implant stability: the T-zone near the mid-palatal suture has been suggested as a favorable insertion region [42],and our plans generally fell within this area. Moreover, bicortical anchorage can provide good initial stability even with limited posterior bone volume [43], supporting the clinical plausibility of the generated plans.

In routine clinical workflows, per-case planning time is an important determinant of usability. In this study, the automated pipeline required approximately 3 min per case, which is substantially shorter than traditional manual planning (approximately 45–60 min per case). This time reduction may alleviate clinician workload and improve the efficiency of digital planning.

Limitations

Despite the favorable performance of the proposed automated MSE positioning system, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the dataset was collected from a single center under a unified imaging protocol, which may limit external validity; multi-center datasets are needed to improve generalizability. Second, the cohort represents a specific population (skeletal Class III with maxillary transverse deficiency), and applicability to other malocclusion types or craniofacial conditions remains uncertain. Third, the workflow was developed and evaluated using a single expander design, and adaptations may be required for different appliance geometries or screw configurations. Fourth, the segmentation module used a 2D U-Net for slice-wise prediction; although computationally efficient, it may not fully leverage 3D contextual information and could affect inter-slice continuity compared with 3D architectures. Finally, the current validation was retrospective against expert digital planning as the reference; no clinical validation of actual implant/placement positioning or treatment outcomes (efficacy) was performed in this study. The system is intended to support—rather than replace—clinical decision-making. Prospective studies are required to confirm transfer accuracy, safety, and clinical outcomes in real-world use.

Future work

As a next step, we plan to translate the current AI-based positioning framework into a modular software prototype to support an integrated “design–transfer–navigation” workflow. In this envisioned pipeline, the structured pose parameters generated by the AI model will serve as inputs to a CAD module for semi-automated design of patient-specific mini-implant insertion surgical guides, followed by high-precision 3D printing. Future prospective studies will evaluate this software integration and its clinical utility (e.g., workflow usability, transfer accuracy, and safety).

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective study, we developed and validated an AI-assisted workflow for automatic MSE positioning using CBCT and intraoral scan (IOS) data. The system demonstrated high agreement with expert digital planning (3D Euclidean error: 0.69 ± 0.36 mm; axial MRE: 0.32 ± 0.32 mm; angular MAE: 1.84 ± 2.13°), met clinically relevant safety requirements (avoidance of critical anatomical structures and maintenance of adequate palatal mucosal clearance), and substantially reduced planning time compared with manual planning. Overall, AI-assisted MSE positioning may enable more standardized and efficient digital planning, but multi-center prospective studies are still required before broader clinical adoption.
