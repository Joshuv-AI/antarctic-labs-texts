Soticlestat as an adjunctive therapy in children and young adults with Dravet syndrome
Authors: Joseph Sullivan, Kette Valente, Vicente Villanueva, Adam Strzelczyk, Rima Nabbout, Eiji Nakagawa, Yuehua Zhang, Marta Zolnowska, Yasir Khan, Cheng Dong, Samuel Hsiao, Sarah I Sheikh, Philipp von Rosenstiel, Mahnaz Asgharnejad, Venkatesha Murthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study evaluated the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of soticlestat as adjunctive therapy in children and young adults with Dravet syndrome (DS).MethodsSKYLINE (NCT04940624) was a multicenter, randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, phase 3 trial that enrolled patients with DS aged 2–21 years with uncontrolled convulsive seizures (≥4/month despite adequate treatment). Participants received oral soticlestat 300 mg (weight adjusted) or matching placebo twice daily. The total study duration was 16 weeks, comprising 4‐week dose titration and 12‐week maintenance treatment periods. The primary endpoint was a comparison of monthly convulsive seizure frequency between baseline and the titration/maintenance periods. Key secondary endpoints included several modified Caregiver and Clinical Global Impression of Improvement (GI‐I) scales for DS.ResultsOne hundred forty‐four participants were randomized (71 placebo, 73 soticlestat) with a mean (SD) age of 10.3 (5.0) years; 72 (50%) were male, and 117 (81.3%) were receiving ≥3 antiseizure medications. Median change from baseline in convulsive seizure frequency over the full treatment period was −8.64% with placebo (n = 71) and −22.16% with soticlestat (n = 73), a difference of −15.64% (p = .061); in the maintenance treatment period, these changes were −11.99% with placebo and −23.29% with soticlestat, a difference of −14.29% (p = .089). The proportion of participants with ≥50% reduction in convulsive seizures was 9.9% with placebo and 27.4% with soticlestat (nominal p = .008). Soticlestat showed clinically meaningful results in the Caregiver and Clinical GI‐I, and Clinical GI‐I Seizure Intensity and Duration scales over the 16‐week treatment period (all nominal p‐values ≤ .004). The most commonly reported treatment‐emergent adverse events related to study drug were somnolence, change in seizure presentation, decreased appetite, and insomnia.SignificanceAlthough statistical significance was narrowly missed, soticlestat showed a numerical benefit over placebo for convulsive seizure decrease. Clinically meaningful benefits across multiple secondary endpoints were observed. No new safety concerns emerged.Keywords: cholesterol‐24‐hydroxylase, Dravet syndrome, soticlestat, TAK‐935

INTRODUCTION

Dravet syndrome (DS) is a developmental and epileptic encephalopathy characterized by pharmacoresistant seizures and the progressive emergence of multiple neurodevelopmental deficits, including cognitive, motor, and behavioral impairments, that evolve and accumulate throughout the disease course. 1 , 2 The majority of patients with DS have a sodium voltage‐gated channel alpha subunit 1 (SCN1A) gene pathogenic variant, leading to loss of function. 3 , 4 , 5 The seizure phenotype evolves over time, typically presenting with prolonged clonic generalized, focal, or unilateral seizures, beginning within the first year of life, often triggered by hyperthermia, and evolving to status epilepticus. In early childhood, myoclonic, absence, and focal seizures increase, followed by a reduction in status epilepticus episodes after age 5 years. Partial stabilization occurs during school age, and a possible slight decrease in seizure frequency or severity during adolescence; however, epilepsy typically persists for life, and neurodevelopmental impairments remain. 6 , 7 , 8

DS is associated with substantial impairment in health‐related quality of life (QoL) for patients and caregivers. In patients with DS, higher seizure frequencies are associated with lower QoL and more comorbidities. 6 , 9 In addition, caring for a family member with DS severely impacts family life, including daily activities, relationships, and social life. 6 , 10 Mortality rates from 3.7% up to 20.8% have been reported among patients with DS, with status epilepticus and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) being the most common causes. 10

Management options for seizure control in DS include antiseizure medications (ASMs) such as valproate or clobazam, with stiripentol, fenfluramine, and pharmaceutical‐grade cannabidiol licensed as adjunctive therapies, along with ketogenic diet therapy and vagal nerve stimulation. 2 , 11 Current management of DS often requires concomitant use of multiple different ASMs, resulting in potential drug–drug interactions and increased concerns surrounding safety and tolerability, but does not achieve seizure freedom. 12 These tolerability issues and adverse events (AEs) can have a negative impact on the QoL of affected families/caregivers. 13 Given the complexity of managing patients with multiple seizure types that are often difficult to control, there remains an unmet need for effective therapies with a favorable safety and tolerability profile.

Soticlestat is a first‐in‐class small‐molecule inhibitor of cholesterol‐24‐hydroxylase, the enzyme responsible for catabolizing cholesterol to 24S‐hydroxycholesterol (24HC), a positive allosteric modulator of N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate receptor, in the brain. 14 , 15 It may help prevent seizures by lowering 24HC levels to reduce nerve overactivity, restoring extracellular glutamate, and decreasing neuroinflammation. 16 In preclinical studies, soticlestat suppressed elevation of extracellular glutamate, thus limiting excessive neural excitation in an epileptic transgenic mouse model. 14 In Scn1a +/− mice, soticlestat reduced seizure burden, protected against hyperthermia‐induced seizures, and completely prevented SUDEP. 17 Furthermore, soticlestat is rapidly absorbed and quickly eliminated, reducing the risk of drug accumulation. 18

In a phase 2 study (ELEKTRA; ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT03650452) in children aged 2 to ≤17 years with DS or Lennox–Gastaut syndrome, participants with DS who received soticlestat (≤300 mg twice daily [BID]) demonstrated a statistically significant placebo‐adjusted median reduction in convulsive seizure frequency of 50.0% (95% confidence interval [CI] −74.2% to −24.7%, p = .0002). 19 Soticlestat was well tolerated at doses up to 300 mg BID in this and other studies, 19 , 20 , 21 and model‐based simulations further supported the use of 300 mg BID as the study dose. 22

This phase 3 trial evaluated the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of soticlestat as an adjunctive therapy in children and young adults with DS.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Trial design

SKYLINE was a phase 3, global, multicenter, 1:1 randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled, parallel‐group study conducted in pediatric and young adult participants with DS (EudraCT Number: 2021‐002480‐22; ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT04940624; Figure 1). Following a 4–6‐week screening/prospective baseline period, eligible participants were randomized to receive either soticlestat or placebo as an add‐on to their current ASM over a 16‐week treatment period, comprising a 4‐week dose titration period and a 12‐week maintenance period. Participants not continuing into an open‐label extension study underwent a 1‐week tapering period and a 2‐week follow‐up period.

Overview of the study design. aWeight‐based dosing in participants weighing <45 kg. SoC, standard of care.

Soticlestat or matching placebo was administered orally or via an enteral feeding tube BID, with dose dependent on weight at screening. The dose titration schedule was 40, 60, and 100 mg BID for participants weighing 10 to <15 kg; 60, 120, and 200 mg BID for participants weighing 15 to <30 kg; 80, 140, and 200 mg BID for participants weighing 30 to <45 kg; and 100, 200, and 300 mg BID for participants weighing ≥45 kg. Following initiation, dose levels were increased at approximately 7‐day intervals if there were no tolerability issues. The dose level at the end of the titration period was the maintenance dose, and although an increase in dose was not permitted during the maintenance period, a decrease was permitted for safety and tolerability reasons.

The study was approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each site and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the International Council on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice: Consolidated Guideline, and applicable local or regional regulatory requirements. All participants or their parents or legal guardians provided written informed consent.

Eligibility

Participants, regardless of sex, aged 2–21 years, weighing ≥10 kg, and with a documented clinical diagnosis of DS (as adjudicated independently by the Epilepsy Study Consortium [TESC]) were enrolled. To be eligible for the study, participants were required to have treatment‐resistant seizures, defined as having seizures despite appropriate trials of one or more ASMs, and be receiving stable antiseizure standard‐of‐care therapy, including zero to four concomitant ASMs. Participants were required to have had ≥12 convulsive seizures over 12 weeks before screening and at least four convulsive seizures per 28 days during the 4–6‐week screening period. Those admitted to a medical facility and intubated for treatment of status epilepticus two or more times in the 3 months immediately before screening were excluded from the study. Additional inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Table S1.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoints were percentage change from baseline in convulsive seizure frequency per 28 days in participants receiving soticlestat compared with placebo during the full treatment period and, for European Medicines Agency (EMA) registration, during the maintenance period only. Convulsive seizures included hemiclonic or focal clonic, focal to bilateral tonic–clonic, generalized tonic–clonic, bilateral clonic, and convulsive status epilepticus, as classified by TESC. Seizure frequency was collected via an electronic daily seizure and medication diary completed by the participant and/or parent or caregiver. Prior to study start, the primary endpoint was agreed in cooperation with researchers in the epilepsy field and epileptologists, in consent with TESC, and consistent with the International League Against Epilepsy seizure classification. 23

Key secondary endpoints were a comparison of soticlestat with placebo on (1) proportion of responders, defined as those with a ≥50% reduction from baseline in convulsive seizures during the full treatment period or maintenance period; (2) Caregiver Global Impression of Improvement (Care GI‐I); (3) Clinical Global Impression of Improvement (CGI‐I) 24 ; (4) CGI‐I Non‐Seizure Symptoms 25 ; (5) change in Quality of Life Inventory‐Disability (QI‐Disability) score 26 ; and (6) CGI‐I Seizure Intensity and Duration during the full treatment period. Further details on these measures can be found in the Supporting Information Methods. Additional secondary endpoints are also detailed in the Supporting Information Methods.

Percentage change from baseline in convulsive seizure frequency per 28 days for the SCN1A + subgroup (documented genetic mutation consistent with DS) was an exploratory endpoint. Details relating to the measurement of plasma 24HC levels (also an exploratory endpoint) can be found in the Supporting Information Methods.

Safety endpoints were the incidence of treatment‐emergent AEs (TEAEs) as well as the incidence of abnormal laboratory values and change from baseline in laboratory values.

Statistical analysis

All efficacy analyses were conducted in the modified intent‐to‐treat population, defined as randomized participants who received at least one dose of the study drug and were assessed for seizures for ≥1 day in the treatment period. The safety analysis population included all participants who took at least one dose of the study drug. Descriptive analyses are provided for the safety endpoints. Data are provided as number and percentage for categorical variables, and as mean, SD, median, and range for continuous variables. The seizure frequency was calculated based on all data collected during the prospective 4–6‐week baseline period and either the full treatment period or maintenance treatment period for EMA registration.

The primary endpoint of percentage change from baseline in convulsive seizure frequency per 28 days in participants receiving soticlestat compared with placebo was analyzed using rank analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with treatment group, age stratum (≤6 years, >6 years), and rank of baseline convulsive seizure frequency as fixed effects. The Hodges–Lehmann estimate and corresponding 95% CI comparing soticlestat and placebo are reported. Sensitivity analyses of the primary endpoint were performed, including rank ANCOVA using multiple imputations, the van Elteren test, Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, and rank ANCOVA with sex added as a factor.

The global type I error was controlled using a hierarchical gatekeeping procedure on the primary and key secondary endpoints. Key secondary endpoints were analyzed in order of hypothesis testing (1–6 as listed in the Study Endpoints section). If a test was not statistically significant at a two‐sided 5% significance level, subsequent hypotheses were not tested, and any reported p‐values should be considered nominal (not adjusted for multiplicity).

The proportion of responders was analyzed using a Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test stratified by age stratum (≤6 years, >6 years) and compared between soticlestat and placebo groups over the treatment period (or maintenance period for EMA registration). For Care GI‐I, CGI‐I, CGI‐I Seizure Intensity and Duration, and CGI‐I Non‐Seizure Symptoms, ordinal responses were compared between soticlestat and placebo groups at the last visit when these instruments were utilized, using a cumulative logit model. Change in QI‐Disability from baseline to the end of the treatment period between the soticlestat and placebo groups was compared using a mixed model for repeated measures.

RESULTS

Study population

Participants were enrolled at 53 study sites in 17 countries between December 7, 2021 and April 11, 2024. A total of 144 participants were randomized to receive placebo (n = 71) or soticlestat (n = 73; Figure 2).

CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) flow diagram showing participant enrollment, randomization, allocation, follow‐up, and analysis.

Baseline characteristics were generally well balanced between the placebo and soticlestat arms (Table 1). There was an almost equal split between male and female participants, and almost half were White. More than 75% of participants were SCN1A + . Median convulsive seizure frequencies per 28 days were 10.8 and 11.6 for the placebo and soticlestat arms, respectively. Overall, 117 (81%) participants were taking three or four ASMs, the most common being valproic acid and clobazam, which is consistent with standard of care in clinical practice. 12 , 27 The median (range) of treatment interventions, as defined by the total number of prior or baseline ASMs, was 8 (3–27). Mean (SD) treatment compliance was 98.4% (10.0%) for the placebo group and 99.6% (2.9%) for the soticlestat group.

Effect on convulsive seizure frequency

Soticlestat reduced convulsive seizure frequency compared with placebo in participants with DS, but this difference did not reach statistical significance. During the full treatment period, the median reduction from baseline in convulsive seizure frequency per 28 days was 8.64% for placebo and 22.16% for soticlestat (Figure 3A). The difference was a reduction of 15.64% (95% CI = −31.30 to .24, p = .061). A similar convulsive seizure frequency reduction was observed during the maintenance period (23.29% for soticlestat vs. 11.99% for placebo; Figure 3B), with a difference of 14.29% (95% CI = −30.51 to 1.53, p = .089). Results of the prespecified sensitivity analyses of the primary endpoint were consistent with those of the primary analysis, during both the full treatment period and the maintenance period (Table S2).

In the SCN1A + subgroup (soticlestat, n = 58; placebo, n = 56; exploratory endpoint), the median reductions in convulsive seizure frequency per 28 days were 23.34% and 12.70% in the soticlestat and placebo arms, respectively (Figure 3C). The difference was a reduction of 17.38% (95% CI = −34.29 to −1.21, nominal p = .045). In the SCN1A − subgroup, soticlestat (n = 14; placebo, n = 15), the median percentage reduction was 11.10% and 5.02% in the soticlestat and placebo arms, respectively. This corresponded to a nonsignificant reduction of 3.01% (95% CI = −50.57 to 50.50, nominal p = .713).

In a post hoc analysis by previous ASM quartiles, participants who had received fewer ASM treatment interventions (prior or current) showed numerically favorable reductions in convulsive seizure frequency. The placebo‐adjusted median percentage change from baseline for participants in quartile 1 (three to five ASMs) was −34.9% (placebo, n = 18; soticlestat, n = 13); it was −30.1% (placebo, n = 17; soticlestat, n = 20) in quartile 2 (six to seven ASMs); and −22.5% (placebo, n = 13; soticlestat, n = 11) in quartile 3 (eight ASMs). Participants in quartile 4, who were the most treatment‐resistant group, receiving nine or more ASMs, showed an unfavorable treatment effect of +15.0% compared with placebo (placebo, n = 23; soticlestat, n = 29).

Key secondary endpoints

A greater proportion of participants receiving soticlestat had a ≥50% reduction from baseline in convulsive seizure frequency (responders) during both the full treatment period (Figure 4A) and the maintenance period (Figure 4B). At the end of the full treatment period, participants receiving soticlestat showed a greater improvement than placebo in Care GI‐I, CGI‐I, and CGI‐I Seizure Intensity and Duration (Figure 4C–E). These key secondary endpoints showed nominal statistical significance, but because the primary endpoint did not reach statistical significance, positive outcomes cannot be declared for secondary endpoints. No meaningful differences were observed between treatment groups in CGI‐I Non‐Seizure Symptoms (i.e., alertness, communication, disruptive behaviors; Figure 4F–H). Furthermore, at the end of the full treatment period, use of soticlestat did not lead to any worsening of the Non‐Seizure Symptoms or the QI‐Disability score (Figure 4I). Safety evaluation

A total of 112 (77.8%) participants reported TEAEs during the study: 53 (74.6%) in the placebo group and 59 (80.8%) in the soticlestat group (Table 2). In both groups, most TEAEs were considered not related to the study drug and were mild or moderate in severity. TEAEs considered related to the study drug were experienced by 26.8% of participants in the placebo group compared with 43.8% of participants in the soticlestat group. In the soticlestat group, treatment‐related TEAEs reported by ≥5% of participants were somnolence, change in seizure presentation, decreased appetite, and insomnia. The only treatment‐related TEAEs reported by ≥5% of participants in the placebo group were somnolence and change in seizure presentation.

Serious TEAEs were reported by 14.1% of the placebo and 9.6% of the soticlestat groups, with the same number of participants in each group experiencing a treatment‐related serious TEAE (n = 2). In total, 15 participants (four in the placebo group and 11 in the soticlestat group) had TEAEs leading to study drug discontinuation (Table 2). The only TEAE leading to study drug discontinuation that was reported by more than one participant in either group was change in seizure presentation. Most TEAEs leading to study drug discontinuation were treatment related, and all resolved or were resolving by the end of the study, except for the TEAE of SUDEP (n = 1).

No clinically relevant findings were observed for any of the additional safety parameters recorded. Laboratory values that were considered clinically meaningful by the investigator were reported as TEAEs.

DISCUSSION

This phase 3 trial evaluated the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of soticlestat as an add‐on therapy to standard of care in pediatric and young adult participants with DS. This trial was initiated following positive outcomes in the phase 2 (ELEKTRA) study. 19 The results for the primary endpoint showed a modest reduction in convulsive seizure frequency in participants treated with soticlestat compared with placebo, although statistical significance was narrowly missed (p = .06). Potential contributors to this narrowly missed statistical significance on the primary outcome are a higher placebo response than in the phase 2 study and a generally more refractory patient population (median [range] number of prior or baseline ASMs was 8 [3–27]).

The number of ASMs used is related to symptom severity and age, and this phase 3 study included young adults as well as children (an enrollment criterion was age 2–21 years). The availability of fenfluramine and cannabidiol for the treatment of DS may have selected patients whose condition was more difficult to control for this clinical trial, and as more therapies become available, it is likely that participants in future trials will have taken or be taking a larger number of ASMs at enrollment, which may impact trial outcomes. This observation may warrant consideration for alternative clinical trial designs.

The exploratory SCN1A + subgroup analysis (79% of study participants) showed a nominally significant reduction from baseline in convulsive seizure frequency (p = .045) with soticlestat versus placebo. As ≥80% of patients with DS have known SCN1A mutations, 3 the observed results are potentially due to inaccurate characterization and diagnosis of these participants, and the results from the whole study may have been confounded by the heterogeneity of those who were SCN1A − . In support of this, the proportion of responders was 27.6% on soticlestat versus 8.9% on placebo for participants who were SCN1A + (odds ratio [OR] = 4.31, 95% CI = 1.32–14.02, nominal p = .011), and 28.6% vs. 13.3% for participants who were SCN1A − (OR = 2.59, 95% CI = .39–17.10, nominal p = .325).

The population size in the phase 3 study was larger than that of the phase 2 study and was adequately powered, as the sample size calculation was based on the phase 2 study and other data. 19 , 21 There was also adequate compliance with study medication, as confirmed by pharmacokinetic samples (data not shown). Differential compliance rates between the groups cannot explain the difference either, as the overall compliance (98.4% vs. 99.6%) and participants with significant noncompliance (compliance < 80%: 2.8% vs. 1.4%) were similar between placebo and soticlestat, respectively. Decreased plasma 24HC levels clearly confirmed the pharmacological effect of soticlestat (Figure S2). Narrowly missed significance on the primary endpoints is unlikely to be due to a high placebo response, as the placebo rate was in line with clinical trials of other approved drugs for the treatment of DS. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 However, as mentioned above, the observed placebo response in this trial was higher than in the phase 2 study.

As hierarchical testing was in place, none of the secondary or exploratory endpoints can be considered significant. However, the majority of key secondary endpoints showed numerically favorable and clinically meaningful treatment effects based on caregiver‐ and clinician‐reported measures. Importantly, favorable effects were the responder rate (proportion of participants with a ≥50% reduction in convulsive seizures from baseline) and that seizures were less intense and/or of shorter duration as reported by caregivers. These results, as well as the proportion of participants with a >75% response (10 with soticlestat vs. one for placebo; Table S3), provide evidence that soticlestat may have a benefit for seizures versus placebo, as some patients did respond favorably to treatment. As shown in Figure S1, although the majority of participants showed reductions in convulsive seizure frequency compared with the placebo group, a minority showed an increase in convulsive seizure frequency, which is not unexpected in clinical trials in epilepsy.

Care GI‐I and CGI‐I are health outcome/QoL measures, and they are used to assess changes in global functioning, including the effect of symptoms on aspects of life such as school, home, and relationships. 24 Although seizure control remains the major target of therapy, other important treatment goals are QoL (of both the patient and their family), as well as limiting medication side effects. 11 The impact of nonseizure symptoms is also a growing area of interest, as seizure reduction alone may not fully reflect the therapeutic benefit of an intervention. 32 Results using the Care GI‐I and CGI‐I captured beneficial changes with soticlestat beyond the outcome on seizures, providing a more comprehensive view of treatment effects.

Safety findings from SKYLINE show that TEAEs were generally mild to moderate in severity and occurred at a similar frequency for both the placebo and soticlestat groups. These results are in line with previously published literature involving soticlestat, 19 , 21 , 33 , 34 and no new safety signals were observed.

CONCLUSIONS

Soticlestat showed a numerical benefit on convulsive seizure frequency over placebo that narrowly missed statistical significance. Multiple secondary endpoints showed clinically meaningful efficacy. Soticlestat was well tolerated, with no new safety signals observed. The totality of the data from SKYLINE suggests that soticlestat may have modest antiepileptic effects, which failed to achieve statistical significance in patients with DS.
