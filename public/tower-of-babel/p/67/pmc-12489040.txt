Effect of 2-hydroxyethylammonium acetate based protic ionic liquids on thermophysical properties and taste behavior of D-fructose in aqueous solutions at different temperatures
Authors: Mohammad Amin Morsali, Hemayat Shekaari, Abbas Mehrdad, Masumeh Mokhtarpour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study reports the density, viscosity, and refractive index data of aqueous solutions of D-fructose in protic ionic liquids (PILs): (2-hydroxyethyl) ammonium acetate [2-HEA]Ac, bis (2-hydroxyethyl) ammonium acetate [bis-2-HEA]Ac, and tris (2-hydroxyethyl) ammonium acetate [tris-2-HEA]Ac at temperature range of (298.15 to 318.15) K. The density data were utilized to calculate the apparent molar volume (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V_\varphi }$$\end{document}) and standard partial molar expansibility (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{\varphi }^{0}$$\end{document} ). Also, the calculated standard partial molar volumes were used to study solute-solvent interactions. The results indicate stronger interactions between [2-HEA]Ac and D-fructose in water which was found to be 118.443 m3·mol−1, compared to the other protic ionic liquids. Additionally, the viscosity B-coefficient was derived using the Jones-Dole equation. The molar refraction (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R_M}$$\end{document}) was calculated via the Lorentz–Lorenz equation, while COSMO analysis revealed that [2-HEA]Ac exhibits the most substantial interaction with D-fructose among the studied systems. The volumetric properties are also applied in the taste behavior of solutions, where changes in hydrogen bonding and solute-solvent interactions influence the perceived sweetness intensity of D-fructose.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15449-z.

Introduction

Fuels play significant role in human daily life by providing energy for its essential needs. However, consuming fuels, especially fossil fuels to obtain energy is considered a controversial issue, to this note finding green and cost-effective resources is of great importance as it enable us to reduce the global warming, air and ocean pollution a most discussed concern nowadays1–5. One of the promising candidates for fossil fuels substitution, by having incredible aspects such as greenness, cost-effectiveness, bio-degradability and eco-friendliness features is bioethanol6–8. This remarkable fuel can be produced through a carbohydrate conversion mechanism; however, the poor carbohydrate conversion mechanism efficiency gets attention of the researchers around the world, thus, optimization of this mechanism efficiency is crucial, there have been various attempts to achieve this goal, and among these, solvents have been chosen as they have the ability to ease the hydrogen bonds breaking and to provide a suitable media to achieve high-quality bioethanol9–11. Among solvent utilized for this purpose, protic ionic liquid (PILs) has been getting more attention due to their outstanding features such as low vapor pressure, negligible toxicity, easy synthesis, and wide range of tunability. PILs act as catalysts or reaction media, facilitating the breakdown of D-fructose into simpler compounds and promoting the subsequent fermentation process12–15.

Among sugar utilized for bioethanol production, D-fructose shows magnificent carbohydrate conversion rate. D-Fructose is C6 D-glucose isomer, a monosaccharide that has been preferred over other sugar due to its low cost, and perfunctory in fruits, plants and agricultural residuals. Protic ionic liquids (PILs) are formed by proton transfer from Brønsted acid to Brønsted base through utilization of stronger acids and stronger bases the proton transfer can be enhanced. These PILs can facilitate the dissolving of carbohydrates in high level.

When D-fructose, the most plant derivative carbohydrate is exposed to PILs, it undergoes a series of chemical reactions which leads to the production of bioethanol. One of the key advantages of PILs in this context is their ability to dissolve and stabilize carbohydrates, including D-fructose, thereby enhancing the efficiency of enzymatic hydrolysis. PILs showcases excellent solubility for D-fructose, enabling optimal conditions for enzymatic reactions to occur. This solubility promotes higher conversion rates and improved yields of bioethanol. Additionally, PILs possess desirable thermal and chemical stability, allowing for sustained and efficient reactions. Furthermore, PILs offer the advantage of being less volatile than conventional organic solvents, reducing the risk of emissions and enhancing safety in the bioethanol production process16–18.

Overall, the utilization of PILs in the conversion of D-fructose into bioethanol represents a significant advancement in sustainable energy production. These remarkable solvents enable efficient enzymatic hydrolysis, enhance the solubility of carbohydrates, and provide a safer and more environmentally friendly alternative to traditional solvents19–21. PILs present a promising alternative to conventional solvents, potentially enhancing reaction rates, selectivity, and overall yields in bioethanol production. By investigating how PILs affect the solubility and reactivity of sugars, this study aims to identify optimal conditions for maximizing bioethanol output. Moreover, this research contributes to the broader goal of developing greener chemical processes, aligning with global sustainability initiatives. The findings could pave the way for innovative applications of PILs, ultimately supporting the transition to renewable energy sources and reducing reliance on fossil fuels. Thus, the article is significant not only for its scientific contributions but also for its potential impact on environmental sustainability and the biofuels sector21–23.

In the present work, thermodynamic and transport properties such as volumetric, viscosity and refractive index of D-fructose in aqueous PILs solutions have been measured at temperature range (288.15 to 318.15) K under ambient pressure of 0.086 MPa. The utilized PILs in current study are ethanolamine base which are 2-hydroxyethylammonium acetate [2-HEA] Ac, bis-(2-hydroxyethylammonium acetate [bis-2-HEA] Ac and tris-(2-hydroxyethylammonium) acetate [tris-2-HEA]Ac. Thermophysical parameters like apparent molar volume of solutions were calculated based on volumetric information. Also, viscosity B-coefficient was determined based on Jones–Dole equation. Molar refraction was calculated by using of Lorenz-Lorentz equation which is described polarity of interactions. Also to achieve a more profound understanding of the molecular interactions of sugar with studied PILs, the COSMO analysis has been used. Through these calculation, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO), sigma profile and dielectric solvation energy has been obtained.

Synthesis and characterization of PILs

In this study, several protic ionic liquids (PILs) were synthesized and purified including 2-hydroxyethylammoniumacetate, bis (2-hydroxyethylammonium) acetate and tris (2-hydroxyethylammonium) acetate. The synthesis process involved the gradual addition of acetic acid to ethanolamine, which was maintained in an ice bath and subjected to vigorous stirring with a magnetic stirrer at room temperature for a duration of 24 h. To achieve a higher degree of purity, the resultant ionic liquids were subsequently dried for three hours using a vacuum pump, specifically a D25 model manufactured in the USA. The synthesized protic ionic liquids were characterized employing Fourier Transform Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (FT-NMR) (Figures S1 to S3 in supplementary file) and Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy (Figure S4 in supplementary file), in addition to Karl Fischer titration analysis for water content assessment. The results of these characterizations are detailed in the supporting information section. The Karl Fischer titration technique (utilizing the TitroLine KF method) revealed a residual water content of less than 30 ppm after purification, which could not be eliminated and was subsequently accounted for in the preparation of the studied solutions.

Apparatus and procedure

This work presents a study of ternary solutions comprising D-fructose, protic ionic liquids (PILs), and water. The solution for investigating the volumetric and transport properties of the studied systems was prepared using the Shimadzu Aw-220 analytical balance, which has an uncertainty of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 2 \times {10^{ - 4}}$$\end{document}. Density measurements were conducted using a digital vibrating U-tube densitometer (Kyoto Electronics DA-210), which provides a resolution of ± 1 × 10−4 g·cm−3. The instrument was calibrated prior to each set of measurements using certified reference materials, specifically air and double-distilled water, at a controlled temperature of 298.15 K. Calibration ensured that the measured densities corresponded to standard reference values within the manufacturer’s specified tolerance. The temperature of the measuring cell was regulated by thermostat with a precision of ± 0.01 K, thereby guaranteeing the reliability and reproducibility of the data. The overall uncertainty in the density measurements was estimated to be ± 4 × 10−3 g·cm−3. The viscosity of the solutions was determined using a digital viscometer (Lovis 2000 M, Anton Paar), which operates based on the falling ball principle within a capillary tube of 1.59 mm diameter. The device automatically records the average flow time over a set number of successive measurements. Temperature control of the capillary is achieved using a Peltier element, maintaining a precision of ± 0.01 K. The relative uncertainty of viscosity measurements was 1%. The refractive index was measured using the Mettler Toledo Digital Refractometer, which boasts an accuracy of approximately ± 0.0002 units. Calibration of the refractometer was performed twice using doubly distilled water prior to measurement. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded utilizing a Bruker Tensor 270 instrument, while Fourier-transform nuclear magnetic resonance (FT-NMR) spectra were obtained using a Bruker DRX-400 spectrometer. Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) geometry optimization of the protic ionic liquids (PILs) was conducted using the Dmol3 software package, employing the DND (3.5) basis set and the generalized gradient approximation Vosko-Wilk-Nusair-BP (GGA VWN-BP). The BP functional was specifically implemented to replace local correlation in the VWN functional, facilitating COSMO results through a two-step process involving geometry optimization followed by energy optimization.

Results and discussion

COSMO analysis: molecular fingerprint and σ-profile

The Sigma Profile in the context of COSMO (Conductor-like Screening Model) calculations serves as a vital parameter for understanding solvation effects in molecular simulations. It delineates the electronic density distribution on molecular surfaces, providing insights into the interaction between solute molecules and their solvent environment. By employing Sigma Profile data, researchers can enhance the accuracy of polar solvation free energy predictions, facilitating a more nuanced interpretation of solvent effects on chemical reactivity. Additionally, the integration of Sigma Profiles into COSMO calculations allows for the detailed analysis of hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions, illuminating the mechanisms underlying molecular recognition processes. Consequently, the application of the Sigma Profile is instrumental in advancing theoretical frameworks that describe molecular interactions in complex environments. These profiles are derived through advanced computational methodologies, predominantly utilizing density functional theory (DFT) calculations, which are known for their high computational demands. To mitigate these computational costs, alternative methodologies that approximate sigma profiles are accessible within various software tools and databases, thereby facilitating faster analyses, particularly in high-throughput screening contexts. The application of the Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) with the Vosko-Wilk-Nusair (VWN) and Becke-Perdew (BP) functional within the Dmol3 framework, as recommended by the software developers, has demonstrated encouraging efficacy for real solvent systems. In the present investigation, COSMO results were acquired through DFT calculations employing the Dmol3 module of Materials Studio (Biovia, Materials Studio 2023). The geometries of the molecules were optimized utilizing the GGA (VWN-BP) functional. GGA utilizing VWN-BP functional represents a fundamental exchange-correlation functional employed in density functional theory (DFT) calculations. This specific functional is utilized within the Dmol3 module of Materials Studio for comprehensive electronic structure analyses and simulations. The GGA VWN-BP functional integrates the exchange component devised by Vosko, Wilk, and Nusair with the correlation component formulated by Becke and Perdew, thereby providing a robust framework for characterizing electronic interactions. The exchange portion of the functional facilitates the electron exchange among molecular orbitals, while the correlation portion incorporates the effects of electron repulsion and attraction arising from electron-electron interactions. By incorporating these components, the VWN-BP functional effectively approximates the exchange-correlation effects present within the system. As a member of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) class of functionals, it not only considers electron density but also takes into account its gradient, thereby facilitating a more accurate depiction of the electronic structure of the system when compared to simpler functional, such as the local density approximation (LDA). Within the Dmol3 framework, the GGA VWN-BP functional is employed to optimize molecular geometries, compute electronic properties, and conduct a range of DFT calculations. The methodology employed in this study aims to produce reliable and accurate outcomes across a range of applications, particularly in exploring solvation phenomena within real-world solvent systems. In every scenario analyzed during this research, water was consistently utilized as the solvent. Among the molecules investigated, D-fructose was found to exhibit the lowest dielectric energy, suggesting a relatively weaker interaction with the solvent compared to the other compounds. In contrast, the other molecules demonstrated more negative dielectric energies, indicative of stronger and more favorable interactions with the solvent.

Hydration behavior interpretation

As evidenced in Fig. 1, there are no discernible differences among the peaks observed in the anionic region of the protic ionic liquids; however, in the cationic region, three distinct peaks are identified. Notably, the peak corresponding to 2-hydroxyethylammonium acetate exhibits greater intensity compared to the others, indicating its higher polarity relative to the two other protic ionic liquids. This heightened polarity suggests a stronger interaction between water molecules and 2-hydroxyethylammonium acetate. Moreover, it has been determined that the interaction between D-fructose and 2-hydroxyethylammonium acetate is more pronounced than those with the other ionic liquids, corroborating the results obtained from density functional theory (DFT) analyses. Additionally, the dielectric (hydration) energy of the protic ionic liquids is reported to be significantly negative among the analyzed compounds, further emphasizing the strength of interactions between the PILs and D-fructose. The temperature dependence of the hydration behavior has also been investigated, revealing that both the apparent molar expansibility and isobaric thermal expansion coefficients decrease with increasing temperature. This observation indicates that as temperature rises, the hydrogen bonding interactions between D-fructose and water molecules diminish, facilitating the release of certain water molecules from the hydration shell. Such behavior is likely attributed to the disruption of hydrogen bonds between D-fructose and water as temperature increases.

Additionally, the Hepler’s constant is examined as a critical parameter that delineates the structure-making or structure-breaking behavior of D-fructose in the presence of the examined protic ionic liquids (PILs). The sign of the Hepler’s constant either positive or negative serves to indicate whether D-fructose functions as a structure maker or breaker within the solution. Positive values of the Hepler’s constant suggest a structure-making tendency, signifying that D-fructose has the capacity to reorganize the hydrogen bonding network among water molecules and the PILs. This implies that the inclusion of the investigated PILs may modulate the hydrogen bonding interactions between D-fructose and water, potentially affecting the overall hydration behavior. In conclusion, the results presented herein indicate that D-fructose exhibits hydration behavior that is distinct from that of the studied protic ionic liquids [2-HEA]Ac, [bis-2-HEA]Ac, and [tris-2-HEA]Ac.

Taste behavior

Apparent specific volume (ASV) is a thermodynamic property that represents the volume occupied by a unit mass of a substance, including any voids or spaces within its structure. This concept is particularly important in fields such as materials science, engineering, and fluid dynamics, where understanding the behavior of materials under various conditions is crucial. The apparent specific volume can be influenced by factors such as temperature, pressure, and the phase of the material (solid, liquid, or gas). For example, in porous materials, the specific volume accounts for the space within the pores, which can significantly affect the material’s overall properties. Additionally, the interactions between (PILs) and water may weaken under these conditions, contributing to a reduction in molecular cohesion and an increase in free volume. Conclusion

This work presents the hydration behavior of D-fructose in aqueous solutions of three protic ionic liquids based on ethanolamine (2-hydroxyethylammonium) acetate. This investigation proposes that D-fructose exhibits a diminished intermolecular interaction with water molecules relative to the protic ionic liquids (PILs) [2-HEA]Ac, [bis-2-HEA]Ac, and [tris-2-HEA]Ac. This phenomenon is attributed to D-fructose enhanced compressibility and elevated positive dielectric energy. The standard partial molar volume, reflecting solute-solvent interactions between [2-HEA] Ac and D-fructose in aqueous solution, was determined to be 118.443 cm3·mol−1, indicating stronger interactions compared to other protic ionic liquids. With increasing temperature, the hydration shell enveloping D-fructose undergoes notable perturbation, resulting in the liberation of a greater number of water molecules compared to the PIL solutions. This effect is particularly evident in the presence of [tris-2-HEA] Ac, potentially due to its larger molecular size and more intricate structure, which may enable it to compete more effectively with D-fructose for hydrogen-bonding interactions with water. The positive value of the helper’s constant suggests that D-fructose induces a more ordered arrangement in the surrounding aqueous environment. Conversely, the PILs may disrupt the hydrogen-bonding network between D-fructose and water by reconfiguring the water molecules in proximity to D-fructose and establishing their own hydrogen bonds with the solvent. The molecular level of modifications are also reflected in the taste behavior of solutions, where changes in hydrogen bonding and solute-solvent interactions influence the perceived sweetness intensity of D-fructose.
