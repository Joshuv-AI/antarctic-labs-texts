Impacts and lessons learned from coronavirus-19 pandemic in the United States: preparing for future public health emergencies
Authors: Obert Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The coronavirus-19 (COVID-19) public health emergency created an economic crisis with unprecedented and far-reaching impacts across the health sector. Among the greatest challenge areas experienced were in areas of changing supply and demand, rising costs, decreased revenue, and widening health inequities (Blumenthal etal. N Engl J Med 383:1483–1488, 2020; Cypress, Nurs Forum 57:323–327, 2022; Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, National health spending in 2020 increases due to impact of COVID-19 pandemic, 2021). As we emerge from the pandemic, we must capitalize on the opportunity to transform and adapt to be better equipped to handle the next public health crisis. This article examines the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic and preparedness strategies within the context of the United States.

Pandemic impacts on demand and supply on health care services and products

The pandemic severely disrupted the delicate supply and demand balance for health care services and products in the U.S [1]. Lockdown and quarantine orders meant to reduce viral transmission led to business closures and halting of economic operations, all leading to reduced capacity to provide goods and services to consumers. As hospitals responded to the pandemic by seeing increased patient volumes with the disease, so did the demand for essential health care products and medical equipment. This demand exceeded the supply available, and this was further compounded by supply-chain interruptions [1, 2]. As a result, front-line clinicians faced shortages in essential supplies such as personal protective equipment, mechanical ventilators, and life-saving medicines.

The pandemic placed extreme financial pressures on the health care infrastructure. Clinical priorities shifted within hospitals in the face of increased COVID-19 cases. To increase treatment capacity, resources were diverted towards pandemic-focused care, and elective procedures and outpatient services, that are key revenue streams, were postponed or canceled [1]. Total decline of inpatient and outpatient services is estimated around 13% [3]. Workforce shortages drove up staffing costs, while widespread unemployment led to loss of insurance coverage, increasing uncompensated care burdens [3]. Together, these demand and supply disruptions had significant cost and revenue implications for health systems, setting the stage for financial vulnerabilities.

Pandemic impacts on cost and revenue of health care providers, payers, and consumers

Health care providers faced difficulties balancing cost and revenue during the pandemic, with economic shortfalls estimated at close to $150 billion compared to pre-pandemic levels [4]. Clinicians employed in fee-for-service payment models, particularly in non-emergent medical service lines faced salary cuts and layoffs, as patient volume declined with service suspensions [1, 3, 5]. These economic impacts were partially recuperated with reemergence of telehealth models of care. Payers increased coverage to virtual-based care and pandemic-related care. Federal financial relief programs approved by congress, supported, and offset organizations shortfalls. These includes relief from the Coronavirus Aid, Relief and Economic Security (CARES) Act, Provider Relief Fund, Medicare Accelerated and Small Business Paycheck Protection Program [6, 7].

By 2020, many Americans faced unemployment with estimations around 20 million people [5]. This is impactful as many received health coverage through employer sponsored insurance programs. This ultimately led to greater enrolment into marketplace insurance plans, however those not qualifying were left uninsured [5]. Economic difficulties across the health ecosystem were experienced to varying degrees, with financial relief sought via several avenues.

Pandemic impacts on health equity

Health injustice, inequality and inequity are unfortunately inherent within our health society and system. During the pandemic, these disparities amongst minorities, persons of low socioeconomic status, and underserved populations were further exacerbated. In 2020, it is estimated that racial minorities accounted for half of total COVID-19 cases, and more alarmingly, represented 58% of COVID-19-related deaths [5, 8]. Driving this inequity begins with a lack of care access that challenged the ability to access testing, essential medicines, and the newest treatments during the pandemic [5]. Bridging this gap underpins intentions to broaden health access to all through the Affordable Care Act and Medicaid expansion [8, 9].

Recommendations

Based on lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic, six main themes of recommendations are proposed: sustaining revenue streams through uninterrupted clinical operations; stockpiling essential supplies and equipment; strengthening supply chain systems; building hospital protocols to scale capacity and staffing; exploring innovative payment models; and expanding coverage to improve access and reduce disparities. Each of these areas addresses critical vulnerabilities revealed during the pandemic and offers practical approaches to enhance future public health emergency preparedness and resilience (Table 1). Public health emergency preparedness checklist for health systems

Sustaining revenue streams by maintaining uninterrupted clinical operations

Suspension of elective surgery, non-emergent outpatient care, and dental services led to significant economic implications. Development of contingency plans to maintain uninterrupted operations will prove vital for economic health. Augmentation of existing telehealth services proved to be an effective and efficient model in the face of stay-at-home orders [1, 10]. Other digital health models of care, beyond currently established telehealth services, should be explored. This could include innovations in remote patient monitoring, asynchronous consultations, and mobile health technologies.

Stockpiling essential supplies and equipment

It is imperative to significantly expand the institutional and national armamentarium of emergency medical supplies to ensure readiness for future public health crises [11]. Priority items are personal protective equipment, mechanical ventilators, diagnostics, vaccines, and essential pharmaceuticals. Developing an early stockpile can mitigate the risk of inflated procurement costs during emergencies and ensure uninterrupted clinical operations [12].

Strengthening supply chain systems

To build a more resilient healthcare supply infrastructure, systems must diversify and strengthen resource acquisition pathways [13]. Primary strategies include:Partnering with regionally based suppliers to reduce reliance on a single-source vendor.Expand domestic manufacturing capacity to ensure baseline self-sufficiencies despite global supply disturbances.Leveraging multilateral procurement mechanisms such as the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) Regional Revolving Funds, which offer a well-established pooled procurement platform that enables countries in the Americas to purchase vaccines, medicines, and health technologies at competitive prices via collective bargaining [14, 15].

Of importance, the implications of the U.S. intent to withdraw from the World Health Organization create uncertainty about U.S. participation in regional procurement mechanisms and underscore the importance of ongoing relationship development with global health governance organizations to ensure access to essential medical supplies [16, 17]. These mandates careful policy monitoring and adaptive institutional strategies to ensure continued access to essential supplies.

All efforts to create redundancy and alternate supply chain options reduce vulnerability and disruptions to service from geopolitical or health crises [13]. Future governance of supply systems should additionally focus on the growing role of public–private partnerships and local stockpile initiatives. By integrating these strategies, health systems become agile in the face of an ever-changing global health and trade policy landscape, while ensuring continuous medical supply availability. Building hospital protocols to scale capacity and staffing

Preparation for a future public health emergency should consider the increased demand for our hospital and health system. In the COVID-19 pandemic, additional capacity was needed for testing and treatment. Our systems should anticipate a volume surge with plans to establish alternate care space areas, including tents, operating rooms, hallways, and hospital-at-home programs. There should also be plans to increase general medical and intensive care unit capacity. Scalable staffing models and cross-training health workers will also build workforce capacity. By anticipating the next pandemic, we temper economic costs by accounting for a rainy day.

Exploring new payment models

Fee-for-service payment models were most significantly impacted during the pandemic, with declining health attendance. Value-based care models should be further explored to provide revenue stability and economic viability [2]. Additionally, reimbursement for telehealth services should remain a priority for ongoing advocacy.

Expanding insurance coverage to improve access to reduce disparities

Continuing efforts to expand Medicaid are imperative to improving population health. By improving insurance coverage, we move towards equitable health access [5]. Other avenues to improve health access to underserved populations include supporting the creation of free and low-cost centers strategically positioned in underserved communities.
