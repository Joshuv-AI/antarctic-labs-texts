One-pot synthesis of quasi-block fluoropolymers for graded heterojunctions via dual resurfacing
Authors: Zhilong He, Siyuan Li, Xinyu He, Zhe Hao, Jinkang Chen, Bohao Song, Yi Lin, Zheng Tang, Guanghao Lu, Lijian Zuo, Chang Zhi Li, Hongliang Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraded heterojunctions (GHJs) featuring a gradient donor/acceptor distribution represent an ideal architecture for organic solar cells (OSCs), yet remain challenging to realize. Here, we report a series of fluoropolymers that can form GHJs through surface-energy-driven resurfacing in solution processes. The distinct chemical reactivity of fluorinated versus standard monomers enables the formation of quasi-block copolymers in one-pot polymerization, which rivals the two-step synthesized block copolymer in polymer properties and photovoltaic performance. These fluoropolymers can resurface due to the low-surface-energy fluorinated blocks. During the sequential deposition that small-molecular acceptor solution is cast atop the underlying fluoropolymer film, the acceptor penetrates downwards accompanied by donor resurfacing, consequently generating a vertical donor/acceptor gradient. Moreover, a post-treatment namely fluorous solvent vapor annealing (FSVA) is adopted to further regulate the distribution. The unique solubility of fluoropolymers in fluorous solvents enables fluoropolymers to resurface again, yielding an optimal GHJ to deliver an impressive efficiency of 19.60% in OSCs. Notably, block-containing copolymers achieve superior device performance and more optimal GHJ than the random fluoropolymer, highlighting the importance of monomer sequence. Our findings provide an efficient approach to create GHJs by leveraging surface energy, and also attract attentions to explore the precise structure of copolymers, in particular for fluoropolymers.Subject terms: Solar cells, Conjugated polymers, Polymers

Introduction

Heterojunctions comprising two organic semiconductors with cascaded energy levels have been extensively applied in optoelectronics due to their unique merits in accelerating exciton dissociation and enabling ambipolar transport1–3. In organic semiconductors, excitons (i.e., electron-hole pairs) exhibit strong Coulombic binding energy, necessitating heterojunctions to provide sufficient driving force at the donor-acceptor interfaces for efficient exciton dissociation into free carriers, which is crucial for photoelectric conversion devices such as organic solar cells (OSCs)4,5. Early planar heterojunctions (PHJs) feature a bilayer heterojunction placed between two electrodes, but excitons are difficult to reach the interfaces due to limited exciton diffusion lengths and inadequate donor/acceptor interfaces6. Mostly used bulk heterojunctions (BHJs), formed by blend casting donor and acceptor materials7,8, promote phase separation to create numerous interfaces for effective exciton dissociation but inevitably suffer from charge recombination9–11. Recently, quasi-planar heterojunctions (Q-PHJs) have been developed through sequential deposition of donor and acceptor12–14, forming a donor-acceptor mixed layer sandwiched between an acceptor-rich top layer and a donor-rich bottom layer, thereby enhancing ambipolar transport and suppressing charge recombination15,16. Although Q-PHJs have achieved high efficiencies, there is an urgent need to explore more ideal architectures to further advance OSCs. Graded heterojunctions (GHJs) that feature a gradient distribution of donor/acceptor components combine the short exciton diffusion distances of BHJs with the charge gradient of bilayer heterojunctions. However, it remains challenging to achieve GHJs, particularly through solution processing17–19.

The vertical donor/acceptor distribution in solution-processed heterojunctions is primarily dictated by their intrinsic material properties. Current research efforts predominantly concentrate on leveraging the differential solubility and crystallinity of donor and acceptor materials to modulate vertical phase distribution20–22. However, since both donor and acceptor are composed of similar conjugated structures, their subtle differences are insufficient to confer markedly distinct thermodynamic and kinetic properties during the rapid film-formation process23–25. Surface energy, as an intrinsic material property, is a crucial determinant in the stratification of blend films, with components exhibiting lower surface energy tending to spontaneously lift onto the surface during the film-forming process26–28. Polyfluoride moieties (e.g., fluoroalkyl chains) are well-known for their capacity to lower surface energy29–32. Nevertheless, their application in controlling donor/acceptor distribution in OSCs remains limited, possibly owing to the miscibility and compatibility issues that emerge from substantial surface energy disparities between donor and acceptor materials33,34. Block fluoropolymers may present a potential solution to these challenges. In such block copolymers, the fluorine-enriched blocks facilitate surface-energy-driven resurfacing during solution processing, while the other block, with less or no fluorine, maintains acceptable miscibility. Notably, conventional synthetic routes for conjugated block copolymers often involve laborious procedures with limited control over block length and sequence precision. Thus, we are inspired to explore a facile approach to synthesize block fluoropolymers, as well as their applications in tuning the donor/acceptor distribution to realize GHJs for OSCs.

In this work, a series of fluoropolymers containing identical monomers but different monomer sequences are designed and prepared. The different chemical reactivity between the standard monomer and polyfluorinated monomer helps the resulting copolymers to form quasi-block polymer chains rather than random ones in one-pot polymerization. Notably, the one-pot synthesized qb-PF20 rivals the two-step synthesized block copolymer b-PF20 in both structural precision and optoelectronic performance, while block-containing fluoropolymers outperform the random polymer ra-PF20. In the solution cast, the fluorinated blocks tend to resurface owing to the extra-low surface energy, while the standard blocks ensure solubility in organic solvents and miscibility with the acceptor. When employing sequential deposition for heterojunction construction, the small-molecule acceptor percolates downwards, accompanied by the spontaneous resurfacing of the underlying fluoropolymer. This dual action results in a vertical gradient distribution of donor/acceptor. More importantly, a post-treatment, namely fluorous solvent vapor annealing (FSVA), further elevates the donor due to the selective solubility of fluoropolymers in the fluorous solvent, consequently providing a more uniform gradient. Based on the optimal GHJ, the corresponding OSCs achieve a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 19.60% using the one-step synthesized quasi-block copolymer qb-PF20. This work demonstrates the potential of developing new materials with tailored properties, such as surface energy, to meet the critical requirements of optoelectronic devices.

Results

Synthesis and polymerization kinetics of fluoropolymers

In our previous work, heptafluoroisopropoxyl groups terminated side chains have been demonstrated to reduce surface energy and enhance intermolecular interactions35. Thus, polyfluorinated monomers (DF) containing heptafluoroisopropoxyl groups are introduced into copolymers to reduce surface energy, while standard monomers (D) ensure the solubility and miscibility of the resulting copolymers. The detailed synthetic routes for the monomers are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1 and their chemical structures are conclusively verified using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra and mass spectra. The copolymers qb-PFX (X = 10, 20, 30, denoting DF contents of 10, 20, and 30%, respectively) are synthesized via a one-pot Stille cross-coupling reaction of DF, D and the comonomer A (Fig. 1a), while lab-made PM6 (Supplementary Fig. 2) with D and A is prepared as the counterpart. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) is employed to determine the molecular weight (Mn) and polydispersity index (PDI), and the values of as-synthesized fluoropolymers are similar to those of PM6 (Supplementary Fig. 3). Both the qb-PFX series and PM6 exhibit excellent solubility in common organic solvents (e.g., chloroform and chlorobenzene). As expected, the fluoropolymers show slight solubility in fluorous solvents like perfluorotoluene (PFT), whereas PM6 is virtually insoluble (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Notably, the polyfluorinated monomer DF exhibits higher reactivity than the common monomer D, which might be attributed to the strong polarization and coordination ability of heptafluoroisopropoxyl groups36,37. Thus, the existence of DF accelerates the polymerization. Taking qb-PF20 as an example, the mixture solution becomes dark red after heating at 80 °C for 30 min, whereas the PM6 polymerization under identical conditions appears as an orange solution (Supplementary Fig. 5). To further investigate the distinct polymerization kinetics between qb-PF20 and PM6, parallel reactions are conducted at 80 °C with equal-volume aliquots withdrawn at 30, 60, 90 and 120 min for subsequent GPC and NMR characterizations. The GPC elution curves (Supplementary Fig. 6) reveal that PM6 predominantly exhibits monomer signals after 30 min, while qb-PF20 already displays prominent oligomer formation. Notably, monomer signals in qb-PF20 nearly disappear by 120 min, confirming the accelerated polymerization rate of the fluoropolymer. To quantitatively evaluate monomer conversion, 1H NMR measurements are performed on PM6 and qb-PF20 samples at different reaction times using an equimolar amount of 1,4-dibromo-2,5-diiodobenzene as the internal standard. As depicted in Supplementary Figs. 7–9, the progressive decrease in integral ratios of monomer A quantitatively reflects monomer consumption during polymerization, with the fluoropolymer qb-PF20 consistently exhibiting higher monomer conversion rates relative to PM6 throughout the reaction time. Upon extending the reaction to 120 min, monomer signals in qb-PF20 become nearly undetectable with a conversion rate of 0.91, demonstrating the significantly enhanced reaction kinetics imparted by fluorinated monomers. Thus, we speculate this reactivity disparity would influence monomer sequence in polymer chains, i.e., qb-PFX may form as quasi-block copolymers rather than random copolymers. To verify this, matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry is used to analyze oligomer compositions during the early polymerization stage (80 °C for 30 min), with the segments and corresponding molecular mass summarized in Supplementary Fig. 10. As shown in Fig. 1b, the characteristic signals of fluorinated monomer DF-based segments, including DF-A, DF-A-DF, DF-A-DF-A and DF-A-DF-A-DF, consistently exhibit higher intensity than corresponding segments only containing the monomer D, suggesting the higher reactivity of DF drives preferential polymerization to form fluorine-enriched blocks. Meanwhile, the hybrid segments combining both DF and D, like DF-A-D and DF-A-D-A show very weak intensity in the early polymerization stage. To further analyze the distribution of DF and D segments, preparative GPC is employed to separate oligomers with varying molecular weights. Through systematic optimization of the mobile phase, temperature and flow rate, oligomers and monomers can be separated with the retention time peaks of 37.3 and 44.5 min (Supplementary Fig. 11). The two collected fractions (oligomer-1 and oligomer-2) are characterized by 1H NMR after solvent removal. Although aromatic proton signals of oligomers are difficult to resolve, the relative proportions of D and DF segments are determined by comparing integral ratios of the methylene groups connected to thiophene in side chains and heptafluoroisopropoxyl groups. Notably, oligomer-2 contains a markedly higher proportion of DF-containing segments than D-based segments, with an integral ratio of 1:0.28 (Supplementary Fig. 12). As for higher molecular weight oligomer-1, the relative content of DF-based segments is further increased, yielding an integral ratio of 1:0.16. These results suggest that the highly reactive DF preferentially polymerizes to form fluorine-rich blocks with considerable numbers of repeat units, consequently delivering a quasi-block copolymer.

To further elucidate the pivotal role of monomer sequence in determining polymer properties and photovoltaic performance, two well-defined fluoropolymer derivatives, a block copolymer and a random copolymer, are synthesized as control samples. Based on the preliminary data (Supplementary Figs. 13–20 and Supplementary Tables 1–5), qb-PF20 demonstrates balanced physicochemical properties and superior photovoltaic performance among qb-PFX. Thus, the optimal DF content of 20% is also employed in the block and random copolymers. As illustrated in Fig. 1c, d, the block copolymer (b-PF20) and random copolymer (ra-PF20) are synthesized by controlling monomer addition sequences. To obtain the copolymer b-PF20, 0.2 equivalent (eq) of each DF and A monomers are initially reacted at 90 °C for 2 h to form DF-A oligomers, followed by the addition of 0.8 eq of each D and A, and then the mixture is heated to 120 °C and kept for 24 h. For the copolymer ra-PF20, the polymerization of 0.8 eq each D and A monomers is initiated by heating to 120 °C, immediately followed by the dropwise addition of 0.2 eq each DF and A monomers over 50 min. Since the monomer DF shows higher reactivity than D, the dropwise addition of DF in the early polymerization stage ensures the fluorinated monomer will randomly and evenly distribute in oligomers to form a random copolymer. Both copolymers exhibit comparable molecular weights to qb-PF20, thereby minimizing the influence of various molecular weights on polymer properties (Supplementary Fig. 21).

Polymer properties of fluoropolymers with distinct monomer sequences

To study the fluorine effect on surface energy, the contact angle measurements of these three copolymers with different monomer sequences are carried out. As shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 22, the surface energies of ra-PF20, qb-PF20, and b-PF20 neat films are calculated according to Wu’s model, as 19.76, 19.20 and 19.03 mN m−1, respectively. As expected, these fluoropolymers exhibit evidently smaller surface energy than PM6 (20.77 mN m−1). Notably, the surface energy of qb-PF20 is comparable to that of b-PF20 but obviously lower than that of ra-PF20, further confirming that qb-PF20 synthesized via one-pot polymerization is a quasi-block copolymer rather than a random copolymer. Moreover, the measured surface energy reflects the overall property of the (quasi-)block copolymer, while the fluorinated block should inherently possess a lower surface energy. As a result, fluorinated blocks can make qb-PF20 and b-PF20 resurface spontaneously during the solution cast to form a stratified film. To verify this, the stratification of ra-PF20, qb-PF20, and b-PF20 neat films is examined using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS). As illustrated in Fig. 2b, the characteristic (CF3)2(CF)O− ion signals of qb-PF20 and b-PF20 exhibit a similar trend featuring a rapid decline at the initial sputtering stage, which indicates an enrichment of the polyfluorinated moiety in the top layer of these two films. Compared with qb-PF20 and b-PF20, the intensity of (CF3)2(CF)O− ion signal in the random copolymer ra-PF20 is obviously weaker in the top layer because ra-PF20 has a more uniform distribution of polyfluorinated monomers. These results suggest that one-pot synthesized qb-PF20 predominantly consists of localized fluorinated blocks rather than being uniformly and randomly distributed throughout the polymer chains. More importantly, some studies demonstrated that the fluorinated units and molecules preferentially migrate to the surface owing to their low surface energy26,28,38, suggesting these fluorinated blocks, accompanied by the whole polymer chain, will migrate to the surface during the film-formation process. To evaluate the thermodynamic difference among these copolymers, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements are conducted on ra-PF20, qb-PF20 and b-PF20 copolymers. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 23, the melting peak of qb-PF20 (328.1 °C) in the heating scan is closer to that of b-PF20 (329.5 °C) than to that of ra-PF20 (324.9 °C), indicating not only the formation of fluorine-containing blocks in qb-PF20 but also a higher packing order and crystallinity in block copolymers.

To study the molecular packing and morphology of these copolymers with different monomer sequences, grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray diffraction (GIWAXS) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements are conducted, with PM6 used as a control. As shown in the 2D GIWAXS pattern (Supplementary Fig. 24), all polymers exhibit a preferential face-on orientation. The corresponding 1D GIWAXS profiles (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 6) reveal π-π stacking peaks at 1.745, 1.761, 1.767 and 1.769 Å−1, corresponding to d-spacings of 3.601, 3.568, 3.556 and 3.552 Å for PM6, ra-PF20, qb-PF20, and b-PF20, respectively. Furthermore, the crystal coherence length (CCL) values representing the crystallinity are calculated to be 21.34, 22.18, 22.80, and 22.62 Å for the respective polymers. These results demonstrate that incorporating polyfluorinated units in the side chains enhances the molecular packing of the relevant copolymers, relative to the reference polymer PM6. Furthermore, qb-PF20 achieves ordered packing and high crystallinity comparable to b-PF20, due to the existence of fluorinated blocks. As shown in Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 25, the PM6 neat film exhibits a smooth surface with slimmer domains and a low root-mean-square roughness (Rq) of 1.12 nm. In contrast, all fluoropolymers display distinct fibrillar morphologies with increased surface roughness. Notably, the higher Rq values of qb-PF20 (1.47 nm) and b-PF20 (1.56 nm) relative to ra-PF20 (1.29 nm) suggest that block formation enhances molecular aggregation and serves as a crystalline nucleus to promote crystallinity. The optical and electrochemical properties of these copolymers, composed of identical monomers but distinct sequences, also exhibit sequence-dependent behavior. As shown in Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 26a, and Supplementary Table 7, all polymers exhibit similar absorption profiles featuring two characteristic peaks at around 576 and 616 nm corresponding to 0-1 and 0-0 transitions, respectively. All fluoropolymers exhibit higher 0-0/0-1 peak intensity (A0-0/A0-1) ratios than PM6 in both solution and solid states. Notably, both qb-PF20 and b-PF20 display marginally higher molar extinction coefficients and A0-0/A0-1 ratios compared to ra-PF20, suggesting the enhancement of molecular packing induced by block formation. Moreover, the block-containing copolymers feature slightly deeper HOMO/LUMO energy levels (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 26b).

Photovoltaic performance of blend-cast OSCs

To systematically assess the impact of monomer sequence on the photovoltaic performance, OSCs are fabricated by employing ra-PF20, qb-PF20, and b-PF20 as donors along with BTP-eC9 as acceptor. As shown in current-density-voltage (J–V) characteristic curves (Fig. 3a) and corresponding parameters (Table 1) of the optimized devices, the control PM6:BTP-eC9 exhibits a PCE of 17.91% with an open-circuit voltage (Voc) of 0.848 V, a short-circuit current density (Jsc) of 27.52 mA cm−2, and a fill factor (FF) of 76.74%. In contrast, the fluoropolymer-based devices all display superior PCEs with synergistic improvements in photovoltaic parameters. Notably, the qb-PF20:BTP-eC9 device demonstrates a superior PCE of 18.97% to the ra-PF20:BTP-eC9 device (18.54%), attributable to synergistic enhancements in Voc (0.862 V), Jsc (28.05 mA cm−2) and FF (78.47%). The elevated Voc and Jsc of the block-containing copolymer-based device are associated with the suppressed charge recombination and improved charge transport. Remarkably, the qb-PF20:BTP-eC9 device achieves near-identical device efficiency to its block counterpart b-PF20 (19.03%) with comparable photovoltaic parameters, demonstrating that the one-pot synthesized quasi-block copolymer rivals the two-step synthesized block copolymer in photovoltaic performance. External quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra indicate that OSCs based on fluoropolymers exhibit enhanced EQE values compared to the PM6:BTP-eC9 device (Fig. 3b), particularly in the donor absorption region. This improvement is ascribed to optimized packing order and crystallinity induced by fluorinated side-chain incorporation. Furthermore, devices incorporating block-containing copolymers demonstrate superior photoresponse relative to ra-PF20, suggesting more efficient charge transfer and collection. Given the comparable HOMO energy levels of the copolymers yet distinct Voc in the devices, the electroluminescence external quantum efficiency (EQEEL) of the devices is measured to quantitatively analyze the energy losses from different contributions. As summarized in Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 27, and Supplementary Table 8, the radiative recombination losses derived from absorption above (∆E1) and below (∆E2) bandgap exhibit minimal variation, suggesting similar charge-transfer states39. Notably, the non-radiative recombination loss (∆E3) values are calculated to be 0.249, 0.245, 0.236, and 0.239 eV for devices based on PM6, ra-PF20, qb-PF20, and b-PF20, respectively. These results demonstrate that the enhanced crystallinity of block-containing copolymers (qb-PF20 and b-PF20) relative to ra-PF20 effectively reduces energy disorder, thereby mitigating energy loss.

To gain deeper insight into the carrier dynamics, the exciton dissociation (Pdiss) and charge collection probabilities (Pcoll) are estimated from the effective voltage (Veff) versus photocurrent density (Jph) relationship. As shown in Fig. 3d, the devices incorporating fluoropolymers exhibit higher Pdiss and Pcoll values than the PM6-based device. Notably, the qb-PF20-based device demonstrates comparable Pdiss and Pcoll values to those of the b-PF20 counterpart, with both devices exhibiting higher Pdiss/Pcoll values than the ra-PF20-based device. To characterize the charge transport properties, the hole mobility (µh) and electron mobility (µe) are determined by the space-charge-limited current (SCLC) model. As summarized in Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 28, the µh/µe values are 6.88/7.92 × 10−4, 7.54/8.69 × 10−4, 8.03/8.93 × 10−4, and 8.18/9.07 × 10−4 cm2 V−1 s−1 for PM6:BTP-eC9, ra-PF20:BTP-eC9, qb-PF20:BTP-eC9, and b-PF20:BTP-eC9 devices, respectively. Notably, the higher and more balanced mobilities of devices employing block-containing copolymers as donors than the ra-PF20 counterpart correlate with the enhanced packing order and improved crystallinity induced by block formation. To investigate the charge recombination behaviors, we examine the dependence of Voc and Jsc on incident light intensity (Plight). The relationships between Voc/Jsc and Plight can be expressed as Voc∝n(kT/q)ln(Plight) and Jsc∝Plightα, respectively (where k is the Boltzmann constant, T is the Kelvin temperature, and q is the elementary charge). As shown in Fig. 3f, the slope n values are 1.15, 1.13, 1.07 and 1.08 kT/q, and the α values are 0.973, 0.975, 0.982 and 0.980 for PM6:BTP-eC9, ra-PF20:BTP-eC9, qb-PF20:BTP-eC9, and b-PF20:BTP-eC9 devices, respectively. These results demonstrate that devices incorporating block-containing copolymers show progressively reduced trap-assisted and bimolecular recombination, consistent with their enhanced packing order and crystallinity. In summary, the device employing qb-PF20 possesses essentially equivalent carrier dynamics compared with the b-PF20 device, while both systems exhibit superior exciton dissociation, balanced charge transport and suppressed charge recombination relative to the ra-PF20 device. These findings indicate that introducing well-defined block sequences rather than randomly distributed sequences into the copolymer can afford superior optoelectronic properties.

Photovoltaic performance of sequential-deposited OSCs and the effect of FSVA

Based on the above results, the block-containing copolymers qb-PF20 and b-PF20 exhibit the potential to resurface in solution processes, and the one-pot synthesized qb-PF20 rivals the two-step synthesized b-PF20 in photovoltaic performance. Consequently, qb-PF20 is selected as the donor material for the fabrication of GHJs via sequential deposition (SD). However, the initial SD attempt failed to yield a uniform film (inset of Fig. 4a), resulting in a reduced PCE of 17.84% with a large fluctuation of 1.49%, significantly worse than the benchmark BC-processed devices (a PCE of 18.97% with only 0.33% fluctuation). TOF-SIMS analysis (Fig. 2a) has identified a fluorine-enriched surface in the as-cast qb-PF20 film, which may induce a significant surface-energy disparity between the underlying qb-PF20 and overlying BTP-eC9 that impedes the latter solution infiltration. To address this issue, perfluorotoluene is employed as a cosolvent for the process of BTP-eC9, due to its selective solubility for polyfluorinated groups and excellent miscibility with chloroform. Adding only 0.5 vol% PFT into the BTP-eC9 solution enables the formation of a high-quality blend film (inset of Fig. 4a). As summarized in Table 1, the fluorous solvent-assisted SD device (denoted as SD/FS) with uniform blend films achieved a higher PCE of 19.12% associated with a Voc of 0.862 V, a Jsc of 28.25 mA cm−2, and an FF of 78.53%. Notably, the organic/fluorous mixed solvent is also applicable to other fluoropolymers. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 29 and Table 1, the SD/FS processed devices for ra-PF20/BTP-eC9 and b-PF20/BTP-eC9 achieve PCEs of 18.71 and 19.07%, respectively. These results further demonstrate that the quasi-block copolymer qb-PF20 rivals the block copolymer b-PF20 in the devices prepared by various methods.

The significantly different performance of the SD and SD/FS devices prompted an investigation into film formation kinetics using in situ time-resolved UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy40,41. The maximum absorption peaks of qb-PF20 and BTP-eC9 are employed as indicators to reflect the molecular packing, and Supplementary Fig. 30 illustrates the change of maximum absorption peaks following the spin-coating time. During the casting of BTP-eC9, the absorption of qb-PF20 changes in different ways depending on the solvents for BTP-eC9. When the BTP-eC9 chloroform solution is dropped atop the underlying qb-PF20 film, the maximum peak of qb-PF20 declines to 612 nm initially, attributed to solvent-induced swelling of the pre-fabricated film, and then almost equilibrates at 612–613 nm until 0.4 s. In contrast, the deposition of BTP-eC9 in organic/fluorous cosolvent induces a steep decline of qb-PF20 to 608 nm, followed by a gradual rise to 614 nm from 0.1 to 0.4 s. In this period from 0 to 0.4 s, the underlying qb-PF20 will swell, accompanied by the penetration of BTP-eC9. Evidently, the existence of fluorous solvent PFT enhances wettability during the second deposition, promoting qb-PF20 swelling and intermolecular interactions with BTP-eC9. Without PFT, the absorption peak of BTP-eC9 starts to red-shift at 0.4 s and achieves 821 nm at 0.6 s, indicating the molecular aggregation process is around 0.2 s. Meanwhile, the packing of BTP-eC9 takes 0.3 s (from 0.3 to 0.6 s) with the existence of PFT, wherein the prolonged aggregation can facilitate nanoscale phase separation.

Sequential deposition enables the formation of a heterojunction with a donor-rich bottom and an acceptor-rich top, which is beneficial to suppress charge recombination between the active layer and carrier transporting layer. However, the significant difference in surface energy between qb-PF20 and BTP-eC9, combined with the rapid solution process, causes excessive confinement of the donor and acceptor to the bottom and top, respectively. Although redissolution and vertical redistribution of partial qb-PF20 occur during secondary spin-coating, this self-adjustment only alleviates the unfavorable situation. To achieve optimized gradient distribution of donor and acceptor, we implement a FSVA post-treatment based on our recent findings that polyfluorinated polymers undergo selective molecular reorganization with the help of fluorous solvents35,42–44. Thus, the vapor of the fluorous solvent PFT is speculated to provide a slight solubility for qb-PF20 and then induce its resurfacing to further regulate vertical donor/acceptor distribution. After an optimized FSVA post-treatment at 100 °C for 3 min (Supplementary Table 9), the device (defined as SD/FS-FSVA) achieves a champion PCE of 19.60% with a Voc of 0.868 V, a Jsc of 28.42 mA cm−2, and an FF of 79.44% (Fig. 4a and Table 1).

Notably, the FSVA treatment may facilitate the formation of a gradient donor-acceptor distribution to enhance exciton dissociation and mitigate energy loss. EQE spectra demonstrate that the SD/FS-FSVA device exhibits enhanced photoresponse relative to SD and SD/FS devices, particularly in the donor absorption region (Fig. 4b), suggesting FSVA can enhance the packing order of qb-PF20 to facilitate the photon-to-electron conversion. As summarized in Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 31, and Supplementary Table 10, the ∆E3 values are determined to be 0.240, 0.233, and 0.228 eV for the SD, SD/FS, and SD/FS-FSVA devices, respectively, indicating that FSVA treatment can optimize the donor/acceptor distribution and molecular packing to reduce energy loss. To further demonstrate the effect of FSVA on carrier dynamic behaviors, the exciton dissociation, charge extraction, transport, and recombination are systematically measured for SD, SD/FS, and SD/FS-FSVA devices. As shown in Fig. 4d, the SD/FS-FSVA device exhibits Pdiss and Pcoll values of 97.74 and 89.85%, respectively, which are obviously higher than those of the SD and SD/FS devices, evidencing more efficient exciton dissociation and charge extraction after FSVA post-treatment. Regarding charge transport properties, SD/FS-FSVA devices show higher and more balanced carrier mobilities compared to SD and SD/FS counterparts (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 32). To validate suppressed charge recombination, the light density-dependent Voc and Jsc characteristics are measured. As shown in Fig. 4f, the slope n values are 1.10, 1.06, and 1.04 kT/q, and the α values are 0.980, 0.988, and 0.991 for SD, SD/FS, and SD/FS-FSVA devices, respectively. These trends unambiguously demonstrate that FSVA post-treatment effectively suppresses both trap-assisted and bimolecular recombination. Briefly, the SD/FS-FSVA device demonstrates significantly improved exciton dissociation and charge transport, minimized energy loss, and suppressed charge recombination, attributes directly linked to its optimized gradient donor-acceptor distribution.

The post-treatment FSVA is also employed in devices based on ra-PF20 and b-PF20 to elucidate the effect of distinct monomer sequences. After many attempts, the optimal FSVA conditions for b-PF20 and ra-PF20-based devices are 90 °C for 5 min and 100 °C for 5 min, respectively (Supplementary Tables 11 and 12). The lower processing temperature for b-PF20 and the longer annealing time for ra-PF20 compared to qb-PF20 (100 °C for 3 min) can be attributed to their distinct surface fluorine distributions, i.e., polyfluoride moieties are more localized and dispersive in b-PF20 and ra-PF20, respectively. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 33 and Table 1, the SD/FS-FSVA processed b-PF20/BTP-eC9 device achieves a champion PCE of 19.71% with a Voc of 0.866 V, a Jsc of 28.49 mA cm−2, and an FF of 79.87%. The photovoltaic performance based on b-PF20 and qb-PF20 markedly surpasses that of the ra-PF20-based device (18.82%), highlighting the selective efficacy of FSVA treatment on block-containing fluoropolymers. Furthermore, the SD/FS-FSVA method is also more effective for qb-PF20 and b-PF20 in optimizing carrier dynamics (Supplementary Figs. 34 and 35 and Supplementary Tables 13 and 14), further confirming the synergetic role of block-containing fluoropolymers and FSVA treatment in optimizing gradient donor-acceptor distribution.

GHJ formation mechanism and universality

Compared with the blend-cast OSCs, the enhancements in photovoltaic performance and carrier dynamics of sequentially deposited devices, in particular those with FSVA, can be attributed to the optimal vertical phase distribution. To elucidate the GHJ formation, the surface and depth-dependent properties of qb-PF20/BTP-eC9 blend films are characterized. As presented in Supplementary Fig. 36, the surface energy of the SD/FS-FSVA film (20.41 mN m−1) is significantly lower than that of the SD/FS film (21.34 mN m−1), demonstrating FSVA renders the resurfacing of qb-PF20 that consequently enables secondary regulation of the vertical distribution. The essential role of perfluorotoluene is further investigated by applying conventional organic solvent (toluene) vapor annealing (OSVA) post-treatment to qb-PF20/BTP-eC9 devices (denoted as SD/FS-OSVA). As summarized in Supplementary Table 15, the PCEs of SD/FS-OSVA devices remain inferior to SD/FS-FSVA devices while close to SD/FS devices, suggesting the limited ability of toluene to reorganize fluoropolymer. This performance difference may arise from the preferential dissolution of the small-molecule acceptor in toluene over the fluoropolymer, as reflected in the distinct surface energies. Specifically, the SD/FS-OSVA blend film exhibits a surface energy of 21.62 mN m−1 (Supplementary Fig. 36), significantly higher than that of SD/FS-FSVA film and slightly elevated relative to SD/FS films, reaffirming the critical role of fluorous solvent in resurfacing fluoropolymer and optimizing GHJ. Furthermore, the donor/acceptor distribution in the vertical direction is quantitatively analyzed by TOF-SIMS. Depth profiles of CN− (representing the acceptor BTP-eC9) and F− (representing the donor qb-PF20) are shown in Fig. 5a, b. The CN− signal is intense in the initial sputtering stage, decaying gradually with depth, whereas the F- signal shows an inverse trend that increases progressively during sputtering. In simpler terms, BTP-eC9 content gradually decreases, and qb-PF20 content increases from the blend film surface to the substrate, illustrating that these films are GHJs. To verify the effect of FSVA in manipulating the donor/acceptor distribution, the change slopes of CN− and F− signals over a sputtering time of 0–200 s are fitted and presented in the insets of Fig. 5a, b. Compared to the SD/FS film, the SD/FS-FSVA film exhibits smaller slopes in both CN- and F- profiles, i.e., an optimized GHJ with a more even gradient donor/acceptor distribution, owing to the qb-PF20 continually resurfaces during FSVA treatment. To identify the superiority of block-containing fluoropolymers in GHJ formation, TOF-SIMS measurements are also conducted in the ra-PF20 and b-PF20-based films. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 37, compared with the qb-PF20 counterpart, the SD/FS-FSVA-processed b-PF20/BTP-eC9 film displays similar trends with slightly smaller slopes in both CN− and F− depth profiles, while the slopes of the ra-PF20/BTP-eC9 film are obviously larger, indicating an inferior gradient distribution in the case of the random copolymer. These results demonstrate that fluorinated blocks with low surface energy can facilitate the resurfacing of polymer donors to realize optimal GHJs.

TOF-SIMS depth profiles of a CN− (representing the acceptor BTP-eC9) and b F− (representing the donor qb-PF20) distributions in SD/FS and SD/FS-FSVA films. c Film-depth-dependent absorption spectra. d Schematic illustration of the GHJ formation mechanism via dual resurfacing of fluoropolymers in film-formation and post-treatment processes.

Besides the donor/acceptor distribution, molecular packing is investigated by in situ film-depth-dependent light absorption spectroscopy (FLAS). SD/FS and SD/FS-FSVA blend films are sequentially etched by soft plasma, and the absorption spectra of the remaining layers are monitored. The absorption difference between the original and remaining film is transformed to reflect the absorption of each etched layer45,46. As displayed in Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 38, the A0-0/A0-1 ratio of qb-PF20 increases from 1.54 to 2.12 after FSVA with enhanced 0-0 peaks in each etched layer, suggesting that FSVA improves the packing order of the fluoropolymer. Meanwhile, FSVA induces a 3 nm red-shift in qb-PF20 maximum peak, versus only 1 nm shift for BTP-eC9, indicating preferential reorganization of the fluoropolymer. To further validate this, GIWAXS measurements are performed on SD/FS and SD/FS-FSVA blend films (Supplementary Fig. 39 and Supplementary Table 16). Notably, the SD/FS-FSVA film exhibits reduced d-spacing and increased CCL values relative to the SD/FS film, demonstrating that FSVA treatment enhances packing order and crystallinity. Collectively, these results show that FSVA optimizes the GHJ by regulating the vertical donor/acceptor distribution and improving fluoropolymer packing order.

Based on the aforementioned results, the GHJ formation and optimization mechanism is schematically illustrated in Fig. 5d. The fluorinated blocks containing heptafluoroisopropoxyl groups decrease the surface energy of fluoropolymers and thereby endow them with the capability of resurfacing in solution processes. During sequential deposition, the fluorine-enriched surface increases the difficulty for the percolation of BTP-eC9. To mitigate this issue, the organic/fluorous mixed solvents facilitate BTP-eC9 to successfully percolate into the fluoropolymer mesh, while the fluoropolymer partially redissolves and resurfaces during the casting of BTP-eC9. Thus, the first regulation of the vertical distribution is achieved to provide a crude GHJ. Further treating with FSVA, the fluorous solvent penetrates into the GHJ and selectively lifts the fluoropolymer, realizing the secondary regulation of the gradient distribution. This two-stage resurfacing is driven by extra-low surface energy of fluorinated blocks, while the second resurfacing is assisted by the fluorous solvent, ultimately yielding an optimal GHJ with ideal gradient donor/acceptor distribution.

Batch-to-batch reproducibility in OSCs remains a significant challenge due to inevitable variations in Mn and PDI of polymers. Notably, the synthesis of block-containing copolymers addresses this issue through controlled, stepwise polymerization that forms well-defined blocks, effectively minimizing batch-to-batch variations in device performance. To confirm this, three additional batches of ra-PF20, qb-PF20 and b-PF20 with different Mn and PDI are synthesized, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 40 and Supplementary Table 17. These polymers are then associated with BTP-eC9 and fabricate OSCs using the SD/FS-FSVA method to investigate the dependence of PCEs on copolymer Mn (Supplementary Fig. 41 and Supplementary Table 18). While all devices exhibit reduced performance with decreasing polymer Mn, ra-PF20-based devices suffer an obvious decline of PCE (10.4% at Mn of 25.6 kDa) compared to the best batch. In contrast, all batches of qb-PF20 and b-PF20-based devices maintain almost identical Voc and slightly declined Jsc and FF, resulting in mitigated PCE losses of 7.4% (at Mn of 24.6 kDa) and 6.8% (at Mn of 25.7 kDa), respectively. These results demonstrate the superior molecular weight insensitivity of block-containing fluoropolymers compared to random copolymer, highlighting the importance of precise monomer sequence control in optimizing the batch-to-batch reproducibility of OSCs. To further evaluate the universality of the fluoropolymers and FSVA combined strategy, other representative acceptors L8BO and Y6 are paired with ra-PF20, qb-PF20 and b-PF20 to fabricate both BC and SD/FS-FSVA devices. J–V curves and corresponding photovoltaic parameters are presented in Supplementary Fig. 42 and Supplementary Tables 19 and 20. Crucially, SD/FS-FSVA devices demonstrate consistently higher PCEs than their BC counterparts across both L8BO and Y6 systems. Notably, qb-PF20/L8BO and b-PF20/L8BO-based devices via the SD/FS-FSVA method achieve remarkable PCEs of approximately 19.50%. The efficiency improvements prove more pronounced in block-containing copolymers compared to random copolymers, underscoring the superiority and broad applicability of the block fluoropolymer-FSVA synergistic strategy.

Discussion

To realize GHJ in OSCs, we developed a series of fluoropolymers with identical monomers but varying monomer sequences (ra-PF20, qb-PF20 and b-PF20), modulating the donor/acceptor distribution within OSCs via the intrinsic low surface energy of fluorinated blocks and fluorous solvent post-treatment. First, the polyfluorinated monomer exhibits a higher chemical reactivity compared to the standard monomer, favoring the preferential formation of quasi-block copolymer qb-PF20 in one-pot polymerization. Notably, one-pot synthesized qb-PF20 rivals the two-step synthesized block copolymer b-PF20 in both structural precision and optoelectronic performance, while (quasi-)block fluoropolymers outperform the random copolymer ra-PF20. Among these block-containing fluoropolymers, fluorinated blocks endow extra-low surface energy, driving whole polymer chain resurfacing in solution processes to tune the vertical donor/acceptor distribution, while standard blocks maintain solubility in organic solvents and compatibility with small-molecule acceptors. During sequential deposition, where acceptor BTP-eC9 solution is cast onto the underlying fluoropolymer film, the resurfacing capability makes the fluoropolymer donor move upwards, accompanied by the BTP-eC9 percolating downwards, consequently regulating the donor/acceptor distribution to form a crude GHJ. Then, the unique solubility of the fluoropolymer in fluorous solvent settles the probability of the secondary regulation. With the help of FSVA, the perfluorotoluene penetrates the blend film to enable the fluoropolymer to resurface again. Based on this dual resurfacing of the fluoropolymers, an optimal GHJ with a gradient distribution of donor/acceptor in the vertical direction is eventually realized. As a result, the qb-PF20 based GHJ device delivers an impressive PCE of 19.60%. In addition, we systematically evaluate the impact of sequential deposition and FSVA on ra-PF20, qb-PF20, and b-PF20-based devices. As expected, the block-containing polymers achieve higher efficiency and optimal GHJ than the random fluoropolymer, further confirming the synergy of block-containing fluoropolymers and FSVA treatment, and highlighting the impact of monomer sequence on polymer properties. Briefly, this work provides a paradigm to achieve highly ordered GHJs through solution processes and also offers insights to develop fluorinated materials with distinctive properties, like self-organization, resurfacing, selective solubility and so on.

Methods

Polymerization of qb-PF20, b-PF20, and ra-PF20

The chemical structure of monomer DF was confirmed by NMR spectra and mass spectra (Supplementary Figs. 43–54). The quasi-block copolymer qb-PF20 was synthesized via one-pot polymerization. A mixture of DF (25.7 mg, 0.02 mmol), D (75.2 mg, 0.08 mmol), A (76.7 mg, 0.10 mmol), and Pd(PPh3)4 (4.6 mg, 0.004 mmol) in dry toluene/DMF (5 mL/1 mL) was degassed via three freeze-pump-thaw cycles and refluxed at 120 °C for 24 h under an inert atmosphere. For the synthesis of block copolymer b-PF20, a two-step polymerization was employed. A mixture of DF (25.7 mg, 0.02 mmol), A (15.3 mg, 0.02 mmol), and Pd(PPh3)4 (4.6 mg, 0.004 mmol) in toluene/DMF was initially reacted at 90 °C for 2 h, followed by the addition of D (75.2 mg, 0.08 mmol) and A (61.4 mg, 0.08 mmol), and then the mixture was heated to 120 °C and kept for 24 h. For the random copolymer ra-PF20, a mixture of D (75.2 mg, 0.08 mmol), A (61.4 mg, 0.08 mmol), and Pd(PPh3)4 (4.6 mg, 0.004 mmol) in toluene/DMF was first heated to 120 °C, immediately followed by the dropwise addition of DF (25.7 mg, 0.02 mmol) and A (15.3 mg, 0.02 mmol) over 50 min. After the polymerization was completed, the solution was cooled to room temperature and added dropwise into methanol. The precipitate was collected and purified via Soxhlet extraction using methanol, hexane, acetone, chloroform, and chlorobenzene in sequence. The chlorobenzene fractions were concentrated, reprecipitated into methanol, and dried under vacuum overnight to afford qb-PF20, b-PF20, and ra-PF20 copolymers. GPC: qb-PF20: Mn = 44 kDa, PDI = 2.03; b-PF20: Mn = 47 kDa, PDI = 2.24; ra-PF20: Mn = 45 kDa, PDI = 2.00.

General methods

1H NMR and 19F NMR spectra of monomers were recorded on a Bruker AV-400 (400 MHz) spectrometers with CDCl3 solvent. To study the polymerization kinetics, parallel reactions of PM6 and qb-PF20 were conducted at 80 °C with equal-volume aliquots withdrawn at 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. To further investigate the oligomer distributions, a mixture with a DF:D molar ratio of 1:1 was refluxed at 90 °C for 30 min, and the obtained oligomers were measured by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. The preparative GPC was employed to separate the oligomers using tetrahydrofuran at 30 °C with a flow rate of 2.5 mL/min. The molecular weight and distribution were determined by Shimadzu-20A GPC. TOF-SIMS spectra of samples were analyzed by double-beam analysis mode on TOF-SIMS 5-100. DSC measurements were performed on a PerkinElmer DSC8500 calorimeter using a heating/cooling rate of 10 °C/min. Absorption spectra were recorded on the UV-2700 spectrophotometer. Cyclic voltammetry measurements of the materials were conducted on a CHI660D electrochemical workstation. AFM images of films were detected by a Bruker Multimode 8. GIWAXS characterizations of films were performed at the BL14B1 beamline of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. An in situ UV-Vis spectrometer consists of a light source, detector and spin coating instrument. The detector collected the absorption spectra during the spin-coating of the active layer solution. FLAS spectra were obtained with a spectrometer. In-situ oxygen plasma etching was utilized to extract the depth-dependent absorption spectrum for the active layer.

Device fabrication

The opaque device was fabricated with a conventional structure. The pre-pattern ITO was scrubbed and cleaned sequentially using deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol, and treated by UV-Ozone for 10 min. A thin PEDOT:PSS layer was spin-coated onto the substrates at 5000 rpm for 45 s and baked at 150 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, the 0.5 mg mL−1 self-assembled monolayer Ph-4PACz in i-PrOH:toluene (0.6:0.4) was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 45 s with TA at 110 °C for 5 min. For the BHJ structure prepared by the BC method, the polymer and BTP-eC9 are dissolved in chloroform at a polymer concentration of 7.2 mg/mL with 10 mg/mL 1,3,5-trichlorobenzene (TCB) as a solid additive, and stirred at 60 °C for 4 h. After the active layer is spin-coated, the films were treated by thermal annealing at 100 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, the 0.5 mg/mL PNDIT-F3N methanol/acetic acid (0.995/0.005, v/v) solution was spin-coated. Finally, 100 nm Ag was evaporated and deposited as the anode. For SD processed devices, the copolymers (qb-PF20, b-PF20, and ra-PF20) and BTP-eC9 were separately dissolved in chlorobenzene and chloroform with the concentration of 10.5 mg/mL and 9.5 mg/mL. To obtain the high-quality blend film, 0.5 vol% PFT was added into acceptor solution. In detail, the donor solution was firstly spin-coated, then the film was left in a vacuum for 5 min. Afterward, the BTP-eC9 solution with 10 mg/mL TCB was further spin-coated to acquire the active layer film. As for the FSVA post-treatment, the fabricated blend films were placed onto a stand in an airtight brown jar, and 200 μL PFT was added into the jar, and the brown jar was put onto the hotplate with different annealing temperatures and times. After FSVA treatment, the active layers were placed under vacuum for 5 min to ensure the removal of fluorous solvent residues.

Device characterization

The J–V curves of devices were tested under AM 1.5 G illumination (100 mW cm−2) by a Keithley 2420 Source-Meter together with a 300 W Xe lamp solar simulator (Enlitech SS-F5-3A). The EQE spectra were acquired on an Enlitech EQE system (Enlitech QE-M110) with a monochromator and 75 W Xe lamp and checked with a calibrated Si diode. The details of other measurements related to device dynamic behaviors are shown in the Supplementary Information. Veff equals the applied bias minus the voltage where photocurrent is zero, and Jph represents the current density differences between illumination and dark. At a sufficiently high Veff, it is assumed that all excitons are dissociated into free charges, and the photogenerated current reaches the maximum saturation (Jsat). The Pdiss and Pcoll of the devices were calculated from the Jph/Jsat values under short-circuit conditions and maximum power output conditions, respectively. SCLC measurements were carried out using a hole-only and electron-only device with the configurations of ITO/PEDOT:PSS/Active layer/MoO3/Ag and ITO/ZnO/Active layer/PNDIT-F3N/Ag. Subsequently, the carrier mobilities in active layers were determined by fitting the dark current of hole/electron-only devices to the SCLC model. A digital source meter (Keithley 2400) and a picoammeter (Keithley 6482) were utilized for the EQEEL measurements. The former was applied to inject an electric current into the solar cells to emit the photons, which will be collected using a Si diode and form an electric current that can be measured by the latter.
