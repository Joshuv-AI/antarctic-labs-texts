Efficient removal of congo red and cytotoxicity evaluation of biosorbents prepared from chitosan-added watermelon pulp
Authors: Mehmet Semih Bingöl
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of environmentally friendly adsorbents has become increasingly important for treating waste generated by the growing global industry. In this study, new biosorbents were synthesized from an all-natural chitosan and watermelon pulp for the treatment of congo red textile dye from water. Biosorbents were prepared by lyophilizing chitosan-added watermelon pulp (AC-WPC). The prepared biosorbents were characterized by BET, SEM, Zeta Potential and FT-IR analysews. Accordingly, the BET surface area of AC-WPC was 120.92 m2/g. SEM analysis showed that the structures were porous and the results were consistent with the BET analysis. FT-IR analysis confirmed the chemical structures. The isoelectric points of the biosorbents were determined by zeta potential analysis. The AC-WPC biosorbent demonstrated 86% cell viability with cytotoxicity testing. For this reason, it was determined that the AC-WPC biosorbents produced does not cause any serious damage to the cell. Following this, adsorption study of congo red textile dye in water was carried out with these biosorbents. Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms were studied in adsorption experiments, and it was found that the Freundlich isotherms were compatible. Pseudo first and second kinetic models were also studied and found to be compatible with Pseudo Second Kinetic Model. The highest adsorption capacity was observed at 100 mg/l congo red concentration with 98.02% removal and 490.1 mg/g adsorption capacity using AC-WPC. It is thought that these results will potentially contribute to the literature on the removal of textile dyes or other pollutants using chitosan-added watermelon pulp.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74010-6.Keywords: Biosorbent, Chitosan, Cytotoxicity, Watermelon pulp, AdsorptionSubject terms: Pollution remediation, Chemical engineering

Introduction

The rapid growth of technology and production has led to an increase in the amount of waste released into the environment1. These wastes from industry or commerce are mixed with water and released into the environment2. These wastes released into the environment have a direct or indirect negative impact on human health and the environment3. In particular, dye-containing wastewater from the textile and printing industries is considered to be the primary pollutant. The depletion of oxygen in dyeing effluents significantly affects environmental health due to the inability of photosynthesis4. Congo red, one of these dyes, is an anionic synthetic dye. Congo red (CR) dye [1-naphthalenesulfonic acid, 3.3-(4.4-biphenylene bis(azo)bis(4-amino-) disodium salt] is an anionic bisazo dye based on benzidine. Because congo red is readily soluble in water and does not precipitate, it is not easily removed from wastewater. For this reason, the treatment of congo red is important for the environment5.

Many techniques such as electrochemical oxidation, coagulation, photocatalysis, membrane filtration and adsorption are used in wastewater treatment. These techniques can be summarized into three categories. These are physical, chemical and biological treatment. For example, chemical and biological treatments have significant disadvantages such as the use of expensive chemicals, short lifetimes and difficult waste disposal6. Of these, adsorption is the most preferred method due to its ease of processing and economic feasibility7. Therefore, it is important to develop new adsorbents. The mixing of prepared adsorbents in wastewater is also important for environmental health. It is undesirable for adsorption to produce a toxic effect of adsorbents while removing contaminants in wastewater8. For this reason, it is preferred that adsorbents consist of natural and chemical-free components. Generally, activated carbon, graphene, zeolite, biochar and similar biosorbents are used in the preparation of adsorbents9.

Chitosan is a natural biopolymer of glucosamine and N-acetylglucosamine residues10. Chitosan can be produced in large quantities by deacetylation from chitin, which is the most abundant in nature after cellulose. Chitosan has been reported in many articles to have a high adsorption capacity11. In addition, due to its non-toxicity, chitosan is used in many biomedical, cosmetic, textile and food fields12. Watermelon, a common agricultural product, contains some vitamins, antioxidants, lycopene and amino acids13. Since watermelon pulp can hold a large amount of water, it can be considered as a biosorbent with high adsorption capacity.

Biosorbents are inexpensive due to abundant materials and simple manufacturing methods14. Biosorbents are commonly employed to eliminate hazardous heavy metals, textile dyes, and other contaminants. Adsorption is a potential method of wastewater treatment that employs adsorbents and has benefits such as high adsorption capacity and environmental friendliness15. Adsorption qualities of biosorbents are determined by their structural properties, such as pore distribution, specific surface area, and functional groups. Such alterations can result in biosorbents with high adsorption capacities16. Surface functional group modification outperforms other types of modification. The number of effective adsorption binding sites can be enhanced by introducing specific functional groups into the cell surface, such as amino and carboxyl. As a result, more adsorption capacity can be obtained17.

For these reasons, in this study, the adsorption capacity of biosorbents prepared by adding chitosan to watermelon pulp was increased. No chemicals were used in the preparation of the biosorbents. For the first time in the literature, congo red removal from water was investigated using these biosorbents. In addition, Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET), Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) and Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) analyses were performed to determine the chemical structure and surface properties of these biosorbents. In addition, the toxicity of the biosorbents was evaluated. A general summary of this study is given in Fig. 1. It is expected that the biosorbents prepared with these results will be able to remove many pollutants.

Material methods

Raw materials and chemicals

Chitosan, polyvinyl alcohol, hydrochloric acid (37%), and acetic acid were purchased from Merck. Sodium hydroxide was purchased from Sigma. Watermelons were purchased from local markets in Erzurum, Turkey.

Preparation of biosorbents

Using a cylindrical cup, sections of watermelon pulp (WP) with a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 20 mm were taken. These watermelon sections were then soaked in deionized water for 2 days to remove the sugar, and the water was changed every 3 h. These sections were then removed from the water and dewatered with desiccant paper18. These sections were frozen in the refrigerator at -80 °C for 24 h. These sections were removed from the freezer, lyophilized, and dried completely. Chitosan (0.25 g) was completely dissolved in 250 ml water containing 1% acetic acid for 12 h. Lyophilized WP sections were immersed in this solution for 3 h. The sections were then lyophilized again. These WPCs were incinerated in a muffle furnace at 400 °C for 3 h. These biosorbents were stored in a glove box. 4 different biosorbents were prepared to compare the removal of congo red. These were watermelon pulp (WP), AC-WP after incineration of WP, WPC by adding chitosan to WP, and AC-WPC by incineration of WP with chitosan.

Characterization

In order to understand the chemical bonding of the prepared biosorbents, they were analyzed using the Bruker VERTEX 70v model FT-IR (Fourier transform infrared spectrometer) device in the range of 4000–400 cm−1. In order to determine the isoelectric point of the biosorbents, the zeta potential was measured in the range of 2–12 pH using the Malvern Nano ZS device. SEM analysis was performed using Zeiss Sigma 300 to understand the morphology of the biosorbents. Since surface area is important for adsorption, the specific surface area and pore size of the biosorbents were measured by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. This analysis was performed using Micromeritics 3Flex brand BET instrument with N2 gas.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

A cytotoxicity test was performed to determine whether the prepared AC-WPCs are harmful to human cells if they remain in water after adsorption. Human intraoral fibroblast HGF-1 cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin and 1% L-Glutamine. Cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2. HGF-1 cells grown in cell culture flasks were removed from the adherent surface using Trypsin/EDTA. Total cell number was calculated by trypan blue method. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates in triplicate with 1.5 × 103 cells in each well. AC-WPC samples prepared at concentrations of 125, 100, 75, 50 and 25 µg/ml were applied to the wells in triplicate with a total volume of 100 µl in each well. Positive control wells were treated with 10% DMSO. The wells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. At the end of the incubation period, the medium in the wells was removed and medium containing 10% CVDK-8 solution was added to each well. Cells were incubated in the incubator at 37℃ in the dark for up to 3 h. Cell viability was determined by measuring optical density at 450 nm with an Epoch 2 Microplate Spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Changes in cell viability rate were calculated with reference to control groups19.

*OD = Optical density, absorbance.

Adsorption studies

In this study, the congo red textile dye removal efficiency of 4 different biosorbents was determined. Each experiment was performed in an IKA KS 3000i control model shaker at 200 rpm for a specified time, with 50 ml of congo red solution and 0.01 g of biosorbent. At the end of the adsorption experiments, a Shimadzu UV spectrophotometer model UV-3600 Plus was used to determine the concentration of congo red. Concentrations were determined using an absorbance-concentration graph generated by setting the maximum wavelength to 497 nm. Accordingly, adsorption experiments were first carried out between 2 and 12 to determine the appropriate pH for adsorption studies. Congo red solution at 50 mg/l concentration was used in the experiments to determine the optimum pH and adsorption experiments were carried out for 60 min. Then, for isothermal and kinetic studies, adsorption experiments were performed at concentrations of 10, 25, 50 and 100 mg/l and times of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 20, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min. In addition, Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms were used for isothermal studies and pseudo-first order (PFO) and pseudo-second order(PSO) kinetic models were used for kinetic studies. Details of the isotherm-kinetic experiments and the absorbance/concentration graph are given in the Supporting Information.

Result and discussion

FT-IR analysis

FT-IR analysis was used to better understand the chemical bonding of WP, AC-WP, WPC and AC-WPC biosorbents. The spectra obtained are shown in the Fig. 2; Table 1. As a result, four samples showed similar peaks. It is common to observe similar peaks in samples that are believed to contain C, H, N, and O components. Chitosan-added samples tend to have comparable peaks. Unlike with WP and AC-WP, there is a peak of around 1710 cm−1, which is assumed to be caused by stretching of the C = O ester bonds. The peaks seen in the FT-IR spectrum are thought to be due to pectin, cellulose and lignin20,21.

SEM analysis

SEM images of WP, AC-WP, WPC and AC-WPC are shown in the Fig. 3. Accordingly, it was observed that pores were formed in the structure of WP after combustion. However, when chitosan was added to WP, it was observed that chitosan dispersed in a spherical form. It was understood that larger pores were formed in the structure of incinerated WPC compared to WP.

SEM image of biosorbents. Scale bars are 1 μm.

BET analysis

BET surface area measurements were performed for WP, AC-WP, WPC and AC-WPC. Accordingly, it was observed that the burned samples formed pores and thus increased the surface area. In addition, chitosan-doped WPs are believed to increase the surface area of chitosan as seen in the SEM images. BET analysis plots are provided in the Supporting Information.

Adsorption experiment analysis

Effect of ph and zeta potential analysis

The effect of pH on the congo red removal of the prepared biosorbents was investigated. The effect of pH on adsorption was investigated in the pH range 2–12. As shown in the Fig. 4, it was observed that the adsorption capacities were highest at pH 4 and then remained generally constant up to pH 10. In addition, to determine the surface charges, the changes of zeta potential of WP, AC-WP, WPC and AC-WPC samples were studied according to pH. The results obtained are shown in the figure. The zero points (Pzc) for WP, AC-WP, WPC, and AC-WPC biosorbents were 7.4, 6.8, 8.5, and 8.3, respectively. The pKa for the CR dye is 4.1. Therefore, when the pH of the solution is lower than 4.1, the -NH2 group is protonated and becomes positively charged and when it is higher than 4.1 it becomes negatively charged. When the adsorption solution is lower than pH < pHzpc, the OH and NH2groups are protonated and the surface is positively charged22. For this reason, since the adsorption capacity does not change much up to pH 10, and for economic reasons, the natural pH of the congo red solution was set at 6.5 in the following experiments.

(a) Effect of pH on adsorption capacity, (b) Effect of pH on Zeta potential.

Effect of adsorption time and concentration

Adsorption experiments were conducted with 4 different biosorbents at congo red concentration of 10, 25, 50 and 100 mg/l, and at time intervals of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min. The adsorption capacities and percentage removal rates in these experiments are shown in Fig. 5. According to this, it was observed that adsorption occurred very fast in the first 30 min in all biosorbents and then the adsorption rate decreased. This is due to the filling of the active sites on the biosorbents. The adsorption capacities of the biosorbents were determined as WP, AC-WP, WPC and AC-WPC starting from the lowest. It was observed that the adsorption capacity of chitosan biosorbents was slightly higher than that of WP without additives. This is believed to be due to the fact that the surface charge of chitosan is more cationic. Since congo red is an anionic dye, chitosan adsorbs congo red more easily. In addition, AC-WP and AC-WPC have higher adsorption capacity due to the larger pores of the incinerated biosorbents. The highest adsorption capacity was observed at 100 mg/l congo red concentration with 98.02% removal and 490.1 mg/g adsorption capacity.

Effect of contact time and initial concentration on adsorption capacity (a) WP, (b) AC-WP, (c) WPC, and (d) AC-WPC.

Isotherm studies

The Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm methods were applied in isotherm studies. The Langmuir and Freundlich parameter results obtained for 4 different biosorbents are given in Table 2 and their plots are given in Fig. 6. The R2 values of the biosorbents vary between (0.6846–0.7710) in Langmuir isotherm and (0.9734–0.9972) in Freundlich isotherm. Since the R2 value is above 0.97, the adsorption mechanism is in accordance with the Freundlich isotherm. Freundlich isotherm shows that the surface of the biosorbent is heterogeneous. In addition, the prepared biosorbent was compared with other various adsorbents previously studied in the literature for CR removal (Table 3). Although there were no additives, other than chitosan and watermelon pulp, it was observed that the adsorption capacity of the prepared biosorbent was above average compared to the literature.

Kinetic studies

PFO and PSO kinetic models were applied to the experimentally obtained data to investigate the adsorption control mechanism. The kinetic parameter results and graphs are provided in Table 4; Figs. 7 and 8. The experimental findings show strong correlation with PSO. R2 values found between 0.7667 and 0.9762 for PFO, and between 0.9984 and 0.9999 for PSO. Since the R2 value was above 0.99, it was observed that biosorbents were suitable for the congo red adsorption process using the PSO kinetic model. This suggests that chemical adsorption is likely to be the rate controlling step for these biosorbents.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

The MTT assay is used to assess cell viability. The evaluation of cell viability was performed by accepting the data obtained for the control group as 100% and comparing the other data accordingly. The results obtained are given in Fig. 8. As it can be seen from Fig. 9, 25 µg/mL is the concentration with the highest cell viability with 88.94%. Accordingly, cell viability at 50, 75, 100 and 125 µg/mL was 86.87, 86.26, 85.18 and 85.11%, respectively. As can be seen from the results, cell viability decreased as the concentration of AC-WPC increased. However, the amount of this decrease is insignificant. For this reason, it is thought that AC-WPC biosorbents do not cause serious damage to the cell.

Conclusions

In this study, biosorbents were successfully prepared by adding chitosan to watermelon pulp. These biosorbents, which were obtained for the first time in the literature, were investigated for the adsorption of congo red textile dye from aqueous solutions. The chemical structure of the biosorbent was confirmed by the use of FT-IR analysis. When the morphological properties were examined with an SEM device, it was seen that the porous structures were compatible with the results of the BET analysis. A cytotoxicity test was performed to determine whether the prepared AC-WPC biosorbent is harmful to human cells if it remains in water after the adsorption process and an average of 86% cell viability was observed. For this reason, it was determined that the AC-WPC produced does not cause any serious damage to the cell. Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms were studied in adsorption studies and Freundlich isotherms were found to be compatible. PFO and PSO kinetic models were also studied. The PSO kinetic model was found to be compatible for this study. The highest adsorption capacity was observed at 100 mg/l congo red concentration with 98.02% removal and 490.1 mg/g adsorption capacity with AC-WPC. All these results showed that lyophilized chitosan-coated watermelon pulps can be used with different pollutants in future studies.
