Twelve-month lesion reactivation after initial loading injections of faricimab in neovascular AMD: a comparison between three and four loading injections
Authors: Hee Yong Han, Sang Min Park, Ji Hyun Lee, Chul Gu Kim, Jae Hui Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe incidence and timing of first lesion reactivation following initial loading injections of faricimab for neovascular age-related macular degeneration (AMD) were assessed, and factors related to lesion reactivation were analyzed.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with treatment-naive neovascular AMD who underwent either three or four monthly loading injections of faricimab and completed 12 months of follow-up. The incidence and timing of the first lesion reactivation following the initial loading phase were analyzed and compared between the two treatment groups (three-injection and four-injection groups). In addition, the baseline parameters associated with the risk of lesion reactivation were investigated.ResultsSeventy-seven patients were included in the study (35 in the three-injection group and 42 in the four-injection group). Lesion reactivation was observed in 72.7% of patients (56 patients) at a mean of 5.1 ± 2.3 months after the loading injections. The incidence was 74.3% with a mean time to reactivation of 5.6 ± 2.9 months in the three-injection group; it was 71.4% with a mean of 4.6 ± 1.7 months in the four-injection group. No significant difference was noted in lesion reactivation between the two groups (P = 0.983). A greater baseline central retinal thickness was significantly associated with an increased risk of lesion reactivation (P = 0.010).ConclusionsAdministering four loading injections of faricimab may not offer a clear advantage over three injections in suppressing lesion reactivation. A treat-and-extend regimen may be more appropriate in cases with marked retinal edema than an as-needed regimen to prevent further visual deterioration.

Introduction

Intravitreal administration of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) agents is an effective therapy for treating neovascular age-related macular degeneration (AMD). However, the concentration of the intraocularly administered anti-VEGF decreases over time [1], necessitating continuous injections to maintain therapeutic efficacy. Since the introduction of ranibizumab, efforts have been made to develop agents with long-term efficacy [2].

Faricimab is a recently developed anti-VEGF agent designed to meet this requirement [2–5]. It is a bispecific antibody that simultaneously targets VEGF-A and angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2). Inhibition of angiopoietin-2 promotes Tie-2 activation, thereby leading to anti-inflammatory and anti-permeability effects, which help stabilize blood vessels [6, 7]. In clinical trials, faricimab treatment demonstrated an efficacy comparable to that of aflibercept, despite a lower injection frequency [5, 8]. However, a distinguishing feature of the faricimab treatment regimen used in clinical trials, compared to conventional treatment regimens, is the administration of four monthly loading injections during the initial treatment phase [5].

The treatment regimens currently used worldwide include the treat-and-extend regimen [9], which extends injection intervals after the initial treatment, and the as-needed regimen [10], which administers injections only upon lesion reactivation after the initial treatment. A common feature of both regimens is the implementation of an initial three-loading injection protocol [9, 10]. Therefore, further evaluation is needed to assess the clinical outcomes of administering faricimab with a conventional three-loading-injection approach. In particular, understanding the differences in the clinical course between three and four initial loading injections could be valuable for determining the number of loading injections in clinical practice.

Although monthly follow-ups are generally recommended when using an as-needed treatment regimen, it is often difficult to strictly adhere to this schedule in a real-world setting because of the burden of frequent hospital visits. Indeed, real-world studies have shown that the number of hospital visits is often lower than recommended [11]. If there are differences in the rate or timing of lesion reactivation between three and four loading injections, such findings could inform more efficient follow-up strategies by helping clinicians tailor patient monitoring accordingly.

Recent studies have reported that both the four- and three-loading injection regimens are utilized in real-world practice [12–17]. In neovascular AMD, certain patients do not exhibit lesion reactivation following the initial loading injections [18–20]. The frequency and timing of lesion reactivation may slightly vary depending on the agent used [18]. To date, no studies have reported lesion reactivation after initial loading injections of faricimab.

In the present study, we evaluated the incidence and timing of the first lesion reactivation during the first year after initial loading injections of faricimab for neovascular AMD. In particular, we focused on the differences in lesion reactivation based on the number of loading injections and the factors associated with early lesion reactivation.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was carried out at a single center (Kim’s Eye Hospital, Seoul, South Korea). The study received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB, #2025-03-005) and conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients and treatment

This study involved patients diagnosed with treatment-naive neovascular AMD who received three-to-four consecutive monthly injections of faricimab (Vabysmo®; Roche, Basel, Switzerland; 6.0 mg/0.05 mL) between September 2023 and July 2024. The exclusion criteria were (1) follow-up of < 12 months after the loading injections, (2) extensive macular hemorrhage preventing accurate classification via angiography, (3) presence of subretinal fluid (SRF) or intraretinal fluid (IRF) 1 month after the final loading injection, (4) history of vitrectomy or glaucoma surgery, and (5) initiation of a treat-and-extend regimen immediately after the initial loading. Eyes with previous symptoms were included if both eyes met the inclusion criteria.

All patients underwent best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurement at the time of diagnosis. Additionally, fundus photography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), fluorescein angiography, and indocyanine green angiography (ICGA) were performed. Angiographic images were acquired using the Spectralis system (HRA + OCT®; Heidelberg Engineering).

Initial loading injections were administered three or four times at 1-month intervals. No standardized institutional protocol was in place for determining the number of loading injections, and the choice was based on the treating physician’s personal preference, either by adopting the method used in clinical trials (four injections) [5] or following the conventional approach commonly used in previous anti-VEGF treatments (three injections) [9, 10]. The number of loading injections were generally determined before the initiation of treatment. In some cases, residual retinal fluid persisted after three loading injections and resolved after a fourth injection was administered. One month after the final loading injection, fundus imaging and OCT examinations were repeated.

The patients were monitored every 1–2 months following the initial treatment. Retreatment was administered as needed until the first lesion reactivation. The development of SRF, IRF, or the appearance of macular hemorrhage was considered indicative of lesion reactivation. Reactivation was identified through a review of clinical examination findings, OCT scans, and fundus photographs.

Outcome measures

Patients who received three loading injections were classified into the three-injection group, whereas those who received four loading injections were classified into the four-injection group. Among patients who received four loading injections, those who did not achieve complete fluid resolution after the first three injections were excluded from the study.

The timing of incidence was defined as the period following the last loading injection (i.e., after the third injection in the three-injection group and after the fourth injection in the four-injection group). The incidence and timing of the first lesion reactivation within 12 months following the initial loading injections were assessed.

The following baseline characteristics were compared between the two groups: age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, type of macular neovascularization (MNV), BCVA, central retinal thickness (CRT), SRF, IRF, serous pigment epithelial detachment (PED), and subretinal hyperreflective material (SHRM). Additionally, the incidence and timing of lesion reactivation were compared between the three-injection and the four-injection groups. MNV was classified as type 1 (subretinal pigment epithelial), type 2 (subretinal), type 3 (intraretinal) [21, 22], and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV), based on the OCT and angiography results. All images were analyzed by a single experienced examiner (J.H.K.). In this study, the presence of a serous component in PED (pure serous PED or a combination of serous and fibrovascular PED) was identified and analyzed. Cases of fibrovascular PED alone were not included in the PED analysis.

Multivariate analysis was performed to identify factors related to lesion reactivation following the loading injections. including age, sex, type of MNV, BCVA, CRT, SRF, IRF, PED, SHRM, and number of loading injections. To determine the optimal cut-off value for predicting lesion reactivation, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was performed. The optimal CRT threshold was identified using Youden’s J-statistic.

As an additional analysis, patients who experienced lesion reactivation were categorized into two groups: the early-fluid resolution group (fluid completely resolved after a single faricimab injection) and the late-fluid resolution group (fluid completely resolved after two or more injections). Within each group, the duration until lesion reactivation was compared between the three-injection and four-injection regimens. This analysis included only eyes that underwent monthly OCT examinations.

Baseline BCVA was compared between eyes with and without lesion reactivation, and its association with CRT was assessed.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as count and percentage (%), as applicable. The comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or chi-square test. Binary logistic regression was used for multivariate analysis. Kaplan–Meier analysis, along with the log-rank test, was employed to compare lesion reactivation across different groups. The correlation between the baseline BCVA and CRT was analyzed using Pearson’s correlation analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS for Windows (version 21.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The BCVA was significantly worse in the three-injection group than in the four-injection group (mean logMAR 0.63 ± 0.33 [Snellen equivalent = 20/85] vs. 0.45 ± 0.25 [Snellen equivalent = 20/56]) (P = 0.012). Other characteristics, including age (P = 0.481), sex (P = 0.644), hypertension (P = 1.000), diabetes mellitus (P = 0.085), type of MNV (P = 0.439), CRT (P = 0.240), SRF (P = 1.000), IRF (P = 0.473), serous PED (P = 0.171), and SHRM (P = 0.166) were not different between the two groups.

Lesion reactivation was observed in 72.7% of the patients (56 of 77 patients), at a mean of 5.1 ± 2.3 months following the loading injections. The lesion reactivation was noted at ≤ 3 months in 26.8% (15 patients), > 3 and ≤ 6 months in 48.2% (27 patients), and > 6 months and ≤ 12 months in 25.0% (14 patients).

In the three-injection group, lesion reactivation was observed in 74.3% of patients (26 patients) at a mean of 5.6 ± 2.9 months after the loading injections (Fig. 1). In the four-injection group, lesion reactivation occurred in 71.4% of patients (30 patients) at a mean of 4.6 ± 1.7 months. No significant difference was present in lesion reactivation between the three- and four-injection groups (P = 0.983). Figure 2 shows time-dependent lesion reactivation according to the different types of MNV. In type 1 MNV, the incidence of lesion reactivation was 76.2% (16 of 21 patients) at 4.7 ± 2.3 months. The values were 69.2% (9 of 13 patients) and 5.9 ± 2.1 months in type 2 MNV, 68.8% (11 of 16 patients) and 4.7 ± 1.5 months in type 3 MNV, and 74.1% (20 of 27 patients) and 5.2 ± 2.8 months in PCV.

Monthly OCT examinations during the loading injections were performed in 71 eyes and 53 of them experienced lesion reactivation. In these 53 eyes, 30 eyes (56.6%) (15 in the three-injection group and 15 in the four-injection group) included in the early-fluid resolution group and 23 eyes (43.4%) (8 in the three-injection group and 15 in the four-injection group) were included in the late-fluid resolution group. In the early-fluid resolution group, the lesion reactivation observed at mean 5.7 ± 3.2 months in the three-injections group and 4.4 ± 1.4 months in the four-injections group (P = 0.595). In the late-fluid resolution group, the values were 5.1 ± 2.1 months and 4.8 ± 1.9 months, respectively (P = 0.728).

The baseline BCVA was not significantly different between eyes with lesion reactivation (0.47 ± 0.29) and those without reactivation (0.56 ± 0.30) (P = 0.246). A strong association was observed between baseline BCVA and CRT (P < 0.001, r = 0.515).

Discussion

Faricimab is one of the most prominent long-acting anti-VEGF agents, and Ang-2 inhibition is a key contributor to its mode of action. Ang-2 is overexpressed under hypoxic conditions, and it not only increases vascular permeability but, when suppressed, allows Ang-1 to bind more effectively to the Tie-2 receptor, thereby promoting vascular maturation and stabilization [7]. Based on its mechanism of action and previous study data, Sharma et al. suggested that faricimab may offer a theoretical benefit in controlling neovascular AMD [7]. In addition, owing to its long duration of action, faricimab may be associated with a lower societal burden than other anti-VEGF agents [23].

Recent studies analyzing the outcomes of switching from other anti-VEGF agents to faricimab have demonstrated that switching can lead to an extension of the injection interval. Hikichi et al., reported patients previously treated with ranibizumab or aflibercept experienced a significant extension of the mean injection interval from 9.25 weeks to 11.21 weeks during the first year after switching to faricimab [24]. In a study by Saito et al. evaluating treatment outcomes in eyes with aflibercept-refractory neovascular AMD that were switched to faricimab, the injection interval increased markedly—from a mean of 10.0 weeks during aflibercept treatment to 11.8 weeks after switching [25].

Sim et al. evaluated the treatment outcomes in patients who were switched to faricimab after receiving ranibizumab or aflibercept. The mean injection interval increased significantly from 4.2 weeks prior to the switch to 6.9 weeks afterward. Notably, 42.9% of patients were able to extend their interval to ≥ 8 weeks, and 11.4% achieved an interval of ≥ 12 weeks [26]. Todoroki et al. demonstrated that in eyes where treatment was switched from aflibercept to faricimab, both VEGF-A and Ang-2 were suppressed [27]. This suggests that the anatomical efficacy and longer durability of faricimab may be partly attributable to Ang-2 inhibition.

A recent study demonstrated that aqueous Ang-2 concentrations decreased after faricimab injection [27]. In addition, in previous clinical trials, faricimab demonstrated better efficacy than aflibercept in rapidly controlling retinal fluids. The proportions of patients achieving complete resolution of SRF and IRF during the initial loading injections were 77% at 3 months in the faricimab-treated group, whereas it was 67% in the aflibercept-treated group [5]. Moreover, this good initial response was reproduced in recent real-world studies, which reported dry macula in 79.5–93.3% of patients after initial loading injections [13, 15, 16].

However, lesion reactivation after loading injections of faricimab has not yet been well-elucidated. Findings from the TENAYA/LUCERNE study indicated retinal thickness fluctuations from the initial stages in the 12-week and 16-week injection groups [5]. Additionally, a study by Grimaldi et al. reported that when the injection interval was extended by approximately 7 weeks after loading injections, 47% of patients exhibited recurrence or persistence of fluid after the first extension [16]. Thus, it can be inferred that lesion reactivation may occur in certain patients following loading injections.

In this study, we evaluated the incidence and timing of lesion reactivation following faricimab treatment. Overall, lesion reactivation was noted in 72.7% at a mean of 5.1 months after the loading injections, with the majority occurring within 6 months.

In previous studies using ranibizumab and aflibercept, the incidence of lesion reactivation during the first year ranged from 68.8 to 76.2%, with a timing averaging 3.9–5.3 months [28, 29]. Although the incidence was slightly lower with aflibercept compared to ranibizumab (69.0% vs. 71.4%), and the time to lesion reactivation tended to be longer (mean 4.4 months vs. 3.9 months), these differences were not statistically significant [29].

In this study, the lesion reactivation rate following faricimab loading injections was similar to that previously reported for aflibercept treatment [29]. The time to first reactivation tended to be slightly longer with faricimab than with aflibercept (mean 5.1 months vs. 4.4 months). This result may be partly explained by differences in patient characteristics between the studies as well as the possibility that faricimab has a longer duration of action. Both aflibercept and faricimab showed relatively longer durations of first lesion reactivation than ranibizumab [29], which may reflect the longer duration of action of these agents.

One of the key findings of this study was no significant difference in lesion reactivation between the three- and four-loading injection groups. As both the widely used anti-VEGF treatment regimens—treat-and-extend and as-needed—are based on three initial loading injections [9, 10], the potential impact of the number of loading injections on recurrence rates has not attracted considerable attention from investigators. However, with the introduction of a 4-monthly loading injection protocol for faricimab [5], the need for further research in this area has emerged.

No notable differences in lesion reactivation were observed between the three- and four-injection groups throughout the study period. Moreover, the proportion of relatively early reactivation within 6 months was similar, suggesting that administering four consecutive injections may not offer a clear advantage in suppressing lesion reactivation. However, the baseline BCVA tended to be slightly worse in the three injection groups indicating the need for a more controlled study to allow for more accurate comparisons.

In general, proactive treatments, such as the treat-and-extend regimen, result in better visual outcomes than the as-needed regimen [30]. However, lesion reactivation did not occur after the initial treatment in 18.7–25.2% of cases of neovascular AMD [18, 20]. For such patients, proactively administering injections from the beginning may lead to unnecessary treatments [20]. Therefore, in certain patients, starting with an as-needed regimen and switching to a treat-and-extend approach upon lesion reactivation may be an alternative treatment approach to improve efficiency [31].

However, adopting an efficient approach requires an assessment of the lesion reactivation rate and timing specific to each anti-VEGF agent, which can provide valuable information for patient management. Based on the lesion reactivation rates, clinicians can determine whether to initiate treatment with an as-needed approach or adopt a proactive approach from the initial treatment phase. Additionally, when using the as-needed regimen, frequent follow-ups when lesion reactivation commonly occurs may help detect recurrence in a timely manner.

In the present study, the majority of lesion reactivations occurred within 6 months after loading injections of faricimab, emphasizing the importance of frequent patient follow-up during this period when using an as-needed regimen. In addition, a greater baseline CRT was associated with an increased risk of lesion reactivation. A thicker CRT may indicate a more advanced disease stage or a relatively high hydrostatic pressure due to exudation from the MNV. Considering these findings, in cases of significant retinal edema, a proactive approach-such as treat-and-extend regimen-may be more appropriate than an as-needed regimen to prevent further visual deterioration.

Recently, in pursuit of more efficient treatment strategies, approaches such as extending the injection interval directly to 12 or 16 weeks after the initial treatment [5, 32], or more aggressively extending the interval by 4 weeks at a time [33], even within a treat-and-extend regimen, have been introduced. To successfully implement an efficient treatment while minimizing the risk of vision loss due to lesion reactivation, further studies are needed to analyze the risk and timing of lesion reactivation in greater detail.

In our study, the baseline BCVA of the three-injection group was significantly lower than that of the four-injection group. Although eyes that experienced lesion reactivation showed relatively lower baseline BCVA compared with those without reactivation, BCVA was not identified as a significant factor in the multivariate analysis. In contrast, CRT emerged as a significant variable associated with lesion reactivation. The correlation analysis demonstrated a strong association between BCVA and CRT, suggesting that this interrelationship may have been adjusted for in the multivariate model. Consequently, CRT remained a significant predictor, whereas BCVA did not demonstrate an independent association with lesion reactivation. Therefore, the difference in baseline BCVA between the three- and four-injection groups is unlikely to have exerted a meaningful influence on the study outcomes.

The strength of this study is that it is the first to report the incidence and timing of lesion reactivation following initial loading injections of faricimab and to confirm that no difference was present in lesion reactivation based on the number of loading injections. However, this study had certain limitations. First, the study was conducted retrospectively, and no specific criteria were established to determine the number of loading injections. Four loading injections represent a relatively recent regimen introduced with the advent of faricimab, and no established criteria currently exist to guide the choice between three versus four loading injections. In our study, the number of loading injections was determined at the discretion of the treating physician in most cases. Therefore, the lack of standardized guidelines may have influenced the study outcomes to some extent, and this limitation cannot be entirely excluded.

Second, as it was a retrospective study, strict monthly follow-up was not performed, and the intervals between follow-up visits varied among patients. As a result, subtle changes in fluid status between visits could not be evaluated. In a few patients, lesion reactivation may have been detected with delay, potentially leading to an overestimation of the time to lesion reactivation. Furthermore, small amounts of fluid that may have developed and spontaneously resolved between visits could not be identified.

Third, fibrovascular PED was not included in PED-related analyses. Fourth, the proportion of PCV in our patients was lower than that in previous reports based on Asian populations [34], while the proportions of type 2 and type 3 MNV were slightly higher. This study exclusively included patients treated with faricimab; those treated with other anti-VEGF agents, such as aflibercept, were excluded. There were no predefined criteria for the selection of anti-VEGF agents, and treatment decisions were made at the discretion of the treating physician. Therefore, selection bias may have contributed to the observed distribution of MNV subtypes. Finally, all patients were Korean.

In summary, following the initial loading injections of faricimab for neovascular AMD, lesion reactivation was observed in 72.7% of cases at a mean of 5.1 months after treatment. No difference was observed in lesion reactivation according to the number of loading injections. However, greater baseline CRT was associated with an increased risk of lesion reactivation. These findings should be considered while planning effective faricimab treatment strategies. Future investigations should adopt more rigorous study designs and predefined criteria to guide the number of loading injections.
