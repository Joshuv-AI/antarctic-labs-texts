Inaccuracy of Ureteroscopic Biopsy in Upper Tract Urothelial Carcinoma Can Lead to False Therapeutic Decisions: A Case Report
Authors: Ayoub Hidayat‐Allah, Frank Friedersdorff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDo not rely solely on negative endoscopic biopsies when diagnosing upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC). Repeat ureteroscopy with biopsy if initial results are negative to reduce false negatives. Frozen section analysis (FSA) can provide rapid intraoperative confirmation and guide treatment decisions. Emerging tools such as fluorescence confocal microscopy may improve diagnostic accuracy and warrant further study.Keywords: biopsy, Boari flap, nephroureterctomy, upper tract urothelial carcinoma, ureterorenoscopy

Introduction

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) is a malignancy of the urothelial lining from the renal calyces and pelvis to the ureteral orifice [1]. Its global incidence ranges from 1.2 to 4.7 per 100,000 annually [2]. UTUC is a malignancy of the urothelial lining from the renal calyces and pelvis to the ureteral orifice [3, 4]. Hydronephrosis occurs in 37%–80% of cases and may indicate advanced disease [5].

Diagnosis remains challenging. Even with computed tomography urography (CTU), urinary cytology, and ureteroscopic biopsy (URS), results may be inconclusive, with false negatives in up to 15% of high‐grade tumors, leading to underestimation or misclassification [6]. This diagnostic uncertainty complicates appropriate treatment planning.

The European Association of Urology (EAU) and American Urological Association (AUA) recommend URS with biopsy and cytologic washing when imaging or cytology alone is insufficient. For treatment, both guidelines suggest tumor ablation for low‐risk UTUC and radical nephroureterectomy or segmental ureterectomy for surgically fit patients with high‐risk disease [7, 8].

Despite these guidelines, UTUC is frequently underestimated preoperatively. Biopsy limitations can delay definitive treatment, particularly in elderly or high‐risk patients, worsening outcomes.

Case History

An 84‐year‐old male was referred for evaluation of incidentally detected asymptomatic grade II right hydronephrosis found on routine imaging. He reported no uremic symptoms (nausea, vomiting, fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, or cognitive changes) and had good functional status (ECOG 0–1, ASA II). He was pain‐free and had no history of urolithiasis or schistosomiasis.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

CTU showed right‐sided hydronephrosis and bladder wall thickening without ureterolithiasis, suggesting either bladder tumor or UTUC. GFR was mildly reduced (69 mL/min/1.73 m [2]), blood counts were normal. Cystoscopy, retrograde pyelography, and ipsilateral URS were performed. No bladder tumor was seen, but a distal ureteral narrowing with a white sessile lesion was identified. Five biopsies were taken and a stent placed; selective cytology was not obtained. Histology reviewed by two pathologists showed only Brunnian nests and fibrosis, excluding malignancy, and the stenosis was considered inflammatory.

2 months later, the patient developed renal colic with grade‐3 hydronephrosis and a GFR drop to 39 mL/min/1.73 m [2]. An earlier stent removal attempt had failed, and urgent re‐stenting was required. CT showed improved hydronephrosis but persistent bladder wall thickening (Figure 1). Because of recurrent obstruction and failed stent removal, open ureterocystoneostomy was performed. The distal ureter was resected, reimplanted, and a new stent placed. Recovery was uneventful.

Coronal view of a contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT scan showing reduced hydronephrosis on the right side after placement of a double‐J (DJ) stent. The stent is visible in the right ureter. The bladder wall thickening on the right side remains unchanged, indicating the need for further evaluation.

Final histology of the resected specimen revealed high‐grade papillary urothelial carcinoma with periureteric fat invasion (pT3), lymphatic invasion (L1), and negative vascular, perineural and nodal involvement (V0, Pn0, R1). Mitomycin was instilled postoperatively.

This final diagnosis sharply contradicted the initial negative URS biopsy, underscoring the known limitations and undergrading risk of ureteroscopic sampling. Earlier repeat biopsy or frozen section might have enabled timely detection and altered management. Such discrepancies between ureteroscopic biopsy and final pathology are well documented, with tumor upgrading a known issue, emphasizing the risk of underdiagnosis.

Conclusion and Results

Postoperative CT (Figure 2) showed persistent grade II right hydronephrosis despite stenting. The ureter inserted atypically ventrally after resection, and there was diffuse thickening of the right dorsolateral bladder wall with extravesical soft tissue extending toward the distal ureter, suspicious for residual or recurrent tumor. No thoracic, abdominal organ, or nodal metastases were detected.

Coronal view of a contrast‐enhanced abdominal CT scan showing persistent grade II hydronephrosis on the right side, despite the presence of a double‐J (DJ) stent. There is also diffuse thickening of the right bladder wall and soft tissue around the distal ureter, raising suspicion of residual or recurrent tumor.

Discussion

UTUC is a rare but aggressive malignancy, representing 5% of urothelial carcinomas and 10% of renal tumors, with a steadily increasing incidence [9]. At diagnosis, about 60% of cases are already invasive [10]. The global incidence ranges from 1.2–4.7 per 100,000 person‐years, with approximately 2 per 100,000 annually in Western countries [2]. Men are affected three times more often than women [4, 11]. Major risk factors include smoking, arsenic exposure, Balkan nephropathy, and hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC) [12].

Imaging Modalities

Diagnosing UTUC remains challenging. Historically, ureteropyelography and cytology were used, but today CTU and URS with biopsy are considered the most effective combination, providing detailed visualization and histologic information [13].

The sensitivity of cytology and biopsy alone is 64% and 74%, respectively, while combining both improves sensitivity to 84% and accuracy to 68%. However, 15% of high‐grade tumors may still be misclassified as low‐grade [6].

In a study by Abouelkheir et al., CT/CTU demonstrated 97% accuracy and high specificity, though negative CT results cannot fully exclude UTUC, especially in high‐risk patients [14]. CT performs best for distal ureteral tumors but may miss calyceal lesions. When CT is contraindicated, the EAU suggests magnetic resonance urography or 18F‐FDG PET/CT, though the recommendation is weak [7]. PET/CT shows 82% sensitivity and 84% specificity for detecting lymph node metastases, with positive nodes predicting worse recurrence‐free survival [15, 16].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior soft tissue contrast and better assessment of muscle invasion, which is key for risk stratification. Messina et al. reported mpMRI accuracy of 85% for detecting muscle‐layer invasion, with 95% sensitivity and 71% specificity [17].

Thus, UTUC diagnosis should integrate multiple tools—primarily CTU, urinary cytology, and diagnostic URS—to maximize accuracy [4].

Endoscopic Tools

URS plays a crucial role in diagnosing UTUC by enabling direct visualization and tissue sampling. The procedure typically involves four steps: (1) selective urine cytology, (2) retrograde pyelography, (3) thorough inspection of the upper tract, and (4) lesion biopsy [18].

However, biopsy accuracy for grading and staging is limited [18, 19, 20]. Studies show significant discrepancies between URS biopsy findings and final nephroureterectomy pathology. Rojas et al. reported a 92.6% concordance in tumor grade but only 43% in stage [21]. The narrow ureteral lumen restricts instrumentation, often yielding suboptimal samples [13, 22]. While endoscopic biopsies are highly specific, false‐negative rates remain notable, leading to underestimation of UTUC and increased mortality [23, 24].

Overall, URS has an 88% diagnostic success rate, with positive biopsies in 74% of cases, though some cancers are only detected after repeat URS or nephroureterectomy [25].

In our case, false‐negative biopsies resulted in inappropriate management. Ideally, a repeat URS or larger tissue sampling—such as using a stainless‐steel basket—might have improved diagnostic accuracy. Although intravesical recurrence post‐URS is a concern, studies suggest that it does not significantly affect survival [26, 27]. However, the literature does not provide clear guidance on the importance of repeating URS biopsies or the optimal timing for doing so.

False‐negative biopsies can have broad implications. Clinically, they delay or misdirect treatment, allowing disease progression and narrowing curative options. Economically, they increase costs through repeated procedures and advanced disease management. For patients, they create emotional distress, additional invasive interventions, and reduced quality of life.

Molecular Diagnostics

Performing frozen section analysis (FSA) could have provided immediate intraoperative diagnosis, but its accuracy for ureteral tissue is limited. Laukhtina et al. reported sensitivity of 69%–77% and specificity of 83%–96% for ureteral margins, with even lower reliability in UTUC due to small ureteral size and thermal artifacts [28].

Fluorescence confocal microscopy (FCM) may offer a more reliable alternative by producing rapid digital images of fresh tissue while preserving samples for histopathology and immunohistochemistry [29].

A potential complication of intraoperative manipulation is tumor seeding, particularly with high‐grade tumors [30]. Existing literature suggests that tumor seeding is primarily a concern with percutaneous resection, laparoscopic and robotic interventions [26, 31]. The immunodepression determined by pneumoperitoneum and imperfect surgery can together explain the occurrence of peritoneal carcinomatosis related to tumor seeding exclusively with laparoscopy and robotic surgery [32].

When pursuing a kidney‐sparing approach with intraoperative frozen section analysis (FSA), the risk of tumor seeding increases. Preventing this requires removing specimens intact with negative margins and clipping the ureter before excision to avoid cancer cell spread [32]. However, this can be difficult when the procedure aims to mobilize the ureter for reconstruction, such as in Boari flap ureterocystoneostomy. Despite these challenges, FSA remains valuable, especially when initial ureteroscopic biopsies are inconclusive.

Other alternative diagnostic tools can play a significant role in cases like this. Among them, Fluorescence in Situ Hybridization (FISH) is particularly important in the diagnosis of urothelial carcinoma, especially UTUC. FISH is especially useful when urine cytology results are inconclusive, as it offers higher sensitivity and specificity in detecting malignancy [33].

In our case, a positive FISH test could have significantly influenced the therapeutic strategy, leading to earlier diagnosis and treatment planning. The effectiveness of FISH as a diagnostic tool is well supported by research.

A meta‐analysis of 13 studies (1,067 participants) showed FISH has high specificity (95%) and good sensitivity (72%) for UTUC detection [34]. In contrast, urine cytology alone has a high false‐negative rate (51.1%) and limited sensitivity, especially for low‐grade tumors [35]. Another review of 25 studies confirmed that FISH generally outperforms cytology, though variability remains and neither test is completely reliable [36]. Despite their benefits, these tests are not routinely used due to cost, limited availability, and lack of strong guideline support [4].

Liquid biopsy is an emerging, noninvasive approach using circulating tumor cells, DNA, RNA, or extracellular vesicles for diagnosis and prognosis. Although still experimental, early studies suggest potential for predicting recurrence and monitoring disease in UTUC [37, 38].

To reduce diagnostic delays and mismanagement, newer modalities should be considered, including FSA with FCM, advanced imaging such as 18F‐FDG PET/CT and mpMRI, and noninvasive tests like FISH and liquid biopsy (Table 2). Further research is needed to establish their clinical utility.
