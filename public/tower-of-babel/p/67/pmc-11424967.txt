Introduction and importance

The first reference to possible pulmonary tuberculosis was in 1643 when the autopsy of Louis XIII showed ulcerative intestinal lesions associated with a large lung cavity [1]. As the years went by and the use of effective medications against Mycobacterium tuberculosis, in developed countries the pattern of presentation changed to a greater prevalence of extrapulmonary forms that included abdominal tuberculosis.

Approximately 1 to 3% of total cases are extrapulmonary, of which 11 to 16% are abdominal tuberculosis, which occurs due to the reactivation of latent peritoneal tuberculous focus, established by lymphatic or contiguous hematogenous dissemination of a primary focus. In Tamaulipas, Mexico in 2023 was reported 1322 pulmonary cases and 116 other forms cases, without specific origin.

A progressive increase in the number of cases of peritoneal tuberculosis has been observed, finding around 9.27 million in 2007, compared to 9.24 million in 2006, being reported mainly in Asia and Africa, affecting patients between 35 and 45 years of age [2]. There are multiples risk factors for this disease, such as HIV infection, low socioeconomic status, immunosuppression, alcoholism, drug addiction, as well as chromic kidney failure, peritoneal dialysis, and cirrhosis.

The mechanisms by which intestinal tuberculosis originates are through the dissemination of a focus of primary infection, which can spread hematogenously from an active pulmonary focus, swallowing infected sputum from patients with active pulmonary tuberculosis, ingestion of contaminated food, contamination of adjacent organs.

Intestinal tuberculosis is one of the regional manifestations of tuberculosis that is generally transmitted by the lymphatic route through lung infection, with mesenteric lymph nodes in the omentum and peripancreas. Peritoneal tuberculosis accounts for only 1 to 2% of all cases of peritonitis, affecting the digestive system in sites such as the peritoneum, esophagus, stomach, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecal appendix, rectal anus, and mesenteric lymph nodes, mainly [3].

Historically known as “the great mimicker,” intestinal tuberculosis can present resembling a neoplastic, infectious or inflammatory intestinal disease [4]. In 70% of patients there is fever, in 60% weight loss and abdominal pain with abdominal distention and in 15% diarrhea, although the most relevant finding is ascites.

It can present in 3 forms: a) moist, which presents with free ascites; b) fibrocaseous, resulting from ascitic ascites with the formation of tumors due to lax adhesions of intestinal loops, and c) fibroadhesive or dry, in which there is the presence of abdominal masses composed of mesentery and thickened peritoneum.

For diagnosis, computed tomography is the most sensitive technique for the assessment of peritoneal pathology. Using this technique, usually uniform peritoneal thickening with enhancement is observed after the administration of parietal peritoneal contrast. It is accompanied by mesenteric and peripancreatic lymphadenopathy in 90% of cases [5]. Chest x-ray shows pulmonary involvement in 25% of cases. For this reason, the absence of radiological findings does not rule out a possible extrapulmonary infection.

The typical macroscopic finding is the presence of whitish nodules that resemble “millet grains” approximately 5mm in diameter scattered over the peritoneum and viscera [6]. The classic histopathological findings are the chronic granulomatous inflammatory reaction made up of activated epithelioid macrophages, surrounded by a collar of lymphocytes, plasma cells and variable degrees of fibrosis, presence of multinucleated Langhans-type giant cells and central caseous necrosis. Special stains, such as Ziehl-Neelsen, can identify the presence of tubercle bacillus, but this finding is rare [7].

The mechanism that causes the elevation of CA 125 is unknown; it is considered a very useful marker of therapeutic response. Once the definitive diagnosis has been confirmed, CA 125 should be used to monitor the response to treatment, since, in patients with peritoneal tuberculosis, a rapid decrease in CA 125 is parallel to the clinical response and resolution of symptoms. Ascites after antituberculosis treatment [8].

Adenosine deaminase (ADA) is increased in the fluid of ascites of tuberculous origin, which has been proposed as a useful marker, with a sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 97%, using cut-off values of 36 to 40 IU/L, being the optimal 39UI/L.

Treatment is carried out with anti-tuberculous therapy, is the same as for patients who present with pulmonary involvement, the regimen to follow is isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol for 6 to 9months [9]. Some authors suggest prolonging the treatment time up to 9 to 12 months, especially in patients with HIV without antiretroviral treatment [10].

Presentation of the case

We present the case of a 33-year-old man with no significant history, who denies drug addiction.

The patient presented to the emergency department at our hospital with 10 days of abdominal pain. The patient pain increased during the 3 days prior to presentation was predominantly localized to the right iliac fossa. In the hours prior to surgical consultation the patient's pain became intolerable and generalized. Orally, it is accompanied by nausea and vomiting.

Physical examination revealed a distended abdomen, no peristalsis, muscular resistance to palpation, and a positive rebound sign. In laboratory studies, only the elevation of leukocytes was found to be altered.

An ultrasound is requested which reports: At the level of the right iliac fossa, a hypoechoic ovoid image measuring 36 × 10 mm is observed, associated with positive rebound, which may correspond to an appendiceal process.

It was decided to transfer him to the operating room, the findings reported: phase 4 appendix with presence of plastron, presence of unquantified pus, perforation of distal ileum 2cm from the ileocecal valve, ascending colon resection + ileostomy was performed.

The patient remained hospitalized for 5 days due to slow progression of tolerance to the oral route. At the time of discharge, he left with adequate tolerance and with a functional ileostomy.

Intestinal restitution is scheduled approximately after 3 months after follow-up by outpatient clinic and adequate evolution.

During surgery, the findings are reported: abundant loop adhesions to the abdominal wall and interase, presence of a granuloma in the wall with the presence of approximately 5 ml with mucinous-looking fluid, presence of multiple small tumors that look like peritoneal implants at the level of the flank. Right, small intestine and mesentery, presence of plastron at the level of the hepatic flexure with the presence of fat necrosis.

Reason why it was decided to only take biopsies and the procedure was terminated. Tumor markers are requested for suspected malignant disease, viral panel and peritoneal fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA).

Biopsy results are received reporting: peritoneum and mesentery biopsies with chronic non-specific inflammation, peritoneal implants negative for neoplastic cells with fibrosis and chronic non-specific inflammation.

Lab results:

Negative for human immunodeficiency virus and hepatitis B and C viruses.

CA 125: 73.20 U/ml (ref: 0.0‐35.0)

ADA: 16.5 U/L (serum ref.: 0.0‐15).

With the previous results, an epidemiological consultation was requested for probable peritoneal tuberculosis, who indicated treatment with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol for 6 months. Due to adequate evolution, he was discharged by the surgery service and continued with epidemiological follow-up in her first level clinic.

Subsequently, at the consultation, the patient comes at 7 months reporting having already completed the medical treatment, preoperative laboratories and colon by enema are requested (Fig. 1) and intestinal restitution is scheduled.

Preoperative colonic enema showing absent ascending colon (post-surgical change).

In the findings of the intestinal restitution surgery, there are multiple loops adhesions of small intestine, presence of multiple caseous granulomas and firm adhesions to these thin loops and another one at the level of the hepatic flexure with firm adhesions of loops. Adherenciolisis and side-to-side ileó-transverse anastomosis are performed. No drains are left, and intraperitoneal fluid is sent to ADA, as well as a biopsy of granulomas.

He subsequently went to the floor where on the second postoperative day he began to present with tachycardia, fever and signs of peritoneal irritation, so he was taken to the surgery room again due to a probable anastomosis leak.

During surgery, the findings are reported: presence of 500cc of free intestinal fluid, presence of an unnoticed injury in the ileum of 0.5cm, without specifying the distance. The complete anastomosis is visualized and there is no evidence of leakage, a Penrose drain is placed, and it is transferred to the floor.

Subsequently, an ADA report comes out with a value of 122 U/L.

After the first postoperative day, the patient began to have an intestinal appearance due to the drainage of approximately 100 ml (Fig. 2). As the patient did not demonstrate changes in his vital signs or his standard blood laboratory values, this drainage was managed as a controlled fistula; the patient was placed on an oral diet and octreotide was administrated. The fistula output decreased slowly, however, the fistula drainage finally resolved completely. Epidemiology was notified again of the ADA result and subsequently, while questioning the patient, they commented that he did not have adequate follow-up and control by epidemiology, so the anti-tuberculous therapy was started again.

Colostomy bag with Penrose drainage, with intestinal-like liquid output.

Subsequently, a pathology report of the granulomas is provided that mentions:

Adipose tissues with fibrosis, caseous necrosis and dense infiltrate of inflammatory cells of the lymphocytic type (Fig. 3), granulomas with epithelioid cells, multinucleated giant cells of the Langhans type and foreign body type (Fig. 4).

Presence of adipose tissue with fibrosis and caseous necrosis.

Presence of multinucleated Langhans-type giant cells.

Follow-up is provided through the outpatient clinic with adequate monitoring and controls of CA 125: 9.5 U/ml and after 0 U/ml, reflecting an adequate response to medical treatment reason for which he was discharge from our service.

Discussion

According to the literature, patients between 35 and 45 years of age are mostly affected. Our patient is 33 years old. Among the risk factors, the only one present was having a low socioeconomic level, since drug addiction and the result of the viral panel were denied. It was negative, we do not know the initial origin of the disease, but it could have been due to the ingestion of contaminated food since in the imaging studies that were taken, no pulmonary alteration was ever observed, in addition to the fact that the patient never presented pulmonary symptoms. The most common symptoms are the presence of fever, weight loss and abdominal pain, our patient did not have any of these symptoms. At the time of diagnosis, our patient was on surgery, we identified and sampled multiple granulomas during the patient's abdominal surgery, which is why a malignant disease was initially suspected and the diagnosis was confirmed. Diagnosis of tuberculosis based on the results of the ADA, CA 125 and finally the histopathological findings. When a case is suspected it must be notify to epidemiology and they begin the treatment, we notify them until we had the histopathology report. Adequate follow-up was not carried out by our service and epidemiology, the follow is at first level clinics, when the patient came to us, we didn't confirm the patient may had finished the treatment. The only thing reported is follow-up using CA 125 values, which were not requested in the postoperative period, and the patient reported that he did not have adequate adherence to the medical treatment against tuberculosis, so when the intestinal restitution was performed again, the presence of granulomas was still found. In our health system is the most cases are followed by different surgeons and the low resources, difficult an adequate follow up.

Conclusion

Peritoneal tuberculosis with a silent presentation is very rare. Peritoneal tuberculosis should always be kept in mind within the differential diagnosis when unexpected findings are discovered during abdominal surgery, and always defined by laboratory studies and, more importantly, histopathology. It is important to have adequate and close monitoring of the treatment of the patient to see that there is an adequate response to medical management.
