High mobility group box 1: DAMPening the danger molecule in cardiovascular disease with exercise
Authors: Yu-Chieh Liao, Jorming Goh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) is a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP). During cellular stress, it leaves the nucleus and moves into the extracellular space, where it modulates the development of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), a leading global cause of age-related mortality. While evidence indicates that HMGB1 is associated with both the progression and severity of CVDs, it also has a paradoxically beneficial role in mitigating tissue repair. Exercise training improves cardiovascular function and modulates systemic concentrations of HMGB1. Acute exercise induces the release of HMGB1 into systemic concentration, whereas long-term exercise training appears to reduce its systemic levels. This paradoxical response of HMGB1 to either short-term or chronic exercise, alongside its complex role in the pathogenesis of age-associated CVDs, makes it an intriguing subject for research. A potential explanation for this paradox may lie in HMGB1’s capacity in regulating stem cell recruitment and tissue regeneration. This review explores the intricate interactions between HMGB1, exercise and CVDs, and discusses its potential both as a therapeutic target and a novel biomarker for tracking inflammatory signaling during exercise.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) present a significant and growing challenge with the global aging populations and are responsible for ∼19.7 million deaths in 2022, accounting for nearly one-third of all global deaths.1 The comorbidities and health complications associated with CVDs further augment healthcare costs and burden. Consequently, there is a critical need for cost-effective, non-pharmacological interventions to mitigate the risk and progression of CVDs. Exercise training has emerged as a highly effective intervention, not only improving cardiovascular function but also modulating systemic inflammation and tissue remodeling—two key processes in CVD pathophysiology. To better understand how exercise confers these protective effects, recent research has focused on the molecular mediators involved in these adaptations. One molecule that has gained attention in this context is high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), a protein involved in inflammation and tissue repair.

In preclinical models, administration of HMGB1-neutralizing antibodies increased the survival rates of lipopolysaccharide-treated mice by up to 30%, whereas treatment with recombinant HMGB1 was lethal.2 Furthermore, chronological aging is accompanied by a gradual increase in systemic HMGB1. Compared with young adults (18-30 years, n = 19), older adults (≥70 years, n = 18) have ∼ 25% higher serum HMGB1 concentrations.3 A longitudinal study also revealed an age-related increase in plasma HMGB1 from 3.5 ± 1.8 to 4.7 ± 1.5 ng/mL as participants aged from 24.6 ± 3.3 to 30.4 ± 3.4 years,4 suggesting that HMGB1 may reflect age-related inflammatory burden and contribute to the increased cardiovascular risk seen in older populations.

This review explores the interplay between the role of HMGB1 in CVD pathophysiology and its modulation through exercise training. We aim to provide an overview of the current understanding of how exercise influences HMGB1 signaling and activity and to speculate on the mechanisms by which these interactions may contribute to cardiovascular health and disease management.

High mobility group box 1 is a damage-associated molecular pattern

Damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are endogenous molecules rapidly released from injured or dying cells under stress, inducing a potent immune response to address cellular damage.5 HMGB1 is a highly conserved DAMP, about 25-30 kDa in molecular weight and comprised of 215 amino acids. Under homeostatic conditions, HMGB1 is constitutively expressed and resides in the nucleus, where it binds to chromatin and regulates transcription.6 Under cellular stress, particularly oxidative stress, HMGB1 translocates from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and extracellular spaces. Within the nucleus, it acts as a transcription factor, while extracellularly, it serves as a pro-inflammatory mediator.6, 7

HMGB1 can be released by various cell types. The potential cellular sources of HMGB1 include immune cells (macrophages, monocytes, dendritic cells),8, 9 endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells10 and skeletal muscle.11 Wang and colleagues first reported that significant amounts of HMGB1 are released from cultured endotoxin-stimulated macrophages.2 In vivo evidence further showed that HMGB1 is systemically released into the circulation following endotoxin administration, at concentrations comparable to the increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β).2

Oxidative stress is a major cellular stress that alters the redox state of HMGB1, thereby modulating its immune functions. This is determined by three redox-sensitive cysteines: Cys23, Cys45, and Cys106. The redox state of these cysteines determines its nucleocytoplasmic location, extracellular activities, and receptor binding.6 Under normal conditions, nuclear HMGB1 is fully reduced (frHMGB1).12 The mutation of Cys106 in HMGB1 disrupts HMGB1 nuclear localization, indicating the importance of this redox-sensitive residue in nucleocytoplasmic shuttling.12 As an extracellular inflammatory mediator, frHMGB1 acts as a chemoattractant to recruit innate immune cells (such as neutrophils, macrophage, and T cells) and stem cells to the damaged sites, thereby promoting tissue regeneration.13, 14, 15, 16 In contrast, the oxidized disulfide form of HMGB1 (dsHMGB1) induces the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines from the same types of cells that release HMGB1, contributing to host defense during infection or other forms of tissue damage.13, 16, 17

High mobility group box 1 and cardiovascular disease

Emerging evidence has highlighted HMGB1 as a major player in the development and progression of CVDs. As a key mediator of inflammation, HMGB1 drives the pathogenesis and progression of cardiovascular conditions by promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine signaling, while the mechanisms driving tissue damage remain elusive.18

Animal models

In vivo evidence has demonstrated elevated HMGB1 expression in CVDs, suggesting a potential role for HMGB1 as a risk indicator of CVDs. In one preclinical study, the mRNA and protein expression of HMGB1 were significantly elevated in left ventricles as early as 30 min following myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury in mice, with this elevation persisting for 7 days (both mRNA and protein ∼3-fold vs sham-treated mice).19 Kohno et al provided supporting evidence in a rat myocardial infarction (MI) model, showing that both mRNA and protein expression of HMGB1 in the infarcted area increased by day 3 (∼4- and ∼0.5-fold, respectively), peaked at day 7 (∼5.4- and ∼2-fold, respectively), and remained elevated through day 14 (∼3- and ∼0.5-fold, respectively) compared with sham-operated controls.20 In contrast, HMGB1 mRNA in non-infarcted myocardial tissue remained stable over the study period.20 Treatment with recombinant HMGB1 aggravated I/R injury in mice, leading to increased infarction size, impaired left ventricular function, and elevated expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, which are implicated in the pathogenesis of cardiac I/R injury.19

Conversely, mice treated with HMGB1 box A, an antagonist of extracellular HMGB1 cytokine activity, underwent I/R injury and exhibited reduced infarct sizes (21.9% ± 8.1% vs 60.5% ± 8.2%), as well as improved recovery of cardiac function (42.0% ± 1.1% vs 29.0% ± 1.1%) compared to untreated I/R-injured mice.19 Additionally, a 7-day subcutaneous injection of anti-HMGB1 antibody (10 mg/kg/day) in male rats following MI reduced MI-induced mRNA expression of TNF-α and IL-1β (∼50% and ∼25%, respectively), as well as macrophage infiltration (∼14%) in the infarcted area on day 3, compared to untreated MI rats.20 HMGB1 blockade also enhanced infarcted left ventricular wall remodeling in the infarcted region.20 Intraperitoneal injection of anti-HMGB1 antibody (2 mg/kg/day) for 2 weeks in rats with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) suppressed circulating concentrations of TNF-α by ∼50%, reduced leukocytes infiltration in the inflamed tissues by ∼40%, improved pulmonary vascular remodeling (∼40%) and increased the survival rates (∼75%) of PAH rats.21 These findings suggest that, beyond reducing circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines concentrations and leukocytes infiltration at injury sites, HMGB1 neutralization effectively attenuates pathological tissue remodeling, halts diseases progression, and ultimately improves survival rates in preclinical models.20

Human evidence

Observational studies in humans support a pathological role for HMGB1 in CVDs, where elevated systemic concentrations of HMGB1 are associated with various CVD phenotypes, reflect disease severity, and predict adverse clinical outcomes. In a longitudinal study of healthy young individuals (aged 24.6 ± 3.3 years), plasma HMGB1 concentrations were positively correlated with obesity, inflammatory markers (IL-6 and TNF-α), and other cardiovascular parameters, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure and carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity.4 In patients with MI, HMGB1 concentrations rose sharply following the ischemic event. Patients with MI exhibited higher circulating HMGB1 concentrations compared to healthy age-matched controls. At 24 h post-MI, HMGB1 concentrations increased by ∼158-fold (159 ± 54.3 ng/mL vs 1.94 ± 2.05 ng/mL)22 and ∼1.2-fold (20.2 ± 5.8 ng/mL vs ∼9 ng/mL).23 Elevated concentrations persisted at 72 h post-MI, with a ∼2.7-fold increase observed (14.8 ± 6.8 ng/mL vs ∼3 ng/mL).24

In patients with ST-elevation MI (STEMI), serum HMGB1 concentrations were markedly elevated on admission compared to those with chronic stable angina (CSA) (∼4-fold, 4.5 ± 5.3 ng/mL), peaked at 12 h post-MI (∼18-fold, ∼18 ng/mL), and remained elevated on day 7 (∼3-fold, 3.3 ± 5.1 ng/mL).20 Further supporting its prognostic value, HMGB1 concentrations increased with diseased severity, with the highest value observed in MI patients with heart failure (∼1.2-fold, 20.2 ± 5.8 ng/mL), followed by MI patients without heart failure (16.5 ± 5.7 ng/mL) and heart failure patients without MI (11.8 ± 2.8 ng/mL).23

Elevated circulating HMGB1 has also been linked to poor clinical outcomes. Peak circulating HMGB1 were reported observed in STEMI patients with pump failure, cardiac rupture, and in-hospital cardiac death.20 Additionally, in MI patients, higher circulating HMGB1 is also correlated with impaired cardiac function, with higher left ventricular end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) (β = 0.267) and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (β = −0.312) observed.24 Furthermore, in both STEMI patients and non-ST-elevation MI (NSTEMI) patients, serum HMGB1 concentrations were positively correlated with infarct size (STEMI: r2 = 0.81 vs NSTEMI: r2 = 0.74) and residual ejection fraction (STEMI: r2 = 0.40 vs NSTEMI: r2 = 0.25).25

Beyond MI, HMGB1 is implicated in the pathology of coronary artery disease (CAD). Patients with CAD exhibited approximately 1.1-fold higher serum HMGB1 concentrations (∼4.2 ng/mL) compared to individuals without CAD (∼2 ng/mL).26 In patients undergoing coronary angiography, circulating HMGB1 was positively correlated with the severity of coronary stenosis (Gensini score) (r = 0.710).27 Serum HMGB1 concentrations also increased with plaque complexity (r = 0.34), with the highest values detected in patients with remodeled plaque (∼10 ng/mL).28

These findings collectively suggest that systemic HMGB1 concentrations may serve as a surrogate marker and predictor of CVDs, highlighting HMGB1’s essential role in the pro-inflammatory pathways of these diseases. Further investigations are necessary to delineate the underlying mechanisms, as well as the responsiveness of HMGB1 to various pharmacological treatments. As well, it is important to understand whether these diseases and HMGB1 correlate with other cardiac biomarkers or risk factors and to clarify the potential mechanism driving these cardiac tissue-damaging pathways.

Cellular sources of high mobility group box 1 in cardiovascular diseases

Most proteins are secreted via the Golgi apparatus and endoplasmic reticulum.29 As HMGB1 does not have the signal sequence necessary for the classical transport pathway, it relies on non-classical secretion mechanisms to reach the extracellular space.30 Under homeostatic conditions, HMGB1 is primarily localized in the nucleus. Immunohistochemical analyses showed that HMGB1+ signals predominantly reside in the nucleus of myocytes and epithelial and vascular cells from rat myocardium and lungs in the sham-operated rats.20, 21

Under cellular stress, HMGB1 can be actively secreted from inflammatory cells and passively released from necrotic cells.31 Various cell types, including endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and immune cells, contribute to HMGB1 release in CVDs.32 Evidence of this includes strong expression of HMGB1+ signals on infiltrating leukocytes in the I/R injury rat myocardium.19 In human studies, there is also co-localization of HMGB1 with smooth muscle cells in carotid endarterectomy tissues from patients,33 and with CD68+ macrophages in atherosclerotic lesions from autopsy samples.34

The translocation of HMGB1 under cellular stress has been studied, while the underlying mechanisms of its release into extracellular space continue to be elucidated. In a rat model of monocrotaline-induced PAH, nuclear HMGB1+ signals were translocated to the cytoplasm of periarterial infiltrating cells, alveolar macrophages, and bronchial epithelial cells.21 Kohno et al provided evidence of HMGB1 release in a MI rat model: i) at 24 h after MI induction, HMGB1 translocated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm of damaged cardiomyocytes and extracellular spaces in the infarcted area, with HMGB1+ signals also appearing in infiltrating inflammatory cells; ii) HMGB1+ signals were highly expressed in different cells over time (e.g., infiltrating inflammatory cells on day 3, and fibroblasts on day 14); iii) the timing of HMGB1 expression varied across different cell types during phases of damage, repair, and resolution; and iv) given that myocardial HMGB1 mRNA and protein levels peaked one day post-MI and remained elevated for 14 days, the persistence of HMGB1+ signals in inflammatory cells, but their rapid disappearance from myocytes, suggests that the early rise in systemic HMGB1 originated primarily from necrotic myocytes, while the sustained increase was driven by inflammatory cells.20

Ex vivo evidence further shows that recombinant HMGB1 stimulates pro-inflammatory cytokine release in human monocytes, but not in lymphocytes.35 Upon release, HMGB1 plays a crucial role in inflammation by acting on various pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), which can, in turn, further influence HMGB1 release and function.32, 36 In addition to monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells also release pro-inflammatory cytokines.37 HMGB1 stimulates several key immune responses: it promotes the maturation of dendritic cells, the proliferation of naïve T cells, the polarization of Th1 cells, the migration of monocytes, and the activation of neutrophils.32

Under cellular stress, HMGB1 acts as an early mediator of inflammation, primarily by interacting with cell surface receptors like toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and the RAGE. These interactions initiate inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of CVD, including atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, hypertension, and cardiomyopathy.18 The HMGB1 signaling pathway in inducing inflammatory heart disease is characterized by complex interactions. Generally, HMGB1 binding to TLR4 activates the MyD88-dependent pathway, leading to the activation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and subsequent production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.37 Additionally, the HMGB1/TLR4/MD-2 complex has also been implicated in the development of dilated cardiomyopathy.38 Similarly, HMGB1 interaction with receptor for advanced glycation end-products triggers the activation of MAPK pathways, which also contribute to downstream inflammatory responses.39 Further studies have shown that it is mainly the dsHMGB1 that is involved in these processes.13, 17 Furthermore, HMGB1 can form complexes with other molecules, such as CXCL12, to amplify its inflammatory effects by binding to the CXCR4 receptor. As the studies above indicate, HMGB1’s involvement in inflammation and CVD is marked by an intricate network of receptor interactions and signaling cascades, and divergent signaling is dictated by its redox state.7, 18, 36 Despite significant progress in linking HMGB1 to the pathogenesis of CVD, direct human evidence that pinpoints the specific cellular sources and redox-mediated mechanisms of HMGB1 in disease progression is still limited and warrants further research.

High mobility group box 1 and exercise

High mobility group box 1 responds to acute exercise as a damage-associated molecular pattern in human studies

During the progression of CVDs, pathological conditions such as ischemia cause inflammation by inducing cellular injury, which leads to the release of DAMPs. These DAMPs activate immune cells and stimulate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, ultimately resulting in further myocardial tissue damage.41 Interestingly, a single bout of strenuous exercise can mimic certain physiological features of hypoxic trauma. It induces mechanical alterations (characterized by mechanical strain in skeletal muscle tissue) and metabolic stress.42 Metabolic stress refers to a physiological state in which the body’s energy production and utilization are disrupted. For example, during exercise, increased energy demand leads to the accumulation of metabolites like reactive oxygen species (ROS) within muscle cells and challenges oxygen delivery to actively contracting skeletal muscles.42, 43 This may cause transient skeletal muscle damage and trigger the subsequent release of DAMPs, including HMGB1, that in turn activates inflammatory pathways.44

Several studies have demonstrated that acute exercise elevates circulating HMGB1 concentrations (Table 1). For example, a single session of incremental treadmill running in athletes led to an immediate ∼2.3-fold increase in plasma HMGB1, which returned to baseline within 30 min post-exercise.45 Similarly, in recreational marathon runners, serum HMGB1 increased in an intensity-dependent manner, with a ∼140% increase after completing a full marathon and a ∼60% increase after a half-marathon.46 Concurrent exercise (combination of aerobic and resistance exercise) also resulted in a 50% to 70% immediate increase in circulating HMGB1 in healthy young men, and these elevations were positively correlated Collectively, the transient elevation of systemic HMGB1 following acute bouts of exercise likely reflects a physiological response to mechanical and metabolic stress, and the elevated resting HMGB1 concentrations in individuals with CVDs may reflect a heightened inflammatory state and impaired adaptive capacity. This distinction underscores the context-specific behavior of HMGB1—functioning as a beneficial stress-responsive signal in healthy individuals but reflecting reduced cardiorespiratory fitness and a heightened inflammatory state in clinical populations.

High mobility group box 1 responds to long-term exercise training in animal models

Long-term exercise training is an effective healthcare strategy for reducing systemic inflammation.48 In murine models, in vivo transcriptomics and single-cell analysis revealed that five weeks of exercise training (voluntary wheel running) reversed age-associated cellular signaling pathways across nearly all cell types in skeletal muscle,49 suggesting that long-term exercise training rejuvenates cellular interactions in aging skeletal muscle.

High mobility group box 1 responds to long-term exercise training in human studies

In human studies (Table 3), similar trends have been observed. MI patients experienced a ∼58% reduction in circulating HMGB1 after 6 months of moderate-intensity cycling (30 min at 60%-70% VO₂peak, three times per week), with concentrations also lower than those in the untrained control group (∼43% reduction).53 This decline in HMGB1 was strongly associated with improvements in cardiopulmonary function, including reduction in LVEDV and wall motion score and increases in VO2peak and HR recovery.53 In breast cancer patients, one year of aerobic training (30 min of cycling or treadmill running at 75% VO₂peak, 3 times per week for 3 months followed by 1 time per week for 9 months) led to an ∼11% reduction in circulating HMGB1.54 As well, Gmiat et al reported that 12 weeks of regular physical activity (3 times a week, walking for 1 h at 60%-70% maximal effort) decreased serum HMGB1 by approximately 30% in healthy older women, supporting the concept that even low-intensity, consistent physical activity may attenuate exercise-induced DAMPs levels such However, not all exercise interventions result in a significant reduction in systemic HMGB1. In obese, physically inactive individuals, 3 weeks of high-intensity intermittent exercise training did not significantly reduce circulating HMGB1 (∼24 vs ∼28 ng/mL), although a downward trend was observed. This reduction only reached statistical significance in insulin-resistant individuals (∼22 vs ∼19 ng/mL, P < 0.05).56 While both obesity and insulin resistance are associated with elevated baseline inflammation, the greater degree of metabolic dysfunction in insulin-resistant individuals may make them more responsive to the HMGB1-lowering effects of the immunomodulatory adaptations induced by exercise training. These findings suggest that reductions in HMGB1 may depend not only on inflammatory status but also on the underlying metabolic condition of the individual.

Cellular sources of high mobility group box 1 in response to exercise

While some studies have demonstrated the effects of exercise-induced changes in systemic HMGB1, few have identified the cellular sources of HMGB1 following exercise. Evidence indicates that recombinant HMGB1 triggers pro-inflammatory cytokine release in human monocytes, but not in lymphocytes.35 In addition, HMGB1 knockdown in macrophages markedly suppressed activation of the downstream TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway and reduced the production of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, TNF-α.57 These findings suggest that monocytes and macrophages could be the primary sources of HMGB1 after exercise, whereas NK cells, B cells, and T cells may not contribute to exercise-induced HMGB1 expression.47

Notably, the cardioprotective effects of exercise training are partly mediated through the enhancement of mitochondrial homeostasis58 and upregulation of branched-chain amino acid catabolism.59 Recent in vitro evidence showed that replenishing branched-chain amino acid in HMGB1-deficient, RAW261.7 macrophages increased extracellular HMGB1 concentration and pro-inflammatory markers (IL-1β, iNOS, TNF-α) via the mitochondrial-to-nuclear signaling pathway.57 The activated macrophages involved generation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β and the activation of TLR4/NF-κB pathway pathways. This suggests a potential mechanism by which exercise training may mitigate HMGB1 levels and cardiovascular risk: not only through systemic inflammatory effects but also by modulating amino acid metabolism and HMGB1 signaling in immune cells.

The role of high mobility group box 1 in cardiovascular disease and exercise

Stem cell recruitment

Exercise training is a constructive intervention for improving cardiovascular function and modulating systemic inflammation. However, given the dichotomy of acute exercise and long-term exercise training on HMGB1, the association with CVDs remains unclear. HMGB1 appears to have an important role in promoting stem cell recruitment and tissue regeneration.60 A role for HMGB1 in stem cell mobilization has been reported, wherein HMGB1 knockout mice exhibited impaired skeletal muscle regeneration following toxin-induced injury.61 In the same study, leukocyte-derived HMGB1 was required for the activation of satellite cells and vascularization in murine skeletal muscle.61

In the context of cardiac repair, assessing cardiac remodeling 4 weeks after MI in mice injected with frHMGB1 reveals enhanced neoangiogenesis and a reduction in infarcted areas and fibrosis in murine heart sections.62 Notably, HMGB1 also regulates the multipotency and self-renewal of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells at the transcriptional level.63 Ex vivo evidence suggests that HMGB1-treated human CD34+ circulating progenitor cells exhibit increased cell viability, as indicated by higher concentrations of ATP and mitochondrial DNA contents.64 Administration of HMGB1 (200 ng) in the peri-infarcted left ventricle of MI mice improved cardiac stem cell proliferation and differentiation, leading to enhanced myocardial cell regeneration in the infarcted heart and the restoration of normal heart function.65 Ex vivo evidence suggested that cardiac fibroblasts from CVD patients treated with HMGB1-conditioned medium (10 ng/mL) enhanced the release of growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines (such as VEGF, IFN-γ, IL-10, IL-1β, TNF-α, etc.) and induced cardiac stem cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation into endothelial cells.66 Unexpectedly, this response was diminished with a higher dose of HMGB1 (100 ng/mL), suggesting a hormetic response to HMGB1 treatment. Additionally, transcriptomic analysis of HMGB1-treated (200 ng) murine hearts 3 days following MI showed increased expression of genes related to tissue regeneration, particularly in the peri-infarct area.67 These genes were involved in endothelial cell proliferation and invasion, as well as stem cell differentiation and muscle cell formation.67

Interestingly, beyond its regenerative properties, HMGB1 also appears essential for maintaining cardiac physiology. Cardiac HMGB1 knockout mice exhibit impaired heart growth, left ventricular dysfunction, and significant decreases in body mass. This suggests a role for HMGB1 in maintaining normal cardiac development and overall cardiac health in mice.68 In adult mice, deletion of HMGB1 in cardiomyocytes led to the upregulation of 187 genes primarily associated with inflammatory processes and the downregulation of 119 genes mainly related to muscle cell contraction or growth.68 This finding challenges the conventional view of HMGB1 solely as a pro-inflammatory protein, suggesting a more nuanced role in cardiac biology.

Given HMGB1’s role in stem cell dynamics and tissue regeneration, exploring how external mechanical stimuli, such as exercise, modulate HMGB1-mediated pathways may offer deeper insights into improving rehabilitative outcomes in CVDs.

Exercise and inflammation

Systemic inflammation following acute exercise supports the clearing of unhealthy cells in damaged muscle tissues, thereby promoting muscle regeneration.69 Acute bouts of intense exercise redirect blood flow toward contracting skeletal muscles43 and transiently mobilize hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells into peripheral blood circulation in humans.70 A meta-analysis indicated that circulating CD34+ progenitor cells in healthy young men predominantly increase following intense exercise, such as prolonged endurance exercise, resistance exercise, and maximal aerobic exercise.71 However, submaximal treadmill exercise (60 min at 70% V̇O2peak) also elevates circulating progenitor cells, peaking at 20 min into the exercise session in healthy young men, then gradually returning to pre-exercise concentrations 60 min post-exercise.72 Moreover, significant increases in CD34+ cells were observed in human skeletal muscle 24 h after resistance and high-intensity exercise.73, 74 In a murine study of 3 weeks of voluntary wheel running, both control and exercised groups had tibialis anterior muscles injured by a toxin, with wheel running improving muscle regeneration.49 Under physical stress, immune cells are redistributed throughout the body, as shown in vivo, where labeled immune cells increased in skeletal muscle cells but decreased in the lungs following injury.75 An in vitro study also demonstrated that neutrophils migrate to the sites of damage induced by laser pulses.76 These findings suggest that exercise facilitates the redistributing of immune resources and the homing and expansion of bone marrow stem cells into injured tissues.

In addition, ROS drive the transition of stem cells from a quiescent to a proliferative state, thereby initiating myeloid differentiation.77 In response to exercise, ROS are primarily generated by NADPH oxidase in immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages.78 These ROS support exercise-induced inflammation by facilitating the clearance of cellular debris and promoting tissue repair. However, long-term exercise modulates immune-related ROS production and simultaneously improving antioxidant capacity.79 Changes in HMGB1 redox status induced by oxidative stress regulate immune function. Despite this, the cellular sources of HMGB1 and the effects of long-term exercise on circulating HMGB1 remain unclear; evidence report reduced systemic HMGB1 in physically inactive obese individuals with insulin resistance compared with those with normal insulin responses.56 This significance is thought to be linked with elevated baseline inflammation and oxidative stress.80 Given that HMGB1 function is determined by its redox state, it is tempting to speculate that HMGB1 may serve as a molecular bridge coordinating the systemic immune response and localized tissue repair processes in both skeletal and cardiac muscle (Figure 3).Fig. 3Predicted model of HMGB1 response to exercise-induced inflammation. Cardiopulmonary functions

The cardiopulmonary system delivers oxygen to tissues and removes carbon dioxide produced during tissue metabolism.81 CVDs compromise cardiac output and vascular integrity, leading to reduced blood flow to the lungs and other organs, which in turn diminishes oxygen delivery to tissues. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that a high resting heart rate (HR) and low maximal oxygen consumption (VO2max) are associated with an increased risk of CVDs.82, 83 However, individuals with higher maximal aerobic capacity exhibited significantly lower rates (∼50%) of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events.83

Regular exercise induces physiological adaptations (e.g., cardiac remodeling, enhanced vasodilation and tissue perfusion) contributing to improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness and reducing CVDs risk and mortality.84, 85 A systemic review and meta-analysis revealed that cardiac rehabilitation (aerobic exercise or combined aerobic and resistance exercise) increased VO2peak (+1.93 mL/kg/min) and decreased resting HR (−5.68 bpm) in patients who had undergone coronary artery bypass grafting.86

Cardiac remodeling also signifies the progression to conditions such as congestive heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, and reduced cardiac output 87 Interestingly, in clinical populations, elevated baseline HMGB1 concentrations may reflect impaired physiological fitness. In MI patients, higher resting HMGB1 concentrations were associated with reduced cardiorespiratory fitness, as indicated by lower peak maximal oxygen consumption (VO₂peak = 14.4 ± 4.2 mL/kg/min; β = −0.276) and higher ventilatory equivalents for carbon dioxide (VE/VCO₂ slope; β = 0.244), higher LVEDV (β = 0.267), and higher LVEF (β = 0.312) during ramp cardiopulmonary exercise testing at 3 days post-MI (all P < 0.05).24 These evidence suggest that HMGB1, as a pro-inflammatory mediator, not only engages in the pathogenesis of CVDs but is associated with cardiopulmonary functions. Despite the well-established benefits of exercise in the prevention and treatment of CVDs, it remains unclear how HMGB1, as one of the DAMPs, contributes to a pro-inflammatory state in the heart, influences tissue remodeling, and affects cardiac efficiency in oxygen delivery, thereby impacting overall cardiovascular and pulmonary health. Understanding HMGB1's role across all stages of CVDs and its interaction with exercise is crucial for expanding preventive strategies and improving cardiovascular health and aging. However, significant knowledge gaps persist in understanding how HMGB1 specifically influences the relationship between exercise and CVDs.

In summary, the causal link between exercise-induced HMGB1 and improved cardiovascular outcomes may lie in its role as a hormetic molecule. While chronic elevation of HMGB1 contributes to CVD pathogenesis, transient increase triggered by acute exercise may serve as a physiological stimulus for cardiac repair and stem cell mobilization. Preclinical evidence further demonstrates that administration of HMGB1 recruits progenitor cells, mirroring the effects of exercise. Together with the antioxidant adaptations induced by long-term exercise training, these mechanisms may collectively facilitate the shift of HMGB1 from its pro-inflammatory disulfide form to its regenerative reduced form. This redox reprogramming provides a direct insight through which exercise converts a danger signal into a molecular bridge for localized tissue repair.

Conclusion

As a DAMP, HMGB1 plays a dual role in CVD pathology, both contributing to disease progression and facilitating tissue repair. While HMGB1 is implicated in the development of CVDs, emerging evidence also highlights its involvement in the repair and regeneration of damaged cardiovascular tissues. Exercise training rejuvenates stem cells and enhances tissue regeneration in animal models. Of note, HMGB1 exhibits distinct responses to acute versus long-term exercise. The interplay between HMGB1 and progenitor cell mobilization during various exercise regimens underscores a complex regulatory network. Despite these insights, the mechanisms by which HMGB1 influences intercellular and intracellular processes in the context of CVD and exercise training remain largely unclear and are exciting avenues of research. Key questions include identifying the cellular sources of HMGB1 under stress and determining the redox isoforms that mediate regenerative versus pro-inflammatory effects. Clarifying the cross-talk between skeletal muscle–derived HMGB1 and cardiac remodeling is also critical for optimizing rehabilitative strategies in clinical populations. Addressing these questions may reveal novel therapeutic targets to mitigate CVD and enhance human health and longevity.
