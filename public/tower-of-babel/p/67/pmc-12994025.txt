Protocol to annotate dendritic cell maturation types in vivo making use of lipid nanoparticle-based approaches
Authors: Karo Van Lil, Janne Swinnen, Greta R. Webb, Sofie Rennen, Victor Bosteels, Sandra Maréchal, Clint De Nolf, Jessica Vetters, Jiří Březina, Evelien Van De Velde, Farzaneh Fayazpour, Rein Verbeke, Ine Lentacker, Sophie Janssens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe current panels used to annotate mature dendritic cells (DCs) by flow cytometry do not suffice to deconvolute homeostatic from immunogenic mature DCs. Here, we present a protocol to classify DCs along a homeostatic/immunogenic spectrum making use of flow cytometry and/or transcriptional profiling. We describe steps to steer DC maturation in vivo using a lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based approach. We detail procedures to formulate, characterize, and generate these LNPs and outline their application in mice.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Rennen et al.1

Before you begin

The context in which dendritic cells (DCs) perceive an antigen determines whether they will undergo homeostatic or immunogenic maturation.2 This will affect how antigens are being presented to naïve T cells and whether T cells are instructed for tolerance or immunity against cells expressing this antigen. By using different formulations of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), widely used as antigen delivery vehicles in vaccination,3 DC maturation pathways can be steered in vivo.1,4 This creates a powerful tool to control how LNP-encoded antigens will be recognized by DCs. We used this approach to compare homeostatic and immunogenic DC maturation profiles and to design tools that can distinguish the two maturation states.1 We generated both a DC maturation flow cytometry panel and a transcriptional profiling strategy. Both tools allow researchers to classify mature DCs along a homeostatic–immunogenic spectrum based on conserved surface marker expression and on transcriptional signatures, respectively.

The following protocol outlines the complete workflow for formulating and characterizing different types of LNPs (Figure 1). They can be produced carrying different types of antigenic cargo - encoded as mRNA or as peptide and adjuvanted with or without TLR ligands. To monitor uptake, LNPs can be labeled by different approaches to either reveal the fluorescent cargo or one of the labeled lipid components. We include steps for characterization of the LNPs to ensure quality control and to calibrate and normalize the different LNP batches according to the cholesterol content or to the mRNA content (depending on the type of LNPs formulated). This ensures equal injection of similar amounts of LNPs across different experiments which is needed for proper comparison of the results.Figure 1Schematic overview lipid nanoparticle (LNP) workflowCreated in https://BioRender.com.

In a second step, we describe how LNPs can be injected in mice via different administration routes, either intravenously, intramuscularly or directly into the (subcutaneous) tumor. We detail methods to quantify LNP uptake by antigen-presenting cells making use of a fluorescent label covalently attached to one of the LNP lipid components.

Finally, we provide methods to immunophenotype the DC compartment and evaluate DC maturation states across different subsets, induced by uptake of LNPs. To this end, we developed a dedicated flow cytometry panel that enables robust discrimination between homeostatic and immunogenic mature DCs. Furthermore, we provide a list of genes that can be used to distinguish different DC maturation states at the transcriptional level. To support the broader research community, we also created a web-based tool that allows users to assess whether a maturation signature (a set of genes specifically induced in the mature stage (MAT ON))5 is a preferential signature of homeostatic or immunogenic mature DCs. An accompanying interactive application enables researchers to upload their own gene lists related to DC maturation, independent of tissue origin, treatment condition, or DC subset.

Innovation

Currently, the major markers used to annotate mature DCs by flow cytometry (like CCR7 or MHCII) do not suffice to deconvolute homeostatic from immunogenic mature DCs. Still, having appropriate markers to assign these different maturation states is crucial as different DC maturation states will have a different immunological outcome. While immunogenic mature DCs instruct naïve T cells to drive immunity, homeostatic mature DCs do not and may contribute to the induction of tolerance, although this remains to be formally demonstrated.2,6,7,8

With the tools currently proposed in this protocol, researchers will be able to discriminate the two DC maturation states across different organs, subsets and organisms. As such, they could be used to monitor the success of anti-tumor therapies and read out whether tumor antigens are being recognized in an immunogenic manner by DCs. They also have implications for the LNP field as our flow cytometry panel and transcriptional profiling strategy provides a tool to assess and compare the immunogenicity of different LNP formulation.

In sum, the toolbox we generated in Rennen et al., Cell Rep, 2025,1 and described in detail in this protocol to profile mature DC states will be a powerful instrument to characterize how antigenic cargo is being perceived by DCs, which previously remained hidden due to the lack of appropriate markers.

Considerations before the experiment

Before initiating the experiment, several key considerations should be addressed. First, the production and characterization of mRNA–lipid nanoparticles (mRNA-LNPs) require careful selection of the cargo, which may include empty LNPs without cargo, peptides, mRNA, or Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists used as adjuvants. In parallel, appropriate strategies to monitor LNP uptake should be defined, such as fluorescent labeling of the lipid components or labeling of the peptide cargo. The method for LNP production should also be established in advance, with the use of an Automated T-junction mixing device to ensure reproducibility and consistency across formulations.

In addition, the route of administration must be selected based on the desired biological readout. Intravenous administration allows analysis of immune responses in the spleen, intratumoral delivery enables readouts in the tumor and tumor-draining lymph nodes, and intramuscular injection facilitates assessment of responses in the corresponding draining lymph node(s).

Finally, the approach for annotating the dendritic cell (DC) maturation state should be planned beforehand. This includes the design of a suitable flow cytometry panel, quantitative PCR analysis of mRNA obtained from fluorescence-assisted cell sorting (FACS)-sorted DC populations, and the implementation of automated annotation methods based on the expression of maturation-specific genes.

Murine tumor models

Timing: 12 days

Timing: 12 days

You can use LNPs to steer the DC maturation state of tumor-infiltrating DCs in a tumor after intratumoral injection. The murine tumor model used in this protocol is established by subcutaneous injection of MC38 murine colon adenocarcinoma cells into the flank but other cell lines such as B16F10 melanoma cells or MCA205 cells can be used as well.9,10,11

MC38 cells are cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) culture media. Cells should be cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2 incubator) for at least 4–7 days after thawing in an adherent cell culture flask with filter cap, before being collected for engraftment. The culture medium should be replaced every 2–3 days, and cells should be subcultured when they reach 80–90% confluency. LNP formulation and characterization

Timing: 6–8 days

Timing: 6–8 days

To formulate LNPs, we describe an automated method using a T-junction mixing device. Alternatively, you can use manual mixing methods to formulate LNPs,12 although these formulate generally less homogeneous particles than (micro)fluidic methods.13 We also outline the formulation of different LNP types, including empty LNPs (eLNPs) and LNPs loaded with various cargos such as TLR agonists (e.g., polyI:C (pIC)), peptides, or mRNA. TLR agonists were purchased commercially, while mRNA was synthesized in-house. The peptides we used were generated by the Peptide and Tetramer Facility Immunology, Leiden University Medical Center. For tracking purposes, LNPs can be labeled in different ways—either by labeling the lipids or by coupling a fluorochrome to the encapsulated peptide. In addition, some dyes are incorporated in a non-covalent manner during LNP formulation, like DiD.14 In Table 1, the LNPs were labeled with a Cy5 fluorochrome covalently linked to 1,2-dioleoylphosphatidylethanolamine (DOPE). Fluorophore polarity, fatty acid tail length and saturation might impact the stable integration of the fluorophore within the LNP and should therefore be carefully matched.14 Alternatively, you can include a fluorescent label directly in the peptide sequence (see the section synthesis of peptide). Synthesis of a peptide

Rather than being encoded as mRNA, antigen cargo can be encoded as protein or as peptide as well. We used (amongst others) the OVA257-265 peptide SIINFEKL which is the ovalbumin-derived peptide that is presented on MHC-I to OVA-specific CD8 T cells.1 Its incorporation in LNPs allows us to monitor T-cell activation, proliferation, and cytotoxicity of transgenic OT-I T cells that can be adoptively transferred. We coupled the peptide to a E10 tag (a linker with 10 glutamic acids) which ensures proper incorporation in the positively charged LNPs during formulation.12 We used either an unlabeled or a labelled version of the peptide, where the latter one was covalently coupled to the fluorochrome AF488. All peptides were generated by the Peptide and Tetramer Facility Immunology at the Leiden University Medical Center. Alternatively, also full proteins can be incorporated into LNPs.20 However, we noted that most of the commercially available preparations of recombinant proteins do contain some endotoxin contaminants, which influences the outcome on DC maturation (see also further in the section on expected outcomes).18.Prepare a stock solution of the peptide at the desired concentration by dissolving the peptide powder in MiliQ water. Timing: 1 h on the day prior to the readout experiment

We provide a dedicated flow cytometry panel that enables robust discrimination between homeostatic and immunogenic mature DCs and that can be used to identify other innate and adaptive immune cells, such as T cells, B cells, macrophages, neutrophils and monocytes. This panel is generated on the BD FACSymphony™ A3/A5 but can also be run on any conventional or spectral flow cytometer that meets the required configuration. Some adaptions may be needed.

Because many fluorochromes have overlapping emission spectra, fluorescence spillover from a primary detector into secondary detectors commonly occurs for certain fluorochrome combinations. This overlap must be corrected through a process known as compensation. Accurate compensation is essential for correctly distinguishing positive from negative signals and therefore requires careful optimization. The protocol described here employs both compensation beads and single-stained cells as controls for this purpose. Beads are a useful alternative when (positive) cell numbers are limited or when the positive population contains autofluorescent cells compared with the negative population. This step outlines the procedure for mouse dissection and organ collection after LNP injection.Note: Both the organs harvested and the timing of collection vary according to the administration route (Table 10). When injecting intravenously, we take along the spleen as it harbors both a peripheral tissue like site (the red pulp) and a lymphoid like structure (the white pulp). This implies that within one and the same organ all DC maturation stages are present. For the tumor, we assess the effects on DCs both within the tumor microenvironment and in the tumor-draining lymph node, as mature DCs migrate to this site to interact with T cells. For intramuscular injection, we isolate the corresponding draining lymph nodes. As showed below, when injecting into the right quadriceps muscle of the mouse; the right iliac and inguinal lymph node needs to be collected as the draining lymph node. When injecting intravenously or intratumorally, we normally take along two timepoints: 2 h after injection to look at the uptake of LNPs and 8 h after injection to look at the effect on DC maturation. Expected outcomes

Monitoring DC uptake via flow cytometry

In this protocol, we demonstrate how adjuvanted versus non-adjuvanted LNPs can be used to steer DC maturation in vivo. We focus on the contribution of the cargo as major determinant in the maturation phenotype, but we and many others3,14,35,36,37,38 have noticed that any variable, like injection route, tracking method, type of phospholipid or ionizable lipid used, will affect the uptake, endosomal release, transfection efficiency and immunogenicity. Therefore, one should take care to keep these parameters as uniform as possible between different LNP batches.

Three different routes of administration were compared. All major antigen-presenting cells internalize LNPs, although the degree of uptake differs between different cell types. Following intravenous injection, we consistently observe LNP uptake across neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, cDC1s, and cDC2s in the spleen. Uptake is generally higher in cDC1s than in cDC2s (Figures 6A and 6B). When using Cy5-DOPE as a tracking method (fluorochrome covalently linked to the phospholipid), overall signal usually declines after approximately 8–12 h, consistent with degradation of the fluorochrome. This implies that the intensity of the CTV signal does not only reflect the extent of LNP uptake, but also the stability of the fluorochrome, especially when reading out at later time points. This is why we monitor LNP uptake usually at 2h after injection, before any degradation occurs. Both the LNP tracking method used and the organ analyzed will affect the LNP signal (see below). Finally, uptake of TLR ligand-coupled LNPs is typically higher than uptake of eLNPs, probably due to activation of the DC39 (Figures 6A and 6B).Figure 6Comparison of LNP uptake across immune cell populations following different routes of administration(A and B) Engulfment of Cy5-labeled LNPs in splenic immune cell populations 2 and 8h h after intravenous injection.(C) Scheme of MC38 subcutaneous tumor model.(D and E) Engulfment of Cy5-labeled LNPs in tumor and tdLn 8h after intratumoral injection.(F) Percentage of LNP+ cDC1s and cDC2s in the draining (dLN, iliac LN) 24h after intramuscular injection of DiD-labeled eLNPs and pIC-LNPs.The mean ± SEM is shown in (B), (D), (E), and (F).

After intratumoral injection, we isolate both the tumor and the tumor-draining lymph node (tdLN). In these tissues, we observe higher numbers of LNP-positive DCs in the tumor as compared to those in the tdLN, likely reflecting antigen processing that occurs as DCs migrate toward the lymph node. Notably, within the tumor, cDC2s show higher LNP uptake than cDC1s, which is different from what we observed following intravenous administration (Figures 6D and 6E). In the tdLN, antigen processing reduces the detectability of LNP+ cDCs, making accurate gating more challenging. Therefore, we sometimes evaluate CCR7- and CCR7+ cDC subsets instead of relying solely on LNP+ cDCs when assessing maturation markers, as this approach reduces variability (see also Figure 4 on gating strategy).

After intramuscular injection, analysis of the draining lymph node at 24 h shows that the Cy5 signal remains relatively stable, with approximately 40% uptake still detectable, in contrast to the more rapid signal loss observed in the spleen. Similar to the intratumoral setting, cDC2s exhibit higher uptake following intramuscular administration than after intravenous delivery (Figure 6F).

Defining DC maturation states via flow cytometry

Most commonly used DC maturation panels rely on the use of the markers CCR7 and MHCII, which strongly increase during maturation. When plotting the proportion of mature CCR7+ DCs induced by uptake of LNPs, it is clear that adjuvanted LNPs induce a stronger maturation response than eLNPs, and that intravenous injection of eLNPs hardly induces any maturation response in cDC2s, which contrasts the results in cDC1s (Figure 8A). By making use of the additional DC maturation markers as proposed in this protocol, homeostatic from immunogenic maturation states in cDCs can be differentiated (Figures 8B and 8C). The uptake of pIC-LNPs induces an immunogenic maturation state characterized by increased expression of CD80, CD86, CD274, CD200, CD69 and CD201. None of these markers becomes upregulated in steady state mature DCs nor upon uptake of eLNPs. On the contrary, homeostatic mature DCs are marked by enhanced expression of CD63. CD1d levels and ICOSL levels remain highly expressed in homeostatic mature DCs, while being downregulated in immunogenic conditions. All markers can be applied to both cDC1 and cDC2 subsets. When plotting immunogenic versus homeostatic markers, the two maturation states can be easily deconvoluted (Figure 8C). Incorporation of antigenic cargo into LNPs as synthetic peptides does not affect the DC maturation state and still leads to the induction of a homeostatic maturation program after LNP uptake (Figure 8D). On the other hand, incorporating the cargo as mRNA, purified with the LiCl precipitation method described above (step 14h in the section before you begin), does switch the DC maturation profile to immunogenic, despite the use of the methyl pseudouridine modification. Please note that depending on the purification strategy used, mRNA can be rendered less or more immunogenic, which can be monitored by following the DC maturation profile in vivo (Swinnen and Webb et al., manuscript in preparation). Limitations

While the markers proposed to deconvolute homeostatic from immunogenic mature DCs work very well in vivo, they will be less useful to discriminate the two maturation states on in vitro cultivated DCs (see also Rennen et al1). Especially for the costimulatory molecules CD80 and CD86, it is well established that their expression is only downregulated upon interaction with Tregs in the lymph node or white pulp of the spleen. They will dampen the expression of these costimulatory molecules specifically on homeostatic mature DCs in a CTLA4-dependent manner through transendocytosis.40 This implies that these markers cannot be used as discriminating marker in vitro to show the immunogenicity of certain compounds, as has been noted before.41,42,43 The DC maturation flow cytometry panel contains markers that are conserved in humans and can be applied to human DCs to characterize their maturation state. However, we have not successfully validated this panel to assign homeostatic mature DCs in human samples. All human DCs analyzed thus far—whether isolated from spleens obtained through the Transplantation Center at UZ Ghent or from tonsils provided by the Department of Otorhinolaryngology at UZ Ghent—already exhibited (some) expression of immunogenic markers. This does not preclude its use as an approach to assess the effect of certain treatments/inflammatory conditions in human samples by monitoring differences in DC maturation state.

Finally, the field of LNP biology is rapidly evolving and new insights are gained every day that help us define the contribution of each parameter during LNP formulation to uptake, transfection efficiency, endosomal escape, but also immunogenicity of the LNPs. By no means this paper claims to provide a complete workflow of LNP formulation taking into consideration all known and unknown parameters of LNP formulation. We tested three different ionizable lipids (ALC-0315, SM-102 and C12-200), all with similar outcomes, but there are many more on the market. Similarly, we compared DOPE and DSPC and did not detect any difference in DC maturation state by interchanging them, but on the other hand others have shown prominent differences in uptake and endosomal escape when comparing DOPE with DSPC.14 Also the label used, route of administration, method of RNA preparation and purification will all affect the downstream outcome and all need to be optimized.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

It is not possible to draw a clear conclusion on which conditions are immunogenic/homeostatic based on the surface expression levels of the maturation markers.

Potential solution

Please ensure that the different maturation markers used are coupled to fluorochromes in channels where there is minimal spread. Note that we mostly use the DC maturation panel as a relative way to classify and compare different mature DC populations along a homeostatic/immunogenic spectrum, rather than using them as an absolute assignment. Introduce positive and negative controls to define the expected range of maturation marker expression. This will help set meaningful MFI cut-offs for identifying immunogenic versus homeostatic DCs. Make sure to plot MFI rather than the percentage of marker-positive cells, as MFI provides a more informative representation of expression levels across conditions. Please note that we always plot the MFI on the mature CCR7+ population and that it is essential to compare to the proper immature population. In addition, complement the flow-cytometric characterization of maturation marker expression with qPCR profiling (Table 12), as this combined approach provides the clearest distinction between immunogenic and homeostatic mature DCs.

Problem 2

The expression of maturation markers does not look the same in vitro DCs.

Potential solution

As explained in the limitations section and as shown in Rennen et al,1 the maturation flow cytometry panel works very well in vivo but will not be able to discriminate the two maturation states on in vitro cultivated DCs (see also Rennen et al1).

Problem 3

The immunostimulatory effect of TLR agonist becomes excessively strong when they are incorporated into LNPs.

Potential solution

We have observed that the immunogenic maturation profile resulting from injection of pIC alone compared to that induced by injecting LNP-pIC is notably different (Rennen et al1). The response elicited by pIC-LNPs is significantly stronger and more long-lasting, showing the effect of incorporating the antigen and TLR adjuvant into LNPs. Reduce the TLR agonist dose to achieve a more controlled immune response.
