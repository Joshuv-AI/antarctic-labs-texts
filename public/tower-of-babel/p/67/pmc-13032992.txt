Concurrent validation of a novel intraoperative navigation platform for total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Margot Demeulenaere, Orçun Taylan, Maciej Biały, Arne Van de Vyver, Thomas Louwagie, Geert Peersman, Lennart Scheys
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
InroductionThis cadaveric study aimed to assess the accuracy of the Next-AR navigation platform in terms of implant alignment, kinematics, and collateral ligament elongations in comparison to gold standard measurement tools.Materials and methodsComputed tomography of 7 fresh-frozen legs were acquired for patient-specific guide design, identification of bony landmarks and collateral ligaments to acquire real-time feedback for bone resection, alignment and collateral ligament elongations using Next-AR system. The specimens underwent medially-stabilized TKA (Medacta) based on the mechanical alignment technique. The difference between target and achieved implant alignment parameters was assessed. The relative change in tibiofemoral kinematics, medial collateral ligament- (MCL), and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) elongations acquired with the Next-AR system were compared to reference values acquired with bone-pin mounted motion trackers (Vicon, UK) while performing quasistatic squatting in an ex vivo knee simulator.ResultsThe mean differences between achieved and target hip-knee-ankle angle, femoral varus, femoral flexion, femoral rotation, tibial varus, and tibial slope measured -1.5°, 0.5°, 0.07°, -0.14°, 0.79° and -0.36°, respectively, with no statistically significant differences found (p ≥ 0.12). Knee flexion (p=0.32) and valgus orientation (p=0.63), obtained from Next-AR and Vicon systems were comparable. Collateral ligament elongations derived from Next-AR exhibited similar values for the anterior bundle of MCL (p=0.18) and LCL in the anterior and middle bundles (p>0.3) obtained from the Vicon system.ConclusionsOur findings showed that the Next-AR system achieved postoperative implant alignment close to the planned targets, with small systematic deviation (mean bias ≤ 1.5°), limited total error (RMSE ≤ 2.3°), and variability of paired differences ≤ 1.87°, while additionally providing quantitative real-time assessment of tibiofemoral kinematics and collateral ligament elongations.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a highly successful surgical treatment option for patients with end-stage knee osteoarthritis. It alleviates the poor quality of life due to pain and restores mobility during activities of daily living [1, 2]. Although surgical techniques and implant designs have improved over the last decades, about 1 out of 10 patients remains dissatisfied following primary TKA [3, 4]. This is related to various factors including postoperative pain relief, poor clinical functional improvement and preoperative patient expectations [3]. With regards to the latter, achieving patient satisfaction can be especially challenging in the younger, more active population given the higher functional demands [3]. Also, it is important to note that the risk for revision surgery increases in the younger patient population receiving TKA, especially in males under 55 years old [5].

Following infection, the most common indications for revision TKA remain to be mechanical in nature, such as instability, aseptic loosening, breakage and implant failure [6]. Therefore, inaccurate implantation of prosthetic components and malalignment are important predictive factors for TKA failure with a deviation of more than 3 degrees from the mechanical axis being a clinically accepted threshold to prevent early failure, especially with varus angulation [5–7]. In addition, poor soft tissue balancing can also be related to excessive implant wear, excessive tightness or instability and decreased proprioception [8]. The desire to achieve precise, reproducible, and reliable control over these essential surgical parameters prompted the development of computer-assisted navigation systems (CAS) in TKA in the late nineties [8].

CAS systems help surgeons achieve the optimal alignment goal within ±3° of neutral mechanical alignment, which is essential for long-term implant survival through dedicated camera systems, patient-specific guides and, later, robotic systems. Accordingly, the CAS system is designed to assist surgeons during femoral and tibial resection, as well as implant positioning according to a digital pre-operative plan. This is done by using either intraoperative (imageless systems) or preoperative computer tomography (CT) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided registration of bony landmarks (image-based systems) [6, 9]. It has been consistently shown that CAS improves accuracy and component positioning compared to conventional TKA. Indeed, studies have been continuously comparing TKAs using CAS to conventional TKA [10, 11]. A randomised study of Zhang et al. (32 patients, bilateral TKA) has reported that up to 28% of conventional surgical approaches relying on manual instruments fail to restore a targeted neutral MA within 3°, in comparison to 0% with navigation [12]. In a more recent study of Todesca et al. similar results were found, with 11% outliers from neutral MA in conventional TKA versus 4% in navigated TKA [13]. Also regarding functional outcome and patient-reported outcome measures, it is clear that systems controlling for soft tissue balancing provide significantly better outcomes [8, 14]. Finally, there is a small overall benefit in revision rates, especially in the patient population under 65 years old [15].

CAS technology is still evolving and advancing, and it will continue to do so to further increase surgical accuracy and patient satisfaction [8]. As such, the Next-AR Navigation Platform was recently introduced by Medacta International (Strada Regina, Switzerland), which includes a wireless single-use, bone-mounted camera tracking system (see Fig. 1A), based on the identification of bony landmarks and ligamentous insertion in preoperative CT scans, allows for the intraoperatively display of real-time knee alignment parameters, tibiofemoral kinematics, and collateral ligament elongation [16]. The camera tracker’s error is specified to be ≤ 0.5°/0.5 mm in six degrees of freedom (three translations, three rotations). However, there is currently a lack of data regarding the system’s accuracy during surgical procedures to the best of our knowledge [17]. Therefore, the goal of this study was to experimentally document the accuracy of the Next-AR navigation platform in terms of alignment parameters, tibiofemoral kinematics, and collateral ligament elongations, whilst performing medially stabilized GMK Sphere TKA on cadaver specimens.

Materials and methods

Preoperative specimen imaging

Seven fresh-frozen full cadaveric legs (age = 75.5 ± 9.5, two bilateral and one unilateral female donor, one bilateral male donor, height = 165 cm ± 0.5, and weight = 66 kg ± 10.5) were collected following ethical approval from the local Ethics Committee (NH019-2021-04-01). Specimens were screened for possible lower limb disorders or prior surgical interventions. Bi-cortical bone pins of 5 mm were fixed into the femur and tibia to rigidly attach marker frames, each containing four reflective spheres of 12 mm. Computer tomography (CT) scans (Siemens Somatom Force, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) of each specimen were acquired in full extension with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm. Segmentation software (Mimics 25.0, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium) was used to identify the location of the spherical markers and specific anatomical landmarks from the CT images based on an established methodology [17]. These identified landmarks were subsequently used to define a joint coordinate system for the femur and tibia based on the Grood and Suntay convention [18].

In parallel, a complementary series of CT scans were acquired in accordance with the MyKnee protocol (Medacta International SA, Castel San Pietro, Switzerland) for identifying the key alignment parameters, such as the specimens’ native hip knee ankle (HKA) angle, femoral varus, femoral flexion, femoral rotation, tibial varus and tibial slope. Next, these scans also allowed us to define and produce patient-specific surgical guides [19], and, finally, provided the source data to define the following input parameters for the Next-AR Navigation Platform: 3D models of the knee, including bony landmarks locations and the insertions of the collateral ligaments. The latter CT-based origins and insertions of the collateral ligaments were identified based on a previously validated protocol [16, 20]. Each collateral ligament was represented as two lines: one connecting the proximal insertion point to the midpoint of the ligament at the tibial plateau edge, and the other connecting this midpoint to the distal insertion point. The midpoint defined on the tibial plateau edge was the optimal choice for eliminating penetration of the lines into the bone at a lower computational cost than a physics-based contact model. It was worth noting that the above midpoint was fixed for the entire knee range of motion such that any physiologically occuring translation along the tibial plateau edge was not accounted for by the model.

Specimen preparation

The specimens were thawed 24 h before testing. They underwent a single freezing-thawing cycle. The femur and the tibia were resected 32 cm proximally and 28 cm distally to the knee joint, respectively. All the surrounding soft tissues were carefully removed at the mid-diaphyseal region of the bones. The joint capsule, ligaments, and tendons were preserved while the skin and subcutaneous tissue around the knee joint are removed. The quadriceps tendon was exposed and secured within a dedicated metal custom clamp. Additionally, suture loops (2 × 2 non-absorbable polyester braided suture wire; Cardioxyl, Peters Surgical, Bobigny Cedex, France) were passed through the medial and lateral hamstring tendons. The tibia and femur were subsequently embedded in metal pots using an acrylic resin (Struers, Ballerup, Denmark), with the femur positioned at approximately 6° of valgus.

Surgical technique

Each knee underwent cemented cruciate-sacrificing total knee arthroplasty (medially-stabilized, GMK Sphere, Medacta International) using a mechanical alignment technique. To perform TKA, the patient-specific surgical guides were initially affixed to the femur and tibia with dedicated bone pins. The camera and receiver units of the Next-AR navigation platform were rigidly attached to dedicated detachable device housings that were mounted on the guides (Fig. 1A). To assess potential systematic discrepancies between the positions reported by the motion tracker unit—defined on the basis of the bone models generated from the preoperative CT images—and the actual bone positions, 20 points (each) on both tibia and femur were identified using the Next-AR navigation system’s wand (Fig. 1B) [16]. Such discrepancies can occur, for example, due to reduced visibility or absence of cartilage and menisci in the preoperative CT scans. Once the registration process was complete, the surgical guides were removed while maintaining the camera and receiver units fixed.

The absolute change in the collateral ligaments with respect to a reference value, i.e. their length at an extended knee flexion (0°−10°), was digitally recorded while performing multiple passive flexion-extension cycles using the Next-AR Navigation Platform [16]. The change in each collateral ligament length was calculated by summing the Euclidean distances from the kinematically tracked ligament origin to the midpoint and insertion, which we previously defined using CT images.

Sequential resection of the femur and tibia was performed according to the surgical plan using specimen-specific cutting guides (Fig. 1C). Subsequently, a cemented total knee arthroplasty was implanted. Both the camera and receiver were fixed during surgery to allow for subsequent postoperative testing (Fig. 1D). Finally, the postoperative relative collateral ligament elongations were again digitally recorded while performing passive flexion-extension cycles and compared to the preoperative reference range. The Next-AR Navigation Platform could enable intraoperative alignment adjustments facilitated by the use of the camera and receiver units. However, based on the surgeon’s decision (G.P.) no additional cuts or ligament releases were performed during this experiment.

Testing and postoperative imaging

Following TKA, the specimens were mounted into an Oxford-based physiological ex-vivo knee simulator (Fig. 1E). Each specimen was then consecutively subjected to a varus-valgus stress test and quasistatic squatting in sequence. For the varus-valgus stress test, a handheld digital dynamometer (0.1 N resolution; Series 4, Mark-10, Copiague, USA) was used to apply adduction and abduction moment of 10 Nm at 0°, 30°, 60° and 90° of knee flexion. During the varus-valgus stress test, the femoral pot was fully constrained, while the tibia was free to move in all axes, thereby allowing six degrees of freedom at the knee joint. For quasistatic squatting, the tibia pot was mounted into the ankle assembly, and the quadriceps tendon and hamstrings were affixed to dedicated linear electromechanical actuators mounted on the physiological ex-vivo knee simulator. Throughout the quasistatic squatting, active control of the quadriceps (applying various loads) and bilateral hamstrings (applying a constant 50 N each) generated a constant vertical ankle load of 110 N throughout a semi-static knee flexion range with increments of 10°. This step was achieved through a custom LabVIEW program (v2019, National Instruments, Austin, TX, USA).

To assess and validate the absolute changes in the tibiofemoral kinematics and length in the collateral ligaments were measured by the Next-AR Navigation Platform during varus-valgus stress test and quasistatic squatting, these changes were compared to the data derived from a laboratory-standard six-cameras motion capture system (Vero v1.3, Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK) with an 0.03 ± 0.19 mm resolution [21]. Hereto, the insertions of medial collateral ligament (MCL) and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) of the specimens were first exposed. Thereafter, the coordinates of these ligament insertions were digitized using a dedicated optical motion capture system’s wand, equipped with five spherical retro-reflective markers. Based hereon, each collateral ligament computer model was subdivided into its anterior, middle, and posterior bundles. Furthermore, the prevention of any inadvertent MCL bundle penetration into the bone for postprocessing was achieved by defining a shortest path problem along the tibial plateau edge for each bone position, defined as a 3D spline using Mimics. It should be noted that such precautions were indeed not required for the LCL due to the absence of wrapping around the tibial plateau.

Finally, the 3D trajectories of the retro-reflective spherical markers that were rigidly attached to the specimens were recorded with the above, a-priori calibrated optical motion tracking system at a frequency of 100 Hz. In addition, a single Vicon HD camera (2.1MP, Vicon Vue, Vicon Motion Systems, Oxford, UK) was used to synchronously capture and digitize the real-time output displayed on the Next-AR Navigation Platform’s screen throughout the whole experiment. This includes knee flexion, varus-valgus angle, tibiofemoral internal-external rotation, MCL elongation and LCL elongation. This data was used for future comparison with the motion tracking-based reference measurements. Once the test was complete, we obtained postoperative CT scans for each cadaveric knee.

Postprocessing

To determine the postoperative HKA, femoral varus, femoral flexion, femoral rotation, tibial varus angle and tibial slope, a postoperative 3D model of each bone, including their respective implanted components, was generated. Important to note that, following preoperative imaging, the proximal femur and distal tibia were resected during specimen preparation for potting [21]. Furthermore, the femoral condyles and tibial plateau were resected during total knee arthroplasty (Fig. 1C). As a result, some essential anatomical landmarks such as tibial knee center, tibial medial-lateral condyle centers, femoral hip center, femoral knee center and femoral medial-lateral condyle centers that were essential for defining postoperative knee alignment parameters, based on the native bone’s anatomical coordinate system and associated orthogonal planes (frontal, sagittal and transverse), were missing in the postoperative CT scans. As a result, the coordinate system obtained from preoperative imaging served as the reference to evaluate postoperative knee alignment using the native knee’s orthogonal planes. Hereto, a virtual superimposition was performed to align the postoperative 3D models with their corresponding preoperative 3D models using a global registration approach (3-Matic 17.0, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium [22]. Finally, each postoperative alignment parameter could be calculated in the preoperatively defined reference frames and directly compared to the targeted surgical planning values defined based on the mechanical alignment technique [19].

The retro-reflective marker trajectories captured with the Vicon optical motion capture system were processed using dedicated motion tracking software (Nexus 2.9, Vicon, Oxford, UK). Based on this, the elongation of each collateral ligament bundle (anterior, middle and posterior) was computed using a custom Phyton code based on the method described by Marai et al. [23]: at each instance, MCL and LCL elongations were computed by the shortest paths in a three-dimensional space while preventing bone penetration for the MCL through a previously defined 3D spline around the tibial plateau edge. The tibiofemoral kinematics and collateral ligament elongations derived from the Vicon optical motion capture system for varus-valgus stress test and quasistatic squatting were then further computed using custom code in Matlab (R2022a, Mathworks Inc, Natick, MA, USA).

To allow a comparative analysis of the kinematics and collateral ligament elongations obtained from the screen of the Next-AR Navigation Platform with the reference values obtained through the Vicon optical motion capture system, the values for both systems were expressed as differences between the value at each measured knee flexion angle and their respective value at the start of the quasistatic motion (~35° of knee flexion) and varus-valgus laxity testing (0°, 30°, 60° and 90° of knee flexion).

Statistical analysis

Knee alignment parameters, including the HKA angle, femoral varus, femoral flexion, femoral rotation, tibial varus angle, and tibial slope, were obtained from the postoperative CT images. These parameters were compared with the corresponding mechanical alignment–based surgical planning values using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Non-parametric testing was selected due to the small sample size and the non-normal distribution of the data.

Subsequently, a tailored linear mixed models was used to analyze tibiofemoral kinematics derived from cross-sectional data (varus–valgus stress tests) and longitudinal data (quasistatic squatting assessed across knee flexion angles). For datasets containing outliers, a robust linear mixed models was applied to account for the influence of extreme observations and to ensure the reliability of the results. For tibiofemoral kinematic comparisons, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test. To address multiple comparisons when evaluating collateral ligament elongations acquired from the Next-AR Navigation Platform against reference measurements obtained from the Vicon optical motion capture system, Dunnett’s correction was applied to control the family-wise error rate.

Subsequently, Tukey’s HSD post hoc is used to compare the tibiofemoral kinematics. To control the Family-Wise Error Rate, a Dunnett’s correction was applied for multiple comparison for collateral ligament elongations acquired from the Next-AR Navigation Platform with the reference values obtained through the Vicon optical motion capture system.

For the varus-valgus stress test, the Vicon optical motion capture system (reference) and the Next-AR Navigation Platform were expressed as fixed effect (condition), with a random intercept assigned for conditions nested within sample ID to account for repeated measurements. For the quasistatic squatting analysis, the fixed effect consisted of reference measurement system and Next-AR (as condition), while random intercepts were specified for sample ID and the nested condition factor. Additionally, a random slope for knee flexion angle was included within each nested sample ID and condition to model subject-specific changes across flexion angles.

To quantify agreement between the Next-AR Navigation Platform and the reference system, several complementary analyses were carried out. These included the root-mean-square error (RMSE), the standard deviation of the paired differences (Stdd, as a measure of precision), the mean paired difference with its 95% confidence interval (as a measure of systematic bias), and Bland–Altman limits of agreement (LoA) to assess the magnitude and distribution of measurement differences across the range of values. Together, these analyses provided a comprehensive evaluation of both accuracy and precision of the Next-AR system relative to the reference measurements.

For statistically different groups, the Cohen’s d effect size (ES, large ≥ 0.8 and small ≤ 0.2) was additionally computed. All statistical analyses were performed in R (R-Studio Version 2023.03.1, Boston, MA), and the significance level is determined at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the accuracy of the Next-AR navigation platform in terms of implant alignment parameters, tibiofemoral kinematics, and collateral ligament elongations, while performing medially stabilized GMK Sphere TKA on 7 cadaver specimens. In terms of implant alignment parameters, postoperative values obtained using the Next-AR navigation platform demonstrated low systematic bias relative to the surgical plan (mean bias range: −1.50° to 0.79°) and RMSE values ≤ 2.29°. There were no statistically significant differences between planned and achieved alignment values (p ≥ 0.12). However, measurement variability was present, as reflected by the standard deviation of paired differences (0.07°–1.87°) and Bland–Altman limits of agreement, which ranged from −5.17° to 2.17° for HKA and were within approximately ±3° for the remaining alignment parameters (Table 1). Moreover, the dispersion of individual errors was larger for HKA (Stdd = 1.87°), resulting in wider LoA (−5.17° to 2.17°). In contrast, the remaining alignment parameters showed narrower limits of agreement, indicating better consistency at the individual-measurement level. Based on the anatomical planes, the absolute mean difference between the surgical planning and achieved alignment parameters was ≤ 1.5° (p ≥ 0.11) in the coronal plane and ≤ 0.36° (p ≥ 0.38) in the sagittal plane.

In terms of frontal-plane laxity assessment, measurements acquired from the Next-AR Navigation Platform demonstrated small systematic deviations relative to the reference, with no statistical differences observed (p ≥ 0.06). Mean paired differences were −1.12° for valgus laxity and −0.80° for varus laxity, while collateral ligament elongations showed differences between −1.06 mm and −0.22 mm, exhibiting a small and consistent underestimation relative to the reference measurement. The variability of paired differences ranged from 0.95° to 1.18° for angular laxity and from 1.35 to 1.67 mm for ligament elongations, with corresponding RMSE values ≤ 1.45° and ≤ 1.77 mm. Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated absolute limits of agreement of approximately 3° for angular laxity and 4 mm for ligament elongations, indicating that despite small mean bias at the group level, variability at the specimen level was considerably larger.

Numerous studies are available in the literature that similarly compared the surgical accuracy of diverse navigation systems for TKA. For example, the prospective study of Di Benedetto et al. [24] compared the imageless Stryker eNact Precision Knee Navigation System (Stryker; Kalamazoo, Mitch) with postop CT data. They report a mean deviation of < 2° from the target mechanical axis of 0° and a mean deviation of < 3° from the targeted posterior tibial slope of 3°, as well as the targeted femoral rotation of 0°. In our study, these errors measure 1.5°, 0.36° and 0.14° on average (absolute difference), respectively, with an overall RMSE below 2.3°. Also Hannan et al. [25] compare intraoperative parameters of 3D alignment reported by the imageless Exactech Guided Personalised Surgery system (Gainesville, Florida, USA) with postop-CT based reference measurements and report a mean absolute difference of 2.34°±0.83° for whole limb coronal alignment, 2.07°±0.55° for femoral flexion, 1.38°±0.33° for femoral rotation and 1.14°±0.39° for tibial slope. This comparison is again favorable, with absolute mean difference for HKA (1.5°), femoral flexion (0.07°), femoral rotation (0.14°) and tibial slope (0.36°) from our study being well within the reported range. Previous studies comparing CT-based (VectorVision, BrainLAB) [26] and imageless navigation (OrthoPilot, Aesculap, Germany) [27] with conventional arthroplasty demonstrate significantly greater accuracy in mechanical axis alignment and component positioning. Miyasaka et al. demonstrates that 92.3% of patients in the navigation group had a leg axis within a range of 3° versus 74.6% of patients in the conventional group [26]. Selvanayagam et al. finds 40% of patients in the navigation group with a 0° mechanical alignment, versus 24% in the conventional group [27]. The systematic review by Shatrov and Parker [15] states that CAS TKA is more accurate than conventional TKA, especially in the coronal plane, as some navigation systems do not capture the sagittal or axial alignment parameters. In our study, we did not compare CAS with conventional arthroplasty. However, compared with the targeted surgical planning, we achieve coronal alignment parameters with a RMSE < 2.3° (Stdd = < 1.9°), sagittal parameters with a RMSE < 1.1° (Stdd = < 1.06°), and axial alignment parameters < 1.19° (Stdd = < 1.3°),.

The Next-AR Navigation Platform can aid surgeons in achieving optimal soft tissue balancing. It provides real-time feedback on varus/valgus laxity and collateral ligament elongation during varus/valgus stress tests, even without muscle loads [8]. Interestingly, the Next-AR navigation platform’s measurements for such varus/valgus laxity motions do not show any significant differences (Table 2) for varus (p = 0.28, RMSE = 1.27° and Stdd= 1.18°) and valgus (p = 0.13, RMSE = 1.45° and Stdd= 0.95°) orientations, as well as, LCL (p > 0.6, RMSE < 1.5 mm and and Stdd < 1.5 mm) and MCL (p > 0.05, RMSE < 1.8 mm and and Stdd < 1.7 mm) elongations with our reference measurements. This corresponds with the study of Kinoshita et al. which also indicates a strong correlation between rotational soft tissue balance and rotational kinematics in posterior stabilized and cruciate retaining TKA using navigation (Precision Knee Navigation Software version 4.0, Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI) with correlation coefficients between the medial angle of rotational stress and rotational kinematics of 0.97, 0.80, 0.74, 0.71 and 0.70 at 0, 30, 45, 60 and 90° degrees of flexion, in unloaded intra-operative conditions, where a passive internal and external rotation stress was applied during the flexion-extension arc [28]. The prospective RCT of Lee et al. shows significantly less outliers in the flexion and extension gap (12% in navigated versus 25% in conventional TKA) mainly on the medial side, using an image-free navigation system (OrthoPilot™ instrument; B. Braun Aesculap, Tuttlingen, Germany) [29]. This finding supports our study results. The clinical relevance of such measurements is corroborated by a prospective cohort study of Lehnen et al. They focus on the clinical outcome of computer navigation on TKA with ligament referencing technique, hypothesizing that this technique might benefit more from CAS than bone referencing techniques. Consequently, they report significantly better PROMs (WOMAC and Knee Society Score) at 1-year follow-up with CAS (imageless Vector Vision navigation system, BrainLab, Munich, Germany) compared to conventional TKA [30].

To improve functional outcomes, patient satisfaction and the longevity of the implant after TKA, it is crucial to restore normal knee kinematics throughout a functional range of motion. Furthermore, it is widely acknowledged that soft tissue balancing plays a pivotal role in this post-operative kinematics pattern [28]. We therefore conducted a supplementary analysis of the Next-AR’s accuracy in terms tibiofemoral kinematics and collateral ligament elongations during a loaded motor task that spans a large range of motion, namely squatting. Here, the Next-AR platform consistently recorded comparable knee flexion angles, valgus orientations, anterior MCL elongation, and elongations in all bundles of the LCL, except the posterior bundle, which showed no significant statistical differences compared to the reference measurement. Nevertheless, the Next-AR platform demonstrated greater lengthening of the MCL compared to the reference measurement; the latter particularly notable in the middle and posterior bundles (p < 0.03). Similarly, the Next-AR platform exhibited relatively greater LCL lengthening of the posterior bundle (p < 0.01). However, this effect was relatively small, with an effect size of 0.49 (small to moderate), potentially indicating a small, potentially measurable difference (Stdd = 5.39 mm and mean difference = 1.82 mm).

It is worth noting that both reference and Next-AR navigation platforms use the same anatomical landmarks definitions, albeit defined by separate operators. Therefore, the minor differences in terms of tibiofemoral kinematics between the Next-AR navigation platform and reference measurements are likely due to the inter-rater discrepancies in the definition of common anatomical landmarks for both measurement systems. Indeed, Morton et al. quantified the effect of landmark location on kinematic measurements and demonstrated that a standard deviation of up to 2 mm in landmark placement can lead to 6.5° and 4.4 mm deflection in tibiofemoral kinematics [31]. It is also important to note that the collateral ligament elongation in both the Next-AR navigation platform and reference measurements are affected by these disparities in terms of tibiofemoral kinematics. Furthermore, also inter-rater variable in the identification of ligament insertions and the differences in the underlying ligament models described above, further affect our comparison.

It is important to note several other limitations in this study. Unlike our reference measurement method, which subdivided the ligaments into anterior, middle, and posterior bundles, the Next-AR navigation platform uses a single-line definition for each ligament. More specifically, this single line definition of the ligaments is performed based on distal and proximal insertions, located between the anterior and middle bundles. For the varus-valgus stress test, the absolute difference between the unloaded and loaded state (i.e. 10Nm) is typically used to determine the net amount of varus-valgus laxity and collateral ligament elongation at each measured knee flexion angle [32]. However, the Next-AR Navigation Platform exhibits a slight lag compared to the Vicon optical motion capture system. As a result, when comparing the varus or valgus angle and the associated collateral ligament elongation data obtained from both systems, the data acquired from the Next-AR Navigation Platform could not be obtained at 10 Nm due to this lag. Therefore, we used the maximum achieved force for the comparison. In addition, it should be noted that both the Vicon optical motion capture system and the Next-AR Navigation Platform use infrared cameras, which can lead to interferences between the two systems during certain motions. Additionally, the Next-AR Navigation Platform experienced lost-of-seight errors between the camera-receiver pair during some dynamic motions, which may have been caused by to environmental light in the lab. Consequently, only a portion of the Next-AR data could be processed for the varus-valgus stress test. Furthermore, the varus-valgus data for one specimen had to be excluded due to hardware errors during measurement. Nevertheless, for the quasistatic squatting, the data could be extracted entirely. While the reference measurement allowed for the extraction of values with decimal precision, the Next-AR navigation system exclusively displayed integers. Finally, although the same Next-AR setup was used throughout the experiment, there were several software upgrades to improve the robustness of the calculations. It should also be noted that the laboratory-based experimental setup may not fully replicate the surgical theatre environment, and factors such as lighting conditions, environment temperature and intraoperative handling may differ in clinical use.

Conclusion

In this study, we validate and demonstrate the efficacy of the intraoperative Next-AR navigation platform in accurately assessing bony alignment, tibiofemoral kinematics and soft tissue balancing in medially stabilized total knee arthroplasty. While the Next-AR navigation platform effectively facilitates achieving targeted knee alignment and accurate calculation of tibiofemoral kinematics along with collateral ligament elongation, this paper does not address the biomechanics during passive flexion-extension and dynamic squatting. Nonetheless, further research will focus on assessing the capability of the Next-AR navigation platform to restore native knee biomechanics following medially stabilized TKA.
