Contributions of inherited mtDNA to longevity: evidence from extended pedigrees with 176 million kinship pairs
Authors: S. Alexandra Burt, S. Mason Garrison, Xuanyu Lyu, Joseph L. Rodgers, Sarah L. Carroll, Ken R. Smith, Michael D. Hunter

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Mitochondria are bacteria-like organelles with their own DNA (mtDNA) that exist in almost every cell in the human body and are transmitted across generations exclusively down the maternal line. They play a key role in our lives, producing nearly all of the energy we need to sustain life (e.g., breathing, digestion, thinking, moving). Given the role of mitochondria in energy production, it seems possible that inherited mtDNA could be important for human longevity. Studies conducted over the last few decades have been consistent with this hypothesis. First-degree relatives of a longevous person tend to live longer than others, delay or avoid age-related diseases, and have evidence of stronger immune systems and better metabolic health. Moreover, these familial patterns may be especially pronounced in mother-daughter dyads, consistent with the hypothesis that mtDNA plays a role in the human lifespan. Although provocative, such conclusions remain speculative, as studies have yet to conduct the kinds of large-scale biometric research needed to establish a clear role for mtDNA in longevity.

Added value of this study

We developed a new analytic model capable of leveraging the unique maternal inheritance pattern of mtDNA to estimate its effects on longevity. We conducted analyses using this model in the Utah Population Database (UPDB), examining 176,348,110 kinship links amongst 1,018,929 individuals born between 1700 and 1925.

Implications of all the available evidence

Results strongly support increased maternal-line similarity for longevity. Additional analyses indicated that mtDNA accounts for 5% of the variability in longevity, a surprisingly large proportion given the small size of mtDNA (∼16.6 k base pairs versus 2,875,002 k base pairs for nuclear DNA) and the relatively modest size of nuclear DNA contributions (23–26%). Moreover, sharing the maternal line of a longevous relative translated to an average of 11.3 months extra years of life. Such findings suggest that inherited mtDNA may have important effects on human longevity.

Introduction

Mitochondria are our cellular powerhouses, providing nearly all the energy we use for basic (e.g., breathing, digestion) and advanced (e.g., thinking, planning) functions. Interestingly, mitochondria are thought to be descendants of invading bacteria that developed a symbiotic relationship with their hosts.1 They exist in large numbers in each cell's intracellular space (rather than in the nucleus) and, reminiscent of their early origins, have their own bacteria-like DNA (mtDNA; small circular strands of 16.6 K base pairs in humans2). Interestingly, although they reside exclusively within eukaryotic organisms that reproduce themselves sexually (e.g., all mammals), mitochondria reproduce themselves asexually without recombination along the maternal line.3

This unique, non-Mendelian pattern of inheritance has long been leveraged for forensic identity testing.4 As one notable example, King Richard III died at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485 and was buried in an unmarked grave. In 2012, skeletal remains were excavated near the presumed site.4 To confirm the identity of the skeleton, researchers4 identified two living descendants on the maternal line (19 and 21 generations removed from the king; the two descendants were themselves 14th cousins, twice removed). They4 found a perfect mtDNA match with one living relative, and a single-base substitution difference with the other, providing compelling evidence that the skeleton was indeed that of Richard III.

Although mtDNA has been pivotal in resolving historical mysteries, its contributions to human health and disease are still emerging. Recent editorials and reviews have sought to spur biomedical research on mtDNA effects,1,5,6 but a Scopus search conducted in May 2025 indicated that fewer than 2% of genetic studies have taken up this call. The relative dearth of mtDNA research likely stems in part from the fact that mtDNA is foundationally different from nuclear DNA in several key ways—e.g., mtDNA does not recombine and is maternally-transmitted.7 As such, none of the biometric family, twin, or pedigree models developed to quantify overall nuclear DNA effects on mammalian behaviour allow us to also quantify the effects of mtDNA. Twin-family models, for example, have little use for identifying mtDNA, as all twins, full siblings, maternal half-siblings, and maternal cousins share their mtDNA, while paternal half-siblings and paternal cousins do not.

Despite this gap in our methodologies, studies conducted over the last few decades have nevertheless suggested that variation in mtDNA may be associated with critical biological outcomes, including longevity and resistance to age-related diseases. In model organisms, mutations in mitochondrial genes can activate defence pathways associated with increased longevity,8 pointing to a biological mechanism that may be relevant in humans. Human family studies have also indicated that first-degree relatives of a longevous person tend to live longer than others, delay or avoid age-related diseases, and evidence stronger immune systems and better metabolic health.9 At least one study has further suggested that these familial patterns of longevity may be especially pronounced in mother-daughter dyads,10 consistent with the hypothesis that mtDNA plays a role in the human lifespan. Other studies have revealed that specific mitochondrial haplogroups,11,12 as well as particular single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within the mitochondrial genome,13,14 appear more frequently among centenarians and other longevous persons, although results have been inconsistent.15,16 Although interesting, this body of evidence is undercut by several null findings and generally small sample sizes (typically less than 1000 participants, with one key exception14). What is needed is a large-scale biometric and population-based study capable of establishing a role for mtDNA in longevity, similar to the way in which twin studies were used to confirm nuclear DNA effects on longevity17,18 and related outcomes.19

The current paper fills this gap in the literature, developing a new analytical framework able to systematically estimate the impact of mtDNA on human longevity (defined here as the top 10% oldest survivors within their birth cohort). We specifically leveraged the unique non-Mendelian inheritance pattern of mtDNA, comparing longevity similarity in matrilineal relatives versus non-matrilineal relatives. Should mtDNA contribute to longevity, family members related solely via the maternal line should be more similar than those connected by even one paternal linkage, and this pattern should persist even to distant relatives. Indeed, the high degree of mtDNA conservation across even very distant maternal relatives, along with the relatively rapid decay of nuclear genetic relatedness across generations (5th degree relatives are nominally unrelated,20 sharing only 3.1% of their segregating nuclear DNA), suggests to us that the inclusion of distant kinships will be especially useful for isolating the effects of mtDNA. We thus evaluated matrilineal versus non-matrilineal similarity for longevity in 1,018,929 deceased individuals, nested in pedigrees up to 5 generations deep, from the Utah Population Database. In this way, we aimed to quantify the role of mtDNA in the length of the human life span.

Methods

Sample

The Utah Population Database (UPDB;21,22) includes extensive genealogical records from the Utah Genealogical Society, up to 17 generations deep, that have been linked to birth and death certificates. These extended genealogical records are a unique feature of the UPDB. One portion of the genealogies are derived from more than 185,000 Utah families who were initially identified on documents containing demographic and kinship information for three generations drawn by the Utah Genealogical Society. These families have since been linked across generations, along with links derived from vital records, yielding 200,000 multigenerational pedigrees with >11 million individuals.

To construct the family linkages that undergird our analyses, we began by identifying a broad set of individuals around whom to build extended pedigrees. We selected all deceased individuals with genealogical data born in 1925 or earlier whose age at death placed them in the top 25% of survival. We also randomly selected deceased controls matched on sex and born in 1925 or earlier from the middle 50% of the survival distribution (25th–75th percentile for their sex and birth cohort), along with all relatives of both probands. These sets of relatives formed the input for a custom network-based algorithm to construct the structural foundation of family linkages in the UPDB [see Supplementary information for more details].

We then identified probands who met commonly used criteria for longevity—the top 10% oldest survivors within their birth cohort,23 using birth and death dates to assess age at death (see below for more detail). Control probands were selected to match on sex but were otherwise chosen randomly from individuals whose age at death fell between the 25–75 percentile of survival for their birth cohort. We also identified all deceased biological relatives of the longevous and control probands up to 5 generations deep. This approach netted 1,018,929 individuals born between 1700 and 1925 with 176,348,110 unique kinship links amongst them.

Given the very large sample sizes and extended pedigree structure, power to detect correlations significantly different than zero was quite high. With a longevity prevalence defined as the top 10% of cohort survivors and the sample sizes reported in Table 1, we can detect intraclass tetrachoric correlation differences as small as 0.0065–0.0083 with 80% power and 5% alpha. Ethics

The study received IRB approval from the University of Utah (IRB_00143000) and the Resource for Genetic and Epidemiologic Research at the University of Utah. The IRB approved a waiver of consent for this study.

Measures

Death dates from UPDB genealogies for the past 200 years and Utah death certificates beginning in 1904 were compared to the birth dates from genealogies and Utah birth certificates to assess age at death. As noted, longevity was defined as the oldest 10% of survivors within their birth year.23 This operationalisation, along with adjustments for cohort effects, was facilitated using United States Social Security Administration (SSA) life tables, which offer birth year-specific data dating back to 1900 (https://www.ssa.gov/oact/HistEst/PerLifeTables/2017/PerLifeTables2017.html). Subjects whose births were earlier were normed to the earliest year available (1900). This approach allowed for a focused analysis on the potential for mtDNA variants to influence exceptional longevity, minimising any birth cohort effects. Because women often outlive men,23 the 10% cut-off was established through separate norming processes for men and women.

Statistics

The tetrachoric correlation comparison model used here, for a binary outcome with an underlying continuous quantitative distribution, is conceptually similar to the traditional intraclass correlation (ICC) model used to compute nuclear DNA heritability (h2) via the comparison of twin sibling similarity. Namely, when the monozygotic twin correlation (rMZ) is double the dizygotic twin correlation (rDZ), it implies the presence of additive genetic heritability (i.e., h2).24 The proposed mtDNA model builds directly on this approach but differs in three ways. First, because all full siblings have the same mother, they should have identical mtDNA. As such, sibling-only based designs are not useful. To detect mtDNA heritability (mt2), we needed to include more distant relatives in extended family pedigrees. Second, traditional ICC analyses examine similarity by degree of nuclear relatedness, but do not disambiguate these similarity coefficients across maternal and paternal lineage (the approach taken here). Third, the kinship relatedness underlying h2 differs from that underlying mt2, in that the former are defined by a range of values for segregating nuclear DNA between 0 and 1 whereas the latter are defined as either 0 or 1, because paternal/non-maternal relatives do not share segregating mtDNA while maternal relatives do. Collectively, these differences make it relatively straightforward to distinguish between mt2 and h2. As one example, 3rd cousins on the maternal line share 3.1% of their segregating nuclear DNA and all of their segregating mtDNA, whereas 3rd cousins on the paternal line share 3.1% of their segregating nuclear DNA and none of their segregating mtDNA.

We leveraged this basic model to analyse extended relative similarity for longevity, comparing the degree of maternal versus non-maternal similarity to estimate mt2. We then further enhanced our model to consider both the degree of nuclear relatedness (6.25%, 3.125%, etc.) and maternal versus non-maternal lineage to simultaneously estimate h2 and mt2,25 an important extension as prior work has indicated that nuclear DNA co-regulates mtDNA in many cases.26,27 Of note, however, our empirical analyses are silent with regard to heteroplasmies (i.e., mixed populations of wild-type and mutated mtDNA within a cell), as any differences of this sort between mothers and offspring will resemble error in our results given the kinship definitions above.25 Our prior simulation work25 has indicated that this sort of error reduces power and diminishes our ability to detect a true mtDNA effects. As such, our findings should be considered a conservative, lower bound estimate of the specific effects of inherited mtDNA on longevity. Full estimation details are provided in the Supplementary information.

Sensitivity analyses

We evaluated the robustness of our results, clarifying whether they persisted in unusual kinship pairings, when evaluating three other definitions of longevity, and when requiring both members of the dyad to have lived to at least mid-life (i.e., age 50). Results are presented in the Supplementary materials. To confirm that our findings represent the likely effects of inherited mt2 per se, we also evaluated four possible alternate explanations (i.e., heritable maternal nurture effects, X-chromosome effects, paternal imprinted (silenced) variants, and selective male migration effects). As a final step, we evaluated two possible confounders of our results (i.e., patrilineal effects of the Y-chromosome and paternal social inheritance effects).

Results

The average year of birth in the UPDB data was 1874 (SD = 46 years). Consistent with historical population demographics for Utah, most participants with race recorded in their vital records were White, non-Hispanic (99.6%); although we note that there was no information on race available for 37% of participants since historic records in centuries past did not typically gather race information. Most participants were born in the United States and its territories (84%) and in Utah in particular (60%). Finally, although there were more men than women in our overall dataset (52% versus 48%, respectively), women were more likely to qualify as a long-lived than were men (53% versus 47%, respectively). Longevous females lived to a mean age of 90 (SD = 5 years), while longevous males lived to a mean age of 88 (SD = 5 years). Those coded as non-longevous lived to an average age of 53 (SD = 27 years).

Our sample size of just over 1 million individuals in extended pedigrees up to five generations deep yielded 176,348,110 different relationship pairings across the participants (see Table 1). The proportion of first-degree relatives sharing mtDNA was well above 75%, consistent with the fact that mtDNA is shared by all full sibling pairings and by all mother-child pairings. Tetrachoric correlations

Tetrachoric correlations for longevity between pairs of relatives at each degree of nuclear genetic relatedness are presented in Table 1 and plotted in Fig. 2. Both the maternal and non-maternal line data were fully consistent with the presence of nuclear genetic influences on longevity. The correlations were largest for first degree relatives and generally decreased monotonically thereafter. That said, the correlations were relatively small, even for first degree relatives (r's ranged from 0.1145 [95% CI: 0.1115, 0.1176] to 0.1606 [95% CI: 0.1587, 0.1626]), and were zero or near zero by the 4th degree of relatedness (e.g., r = −0.0001 [95% CI: −0.0013, 0.0011]). Such findings suggest that, although clearly present, the nuclear genetic heritability of longevity is relatively modest, consistent with the presence of many environmental or external causes of death (e.g., accidents, natural disasters). Of critical interest for the current paper, we also observed increased kinship similarity for longevity when the relatives shared a common matrilineal heritage (see Table 1 and Fig. 2). Matrilineal relatives who shared their mtDNA were significantly more similar to one another in their longevity outcomes than were non-maternally linked relatives. This pattern replicated across 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th degree kinship pairings. It also persisted when examining most of the more unusual kinship pairings (see Supplementary Table S2) and when employing both stricter and looser definitions of what constitutes exceptionally long longevity (i.e., top 5% and 15% oldest survivors within their birth cohort, respectively, as well as an un-normed longevity definition (age 87) that did not adjust for either sex or birth cohort; see Supplementary Table S3 through S5). Further, this pattern persisted when we restricted the sample to only those dyads in which both members survived past mid-life (at least age 50; see Supplementary Table S6). We also tested a series of alternate explanations for our findings, including heritable maternal nurture effects, X-chromosome effects, paternal imprinted (silenced) variants, and selective male migration effects, none of which better explained the excess matrilineal similarity (see Supplementary Text and Supplementary Table S7).

We then examined two possible confounders of excess matrilineal similarity: effects of the Y-chromosome and paternal social inheritance effects. We continued to observe excess matrilineal similarity over and above the patrilineal line in virtually all cases (see Supplementary Tables S8 and S9). However, our findings indicated that patrilineal social inheritance was also important for longevity. Collectively then, our supplementary analyses bolster the conclusions of our primary analyses in pointing to likely effects of mtDNA heritability on longevity.

Variance estimates

We next sought to compute the proportion of variance in longevity that was accounted for by mt2. When estimated independently, mt2 contributed 10.9% of the variance to longevity. To adjust this proportion for the nuclear genetic heritability, we calculated h2 two ways: by computing the sibling effect on longevity, and by using Falconer's formulas across all kinship levels. Both analyses indicated that 23–26% of the variability in longevity was attributable to variability in h2. When mt2 was estimated over and above this nuclear genetic effect, we continued to find that 5.0% of the variance in longevity was attributable to mt2. Tests of this variance were highly statistically significant: a nested comparison of the sibling effect model to the model with both the sibling effect and mt2 effect yielded a p-value of <0.00001 (χ2 = 402.8 on 1 df). When divided by the number of base pairs per genome, these results place the contributions of mt2 more than four orders of magnitude larger than those of h2. Put another way, although mtDNA consists of fewer than 17,000 base pairs, it appears to ‘punch above its weight’ by predicting at least 5% of the variance in longevity.

Practical effect size

To estimate the magnitude of inherited mtDNA effects on longevity in a concrete and practical way, we calculated the average additional months lived by individuals with matrilineal versus non-matrilineal connections to longevous relatives. Using the same dyadic data, filters, and binning structure as in the primary analyses, we calculated the average age lived within bins stratified by (1) whether the dyad shared mtDNA, and (2) whether the relative was classified as longevous (i.e., in the top 10% of their birth cohort). Within each bin, we computed the difference in average age lived between those with and without connections to longevous relatives. These values were then converted to months and averaged across bins, weighting by the number of dyads per bin. Individuals with matrilineal connections to longevous relatives lived an average of 11.27 more months than those whose matrilineal relative was not longevous. By comparison, individuals with non-matrilineal connections to longevous relatives could expect to live an average of 0.98 more months than those whose matrilineal relative was not longevous.

Discussion

We sought to establish the effect size magnitude of inherited mtDNA variation in longevity. We conceptualised and developed a new extended pedigree analysis, evaluating whether matrilineal relatives were more similar in their longevity outcomes than were those with at least one common paternal ancestor. Analyses were conducted in a sample of just over 1 million deceased individuals with 176,348,110 unique kinship links in pedigrees five generations deep. Results consistently supported the contributions of mt2 to longevity. Across 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 4th, and 5th degree kinships in our primary analyses, maternal line relatives were more similar than non-maternal line relatives. This conclusion held when examining unusual kinship pairings, three different longevity definition thresholds, survival to least age 50, other sources of excess matrilineal similarity, and selective male migration. Variance component analyses of these data further indicated a mtDNA heritability of 5% over and above the nuclear DNA heritability of 23–26%. In more practical terms, we also found that sharing the maternal line of a longevous relative translated to an average of 11.3 months extra years of life.

The relatively modest h2 magnitude of 23–26% observed here is in line with prior studies in these and other samples.10,17,28,29 And although no prior biometric study has formally estimated mt2 effects on longevity (as the methods we developed here did not yet exist), our results are consistent with unpublished UPDB data that examined maternal excess longevity,30 a reassuring similarity that underscores the robustness of our results. They also align with a small handful of molecular studies that have (inconsistently) pointed to a possible link between longevity and particular mitochondrial haplogroups,11,12 although the samples in those studies have typically been very small (N < 500 or 1000) and the specific haplogroup(s) that allegedly promote longevity varied across studies.15,16 Indeed, the single largest molecular genetic study of mtDNA variants to our knowledge (N = 358,916 individuals from the UKBB14) was not able to replicate associations between longevity and particular mitochondrial haplogroups, although they did identify significant associations between attained maternal age (but not attained paternal age) and two mtDNA variants. Regardless, by uncovering biometric evidence of significant mt2 contributions to longevity in a sample with more than 175 million unique kinship links, the current findings confirm the presence of an overall association between mtDNA and longevity, and in doing so, highlight the clear need for future molecular genetic research on this important topic.

The current study benefitted from several key strengths, including our examination of more than 1 million deceased individuals nested in 176,348,110 unique kinship links and our development of a new biometric analytic framework that yields a straightforward interpretation. We also confirmed the presence of excess matrilineal similarity for longevity across a number of potential confounders and robustness checks, including the definition of longevity, survival to at least mid-life, unusual kinship pairings, heritable maternal nurture effects, X-chromosome effects, paternal imprinted (silenced) variants, selective male migration/cohort effects, patrilineal effects of the Y-chromosome, and paternal social inheritance effects.

That said, the study encountered some limitations. First, the current analyses estimated the effects of inherited mtDNA variation, but not the effects of mtDNA mutations that accumulate in post mitotic cells over the course of the lifespan. Although cells efficiently dispose of mutated mtDNA in most cases, some can remain, creating a mixed population of wild-type and mutated mtDNA within a cell (i.e., heteroplasmy). Because we focused on causes of similarity across kinships, we were not able to assess these kinds of environmental, age-related accumulations of mtDNA mutations. Because these mutations are confined to a single generation (women's gametes are formed while she is still an embryo herself, and in any case, these kinds of mutations are selected against during early germ cell development31), we anticipate relatively little effect of age-related mutations on our findings of mt2. That said, mtDNA heteroplasmic point mutations can be inherited across a single generation, leading to differences between mothers and offspring within a single generation,32,33 and moreover, have been shown to predict all-cause mortality.34 Although we are not able to directly test for this sort of intergenerational decay in inherited mtDNA, we note that heteroplasmic differences of this sort between mothers and offspring will necessarily resemble error in our results,25 as they would serve to artefactually reduce the tetrachoric correlation in only the maternal line. Moreover, the fact that our results persisted even when restricting analyses to those dyads in which both members lived until at least mid-life suggests that our findings are not affected by very early mortality related to heteroplasmies (or another cause). Regardless, our findings should be considered a conservative, lower bound estimate of mt2 on longevity.

A second limitation concerns the peculiar but consistent observation that correlations for 4th degree relatives were often slightly smaller than those for 5th degree relatives, with only a handful of exceptions. The former were also more likely to be either negatively signed or to have confidence intervals that included zero. It is not clear to us why this would be the case, although it could perhaps reflect differences in record keeping across the centuries. That said, we note that 4th degree correlations were larger in matrilineal versus non-matrilineal relatives in our primary analyses, and in several of the supplemental analyses, consistent with mt2 effects on longevity.

Another limitation concerns the relatively limited racial and ethnic heterogeneity in our data. According to the 2020 US Census, 77.8% of the Utah state population identifies as White non-Hispanic, with the second largest group identifying as Hispanic or Latino (14.4%; nearly all of whom identify their race as White). This trend of primarily White residents was even more pronounced in prior decades.35,36 In addition to raising concerns regarding the generalisability of our findings beyond European populations, this sort of limited variability is known to suppress correlations.37 Thus, the current finding may again represent a very conservative lower bound of mt2 on longevity. Efforts to replicate our work should directly investigate this possibility.

Relatedly, the participants’ common ancestry raises questions regarding the variability in mtDNA haplotypes in these data. Although we do not have access to mtDNA genomes to answer this question, a prior molecular study conducted in Cache County, Utah indicated substantial mtDNA haplotype variability.38 The Cache County sample includes 5092 individuals (i.e., 90% of residents aged 65 and older in Cache County). Because these participants are also included in the UPDB, the investigators were able to leverage the latter’s multi-generation pedigree data to identify 3151 distinct matrilineages in their sample. They then sequenced mtDNA from 285 of these matrilineages (chosen at random). Results revealed 249 distinct haplotypes among them, which could be further clustered into 102 major haplogroups or sub-haplogroups (or collections of haplotypes that suggest a common ancient ancestor). Such findings suggest the presence of significant variability in mtDNA haplotypes across the matrilineages of Utah.

Finally, our sample comprised deceased individuals born between 1700 and 1925 (mean 1874). Much has changed since this time period, including the development of antibiotics and the dramatically improved survival rate of children under the age of 5 (from 80% to 99+%).39,40 It is not clear how these sorts of historical differences may affect our results, although analyses restricting the sample to dyads in which both members reach mid-life (see Supplementary Table S6) indicate that results are not dependent on early mortality. Similarly, analyses assessing cohort effects more generally (see Supplementary Table S7) indicate that results are consistent before and during the 20th century. Regardless, future work should seek to evaluate, for example, whether and how the magnitude of mt2 contributions to longevity varies with the advent and widespread use of antibiotics, among other medical advances.

Despite these limitations, the current results have important implications not only for our understanding of longevity and diseases of ageing, but for all of biomedical research. One key consequence of the quantitative genetics field’s all-but-exclusive focus on nuclear DNA is that we have yet to establish any Bayesian prior probability distributions (or ‘priors') regarding the role of mtDNA. This absence of clear priors stands in sharp contrast to that for nuclear DNA. Twin-family studies, with their compelling biometric evidence of substantial nuclear genetic heritability,41 provided the field with a very strong set of priors. Indeed, so strong were these priors that, despite many non-replications and expensive failures, the scientific community persisted, developing new statistical techniques and huge genetic consortia to advance our understanding of nuclear genetic influences on human disease. In short, priors are a foundational element of the research process, providing what amounts to a road map for future research. We sought to develop a novel pedigree-based biometric approach for illuminating mtDNA contributions to the total variance in a trait, with the goal of developing priors regarding the effects of mtDNA. Open-source tools for this approach are freely available in the BGmisc package42 https://r-computing-lab.github.io/BGmisc/, and will be useful for biomedical researchers interested in possible mtDNA effects on any number of phenotypes and diseases.

Using this approach, we found compelling evidence that individual differences in longevity are partially a function of differences in their inherited mtDNA. Indeed, despite the tiny size of mtDNA, at least 5% of observed individual differences in longevity could be explained by differences in their segregating mtDNA. Moreover, sharing the maternal line of a longevous relative translated to an average of 11.3 months extra years of life. Such findings offer key insights into the matrilineal origins of human longevity, and in doing so, provide a meaningful ‘prior’ for future molecular genetic studies of mtDNA effects on longevity and related diseases of ageing. Large-scale mtDNA genome-wide association and mtDNA pathway studies are thus poised to significantly advance our understanding of the origins of longevity, particularly when complemented by transgenic animal experiments focused on longevity.
