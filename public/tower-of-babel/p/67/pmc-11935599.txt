Distinct Phenotypic Groups and Related Clinical Outcomes in Patients With Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Soongu Kwak, Jihoon Kim, Chan‐Soon Park, Hyun‐Jung Lee, Jun‐Bean Park, Seung‐Pyo Lee, Yong‐Jin Kim, Hyung‐Kwan Kim, Sang‐Chol Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a heterogeneous disorder with varying risks of clinical outcomes, including sudden cardiac death (SCD). We aimed to identify distinct phenotypes among patients with HCM in relation to SCD risk factors, interpret their clinical characteristics, and examine their outcomes.Methods and ResultsThis retrospective study analyzed 1231 consecutive patients with HCM from 2 tertiary hospitals. We performed latent class analysis to categorize patients into phenotypic groups. Three distinct phenotypic groups were identified using latent class analysis. Group 1 (n=554) consisted of young patients with HCM with minimal SCD risk factors and favorable cardiac remodeling. Group 2 (n=114) comprised young patients with HCM and a high prevalence of SCD risk factors, whereas Group 3 (n=563) included older patients (median age, 68 years). Over a median 6.5‐year follow‐up, 34 SCD‐related events, 131 cardiovascular events, 133 all‐cause mortality events, and 70 noncardiovascular mortality events were observed. Group 2 exhibited the highest rate of SCD‐related events (5‐year SCD rate: Group 1 versus 2 versus 3: 0.8% versus 8.2% versus 4.0%, respectively, P<0.001), and cardiovascular events were more frequent in Groups 2 and 3 compared with Group 1. All‐cause and noncardiovascular mortality were the most frequent in Group 3. A simplified decision tree was developed for the straightforward assignment of phenotypic group membership, demonstrating fair concordance.ConclusionsThis study identified 3 distinct clinical phenotypes in patients with HCM, each associated with different SCD risks and outcomes. Data‐driven phenotyping of patients with HCM offers effective risk stratification and may optimize patient management.Keywords: cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic; death, sudden, cardiac; latent class analysis; mortalitySubject Categories: Echocardiography, Cardiomyopathy

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is an inheritable cardiomyopathy characterized by abnormal thickening of the left ventricle (LV) in the absence of secondary causes. 1 A substantial proportion of patients with HCM experiences one or more HCM‐related complications during their clinical course, including progressive heart failure, symptomatic atrial fibrillation, and sudden cardiac death (SCD). 2 Although the burden of SCD among patients with HCM may be lower than expected, 3 , 4 , 5 it remains one of the most devastating complications, and identifying individuals at high risk for SCD is clinically important for the implementation of optimal prevention strategies. With advancements in the treatment of patients with HCM, noncardiac mortality also constitutes a significant proportion of patient outcomes. 6 , 7 , 8

The population with HCM demonstrates significant diversity in age, comorbidities, and cardiac remodeling patterns. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 An increasing number of patients are being diagnosed with HCM at an older age and exhibit a high prevalence of cardiovascular comorbidities, less severe LV hypertrophy, and a lower incidence of SCD. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Regarding cardiac remodeling, it is important to note that conditions such as LV outflow tract obstruction, LV apical aneurysm, reduced LV systolic function, and left atrial (LA) remodeling often coexist in patients with HCM and are associated with a higher risk of SCD and related complications. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Given the substantial heterogeneity, identifying meaningful patterns involving these risk factors may provide valuable insights for predicting the risk of SCD and delivering tailored care to patients with HCM.

Latent class analysis (LCA) is a probabilistic modeling technique that reveals hidden patterns and subgroups within a data set. 20 , 21 Based on observed variables, each participant is assigned to the group with the highest likelihood of membership. Because this grouping arises from the inherent data structure, categorization is not confined to the current framework and often provides valuable insight into the patterns within a heterogeneous population. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24

This study aimed to identify the distinct patient phenotypes within the population with HCM using LCA. Our goal was to provide essential insight into the clinically meaningful patterns of SCD risk factors in patients with HCM and their association with adverse clinical outcomes.

METHODS

The data underlying this article will be shared on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Cohort Characteristics

This study enrolled adult patients diagnosed with HCM between 2007 and 2020 at 2 tertiary university hospitals (Seoul National University Hospital and Samsung Medical Center). All patients met the standard diagnostic criteria for HCM, including a hypertrophied, nondilated LV with a maximal wall thickness ≥15 mm without abnormal loading conditions sufficient to explain LV hypertrophy; a more limited wall thickness of ≥13 mm was used as a cutoff in cases of a family history of HCM. 25 The exclusion criteria included age < 19 years, congenital heart disease, significant valvular heart disease, and end‐stage renal disease. Patients with Noonan syndrome, Fabry disease, glycogen storage disease, cardiac amyloidosis, or mitochondrial disease were excluded.

This study conformed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of Seoul National University Hospital, which waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the analyses and the use of anonymized data.

Collection of the Clinical and Imaging Data

Clinical information, including risk factors for SCD in HCM, was collected at the time of the HCM diagnosis, following the 2014 European Society of Cardiology and 2020 American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology guidelines. 25 , 26 The 5‐year SCD risk score was calculated according to the 2014 European Society of Cardiology guideline. 26 Nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) was defined as ≥3 consecutive ventricular beats at ≥120 beats per minute, lasting <30 seconds. 26 Standard echocardiography evaluation was performed at the time of the HCM diagnosis. The maximal LV wall thickness was measured at the thickest portion of the myocardium at end‐diastole. The maximal LV outflow tract gradient was recorded as the largest value using continuous‐wave Doppler at rest or during the Valsalva maneuver. An LV apical aneurysm was defined as a segment of the LV apex with thin walls and dyskinetic or akinetic movements detected by echocardiography or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging. LV global longitudinal strain (LV‐GLS) and LA reservoir strain were measured at a core laboratory using vendor‐independent, postprocessing software (Imaging Arena 4.6, TomTec Imaging Systems, Munich, Germany). 16 , 17 , 18 Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) images were obtained 10 to 15 minutes after intravenous gadolinium contrast administration. Regions of LGE were identified as areas with a signal intensity exceeding 6 SDs of the normal myocardium, and the percentage of the total LGE mass divided by the myocardial mass was reported.

Latent Class Analysis

LCA is a statistical method used to identify unobserved (latent) subgroups or classes of individuals based on their responses to a set of observed categorical variables. 20 , 21 , 27 Based on maximum likelihood estimation, LCA aims to identify a limited number of latent classes that effectively capture the underlying patterns of variation observed in the data. Here, we used a random‐effects LCA that incorporated normally distributed subject‐level random effects into the model. This incorporation deals with the potential conditional dependence among covariates and can result in more relevant and interpretable class solutions. 28 , 29

Because our study aimed to identify distinct subgroups of HCM based on patterns of SCD risk factors, we selected 9 variables that are established risk factors for SCD, as outlined in the guidelines. 25 , 26 There were no missing values among the variables included in the LCA except for nonsustained VT data, which had a 27% missingness rate (Figure S1). To meet the categorical variable requirement for the LCA, we transformed continuous variables into binary variables using the median, quartile value, or clinically relevant thresholds, while avoiding categories with less than 10% of the entire sample. The final categories and rationale for the categorization are summarized in Table S1. An LV ejection fraction cutoff of <60% was chosen due to the very low proportion of patients with <50% (2.1%), and based on previous studies demonstrating that a low‐normal ejection fraction (50–60%) is a risk factor for higher cardiovascular events. 19 The optimal number of groups was determined based on the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria, the proportion of each group, and clinical interpretability (Figure S2). 20 , 21

Outcome Assessment

We assessed 4 clinical outcomes within the phenotypic groups: SCD‐related events, cardiovascular events, all‐cause mortality, and noncardiovascular mortality. SCD‐related events included SCD, aborted SCD, and appropriate implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator shock delivered to terminate the fatal ventricular arrhythmia. Cardiovascular events include SCD‐related events, heart failure admission, heart transplantation, and cardiovascular death. Cardiovascular death was defined as SCD or death attributable to heart failure, myocardial infarction, major vascular disease, or cerebrovascular events. Noncardiovascular death was defined as any mortality other than cardiovascular death, including deaths attributed to cancer or infection. Clinical events and mortality were collected based on the electronic medical records at each study center and the official death records provided by Statistics Korea.

Simplified Decision Tree for Assigning Phenotypic Group Membership

To improve clinical applicability and interpretability, we built a simplified decision tree to assign a phenotypic group membership to each individual. We used recursive partitioning and regression tree techniques with age and SCD risk score variables, taking into account the substantial differences in these features across the 3 groups. The concordance between the original phenotypic group membership and the simplified decision tree was evaluated using the accuracy and the F1 score.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as median values with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted for the phenotypic groups and compared using the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazard regression analysis was used to assess the association between phenotypic groups and the risk of clinical events, with results reported as hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CIs. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was adjusted for baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and body mass index. The Cox proportionality assumption was tested using the Schoenfeld residuals.

Statistical significance was set at a 2‐tailed value of P<0.05. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Cohort Characteristics

In total, 1231 patients diagnosed with HCM were included in the study (median age, 59 years; 73% men). Among them, 120 (9.7%) had a family history of SCD, 201 (22.3% of those with known VT status) had nonsustained VT, and 147 (11.9%) had a history of syncope. In terms of echocardiographic characteristics, the median LV ejection fraction and LV maximal wall thickness were 65% and 18.0 mm, respectively. A total of 183 patients (14.9%) had an LV outflow tract gradient measured ≥30 mm Hg at rest or with the Valsalva maneuver. The median 5‐year SCD risk score for the entire population was 1.8%.

Clinical Characteristics of 3 Phenotypic Groups

Three phenotypic groups were identified by LCA (Figure S2). Figure 1 illustrates the marginal probability of each variable category for each phenotypic group. Notably, patients in Group 3 exhibited the highest likelihood of being aged ≥60 years, whereas those in Group 2 showed the highest probabilities of having nonsustained VT, syncope and maximal LV wall thickness ≥20 mm.

Cardiac Remodeling Characteristics of 3 Phenotypic Groups

Group 1 exhibited the most favorable cardiac structure and function among the 3 groups, characterized by the highest LV ejection fraction and the lowest LA dimension and volume (Table 2). Notably, the most significant echocardiographic finding in Group 2 was the thickest LV maximal wall thickness (Group 1 versus 2 versus 3: 18.0 versus 20.0 versus 18.0 mm, P<0.001). LV apical aneurysms were most frequently observed in Group 2 (15.8%), and the percentage of LGE, including the proportion of patients with extensive LGE (≥15%), was the highest in this group (LGE% ≥15%: 34.4% for Group 2). In contrast, patients in Group 3 showed the largest LA size, as indicated by the LA volume index (Group 1 versus 2 versus 3: 38.8 versus 42.9 versus 46.9 mL/m2, P<0.001). The prevalence of a significant LV outflow tract gradient (≥30 mm Hg) was lower in Group 3 than in Groups 1 and 2.

Clinical Outcomes of 3 Phenotypic Groups

During a median follow‐up of 6.5 years (interquartile range, 2.7–9.8 years), we observed 34 (2.8%) SCD‐related events, 131 (10.6%) cardiovascular events, and 133 (10.8%) cases of all‐cause mortality, with 70 (5.7%) attributed to noncardiovascular causes.

Group 2 showed the highest incidence of SCD‐related events, followed by Group 3 and then Group 1 (5‐year cumulative incidence, Group 1 versus 2 versus 3: 0.8% versus 8.2% versus 4.0%, P<0.001; Figure 2A). Groups 2 and 3 showed a higher incidence of cardiovascular events compared with Group 1 (5‐year cumulative incidence, Group 1 versus 2 versus 3: 2.7% versus 13.3% versus 11.8%, P<0.001; Figure 2B). All‐cause mortality was most frequently observed in Group 3 (5‐year cumulative incidence, Group 1 versus 2 versus 3: 3.3% versus 5.2% versus 12.3%, P<0.001; Figure 2C). Patients in Group 3 also had the highest rate of noncardiovascular mortality (5‐year cumulative incidence, Group 1 versus 2 versus 3: 2.6% versus 2.1% versus 6.6%, P<0.001; Figure 2D).

Simplified Decision Tree for Assigning Phenotypic Group Membership

A simplified decision tree to assign phenotypic group membership is shown in Figure 3A. Group A (age <60 years and SCD risk score <4%) corresponded to Group 1, Group B (age <60 years and SCD risk score ≥4%) corresponded to Group 2, and Group C (age ≥60 years) to Group 3. The groups divided by the decision tree aligned well with the original groups derived from LCA (accuracy 92.8%, F1 score 0.892; Figure 3B). The groups classified using the decision tree also demonstrated distinct associations with clinical outcomes, with Group B being associated with higher SCD‐related events and Group C with higher all‐cause and noncardiovascular mortality (Figure S3).

Sensitivity Analysis by Study Center

We conducted a separate LCA within each study center (Table S2). The 3 groups identified by LCA among patients from Seoul National University Hospital generally corresponded to the results from the total population, consisting of 2 groups of young patients with varying risks of SCD and an older group (Table S3). Similarly, a higher incidence of SCD‐related and cardiovascular events was noted for Group 2, whereas all‐cause mortality and noncardiovascular mortality were most frequent in Group 3 (Figure S4). A similar pattern was observed in the analysis of patients from Samsung Medical Center (Table S4 and Figure S4).

DISCUSSION

We identified 3 distinct phenotypes using LCA. The 3 phenotypic groups displayed significant age differences, with the 2 younger groups showing varying levels of SCD risk. Notably, SCD‐related events most frequently occurred in the younger group with a high SCD risk (Group 2), whereas all‐cause mortality and noncardiovascular mortality were predominantly observed among older patients with HCM (Group 3). The simplified decision tree facilitates the straightforward assignment of phenotypic group membership, enhancing clinical applicability and generalizability. This study provides valuable insight into the patterns of SCD risk factors within a heterogeneous population with HCM and their associated clinical outcomes, offering the potential for more tailored treatment and prevention strategies for patients with HCM. As most patients with HCM have a normal lifespan, 30 there is a growing subset of patients with HCM who present with both cardiovascular and noncardiovascular comorbidities associated with the natural process of aging. 10 , 11 , 31 Cardiac remodeling also varies significantly among patients with HCM, including LV systolic and diastolic function, LA dilation, and the presence of obstructive physiology. 32 These clinical and echocardiographic risk factors are closely associated with the clinical outcomes in patients with HCM. 25 , 26 Therefore, identifying unique patterns of these risk factors within the population with HCM holds the potential to provide novel insights for optimizing patient management strategies.

Group 2, although the smallest in size, consisted of young patients at a high risk of SCD, with a high prevalence of nonsustained VT, unexplained syncope, and the greatest LV maximal wall thickness among the 3 groups. Consequently, this group had the highest incidences of SCD‐related events. Recent studies have suggested that the SCD event rate in HCM may be lower than previously reported. 3 , 4 , 5 Maron et al. reported that in a cohort of 1000 patients with HCM, 40 HCM‐related deaths occurred over a mean follow‐up of 7.2 years (0.53% per patient‐year), of which only 17 deaths were arrhythmic SCD (0.23% per patient‐year). 4 However, this event rate appears higher than the SCD rate of 0.03% to 0.10% per patient‐year among the general population. 33 , 34 Because SCD is the most devastating complication of HCM, there are ongoing efforts to identify clinical and imaging risk factors to stratify patients according to their level of SCD risk. Notably, Group 2 showed significantly lower levels of absolute LV‐GLS and LA reservoir strain compared with other groups. Recent studies have highlighted the significant predictive value of these novel imaging markers for adverse outcomes in patients with HCM. 16 , 17 , 18 Although the majority of patients in all 3 groups had normal LV ejection fractions (>60%), the LV‐GLS values fell below the normal range, 35 particularly in Group 2. Group 2 also exhibited the lowest LA reservoir strain. These findings may imply that LV‐GLS and LA reservoir strain reflect the early impairment of systolic and diastolic functions in HCM. The combined use of LV‐GLS and LA reservoir strain has been reported to improve the prediction of patients with HCM at a higher risk of SCD, even among those already categorized as high‐risk candidates for implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator implantation according to the guidelines. 36

The survival rate in Group 3 was the lowest among the phenotypic groups, primarily because of advanced patient age. Notably, approximately half of the mortality cases in Group 3 were attributed to noncardiovascular causes. This finding aligns with recent data demonstrating that noncardiovascular deaths, such as cancer, constitute a significant proportion of deaths in patients with HCM, 8 particularly in older patients. 7 , 9 This observation may be attributed to the fact that patients with HCM presenting at an advanced age tend to have a higher prevalence of nonfamilial HCM, which is associated with milder cardiac remodeling and a lower incidence of SCD. 37 Thus, although current management guidelines primarily focus on preventing HCM‐related events such as SCD and heart failure, it is crucial to recognize that noncardiovascular mortality also significantly affects prognosis, particularly in older patients with HCM.

The current study revealed distinct clusters in heterogeneous populations with HCM. Recent advancements in data‐driven analytical methods have led to the redefinition of complex disease phenotypes, the discovery of new therapeutic targets, and the potential enhancement of targeted therapeutic interventions. This innovative approach in the population with HCM may help clinicians deliver more precise and personalized care. Future studies are required to ascertain whether clinical decisions in HCM, including SCD prevention, can be improved through comprehensive phenotyping of patients with HCM.

Limitations

First, despite the inclusion of a large cohort with HCM, the LCA results might have been influenced by the baseline cohort characteristics. Nevertheless, the LCA conducted at each study center yielded similar results, despite significant differences in baseline characteristics. Second, to ensure generalizability, investigating whether the categorization by the decision tree yields similar phenotypes and related clinical outcomes in an external population with HCM would strengthen our findings. Third, SCD‐related events were rare, consistent with findings from contemporary populations with HCM. 4 Therefore, assessing whether the phenotypic categorization of LCA provides additional predictive information for SCD‐related events is necessary in a cohort with higher SCD‐related event rates. Fourth, there were some missing data on nonsustained VT status (Figure S1). However, a sensitivity analysis excluding those with missing nonsustained VT data produced similar results, demonstrating the robustness of our findings (Table S5 and Figure S5). Fifth, we did not use the established cutoff values for LV ejection fraction (<50%) and LV maximal wall thickness (≥30 mm) because the proportion of patients with these conditions was too low to fit into the model (2.1% and 2.3%, respectively). However, using these cutoffs in LCA also yielded similar phenotypes (young patients with low and high SCD risk and older patients; Table S6). Lastly, we were unable to incorporate more detailed information on HCM, including genetic information. Incorporating these data may provide additional insights into the relationship between phenotypic groups and genotypes, as well as the genotype–phenotype relationship within each group.

CONCLUSIONS

The 3 distinct phenotypic groups in the population with HCM exhibited significant differences in clinical and echocardiographic characteristics with different risks for SCD and clinical outcomes. Data‐driven patient grouping in HCM holds the potential to improve risk stratification and guide patient management.
