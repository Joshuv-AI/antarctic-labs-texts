Non-apoptotic regulated cell death based prognostic risk model for colorectal cancer using machine learning guided two-step framework
Authors: Avik Sengupta, Sushree Sangita Kar, Rahul Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractColorectal cancer (CRC) prognosis is severely limited by tumor heterogeneity. To address this, we leveraged the emerging role of non-apoptotic regulated cell death (NARCD) pathways to develop a two-step machine learning (ML) framework to develop a prognostic risk model. We used two largest and independent CRC patient cohorts (TCGA and E-MTAB-12862). Our novel pipeline consists of two steps, first, a library of 46 combination ML survival models was applied to each of the 13 NARCD pathways to establish robust, pathway-specific prognostic models. Second, logistic regression was used to integrate these models, revealing that the synergistic combination of ferroptosis, NETosis, pyroptosis, and autosis yielded the highest predictive power. The resulting 43-gene prognostic risk model, the combined-regulated cell death index (c-RCDI), robustly stratified patients and proved to be a powerful independent prognostic factor (HR = 55.1, P < 0.001), with high predictive accuracy (5-year AUROC = 0.88). Notably, this prognostic power was exclusive to CRC. Biologically, the high-risk class showed enriched angiogenesis and EMT pathways, an immunosuppressive microenvironment, reduced immunotherapy response, and predicted increased sensitivity to CDK, MEK, and metabolic inhibitors, while the low-risk class showed increased sensitivity to the drug “Obatoclax Mesylate_1068”. c-RCDI developed in this study using a novel two-step ML framework based on NARCD pathways showed robust predictive ability for CRC patients, with a potential for improving diagnosis and therapy.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), as per GLOBOCAN 2022, is ranked as the third-most prevalent cancer with an incidence rate of 9.6% and as the second-most death-causing cancer (9.3%) among all the cancer types worldwide [1]. Though the 5-year survival of CRC has improved throughout the previous years due to early diagnosis, surgical precision, improved chemotherapy and radiotherapy, clinical improvements are still needed [2]. CRC heterogeneity of molecular and genetic mechanisms still holds the baton for inaccurate survival predictions for CRC patients [3]. Also, the prognostic factors generally associated with CRC patients are sex, age, and tumor stage, which may not lead to an effective diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, novel survival-based methods to predict patient prognosis are the need-of-the-hour to boost the personalized therapeutic strategies for CRC [4].

Currently, studies are exploring the pathogenesis and therapeutic resistance of CRC being linked to dysregulation of non-apoptotic regulated cell death (NARCD) pathways, including pyroptosis, ferroptosis, cuproptosis, and others [5–8]. Unlike apoptosis, which is frequently impaired in CRC due to genetic and epigenetic alterations, these non-canonical pathways offer alternative mechanisms for targeting cancer cells that evade traditional treatments [9, 10]. NARCD-based research is driven by the need to overcome apoptosis resistance, leverage immune-stimulatory effects of cell death, and exploit CRC-specific vulnerabilities for more effective treatment strategies. Studies have demonstrated that pyroptosis, through gasdermin-mediated cytokine release, enhances antitumor immunity in CRC, while necroptosis and ferroptosis exploit unique metabolic and microenvironmental vulnerabilities [11]. Cuproptosis-related genes, e.g. are dysregulated in CRC through epigenetic mechanisms, with therapeutic copper ionophores and chelators showing promise for targeted treatment strategies [12]. In CRC, multi risk models, such as ferroptosis-, pyroptosis-, and cuproptosis-related gene signatures [7, 13, 14] have shown superior predictive power for patient outcomes and therapy responses compared to single-gene markers. These signatures capture the complexity of pathway-level dysregulation and immune interactions, providing robust methods for patient stratification and personalized therapy. Cao et al. [15] demonstrated that integrating multiple regulated cell death pathways into a unified prognostic signature significantly improved outcome prediction in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, highlighting the promise of multipathway approach for prediction of cancer prognosis. In recent years, the rapid development of machine learning (ML) algorithms as transformative tools in oncology enabled the development of robust prognostic signature using multimodal data [15]. Unlike conventional Cox regression, ML survival models capture non-linear relationships and high-dimensional interactions between clinical, molecular, and imaging features. For instance, Xun et al., employed 97 ML algorithms to construct a 13-gene CRC signature, outperforming traditional models in survival prediction and immune microenvironment characterization [16]. Another study by Liu et al. showed the development of an immune-related lncRNA signature (IRLS) from 101 ML algorithms [17]. These approaches address tumor heterogeneity and non-proportional hazards, enabling dynamic modeling of pathway crosstalk (e.g. RCD-immune interactions) and sample-specific prognostic patterns. By automating gene selection from complex datasets, ML frameworks advance precision oncology, shifting from single-level biomarkers to systems-level signatures that guide personalized therapeutic strategies.

This study capitalized on these findings to utilize ML-based survival models to identify a 43-gene prognostic risk model, called the combined-regulated cell death index (c-RCDI), using a novel two-step ML framework. For a comprehensive study, we used two largest CRC expression datasets, i.e. E-MTAB-12862 and TCGA datasets, to develop the prognostic risk model. Further, we validated the prognostic value of the c-RCDI on two independent datasets. The c-RCDI effectively stratifies patients into low- and high-risk classes, providing a robust framework to systematically investigate differential immune infiltration, immunotherapy response, and drug sensitivity. Crucially, the c-RCDI also serves as an independent prognostic factor for predicting CRC survival. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate ML-based survival models with 13 NARCD pathways to identify a prognostic risk model for CRC, developed utilizing a novel two-step ML framework. Methods

Data collection and preprocessing

Gene expression data for primary tumor CRC were obtained from multiple publicly available RNA-seq and microarray datasets. E-MTAB-12862 dataset (n = 1063) containing RNA-seq raw counts and transcripts per million (TPM)-normalized data was downloaded from ArrayExpress database (website: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/arrayexpress/studies/E-MTAB-12862, accessed: August 26, 2024) [18]. RNA-seq data from GDC The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-COAD (n = 476) and GDC TCGA-READ (n = 170), were retrieved from the UCSC Xena Browser (website: https://xenabrowser.net/datapages, accessed: September 5, 2024). The two TCGA datasets were merged using z-scores normalization to generate a combined “TCGA-CRC” dataset. The datasets GSE39582 (n = 585) and GSE161158 (n = 250) containing microarray data were downloaded in “CEL” format from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (website: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/, accessed: September 12, 2024 and September 24, 2024 respectively). For all datasets, we excluded the samples with overall survival (OS) time or disease-free survival (DFS) time < 1 month or inconsistent data (i.e. NA values), to ensure data quality. We further combined E-MTAB-12862 and TCGA-CRC (z-score normalization) to create a unified RNA-seq dataset with 1655 samples. We split this dataset into 80% (n = 1300) for training and 20% (n = 355) for test dataset for downstream analyses. The datasets GSE39582 and GSE161158 were used as independent validation cohorts. The training dataset was used for the identification of NARCD-based prognostic risk model. Clinical characteristics of patients with complete data across datasets are summarized in Table 1. Breast cancer (BRCA), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), and skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) datasets were also downloaded from UCSC Xena Browser.

We collected the 13 NARCD pathway genes from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) [19, 20] and literature study (Supplementary Table S1). The pathways are, namely, anoikis (n = 34), autophagy (n = 380), autosis (n = 65), cuproptosis (n = 64), entosis (n = 55), ferroptosis (n = 259), immunogenic cell death (ICD, n = 534), lysosomal cell death (LCD, n = 218), necroptosis (n = 209), NETosis (n = 247), oxeiptosis (n = 88), parthanatos (n = 132), and, pyroptosis (n = 57).

Identification of differentially expressed genes

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across the two transcriptomics datasets i.e. E-MTAB-12862 and TCGA-CRC were identified using the “limma” package in R, with the cutoffs, log2 fold-change > 0.5 and Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) adjusted P < 0.05. Further, we listed the common DEGs from these two datasets to develop the prognostic risk model. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), Gene Ontology (GO), and REACTOME analysis were performed with a cutoff adjusted P-value (BH) < 0.05, using “clusterProfiler” (v4.16.0) [21], “org.Hs.eg.db” (v3.21.0) [22], and “ReactomePA” (v1.52.0) [23] packages in R respectively.

Development of a combined prognostic risk model

To develop our prognostic risk model, we implemented a novel two-step ML framework. In the first step, we aimed to identify a robust prognostic risk model for each of the 13 NARCD pathways independently. To achieve this, we applied a curated library of 46 model combinations to the DEGs within each pathway. This library was systematically constructed to be computationally feasible while representing diverse survival algorithms and testing established “filter-and-select” pipelines, returned post filtering of model combinations with robust performance across all four datasets (C-index > 0.56) [17]. These combinations were derived from nine distinct survival models, including the classical univariate Cox model, embedded ML methods (LASSO, Ridge, CoxBoost, StepCox), and advanced ensemble ML techniques (RSF, GBM, SuperPC, plsRcox). These nine distinct and 46 survival model combinations are enlisted in Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Table S3 respectively. For each pathway, all 46 model combinations were systematically evaluated based on their predictive power, as measured by the Concordance Index (C-index) [24]. We created a pool of robust candidates by filtering for model combinations with a mean C-index ≥ 0.6 across all four (training, test and two validation) datasets. From this final pool, the optimal model combination was selected based on the highest mean C-index, with parsimony (≤15 genes) used as a tie-breaker. In the second step, we constructed the final, unified model. Following a strategy similar to Cao et al. [15], we employed a logistic regression algorithm to systematically evaluate combinations of 2–4 of the independent NARCD pathway prognostic risk models developed in step one. The best-performing combination, as determined by the highest C-index, was established as our final prognostic risk model (i.e. the prognostic risk score), termed the “combined-regulated cell death index” (c-RCDI).

Prognostic performance and validation of the c-RCDI

We checked the predictive capability of the c-RCDI by dividing the CRC patients into high-risk and low-risk based on the optimal cutoff value of c-RCDI using “surv_cutpoint()” function of “survival” package in R [25, 26]. Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival analysis was then conducted on the training cohort using the “survival” and “survminer” [27] packages in R to assess survival differences between low- and high- risk classes. Time-dependent area under receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) analysis was performed at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals using the “timeROC” package in R [28] and also compared the variation of survival time with c-RCDI. For independent validation, we used two microarray-based validation datasets i.e. GSE39582 and GSE161158. We performed hierarchical clustering using the expression profiles of the c-RCDI genes to confirm its biological relevance, using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package in R [29]. The resulting patient clusters were then correlated with the c-RCDI-defined risk classes to validate predictive capability of the c-RCDI.

Evaluation of the c-RCDI genes

First, we compared the expression of the c-RCDI genes in CRC and normal samples in both training and test datasets. Multivariate Cox analysis of these genes was performed using “coxph()” function in “survival” package in R to understand the cumulative effect of each gene [30]. To analyze the protein–protein interactions of the c-RCDI genes, we employed the STRING database (v12.0) [31] and further implemented “CytoHubba” (v0.1) extension of “Cytoscape” (v3.10.3) to identify the hub genes within the network. For cell-type specific expression, we performed single cell analysis using “Seurat” package (v5.0) in R [32] on the dataset GSE132465 comprising of 23 primary CRC tumor and 10 normal samples. Using “DepMap” portal (v. DepMap Public 23Q4) [33, 34], we downloaded the CRISPR-Cas9 based knockout screens data and performed gene dependency analysis. Further, for identifying gene modules and studying systems-level gene co-similarities and prognostic correlation, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) analysis in R [35], utilizing a soft-powering threshold of β = 4, to ensure robust module structure.

Assessment of independent prognostic value and clinical utility of c-RCDI

To ascertain the independent prognostic value of the c-RCDI, its performance was evaluated against three established clinical covariates, namely, age group, sex, and tumor stage. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, incorporating the c-RCDI and all clinical variables, was constructed to determine its significance after adjusting for other factors. Furthermore, the predictive accuracy of the c-RCDI relative to these covariates was assessed via time-dependent ROC analysis at 1-, 3-, and 5-year intervals. Finally, a nomogram was constructed using the “regplot” R package [36] to integrate all these variables, thereby providing a graphical tool for estimating individual patient survival probability.

Immune cells enrichment analysis

To further showcase the differences between the low- and high-risk classes, we quantified the fractions of the 22 immune infiltrating cells using the “CIBERSORTx” algorithm [37]. Analysis parameters were set according to data type: B-mode batch correction was used for RNA-seq data, while quantile normalization was applied to microarray data, with both run in absolute mode with 1000 permutations. To estimate the tumor microenvironment within the risk classes, we applied the Estimation of STromal and Immune cells in MAlignant Tumor tissues using Expression data (ESTIMATE) algorithm using “estimate” package in R, to calculate the “ESTIMATE Score”, “Immune Score”, and “Stromal Score” to check the degree of infiltration by the CRC samples [38]. We also calculated the correlation between the gene expression of the c-RCDI genes and their immune infiltration from the “GSCA Lite” tool [39].

Tumor immunotherapy analysis

We used the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm [40] to determine the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade therapy on the low- and high-risk classes. TIDE algorithm generates a predictive score by integrating gene expression signatures of T-cell dysfunction and exclusion, the two primary mechanisms of tumor immune escape.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To identify the effective drugs against the low- and high-risk classes, we calculated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values from the gene expression with GDSC2 dataset, using “oncoPredict()” R package [41]. We identified the candidate drugs showing differential sensitivity between the risk classes (with P < 0.05 and median logIC50 < 1.5). Further, using “GSCA Lite” tool, we analyzed the relationship between drug effect and c-RCDI gene expression [39].

Evaluation of risk class separability using machine learning classifiers

To assess the separability of the c-RCDI-defined risk classes, we evaluated six ML classifier models, namely, Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest Classifier (RFC), XGBoost, K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Naive Bayes. These classifiers were trained to distinguish between these low- and high-risk classes using the expression profiles of the 43 c-RCDI genes as input features, within a stratified 10-fold cross-validation framework. Class imbalance was addressed using the “imblearn” library. To gain a consolidated feature importance profile for c-RCDI genes, we calculated the mean absolute SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values from all the six classifiers, permutation importance scores for the non-tree-based classifiers (Logistic Regression, SVM, KNN, and Naive Bayes), and the intrinsic feature importance scores from the Random Forest and XGBoost classifiers. All classifier models were developed in Python (v3.9.16) using “Scikit-learn”. “Pandas” and “NumPy” were used for data handling, and “Matplotlib” and “Seaborn” were used for data visualization.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses in this study were performed using R (v4.5.1). To create boxplots, violin plots and correlation plots, we used “ggplot2()” and “ggpubr()” functions in R. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) was used to study statistical correlations. We performed student’s t-test method for statistical significance. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Benjamini-Hochberg method to control false discovery rate (FDR).

Web application for c-RCDI calculation

We also developed a free and interactive R Shiny web application that automatically calculates a patient's c-RCDI risk score from gene expression data, to complement its clinical applicability, publicly available at: https://asgda.shinyapps.io/r_shiny/.

RESULTS

Identification and pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

We conducted differential gene expression (DGE) analysis independently on E-MTAB-12862 and TCGA-CRC datasets and identified DEGs using thresholds of adjusted P < 0.05 and log2 fold-change > 0.5. Total of 8587 and 12,686 DEGs were identified for E-MTAB-12862 and TCGA-CRC datasets respectively (Fig. 2A and B). Among these two datasets, 6253 overlapping DEGs (2725 upregulated, 3528 downregulated) were identified for downstream analysis (Fig. 2C). We further intersected these common 6253 DEGs with the 13 NARCD pathway genes to identify the NARCD pathway genes, which we used for the development of the prognostic risk model. The complete list of the 13 NARCD pathways along with their DEG profiles are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

GO analysis identified distinct functional enrichments, with upregulated genes involved in ribosome biogenesis (BP), chromosomal localization (CC), and DNA catalytic activity (MF), while downregulated genes were primarily associated with regulation of membrane potential (BP), the immunoglobulin complex (CC), and antigen binding (MF) (Fig. 2D). Similarly, for KEGG analysis, enrichment of cell cycle and calcium signaling pathways were observed for upregulated and downregulated common DEGs respectively, and on performing REACTOME pathway analysis, cell cycle checkpoints and neuronal system pathways were enriched for upregulated and downregulated genes, respectively (Fig. 2E and F). The number of enrichment terms for each functional category have been enlisted in Supplementary Table S5. Independent pathway enrichment analysis revealed consistent GO, KEGG, and REACTOME pathway alterations between E-MTAB-12862 and TCGA cohorts, thus corroborating to our multidataset analysis, demonstrating upregulation of cell cycle checkpoints, rRNA processing, and mitotic processes, while downregulating neuronal system, muscle contraction, cardiac conduction, and GPCR ligand binding pathways, confirming reproducible molecular signatures in CRC (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Development and evaluation of the prognostic risk model

In the first step of our framework, the prognostic risk model for the ferroptosis pathway, constructed via the LASSO and RSF algorithms, yielded the highest predictive performance with a mean C-index of 0.674 across four datasets. The complete results obtained after first step of our ML framework detailing the optimal models for all 13 NARCD pathways are provided in Supplementary Table S6 and their performances are shown in Fig. 3A. The final prognostic risk model resulting from second step of the framework consisted of combination of ferroptosis, NETosis, pyroptosis, and autosis pathways achieving the highest mean C-index of 0.6981 across all four datasets (Fig. 3B).

The c-RCDI consists of 43 genes, detailed in Supplementary Table S8 including their brief functional and CRC prognosis landscape from literature study. The c-RCDI demonstrated superior predictive accuracy (mean C-index: 0.698) compared to individual NARCD pathway prognostic risk models, namely, ferroptosis (0.674), NETosis (0.666), pyroptosis (0.652), and autosis (0.642) (Supplementary Fig. S2A). KM analysis demonstrated significant prognostic stratification with hazard ratios of 8.29 (training, P < 0.0001) and 3.22 (test, P < 0.0001), confirming improved outcomes in low-risk patients (Fig. 3C and D). Patients were stratified with optimal cutpoints of 0.29 (training) and 0.36 (test) into low-risk (< cutpoint) versus high-risk (≥cutpoint) classes (Fig. 3E). The AUROC values of c-RCDI were calculated for 1-year (train: 0.86, test: 0.71), 3 years (train: 0.87, test: 0.69) and 5-years (train: 0.88, test: 0.69), which showed high prediction accuracy (Fig. 3F). Further, correlation analysis between c-RCDI and survival time in both training and testing datasets revealed a significant inverse relationship, demonstrating that elevated risk scores correspond to substantially diminished patient survival outcomes (Fig. 3G). Similarly, KM survival analysis with optimal cutpoints, time-dependent AUROC performance metrics, and c-RCDI-survival correlation assessments in validation cohorts GSE39582 and GSE161158 demonstrated the model's robustness in risk stratifications, with high-risk patients exhibiting significantly poor clinical outcomes compared to low-risk counterparts (Supplementary Figs S2B–E). Further, the median survival between high-risk and low-risk classes is significantly different, with high-risk class patients (P < 2e-16, training; P = .00025, test; P = 3.9e-05, GSE39582; P = .051, GSE161158) having significantly poor prognosis than low-risk classes (Supplementary Fig. S2F).

Evaluation of c-RCDI in other cancers

To assess whether c-RCDI is specific to CRC, we implemented the c-RCDI on four other cancer types from TCGA i.e. breast cancer (BRCA, n = 1059), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM, n = 145), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD, n = 177), and skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM, n = 98). We found that c-RCDI is not able to stratify the patients into low- and high-risk classes in any of these four cancer types (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. S3). This observation indicates the exclusivity of c-RCDI and its robustness for CRC.

Hierarchical clustering reveals prognostic importance of the c-RCDI

On performing hierarchical clustering on the four datasets based on the gene expression of the c-RCDI genes using “ConsensusClusterPlus” package in R, we observed the optimal number of clusters to be two (i.e. k = 2) (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S4A). We plotted the results using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and found distinct separation between cluster 1 and cluster 2 (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4B). Also, we extrapolated the clusters for their clinical data and observed that one cluster has significantly poor prognosis than the other (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. S4D). Further, we mapped these clusters with the risk classes in all the four datasets and identified that majority of the high-risk class patients correspond to cluster with poor survival (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S4E).

Evaluation of the c-RCDI genes

We evaluated the differences in the gene expression levels between the CRC and the normal sample in both E-MTAB-12862 and TCGA datasets (Supplementary Fig. S5A). Except two genes, RGS4 and TP63, all other 41 genes show significant differences across the two datasets between CRC and normal samples (P < 0.05). Further, on checking the gene effects using Cox multivariate analysis as shown in Fig. 5A, we found that the gene STAT1 is the most significant gene with lowest hazard ratio (0.76), whereas the gene YAP1 has the highest hazard ratio (1.21) with P < 0.05. To check the interconnection of these 43 c-RCDI genes, we performed STRING analysis and found that MYC had the highest number of network connections (n = 18) with other genes, and eight genes (MAP1B, SUN2, CDO1, ATP2B4, TRAF3IP2, PLIN4, CLN3, and ALOXE3) did not have any interactions with any other genes (Fig. 5B). Using DepMap data, we analyzed selective vulnerabilities of the 43 genes from CRISPR-KO screens and observed that MYC, CHMP6, AURKA, and CDK4 demonstrated the most pronounced essentiality profiles (CERES scores < −0.5), and metabolic regulator SLC2A3 exhibited heterogeneous effects alongside the cell cycle kinase CDK6 (Fig. 5D). Determination of the c-RCDI as an independent prognostic factor

Multivariate Cox regression analysis of the c-RCDI with the clinical features on the training dataset revealed c-RCDI to have significant difference in survival and a high HR (55.1, Training; 17.1, test; 41.1, GSE39582; 27.07, GSE161158 datasets), along with “Age groups” (≥80/≤65 and 66–79/≤65) and “Tumor stage” (Stage III—IV / Stage I—II) variables also showing significant survival differences (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. S7A). We created a graphical nomogram of the four clinical variables i.e. sex, age, tumor stage, and c-RCDI, to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival. We found profound predictive nature of the c-RCDI, with AUROC of at least 0.70 across all datasets (Fig. 7B and D, and Supplementary Fig. S7B and D). The AUROC values of the risk class variable (training dataset, 0.76–12 months, 0.77–36 months, 0.79–60 months) was greater than the other clinical factors sex, age group and tumor stage as shown in Fig. 7C. The same trend was observed for the test datasets (risk class AUROC ≥ 0.61) (Supplementary Fig. S7C and Supplementary Table S10). Moreover, on performing GSVA enrichment analysis of the HALLMARK and KEGG LEGACY pathways, greater enrichment on pathways leading to CRC carcinogenesis and progression were observed for high-risk compared to low-risk class (Supplementary Fig. S8A, and Supplementary Table S11). The expression of c-RCDI genes across all the CRC samples of four datasets in the form of a heatmap with clinical annotations, is shown in Supplementary Fig. S8B.

Immunotherapy response predictions and correlation with c-RCDI

TIDE analysis revealed that the high-risk class had significantly higher TIDE scores than the low-risk class for all datasets (Fig. 8C and Supplementary Fig. S9B). The TIDE score was found to be positively correlated with the c-RCDI (r = 0.19, P = 1.2e-11, training dataset; r = 0.07, P = .178, test; r = 0.28, P = .000112, GSE39582; r = 0.23, P = 7.72e-08, GSE161158), which signifies the increase in immunotherapy resistance with increasing c-RCDI (Fig. 8D and Supplementary Fig. S9B). Also, the population of non-responders were higher in high-risk class and the non-responders had higher risk scores than the responders (Fig. 8E and Supplementary Fig. S9B). Further, we extrapolated the risk classes with the TIDE score and response status using Sankey plots to understand the relationship across these factors. Patients of low-risk class were seen to have lower TIDE scores and mostly belonged to the responder category (Fig. 8F and Supplementary Fig. S9B). Also, non-responders correspond to higher c-RCDI than the responders across all cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S9B).

Prediction of small-molecule drugs and their correlation with c-RCDI

Using drug sensitivity analysis, we found three drugs i.e. “PD0325901_1060”, “Gemcitabine_1190”, and “CDK9_5038_1709” with median logIC50 < 1.5 for high-risk class (Fig. 8G). The drug “Obatoclax Mesylate_1068” was shown to have median logIC50 < 1.5 for the low-risk class whereas for high-risk class shows median logIC50 > 1.5 (Fig. 8H). “Docetaxel_1007” and “Bortezomib_1191”, show the lowest median logIC50 values (median logIC50 < −4) for low-risk class (Fig. 8I), and Supplementary Fig. S9C shows the rest of the drugs with median logIC50 < 1.5 for low-risk class. Correlation analysis between individual gene expression of c-RCDI genes and drug sensitivity revealed distinct molecular associations (Fig. 8J). Higher expressions of YAP1, CXCL2, and EGFR correlated with increased sensitivity to chemotherapies (e.g. bleomycin, docetaxel) but with resistance to targeted agents (e.g. vorinostat, navitoclax). Conversely, higher expression of TCF7, NFATC1, and SUN2 was associated with increased sensitivity to these targeted therapies.

Evaluation of the functional enrichment of the c-RCDI

Functional pathways analysis of the c-RCDI genes showed that upregulated genes were mostly involved in cell cycle regulation, cell senescence, and p53-mediated signaling, while downregulated genes were associated with cell death and complement activation pathways, thereby highlighting the predictive ability of c-RCDI to capture the most prognostically relevant aspects of CRC pathobiology (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Using ML classifier models, we validated the separability of the c-RCDI-defined high and low-risk classes. Discussion

CRC heterogeneity limits the predictive potential of the traditionally conferred prognostic factors like age, sex and tumor stage, and this also results in inefficiency of individual genes to act as prognostic biomarkers [46]. Confronting this challenge, this study establishes a 43-gene prognostic risk model, called the c-RCDI, as a robust and independent risk model to predict CRC patient survival, developed using a novel two-step ML framework. The c-RCDI robustly stratifies patients into distinct low- and high-risk classes with significantly different survival outcomes, as demonstrated by Kaplan–Meier (KM) analysis (HR = 8.29, P < 0.0001). Furthermore, its exceptional predictive accuracy is complemented by high AUROC values (0.86, 1-year; 0.87, 3-years; 0.88, 5-years), underscoring the robustness of biological signal.

In this study, we identified 43 c-RCDI genes, of these AURKA, CDK4, CXCL2, EGFR, GDF15, GSDMC, IL18, and MYC have been thoroughly investigated and repeatedly confirmed as independent prognostic markers. They operate within core CRC molecular circuits, including NF-κB–driven inflammation, TGF-β–triggered epithelial–mesenchymal transition, WNT/β-catenin transcriptional activation, and tight cyclin-dependent cell-cycle control, all crucial for tumor expansion, immune evasion, and metastasis, therefore suggesting that the c-RCDI successfully captured core oncogenic circuits in CRC [47–54]. This complements that the c-RCDI is not an abstract statistical construct but is grounded in the essential machinery of CRC pathogenesis.

Delving deeper into this machinery, a key strength of our data-driven model is its ability to identify prognostically superior markers for the selected four NARCD pathways over their canonical regulators, GPX4 (ferroptosis), CASP1 (pyroptosis), PAD4 (NETosis), and ATG5 (autosis) [8, 55, 56]. For instance, the c-RCDI offers powerful case studies, capturing ferroptosis through GPX2's role in oxidative defense, pyroptosis via the executor GSDMC, and NETosis with NOX4's function as a direct ROS trigger for trap formation [57–59]. These examples reflect that c-RCDI is a functionally coherent model that prioritizes the most impactful players in CRC biology.

However, the c-RCDI’s novelty lies in identifying under-explored genes that may reveal adaptive strategies of CRC. For instance, ATP2B4 and CHMP6 point to the importance of cellular homeostasis, suggesting a role in calcium-dependent autophagic cell death (autosis) and apoptotic resistance and maintaining plasmalemmal integrity during stress [60, 61]. The model also captures the tumor's influence microenvironment through CR1 and KNG1, which are tied to immune evasion and inflammation [62, 63]. Finally, the model reflects the aggressive nature of metastatic cells, highlighted by MAP1B, an architect of cytoskeletal remodeling, and PLIN4, a manager of the lipid metabolism that fuels metabolic resilience [64, 65]. By combining these diverse biological functions, the c-RCDI gives a prospective picture of CRC aggression and identifies a new set of genes that could be explored as potential biomarkers.

A critical finding of this study is that the c-RCDI is not only biologically coherent but also a remarkably powerful and independent predictor of patient prognosis. In a multivariate Cox regression analysis designed to test its true value, the c-RCDI demonstrated a hazard ratio of 55.1 (P < 0.001). This drastically outweighs the prognostic impact of traditional clinical factors such as tumor stage (HR = 1.5) and age (HR = 2.1–3.3). To translate this into a practical tool for researchers, we developed a nomogram integrating the c-RCDI. This model showed outstanding predictive accuracy, with AUROC values of 0.873, 0.885, and 0.895 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, respectively. This sustained high performance underscores the c-RCDI's real-world potential to refine patient stratification and guide clinical decision-making beyond current standards.

Biologically, the high-risk class is defined by hyperactivated oncogenic pathways. Gene set analysis revealed enrichment of E2F and MYC targets driving cell proliferation, alongside upregulation of EMT and angiogenesis programs that enable metastasis. This aggressive state is compounded by the downregulation of intrinsic antitumor pathways, including cell death and complement activation. This dual gain of oncogenic function and loss of suppression provides a clear mechanistic basis for the poor prognosis of high-risk class [66–72].

The c-RCDI also captures the tumor's immune context. High-risk tumors exhibited elevated ESTIMATE (P < .01) and stromal scores (P < .0001), indicating a dense, immunosuppressive microenvironment known to exclude T-cells suggesting increased invasion through matrix remodeling and lymph node metastasis [73], consistent with studies linking high immune scores to increased immunosuppressive microenvironment [74–77]. Consequently, high-risk class had significantly higher TIDE scores (P = 8.7e-06), strongly predicting non-responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitors. This positions the c-RCDI as a dual prognostic and predictive biomarker for guiding immunotherapy decisions in CRC.

The c-RCDI's robustness was confirmed by its ability to partition patients into distinct prognostic clusters based on c-RCDI genes and by ML classifiers that separated risk groups with high accuracy (AUROC ≥ 0.85). Critically, this prognostic power was exclusive to CRC, as the model failed to stratify patients in four other major cancers, namely BRCA, GBM, PAAD and SKCM. This specificity suggests the c-RCDI has captured the unique context of regulated cell death in the colorectum, rather than a generic proliferation signature, strongly supporting its clinical potential.

Additionally, the c-RCDI provides a rational basis for treatment selection. Pharmacological analysis predicted that high-risk tumors are vulnerable to “CDK9_5038_1709” (CDK inhibitor) [78], “Gemacitabine_1190” (metabolic inhibitor) [79] and “PD0325901_1060” (MEK inhibitor) [80], aligning perfectly with their hyper-proliferative and metabolically reprogrammed state. While requiring experimental validation [81], these findings suggest the c-RCDI can guide personalized therapeutic strategies, especially for patients predicted to fail immunotherapy.

The credibility of our findings rests on our novel two-step ML framework, uniquely designed to ensure both robustness and synergy. First, to establish robustness, we explored beyond conventional Cox proportional hazards models and applied a library of ML survival models at the individual NARCD pathway level. Second, to account for synergy, the integration of these individual robust prognostic models demonstrates that the interplay between pathways is a key predictor of CRC survival than any single pathway alone. This framework, validated across over 800 patients from two independent cohorts, provides exceptional confidence in the final prognostic risk model, i.e. the c-RCDI.

We anticipate this robust 43-gene prognostic risk model, named c-RCDI, developed using a novel two-step ML framework based on the 13 NARCD pathway genes, would be a critical tool for efficient CRC prognostic prediction and aid in enhancement of personalized medicine landscape for CRC.
