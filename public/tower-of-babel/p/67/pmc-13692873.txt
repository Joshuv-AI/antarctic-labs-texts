Molecular epidemiological investigation of swine hepatitis e virus (sHEV) in Guangdong Province, China: prevalence, genotypic characteristics, and zoonotic risk
Authors: Zhenwen He, Dingyu Liu, Qin Luo, Baoling Liu, Dongqi Chen, Hua Xiang, Xiaohu Wang, Gang Wang, Yuan Huang, Jing Chen, Rujian Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis E virus (HEV) is a major zoonotic foodborne pathogen causing human acute hepatitis, with genotype 4 endemic in China and swine as its main reservoir. Guangdong has a large intensive swine industry, yet stratified molecular epidemiological data on swine HEV are lacking. This study explored the prevalence, risk factors and genetic traits of swine HEV in Guangdong to support zoonotic disease control.MethodsA total of 1280 pig fecal samples from farms and abattoirs were tested for HEV RNA via RT-nPCR targeting ORF2. All positive samples underwent Sanger sequencing, and partial isolates were amplified for near-full-length genomes. Phylogenetic trees were built using the maximum likelihood method with 1000 bootstraps. Samples were grouped by region, season and sampling site; subgroup detection rates were compared via χ² and Fisher’s exact tests, and binary logistic regression identified independent risk factors (P < 0.05).ResultsThe overall HEV positive detection rate was 2.66% (34/1280, 95% CI: 1.85–3.69). Detection rates differed markedly by region (P < 0.001) and season (P < 0.05), with no discrepancy between farms and abattoirs (P > 0.05). Region and season were independent risk factors. All isolates were HEV genotype 4, dominated by subtype 4a plus minor 4b and 4d strains, with consistent genotyping results from ORF2 and full-length genomes. Wide genetic divergence existed among local strains, and amino acid mutations concentrated in the ORF1 hypervariable region.ConclusionSwine HEV prevalent in Guangdong Province mainly belongs to genotype 4 and exhibits obvious regional and seasonal distribution characteristics. Continuous and targeted surveillance in high-risk regions and peak epidemic seasons is recommended to reduce the zoonotic transmission risk of swine HEV and ensure public health safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05703-5.

Introduction

Hepatitis E virus (HEV), which belongs to the Hepeviridae family and the Paslahepevirus genus [1], has been identified as the etiological agent responsible for hepatitis E. This spherical, nonenveloped virus has a diameter of approximately 30–34 nm and possesses a genome composed of a single-stranded positive-sense RNA molecule, approximately 7.2 kb in length, which contains three partially overlapping open reading frames (ORFs) [2, 3]. ORF1 encodes nonstructural proteins essential for viral replication; ORF2 primarily encodes the viral capsid protein; and ORF3 is implicated in viral infection and release processes [4–6]. Homology analysis of genomic sequences has classified HEV into eight genotypes (genotypes 1–genotypes 8) [7]. Among them, genotypes 3 and 4 are zoonotic and capable of infecting both humans and animals [8].

The global burden of hepatitis E presents a substantial public health challenge. In developing countries, suboptimal sanitation and inadequate public health infrastructure result in frequent contamination of water sources, thereby inducing widespread outbreaks predominantly driven by HEV genotypes 1 and 2 [9]. Conversely, in developed nations, despite advancements in sanitation, there has been an observable increase in foodborne and zoonotic hepatitis E cases attributable to genotypes 3 and 4 [10]. Initially, human HEV infections in developed countries were presumed to originate from international travel. Nevertheless, accumulating clinical cases reported in the United Kingdom, the United States, Japan and other developed nations have confirmed that consumption of HEV-contaminated animal products serves as the dominant cause of autochthonous infections. Approximately 21% of slaughter swine at abattoirs in the UK are HEV RNA-positive [11], whereas 6.3% of swine at US abattoirs test positive for HEV RNA [12]. Accordingly, ingestion of raw or undercooked pork products may represent a potential important route for zoonotic HEV transmission to humans.

In China, genotype 4 HEV predominates and swine serve as its principal reservoir host [13]. Accordingly, occupational staff with regular swine exposure, including pig farm and abattoir workers, may face an increased risk of HEV infection [14]. In addition, epidemiological investigations of multiple familial HEV outbreaks indicate that consumption of undercooked contaminated pork, especially pork liver, is a leading contributor to such clustered infections [15]. These findings raise substantial public health concerns regarding the prevalence of HEV among swine herds and its adverse impacts on human health.

Guangdong Province, as a major economic hub and populous region in China, boasts a robust swine industry, ranking among the highest nationwide in terms of both live swine inventory and slaughter volume. However, the province’s unique climatic conditions and high-density farming practices create an environment conducive to HEV transmission. Previous studies have reported a high incidence of HEV in Guangdong swine populations [16]. For example, a serological survey conducted between 2009 and 2011 involving 484 serum samples from various farms reported an average antibody positivity detection rate of 71.9%, whereas a molecular epidemiological investigation of 69 bile samples from diseased pigs identified all isolated strains as genotype 4, with a positivity detection rate of 71.0% [17]. From 2014 to 2016, 1127 swine serum smples from 10 pig farms in Guangdong Province were tested for anti-HEV antibodies, and 300 faecal and liver samples were subjected to HEV nucleic acid detection by PCR; all PCR-positive samples were sequenced for genotyping. The results revealed an overall HEV seroprevalence of 69.2% and a nucleic acid positive detection rate of 46.7%, among which 29 out of 30 sequenced strains belonged to HEV genotype 4 and the remaining one was genotype 3 [18]. These findings underscore the extensive circulation of genotype 4 HEV in Guangdong’s swine population. In addition, previous studies have indicated that abattoirs serve as critical nodes in the swine supply chain [19]. HEV-positive pigs may contaminate the surrounding environment via faeces and blood, posing potential infection risks to consumers who consume contaminated or undercooked pork products. High daily slaughter throughput further exacerbates environmental contamination and the subsequent spread of foodborne illnesses [19].

Despite notable progress in understanding HEV dynamics within swine populations in Guangdong, several critical gaps remain. Comprehensive investigations into infection rates and transmission dynamics across diverse swine herds and farm sizes are lacking. Furthermore, the molecular genetic characteristics of swine-derived HEV, along with its associations with human infections, require further elucidation. Therefore, this study investigated the molecular epidemiology of swine hepatitis E virus (sHEV) in Guangdong Province. The objectives include elucidating the prevalence, transmission dynamics, and molecular characteristics of HEV in local swine populations, assessing the associated risks to human health, and providing essential data to inform effective prevention and control strategies. This research holds substantial significance for the sustainable development of the swine industry and safeguarding public health security.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and processing

A total of 1280 pig fecal samples were systematically collected from pig farms and abattoirs across various regions of Guangdong Province between March 2023 and June 2024 (Fig. 1); prior to sampling, the anus and perianal skin of restrained growing pigs were sequentially disinfected three times using sterile gauze saturated with normal saline followed by 75% ethanol, and operators wearing sterile latex gloves pre-moistened with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 0.01 mol/L, pH 7.2–7.4) collected fecal specimens via rectal palpation before immediately transferring all specimens into sterile 50 mL centrifuge tubes, after which an aliquot of each fecal sample was transferred to 2 mL sterile screw-cap centrifuge tubes using sterile swabs whose tips were excised with sterile surgical scissors and retained inside the tubes, and each fecal specimen was homogenized in PBS (0.01 mol/L, pH 7.2–7.4) to prepare a 10% (w/v) suspension that was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, with the resultant supernatant preserved at − 80 °C pending downstream analysis.

HEV RNA detection

Total RNA was extracted from 200 µL pig fecal supernatant using the Stool RNA Kit (Omega Biotek, Inc., Norcross, GA, USA). RNA reverse transcription to complementary DNA (cDNA) was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions with the HiScript® II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China).

RT-nested PCR (RT-nPCR) targeting the conserved region of the HEV ORF2 gene was performed for HEV RNA detection. The primer sequences used for RT-nPCR were referenced from Tengfei S et al. [20] (Supplementary Material 1), and the target amplicon length was 348 bp. Negative and positive controls were set up in each run to rule out contamination and amplification failure.

Post-amplification products were separated via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis for identification. Amplicons exhibiting bands consistent with the expected fragment size were purified using the HiPure Gel Pure DNA Mini Kit (Magen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) and stored at − 20 °C for subsequent downstream analyses.

Full-length genome amplification of HEV

Specific primers covering 11 overlapping genomic fragments were designed using SnapGene software based on published HEV genome sequences deposited in GenBank (e.g., accession number AJ272108; Supplementary Material 2). These primer pairs were used to amplify HEV genomes from all HEV RNA-positive samples. Adjacent amplicons contained partial overlapping sequences at both termini to facilitate subsequent assembly of the complete viral genome. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Amplified products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis, purified, and preserved for downstream experiments.

Sequencing and phylogenetic analysis

The gel-purified PCR amplicons were subjected to T/A cloning using the pMD™18-T Vector Cloning Kit (TaKaRa Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). Positive clones derived from each sample were sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. for Sanger sequencing, and the resulting sequencing data were submitted to the NCBI BLAST server for homology verification.

Reference sequences were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, including canonical representative strains covering HEV genotypes 1 to 8 and diverse subtypes of HEV genotype 4 (Supplementary Material 3) [21, 22].

Multiple sequence alignments between the obtained sequences and reference sequences were performed using the MegAlign program embedded in the Lasergene software package (Version 7.1.0, DNASTAR Inc., Madison, Wisconsin, USA). Nucleotide identity was calculated, amino acid mutation analysis was conducted, and major mutation sites were identified. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the aligned sequence dataset using MEGA 11 software, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates implemented to calculate the bootstrap support values for each tree branch.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2019 was used to group and summarize the sample information. Sample origins were divided into four regions: Eastern Guangdong, Western Guangdong, Northern Guangdong, and the Pearl River Delta (PRD). Eastern Guangdong covers Jieyang, Meizhou, Shanwei and Chaozhou; Western Guangdong includes Yunfu, Zhanjiang, Yangjiang and Maoming; Northern Guangdong consists of Heyuan, Shaoguan and Qingyuan; while the PRD comprises Guangzhou, Foshan, Jiangmen, Zhongshan, Zhaoqing, Huizhou, Zhuhai and Shenzhen. Samples were also categorized by collection month into four seasons: spring (March to May), summer (June to August), autumn (September to November) and winter (December to February). In addition, sampling sites were divided into pig farms and abattoirs.

The HEV positive detection rate was calculated as the proportion of HEV-positive samples among all tested specimens, with 95% binomial confidence intervals (CIs) estimated accordingly. Data comparisons were performed using the chi-square test (χ2 -test) with Fisher’s exact test [23].

Groups showing statistically significant differences in univariate analysis were screened as potential risk factors and subsequently subjected to binary logistic regression analysis. Case weighting was applied based on the frequency distribution of detection outcomes. HEV infection status was set as the dependent variable, while the screened potential risk factors were treated as covariates. All categorical covariates were converted into dummy variables, and the category with the lowest HEV positive detection rate was designated as the reference group. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was performed to evaluate the model fitness.

A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance. All statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS Statistics version 27.0.

Results

HEV RNA detection

In total, 34 samples were HEV RNA-positive, corresponding to an overall positivity detection rate of 2.66% (34/1280, 95% CI: 1.85–3.69). Detailed detection results stratified by region are presented in Supplementary Material 4. Northern Guangdong exhibited the highest HEV RNA positivity detection rate at 5.54% (18/325, 95% CI: 3.32–8.61), followed by the Pearl River Delta (PRD) at 3.10% (4/129, 95% CI: 0.85–7.75). The positivity detection rate in Western Guangdong was 1.78% (12/676, 95% CI: 0.92–3.08), while no HEV-positive samples were identified among the 150 specimens collected from Eastern Guangdong. The Fisher’s exact test (Table 1) revealed significant interregional differences in HEV positivity detection rates (P < 0.001) .

Stratified by sampling season, the highest positivity detection rate was observed in spring at 3.71% (25/673, 95% CI: 2.42–5.44), followed by summer at 2.19% (5/228, 95% CI: 0.72–5.04). The winter positivity detection rate was 1.82% (3/165, 95% CI: 0.38–5.22), whereas autumn showed the lowest detection rate of 0.47% (1/214, 95% CI: 0.01–2.58). The Fisher’s exact test indicated a statistically significant seasonal difference in HEV positivity detection rates (P < 0.05).

The HEV RNA positivity detection rate of samples collected from pig farms was 2.68% (22/822, 95% CI: 1.68–4.02), and the positivity detection rate for abattoirs samples was 2.76% (12/458, 95% CI: 1.51–4.58). No statistically significant difference in HEV positivity detection rates was detected between samples from different sampling sites according to the Fisher’s exact test (P > 0.05).

Results of binary logistic regression analysis

Based on the outputs of the Fisher’s exact test, region and season were incorporated as covariates and converted into dummy variables for binary logistic regression analysis (Table 2). Region and season were treated as categorical covariates and recoded into dummy variables, with Western Guangdong and autumn designated as the reference categories, respectively.

The Omnibus test of model coefficients yielded P < 0.001, indicating that the overall regression model was statistically significant. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test produced a P value of 0.963 (P > 0.05), which suggested satisfactory model fitness.

Regional factors were identified as key risk factors significantly associated with the HEV RNA positivity rate (P < 0.001). Samples collected from Northern Guangdong exhibited significantly higher odds of HEV RNA positivity relative to the reference group (P < 0.001), with an odds ratio (OR) of 8.22 (95% CI: 3.70–18.26). Season also exerted a significant effect on HEV RNA detection (P < 0.001). Specimens sampled in spring had significantly elevated odds of HEV positivity (P = 0.002), with OR = 24.58 (95% CI: 3.23–187.26). Although autumn samples showed an OR of 22.30 (95% CI: 2.31–215.78), this association did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.07).

Phylogenetic analyses

The ORF2 gene sequences of all 34 HEV RNA-positive samples were successfully acquired (NCBI GenBank accession numbers: PV785675–PV785685, PV785688, PP731807–PP731823, PZ533472–PZ533473, PV809683–PV809685). Three complete full-length HEV genomes (PV809683–PV809685) were successfully assembled from the amplified fragments.

Multiple sequence alignments were performed between the 34 ORF2 sequences generated in this study and reference HEV sequences, based on which a phylogenetic tree was constructed (Fig. 3). Phylogenetic reconstruction revealed that all 34 isolates clustered within HEV genotype 4. Subtyping results demonstrated that 88.2% (30/34) of the isolates belonged to subtype 4a, whereas 5.9% (2/34) were assigned to subtype 4b and subtype 4d, respectively (Supplementary Material 5).

Sequence identity analysis of the 34 HEV ORF2 fragments showed intrastrain nucleotide identities ranging from 75.8% to 100% (Supplementary Material 6). The nucleotide identity between our isolates and reference strains of all HEV-4 subtypes varied from 75.0% to 96.9% (Supplementary Material 7). Within subtype 4a, the nucleotide identity between the study isolates and subtype 4a reference strains was 82.4%–93.0%. For subtype 4b isolates, the identity against subtype 4b reference strains ranged from 91.4% to 93.8%. The nucleotide identity between subtype 4d isolates and subtype 4d reference strains was 87.5%–96.9% (Table 3).

Near-complete genome sequences ranging from 7067 bp to 7227 bp were successfully obtained for three isolates, designated HY6, HY15 and YF14, via full-length genomic amplification.

The near-complete genome of HY6 was 7155 bp in length, comprising ORF1 (positions 1–5010), ORF2 (5007–7028), ORF3 (5035–5376), and the 3′ UTR (7029–7155). The base composition was 18.94% A, 26.76% T, 25.72% G, and 28.58% C.

The HY15 near-complete genome spanned 7227 bp, including ORF1 (1–5081), ORF2 (5078–7099), ORF3 (5106–5447), and the 3′ UTR (7100–7227), with nucleotide frequencies of 18.75% A, 26.90% T, 25.97% G, and 28.38% C.

The YF14 near-complete genome was 7067 bp long, containing ORF1 (1–5023), ORF2 (5020–7044), ORF3 (5048–5392), and the 3′ UTR (7045–7067); its base contents were 18.30% A, 26.48% T, 26.31% G, and 28.92% C.

Phylogenetic analysis based on the three near-complete genomes demonstrated that all isolates belonged to HEV genotype 4. HY6 and HY15 clustered into subtype 4d, while YF14 fell into subtype 4b (Fig. 4), consistent with the phylogenetic results derived from partial ORF2 sequences. Nucleotide identity analysis revealed that the pairwise identities among the three near-complete genomes ranged from 83.4% to 99.0% (Supplementary Material 8). HY6 and HY15 shared the highest nucleotide identity (90.0%–90.5%) with the human-origin Chinese HEV reference strain T1 (GenBank accession AJ272108). YF14 clustered together with the swine-origin Chinese HEV reference strain (GenBank accession DQ279091), with a nucleotide identity of 90.8%.

Open reading frames (ORFs) of HY6 and HY15 were analyzed using the human HEV strain T1 (AJ272108) as the reference.

For HY6, ORF1 encoded a 1670-amino-acid polypeptide with 94.07% amino acid identity to the reference strain. The hypervariable region (amino acids 647–761) exhibited only 69.30% identity relative to the reference sequence. In total, 99 amino acid substitutions were detected in HY6 ORF1, among which 35 substitutions were located within the hypervariable region. ORF2 and ORF3 encoded 674 and 114 amino acids, with amino acid identities of 97.03% and 93.81% compared to the reference, corresponding to 20 and 7 amino acid mutations, respectively.

For HY15, ORF1 encoded a 1693-amino-acid protein sharing 95.27% amino acid identity with strain T1. The hypervariable region (aa 671–785) displayed 69.30% identity to the reference. A total of 80 amino acid mutations were identified in HY15 ORF1, 35 of which resided in the hypervariable region. Similar to HY6, HY15 ORF2 and ORF3 encoded 674 and 114 amino acids, with 97.03% and 93.81% amino acid identity to T1, accompanied by 20 and 7 substitutions, respectively.

The ORFs of YF14 were aligned and analyzed against the Chinese swine HEV reference strain DQ279091. YF14 ORF1 encoded 1673 amino acids with an overall amino acid identity of 95.69% to the reference. Its hypervariable region (aa 671–783) showed 76.79% identity, and 73 amino acid substitutions were found across ORF1, 26 of which mapped to the hypervariable region. ORF2 of YF14 encoded 675 amino acids with 98.52% identity to the reference, harboring 10 amino acid mutations. ORF3 encoded 115 amino acids and shared 94.78% identity with the reference strain, carrying six amino acid substitutions (Fig. 5).

Discussion

HEV infection remains an important global public health concern. HEV is not only associated with liver disease in swine but also poses a potential zoonotic risk to humans through the consumption of contaminated pork products. According to World Health Organization (WHO) data, the number of deaths caused by viral hepatitis increased from 1.1 million in 2019 to 1.3 million in 2022 across 187 countries, highlighting a continuing global public health burden [24]. With the increasing consumption of pork, pig farms and abattoirs have attracted growing attention as key nodes in HEV transmission. In recent years, multiple human HEV infections linked to pork consumption have been reported [25–26]. In addition, HEV has been detected in retail pork, pork liver, and other pork-derived products [27]. As critical components of the pork production chain, pig farms and abattoirs harboring sHEV may pose occupational risks to workers as well as food safety risks to consumers.

This study employed molecular epidemiological investigations to determine the current prevalence of HEV in swine populations in Guangdong Province. He Wei et al. reported a HEV RNA detection rate of 10.6% (11/104) in swine across Guangxi [28]; Ji Fangxiao et al. conducted a molecular epidemiological survey on HEV in Guangdong in 2015, which revealed a HEV RNA detection rate of 6.8% (4/73) [16]. The results of this study (2.66%) were lower than those of the aforementioned studies, likely due to differences in geographical factors and sample sizes. According to our findings, the HEV RNA detection rate was significantly higher in northern Guangdong compared to other regions, while no positive samples were detected in eastern Guangdong. This significant disparity may stem from variations in geographical environment or local farming practices, or could also be attributed to differences in sampling quantities across regions. Nevertheless, these results indicate regional variations in HEV detection rates among swine in Guangdong, providing a basis for regional prevention and control strategies.

Seasonal distribution analysis revealed a significant increase in the HEV RNA detection rate in spring (3.71%), which may be associated with climatic factors. Furthermore, the post-Spring Festival restocking peak leads to mixing stress among pigs, increasing transmission opportunities. Low temperatures in winter may temporarily suppress pig immunity, resulting in a surge in infection rates in spring.

Lu et al. [29] demonstrated that climate, the environment, population mobility, living customs, and other factors can affect viral prevalence. Therefore, multiple factors must be considered when investigating viral transmission patterns.

No significant difference was observed in the HEV RNA detection rates between pig farms and abattoirs (P > 0.05). The results are consistent with our previously published meta-analysis, indicating that the sHEV carriage status in swine populations does not undergo significant changes from the breeding stage to the slaughtering and processing stage. This suggests that HEV infection may persist throughout the entire production chain from breeding to slaughter [19]. These findings provide valuable references for the formulation of prevention and control measures for swine-derived HEV, emphasizing that viral monitoring and intervention should be implemented throughout the entire swine production chain rather than focusing solely on a specific link.

Multiple studies have verified that the gene fragment adopted for HEV RNA detection in this study (the 5994–6294 nt region near the 5′end of ORF2) is suitable for HEV genotyping [20, 30–31]. The results revealed that genotype 4 HEV (HEV-4) predominates in swine herds in Guangdong Province, China, with subtype 4a as the dominant strain; subtypes 4b and 4d were also circulating in the region. These findings are consistent with previous epidemiological investigations conducted in Sichuan and Yunnan Provinces of China [32–33]. Phylogenetic analysis based on three nearly complete HEV-4 genomes demonstrated that strains HY6 and HY15 clustered within subtype 4d, whereas strain YF14 belonged to subtype 4b, which matched the phylogenetic results derived from partial ORF2 sequences. Nucleotide identity analysis indicated that the nearly full-length genomic sequences of HY6 and HY15 shared the highest similarity (90.5%) with the human-origin HEV strain T1 (GenBank accession No. AJ272108). This finding further suggests the genetic potential of swine-origin HEV for cross-species transmission to humans, although additional epidemiological surveillance of human HEV cases is required to validate this association.

ORF1 encodes the multifunctional non-structural protein of HEV. The non-structural protein has low sequence conservation and is prone to mutations, and it exerts vital functions during viral replication and infection [34]. The polyprotein contains multiple functional domains, including viral methyltransferase (vMET), Y domain, papain-like protease (PCP), hypervariable region (HVR), X domain, viral helicase (vHEL), and RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), most of whose biological functions remain unclear [35]. Amino acid alignment of the three nearly complete genomic sequences HY6, YF14 and HY15 revealed that amino acid mutation hotspots were mainly distributed in three regions: 16–260 aa, 507–860 aa and 1305–1643 aa. Most of these variable sites are located within the vMET, PCP, HVR and RdRp domains. These findings provide a theoretical basis for further investigations into the genetic variation profiles of HEV and the functional roles of this multifunctional non-structural protein throughout the viral replication and infection cycle.

This study has several limitations. First, the uneven geographical distribution of collected samples may lead to an underestimation of regional epidemiological differences in HEV prevalence. Second, no samples from wild animal hosts, including wild boars, rabbits, deer, camels, and rats, were collected in this study; thus, the contribution of natural foci to HEV epidemic transmission could not be evaluated. Technically, full-length genomic sequences of all isolated strains were not successfully obtained in the present study. Although phylogenetic analysis based on the ORF2 fragment is widely applied for HEV genotyping and molecular epidemiological investigation, analyses relying on partial genomic sequences may overlook critical genetic variations in other genomic regions. In addition, in vitro infection assays were absent to verify the pathogenic differences among different HEV strains. Therefore, the phylogenetic relationships and nucleotide similarity results reported in this study should be interpreted with consideration of the limitations of the analyzed genomic region.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that sHEV circulating in Guangdong Province is predominantly genotype 4, with an overall detection rate of 2.66%. Higher detection rates were observed in northern Guangdong and during spring, indicating potential regional and seasonal differences in virus circulation. Phylogenetic analysis revealed close genetic relatedness between local sHEV strains and a human-derived strain, suggesting a potential zoonotic risk. However, further epidemiological and virological evidence is required to confirm interspecies transmission. These findings highlight the importance of continuous surveillance of HEV across the entire pig production chain and targeted monitoring in high-risk regions and seasons to support evidence-based prevention strategies.
