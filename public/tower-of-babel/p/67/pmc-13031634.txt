A novel risk-scoring system based on endoscopic ultrasound and clinical characteristics for the preoperative diagnosis of small gastric gastrointestinal stromal tumors
Authors: Luojie Liu, Yunfu Feng, Sijie Zheng, Bin He, Chao Ma, Zhibing Wang, Xiaodan Xu, Jian Chen, Ying Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe preoperative differentiation of small gastric gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) from other submucosal lesions remains clinically challenging due to overlapping endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) features. This study aimed to develop and validate a novel risk-scoring system integrating EUS imaging and clinical characteristics to improve preoperative diagnostic accuracy for small gastric GISTs versus non-GIST submucosal tumors. We retrospectively analyzed 1303 patients with gastric submucosal tumors (SMTs), who were divided into a training cohort (n = 670), an internal validation cohort (n = 287), and an external validation cohort (n = 346). Clinical and EUS characteristics were compared between small GISTs and non-GIST SMTs. Independent predictive factors identified through multivariate logistic regression were used to construct a risk-scoring model. The model’s diagnostic performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV), and accuracy. Small gastric GISTs accounted for 614 cases (47.1%). Four independent predictors were identified: tumor location in the cardia/fundus (1 points), origin within the muscularis propria (2 points), hypoechoic echogenicity (1 point), and extraluminal growth pattern (2 points). The scoring system demonstrated strong discriminatory power, with AUCs of 0.776 (95%CI: 0.727–0.826) in the internal and 0.844 (95%CI: 0.805–0.884) in the external validation cohorts. Sensitivities were 0.867 (95%CI: 0.800–0.918) and 0.828 (95%CI: 0.767–0.878), and NPVs were 0.827 and 0.785, respectively. Risk stratification revealed that in the internal cohort, 17.3% of lesions were low-risk (0–2 points), 52.8% intermediate-risk (3–4 points), and 96.2% high-risk (5–6 points). Corresponding proportions in the external cohort were 21.5%, 73.9%, and 95.0%. The proposed EUS-based risk-scoring system shows robust performance in preoperatively diagnosing small gastric GISTs. Introduction

Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) represent the most prevalent mesenchymal neoplasms of the gastrointestinal tract, with gastric GISTs accounting for the majority of cases1,2. Small gastric GISTs, defined as tumors with a diameter < 2.0 cm, constitute a distinct subgroup that, despite their relatively indolent biological behavior in most instances, retains malignant potential and carries a non-negligible risk of metastasis3. Epidemiological evidence from the SEER database underscores this concern: among 378 patients with newly diagnosed GISTs, 11.4% presented with local progression or distant metastasis at initial diagnosis, with these patients exhibiting a significantly elevated 5-year cause-specific mortality rate compared to those without metastasis4. These findings align with consensus guidelines from European and Japanese experts, which advocate surgical resection for all pathologically confirmed GISTs, irrespective of tumor size, to mitigate the risk of disease progression5,6. Consequently, the accurate preoperative differentiation of small gastric GISTs from other gastric submucosal tumors (SMTs) is paramount for guiding optimal clinical management.

Endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) has emerged as the premier imaging modality for the initial assessment and characterization of gastric SMTs, providing high-resolution visualization of tumor size, location, layer of origin, echogenic patterns, and morphological features—parameters critical for distinguishing benign from malignant lesions3,7,8. However, the diagnostic utility of EUS is constrained by the overlapping imaging characteristics of GISTs and benign SMTs, particularly leiomyomas, which frequently complicate traditional radiological interpretation9. Prior studies report that EUS alone achieves a diagnostic accuracy of approximately 70% for GISTs, with performance further diminished in small gastric GISTs due to their subtle and often atypical imaging features10. To address these limitations, recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) have introduced deep learning-based systems that demonstrate remarkable capabilities in analyzing EUS images to differentiate GISTs from benign lesions, including leiomyomas. These models have shown superior accuracy compared to human readers in certain studies, highlighting their potential to enhance clinical decision-making. Emerging research even suggests their potential for direct risk stratification, which could further optimize treatment strategies11–13. However, it is important to acknowledge that while AI models offer significant promise, they also face challenges. One key limitation is the inherent “black box” nature of some systems, which renders their decision-making processes inscrutable to clinicians and may limit trust in their outputs14. Additionally, existing AI frameworks often process EUS images in isolation, potentially neglecting essential clinical context—such as patient demographics, symptoms, or endoscopist observations—that could influence diagnostic certainty. These gaps highlight the need for a more transparent, clinically integrated approach to preoperative GIST diagnosis.

In this study, we aimed to develop and validate a novel, reproducible, and clinically applicable EUS-based risk-scoring system that synergizes key endoscopic ultrasonography imaging biomarkers to enhance the accuracy and interpretability of small gastric GIST diagnosis. By leveraging standardized EUS-derived characteristics, this tool seeks to provide clinicians with a practical, evidence-based framework for preoperative risk stratification and informed personalized treatment decisions for gastric SMTs.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective investigation examined medical records of individuals presenting with upper gastrointestinal manifestations who received gastroscopic and EUS examinations across several medical institutions from January 2015 to December 2024. All EUS examinations were performed by experienced endoscopists with a minimum of 5 years of dedicated EUS practice, each having performed over 500 diagnostic EUS procedures, using an ultrasound mini-probe (Olympus, Japan) with a standard frequency of 12 MHz. The EUS characteristics of the gastric SMTs were assessed based on standardized EUS reports and corresponding ultrasound images. Eligible participants met the following conditions: (1) gastroscopic and EUS confirmation of gastric SMTs; (2) lesion diameter below 2.0 cm; and (3) receipt of endoscopic intervention. Cases were excluded based on the following: (1) SMT measurements of 2.0 cm or larger; (2) absence of surgical management; (3) ambiguous pathological findings; or (4) insufficient clinical documentation. For analysis, subjects from the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University were randomly assigned to either a training cohort (TC, n = 670) or an internal validation cohort (IVC, n = 287) at a ratio of 7:3. Additionally, patients from Nanjing First Hospital, Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University, the Affiliated Zhangjiagang Hospital of Soochow University, and No.1 People’s Hospital of Kunshan were collectively designated as the external validation cohort (EVC), comprising 346 cases. The study design is summarized in Fig. 1. The research protocol received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Changshu Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University (Approval No: L2025055), which served as the central ethics committee for this multi-center study, with additional approvals obtained from the local ethics committees of all other participating institutions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Quality control in multicenter data and pathology

To ensure consistency and reliability across multiple centers, stringent quality control measures were implemented. First, a pathological review was conducted for all cases. Specifically, pathological specimens and diagnostic reports from each participating institution were re-assessed independently by two experienced gastrointestinal pathologists, who were blinded to the original diagnoses and patient groupings. Discrepancies were resolved through joint discussion to establish a final consensus diagnosis. Second, a standardized operating procedure for EUS examination and image interpretation was developed and distributed to all involved endoscopists. Finally, a dedicated data management team performed periodic audits of the submitted clinical and EUS data, checking for missing values, outliers, and logical inconsistencies. While formal statistical analysis of intra- and inter-observer agreement (e.g., Kappa values) for EUS features like echogenicity was not performed in this retrospective design, several steps were taken to promote consistent interpretation and data quality across centers. These included the implementation of standardized operating procedures and centralized data audits, as described above. A formal assessment of observer variability is planned for the prospective validation phase of this scoring system to further establish the reliability of these key EUS characteristics.

Data collection

The data collected in this study included the following variables: gender, age, tumor location, presence of surface ulceration, tumor growth pattern, layer of origin as determined by EUS, echogenic features (including echogenicity and homogeneity), lesion morphology, margin demarcation, and postoperative pathological results. All EUS examinations were performed by experienced endoscopists. The EUS characteristics of the gastric SMTs were assessed based on standardized EUS reports and corresponding ultrasound images.

Definitions

Tumor size was measured by EUS. Based on the morphological characteristics observed during EUS examination, the tumors were classified as either regular (demonstrating round, oval, or nodular shapes) or irregular (exhibiting a branching or lobulated structure). Growth pattern was defined as intraluminal if more than 50% of the tumor was located within the gastric wall, and extraluminal if more than 50% extended beyond the gastric wall15,16. For the assessment of echogenicity, lesions with mixed or heterogeneous echo patterns were classified based on the predominant (> 50%) echo pattern observed. If no single pattern predominated, the lesion was categorized as having medium echogenicity. For the purpose of this study, a “small gastric GIST” was defined as a lesion with a maximum diameter of less than 2.0 cm as measured by EUS, with the diagnosis subsequently confirmed by postoperative histopathological examination and positive immunohistochemical staining for CD117 (c-KIT), CD34, and/or DOG-1.

Statistical analysis

When presenting categorical data, we utilized frequencies and proportions, and employed the Chi-square test for comparing differences between groups. To distinguish between small gastric stromal tumors and non-small gastric stromal tumors, univariate analysis was performed first. Variables demonstrating statistical significance (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were subsequently incorporated into a multiple logistic regression framework. A stepwise selection method (both forward and backward) was employed to identify the final independent predictors, using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) as the stopping rule. Before constructing the final model, multicollinearity among the candidate variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a VIF value of less than 5 indicating no significant collinearity. The scoring system was then constructed by standardizing the regression coefficients (β values) derived from the final multivariate model. The variable with the smallest absolute β coefficient was chosen as the reference (assigned a score of 1), and the scores for the remaining variables were determined proportionally based on the ratios of their coefficients, with the results rounded to the nearest whole number. For model validation, two separate analytical approaches were adopted:1 discrimination was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) calculated for quantification2, goodness-of-fit was assessed using calibration plots, and3 clinical utility estimation through decision curve analysis (DCA). To gauge model performance, diagnostic accuracy indicators were computed, encompassing sensitivity, specificity, overall accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). The primary endpoint of this study was the discriminative performance of the developed risk-scoring system for the preoperative diagnosis of small gastric GISTs, assessed by the AUC. Secondary endpoints included sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, overall accuracy, calibration, and clinical utility. All computational tasks were executed using the R statistical computing environment (version 4.1.0), with the significance threshold (α) set at 0.05 to determine statistical significance.

Comparison of characteristics between small gastric stromal tumor group and non-small gastric stromal tumor group

Based on postoperative pathological findings, gastric SMTs were classified into gastric stromal tumors and non-gastric stromal tumors. In the TC, gastric stromal tumors occurred most frequently in the gastric cardia/fundus (57.7%), with the vast majority (84.7%) arising from the muscularis propria layer. Most lesions (81.0%) appeared hypoechoic. Additionally, compared to non-gastric stromal tumors, a significantly higher proportion of gastric stromal tumors showed extraluminal growth (22.3% vs. 3.2%). Similarly, in the EVC, these four characteristics also differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.05), as detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Risk factors for predicting gastric submucosal tumors as gastric stromal tumors and the development of a scoring system model

In the TC, univariate analysis identified tumor location, layer of origin, growth pattern, and echogenicity as risk factors for predicting gastric stromal tumors among gastric SMTs (Table 2). Multivariate analysis confirmed these four characteristics as independent predictors (Table 3). Specifically, SMTs located in the cardia/fundus carried a significantly higher risk of being classified as gastric stromal tumors compared to those in the gastric body (OR = 2.96, 95% CI: 1.96–4.63, P < 0.001). Tumors originating from the MP were more likely to be gastric stromal tumors than those from the MM/SM (OR = 8.80, 95% CI: 5.80-13.66, P < 0.001). Hypoechoic lesions on EUS had a higher risk than those with medium/high echogenicity (OR = 2.65, 95% CI: 1.74–4.08, P < 0.001), and tumors with extraluminal growth were more likely to be stromal tumors than those with intraluminal growth (OR = 8.65, 95% CI: 4.33–18.71, P < 0.001). Based on the β-coefficients from the multivariate analysis, each risk factor was assigned a score: tumor location (cardia/fundus: 1 point; gastric body/antrum: 0 points), layer of origin (MP: 2 points; MM/SM: 0 points), echogenicity (low: 1 points; medium/high: 0 points), and growth pattern (extraluminal: 2 points; intraluminal: 0 points) (Table 3). The cutoff score of ≥ 3 was chosen based on clinical interpretability and risk stratification, corresponding to the intermediate- and high-risk categories for suspected GISTs.

Validation of the scoring system

In the TC, the proportion of gastric stromal tumors increased with higher risk scores: 8.5% (score 0), 9.4%1, 30.4%2, 55.6%3, 76.5%4, 96.4%5, and 100.0%6 (Fig. 4). The scoring system achieved an AUC of 0.838 (95% CI: 0.808–0.867; Fig. 5A), reflecting strong discriminative performance. Good agreement between predictions and observations was indicated by the calibration curve (P > 0.05; Fig. 5D). In the TC, the DCA curve analysis of the scoring system showed that when the predicted threshold probability for small gastric GISTs fell within the range of 20% to 70%, clinical interventions could achieve a net benefit of approximately 33% (Fig. 5G). Diagnostic metrics included a sensitivity of 0.833, specificity of 0.708, accuracy of 0.764, PPV of 0.698, and NPV of 0.840 (Table 4). Based on score ranges, risk categories were defined as low (0–2; 16.0%), intermediate (3–4; 64.9%), and high (5–6; 98.1%) (Fig. 6). Within the IVC, a progressive increase in gastric stromal tumor incidence was observed across ascending scores: 3.7% (score 1), 22.5%2, 44.9%3, 67.9%4, 93.8%5, and 100.0%6 (Fig. 4). The model yielded an AUC of 0.776 (95% CI: 0.727–0.826; Fig. 5B), demonstrating acceptable discrimination. Calibration performance was satisfactory, with no significant deviation from ideal predictions (P > 0.05; Fig. 5E). The DCA curve (Fig. 5H) shows that when the threshold probability of small gastric GISTs predicted by the scoring system is within the 20%-60% range, implementing interventions can achieve a net benefit of approximately 33%. The system showed a sensitivity of 0.867, specificity of 0.509, accuracy of 0.669, PPV of 0.587, and NPV of 0.827 (Table 4). Tumor rates by risk category were 17.3% (low), 52.8% (intermediate), and 96.2% (high) (Fig. 6). In the EVC, gastric stromal tumor prevalence also correlated positively with assigned scores: 3.4% (score 0), 18.0%1, 33.9%2, 65.1%3, 84.5%4, and 95.0% (both scores 5 and 6) (Fig. 4). The AUC was 0.844 (95% CI: 0.805–0.884; Fig. 5C), indicating high discriminatory power. Calibration was accurate, with good alignment between predicted and observed outcomes (P > 0.05; Fig. 5F). A net benefit of 40% was observed for interventions when the predicted probability of small gastric GISTs was between 20% and 80%, according to the DCA (Fig. 5I). Performance measures included sensitivity of 0.828, specificity of 0.731, accuracy of 0.783, PPV of 0.782, and NPV of 0.785 (Table 4). Stratification into risk groups revealed tumor rates of 21.5% (low), 73.9% (intermediate), and 95.0% (high) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and rigorously validated a novel EUS-based risk-scoring system that integrates critical endoscopic and clinical characteristics to facilitate the preoperative differentiation of small gastric GISTs from other SMTs. The model, constructed using four independent predictors—tumor location in the cardia/fundus, origin within the muscularis propria, hypoechoic echogenicity, and extraluminal growth pattern—demonstrated strong diagnostic performance in both internal and external validation cohorts, with AUC values of 0.776 and 0.844, respectively. This scoring system enables effective risk stratification, where high-risk scores (5–6 points) are strongly associated with GIST diagnosis, underscoring its potential utility in guiding preoperative decision-making and optimizing treatment strategies for gastric SMTs.

EUS remains the most widely utilized modality for the diagnosis of GISTs due to its ability to clearly visualize key features such as tumor location, layer of origin, echogenicity, morphology, and growth pattern17,18. However, previous studies have highlighted its limitations in distinguishing GISTs from other mesenchymal tumors, such as leiomyomas and schwannomas, often necessitating histopathological confirmation19–22. For instance, Iwahashi et al.10 reported that EUS exhibited a sensitivity of 83.3% and specificity of 66.7% for diagnosing gastric stromal tumors, with sensitivity dropping to 73.3% for tumors smaller than 3 cm. Although EUS-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) is a standard tool for evaluating gastric SMTs and enables ultrasound-guided sampling for pathological diagnosis, it has several drawbacks, including limited specimen yield, risk of false-negative results, technical complexity, high operator dependency, and potential complications such as tumor rupture and peritoneal seeding23,24. The diagnostic performance of EUS-FNA is particularly suboptimal for gastric SMTs smaller than 2.0 cm25,26. In contrast, contrast-enhanced harmonic EUS (CEH-EUS) offers a less invasive alternative, minimizing patient trauma and procedure-related risks while facilitating real-time assessment of enhancement patterns and dynamic microvascular perfusion, which aids in distinguishing malignant-potential GISTs from benign SMTs27,28. Nonetheless, according to the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) clinical guidelines, CEH-EUS also has limited diagnostic value for sub-2 cm GISTs26. A meta-analysis of four studies involving 187 patients with SMTs who underwent contrast-enhanced EUS reported diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 89%, 82%, and 89%, respectively, for GISTs29. It is important to contextualize the performance of our EUS-based scoring system (sensitivity: 0.867 and NPV: 0.827 in the IVC) against these modalities. While the specificity of our model is lower than that reported for CEH-EUS, its strength lies in its high sensitivity and, most notably, its high NPV. This performance profile is particularly suited for a ‘rule-out’ clinical strategy. A high NPV means that a low-risk score can reliably identify lesions that are unlikely to be GISTs, potentially helping to avoid unnecessary invasive diagnostic procedures or treatments for a substantial subset of patients with benign SMTs. Therefore, our simple scoring system and CEH-EUS may serve complementary roles: the former as an accessible, initial triage tool to identify low-risk lesions, and the latter as a more advanced, albeit less widely available, technique for further characterizing indeterminate or high-risk cases. Despite the improved accuracy achieved with contrast agents, over 10% of cases remain indeterminate, and the use of contrast carries inherent risks. Collectively, these limitations underscore the persistent clinical need for reliable non-invasive methods to accurately differentiate small gastric SMTs.

Scoring systems serve as widely adopted quantitative tools in medical research, integrating multidimensional clinical and biological parameters to offer an objective foundation for disease diagnosis, risk stratification, prognosis estimation, and treatment planning. By transforming subjective or intricate assessments into quantifiable scores or categories, they facilitate scientific decision-making, evaluation, and comparative analysis. In a multicenter retrospective study, He et al.16 identified risk factors associated with intraoperative perforation during endoscopic resection of gastric stromal tumors and established a scoring model that exhibited strong predictive accuracy in both IVC and EVC, offering clinicians an efficient and practical tool for procedural planning. Similarly, a difficulty scoring system previously developed by our research team also demonstrated robust predictive performance in preoperatively assessing challenges in endoscopic resection of gastric stromal tumors30.

In the diagnostic evaluation of gastric SMTs, accurately distinguishing GISTs—which carry malignant potential—from non-GIST lesions is of critical importance. This study identified four independent predictive factors for gastric GISTs: location in the cardia/fundus, origin within the muscularis propria, hypoechoic echogenicity, and extraluminal growth pattern. We observed that gastric GISTs occurred predominantly in the cardia and fundus, whereas non-GIST SMTs were more frequently located in the gastric body and antrum. This distribution is consistent with previous reports31–33 and may be attributed to differences in embryonic origin, local tissue microenvironment, and anatomicophysiological features. The cardia and fundus, derived from the foregut, harbor a high density of interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC)—the cell of origin for GISTs—or their precursors. The thicker muscularis layer and distinct neurovascular supply in this region may further support GIST development. In contrast, the gastric body and antrum are characterized by heightened mechanical stress and acid exposure, which may favor the development of other SMTs. The exact mechanisms underlying this anatomic predilection, however, require further elucidation.

The origin of GISTs within the MP reflects their fundamental biology and serves as a key diagnostic discriminator. As GISTs typically arise from ICCs, which are predominantly located within the muscularis propria, this layer represents the most common site of tumor initiation. EUS studies have consistently shown that a muscularis propria origin is strongly associated with GIST diagnosis, likely due to the permissive stromal environment that facilitates tumor growth and invasion. This site specificity may also relate to molecular markers characteristic of ICCs, such as DOG1 expression34,35. Furthermore, our results confirm that hypoechoic appearance on EUS is a typical feature of GISTs, which can be explained by their histologic composition. The tumors are composed of densely packed, homogeneous cells with hypervascular stroma and scant fibrous tissue, leading to reduced ultrasound reflection and lower echogenicity. Acoustic impedance differences between the tumor and adjacent normal layers may also contribute to this sonographic pattern.

Additionally, we found that GISTs accounted for a significantly higher proportion of SMTs with extraluminal growth compared to non-GIST subtypes. This growth pattern aligns with the aggressive biological behavior and anatomic origin of GISTs. Arising from the muscularis propria, GIST cells often exhibit infiltrative growth, penetrating muscle layers and expanding outward toward the subserosa and peritoneal cavity—areas of lower mechanical resistance. This directional tendency may also be influenced by tumor vascularization and adaptive interactions with the local microenvironment. In contrast, most benign SMTs (e.g., leiomyomas or lipomas) typically demonstrate slow growth confined to the gastric wall or intraluminal protrusion, seldom showing extraluminal extension.

Our study identified four independent predictors—location in the cardia/fundus, origin within the muscularis propria, hypoechoic echogenicity, and extraluminal growth pattern—while other EUS features such as irregular margins, lesion morphology, and the presence of surface ulceration, which were also collected and analyzed, were not retained in the final model. This contrasts with some previous studies that have suggested a potential role for features like irregular borders or ulceration in diagnosing GISTs19,23. The discrepancy may be attributable to several factors specific to our study cohort. First, the inclusion was restricted to small gastric SMTs (< 2 cm), among which malignant features like irregular margins or ulceration might be less developed or apparent compared to larger lesions. Second, in the context of small tumors, the four identified predictors may represent more fundamental and dominant characteristics related to the origin and intrinsic biology of GISTs, potentially overshadowing the contribution of other morphological features in multivariate analysis. Furthermore, the high collinearity between certain features (e.g., an extraluminal growth pattern and irregular borders) might have resulted in only the strongest independent predictor being selected. While the omission of these other features could be perceived as a limitation, the robust performance of our parsimonious model across validation cohorts suggests that the selected four variables capture the most critical discriminatory information for small gastric GISTs, supporting its utility as an effective and streamlined preoperative tool. Future studies incorporating larger lesions or additional imaging modalities might further elucidate the role of these other characteristics.

Despite the promising performance of our risk-scoring system, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and the inclusion of only resected lesions may introduce verification bias, as all analyzed tumors were confirmed pathologically following intervention. This could lead to an overestimation of the model’s sensitivity, since lesions that were not resected—and might have been false negatives—were not included. However, our scoring system is intended for clinical scenarios where resection is already under consideration, and its high negative predictive value may still offer utility in ruling out low-risk lesions within this context. Second, all EUS examinations were performed by experienced endoscopists, which may not reflect the variability in operator skill and experience across different clinical settings. Third, although the scoring system integrated key EUS and clinical features, other potential predictors such as contrast-enhanced EUS characteristics or molecular markers were not included. Future studies incorporating advanced imaging modalities and biomarker data may further improve diagnostic accuracy. Fourth, while our model aids in diagnosis, future iterations should aim to incorporate established risk criteria (such as mitotic count from biopsy specimens or enhanced imaging features) to predict the malignant potential of diagnosed GISTs and thus more directly guide treatment decisions. Fifth, it should be noted that the current model exhibits a performance trade-off characterized by high sensitivity and NPV but modest specificity. While this makes it an effective tool for ruling out GISTs, the relatively lower specificity could lead to false-positive results, potentially recommending further intervention for some benign lesions. The clinical utility of the model therefore rests on its application as a high-sensitivity screening and triage instrument. Sixth, our study did not incorporate advanced molecular imaging techniques such as EUS-elastography or contrast-enhanced EUS (CE-EUS), nor did it include a cost-effectiveness analysis of the proposed scoring system. Finally, the model was developed and validated in a Chinese population. Its applicability to other ethnic groups and healthcare settings (e.g., Western populations) remains to be evaluated. Differences in GIST prevalence, genetic backgrounds, endoscopist EUS experience, and technical equipment could potentially influence the performance and calibration of the scoring system. External validation in diverse, international cohorts is a necessary next step before widespread global adoption can be recommended.

The proposed EUS-based risk-scoring system can be readily incorporated into existing diagnostic algorithms for small gastric subepithelial lesions, such as those recommended by the ACG guidelines. As a first-line triage tool following standard EUS examination, the scoring system can assist clinicians in stratifying lesions into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories. For low-risk lesions (score 0–2), a watchful waiting approach with periodic surveillance could be adopted, aligning with current recommendations for likely benign SMTs. Intermediate-risk lesions (score 3–4) may warrant further diagnostic evaluation, such as EUS-FNA or contrast-enhanced EUS, to clarify pathological diagnosis before deciding on resection. High-risk lesions (score 5–6) could be prioritized for endoscopic or surgical resection in accordance with guideline-based management of suspected GISTs. This stratified approach not only complements existing protocols but also provides a reproducible, quantitative framework to standardize decision-making across different clinical settings.

In summary, we developed and validated a practical EUS-based risk-scoring system that effectively integrates tumor location, layer of origin, echogenicity, and growth pattern to preoperatively differentiate small gastric GISTs from other SMTs. The model demonstrated robust diagnostic performance and reliable risk stratification across both internal and external validation cohorts, offering a non-invasive, reproducible, and clinically applicable tool to support individualized treatment planning. While further prospective validation is warranted, this scoring system has the potential to enhance preoperative decision-making and optimize management strategies for patients with small gastric SMTs.
