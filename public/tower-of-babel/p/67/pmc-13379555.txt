Mesalazine‐Induced Myocarditis: A Challenging Diagnostic Journey
Authors: Mohamed Zakaria Bouayed, Iliass Benaini, Hajar Koulali, Safaâ Kachmar, Samia Berrichi, Amine Bensaid, Ghizlane Kharrasse, Houssam Bkiyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMesalazine‐induced myocarditis, though rare, should be suspected in patients presenting with acute chest pain and elevated cardiac biomarkers shortly after treatment initiation. Cardiac MRI plays a pivotal role in diagnosis. Prompt drug discontinuation often leads to rapid clinical recovery, highlighting the importance of early recognition to prevent severe complications.

Introduction

Mesalazine, also known as mesalamine or 5‐aminosalicylic acid, is an anti‐inflammatory drug widely used as a first‐line treatment for mild to moderate ulcerative colitis [1]. It exerts its therapeutic effect primarily through inhibition of cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways, resulting in decreased production of pro‐inflammatory mediators within the colonic mucosa [2]. Compared to sulfasalazine, mesalazine has a more favorable safety profile, with fewer systemic adverse effects [3].

Although generally well tolerated, mesalazine has been associated with a range of adverse events, occurring in approximately 15% of patients. The most commonly reported side effects include gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhea (0.5%–10.8%), nausea (0.2%–23.3%), and abdominal pain (up to 5.9%), as well as headache (0.5%–13.7%). More serious complications, including pancreatitis (0.3%–1.8%), hepatotoxicity (up to 4%), and renal impairment (up to 1%), are uncommon. Cardiac adverse events remain rare, with an estimated incidence of 0%–0.3%, while myocarditis and pericarditis have primarily been described in case reports [1, 4].

Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease of the myocardium characterized by a wide spectrum of clinical presentations, ranging from mild chest pain to cardiogenic shock [5]. Its pathophysiology is complex and may involve direct toxic effects or, more commonly, immune‐mediated mechanisms such as hypersensitivity reactions [5]. In the context of mesalazine therapy, myocarditis is thought to result predominantly from a cell‐mediated immune response [6].

Given its rarity and nonspecific presentation, mesalazine‐induced myocarditis remains a diagnostic challenge, particularly in settings where advanced imaging modalities are not readily available.

Case History / Examination

We report the case of a 46‐year‐old male patient with no prior medical history, who was diagnosed with ulcerative colitis. The initial symptoms began 2 years prior to this presentation, with the onset of abdominal pain and mucous bloody diarrhea of varying frequency and intensity, which the patient initially ignored. The symptoms worsened 2 weeks prior to his admission, with an increase in bowel movements up to 10 times per day, prompting the patient to seek medical attention.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Prior to admission to our institution, a colonoscopy was performed, revealing continuous inflammation without healthy mucosal intervals, extending from the lower rectum to the right colic angle. The patient was referred to our hospital's gastroenterology department, where a clinical assessment revealed diffuse abdominal tenderness and mucous discharge on rectal examination. Laboratory tests showed hemoglobin at 7.5 g/dL, WBC count at 16,130/mm3 (neutrophils 12,280/mm3), CRP at 221.56 mg/L, and procalcitonin at 0.25 μg/L. Parasitological stool tests revealed amoebic vegetative forms.

An abdominal contrast‐enhanced CT scan showed extensive inflammatory thickening of the bowel wall from the rectum to the right colic angle, suggestive of an inflammatory flare of ulcerative colitis. Recto‐sigmoidoscopy revealed a congested, erythematous, and fragile mucosa with superficial ulcers at 20 cm from the anal margin, consistent with active ulcerative colitis. Biopsy results confirmed acute ulcerative proctitis with histological features indicative of active ulcerative colitis.

Therapeutic management included oral (4 g/day) and rectal (1 g/day suppository) mesalazine, Ceftriaxone (2 g/day), Metronidazole (500 mg every 8 h), and thromboprophylaxis with enoxaparin sodium (4000 IU/day) showing ST‐segment elevation in the anteroseptal leads with reciprocal changes in the lateral territory.

The patient's digestive symptoms improved, with reduced bowel frequency and resolution of abdominal pain. However, 6 days into treatment, the patient developed retrosternal chest pain. An EKG revealed a regular sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 91 bpm, showing an ST‐segment elevation pattern suggestive of acute myocardial injury in the antero‐septal region with a mirror image in the apical‐lateral region (Figure 1). The 0/1‐h High‐sensitivity (HS) troponin I levels were 2393.4/7011.1 ng/L, and both transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and coronary angiography (shown in Figure 2, with the complete angiographic sequence provided in Supplementary Video S1) were normal.

Initially, the treatment approach was to rule out a type 2 myocardial infarction secondary to poorly tolerated anemia, and the patient was transfused with 3 units of PRBCs. Within hours, the patient developed acute dyspnea, with oxygen saturation dropping to 80% on ambient air, bilateral mid‐field crackles on lung auscultation, and profuse sweating. Arterial blood gas analysis showed a PaO2 of 58 mmHg, and his blood pressure was 156/77 mmHg. A contrast‐enhanced chest CT scan revealed alveolo‐interstitial pulmonary edema and mild bilateral pleural effusion, without signs of pulmonary embolism (Figure 3). The diagnosis of transfusion‐associated circulatory overload (TACO) was made, and the patient was treated with an intravenous bolus of furosemide (120 mg), followed by 40 mg every 6 h, resulting in slight improvement in dyspnea and reduced oxygen requirements.

Contrast chest CT scan showed alveolo‐interstitial pulmonary edema and mild bilateral pleural effusion.

Due to recurrent dyspnea and the onset of hypotension, the patient was transferred to our ICU for specialized management. An initial clinical assessment revealed a conscious patient with a GCS of 15/15, febrile with a temperature of 38.8°C, blood pressure of 79/44 mmHg (mean arterial pressure of 56 mmHg), heart rate of 119 bpm, absence of congestive signs, respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 81% on ambient air (94% with 10 L/min oxygen therapy). Left basilar crackles persisted on lung auscultation. A repeat TTE showed left ventricular hypertrophy, akinesia of the apex, septal, and inferior walls, as well as the mid and apical segments of the lateral wall, with severe left ventricular dysfunction (LVEF of 30%), elevated left ventricular filling pressure (LVFP), a dilated left atrium, and normal right ventricular function without pericardial effusion. A cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) was subsequently performed within the first day of ICU admission to further assess myocardial involvement, revealing typical findings of acute myocarditis (Figure 4).

Cardiac MRI showing: (a) T1 mapping sequence with homogeneous myocardial signal values and no significant elevation; (b) no perfusion defect on first‐pass perfusion imaging; (c, d) late gadolinium enhancement (arrows) involving the septal and lateral walls, with a non‐ischemic pattern consistent with acute myocarditis.

Laboratory tests showed an inflammatory syndrome with WBC of 23,200/mm3 (neutrophils 20,280/mm3), CRP of 299.27 mg/L, procalcitonin of 18.43 μg/L, elevated cardiac enzymes with NT‐ProBNP of 67,426.4 pg/mL and BNP of 4964 pg/mL, and hemoglobin of 9.7 g/dL. Arterial blood gas analysis showed a pH of 7.50, PaO2 of 76 mmHg (with 10 L/min oxygen supplementation), PaCO2 of 24.2 mmHg, HCO₃− of 18.8 mmol/L, and lactate of 1.51 mmol/L. A cytobacteriological examination of urine (CBEU) revealed a urinary tract infection with leukocyturia of 74,000/mm3, and urine culture confirmed the presence of Escherichia coli . Multiplex PCR testing on nasopharyngeal and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples returned negative results, and blood cultures were also negative.

Our therapeutic management included the discontinuation of mesalazine, oxygen therapy, cautious fluid resuscitation, hemodynamic optimization using continuous intravenous infusion of dobutamine via a central internal jugular catheter to maintain a mean BP of approximately 65 mmHg, continued antibiotic therapy with intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole, and minimally invasive continuous hemodynamic monitoring using the FloTrac/EV1000 system (Figure 5).

He was discharged from our ICU with a blood pressure of 123/68 mmHg, a heart rate of 92 bpm, and an oxygen saturation of 98% on ambient air and was transferred to the gastroenterology department for further management.

The patient was subsequently started on Mercaptopurine as an alternative treatment for ulcerative colitis. A follow‐up transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) 2 months later revealed normal left ventricular contractility and function, with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 55%. Currently, he reports an average of three bowel movements per day, without mucus or blood, and no associated abdominal pain, rectal syndrome, or extra‐intestinal manifestations. However, he continues to experience anemia, for which he receives periodic intravenous iron supplementation.

Given the temporal relationship between the initiation of mesalazine and the onset of cardiac symptoms, the electrical, biological, and echocardiographic abnormalities in contrast with normal coronary angiography, and most importantly, the MRI findings as well as the patient's improvement following the discontinuation of mesalazine, we concluded that the diagnosis was acute heart failure secondary to mesalazine‐induced myocarditis.

Discussion

Mesalazine is a first‐line treatment for mild‐to‐moderate ulcerative colitis, with well‐established efficacy in both inducing and maintaining remission, and overall good tolerability [1].

Acute myocarditis is a rare complication of mesalazine therapy [4, 6, 7, 8]. Although its exact pathogenesis remains unclear, it is more likely attributed to a cell‐mediated hypersensitivity reaction rather than direct cardiotoxicity [9]. Clinically, it can present with a wide range of nonspecific symptoms, from mild chest pain to cardiogenic shock.

Myocarditis is a complex condition often requiring a multifaceted diagnostic approach for accurate assessment. Although the definitive diagnosis relies on sophisticated techniques such as cardiac MRI, endomyocardial biopsy, and advanced molecular testing [10], which may not be readily available in all healthcare settings, it can also be strongly suggested by a combination of clinical, biological, electrocardiographic, and echocardiographic findings [11].

In our case, the patient's symptoms, along with elevated cardiac enzymes, an ST‐segment elevation pattern on EKG, and echocardiographic abnormalities despite normal coronary angiography, strongly suggested mesalazine‐induced acute myocarditis. Cardiac MRI demonstrated a non‐ischemic pattern of late gadolinium enhancement involving the septal and lateral walls, a finding typical of acute myocarditis, according to the Lake Louise criteria [10], further supporting the diagnosis.

This was reinforced by the temporal relationship with mesalazine use, the resolution of symptoms following its discontinuation, and negative multiplex PCR and blood cultures.

The therapeutic management of mesalazine‐induced myocarditis begins with discontinuation of the offending drug and may involve additional treatments such as corticosteroids [6] which were considered as potential second‐line treatments in this case. In addition to mesalazine discontinuation, our patient's hemodynamic instability warranted the use of vasopressors. However, we believe the hypotension was not solely due to heart failure but also likely resulted from the misuse of furosemide without adequate hemodynamic monitoring. This situation made us reluctant to definitively diagnose cardiogenic or hypovolemic shock, particularly in the absence of signs of hypoperfusion, such as elevated lactate levels. Our management strategy focused on stabilizing the patient while closely monitoring for any signs of worsening cardiac function. Mechanical circulatory support devices were considered as contingency options but were not required due to the patient's positive response to initial treatments.

Although many cases of drug‐induced myocarditis resolve following discontinuation of the offending agent, long‐term outcomes remain variable. Some patients may develop persistent myocardial fibrosis, leading to chronic heart failure or arrhythmias, and in severe cases, progression to end‐stage cardiomyopathy requiring heart transplantation has been reported [12, 13]. These observations underscore the importance of early recognition and close follow‐up. In addition, growing evidence suggests that inflammatory pathways play a central role in myocardial injury, opening perspectives for targeted therapies such as interleukin‐1 inhibition and other immunomodulatory strategies [14].

Incorporating the patient's perspective into this case report, it became evident that he experienced significant confusion and a sense of betrayal by his own body. He expressed deep concern and anxiety over the possibility that future treatments might lead to similar complications. These emotions underscore the substantial psychological burden associated with chronic diseases like ulcerative colitis and the uncertainty surrounding treatment options, emphasizing the importance of a holistic approach that integrates psychological support alongside medical management. Addressing these concerns is essential to ensure treatment adherence and improve overall patient outcomes.
