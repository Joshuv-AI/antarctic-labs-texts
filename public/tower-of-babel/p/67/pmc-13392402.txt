A physical model test study on the influence of particle size on the landquake characteristics of rock avalanches
Authors: Fengge Shi, Zhongfu Wang, Dan Bi, Jingying Wang, Xusheng Zhang, Zixuan Wang, Leyu Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs sudden-onset geological disasters, the dynamic behavior of rock avalanches is significantly influenced by the size of the debris particles formed during rock mass disintegration. In this study, 59 sets of laboratory model tests were conducted to systematically investigate the effects of particle size on the characteristic parameters of landquake signals (Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, mean frequency, and vibration energy), and to explore the roles of release height and particle mixing. The results indicate that as particle size increases, the intensity-related parameters (Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, and vibration energy) exhibit exponential growth, while the mean frequency decreases exponentially. This suggests that larger particles release higher energy at lower frequencies—a particle size effect that is independent of release height and thus of universal validity. Granular flows with mixed particle sizes display an inverse grading characteristic during movement, causing the landquake signal characteristics to be biased toward those of the finer particles. This confirms that the landquake signal is primarily generated by the interaction between the lowermost layer of particles and the base plate. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for inverting rock avalanche particle size characteristics from landquake signals and for disaster early warning.Keywords: Rock avalanche, Laboratory model test, Landquake, Granular flow, SpectrogramSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Rock avalanches are sudden-onset geological disasters characterized by enormous volume, exceptionally high velocity, and unpredictably long run-out distances, often resulting in catastrophic consequences. Consequently, they represent a frontier and hotspot in international geohazard research. During movement, the collapsing mass undergoes fragmentation, disintegrating into debris particles that migrate or diffuse in a fluid-like manner, forming a rock avalanche1–8 This fragmentation process is key to the long-run-out mechanism of rock avalanches, and the particle size of the resulting debris is the most direct reflection of the degree of rock mass fragmentation9. Influenced by factors such as lithology, and the type and development degree of rock structural planes, the rock mass undergoes varying degrees of fragmentation during movement, producing debris particles of different sizes. These particle sizes, in turn, significantly affect the dynamic characteristics of the rock avalanche10–12.

However, the source areas of rock avalanches are often located in sparsely populated regions, and the lack of sufficient observational data complicates the study of their movement processes and mechanisms13–15. In recent years, with the continuous improvement of global seismic networks and the deepening research by seismogeologists on seismic waves, utilizing seismic signals to extract critical information about geological disasters has gained increasing attention16,17. Existing research shows that during geological processes such as rockfalls, landslides, and debris flows, seismic stations record ground motion signals distinct from tectonic earthquakes—termed “landquakes”18,19,20. Studying landquake signals has emerged as a novel approach for geological disaster research, offering distinct advantages and complementarity in investigating rock avalanche kinematics21,22.

Compared to low-frequency components, high-frequency landquake signals contain richer information related to geological disasters. By investigating the relationship between high-frequency signal characteristics and key event properties, quantitative evaluation and inversion of disaster features become feasible. Analysis of rockfall events and their associated seismic signals in the Alps demonstrates that seismic waves can effectively identify the location, volume, geometry, and movement evolution of rockfalls23,24. Furthermore, the ratio of seismic energy released (E_S) to potential energy reduction (E_P) during landquakes falls within the range of 10−3 to 10−525. The propagation of high-frequency landquake signals is influenced by multiple factors, including lithology, topography, and geological structure, which can lead to signal suppression, dispersion, or attenuation. Conducting small-scale model tests in controllable laboratory environments equipped with monitoring instruments can help overcome the limitations and shortcomings inherent in field studies20. Farin et al.26 through laboratory single-particle impact tests, established an analytical relationship between impactor mass, velocity, and seismic signal characteristics based on Hertz impact theory, demonstrating that impactor parameters could be inverted from seismic signals within a factor of three. Yu et al.27 systematically controlled particle size variables using particle image velocimetry combined with simultaneous measurements of basal force and acceleration. They found that seismic energy correlated positively with normalized stress fluctuations to a power of approximately 1.5, with increasing inertial number intensifying inter-particle collisions and thus enhancing signal strength. Li et al.28 conducted flume tests controlling particle size while simultaneously measuring basal force fluctuations and seismic signals. They observed that both followed a generalized Pareto distribution, and that particle size significantly influenced their negatively correlated evolution. These controlled laboratory experiments, by precisely varying parameters like particle size, have revealed intrinsic links between granular flow dynamics and seismic signals, providing methodological references for this study on the influence of particle size on landquake characteristics.

Currently, research on landquake signals largely focuses on inverting force-time functions from low-frequency signals or establishing empirical relationships between high-frequency signals and dynamic parameters. Farin et al.29,30 systematically investigated the relationship between granular flow dynamics and seismic signals through laboratory granular column collapses, further establishing empirical scaling laws linking radiated seismic energy to factors such as particle diameter, column mass, and slope angle. However, their use of spherical particles, which predominantly roll, differs from the sliding motion typical of actual rock avalanche debris. Bachelet et al.31 analyzed high-frequency seismic signal characteristics during the steady movement phase of granular flows in flume tests, but their focus was on the steady flow stage, not the critical process of the flow impacting the slope toe. Furthermore, these studies primarily employed either single particle sizes or narrow size ranges, without systematically investigating the quantitative impact of a wider range of particle sizes and mixtures on landquake signals. A key feature distinguishing rock avalanches from other hazards is the rapid disintegration of the rock mass during motion, where particle size differences directly influence the complex internal dynamics of the granular flow. Therefore, this study utilizes naturally crushed gravel in physical model tests to simulate the complete process of a rock avalanche granular flow sliding and impacting the slope toe. It systematically investigates the quantitative relationship between particle size and landquake signal characteristics, aiming to provide experimental data and a scientific basis for emergency response and rapid assessment of rock avalanche disasters.

Laboratory model test design

Model setup

Rock avalanches typically develop on steep, free faces and exhibit failure modes such as sliding, toppling, or falling. The most common kinematic characteristic is high-velocity movement along the slope after detachment, followed by a high-angle impact at the slope toe. To analyze the process by which a rock avalanche impacts the slope toe and radiates seismic waves under this movement mode, this study designed and conducted multiple sets of sliding impact tests using granular flows with different particle sizes, employing the granular flow to simulate the movement and impact process of the unstable mass.

The laboratory physical model setup primarily consists of a retaining gate, a flume, an impact base plate, data acquisition instruments, and a support structure. Previous studies have shown that uniformly composed acrylic sheets ensure stable propagation of seismic waves and guarantee good repeatability of experimental phenomena and typical seismic signal characteristics32,33. Accordingly, both the flume and the base plate in this experiment were fabricated from acrylic sheets, with dimensions shown in Fig. 1. The setup is supported by a steel frame with a height of 20 cm. Five slots at different heights (H1–H5) are carved into the interior of the flume, allowing the retaining gate to be placed at different positions to adjust the particle release height.The data acquisition system is controlled by a Donghua DH8302 dynamic data acquisition instrument, with a sampling rate set to 100 kHz to fully cover the frequency band of the test signals. An accelerometer (model DH1A314E, range 0–1000 m/s2) is fixed to the back of the impact base plate. Prior to installation, it was calibrated using a standard vibration shaker to ensure a flat frequency response within the test frequency band. The sensor is connected to a high-performance computer via the acquisition instrument for real-time data display and storage. A high-speed camera (frame rate 850 fps) is fixed laterally to the model to monitor the sliding-impact process and the final deposition morphology of the granular flow.

Test material selection

Conducting small-scale similarity tests by selecting appropriate raw materials to create analogous materials has been widely applied in various geological disaster studies. Based on the source characteristics of natural geological disasters such as rock avalanches, selecting raw materials to produce similar test materials for small-scale laboratory experiments, and applying the similarity principle to analyze the results, can yield conclusions close to real-world conditions.

Currently, two types of materials are commonly used in physical model tests for landquake research. One type is irregular, angular quartz sand particles, and the other is physically and chemically stable steel or glass beads, with the latter often employed for theoretical analyses34. However, quartz sand typically has a narrow particle size range, while spherical materials like steel or glass beads exhibit movement patterns in flume models that differ from actual rock avalanches. Steel beads generally move by rolling in the flume, whereas the primary movement mode of actual rock avalanche particles should be sliding—a substantial difference between the two. Therefore, to make the test results more representative of reality, the granular material used in this study is naturally crushed, angular gravel (marble lithology) with particle sizes ranging from 2 mm to 14 mm (see Fig. 1). This gravel is hard, resistant to fragmentation, maintains good physical stability, and yields better experimental outcomes (Table 1).

Selection of characteristic parameters

Time-domain characteristic parameters of landquake signals mainly include signal peak amplitude, mean of the signal envelope, and Arias intensity. These parameters are obtained directly from calculations performed on the time-domain curve without complex processing, offering an intuitive description. Frequency-domain characteristic parameters, including predominant frequency, mean frequency, and bandwidth, are calculated from the frequency-domain curve of the preprocessed landquake signal, which is obtained via Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). Compared to time-domain parameters, frequency-domain parameters are less directly descriptive; patterns difficult to discern in the time domain often become apparent in the frequency domain. Furthermore, during movement, a rock avalanche radiates vibration energy in the form of elastic waves. This seismic energy holds significant value for estimating dynamic parameters such as event volume35.

Therefore, this study selects the Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, mean frequency, and vibration energy of the signals from different test conditions as characteristic parameters for analysis. The physical meaning and calculation methods for these four parameters are as follows. Before formal calculation, all acceleration signals undergo preprocessing: obviously anomalous waveforms are removed, a 5 Hz high-pass filter eliminates low-frequency drift, and a 50 kHz low-pass filter suppresses high-frequency electrical noise. The cutoff frequency of 50 kHz was selected based on the estimated contact duration of the smallest particles (2 mm). According to Hertzian impact theory, the contact duration for a 2 mm marble particle impacting at a typical velocity (~ 2 m/s) is approximately 15–30 µs, corresponding to a characteristic frequency of 30–70 kHz. Thus, the 50 kHz low-pass filter covers the dominant frequency range of the smallest particles, ensuring that high-frequency components are not substantially lost. A Hanning window is applied to the signal before Fourier transformation to reduce spectral leakage. For spectral analysis, FFT was used to convert the time-domain signal to the frequency domain.

Analysis of results

To assess the repeatability of the experiments, each test condition was repeated three times. To reduce random errors, the spectral amplitudes from the three repeated tests were averaged, and the resulting spectral curves were smoothed using a 5-point smoothing method. The error bars in Figs. 3, 4, 5, and 6 represent the standard deviation of the three repeated measurements. The calculations show that, under all conditions, the standard deviation of each parameter is less than 8% of the mean value, indicating good experimental repeatability. Therefore, the data scatter observed in the figures mainly arises from systematic variations across different test conditions (e.g., particle size, mixing ratio, drop height). In the statistical analysis, the relationship between characteristic parameters and particle size was examined by least-squares regression analysis. Depending on the distribution of the data points, either linear or nonlinear models were selected for fitting, and the 95% confidence intervals of the regression curves were calculated. Influence of particle size

Collapsing masses of different lithologies undergo varying degrees of fragmentation during movement. Hard rock types typically experience relatively low fragmentation, tending to form debris flows with relatively larger particles, whereas soft rock types undergo high fragmentation during movement, easily forming fine-particle debris flows. Differences in debris particle size significantly influence the characteristic parameters of their landquake signals.

By applying FFT to the acceleration signals from test conditions A1–A7, we obtained the amplitude-frequency curves (left column of Fig. 7) and spectrograms representing energy distribution (right column of Fig. 7) for each condition. The frequency-domain curves (Fig. 7, left) show that particle size significantly affects frequency characteristics. As particle size increases from 2 mm to 14 mm, the spectrum undergoes a systematic shift: the amplitude of high-frequency components (> 30 kHz) attenuates markedly, while the amplitude of low-frequency components (< 10 kHz) increases sharply, leading directly to a significant decrease in the mean frequency of the signal. This phenomenon has a clear physical mechanism: larger particles, possessing greater mass and inertia, experience longer contact durations during collisions with the base, thereby exciting more low-frequency radiation energy. In contrast, collisions of fine particles exhibit broadband characteristics with richer high-frequency content, consistent with the elastic collision theory where “contact time is proportional to particle radius.” The spectrograms (Fig. 7, right) intuitively confirm this frequency shift. With increasing particle size, the energy distribution contracts and concentrates from a broad band (0–50 kHz) towards the low-frequency end (< 10 kHz), where energy in the high-frequency region (> 30 kHz) nearly disappears, while total energy intensity significantly increases. This indicates that impacts from larger blocks are not only higher-energy release events but also concentrate their released energy in lower frequency bands, implying that increasing particle size fundamentally alters the source energy radiation spectrum. In natural rock avalanches, this suggests that the frequency content of seismic signals received at stations could serve as a key diagnostic tool for inferring the fragmentation degree and block size distribution in the source area.

Influence of release height on particle size effects

The results from Group A indicate that particle size significantly influences the characteristic parameters of rock avalanche landquake signals, exhibiting an approximate exponential relationship. However, whether this particle size effect is affected by release height remains unclear. To investigate this question, we designed Group B tests (B1–B25) comprising five release heights, with tests B11–B15 serving as overlapping conditions identical to A1–A5.

Taking release height H4 as an example, Fast Fourier Transform was applied to the acceleration signals from tests B16–B20, yielding the amplitude-frequency curves (Fig. 8, left) and spectrograms (Fig. 8, right) for this height. As shown in Fig. 8, with increasing particle size under the H4 condition, the variation patterns of both the frequency-domain curves and spectrograms closely resemble those observed in Group A (H3 condition, tests A1–A7) presented in Fig. 7.

It can be observed that, across different release heights, the trends of the vibration signal characteristics with increasing particle size are largely consistent: the Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, and vibration energy all exhibit a near‑exponential growth, while the mean frequency shows a linear decrease. These findings suggest that the influence of particle size on landquake signal characteristics is highly independent of release height – whenever the particle size changes, the characteristic parameters of the signal change accordingly, and this behaviour does not vary with release height.

Influence of particle mass proportion

Groups A and B employed sieved single-size particles to facilitate the identification of the influence of particle size on landquake signal characteristics. However, in natural rock avalanches, the particle size composition of the debris is extremely complex during movement, typically spanning from 0.1 m to 20 m. Such complex size compositions inevitably lead to more intricate variations in landquake signal characteristics. Therefore, to investigate how the characteristic parameters of landquake signals change when particles of different sizes are mixed, we designed tests C1–C9 by mixing fine particles (2 mm) and coarse particles (14 mm) in different mass proportions while keeping the total mass constant, systematically examining the effect of particle mass proportion on landquake signal characteristics.

We selected the amplitude-frequency curves (Fig. 9, left) and spectrograms representing energy distribution (Fig. 9, right) for conditions C1, C3, C5, C7, and C9. We observed that as the proportion of coarse particles increased, the dominance of low-frequency energy strengthened: the amplitude of low-frequency components (< 10 kHz) increases dramatically, and energy concentrates significantly towards the low-frequency end. This is consistent with the Group A conclusions, stemming from the low-frequency radiation characteristics of large mass blocks. Unlike Group A, where high-frequency energy (> 30 kHz) nearly vanished with increasing particle size, in the mixed-size tests, the high-frequency energy amplitude persists without significant attenuation. The spectrograms clearly show that even when the coarse particle proportion reaches 90% (C9), the high-frequency region (> 30 kHz) retains notable energy. This apparent contradiction points to the intrinsic nature of the mixed system: high-frequency signals primarily originate from sustained friction and collisions between fine particles and the base plate; low-frequency signals primarily originate from impacts of coarse particles. In a mixed system, these are not simply substitutive but coexist and compete. When a small number of coarse particles (e.g., C1, 10% proportion) are added to a fine-particle matrix, their impact energy may be masked by the abundant high-frequency signals from fine particles, making the spectrum resemble that of pure fine particles. Conversely, when coarse particles form the matrix (e.g., C9), even though fine particles are few, their direct contact with the base plate can still excite measurable high-frequency signals.

Amplitude and spectrogram under test conditions C1-C9.

To investigate the cause of this phenomenon, we compared the post-flow deposit photographs of cases C1 and C9. Figure 10 shows the grain stacking structures after impact for both cases, both exhibiting clear inverse grading—fine particles at the bottom and coarse particles enriched at the top. This inverse grading is a typical product of size segregation in dense granular flows. Mechanistically, Savage and Lun41 attributed size segregation in sheared granular flows to two coupled processes: kinetic sieving, where fine particles preferentially fall into gaps between coarse particles under gravity and migrate to the bottom, and squeeze expulsion, where coarse particles are pushed upward by unbalanced contact forces. Gray and Thornton42 further developed a theoretical model for shallow granular avalanches, revealing from a continuum perspective the dynamic segregation process in which fine particles accumulate at the base and coarse particles move to the surface. These theoretical mechanisms are visually confirmed in our experiments. As shown in Fig. 11, to determine when inverse grading emerges and influences the vibration signals, we analyzed high‑speed camera images from the lower part of the chute in case C1. Inverse grading was already evident as the granular flow reached the lower chute, with coarse particles predominantly distributed in the upper half of the flow. Tracking the trajectory of one coarse particle initially at the bottom (red dashed line in Fig. 11, right panel) shows that it gradually moves upward due to the squeezing effect of the surrounding fine particles – a direct experimental manifestation of the squeeze‑expulsion mechanism.

It is worth noting that the theoretical models of Savage and Lun41 and Gray and Thornton42 are primarily based on steady‑state shear flow assumptions and effectively describe the final segregated state, but they do not provide direct quantitative analysis of the transient evolution from well‑mixed to fully inverse‑graded conditions, nor of the consequent dynamic basal contact. Our study, using high‑speed imaging, captures the dynamic development of inverse grading and further links the segregation outcome to the vibration signal characteristics generated when the flow impacts the base plate. Combining the observations in Figs. 10 and 11, it can be inferred that fine particles already occupy the bottom position through kinetic sieving before the debris flow impacts the base plate. Consequently, the primary dynamic contact with the base plate is with the fine‑particle layer at the flow base. Regardless of the coarse particle proportion, as long as fine particles are present, they tend to occupy the basal contact layer and continuously excite high‑frequency vibrations. Coarse particles can only generate strong low‑frequency radiation if they penetrate or influence this basal fine layer and effectively transfer their large impact momentum to the base plate. When coarse particles are few and have been segregated to the surface (e.g., case C1), their contribution to basal vibrations is negligible, resulting in very weak low‑frequency signals. This finding is in good agreement with the inverse‑grading segregation model of Gray and Thornton37 the final segregated state – fine particles sinking and coarse particles rising in a dense granular avalanche – directly determines the dynamic contact characteristics between the flow base and the base plate.

High-speed imaging reveals that inverse grading does not appear instantaneously but develops progressively as the granular flow travels along the flume. At the initial stage after release, the particles remain relatively mixed. As the flow moves downstream, kinetic sieving allows fine particles to percolate downward through the gaps between coarse particles, while squeeze expulsion gradually pushes coarse particles toward the upper layers. Consequently, the degree of inverse grading becomes more pronounced with increasing travel distance. By the time the flow reaches the lower flume and impacts the base plate, a well-stratified structure has already formed, with fine particles dominating the basal contact layer. This distance-dependent intensification of inverse grading implies that for longer transportation paths—such as those typical of large-scale rock avalanches—the basal contact layer will be even more enriched in fine particles. As a result, the landquake signal characteristics will be further biased toward those of the finer fraction, regardless of the overall particle size distribution of the debris.

By plotting the mixed-size data (against their mean particle size) alongside the single-size fitted curves from Group B, the mixing effect is quantified. The data points for Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, and vibration energy from mixed-size tests systematically fall below the fitted values for single-size particles corresponding to the same mean size. This indicates that, for the same total mass, the vibration efficiency of a mixed-size system is lower than that of a single coarse-particle system. Some energy is likely absorbed or dissipated during transfer from coarse particles to the base plate through the intervening fine-particle buffer layer. The data points for mean frequency from mixed-size tests are systematically higher than the fitted values for single-size particles corresponding to the same mean size. This directly confirms the contribution of the basal fine-particle contact layer to high-frequency signals, raising the overall mean frequency. Both observations are attributed to the influence of the inverse grading characteristic described above.

To verify whether the above pattern holds true for mixed particle systems in general, additional mixing schemes were designed: mixtures of 2 mm and 8 mm particles (conditions C10–C18) and mixtures of 8 mm and 14 mm particles (conditions C19–C27). The resulting comparison plots of characteristic parameters between mixed-size and single-size particles are shown in Fig. 6. The comparison curves clearly demonstrate that for both new particle size combinations, the vibration characteristic parameters (Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, vibration energy, and mean frequency) of the mixed particles exhibit systematic deviation patterns identical to those observed in Fig. 5, when compared to the single-size fitted values based on the mean particle size. This repeated verification provides strong evidence that the pattern—“the landquake signal characteristics of a mixed-size system are systematically biased toward the contribution of the smaller size fraction”—is not an exception but a core physical phenomenon that holds true across different particle size ranges. Whether mixing fine-medium, medium-coarse, or fine-coarse particles, the dynamic response of the system is significantly modulated by the presence of the smaller particles. This pattern represents a manifestation of fundamental physical mechanisms such as flow segregation and momentum buffering across different scales.

Discussion

This study reveals quantitative relationships between particle size and characteristic parameters of landquake signals through physical model tests. The results show that as particle size increases, Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, and vibration energy increase exponentially, while mean frequency decreases exponentially. This finding is consistent with the negative correlation between mass and frequency established in single-particle impact tests by Farin et al.26. However, this study extends the investigation to granular flow systems and further reveals the modulating effect of inverse grading on landquake signals.

Nevertheless, when extrapolating laboratory results to natural scales, the influence of scaling effects must be carefully considered. In these tests, particle sizes ranged from 2 to 14 mm, and the acrylic plate thickness was 10 mm. In contrast, natural rock avalanche debris can reach meters to tens of meters in size, and the elastic modulus of rock is far greater than that of acrylic. According to Hertz impact theory, impact duration is proportional to particle radius and inversely proportional to the square root of the material’s elastic modulus29. Therefore, the dominant frequencies of natural rock avalanche landquake signals are typically below 10 Hz, whereas the signals in these tests reach the kHz range. This difference in frequency magnitude does not invalidate the trend of a negative particle size-frequency correlation but indicates that the conclusions here are primarily applicable for interpreting the directional influence of relative changes in particle size on landquake characteristics, rather than for direct numerical comparison. Iverson43 noted in a review of physical modeling of granular flows that while small-scale models cannot fully replicate the absolute magnitudes of natural events, they are invaluable for revealing controlling factors and evolutionary laws.The inverse grading observed in these tests and its modulating effect on landquake signals offer a new perspective for understanding the source mechanisms of natural rock avalanches44. During granular flow, small particles progressively percolate to the base, dominating interactions with the substrate. This is consistent with particle segregation phenomena observed in flume tests by Goujon et al.45. This mechanism implies that even if the source area of a natural rock avalanche has a low degree of fragmentation and a high proportion of coarse particles, the signals received by seismic stations might still be dominated by components excited by fine particles. This could partially explain the anomalously rich high-frequency content observed in landquake signals from some large rock avalanche events24. Therefore, when inverting particle size distribution from landquake signals, one must consider the amplifying effect of particle segregation on the contribution of the shallow source layer; otherwise, the actual proportion of coarse particles might be systematically underestimated. It should be noted, however, that the acrylic base plate used in this study (E = 4.4 GPa) is softer than natural rock substrates (E = 10–100 GPa), which may affect the absolute values of frequency and energy. Nevertheless, the relative trends and the inverse-grading modulation are robust and independent of substrate material, supporting their applicability to natural settings. Future studies employing rock-like panels could further validate quantitative extrapolation.

From the perspective of disaster monitoring applications, the particle size-signal characteristic relationships established here have clear practical value. A decrease in mean frequency could serve as a precursor indicator for the onset of coarse particle dominance and increased energy release risk. The exponential increase in Arias intensity suggests that once large particles begin to mobilize, the destructive potential escalates rapidly. Recently, some researchers have attempted to apply empirical relationships calibrated from laboratory tests to interpret field monitoring data46,47, providing preliminary validation of the effectiveness of frequency-based indicators in estimating particle size.

Conclusions

This study investigated the influence of rock avalanche particle size on four key characteristic parameters of landquake signals through laboratory model tests, and further examined the effects of release height and particle mixing on signal characteristics. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) As particle size increases, the Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, and vibration energy of landquake signals exhibit an exponential increase, while the mean frequency shows an exponential decrease. This indicates that larger rock avalanche blocks release higher energy at lower frequencies.

(2) The influence of particle size on landquake signal characteristics is independent of release height. Regardless of the height from which particles are released, any change in particle size leads to corresponding changes in the characteristic parameters. This implies that in real-world rock avalanche events, even with different movement paths, the fragmentation process consistently exerts a significant influence on landquake signals.

(3) When coarse and fine particles are mixed, the inverse grading characteristic causes fine particles to preferentially contact the base plate and excite landquake signals. Consequently, the landquake signals of mixed particles are more strongly influenced by the particle size of the lowermost layer, and the characteristic parameters are systematically biased toward those of the finer particles. For a given mean particle size, the Arias intensity, mean of the envelope, and vibration energy of mixed-size particles are noticeably lower than those of single-size particles, while the mean frequency is noticeably higher.

(4) Future research could further explore the following directions: ① introducing particles of different lithologies to investigate the influence of elastic modulus on landquake signals; ② combining numerical discrete element simulations to reveal the energy distribution mechanisms between inter-particle collisions and basal interactions within the granular flow; ③ conducting large-scale centrifuge tests to validate the quantitative effects of scaling; and ④ comparing test results with field-measured landquake signals to establish empirical models for particle size inversion.
