A Machine Learning Driven Approach to Quantifying Coronary Artery Tortuosity
Authors: Jose Roberto Tello Ayala, Kelvin Supriami, Siddharth Swaroop, Samuel F. Friedman, Ozan Unlu, Jennifer Halford, Mahnaz Maddah, Roukoz Abou-Karam, Eugene Pomerantsev, Patrick T. Ellinor, Finale Doshi-Velez, Akl C. Fahed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe associations between coronary artery tortuosity and age, sex, and cardiovascular risk factors are not fully established. Prior studies enrolled fewer than 1,000 subjects and relied on heuristic metrics.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to develop and validate an automated right coronary artery (RCA) tortuosity measure using machine learning (ML)–based vessel segmentation and examine its association with sex, age, coronary artery disease (CAD), and cardiovascular risk factors.MethodsWe developed an ML-enabled pipeline to quantify RCA tortuosity as a continuous measure in 38,691 RCA angiograms from 22,334 patients and evaluated concordance with blinded interventional cardiologist review in a subset of 300 angiograms. Regression models were used to study the association of tortuosity with age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, smoking, and presence of CAD.ResultsTortuosity ranged from 0.007 to 0.289. Blinded interventional cardiologist classification of high tortuosity vs not had 85.6% precision. Tortuosity was higher in women (β per SD = 0.17; P < 0.001) and higher with hypertension (β per SD = 0.06; P = 0.002), but lower with diabetes (β per SD = -0.11, P < 0.001. After adjustment for risk factors, age was not independently associated with tortuosity. Hypercholesterolemia and smoking were not associated. Higher tortuosity was associated with CAD (OR per SD = 1.05; P < 0.001), severe CAD (OR per SD = 1.09; P < 0.001), and higher Gensini score (β per SD = 0.05; P < 0.001), even after adjustment.ConclusionsWe derived a scalable ML-enabled measure of RCA tortuosity from coronary angiography and found associations with sex, hypertension, diabetes, and presence and severity of CAD.

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of mortality and morbidity in the world,1 yet understanding of the genetic and clinical drivers of disease remains limited. Prior research efforts have relied on: 1) large data sets with limited phenotypes—such as genetic association studies using a binary diagnosis code for CAD;2,3 and 2) small data sets with deep phenotyping, including coronary physiology, intravascular imaging, or biomarkers.4

Coronary angiography is the gold standard diagnostic test for CAD, as it provides anatomic characterization of coronary atherosclerosis, which can prognosticate disease and guide treatment decisions.5 Coronary angiography films are a rich source of information about the patient and disease state, including coronary tree anatomy, plaque distribution and burden, and degree of calcification, but remain underutilized in the research setting. The adoption of machine learning (ML) methods in health care has the potential to change how these data are used. One key feature in coronary imaging is arterial tortuosity, defined as high curvature or excessive bending or kinking of the coronary vessel structure.6 Visual and automated computational approaches have been proposed to quantify coronary tortuosity, but the field has not arrived at a consensus on a rigorous, standardized tortuosity metric.6

Prior studies performed tortuosity measurements manually based on binary visual coding (eg, tortuous vs nontortuous) and pattern descriptions (eg, S- or C-shaped elongations, kinking, looping, coiling).7, 8, 9 While useful for initial phenotyping, they lack continuous measures and suffer from poor reproducibility and interobserver noise.10 Earlier tortuosity metrics, such as the Tortuosity Index,7 Sum-of-Angles,11 and Inflection-Count Metric,12 provide reproducible scalar values, yet their reliance on global quantities (arc length, total turning angle, inflection count) leads to oversight of local morphological cues. As a result, a curve with more twists and turns can be assigned the same score as a smoother one.13

Within the field of cardiology, computer vision techniques have enabled the extraction of imaging features at unprecedented scale from echocardiograms14 and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging.15 Studies have shown that multiple ML-derived biomarkers can be derived with computer vision (eg, aortic size, body fat distribution, cardiac fibrosis, liver fat)15, 16, 17 and used to reveal associations with cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, improving our understanding of cardiac disease endophenotypes and subclinical imaging features. However, large-scale, standardized computations of coronary arterial tortuosity have not yet been performed, nor has the relationship between tortuosity, CAD, and related cardiovascular risk factors been explored.6

Preliminary studies showed that coronary tortuosity is associated with lower risk of CAD,18,19 but positively correlated with risk factors such as hypertension20 and conditions such as spontaneous coronary artery dissection.21 Some research also suggests that tortuous arteries are more common in older patients and females.22, 23, 24, 25 However, these studies have been limited by small sample sizes and heuristic measurements of tortuosity. Previous image processing of coronary angiography has mainly focused on segmenting coronary arteries for comparison with quantitative coronary angiography rather than on extracting clinically relevant phenotypes.26, 27, 28 Here, we develop a scalable and reproducible ML-enabled approach to measure coronary artery tortuosity, apply it to measure tortuosity in 38,691 right coronary artery (RCA) angiograms from 22,334 patients, and describe the associations between tortuosity and age, sex, cardiovascular risk factors, and CAD.

Methods

Study cohort

This study was conducted using data from the Massachusetts General Hospital cardiac catheterization registry. This registry includes 81,798 coronary angiograms performed between 2000 and 2021 on 31,513 patients with mapping of deidentified Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images and metadata. From this registry, we derived a novel ML-powered approach to quantify RCA tortuosity on 38,691 right coronary angiography loops from 22,334 patients using the left anterior oblique (LAO) view. This study used an LAO view with primary angle ranging from −10◦ to 100◦, wider than the conventional range of −5◦ to 95◦,29 to account for measurement variability and ensure comprehensive inclusion of LAO images.30 Patients without an RCA angiogram loop in LAO view (n = 5,172), with poor angiography quality or artifacts such as wires and devices over the coronary (n = 1,438), and with inappropriate length of the RCA related to magnification or panning (n = 2,569) were excluded (Supplemental Figure 1). In this context, “38,691 angiograms” denotes angiographic loops rather than distinct procedures or follow-up angiograms; temporal follow-up information was not used in this analysis. Some patients contributed more than one eligible LAO RCA loop from the same procedure (n = 10,534; median 2 loops per patient among those with more than 1 eligible loop), reflecting multiple acquisitions during a single angiogram study. Tortuosity from all eligible loops was used for training the transformer model, whereas patient-level analyses used one loop per individual, selecting the loop in which the RCA appeared longest. We did not exclude patients with prior coronary revascularization 28.9% (n = 6,459) had a history of percutaneous coronary intervention and 15.5% (n = 3,455) had a history of coronary artery bypass grafting. Segmentation of the right coronary artery

We employed ML techniques to quantify RCA tortuosity from the raw input of the angiography of the RCA in the LAO view in a fully automated manner, eliminating the need for human annotation. Computing tortuosity requires a centerline representation of the artery. To extract this representation, segmentation masks of the RCA were first obtained and then skeletonized from the background of the image. Below, we detail the steps in this process.

First, we trained a U-Net convolutional neural network31 to segment the RCA on an independent external data set, ARCADE28 (Supplemental Methods). The ARCADE angiogram data set comprises 1,500 labeled frames (one representative frame per angiographic loop) with ground-truth segmentation masks and SYNTAX score annotations.32 We used a subset of 1,000 images for training and selected the threshold that maximized the Dice coefficient over the remaining 500 images held out for validation. The Dice coefficient33,34 is defined as:Dice(P,G)=2|P∩G||P|+|G|where P and G denote the predicted and ground-truth masks, respectively. By quantifying their overlap, the Dice coefficient yields a score between 0—indicating no shared region—and 1—indicating perfect spatial agreement—providing a clear measure of segmentation accuracy35 (Supplemental Table 1).

Next, we applied the trained U-Net segmentation model to our cohort. We segmented the middle 20 frames of each coronary angiography loop to coincide with peak diastolic epicardial flow. This is when the coronary arteries are most visible, as flow predominates during diastole and motion artifacts are minimized.36 We then skeletonized the segmented masks to obtain a center line representation of the RCA. As the arterial branches define a nonregular curve (ie, the branching pattern defines multiple local trajectories that emerge from a single position, and curvature must be defined on a single centerline), we selected the main branch as the longest path from the beginning of the RCA to the distal end of the posterior descending artery. The main-branch selection was performed automatically, with no manual intervention. This approach maximizes the captured length of the artery while minimizing disconnected segments and reducing artifacts.

From each set of 20 frames per angiographic loop, we automatically identified the frame with the longest visible artery to represent the artery for that loop because it was most likely to accurately capture the vessel’s geometry. We retained loops in which that artery had at least 20 or more discrete points identified along the arterial wall with no interruption, which ensured that we had selected a relatively complete artery rather than artifacts (eg, the catheter or a short vessel segment due to blockage). This final filtering process resulted in a total of 38,691 angiograms from 22,334 patients with a high-fidelity segmented RCA ready for tortuosity computation (Supplemental Figure 1).

Quantification of tortuosity

This definition has been previously used by Bribiesca38 to study the tortuosity of retinopathy fundus images but has not been applied to at scale analysis of arterial coronary tortuosity.38 Unlike prior methods for computing curvature, the discrete-curvature approach is invariant to translation, rotation, and scaling,38,39 which is essential for a standardized measurement of tortuosity.6,10 We average the absolute magnitudes of discrete curvature angles at each skeletonized point and can then be averaged to produce a single global tortuosity measure.

We take the absolute value of each intersegment angle, |θi|, because the sign of θi depends only on the traversal direction (xi−1→ xi vs xi → xi−1) and conveys no morphological information; the angle’s magnitude is invariant to this choice. Finally, we normalize this measure by π (the maximum turning angle in radians) to produce a tortuosity metric that has a continuous score between 0 (lowest tortuosity) and 1 (and highest tortuosity).

In our results, we validate our score by first stratifying it into low tortuosity (bottom decile), average tortuosity (middle 8 deciles), and high tortuosity (top decile) (Figure 2). We then randomly sampled 100 angiograms from each of the 3 categories (total 300 angiograms). For concordance testing against expert review, the 3 strata were dichotomized into nontortuous (low and average categories; n = 200) and tortuous (high category; n = 100). A board-certified interventional cardiologist, blinded to the computed tortuosity values, decile thresholds, and ML-assigned labels, then classified each angiogram as tortuous or nontortuous based on visual assessment, and we compared this expert classification to our ML-assigned classification. Coronary artery disease and risk factors

The patient’s age and cardiovascular risk factors were obtained from electronic health records at the time of catheterization. The presence of CAD was obtained from the findings associated with the angiogram from which we computed the tortuosity. CAD was defined as any presence of stenosis (mild or more) in the RCA (primary analysis) or anywhere in the coronary tree (sensitivity analysis), as determined by the interventional cardiologist performing the coronary angiography. A continuous measure of angiographic burden of CAD was obtained using the modified Gensini score.41

To determine the presence of the remaining risk factors—hypertension, type 2 diabetes, smoking, and hypercholesterolemia—we used a combination of diagnostic codes, lab measurements, and usage of outpatient medications. International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes were extracted from the start of the patient’s record to 3 months after the angiogram. Smoking status was determined using ICD codes, consistent with prior published work.42 Systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure, glycated hemoglobin, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels, and the use of antihypertensive and lipid-lowering medications were extracted from the most recent outpatient labs and prescription records up to 3 months after the catheterization and were used to study the effect change.

From these extracted measurements, we estimated untreated values of LDL-C by adjusting for intake of lipid-lowering medications by applying a constant-multiplier correction (multiplying the observed LDL-C by 1/(1−0.30) ≈ 1.43).43,44 The estimated untreated values of systolic blood pressure and diastolic blood pressure were obtained by adjusting for intake of antihypertensive medications by adding 10 mm Hg to systolic and 5 mm Hg to diastolic values.45,46 Finally, based on these values, we determined the presence of the risk factors using the logic in Supplemental Table 2.

Statistical analyses

We examined the association between our global tortuosity score and: 1) age and sex; 2) 4 cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, type 2 diabetes, and smoking; and 3) the presence of CAD in the RCA or entire coronary tree and its burden by Gensini score using multilinear regression models. The Python language with the statsmodels statistical computing library version 0.13.5 was used for analyses.

Transformer-based local tortuosity modeling for CAD classification

We also tested whether the collection of individual tortuosity angles might provide a stronger association with CAD than our global tortuosity score. To this end, we designed a local-attention neural network transformer that operates on the ordered vector of local tortuosity along the artery, enabling the model to learn which vessel segments contribute most to CAD discrimination, together with age and sex (Supplemental Methods). We trained the transformer to predict CAD given age, sex, and the local tortuosity vector. We compared the C-statistic of this locally aware tortuosity model in classifying CAD in the RCA compared to logistic regression model using the global tortuosity score.

Results

Distribution of right coronary tortuosity in more than 38,000 angiograms

Our ML-derived coronary tortuosity score was calculated in 38,681 coronary angiograms from 22,334 patients (mean age 64.7 ± 12.5 years, 37% female, 83.7% White) (Table 1). RCA tortuosity ranged from 0.007 (lowest tortuosity) to 0.289 (highest tortuosity), with a median (IQR) of 0.107 (0.029-0.190). The distribution of tortuosity had a skewness of 0.744 and kurtosis of 3.879, indicating a heavier right tail than a standard normal distribution (the kurtosis of a standard normal is 3). Extreme values above the 99th percentile (>0.199; n = 224) are visible in the right tail but represent just 1.00% of the cohort (Figure 2). The 90th percentile threshold for high tortuosity in the overall cohort was 0.152 (Supplemental Figure 2, Supplemental Table 3). This threshold was similar across race strata (range: 0.151-0.153) and modestly higher in women than in men (0.158 vs 0.148) (Supplemental Figure 2, Supplemental Table 3). Sex-stratified adjusted linear regression models showed similar covariate patterns in women and men, supporting that the main associations are not driven by a single subgroup (Supplemental Figure 3). The decile cutoffs were used for stratification and visualization, but the tortuosity score was analyzed as a continuous measure in the association models. As an absolute reference point, the top-decile cutoff clustered around ∼0.15 in this cohort (Figure 2, Supplemental Figure 2, Supplemental Table 3), which corresponds to roughly ∼27° of average angulation. On an NVIDIA T4 GPU (16 GB), tortuosity calculation required 1.476 ± 0.576 seconds per 20-frame video loop (mean ± SD; n = 1,000).

Concordance between clinician ratings and ML-derived tortuosity spectrum

A total of 300 coronary angiograms were analyzed manually for inspection. Based on the computed tortuosity metric, the images were distributed into 2 categories: 200 were classified as “nontortuous” and 100 as “tortuous.” The board-certified cardiologist’s evaluation yielded agreement rates of Performance metrics for the 2 confidence buckets are summarized in Supplemental Table 4. In the low bucket (n = 200), the algorithm achieved precision = 85.6%, recall = 83.5%, and F1 = 84.6%. In the high bucket (n = 100), it achieved precision = 75.3%, recall = 58.0%, and F1 = 65.5%. Overall agreement beyond chance was moderate, with Cohen’s κ = 0.52 (95% CI: 0.41-0.63).

Association between tortuosity and CAD

Each SD increase in our measure of tortuosity was associated with a 5% increase in the odds of presence of CAD. In a multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age, sex, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypercholesterolemia, and smoking, this corresponded to an OR of 1.05 (95% CI: [1.03-1.08] P < 0.001) (Supplemental Figure 4, Supplemental Table 5).

We next assessed the relationship between tortuosity and CAD severity—categorized as mild (5-49% stenosis), moderate (50-69% stenosis), or severe (70-100% stenosis)—using a multinomial logistic regression model, with the absence of CAD as the reference category (Supplemental Table 6 and Supplemental Figure 5). Among CAD severity categories, higher tortuosity was associated with severe CAD (OR: 1.09; 95% CI: 1.06-1.13; P < 0.001), indicating that higher tortuosity is associated with increased odds of severe disease. Tortuosity was not significantly associated with mild or moderate CAD (P = 0.154 and P = 0.956, respectively). Additionally, both age and sex were consistently positive and significant across all severity levels, suggesting that older patients and those who are male have higher odds of CAD severity.

To study the relationship between tortuosity and burden of disease, we fit linear regression models with the Gensini score40 as the dependent variable (Supplemental Figure 6, Supplemental Table 7). When considering burden of disease as the Gensini score (not adjusting for collaterals), an increase in SD of tortuosity has an increase of 0.05 (95% CI: 0.04-0.07; P < 0.001) per SD of Gensini score.

Association of tortuosity with age and sex

Women exhibited higher tortuosity compared to men (Figure 3A). Age predicted tortuosity in an age- and sex-adjusted model but lost significance after further adjustment for risk factors. To study the relationship between tortuosity and demographic factors, we fit 2 linear regression models with tortuosity as the dependent variable. The first model adjusted for age and sex as clinical predictors. The second adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, T2DM, hypercholesterolemia, and smoking. In a base model including age and sex, age was a significant predictor of tortuosity, β = 0.02; 95% CI: 0.01-0.03 per SD of age. However, when cardiovascular comorbidities were added to the model, the age effect was attenuated (adjusted β = 0.01; 95% CI: −0.00 to 0.03) and lost statistical significance (P = 0.121), suggesting that the age–tortuosity association may be mediated by underlying cardiovascular disease. Moreover, using men as the reference group, female sex was associated with higher tortuosity (β = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.14-0.20; P < 0.001), indicating that women exhibited more tortuous arteries than men (Supplemental Table 8). When stratified by 5-year age groups, women had higher mean tortuosity than men across most age categories, with the separation becoming more apparent from midlife onward (Supplemental Figure 7). We also tested an age and sex interaction to assess whether the age-related change in tortuosity differed by sex. Association between tortuosity and risk factors

With a multivariable linear regression models predicting tortuosity—adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, T2DM, hypercholesterolemia, smoking status, and presence of stenosis in the RCA—hypertension was positively associated with tortuosity (β = 0.06; 95% CI: 0.02-0.10; P = 0.002), whereas T2DM was inversely associated (β = −0.11; 95% CI: −0.14 to −0.08; P < 0.001). Hypercholesterolemia did not show a significant effect (β = −0.00; 95% CI: −0.03 to 0.04; P = 0.900)). Smoking status (β = −0.02; 95% CI: −0.05 to 0.01; P = 0.216) was also not significantly associated with tortuosity (Figure 4, Supplemental Table 8). Using the classical tortuosity index (arc length/chord length), only age and RCA stenosis remained significant, and RCA stenosis showed a weak inverse association (β = −0.03; 95% CI: −0.06 to 0.00; P = 0.042) (Supplemental Table 10, Supplemental Figure 8). Our local tortuosity-based score showed modest agreement with the classical arc length/chord length index (Pearson r = 0.116; P < 0.001; Jaccard = 0.103 for top-decile overlap and 0.103 for bottom-decile overlap), and discordant examples demonstrate that our metric ranked visually tortuous vessels high while the classical index ranked them low, and vice versa (Supplemental Figure 9). We also tested whether tortuosity relates to the presence of multiple risk factors using a simple risk-factor count (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and smoking; 0-3), while modeling T2DM separately. Local tortuosity discriminates CAD better than global score

As shown in Supplemental Table 12, for predicting CAD in the RCA, a transformer neural network model having as inputs the full array of local tortuosity along with age and sex achieved an area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve of 0.67 (95% CI: 0.65-0.69), outperforming a logistic regression model based on mean tortuosity, age, and sex (area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve of 0.60; 95% CI: 0.58-0.63. These results indicate that leveraging the complete tortuosity profile provides greater discriminative power for detecting CAD than relying on the global score of mean curvature alone (Supplemental Figure 10).

Discussion

In this cohort of 38,691 coronary angiograms, we extracted a mathematically rigorous, scale- and rotation-invariant global tortuosity score and showed that greater curvature is independently associated with CAD and hypertension but inversely related to T2DM. Tortuosity mirrored atherosclerotic burden. Patients with angiographically confirmed CAD exhibited higher curvature than those without CAD, contrasting with smaller studies that relied on heuristic measurements of tortuosity and reported inverse or unclear associations.18,19 One plausible mechanism is that increased curvature produces regions of low or oscillatory wall shear stress, a well-established promoter of plaque formation.47 Conversely, plaque deposition could stiffen the vessel wall and exaggerate curvature.48 Regardless of directionality, our study demonstrates that tortuosity is more than an incidental angiographic finding.49

Coronary artery tortuosity may be clinically relevant through several mechanisms: 1) increased shear stress in tortuous vessel may accelerate atherosclerosis and plaque instability, as described above; 2) increased difficulty of delivering percutaneous interventions to nearby lesions and higher risk of stent-related complications; and 3) serving as a marker of underlying arteriopathy. Prior studies of tortuosity have primarily focused on coronary angiograms, but computed tomography angiography-derived coronary tortuosity has also been associated with subclinical atherosclerosis and increased coronary artery calcium. The growing prevalence of computed tomography angiography provides an opportunity to noninvasively screen for tortuosity in larger cohorts.21,22,50, 51, 52

Traditional risk factors affected tortuosity in distinct ways. Hypertension, older age, and female sex each correlated positively with tortuosity, consistent with biomechanical and animal models showing that chronic pressure or reduced axial tension induces vessel elongation and kinking.18,20,53, 54, 55 In women, hormonal differences and smaller arterial diameters may predispose to greater remodeling, while the mechanical advantage conferred before menopause likely reverses thereafter.24,25,56, 57, 58, 59 In a linear regression adjusted only for age and sex, older age showed a positive association with tortuosity; the correlation weakened when cardiovascular conditions were added to the model. As seen in Figure 3B, curvature increases qualitatively with age. Although these findings echo prior reports,22,23 the attenuated effect after adjustment suggests that the observed increase in tortuosity with advancing age may be driven more by the cumulative development of cardiovascular pathology60 than by age itself. This attenuation suggests that the apparent age effect may largely capture cumulative exposure to cardiovascular risk (eg, longer-standing hypertension) and its downstream vascular remodeling. We could not directly account for duration of risk factors or time on treatment, which may influence the degree of remodeling, but these time-dependent exposures are a plausible explanation for the loss of an independent age association after adjustment. In addition, women showed higher tortuosity from midlife onward, which may reflect time-dependent factors we could not measure, such as menopausal status. With respect to the inverse association between tortuosity and T2DM, similar observations have been made in retinal vessels,61 where decreased tortuosity has been associated with an increased risk of ischemic heart disease. Hypercholesterolemia did not emerge as an independent determinant once other factors were considered, aligning with prior retinal observations.62

These findings position RCA tortuosity as an imaging biomarker that can be used in future studies to understand relationship of anatomy to cardiovascular risk factors and atherosclerosis formation including better understanding of directionality and causality.22,25 Tortuosity adds incremental information to conventional risk models—particularly in individuals with severe CAD.

Study limitations

Our study focused on the RCA, anatomy which may not fully capture the more complex branching of the left coronary system21 and thus may not be generalizable to the entire coronary tree. We focused on the RCA because its course and branching are more predictable and typically easier to segment reliably with an automated pipeline than the left coronary system, which has more complex bifurcations and more frequent overlap and foreshortening. In addition, the retrospective design can introduce selection bias and variability in imaging protocols. Segmentation was successful in 78.7% (n = 38,691 of 49,189 eligible LAO RCA loops); the remainder were excluded during quality control, largely because real-world angiograms were not acquired with tortuosity measurement in mind (eg, suboptimal frame rate or limited orthogonal views) (Supplemental Figure 1). Future implementation-focused software will likely require minimum acquisition standards to reduce these exclusions as is the case of other angiography based physiology software currently in use.63 Extension of this framework to the left coronary system will likely require analyzing the left anterior descending and left circumflex arteries separately rather than grouping the entire left tree into a single vessel, using standardized projections (eg, cranial views for left anterior descending-dominant visualization and caudal views for left circumflex-dominant visualization, when available). The segmentation component would require additional left circulation system labels, while the transformer model could use transfer learning from the current pipeline (eg, initializing with pretrained weights and fine-tuning on left-sided inputs) to support a unified model across vessels. Prospective validation and broader application of the discrete curvature framework—especially in evaluating the left coronary tree—will be necessary to confirm the utility of this metric in different populations and clinical settings. Nonetheless, our results lay the groundwork for future investigations, including prospective outcome studies that clarify how tortuosity interplays with other cardiovascular conditions and influences long-term cardiovascular events.

Conclusions

We derived an automated and scalable measure of RCA tortuosity. Using a large data set, we demonstrate that tortuosity was higher in females, people with hypertension and CAD, but lower in people with type 2 diabetes. Tortuosity is associated with CAD after adjusting for age, sex and risk factors, suggesting a potential role of this imaging biomarker in atherosclerosis pathophysiology. Overall, this study lays the groundwork for integrating ML approaches to assist the quantitative assessment of coronary artery morphology into our understanding of coronary atherosclerosis.
