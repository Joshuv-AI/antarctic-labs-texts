A novel approach to the minimally invasive application of a tension band for transverse patellar fractures assisted by a nice knot
Authors: Zhixiang Gao, Shaowei Sun, Shaoyun Zhang, Kai Yao, Xiaofeng Zheng, Wei Yu, Yixin Dai, Lijuan Liu, Cong Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe tension band internal fixation is a well-established surgical technique employed for the managing of transverse patellar fractures. Nevertheless, there is a lack of comprehensive documentation on minimally invasive tension band fixation in academic literature. In this study, we investigated the utilization of Nice knot assistance during the procedure and assessed its impact on clinical outcomes in comparison to traditional open surgery.MethodsFourty-two patients with transverse patellar fractures participated in this study, twenty of whom underwent minimally invasive surgery and twenty-two underwent open surgery. In the minimally invasive group, intraoperative tension band internal fixation was performed using Nice knot assisted reduction. Postoperative knee pain, knee mobility, operative time, complications, number of intraoperative C-arm fluoroscopy scans, length of hospital stay (in days), and Bostman score were recorded for each patient in both groups.ResultsThe minimally invasive group had significantly longer operating times compared to the open surgery group (65.9 ± 9.3 min VS 60.8 ± 7.3 min, P = 0.054). Fluoroscopy during surgery was notably more frequent in the minimally invasive group compared to the open surgery group (4.1 ± 0.8 vs. 2.5 ± 0.6, P < 0.001). The patients had significantly higher VAS scores in the open surgery group compared to the minimally invasive group at multiple postoperative time points. The difference was not significant at 3 months (P = 0.025 ). Furthermore, the minimally invasive group exhibited a higher mean knee flexion angle and the Bostman score compared to the open surgical group.There were complications in 4 patients (18.2%) in the open surgery group and 2 patients (10.0%) in the minimally invasive surgery group, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.752).ConclusionsIt has been demonstrated that using the Nice knot in the minimally invasive treatment of transverse patellar fractures improves the reduction effect and decreases the incidence of iatrogenic fractures.Patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery experienced early postoperativepain reduction, improved knee range of motion, improved Bostman scores, early postoperative, discharge and incidence of complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03166-3.Keywords: Patellar fractures, Nice knot, Minimally invasive

Introduction

Patellar fracture is involved in approximately 1% of all skeletal fractures [1]. Transverse patellar fractures are a common type of intra-articular fracture caused by indirect trauma and tensile stress, making up about 55% of all fractures [1, 2]. The quadriceps muscle and patellar tendons attach to the patella. The knee extensor mechanism is disrupted as a result of patellar fracture. For a transverse patellar fracture, more than 3 mm fracture displacement and more than 2 mm misalignment of the articular surface indicate surgery [3]. The goal of this procedure is to restore the integrity of the extensor mechanism as well as the integrity of the articular surface and to reduce the incidence of patellofemoral arthritis.

Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) is the best way to manage transverse patellar fractures for stability during knee movement [3–6]. In open surgeries, the field of view is good, but there are still obvious drawbacks, such as longer surgical incisions, extensive soft tissue dissection leading to a higher likelihood of postoperative adhesions, significant postoperative pain, unaesthetic scarring, and the need for prolonged rehabilitation. The reported incidence of complications in the literature is relatively high. These complications include infection (up to 14%) [7], delayed wound healing, and peripheral nerve (e.g., saphenous and infrapatellar nerve) injury [5, 8, 9].

To address the clinical drawbacks associated with traditional open reduction and tension band internal fixation in the treatment of transverse patellar fractures, we propose a minimally invasive surgical approach for their management. This study aimed to assess the clinical feasibility, safety, and efficacy of a minimally invasive percutaneous technique that integrates temporary Nice knot-assisted fracture reduction with Kirschner wire-based tension band fixation for the management of transverse patellar fractures. Conventional open approaches are often associated with limitations such as extensive soft tissue dissection, prolonged recovery periods, and hardware-related complications. Therefore, a modified technique that enables percutaneous reduction while maintaining fixation stability is highly desirable. We hypothesized that employing the Nice knot temporarily to assist in fracture reduction, followed by definitive fixation using Kirschner wires and a tension band construct, would facilitate precise reduction through smaller incisions, minimize soft tissue disruption, and result in satisfactory radiographic healing and functional outcomes.

Patients and methods

This prospective study included 42 patients treated at The Third hospital of Mianyang between March 2019 and September 2022. The diagnosis of transverse patellar fractures in all patients was established through the evaluation of anteroposterior (AP) and lateral knee radiographs.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) over 18 years of age; (2) closed patella fracture; (3) AO Trauma/Orthopedic Trauma Association (AO/OTA) type 34-C1; (4) X-ray evidence of a transverse patellar fracture displaced by > 3 mm. Exclusion criteria included: (1) open fractures; (2) comminuted patella fracture, multiple trauma or multiple fracture; (3) pathological fracture; (4) A history of patellar surgery or peri-knee fractures, along with associated neurovascular disorders in the relevant lower limb. (5)preoperative knee dysfunction; and (6) severe medical disease that precluded surgical treatment.

This study is a prospective investigation in which patients were categorized into two groups: the minimally invasive surgery (MIS) group and the open surgery (OS) group, based on the surgical approach employed. At a 1:1 ratio, the patients were assigned to groups—the MIS group (20 patients) and the OS group (22 patients). GZX performed all surgeries, and three authors collected and analyzed the data(DYX, SSW, LLJ).

Surgical procedures

General anesthetics were administered to all patients following evaluation by the anesthesiologist, and consent was obtained from the patients. Participants were positioned in the supine posture for the duration of the procedure, with no utilization of a tourniquet on the affected limb. At the superior and inferior poles of the patella, 2-cm longitudinal incisions were made (Fig. 1A). The aponeurosis of the tendon had to be preserved by layering the skin and subcutaneous tissue at the inferior pole of the patella. Skin and subcutaneous tissue were separated layer by layer at the superior pole of the patella up to the quadriceps muscle and split longitudinally to the joint cavity (Fig. 1B). Normal saline was used to irrigate the joint cavity to remove the tissue fragments and blood clots. The Kirschner pin was lodged between the patella and the articular surface of the femur (Fig. 1C). It is imperative to avoid causing any potential damage to the articular surfaces during the placement of Kirschner pins. A Kirschner pin is used to route non-absorbable wires (Fig. 1D). Wire A was navigated through the periosteal and subcutaneous tissue channel until reaching the inferior pole of the patella for Nice fixation. C-arm fluoroscopy was performed to ensure that the patellar articular surface was satisfactory (Fig. 1E). If the articulating surfaces were deemed unsatisfactory, adjustments were made under C-arm surveillance. The inferior pole of the patella was elevated to allow the insertion of two patellar pins under the guidance of the C-arm (Fig. 1F). The wire was threaded through the bone pin orifice and the subcutaneous channel of the patella to create a figure-eight tension band wiring configuration. The quadriceps muscle was incised longitudinally to expose the patellar pin, and the wire was maneuvered beneath the patellar pin with the aid of surgical instruments (Fig. 1G). Internal fixation was accomplished with a figure-eight tension band, and nonabsorbable wires were then removed (Fig. 1H). Subsequently, the quadriceps muscle, skin, and subcutaneous tissues were meticulously sutured in a sequential manner.

The Open surgery(OS) group underwent traditional surgical procedures, which involved a 10 cm incision anterior to the knee, the implantation of Kirschner wires under C-arm fluoroscopy, and subsequent internal fixation using the figure-of-eight tension band technique.

C-arm fluoroscopy was utilized during the intraoperative procedure to assess reduction quality, specifically looking for fracture gaps exceeding 3 mm and articular step-offs exceeding 2 mm [3].

Postoperative management

Both groups of patients had their knees wrapped with elastic bandages postoperatively to reduce swelling and hematoma formation. Upon regaining consciousness, patients immediately began both active and passive functional exercises for knee flexion. Passive exercises were conducted using a Continuous Passive Motion (CPM) device for three sessions per day, each lasting 30 min, with a daily increase of 15° until achieving 90°. One day after surgery, patients performed straight leg raises and ambulated with the assistance of crutches.

The discharge criteria are as follows: (1) The wound shows no signs of redness, swelling, or heat; (2) the knee joint is actively flexed to 90°; (3) the patient is able to walk with or without the assistance of a walker while bearing weight.

Postoperative follow-up and evaluation

Clinical and imaging follow-ups were conducted at 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and 12 weeks post-surgery. Subsequent follow-ups were scheduled every three months. All X-ray images were independently interpreted by two trained orthopedic surgeons (YW and DYX). Pain levels were measured using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) on postoperative days 1 and 3, as well as at weeks 1 and 2, and months 1 and 3, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating the most severe pain. The knee range of motion was measured using a goniometerr at predetermined postoperative time points, specifically at weeks 1 and 2, and months 1, 3, 6, and 12. Clinical efficacy was assessed using the Bostman scoring system at 6 and 12 months post-surgery.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS statistical software (version 22.0). Continuous variables were presented using descriptive statistics, including the mean with standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented using percentage.P<0.05 indicates that the difference is statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 displays patient demographics for each group. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups of patients in terms of age, gender, injury site, cause of injury, and operative time (P>0.05). Both groups of patients had similar preoperative conditions, and the MIS procedure was significantly longer than the OS procedure was (65.9 ± 9.3 min vs. 60.8 ± 7.3 min, P = 0.054). According to the results, MIS and OS groups had significantly different frequencies of intraoperative fluoroscopy (4.10 ± 0.8 vs. 2.5 ± 0.6, P<0.001). In the MIS group, hospital stays and incision lengths were significantly shorter than those in the OS group (P<0.001) (Table 1). There were no statistically significant differences in intraoperative reduction between the MIS group and the OS group, regardless of whether the fracture gap exceeded 3 mm (P = 0.909) or the articular step-off was greater than 2 mm (P = 0.931). A total of four (18.2%) patients, in the OS group, suffered iatrogenic fractures during intraoperative repositioning with the Weber clamp.

Discussion

The main findings of this study indicate that the minimally invasive percutaneous Nice-assisted tension band technique is a safe and effective surgical method for treating transverse patellar fractures. Compared to traditional open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF), this technique offers numerous advantages, such as reduced surgical trauma, smaller incisions, lower risk of infection, and faster postoperative recovery. These advantages are particularly significant considering the superficial subcutaneous location of the patella, which makes it susceptible to incision-related complications.

In knee extension, the patella plays an essential role in facilitating biomechanical processes. The main approach to treating patellar fractures involves employing strong internal fixation techniques to enable prompt rehabilitation via functional exercises and weight-bearing activities [10]. Minimally invasive techniques offer numerous advantages over traditional surgery, including reduced trauma, decreased bleeding, accelerated recovery, and lower rates of complications. Consequently, these techniques have been extensively utilized and endorsed within the medical community. Minimally invasive techniques currently available for transverse patellar fractures include Kirschner wires with cerclage [11, 12], cannulated screws [8, 12–16], and arthroscopic assisted techniques [17]. Nonetheless, the use of the Weber clamp for reduction assistance in surgery increases the risk of iatrogenic fracture, which may impede fracture reduction and thereby complicate the procedure. In the course of this investigation, iatrogenic fractures of the inferior pole of the patella were identified intraoperatively in four patients within the open surgery cohort, presenting notable complexities during the operative intervention. All four patients exhibited osteoporosis; however, iatrogenic fractures were not observed during minimally invasive treatment with the Nice knot.

Boileau et al. [18] introduced the Nice knot technique, a new suture fixation method using a sliding and self-locking double-chain knot to stabilize bone fragments. In this minimally invasive procedure, the periosteum covering the fracture site is not fully incised. The Nice–Knot technique involves passing a wire beneath the patellar articular surface and creating a “pocket” with the periosteum above it to envelop the fracture fragment, thereby generating compression upon tightening the knot to aid in reduction. The MIS procedure was able to achieve the same reduction standard as the OS procedure regardless of whether the fracture gap exceeded 3 mm or the articular step-off was greater than 2 mm Simultaneously, it was determined that the Nice knot exerted efficacious pressure on the fracture block in patellar fracture surgery, thereby mitigating the risk of fracture redisplacement [19, 20]. Figure 2 verifies that the fracture block remained nondisplaced at the maximum flexion angle of the knee when the Nice knot technique was applied. Chen et al. [21] found that the Nice knot technique improved outcomes for comminuted patellar fractures by reducing operative time, blood loss, and improving fixation stability and patient satisfaction.

Jia et al. [13] reported that as a minimally invasive fixation technique, Nice knot-assisted reduction fixation for the treatment of patellar fractures significantly reduced the fracture reduction time without extending the operative duration. In this study, we employed Nice knot-assisted reduction and fixation for transverse patellar fractures in the MIS group, which resulted in a longer operative time than that in the OS group. However, the difference in the operative time between the two groups was not statistically significant. The frequent use of C-arm fluoroscopy during the minimally invasive procedures contributed to a prolonged operative time. The number of intraoperative fluoroscopy applications was significantly higher in the minimally invasive group compared to the open surgery group.

Minimally invasive patella surgery has been demonstrated to be associated with early mobilization and rehabilitation, serving as a crucial component in the postoperative rehabilitation of patients and leading to enhanced short- and long-term outcomes, expedited ambulation, and fewer postoperative complications [13, 14, 22, 23]. In this study, the average incision length was 4.3 ± 0.4 cm, and the surgical technique involved only the creation of subperiosteal channels without extensive soft tissue stripping, leading to less tissue damage, postoperative pain, and postoperative joint adhesion or stiffness. Throughout all follow-up intervals in the initial 3 months following surgery, VAS scores consistently demonstrated lower values in the MIS group compared to the OS group, as outlined in Table 2. After the surgery, the MIS group had higher mean angle of active knee flexion and Bostman score compared to the OS group. This finding suggested that the MIS procedure involved minimal soft-tissue stripping, resulting in improved early postoperative knee motor function. Consequently, patients were able to resume normal activities sooner, experienced less pain, exhibited enhanced knee function, and reported higher levels of satisfaction. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Chiang et al. [8] and Luna-Pizarro et al. [11]. Simultaneously, individuals in the MIS group experienced mild postoperative wound pain, potentially attributable to both the minimally invasive nature of the procedure and the absence of lower extremity tourniquet utilization [24]. Furthermore, in our procedure, the wire tails were buried either under the periosteum or within the quadriceps muscle, resulting in the absence of postoperative soft tissue irritation complications.

Smith et al. [25] found that 22% of fractures were displaced by 2 mm after internal fixation of patellar fractures, with only 5% needing surgery. In our research, one patient experienced postoperative loss of reduction, as illustrated in Fig. 3. One possible explanation may be a technical deficiency in the surgical procedure, resulting in the Kirschner or patellar pins being positioned near each other. The Kirschner pin or patellar pin is designed to endure tension from the patellar tendon and quadriceps muscle, while the stainless steel wire is capable of withstanding bending forces during knee flexion. Consequently, precise placement of the Kirschner or patellar pin is crucial for preventing the displacement of fracture fragments and withstanding tension. Previous research [5, 6, 26, 27] has indicated that placing a Kirschner or patellar pin in the optimal coronal or sagittal position approximately 5 mm from the articular surface of the patella, dividing the patella into three equal parts, can increase pressure on the fracture site, facilitating direct bone healing while also minimizing the risk of malalignment.

Our study was limited by a small sample size and short follow-up period. Furthermore, a lower extremity tourniquet was not used in the MIS group, while it was used in the OS group, potentially biasing the assessment of postoperative wound pain. To enhance the clinical validity of this surgical procedure, future research should include a larger sample and longer follow-up period.

Conclusions

It has been demonstrated that using the Nice knot in the minimally invasive treatment of transverse patellar fractures improves the reduction effect and decreases the incidence of iatrogenic fractures. Patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery experienced early post operative pain reduction, improved knee range of motion, improved Bostman scores, early postoperative, discharge and incidence of complications.
