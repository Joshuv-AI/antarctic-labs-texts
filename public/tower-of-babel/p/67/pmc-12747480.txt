Review of Retinopathy of Prematurity Management in the Anti‐VEGF Era: Evolving Global Paradigms, Persistent Challenges and Our AI‐Assisted Future
Authors: Kyle V Marra, Jimmy S Chen, Eric Nudleman, Shira L Robbins
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRetinopathy of prematurity (ROP) remains a major cause of preventable blindness in premature infants worldwide, with increasing incidence due to advancements in neonatal care. Management of ROP has been revolutionised by anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (anti‐VEGF) treatments. Pivotal clinical trials have demonstrated the efficacy of anti‐VEGF in the management of Type 1 ROP, while investigation of safety and long‐term effects is ongoing. However, infants with ROP often have persistent avascular retina (PAR) despite treatment and require lifelong monitoring for myopia, glaucoma, amblyopia, strabismus, significant refractive error, retinal tears and detachment and adult reactivation of ROP. Alternative therapeutics, including beta‐blockers, polyunsaturated fatty acids and vitamin A, remain under investigation. Alongside therapeutic advancements, artificial intelligence (AI) and telemedicine programmes have the potential to expand screening accessibility, particularly in underserved regions, and improve inter‐observer variability, though challenges in implementation remain. Together, advanced therapeutics and AI‐enhanced screening hold promise for improving outcomes and reducing ROP‐related blindness globally.Keywords: anti‐VEGF, artificial intelligence‐based telemedicine, premature birth, retinopathy of prematurity

Introduction

Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is a vasoproliferative retinal disorder that is a leading cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide. Rising preterm births and survival rates have led to increased ROP cases, with at least 50 000 children blind from ROP worldwide [1]. Despite advances, approximately 600 US infants become legally blind from ROP annually [1]. Historically, ROP management relied on ablative therapies like cryotherapy and laser photocoagulation, which destroy the peripheral avascular retina to halt disease progression but risk long‐term complications. The introduction of anti‐vascular endothelial growth factor (anti‐VEGF) therapy has revolutionised ROP management by targeting abnormal neovascularisation underlying ROP progression, offering a less destructive approach with improved anatomic and functional outcomes. Despite advances, significant disparities in ROP care persist between high‐income countries (HICs) and low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). In LMICs, increasing neonatal survival is accompanied by delayed screening and more advanced disease presentations. Limited access to neonatal technologies, such as precise oxygen delivery systems, exacerbates disease severity. A major challenge to effective ROP care is the shortage of trained ophthalmologists, particularly in LMICs. Innovative solutions such as telemedicine and artificial intelligence (AI)‐driven screening offer promise. Bedside fundus photography, remotely interpreted by experts or AI‐assisted systems, has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for detecting treatment‐requiring ROP, providing scalable solutions for resource‐limited regions. This manuscript explores evolving paradigms in ROP management during the anti‐VEGF era, reviews socioeconomic gaps in care, and highlights strategies to achieve equitable access to screening and treatment worldwide.

Pathophysiology

ROP develops due to ischemia‐driven pathological neovascularisation, which can progress to fibrosis, traction and eventual retinal detachment if untreated. Premature infants often have incompletely vascularised retinas [2]. The retinas of premature infants are exposed to hyperoxia that suppresses VEGF and IGF‐1, preventing normal retinal vascularisation. Upon oxygen withdrawal, ischemia at the vascular‐avascular junction stimulates excessive VEGF release, driving pathological neovascularisation [2, 3, 4, 5]. During the first weeks of life, the avascular retina may vascularise physiologically or progress pathologically. Small areas of avascularity may resolve when near‐normal VEGF levels promote vascular growth. Extensive avascularity induces VEGF overproduction, leading to abnormal arterial–venous shunts (Stages 1 and 2), neovascularisation (Stage 3) and plus disease characterised by vascular dilation and tortuosity. Persistent VEGF elevation can result in fibrovascular proliferation, scarring, and retinal traction, culminating in detachment (Stages 4 and 5) [6].

Classification and Natural History of ROP

The International Classification of Retinopathy of Prematurity, which started in 1984 and is now in its Third Edition (ICROP3), standardised ROP classification based on disease location (Zones I–III), severity (Stages 1–5) and concomitant disease [7]. Each eye is classified by zone, stage, plus disease and extent.

Zones describe retinal location: Zone I (centred on the optic disc), Zone II and Zone III (the outermost crescent of the retina). ‘Posterior Zone II’ begins at the margin of Zone I and extends two disc diameters into Zone II. In Zone III, nasal vessels must extend to the ora serrata. In ICROP3, the concept of ‘notch’ is defined as ROP extending 1–2 clock hours into a more posterior zone.

Stages define disease severity: Stage 1 describes the existence of a demarcation line, Stage 2 indicates the existence of a ridge and Stage 3 denotes extraretinal neovascular proliferation or flat neovascularisation. Stages 4A and 4B are characterised by partial fovea‐on and fovea‐off retinal detachments, respectively. Stage 5 is total retinal detachment, further divided into three subcategories: Stages 5A (retinal detachment with a visible optic disc, suggesting open‐funnel detachment), 5B (retinal detachment with obscured view of optic disc secondary to retrolental fibrovascular tissue or closed‐funnel detachment) and 5C (5B with anterior segment changes like shallow anterior chamber, irido‐corneo‐lenticular adhesions or corneal opacification).

Plus disease is diagnosed by retinal vessel dilation and tortuosity, while pre‐plus disease is characterised by milder changes in vascular dilation and tortuosity. Before ICROP3, a reference posterior pole photograph defined plus disease, excluding most of the posterior pole vasculature; however, ICROP3 re‐defined plus disease as existing along a spectrum and including more of the posterior pole vasculature. Advances in imaging and computational tools are helping to standardise these vascular changes by providing objective metrics [8, 9, 10]. Aggressive posterior ROP (AP‐ROP) was renamed as aggressive ROP (A‐ROP) in ICROP3, recognising that severe, rapidly progressive forms can occur beyond the posterior retina and in larger preterm infants, especially in under‐resourced regions. The defining feature of A‐ROP is the rapid onset of pathological neovascularisation and severe plus disease, occurring without the usual progression through the standard stages of ROP. Eyes with A‐ROP may develop Stage 3 neovascularisation that is deceptively featureless and ‘flat,’ making visualisation with a 28‐dioptre (28‐D) lens challenging. The use of greater magnification (20D) or fluorescein angiography (FA) may be necessary. ROP may regress spontaneously or after treatment with laser or anti‐VEGF therapy. Post‐treatment regression can be complete with full peripheral vascularisation or leave areas of persistent avascular retina (PAR), which may reactivate in treated and possibly untreated eyes. The risk of reactivation in untreated eyes is debated, but cases of reactivation in untreated patients with PAR have been reported in teenagers, which highlights the need for close follow‐up [11]. Long‐term sequelae of ROP can include late retinal tears and detachments, PAR, cataract and glaucoma [12].

Epidemiology, Risk Factors and Screening

Globally, approximately 184 700 of 14.9 million preterm births in 2010 developed any stage of ROP, with an estimated 20 000 children becoming blind (visual acuity < 20/400) or severely visually impaired (< 20/200 to ≥ 20/400). In HICs, 6300 out of 32 700 cases required treatment, and nearly 27% of these treated infants experienced blindness or severe visual impairment [13].

The incidence of ROP varies geographically. In the United States, ROP incidence increased from 4.4% in 2004 to 8.1% in 2019, with a disproportionate rise among Black (5.8%–11.6%) and Hispanic (4.6%–8.2%) infants [14]. The Early Treatment for Retinopathy of Prematurity (ET‐ROP) study found that 68% of infants < 1251 g birthweight (BW) developed ROP, with 36.9% reaching clinically significant stages [15]. In wealthier countries like Australia and New Zealand, severe ROP (Stage 3 or 4) occurs in 5%–6% of infants under 31 weeks gestational age (GA) or BW < 1250 g, with fewer than five cases of Stage 4 annually [16]. The epidemiology and risk factors for ROP in LMICs, however, are vastly different from those in high‐resource areas. LMICs account for 65% of cases, particularly in Latin America, East and South Asia, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia [13, 17]. India faces a ‘third epidemic’ of ROP due to its high preterm birth rates (3.5 million per year), with over 5000 severe cases annually and 2900 children with visual impairment in 2010 [17, 18]. Infants in LMICs have a higher average BW and GA compared to HICs [19]. Limited awareness and a shortage of specialised professionals exacerbate ROP severity, especially in rural areas [18, 20].

Screening guidelines reflect regional differences in socioeconomic factors and healthcare resources. US guidelines recommend structured screening for infants < 1500 g BW or < 30 weeks GA, with detailed criteria summarised in Table 1 [21]. Of note, some HICs are evaluating lowering the screening criteria for BW to < 1250 g [22]. In several LMICs, severe or rapidly progressive ROP has been observed in older or heavier infants, leading to revised screening thresholds of < 34 weeks GA and BW < 2000 g, along with consideration of additional systemic medical conditions or risk factors. While determining and implementing locally appropriate guidelines is still under investigation in many areas, established country or regional screening guidelines have been clearly reviewed elsewhere [23].

Numerous risk factors affect the development and progression of ROP. The two most critical include GA < 32 weeks and BW < 1.5 kg [24]. Slow postnatal weight gain is associated with ROP, possibly due to decreased insulin‐like growth factor 1 (IGF‐1). IGF‐1 plays a critical role in coordinating vascular and neural development in the retina, and low levels delay normal vascularisation, increasing susceptibility to ROP. Current ROP screening criteria, based on BW and GA, have high sensitivity but low specificity. A goal of screening is to maintain 100% sensitivity alongside high specificity to reduce unnecessary exams. Predictive models have demonstrated 100% sensitivity for Type 1 ROP. The G‐ROP (Postnatal Growth and ROP) and Colorado ROP prediction models do so, accounting for GA, BW, and postnatal weight gain [34, 35]. The WINROP (weight, insulin‐like growth factor, neonatal) algorithm similarly achieves 100% sensitivity by tracking body weight and serum IGF‐1 [36]. The FIRST‐ROP study analysed medium‐risk infants who did not meet criteria for microprematurity or nanoprematurity (i.e., those born ≥ 27 weeks weighing ≥ 800 g) who were screened for ROP across three NICUs. None developed treatment‐warranted ROP detectable at the first or second inpatient examination. Based on these findings, the authors proposed the FIRST‐ROP algorithm, suggesting that screening for this cohort may safely begin at 34 weeks' postmenstrual age (PMA), potentially reducing inpatient examinations by over 30% without compromising detection sensitivity. These algorithms may provide a tailored approach to identifying at‐risk infants [37].

Management of ROP

The decision whether to treat ROP depends on the disease classification into Type 1 or Type 2 ROP [15]. Type 1 ROP requiring treatment includes Zone I, any stage with plus disease, Zone I Stage 3 without plus disease, Zone II Stage 2 or 3 with plus disease, and A‐ROP in any zone. Type 2 ROP requires close monitoring rather than treatment. Although many NICUs around the world utilise laser photocoagulation as first‐line therapy, modern treatment paradigms often include intravitreal anti‐VEGF therapy (i.e., bevacizumab, ranibizumab and aflibercept) as a first‐line option, particularly for Zone I disease, with laser photocoagulation serving as a rescue therapy if necessary. In 2010, the Bevacizumab Eliminates the Angiogenic Threat for Retinopathy of Prematurity (BEAT‐ROP) study was the first clinical trial to investigate the effects of intravitreal bevacizumab (IVB) monotherapy (0.625 mg in 0.025 mL) vs. laser ablation on disease recurrence for Zone I Stage 3+ or posterior Zone II at 54 weeks GA. Treatment of Zone I disease with bevacizumab significantly reduced ROP recurrence rates relative to eyes treated with laser (6% vs. 42%, respectively). Recurrence of ROP following bevacizumab compared to laser therapy was fewer (4% vs. 22%, respectively) and occurred later (median interval of 16 weeks after injection vs. 6 weeks). Differences in rates of recurrence for eyes with Zone II ROP were not statistically significant between bevacizumab and laser (5% vs. 12%, respectively) [41]. Vascularisation of the peripheral retina progressed in eyes treated with bevacizumab, while laser therapy caused permanent retinal destruction. However, BEAT‐ROP's relatively small sample size and limited follow‐up left questions about the long‐term systemic safety of bevacizumab and the incidence of delayed ROP recurrence, which are the subject of ongoing research. Other studies demonstrated that bevacizumab was comparable to laser treatment for Zone 2 disease [48]. Both treatments were effective, with ROP regression of 96.8% following IVB (requiring additional injections in 3.2% of cases) and 97.4% for laser. Complications were rare, with cataract formation in 0.63% of IVB cases and retinal folds/traction in 2.6% of laser cases. Refractive outcomes were similar between groups at 90 weeks PMA. IVB may offer a less destructive option for Zone II compared to laser, but the retreatment requirement may be higher following IVB treatment, necessitating close follow‐up to monitor for recurrence.

More recently, Ranibizumab was compared to laser in the Ranibizumab Compared to Laser Therapy for the Treatment of Infants Born Prematurely With Retinopathy of Prematurity (RAINBOW) studies [44, 45, 46]. The RAINBOW trial compared the efficacy and safety of two doses of intravitreal ranibizumab (0.2 mg and 0.1 mg) against laser therapy in very low BW infants. At 24 weeks, 80% of infants treated with ranibizumab 0.2 mg achieved the primary outcome of no active ROP, no unfavourable structural outcomes, or no need for additional treatments, compared to 66% after laser therapy. Infants receiving ranibizumab 0.1 mg showed a 75% success rate. Structural outcomes were better in the ranibizumab groups, with fewer unfavourable structural outcomes compared to laser therapy [45]. At 2 years of age, no new structural ocular abnormalities were observed, and high myopia (> −5 dioptres) was significantly less common in the ranibizumab 0.2 mg group (5%) compared to the laser group (20%). Non‐ocular outcomes, including developmental milestones, growth, respiratory health, and overall health, were similar across all treatment groups, and no adverse events were attributed to the treatments. Overall, ranibizumab 0.2 mg demonstrated superior ocular outcomes without affecting general development or health at 2 years [44]. Longer‐term follow‐up studies are pending.

Data from the FIREFLEYE and BUTTERFLEYE trials supported the FDA approval for the use of aflibercept in ROP treatment in 2023. The FIREFLEYE randomised clinical trial compared the efficacy and safety of a 0.4‐mg dose of intravitreal aflibercept vs. laser photocoagulation. Aflibercept's treatment success rate (85.5%) was comparable to laser photocoagulation (82.1%). The primary endpoint of noninferiority (defined as a difference in treatment success greater than 8%) was not met; however, laser demonstrated comparable levels of efficacy that were higher than what has been historically observed in similar ROP trials. Rescue treatments were needed less frequently in the aflibercept group (4.8%) compared to the laser group (11.1%). Rates of adverse ocular events in aflibercept vs. laser‐treated eyes were reported and included retinal haemorrhage (8.0% vs. 15.8%), myopia (10.7% vs. 7.9%), astigmatism (6.7% vs. 7.9%) and retinal detachment (6.7% vs. 5.3%) [47]. The FIREFLEYE Next study found that intravitreal aflibercept was well tolerated through 2 years of age, with no treatment‐related adverse effects on growth or neurodevelopment. The incidence and types of ocular and systemic adverse events were consistent with the expected comorbidities of a preterm population, and no new retinal detachments or serious safety concerns emerged during follow‐up [49]. Findings from a second randomised phase 3 BUTTERFLEYE trial corroborated the efficacy and safety profile of aflibercept [50, 51].

The dosing and safety of anti‐VEGF treatments, especially concerning systemic toxicity, remain an area of active research. Since VEGF is necessary for the normal development of numerous tissues, blocking VEGF using anti‐VEGF agents in infants raises concern for increased risk of neurodevelopmental disability and delayed retinal vascular treatment that requires longer outpatient follow‐up [52]. The PEDIG (Pediatric Eye Disease Investigator Group) Phase 1 Dosing Study assessed the effectiveness of lower doses in treating ROP while minimising dose‐dependent systemic toxicity. The CARE‐ROP (Comparing Alternative Ranibizumab Dosages for Safety and Efficacy in Retinopathy of Prematurity) study investigated the therapeutic success and systemic effects of low‐dose ranibizumab.

In the PEDIG dose de‐escalation trial, 61 premature infants with Type 1 ROP were treated with different doses of bevacizumab (0.25, 0.125, 0.063 and 0.031 mg). Treatment success, defined as improvement within 5 days and no recurrence within 4 weeks, was achieved across all doses, indicating that lower doses may reduce systemic toxicity while still managing ROP [42]. The relatively short follow‐up period in this study limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions, particularly given that the median recurrence interval of 16 weeks following bevacizumab treatment, as reported in the BEAT‐ROP trial, was approximately 3 months longer than the PEDIG study follow‐up.

Ranibizumab, a shorter anti‐VEGF molecule than bevacizumab, was evaluated in the CARE‐ROP dose de‐escalation trial. This trial compared 0.12 and 0.20 mg doses, which both effectively controlled acute ROP (with a 94% success rate at 0.12 mg and 93% at 0.20 mg). Additionally, 87.5% of infants (93.8% of eyes) required no further treatment within 24 weeks. No adverse events were directly linked to ranibizumab injections [53]. At the ophthalmologic assessment at 1 year, nearly all infants demonstrated the ability to fixate and track objects. Refractive outcomes generally showed mild myopia, while a few infants experienced strabismus and nystagmus. By 2 years, most children had mental development scores within normal limits for their adjusted age. Some developmental delays were observed, primarily in those with pre‐existing brain conditions like intraventricular haemorrhage, although this finding is limited by the small subgroup sample size of 10 patients. Late reactivation of ROP was observed in one case in the 0.20 mg group, where retreatment was not possible in time to prevent progression to Stage 5.

A retrospective study of 139 eyes with Type 1 ROP compared the safety and efficacy of intravitreal bevacizumab, ranibizumab and a ranibizumab biosimilar (Razumab) [54]. Retreatment rates were 17.6% for bevacizumab, 32.2% for ranibizumab IVA and 25% for Razumab. The proportion of eyes achieving complete vascularisation to the ora serrata was similar with all three agents (bevacizumab: 60%, ranibizumab: 61% and Razumab: 50%), with no significant differences in adverse events (intraocular inflammation or cataracts). Notably, bevacizumab was associated with a significantly delayed time to reactivation (mean 20 weeks) compared to ranibizumab and its biosimilar (8.8 and 10 weeks, respectively). The study suggests a comparable safety and efficacy profile for all three drugs.

Use of anti‐VEGF treatments changes the natural history of ROP, necessitating a different monitoring protocol to ensure that reactivations are identified (oftentimes much later than with primary laser treatment) and to monitor the final extent of vascularisation. This extended frequent follow‐up poses challenges for both families and physicians. Dilated depressed retinal examinations require technical expertise and become more difficult as the infant grows. The parents of preterm infants carry the burden of needing multiple medical subspecialists, which requires many days off work, impacting the families' often strained economics (work pay, transportation, childcare for older children, etc.). Social support and medical complexity of the family and patient may bias the treating physician towards early primary or secondary laser treatment for PAR to bridge potential outpatient visit non‐compliance and decrease the number of visits.

Outcomes and Safety of Anti‐VEGF Treatment

Studies on long‐term outcomes in ROP highlight the need for ongoing assessment as treated infants age. In 6‐year follow‐up studies of infants who received IVB for Type 1 ROP, 85% achieved normal visual acuity (20/40 or better) [55]. While anti‐VEGF treatments offer promising structural and functional results, concerns about systemic and ocular complications remain. Some studies suggest an association between anti‐VEGF agents and neurodevelopmental issues, including motor and cognitive delays, though findings are mixed [56]. Ongoing studies aim to clarify the long‐term systemic effects of VEGF inhibition, as anti‐VEGF treatment in neonates may carry risks like thromboembolic events, respiratory issues, and hepatic or kidney dysfunction [56].

‘Crunch syndrome’ describes the rapid contraction of fibrovascular membranes after anti‐VEGF therapy, possibly resulting in retinal detachment. This effect is primarily attributed to an unopposed increase in TGF‐β, a profibrotic cytokine, following VEGF suppression. VEGF and TGF‐β normally exist in balance, with VEGF promoting angiogenesis and TGF‐β regulating extracellular matrix remodelling and fibrosis. TGF‐β activity becomes disproportionately elevated as premature infants naturally experience a rise in TGF‐β levels when they approach term. This heightened TGF‐β activity drives excessive fibrovascular contraction, which can rapidly worsen tractional retinal detachment in the setting of anti‐VEGF therapy [57]. Given the challenges in repairing such detachments and the associated risk of poor anatomical outcomes, careful patient selection and thorough evaluation for fibrotic changes prior to initiating anti‐VEGF treatment are critical to minimising this risk.

Other ocular risks from intravitreal injection include intraocular inflammation, cataract formation, corneal opacification and increased intraocular pressure, with rare but serious complications like retinal detachment, vascular sheathing, choroidal ischaemia and endophthalmitis. Delayed ROP recurrence remains a concern, necessitating close long‐term monitoring post‐treatment. While anti‐VEGF agents generally reduce high myopia rates compared to laser, abnormal vascular branching, PAR, and late‐onset refractive errors are potential long‐term effects that require study. The SAFER‐ROP protocol was developed to standardise and enhance safety for intravitreal anti‐VEGF injections in neonates. SAFER, an acronym for Short Needle, Antiseptic/Antibiotic, Follow‐Up, Extra Attention to Detail and Recheck, addresses neonatal‐specific challenges, such as infection prevention in the NICU and careful post‐injection monitoring [58].

Alternative Therapeutics

Emerging Anti‐VEGF Therapeutics

Conbercept (KH902, Chengdu Kanghong Biotech Co) is a novel recombinant fusion protein with key domains 2, 3, and 4 from human VEGF receptors 1 and 2 [59]. With high affinity for both VEGF A and B as well as placental growth factor, conbercept is an approved therapeutic for the treatment of multiple retinal vascular disorders in China. Emerging evidence suggests a role for conbercept in the treatment of ROP. A multicentre prospective, randomised controlled trial comparing intravitreal conbercept (IVC) to ranibizumab found comparable efficacy within 6 months for Type 1 ROP. Recurrence rates were also similar between the two agents (16.7% vs. 23.3%, p = 0.36), with no significant differences in timing of reactivation or adverse events [60]. A retrospective study found conbercept to be effective in treating ROP at low doses (0.15 mg/0.015 mL), resulting in a 15.8% retreatment rate [61], which is similar to values reported in dose studies [60, 62, 63]. A larger retrospective study comparing conbercept and aflibercept demonstrated similar efficacy in disease regression but revealed significantly earlier recurrence after IVC than after aflibercept (5.4 vs. 15.5 weeks, p < 0.001), suggesting a possible need for closer follow‐up following conbercept administration [64]. As conbercept use expands, long‐term studies are warranted to assess its systemic safety, refractive outcomes, and ideal retreatment regimens in ROP.

Promising ROP Therapies Under Investigation

Various agents are under investigation for ROP prevention and treatment based on both clinical and preclinical studies. Preclinical studies commonly test agents in the murine oxygen‐induced retinopathy (OIR) model, which mimics hallmark features of ROP [9, 10, 65]. Specifically, beta‐blockers, polyunsaturated fatty acids, caffeine and vitamin A have shown potential for use in ROP management [66].

Clinical trials and meta‐analysis demonstrated that oral propranolol given as a preventive treatment in premature newborns successfully prevented severe ROP in infants with Stage 2 ROP at doses up to 2 mg/kg/day, similar to doses for infantile haemangioma treatment [67, 68]. However, systemic propranolol may cause cardiorespiratory complications in unstable preterm infants, which prompted exploration of topical propranolol (0.1% and 0.2%). While 0.1% drops were safe but ineffective [69], the 0.2% drops were both safe and effective, especially when initiated at ROP Stage 1. Topical propranolol was found beneficial only during the proliferative phase, not as a primary prevention [70]. A micropipette has been developed to increase the safety profile by controlling dosing. Long‐term studies for propranolol are currently missing.

The Mega Donna Mega Trial explored the effect of arachidonic acid (AA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) supplementation on the incidence of severe ROP. This randomised clinical trial, conducted across three Swedish hospitals from 2016 to 2019, involved 206 infants born at less than 28 weeks GA. The intervention group received daily enteral supplementation with AA and DHA from birth until 40 weeks PMA. Supplementation reduced severe ROP by 50% (15.8% in the intervention group vs. 33.3% in the control group), without increasing complications such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia, intraventricular haemorrhage, sepsis, or mortality rates [71]. An unpublished trial from the University of California San Diego, comparing Omega‐3 parenteral and enteral supplementation to standard of care Omega‐6 supplementation in a relatively small sample size of 40 infants did not show a reduction in ROP severity [72]. A Spanish study also did not find a clinical difference in ROP severity between infants receiving Omega‐3‐rich vs. Omega‐6‐rich enteral feeds [73].

Caffeine, commonly administered for apnea of prematurity, was shown to inhibit ROP progression in the Caffeine for Apnoea of Prematurity trial and other meta‐analyses [74]. Caffeine downregulates angiogenesis‐related factors like VEGF and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [75, 76]. Vitamin A, a fat‐soluble vitamin essential for vision [77], reduces retinal angiogenesis in the OIR model of ROP also by inhibiting VEGF production [78]. Clinical trials and meta‐analyses show vitamin A supplementation decreases both ROP incidence and progression, suggesting it may serve as a preventative strategy [79, 80, 81].

Ongoing studies present novel strategies aimed at preventing ROP. For example, the BORN (umBilical blOod to tRansfuse preterm Neonates) study is planned to investigate the effects of cord blood red blood cell (CB‐RBC) transfusions on the severity of ROP. Preclinical data support the hypothesis that CB‐RBC transfusions help maintain foetal haemoglobin levels, which may protect against severe ROP. Recruitment ended in December 2023; results are pending publication [82].

Surgical Management of Advanced ROP

Fortunately, appropriate screening and management have reduced the progression to advanced ROP dramatically. However, surgery remains necessary, particularly in regions without universal screening programmes. Lens‐sparing vitrectomy achieves retinal reattachment by relieving retinal traction without inducing retinal breaks while preserving the lens. Preservation of the infantile lens reduces the risk of aphakic deprivation amblyopia or anisometropic amblyopia. Cataract formation following LSV is rare during the first decade of life, but when present, it occurs most frequently within 1 year of surgery [83]. Decades of work have demonstrated anatomic success rates of 74%–91% in Stage 4A detachments, 62%–92% in Stage 4B detachments and 22%–48% in Stage 5 detachments. Expected visual outcomes may be 20/80 or better following successful Stage 4A reattachment, ambulatory vision following Stage 4B repair, and hand motion vision after Stage 5 repair [84].

Management of ROP After the NICU

PAR

Many infants with ROP fail to achieve complete retinal vascularisation, resulting in areas of PAR. PAR may occur both after anti‐VEGF treatment and following vascular regression in untreated eyes. While PAR following anti‐VEGF therapy has been studied [85, 86], there are no formal management guidelines, and even fewer studies exist for PAR in untreated cases.

PAR is prone to vascular abnormalities, especially at the vascular‐avascular junction, where shunt vessels and finger‐like branching patterns/anastomoses form [87]. The pathophysiology of PAR remains incompletely understood, but research aims to identify quantifiable retinal imaging metrics to predict the risk of reactivation or PAR [88].

PAR increases the risk of ROP reactivation, retinal tears and detachments [89, 90]. Current management focuses on preventing these complications through laser ablation of avascular areas, though the optimal timing remains uncertain. In one case series, infants with PAR received laser photocoagulation after 45 weeks PMA. While possibly effective, one significant caveat is that some of these patients may fully vascularise without treatment. Physicians may elect to closely follow these patients without laser treatment since there is no prospective, randomised data currently available to demonstrate whether treatment is superior or inferior [91]. Another retrospective study found that bevacizumab followed by prophylactic laser at 60 weeks PMA was associated with a low rate of unfavourable structural outcomes [92]. Guidelines for PAR treatment are still under development. Ongoing clinical trials aim to identify which infants benefit most from secondary laser therapy and clarify the optimal timing of treatment [93].

FA can help identify eyes at high risk of reactivation, particularly in A‐ROP cases with significant nonperfusion and leakage [92]. Performing FA at 60 weeks PMA aids treatment decisions while optimising anaesthesia safety, as older infants are more resilient to sedation. Examination under anaesthesia can provide a detailed peripheral retinal assessment, which is challenging in larger infants, and many hospitals allow same‐day discharge, avoiding overnight hospitalisation.

Adult ROP

ROP is now recognised as a lifelong disease with significant implications in adulthood, necessitating ongoing ophthalmic follow‐up. A multicentre retrospective study of 363 eyes (186 patients) with untreated ROP revealed high rates of late retinal findings: 54.0% had lattice‐like changes, 34.7% had atrophic holes and 38.6% experienced RDs [89]. Incomplete retinal vascularisation, especially stalled vasculature posterior to Zone 3, significantly increased RD risk, highlighting the role of PAR in late complications. Identified risk factors include atrophic retinal holes at the vascular‐avascular junction and vitreous condensation ridge‐like interfaces, which exert persistent traction and increase the risk of retinal tears and detachment. Most detachments were rhegmatogenous or combined rhegmatogenous‐tractional. FA findings showed leakage or neovascularisation in 52.2% of eyes, indicating ongoing vascular activity in stalled vasculature.

Treated eyes also face long‐term anterior and posterior segment complications. Cryotherapy and laser treatments may be associated with anterior segment issues, including cataracts, glaucoma and corneal problems due to inflammation. The risk of angle‐closure glaucoma increases with anterior chamber shallowing, a steeper cornea, and a thicker lens. Posterior ciliary nerve damage may cause accommodative insufficiency and neurotrophic cornea, predisposing patients to dry eye syndrome and epithelial breakdown. In contrast, anti‐VEGF therapy better preserves anterior segment anatomy, reducing these risks and resulting in anterior segment structures similar to full‐term infants. In the posterior segment, PAR persists in many adults treated with anti‐VEGF therapy, increasing the risk of lattice degeneration, retinal tears, and detachments. Vitreous adhesion to the ROP ridge leads to retinal traction, while premature vitreous liquefaction accelerates posterior vitreous detachment, compounding detachment risk [94].

Patients with a history of ROP—treated or untreated—are at increased risk of ocular hypertension (OHT) and glaucoma. Reports suggest OHT occurs in 23.2% of untreated acute ROP that spontaneously regressed, in 23.3% in eyes that were treated with ablative therapy alone and in 58.5% in eyes that underwent acute retinal surgery. Stage 4 eyes had lower OHT rates (40.5%) than Stage 5 eyes (66.7%) and, within these patients with advanced disease, phakic patients had decreased OHT incidence (27.8%) relative to aphakic patients (69.8%) [95]. In a large retrospective study, 10.0% of patients with advanced ROP developed glaucoma within a 3‐year mean follow‐up. Glaucoma incidence was 6.9% in Stage 4A, 12.0% in Stage 4B and 33.3% in Stage 5 ROP patients who underwent LSV. The risk of developing glaucoma was 6.76 times higher in eyes with Stage 5 ROP compared to those with Stage 4A. Patients who underwent lensectomy were 2.76 times more likely to develop glaucoma than those who retained their lenses [96].

Machine Learning (ML) and AI in ROP

The growing burden of ROP screening and limited access to specialised care in LMICs have spurred interest in AI‐based solutions, particularly ML and deep learning (DL). These technologies could circumvent inter‐observer variability in diagnosing features of ROP‐like plus disease [97] and improve access to care through telemedicine, especially in regions lacking ROP‐trained ophthalmologists.

Early ML approaches involved manual and semi‐automated techniques, requiring manual extraction of imaging features (like blood vessels), computation of vascular characteristics (i.e., tortuosity) and classifier training using ground‐truth labels. Feature‐based image algorithms focused on surrogate features for plus disease, such as vascular tortuosity using integral‐curvature based [98, 99], angle‐based [100], and chord length‐based measurements [101, 102]. An algorithm developed by Ataer‐Cansizoglu et al. validated a combination of these features to grade plus disease and achieved performance comparable to ROP experts [103]. However, these methods required labour‐intensive manual vessel segmentation, limiting real‐world implementation.

DL brought significant advances by eliminating the need for user‐defined features, although many models still required ground‐truth labelled training data. DL models have been developed to identify ROP zone [104], stage [105, 106, 107, 108] and plus disease. Brown et al. created an automated pipeline using data from the iROP consortium to segment retinal vasculature and predict normal, pre‐plus, and plus disease [109], and demonstrated excellent performance and generalisation to both HICs and LMICs populations including India, Nepal and Mongolia [110, 111]. From this model, a continuous vascular severity score (VSS) was derived [112, 113], offering potential for standardising and improving the precision of plus disease diagnosis. Campbell et al. demonstrated that VSS correlates well with international expert grading of plus disease and stage [114].

While AI holds promise for transforming ROP care, challenges remain in ensuring generalisability, maintaining clinical workflow integration, and addressing regulatory, logistical and ethical issues [106]. Real‐world performance often lags behind controlled laboratory results, underscoring the need for ongoing validation and careful implementation.

Telemedicine

Telemedicine facilitates remote expert interpretation of fundus photographs acquired bedside from NICU infants, offering significant benefits for ROP screening. Such benefits include improved access to care, decreased screening burden on ophthalmologists and potential integration with AI for assisted or autonomous screening. Initial telemedicine programmes using RetCam imaging devices (Natus Medical Inc., Pleasanton, CA, USA, one of several companies with widefield imaging technology) raised concerns about image quality [115, 116], but subsequent studies confirmed low false‐negative rates, ensuring treatment‐requiring cases are not missed [115, 117, 118]. The e‐ROP study demonstrated that non‐physician readers could reliably identify high‐risk ROP from remote fundus photograph evaluations [119].

Numerous telemedicine programmes have been implemented globally. In North America, the Stanford University Network for Diagnosis of Retinopathy of Prematurity (SUNDROP) deployed a hybrid model across six satellite NICUs and demonstrated that telehealth screening with live examination within 1 week of NICU discharge is safe and reliable with expert ROP examiners [120]. The SUNDROP model has been successfully applied to other programmes, including in Montana [121]. Internationally, telemedicine programmes implemented in India [122], Chile [123], France [124] and Peru [125] have achieved high sensitivity and specificity for ROP detection. AI and telemedicine present promising solutions for addressing the shortage of ROP‐trained ophthalmologists in LMICs, where ROP incidence is rising alongside preterm survival rates.

Barriers to telemedicine adoption include creating efficient workflows that incorporate bedside image capture and transmission from NICUs to expert image interpreters. Poor image quality would necessitate repeat imaging or live exam from an ROP specialist. However, the absence of local experts in certain regions complicates management, especially if treatment is required. In such cases, options include repeating imaging, transferring the infant, or having an ophthalmologist visit the NICU. Parental compliance with outpatient visits poses an additional challenge, particularly in settings reliant on telemedicine without on‐site ROP specialists.

Defining AI's role in clinical decision‐making also remains critical. Should AI function autonomously, provide preliminary assessments, or assist ophthalmologists and neonatologists collaboratively? Autonomous AI may alleviate screening burdens by identifying mild or no disease, but regulatory and ethical considerations—especially regarding accountability and patient safety—must be addressed.

The cost of telemedicine programmes, including high‐quality imaging equipment, AI software, and infrastructure, is another significant obstacle [126]. These technologies can greatly improve healthcare, but without proper maintenance, they may fail—even after an initially successful implementation. Long‐term maintenance plans and resource allocation are critical to ensure programme sustainability, especially after initial implementation in LMICs. Future studies should evaluate the cost‐effectiveness of telemedicine with AI, explore sustainable funding models, and optimise workflows to improve access and family compliance in diverse healthcare settings.

Future Directions

Advancements in peripheral imaging and handheld optical coherence tomography (OCT) could improve ROP screening with reduced stress for infants. Handheld OCT provides high‐resolution retinal images, enabling precise staging and retinal development evaluation [127]. Future studies should standardise imaging protocols for handheld OCT in neonates and explore AI integration to enhance diagnostic accuracy in settings without specialised ROP examiners. Biomarkers of disease may allow clinicians to identify infants at high risk of developing ROP and could inform screening protocols. Ongoing work aims to discover sensitive metrics that are quantifiable, reliable, and easy to obtain [128].

While anti‐VEGF therapies have revolutionised ROP treatment, concerns about long‐term retinal and systemic effects remain. As more anti‐VEGF‐treated patients reach adulthood, longitudinal studies are essential to assess risks such as retinal detachment, refractive errors, and other ocular and systemic complications. Understanding how ROP stages impact later complications could refine follow‐up strategies.

PAR remains a conundrum in ROP management, as it increases the risk of late reactivation and retinal detachment. Optimal timing for laser treatment in infants with PAR is under investigation; some evidence suggests prophylactic laser at 60 weeks PMA may reduce adverse outcomes [92]. Future studies should identify predictive markers for reactivation and explore adjunctive imaging, such as FA, to guide personalised treatment plans that minimise reactivation risks while preserving healthy tissue.

Establishing standardised multinational ROP registries would be critical to understanding long‐term treatment outcomes and guiding best practices. The development of harmonised data parameters, such as in the EU‐ROP and Fight Childhood Blindness! ROP Registries, enable consistent documentation of clinical characteristics, treatment modalities and both ophthalmic and neurodevelopmental outcomes across diverse care settings [129]. Such registries are designed to capture real‐world data—including treatment complications, recurrence patterns, and visual outcomes—with an emphasis on feasibility and minimal data burden in routine clinical workflows. Importantly, they should also track long‐term sequelae such as PAR, myopia, strabismus, retinal detachment, and long‐term neurodevelopment, offering a framework to monitor the lifelong impact of anti‐VEGF therapy. As more children treated with anti‐VEGF agents reach school age and beyond, these registries will be essential in characterising late‐onset complications and shaping international treatment guidelines through pooled, population‐level evidence.

Conclusions

ROP remains a leading cause of childhood blindness, especially in regions with limited neonatal and screening services. Anti‐VEGF agents have emerged as effective treatments with or without laser, offering targeted intervention with favourable efficacy and safety. Nonetheless, the potential systemic impact of anti‐VEGF therapy in a vulnerable neonatal population and the risk of late disease recurrence underscore the need for vigilant follow‐up and longitudinal data. Future priorities include large‐scale studies to refine PAR management and develop protocols to mitigate reactivation risks. Research into adjunctive therapies like propranolol, omega‐3 fatty acids and caffeine may further reduce disease progression with minimal side effects. Biomarkers may identify infants at higher risk to further inform screening protocols. Telemedicine and AI‐driven screening are poised to expand ROP management in underserved regions, promoting early detection and intervention to reduce global ROP‐related blindness. By advancing therapeutic strategies and ensuring equitable access to care, the field of ROP management is well‐positioned to protect the vision of preterm infants worldwide.
