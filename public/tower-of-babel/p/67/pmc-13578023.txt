The hidden primary glaucoma in retinal vein occlusion: a call for systematic screening
Authors: Qing Bao, Yan Zheng, Wen Zeng, Tian Zheng, Yang Liu, Zhouyang Ye, Yuting Li, Xiuqin Li, Handong Xia, Xiaomin Chen, Min Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo investigate the epidemiological characteristics of primary glaucoma in patients with retinal vein occlusion (RVO), providing evidence-based insights for clinical diagnosis and management.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients diagnosed with RVO at the Department of Ophthalmology, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, from January 2018 to April 2024. Background

Retinal vein occlusion (RVO) is the second most common retinal vascular disorder after diabetic retinopathy and a major cause of irreversible vision loss worldwide [1]. Its precise etiology remains unclear, however, both systemic and ocular factors have been implicated. Among systemic risk factors, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes are well established, whereas local ocular factors—particularly glaucoma—play an equally critical role in the pathogenesis of RVO [2–4].

Glaucoma, characterized by progressive optic neuropathy and visual field loss, is one of the leading irreversible causes of blindness worldwide [5, 6]. Unlike RVO, which typically manifests unilaterally with acute, symptomatic vision loss, glaucoma progresses bilaterally, insidiously, and chronically, often resulting in underdiagnosis. Therefore, implementing systematic glaucoma screening in patients with RVO is essential, as it enables early identification and management of glaucoma, thereby offering an opportunity to preserve visual function, particularly in the fellow eye that is free of RVO involvement.

The interplay between glaucoma and RVO is both complex and bidirectional. Glaucoma not only influences the entire disease course of RVO but also actively contributes to its pathogenesis and progression through specific subtypes, such as primary glaucoma. According to international RVO guidelines, primary glaucoma is recognized as a significant risk factor for RVO [1–4]. The coexistence of any type of glaucoma and RVO not only exacerbates visual impairment but also poses substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges for ophthalmologists.

Despite some studies examining associations between glaucoma and RVO, international guidelines provide limited insight into the prevalence and distribution of primary glaucoma subtypes in this population. As RVO and glaucoma belong to two distinct subspecialties within ophthalmology, comprehensive large-scale investigations have been relatively scarce. To address this gap, the present study evaluates the epidemiological characteristics of primary glaucoma among patients with RVO, aiming to generate evidence to improve clinical diagnosis, screening, and management strategies.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University as well as the China Ethics Committee for Registering Clinical Trials (registration number: NCT06757595). We performed a retrospective study including patients diagnosed with RVO at the Department of Ophthalmology, Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University, between January 2018 and April 2024.

Exclusion criteria were: pathological myopia, uveitis, ocular trauma, retinal detachment, proliferative diabetic retinopathy, or incomplete ophthalmic examinations precluding definitive glaucoma diagnosis. A flowchart summarizing the inclusion and exclusion process is presented in Fig. 1, Panel A. Of the 737 initially screened patients, 127 were excluded from the final analysis. The exclusion reasons were as follows: 38 patients due to incomplete clinical data, 21 patients with pathological myopia, 31 patients with coexisting retinal diseases (e.g., diabetic retinopathy, retinal detachment, age-related macular degeneration), 2 patients with uveitis, 2 patients with ocular trauma, 16 patients with prior ocular surgeries affecting optic nerve assessment, and 17 patients who were lost to follow-up. These criteria were applied to ensure data completeness and diagnostic accuracy.

RVO classification

RVO was diagnosed by retinal specialists according to the Expert Consensus on Clinical Diagnosis and Treatment Path of Retinal Vein Occlusion in China (2024) [1]. The diagnostic process involved various advanced imaging modalities, including fundus photography using the Topcon TRC-50dx (Topcon Medical Systems, Japan), optical coherence tomography (OCT) with the Cirrus HD-OCT 5000 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA), VG200D (Intalight Inc., San Jose, CA), and the Heidelberg Spectralis HRA + OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Additionally, fluorescein angiography was performed with the Heidelberg Spectralis HRA + OCT for comprehensive evaluation. Classification was based on the anatomical site of venous occlusion and associated clinical features. Two primary types were defined:

Central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO): Obstruction of the central retinal vein, resulting in impaired venous drainage of the entire retina.

Central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO): Obstruction of the central retinal vein, resulting in impaired venous drainage of the entire retina.

Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO): Obstruction of a branch of the central retinal vein, leading to sectoral retinal involvement.

Branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO): Obstruction of a branch of the central retinal vein, leading to sectoral retinal involvement.

Hemiretinal vein occlusion (HRVO) : Obstruction occurring at the disc that commonly involves half of the neurosensory retinal venous drainage, either the superior or inferior hemifield. This pattern occurs in 90% of HRVOs. In general, HRVOs are clinically similar to BRVOs and have a visible occlusion near a branch point. According to the viewpoint of expert consensus [1, 7], RVO was divided into CRVO and BRVO to simplify the classification and facilitate clinical operation.

Glaucoma diagnosis

Glaucoma was diagnosed according to the Chinese Glaucoma Guidelines [8]. Diagnosis was based on a comprehensive evaluation of clinical findings, patient history, and relevant diagnostic tests. Final diagnoses were confirmed by consensus among three glaucoma specialists (Min K, Xiaomin C, Qing B).

Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma (POAG)

Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma (POAG)

In China, POAG is categorized as normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) or high-tension glaucoma (HTG) based on intraocular pressure (IOP).

Diagnostic criteria: (1). IOP: HTG defined as IOP > 21 mmHg in one or both eyes; NTG defined as glaucomatous optic neuropathy with untreated IOP and 24-hour IOP peaks consistently ≤ 21 mmHg. (2). Characteristic structural changes in the optic disc: such as an increased cup-to-disc ratio, thinning of the neuroretinal rim, and wedge-shaped defects in the retinal nerve fiber layer. (3). Visual field loss: Typical glaucomatous field defects. (4). Anterior chamber angle: Open angle confirmed by gonioscopy, without structural obstruction. Exclusion: Any secondary cause of elevated IOP or optic neuropathy. (5). To ensure accurate IOP assessment, central corneal thickness (CCT) was measured in all patients. Measured IOP values were corrected using the Ehlers formula [9]: Corrected IOP = Measured IOP + 0.071 × (545 − CCT). All IOP data presented in this study are corrected values.

2. Primary Angle-Closure Glaucoma (PACG)

Primary Angle-Closure Glaucoma (PACG)

PACG is classified as acute (AACG) or chronic (CACG). In China, the term PACG is used for all cases across the disease spectrum, without a separate PAC category. Cases without optic nerve damage are considered an early stage of PACG. Thus, what we term “acute PACG” is clinically equivalent to acute primary angle closure (APAC) in the ISGEO classification.

Diagnostic criteria for AACG: (1). Sudden onset of symptoms (ocular pain, headache, nausea/vomiting). (2). Markedly elevated IOP, usually > 40 mmHg. (3). Gonioscopy: iridotrabecular contact in ≥ 2 quadrants. (4). Mid-dilated, poorly reactive pupil. (5). Decreased visual acuity (often due to corneal edema or high IOP).

Diagnostic criteria for CACG: (1). Gradual onset with subtle, progressive symptoms. (2). Elevated IOP (generally less severe than acute cases). (3). Gonioscopy: narrow or closed angle, with peripheral anterior synechiae. (4). Pupil abnormalities (irregular or sluggish response). (5). Characteristic structural changes in the optic disc: such as an increased cup-to-disc ratio, thinning of the neuroretinal rim, and wedge-shaped defects in the retinal nerve fiber layer. (6). Visual field defects consistent with glaucoma.

Glaucoma screening

Glaucoma screening followed a two-step protocol: (1) preliminary evaluation using existing ophthalmic records; and (2) confirmatory testing with additional examinations for suspected cases.

Patients with incomplete diagnostic data were scheduled for repeat visits. Those unable to complete the required examinations were excluded from the study.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted as follows: Categorical variables, Fisher’s exact test; Continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. All tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the proportions of successes were calculated using the Wilson score interval method.

Results

Study population

A total of 737 RVO patients were initially screened, with 610 completing the glaucoma evaluation, yielding a response rate of 82.8%. Of the initial cohort, 127 patients were excluded due to incomplete examinations or coexisting conditions that interfered with the diagnosis of glaucoma. The mean age of the included patients was 62.1 years, with an age range of 19 to 90 years. Among the participants, 303 (49.7%) were female. Primary glaucoma was diagnosed in 94 patients, representing 15.4% of the total.

Among the 206 patients with CRVO, which comprised 33.8% of the study, the mean age was 61.9 years, with 88 (42.7%) being female, and 38 (18.4%) diagnosed with primary glaucoma. In the group of 404 patients with BRVO, comprising 66.2% of the cohort, the mean age was 62.3 years, with 215 (53.2%) females, and 56 (13.9%) having primary glaucoma. Glaucoma screening

Among the 610 RVO patients, 94 (15.4%) were diagnosed with primary glaucoma. Of the 94 primary glaucoma cases, 40.4% (38/94) were diagnosed with PACG and 59.6% (56/94) with POAG. 81.9% (77/94) of patients with primary glaucoma (56 with POAG and 21 with CACG) exhibited typical glaucomatous changes in the fundus, including increased vertical cup-to-disc ratio, optic disc hemorrhage, notching, neuroretinal rim thinning, and nerve fiber layer defect. PACG was significantly more frequent in CRVO patients than in those with BRVO (11.7% vs. 3.5%, p = 0.001). Additionally, both chronic primary angle-closure glaucoma (CACG) and acute primary angle-closure glaucoma (AACG) were more frequent in CRVO patients (CACG: p = 0.0086; AACG: p = 0.0089). However, the frequency of POAG was lower in CRVO patients compared to BRVO patients (6.8% vs. 10.4%), though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.182).

The screening process identified 43 previously undiagnosed cases of primary glaucoma, accounting for 45.7% of the total primary glaucoma diagnoses. Among the 43 newly identified cases, 37 were POAG (including 20 with NTG) and 6 were CACG. A representative case is illustrated in Fig. 2: a 55-year-old male with CRVO in the left eye and NTG detected in the fellow eye. In the left eye, it highlights the features of CRVO, such as optic disc edema and retinal hemorrhages. In contrast, the right eye showcases glaucomatous changes, including the vertical cup-to-disc ratio and nerve fiber layer defects. The presence of glaucoma in the fellow eye prompted a comprehensive evaluation, which subsequently confirmed the diagnosis of NTG based on its own structural and functional criteria.

Discussion

Our study provides a preliminary analysis of the prevalence and clinical characteristics of primary glaucoma in 610 patients with RVO, offering insights into the potential association between these conditions, though causal relationships cannot be established due to the observational design of this study. We found that 15.4% of patients had primary glaucoma, and this is, to our knowledge, the first report to outline the spectrum of primary glaucoma in Chinese RVO patients according to the diagnostic framework of the Chinese Glaucoma Guidelines [8].

The association between RVO and glaucoma has been well-established, with the earliest description dating back to 1913 by Verhoeff [12]. Subsequent studies have confirmed that pre-existing glaucoma, particularly POAG, predisposes eyes to CRVO [13, 14]. While many studies have focused on the association between RVO and the risk of POAG, research on the specific prevalence rates of glaucoma in RVO patients remains limited. In Caucasian populations, the prevalence of POAG among RVO patients can be as high as 12.4% [13]. A study in Korea reported that the incidence of POAG in newly diagnosed RVO patients over a three-year period was 1.83%, approximately 7.8 times higher than in the general population [15]. A study from Peking University Third Hospital in China reported a POAG prevalence rate of 8.6% among RVO patients [10]. These studies highlight the increased risk of POAG in RVO patients. However, studies exploring the relationship between RVO and PACG remain scarce, possibly due to the lower prevalence of PACG in Caucasian populations compared to Asian populations [16–18]. In our study, the observed prevalence of POAG (9.2%) and PACG (6.2%) was higher than that estimates reported in the general Chinese population (POAG: 2.1 ~ 2.6% [19], PACG: 1.0 ~ 1.5% [20]). However, these comparisons are based on published data and should be interpreted with caution. The findings suggest a potential association, but given the hospital-based design and the absence of an internal population comparator, they should be considered hypothesis-generating. The implications for routine glaucoma evaluation in RVO patients require further investigation in prospective studies.

One particularly striking finding was that 45.7% of primary glaucoma cases were underdiagnosed at the time of RVO presentation, including all 20 cases of NTG. NTG, known for its difficulty in diagnosis due to normal IOP, presents a diagnostic challenge. In China, NTG accounts for up to 70% of POAG cases [8]. However, most prior RVO studies have defined POAG solely as open-angle glaucoma with IOP > 21 mmHg [21, 22], potentially underestimating the true burden of NTG in this population.

In addition, when NTG occurs in the context of RVO, patients and non-glaucoma ophthalmologists often focus more on the retinal changes. This can lead to the condition manifesting with more “hidden” features, making it particularly susceptible to underdiagnosis. Beyond the structural changes associated with RVO, NTG itself typically progresses silently and may often be asymptomatic, presenting with normal IOP, which, combined with an exclusive diagnosis, can create a false sense of reassurance regarding the patient’s condition. In the early stages of RVO, retinal edema may cause increased retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness in the affected region, while during the late phase, RNFL thickness may decrease. These dynamic changes can compromise the accuracy of RNFL and optic disc assessments. While detection of glaucoma in the fellow eye provides a critical risk indicator for RVO-affected eyes, it does not replace the need for definitive diagnosis based on comprehensive optic disc and functional assessment, given the asymmetric nature of glaucomatous damage. The high underdiagnosis rate of NTG found in our study underscores the importance of comprehensive screening protocols, including optic nerve head evaluation rather than relying solely on tonometry. Our data underscore the value of multimodal assessment—including optic nerve imaging, RNFL analysis, central corneal thickness, and visual field testing, especially in the fellow eye, where diagnostic accuracy is less affected by RVO-related changes.

These findings highlight a nuanced relationship between different subtypes of primary glaucoma and the risk of RVO, but should be interpreted cautiously due to the observational design of the study. Although the overall burden of primary glaucoma appears consistent, the type of glaucoma significantly influences the nature of this association. Notably, PACG demonstrates a markedly stronger link with CRVO compared to BRVO. In Table 2, published studies in the Chinese RVO population indicate that the ratio of PACG to POAG in CRVO is consistent. The discrepancy observed between our findings and these earlier data may be attributed to differences in sample size, and larger sample sizes will be needed for further validation in the future.

In our study, 81.9% of patients with primary glaucoma (56 with POAG and 21 with CACG) exhibited typical glaucomatous changes. It is important to note that the role of IOP in NTG should not be dismissed despite its statistically normal range. The Collaborative Normal-Tension Glaucoma Study demonstrated that a 30% IOP reduction significantly slows disease progression, underscoring that IOP remains a key pathogenic factor even at levels conventionally considered “normal” [23]. We speculate that the impact of primary glaucoma on RVO is more closely related to chronic IOP-induced structural alterations in the optic nerve head rather than the anterior chamber angle configuration itself. Potential mechanisms include enlargement of the cup-to-disc ratio leading to mechanical displacement of the central retinal vein, which may stretch and weaken the venous wall. In addition, loss of supportive glial tissue within the optic nerve may further reduce vascular protection, allowing elevated IOP to be directly transmitted to the vein, thereby compressing and collapsing the vessel and ultimately resulting in venous stasis [24–26].

Interestingly, the remaining 19.1% of primary glaucoma cases (17 patients) presented with AACG attacks but without optic nerve damage. We observed AACG occurring in patients with RVO, but the temporal relationship between these two conditions remains unclear. Previous reports on AACG coexisting with RVO are limited and have predominantly focused on CRVO [27–29]. Scholars have noted that, in CRVO eyes, the anterior chamber is often shallower than in the fellow eye, with a tendency to deepen within six weeks [29]. Moreover, one study documented that 17% of patients developed AACG within one month after CRVO onset [27]. Michaelides and colleagues reported 19 RVO patients (14 CRVO, 2 HRVO, and 3 BRVO) with concurrent primary angle closure (PAC), 75% of whom were diagnosed with RVO and PAC during the same clinical visit—findings consistent with our observations.

The precise mechanism underlying the relationship between AACG and RVO remains unresolved, partly due to limitations of current studies, including retrospective design and small sample sizes. It is possible that the pathogenesis of RVO in eyes without optic nerve damage differs from that in eyes with glaucomatous damage. One proposed mechanism is that vascular congestion and posterior segment edema may induce forward rotation of the ciliary body—lens diaphragm, thereby precipitating closure of previously narrow but open angles [28–30].

The discrepancy in BRVO and CRVO incidence between our study and that of Xu et al. [10, 11] may be due to differences in sample selection or patient populations from different regions. Additionally, our findings reveal a higher incidence of CRVO and BRVO in POAG patients, which underscores the importance of monitoring retinal vascular health in these individuals. Despite the minor statistical differences between the two studies, our findings provide a new perspective on managing retinal vascular complications in glaucoma patients. Future studies should further investigate the underlying mechanisms of retinal vein occlusion in glaucoma and examine the changes in retinal vascular health among different types of glaucoma patients.

Limitations and implications

This study has several limitations. First, the single-center, retrospective design may introduce selection bias, as patients from a tertiary referral center tend to present with more severe or complex cases, which could affect the observed prevalence and clinical spectrum of glaucoma. The absence of a population-based comparator limits the generalizability of these findings, and further validation in multi-center cohorts is needed, especially considering the geographic and ethnic variability in NTG prevalence.Second, the temporal relationship between glaucoma and RVO is unclear. It is possible that glaucoma was pre-existing but undiagnosed, which complicates the interpretation of “underdiagnosed” cases and the need for systematic screening.Third, we did not systematically screen for secondary glaucoma (e.g., pseudoexfoliation), and lens status was not assessed as a potential confounder for angle-closure.Given these methodological limitations, the recommendation for routine glaucoma screening in all RVO patients should be made with caution, as such practice-level recommendations exceed the current level of evidence and require validation through prospective, population-based studies.

Conclusions

In summary, this hospital-based study is the first to systematically evaluate primary glaucoma subtypes in Chinese RVO patients using standardized national diagnostic criteria. We found that the prevalence of primary glaucoma in RVO patients is significantly higher than rates reported in the general population, with nearly half of cases remaining undiagnosed, particularly NTG. These findings highlight the potential value for systematic primary glaucoma screening and long-term surveillance within RVO management pathways. Clinicians managing RVO patients, including retinal specialists, may benefit from heightened awareness of this association, with particular attention to evaluating the fellow eye and utilizing multimodal imaging techniques to facilitate earlier detection. Given the observational nature of this study, however, these findings warrant confirmation in prospective, multi-center studies before definitive screening recommendations can be established. Nevertheless, timely glaucoma detection remains an important opportunity to preserve visual function in this high-risk population.
