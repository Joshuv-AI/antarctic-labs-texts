Ethnobotanical assessment of wild edible plants and associated threats in Guraferda district, Bench-Sheko Zone, Southwest Ethiopia
Authors: Ashebir Awoke, Temesgen Tigab, Yitayih Dessie, Gizachew Bitew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWild edible plants (WEPs) play a vital role in food security, nutrition, and cultural heritage in Ethiopia. In the Guraferda district, communities depend on WEPs, but systematic documentation of their diversity, uses, and associated traditional knowledge is limited. This study aimed to document WEPs diversity, utilization patterns, ethnobotanical knowledge, and conservation status.MethodsData were collected from 96 informants across eight purposively selected kebeles between June 2023 and May 2024 using semi-structured interviews, guided field walks, focus group discussions, and market surveys. Species identification followed standard herbarium procedures. Ethnobotanical significance and threats were assessed through preference, direct matrix and threats ranking. Quantitative analyses, including the Botanical Ethnoknowledge Index (BEI), t tests, ANOVA, and correlation analyses, were conducted in R software.ResultsA total of 64 WEP species belonging to 47 genera and 25 families were recorded, with Moraceae (7 species) most represented. Fruits (42.2%), leave (29.7%), and tubers (10.9%) were the most consumed parts, and herbs were the dominant life form. Forests were the primary habitat, with peak availability in spring. Common harvesting methods included picking (53.2%), plucking (32.8%), and digging (14%). Ethnobotanical knowledge varied significantly among demographic groups (P < 0.05), and Jaccard’s similarity index showed both shared and unique patterns. Culturally and nutritionally important species included Colocasia esculenta (L.) Schott and Coccinia abyssinica (Lam.) Cogn. Major threats were deforestation and overharvesting, while traditional practices such as sacred groves and home gardens supported conservation.ConclusionWEPs are crucial for dietary diversity, nutrition, and income in Guraferda but face growing human-induced threats. Sustainable management, community-based conservation, and preservation of traditional knowledge are essential, alongside nutritional assessments of key species to strengthen their role in local food systems.

Background

Plants have always been vital to human survival, supplying food, fodder, energy, medicine, building materials, and ornamentals [1, 2]. Within this spectrum, wild edible plants (WEPs) hold special importance. These species grow naturally without cultivation and are gathered directly from their habitats for consumption [3]. Across the world, plants have long served not only as food but also as sources of medicine and cultural value [4, 5]. The Green Revolution boosted agricultural productivity but also replaced many diverse traditional crops with a limited number of high-yielding varieties [6]. Nevertheless, in many developing regions, WEPs continue to play a crucial role in rural diets, often acting as a buffer during periods of food scarcity [7].

Across Africa, WEPs are deeply integrated into traditional diets and cultural practices, where they have long served as vital components of local food systems [8, 9]. They contribute to food and nutrition security by supplying energy, vitamins, and minerals, while also providing income opportunities for marginalized households [10]. Ethnobotanical studies across the continent underscore their importance, particularly during seasonal food shortages [11, 12]. For example, research among the Yao people of Jianghua, China, showed that some low-income families substituted cultivated foods with wild fruits in nearly one-quarter of meals during the dry season [13].

Ethiopia is recognized as a center of plant biodiversity, hosting approximately 7000 higher plant species, of which about 413 (5%) are identified as wild edibles [14, 15]. These plants are especially important in food-insecure areas, where they supplement household diets and serve as critical survival foods. Nevertheless, despite their ecological and cultural significance, WEPs particularly fruit-bearing species remain underrepresented in research and development initiatives. This gap is especially evident in the Guraferda district of the Bench-Sheko Zone, southwestern Ethiopia. The district, inhabited by indigenous groups (Meinit, Sheko, Majang) and settler communities (Amhara, Benchi, Kaffa, Oromo, Tigray, and others), is characterized by marked ecological and cultural diversity shaped by complex topography and bimodal rainfall patterns [16].

Despite this diversity, ethnobotanical knowledge associated with WEPs in Guraferda remains poorly documented. Pressures such as deforestation, agricultural expansion, urbanization, and the commercialization of forest products are accelerating the loss of both plant diversity and the indigenous knowledge systems that sustain their use [16, 17]. WEPs are nutritionally significant especially for children due to their richness in essential fats, proteins, vitamins, and minerals [18]. Yet, their contributions are often overlooked in national food security and planning frameworks [7, 16]. This underscores the urgent need to document traditional food knowledge as a strategy for achieving global sustainability targets, including Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG 15 (Life on Land) [16].

In several regions of Ethiopia, including Oromia, Tigray, and Amhara, traditional beliefs, religious restrictions, and limited awareness have constrained both the domestication and wider use of WEPs [7, 16, 19]. Urbanization, industrialization, and rural-to-urban migration further erode traditional knowledge, while land-use changes particularly the conversion of forests and rangelands into agricultural or urban areas intensify biodiversity loss and weaken associated cultural practices [16, 17].

Although WEPs are indispensable to the livelihoods of many low-income households, they are rarely included in national food and agricultural statistics [7, 16]. In Guraferda, WEPs are consumed both in times of abundance and scarcity, yet little is known about the cultural and ecological dimensions of their use. Ethnobotanical practices often vary across communities due to differences in culture, ecology, and socioeconomic context. Understanding such variation is essential for biodiversity conservation and for strengthening the resilience of local food systems.

This study was designed to document the traditional knowledge and practices related to the harvesting, preparation, and use of wild edible plants (WEPs) in Guraferda district, to assess variations in ethnobotanical knowledge across demographic groups such as gender, age, education, and ethnicity and to identify key threats to WEPs with particular attention to their implications for food security and biodiversity conservation. The research was guided by two hypotheses: first, that traditional knowledge of WEPs significantly varies among demographic and ethnic groups in the district, and second, that multipurpose WEPs are more vulnerable to threats due to higher harvesting pressure compared to species with limited uses.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was carried out in Guraferda district, located within the Bench-Sheko Zone of southwestern Ethiopia, approximately 644 km southwest of Addis Ababa and 42 km from Mizan Aman. Geographically, the district lies between 6°51′24.6″N latitude and 35°20′02.1″E longitude, with elevations ranging from 500 to 2500 m above sea level (Fig. 1). Climate records from Biftu town (2002–2022), obtained from the Ethiopian National Meteorological Agency, indicate a mean annual temperature of 21.1 °C, with monthly minimum and maximum averages of 14.4 °C and 30.3 °C, respectively [16]. The district receives an average annual rainfall of 1974 mm, following a unimodal pattern that sustains partly moist evergreen Afromontane forests with rich species diversity [17].Fig. 1Map of the study area [17]

Map of the study area [17]

Guraferda encompasses an area of about 2565 km2 and is administratively divided into 32 kebeles, with Biftu serving as the district capital. The agroecology is predominantly lowland (wet qolla, 78%), with midland (woina dega) areas accounting for the remaining 22% [16]. It is bordered by Sheko District to the north, Meinit Shasha to the south, South Bench to the west, Surma to the southwest, and the Gambella Region to the northwest. According to the Central Statistical Agency (CSA, 2017), the district had a population of 43,137, comprising 23,473 men and 19,664 women. Approximately 21% of the population resides in urban centers, while 79% live in rural areas [17].

The district is ethnically and linguistically diverse. The major ethnic groups include Amhara (39.2%), Kaficho (25.8%), Oromo (14.7%), Sheko (7.9%), Sidamo (4.3%), Meinit (3.7%), Majang (1.1%), and others (3.8%). Amharic is the most widely spoken language (62.9%), followed by Kafa (15.5%), Afan Oromo (8.4%), Sheko (7.6%), and Meinit (3.2%) [17]. The dominant religions are Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity (44.3%), Protestant Christianity (38.8%), and Islam (16.9%) [17]. The Meinit community primarily inhabits the northeastern part of the district, between Biftu and Kuja; the Sheko reside mainly in the northwest, while the Majang population is concentrated further south, close to the Gambella border.

The district supports diverse vegetation types, including species such as Celtis africana Burm.f., Cordia africana Lam., Croton macrostachyus Hochst. ex Delile, Albizia gummifera (J.F.Gmel.) C.A.Sm., and Antiaris toxicaria Lesch [16, 17]. At lower elevations (500–1900 m a.s.l.), especially near the Ethio-Sudan border, broad-leaved deciduous forests predominate [17]. These are composed of species such as Combretum paniculatum Vent., Oxyanthus speciosus DC., Bersama abyssinica Fresen., Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata (Wall. ex G.Don) Cif., Annona senegalensis Pers., and Stephania abyssinica (Quart.-Dill. & A.Rich.) Walp., with an understory of mixed grasses and herbs. These areas also present high potential for apiculture and honey production [16].

Agriculture is the main livelihood activity in Guraferda. Major crops include Oryza sativa L., Zea mays L., Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench, and Sesamum indicum L., with Coffea arabica L. serving as the principal cash crop and leading source of household income. Ensete ventricosum (Welw.) Cheesman (false banana) is also widely cultivated as a staple. Livestock husbandry, daily wage labor, house or land rental, and petty trading provide supplementary sources of income for many households [17].

Data collection

A preliminary survey was conducted from 12 to 29 May 2023 to map the selected kebeles, identify participants, and plan data collection. This phase involved consultations with elders, forest guards, and kebele administrators, supported by information from the Guraferda District Office of Agriculture. The study area was purposively chosen due to its reliance on wild edible plants (WEPs) and absence of formal conservation initiatives. Out of 32 kebeles in Guraferda, eight were selected based on WEP dependence, vegetation cover, land-use patterns, population density, and conservation status (Table 1). A total of 96 informants (56 men and 40 women) participated, with 12 selected from each kebele to ensure gender and ethnic diversity. Participants included Meinit (29), Sheko (24), Amhara (19), Kaffa (9), Oromo (6), Benchi (4), Majang (3), and Sidama (2). Thirty-two were key informants recommended by the community, while 64 were general informants recruited through snowball sampling [17, 20]. Informants were grouped into young adults (18–30 years), middle-aged (31–55 years), and elderly (56–80 years) following established ethnobotanical classifications [21, 22]. Fieldwork was carried out between June 2023 and May 2024 using qualitative and quantitative ethnobotanical methods [20, 23]. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, guided field walks, direct observations, and focus group discussions. Information gathered included vernacular names, habitats, edible parts, preparation methods, harvesting periods, collection techniques, market value, additional uses, threats, and conservation practices. Interviews and discussions were conducted in Amharic and local languages with the help of native translators.

Plant specimens were photographed, labeled, and collected during guided walks, then pressed, dried, and mounted following standard herbarium protocols. Identification was performed in the field and at the Department of Biology’s mini-herbarium, Mizan-Tepi University, using the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea and with assistance from senior botanists. Additional tools included PlantNet, Flora Finder, PlantSnap, Google Image Search, the African Plant Database, and the World Checklist of Selected Plant Families [17, 24]. Taxonomic verification was conducted using JSTOR Global Plants and Plants of the World Online (Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew). Voucher specimens of all documented species were deposited in the Mini Herbarium, Department of Biology, Mizan-Tepi University, under the deposition number MTU002024, for future reference and taxonomic research.

Focus group discussions were held in each kebele with five informants representing gatherers, vendors, and elders of both genders [25]. Discussions addressed culturally significant species, preferred edible parts, and changes in plant availability, as well as the most frequently used and preferred species under different seasonal conditions.

A market survey was conducted in five major marketplaces Megenteya, Semerta, Bebeka, Meleya, and Gabika to document WEPs in trade. Vendors were interviewed regarding species sold, plant parts marketed, trade volume, and income derived, providing insights into the commercial role of WEPs and triangulating field data.

Of the 96 informants, 53.1% (n = 51) were elderly (56–80 years), 66.7% (n = 64) were general informants, and 33.3% (n = 32) were key informants. Most participants (70.8%) were illiterate, while 21.8% had completed elementary education. The majority were married (82.2%), with 13.5% single. Knowledge of WEPs was transmitted mainly through oral tradition: 70.8% (n = 68) learned from elders during childhood, 23% (n = 22) through personal observation, and 6.2% (n = 6) via traditional songs and riddles in local languages, highlighting the cultural role of oral heritage in sustaining ethnobotanical knowledge.

The Botanical Ethnoknowledge Index (BEI) is designed to quantify the complex interplay of factors influencing ethnobotanical knowledge within a specific group and enables comparative analysis across groups sharing similar ecological and floristic settings [26]. The index incorporates several key variables: the total number of plant species reported by all members of a group, the mean number of species reported per participant, the mean number of citations per species, the number of participants in the group, and the total number of species reported across all groups in the study. BEI values range from just above 0 to a maximum of 2. A higher BEI indicates a greater depth of ethnobotanical knowledge within the group. Although values between 1 and 2 are theoretically possible, they are relatively rare and typically reflect distinct or highly specialized knowledge compared to other groups.

Jaccard’s similarity index was used to compare the composition of wild edible plants identified in this study with findings from other regions of the country. The similarity index was determined using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{JSI}} = \frac{c}{a + b - c} \times 100$$\end{document}JSI=ca+b-c×100where JSI represents the Jaccard similarity index, which measures the degree of similarity between two study areas. a refers to the number of plant species recorded in the current study area. b denotes the number of species identified in a different study area. c represents the number of species common to both study areas. Preference ranking

Preference ranking of WEPs was conducted following established ethnobotanical approaches [22, 28]. Thirty-two key informants were asked to evaluate ten of the most frequently cited WEPs based on taste, availability, accessibility, cultural significance, and potential for income generation. Each informant assigned values ranging from 1 (least preferred) to 10 (most preferred). The species perceived as having the sweetest taste or highest desirability was given a score of 10, whereas the least preferred species received a score of 1. Scores from all informants were then summed to determine the overall preference rank of each species. This method provided insight into the most culturally and nutritionally valued WEPs within the study communities.

Direct matrix ranking

In addition to their dietary role, WEPs serve multiple functions for local households, including house construction, furniture making, livestock fodder, medicine, fuel wood, and the production of farm tools. To assess these diverse uses, direct matrix ranking (DMR) was employed for five multipurpose species, following Martin [28] and Cotton [22]. Thirty-two key informants were asked to assign scores ranging from 0 (no use) to 5 (highest use) across seven categories: construction, firewood, agricultural tools, furniture, medicine, charcoal production, and food. The average values for each species in each category were calculated, and cumulative scores were used to rank species according to their multifunctional importance. This approach highlighted species most heavily relied upon by local communities, thereby identifying those under the greatest utilization pressure.

To evaluate the major threats affecting WEPs, ranking was conducted with the same 32 key informants. Five major threats were considered: deforestation, overharvesting, fire wood, modernization, and charcoal making. Informants scored each threat on a scale of 1 (least severe) to 7 (most severe), following standard ethnobotanical procedures [22, 27, 28]. Scores from all informants were summed to generate a composite ranking, which revealed the most critical drivers of WEP decline in the study area. This method allowed the identification and prioritization of threats, thereby providing a clearer understanding of the ecological, economic, and cultural dynamics influencing the utilization and conservation of wild edible plants.

Data analysis

Field data were systematically organized and categorized, including the local and scientific names of plants, their botanical families, life forms, edible parts, and habitats. All records were documented using Microsoft Word 2019. Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the data, with frequency distributions presented through tables, pie charts, and bar graphs. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2).

To explore cross-cultural variation among ethnic groups, the Botanical Ethnoknowledge Index (BEI), a recently introduced metric, was applied. The normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test prior to parametric analyses. Independent t tests were performed to evaluate differences in wild edible plant knowledge between genders and across educational levels, as well as between participants with and without healing experience. One-way ANOVA was used to assess knowledge variation among age groups, while Pearson’s correlation analysis examined the relationship between age and the number of wild edible plants reported [21, 27].

Ethical considerations

Prior to fieldwork, official permission was obtained through a support letter from the Department of Biology, Mizan-Tepi University (MTUB233/2016), and approved by the Guraferda District administrative offices (2615/2452/3). The study was conducted in full compliance with the Code of Ethics of the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE). Prior informed consent was secured from all participants after clearly explaining the objectives, scope, and significance of the research. Informants were assured that participation was voluntary and that the study carried no commercial intent, being undertaken solely for academic and community benefit.

Cultural values and traditional knowledge systems were respected throughout the study. Participants were informed that their contributions would support the documentation and preservation of ethnobotanical knowledge on wild edible plants, which are integral to local food security, cultural identity, and biodiversity conservation. Emphasis was placed on promoting the sustainable use of these resources for the well-being of both present and future generations.

Results and discussion

Variability of ethnobotanical knowledge on wild edible plants among ethnic groups

Ethnobotanical knowledge of WEPs was compared among the Meinit, Sheko, and Amhara ethnic groups, which were selected based on their high citation frequency. The Meinit, an indigenous ethnolinguistic group of southwestern Ethiopia, are traditionally subsistence farmers and artisans who maintain a clan-based society with strong oral traditions. The Sheko, also native to the region, practice mixed agriculture while preserving distinct cultural rituals, traditional music, and their unique language despite external pressures. The Amhara, Ethiopia’s largest highland group, are historically associated with Orthodox Christianity and imperial rule, and are widely dispersed as settlers across the country, including in Guraferda district.

The Meinit reported the highest number of WEPs (n = 12), followed by the Sheko (n = 9) and the Amhara (n = 6). In total, 22 distinct species were recorded across the three groups, representing 34.3% of all documented species. Venn diagram analysis revealed partial overlap: 12.5% of species were shared between the Meinit and Sheko, 6.2% between the Meinit and Amhara, and 4.7% between the Sheko and Amhara (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Venn diagram of wild edible plants cited by three ethnic groups

Consumption patterns showed notable differences. The Meinit reported eating seeds of Ocimum lamiifolium Hochst. ex Benth. and Amaranthus caudatus L., fruits of Ehretia cymosa Thonn., and tubers of Coccinia abyssinica (Lam.) Cogn. and Dioscorea bulbifera L. Both the Meinit and Sheko consumed tubers of Manihot esculenta Cranz and Colocasia esculenta (L.) Schott, as well as fruits of Cordia africana Lam. and Ficus spp. The Amhara uniquely reported fruits of Morus alba L., Ehretia schimperi DC., and Cucurbita pepo L. Ficus sur Forssk. was also recorded exclusively among Amhara respondents. While fruits were widely consumed by all groups, only the Meinit and Sheko reported leaves, tubers, and gums, including leaves of C. pepo, Solanum americanum Mill., and Commelina benghalensis L., which were absent in Amhara diets.

The BEI confirmed these intergroup variations. The Meinit exhibited the highest BEI (0.132), followed by the Sheko (0.128), and the Amhara (0.087) (Table 2). These results were consistent with other indices, such as the mean number of species reported per participant and mean citations per species. The higher ethnobotanical knowledge of the Meinit reflects their strong dependence on wild resources, as they rely less on intensive agriculture or livestock. The Sheko also retain significant ethnobotanical knowledge, though evidence suggests an ongoing socioecological transition. In contrast, the Amhara, as settler communities with more agriculturally oriented livelihoods, showed reduced familiarity with local wild flora. The Meinit, living close to forested landscapes, continue to practice and transmit traditional knowledge of wild plant use. The Sheko balance traditional practices with adaptive changes, while the Amhara rely primarily on cultivated foods and show limited integration of wild species into their diets. Comparable inter-ethnic variations in WEP knowledge have been reported elsewhere in Ethiopia, such as among the Gumze, Agew, and Amhara in Metema and Quara [27], and between the Maale and Ari in southern Ethiopia [29]. Similar trends were observed in Northeast India, where the Hmar community reported significantly greater WEP knowledge than the Paihte [30].

Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of safeguarding indigenous plant knowledge, particularly among the Meinit and Sheko, where wild resources remain vital for food security and cultural identity. Their detailed knowledge of edible parts and preparation methods highlights the nutritional and cultural significance of WEPs, particularly during periods of food scarcity. Integrating such knowledge into conservation planning and sustainable land management is essential for both biodiversity preservation and community resilience.

Taxonomic diversity of wild edible plants

A total of 64 WEP species, distributed across 47 genera and 25 families, were documented in the Guraferda area (Table 3). This number is slightly lower than the 66 species reported from North Wollo, Ethiopia [31], but higher than the 36 species recorded in Tach Gayint District [32] and the 26 species reported from East Hararghe Zone [33]. It also exceeds the 50 species noted in Midakegn District [14] and the 27 species recorded in the Mabira Central Forest Reserve, Uganda [34]. Such variations in WEP diversity can be attributed to ecological and cultural factors, including altitude, climate, soil type, vegetation cover, and the scope of ethnobotanical surveys, as supported by earlier studies [32, 35–37]. The most represented family was Moraceae (7 species), followed by Lamiaceae, Solanaceae, Verbenaceae, and Asteraceae (5 species each). Amaranthaceae and Fabaceae contributed four species each, while Rubiaceae (5 species) and Malvaceae (3 species) were also notable. The dominance of these families is consistent with previous ethnobotanical studies in Ethiopia [38–40] and comparable findings in India [41], where their abundance and accessibility make them among the most commonly utilized wild resources.

The high taxonomic diversity of WEPs plays a crucial role in ensuring food security, offering a wide range of essential nutrients that support dietary balance and overall health [27]. Integrating these plants into local diets not only enhances nutritional intake but also strengthens cultural resilience and community well-being, underscoring the importance of WEPs in linking biodiversity with nutrition and health outcomes [23, 27, 42].

Life forms of wild edible plants

The wild edible flora of the Guraferda area comprises herbs, shrubs, trees, and climbers, with herbs being the most prevalent, followed by shrubs, trees, and climbers (Fig. 3). The predominance of herbs may be attributed to favorable climatic conditions, such as abundant unimodal rainfall, and the widespread use of certain herbaceous species within local agroforestry and farming systems. Similar patterns have been observed in Ethiopia [41, 43], China [44], Nepal [45], and India [46], where herbs constitute the majority of WEPs. Studies from southern Yemen [15, 47] and India [39] have also highlighted herbs as the most commonly utilized plant parts.Fig. 3Life forms of wild edible plants

Life forms of wild edible plants

In contrast, some ethnobotanical studies report trees as the dominant growth form of WEPs, including research in Uganda [33] and other studies in Ethiopia [27, 37, 38, 40]. Shrubs were noted as the most common in surveys by [14, 31]. These differences likely reflect variations in ecological conditions, vegetation types, and altitudinal gradients. The higher occurrence of trees and shrubs in certain regions is often associated with their greater adaptation to lower altitudes and the abundant availability of edible fruits, emphasizing the need for conservation of tree species.

Thus, the observed variation in life forms underscores the influence of local ecological contexts and cultural practices on the utilization of WEPs, highlighting the importance of integrating species-specific conservation strategies with community-based ethnobotanical knowledge [27].

Habitat of wild edible plants

This study examined the habitats from which local communities gather WEPs. The results indicate that the majority of WEPs (42.2%) are collected from forests, followed by roadsides (25%), farmlands (18.8%), home gardens (7.8%), and riversides (6.2%) (Fig. 4). These findings align with previous studies in various regions of Ethiopia [29, 31, 48], where communities harvest WEPs from both natural vegetation and managed areas according to seasonal availability.Fig. 4Habitat of wild edible plants

Habitat of wild edible plants

In contrast, some research has highlighted riverine and farmland habitats as primary sources of WEPs [19, 27, 40, 49], likely due to higher soil moisture and favorable microclimatic conditions that support plant growth [27, 40].

The study also revealed limited cultivation of WEPs within home gardens, indicating an opportunity for enhancing resource availability. Promoting the domestication of frequently used and multipurpose WEP species in home gardens, integrating them into crop fields, or incorporating them into live fences could support sustainable management. Such strategies would not only conserve wild plant diversity but also strengthen local food security, nutrition, and livelihood resilience.

Wild edible plant parts used as food

The study revealed that local communities predominantly consume fruits (42.2%) of WEPs, followed by leaves (29.7%) and tubers (10.9%). Other plant parts, including shoots, nectar, seeds, stems, gums, bulbs, and roots, were used less frequently, accounting for 1.2–6.1% of total consumption. The preference for fruits is likely due to their accessibility, minimal processing requirements, and palatability [37]. Both local and international studies, including research from Kenya [50] and India [39], underscore the central role of fruits in traditional diets, while Ethiopian studies similarly highlight fruits as the most commonly consumed part [11, 37]. Conversely, some reports indicate that leaves are the predominant edible part, reflecting their high nutritional value and health benefits [23]. Such differences are influenced by variations in local flora, ecological availability, and cultural food practices.

Fruits are particularly valued for their availability during dry seasons, convenience as raw foods, extended shelf life, and appealing taste [27, 31, 49]. In Guraferda, WEPs are primarily consumed as supplementary foods during periods of food abundance and as emergency foods to mitigate hunger during travel, farming, herding, or food scarcity. Approximately 85% of WEPs serve as supplementary foods, while 15% are consumed specifically during famine or drought. Other Ethiopian regions report higher dependence on WEPs as famine foods [27, 40], reflecting regional differences in staple crop availability and food security. Household income also affects consumption patterns, with lower-income households relying more heavily on WEPs as essential nutritional supplements and fallback foods.

Harvesting techniques and modes of consumption

Local communities employ a variety of harvesting techniques depending on the plant part. Picking was the most common method, followed by plucking and digging (Fig. 5). A traditional tool, locally known as Kanta, was frequently used to facilitate collection. Regarding consumption, 46.8% of WEPs were eaten raw, 42.1% cooked, and 10.9% consumed as juice. Many species required minimal or no processing, making them convenient sources of nutrition during both routine periods and times of food scarcity. These findings are consistent with prior studies in Ethiopia [11, 37, 49] and Uganda [33], though some reports from Soro District, Ethiopia, emphasize boiling as the predominant preparation method [15, 23].Fig. 5Harvesting techniques of wild edible plants

Harvesting techniques of wild edible plants

Fruits such as C. arabica, S. guineense, P. guajava, E. schimperi, M. alba, F. sur, and C. africana were primarily consumed fresh or dried form. Leafy WEPs were generally cooked and commonly served with Kocho, a traditional bread prepared from E. ventricosum. For example, C. arabica leaves were boiled with water and spices to prepare Chemo, illustrating the diverse culinary applications of WEPs shaped by local traditions and ethnobotanical knowledge [51].

The study also highlighted the role of women and economically disadvantaged individuals as primary harvesters. Women frequently interact with WEPs during cattle herding, water collection, and firewood gathering, while poorer households depend on these plants for both food and income during periods of scarcity. Consumption occurs across all household members, emphasizing the significance of WEPs in daily diets and aligning with previous findings [4, 49].

Antagonistic side effects of consuming wild edible plants

The findings indicate that most wild edible plants (WEPs) in Guraferda are safe for consumption, with minimal adverse health effects. However, certain species may cause mild reactions, often resulting from improper consumption. For instance, excessive intake of C. africana seeds was associated with abdominal pain, while unripe fruits of E. cymosa and M. alba caused temporary oral irritation. Overconsumption of F. sur, E. schimperi, P. guajava, and C. arabica occasionally led to stomach discomfort. Additionally, overripe fruits of P. guajava and F. sur were reported to attract worms, posing further health risks. Tubers of M. esculenta could be toxic if the woody core was not properly removed before cooking. Similar observations have been reported in other studies, where adverse effects arose primarily when WEPs were eaten unripe, in large quantities, or on an empty stomach [27, 49]. These findings highlight the importance of proper handling, ripening, and preparation to mitigate potential health risks.

Beyond health considerations, some WEPs present harvesting challenges. Species such as C. spinarum, M. alba, and Citrus aurantifolia possess sharp thorns, requiring careful handling. Fruits from tall trees, including C. africana, F. sur, F. ovata, B. aethiopum, and S. guineense, are difficult to reach, while underground tubers like M. esculenta, C. abyssinica, C. esculenta, and D. bulbifera demand intensive labor for collection. Local communities employ traditional techniques long sticks for high fruits and robust digging tools for tubers demonstrating extensive ethnobotanical knowledge and a strong connection with their wild plant resources.

Seasonal availability of wild edible plants

WEPs are harvested year-round in Guraferda, spanning all four seasons: spring (September–November), winter (December–February), autumn (March–May), and summer (June–August). The highest proportion of WEPs (34.3%) is collected in spring, followed by winter (28.1%), summer (18.7%), all seasons combined (12.5%), and autumn (6.2%) (Fig. 6). Plants are sourced from both natural vegetation and home gardens, reflecting staggered maturation periods across species. Preferred harvesting periods mid-rainy spring and dry winter coincide with optimal sprouting and fruiting conditions. These seasonal trends are consistent with other Ethiopian studies [11, 48, 51].Fig. 6Collection season of wild edible plants

Collection season of wild edible plants

However, some regions of Ethiopia report peak collection during dry and early rainy seasons [27, 40, 49], reflecting local agro-ecological, climatic, and species composition differences [19, 48, 51, 52]. Harvest patterns are also influenced by plant growth forms and edible parts. In Guraferda, herbs harvested for leaves and fruits dominate. Similarly, in India, leafy WEPs such as S. americanum, C. annuum, S. pyramidalis, A. ornata, and A. dubius are most abundant during the rainy season [53].

Tuberous species, including C. abyssinica, Dioscorea spp., and M. esculenta, can be stored underground, ensuring year-round availability. This continuous access is critical for supplementing diets, meeting nutritional requirements, and supporting local trade. Recognizing these seasonal patterns underscores the need for sustainable management and conservation strategies to preserve WEPs and maintain their contributions to food security, nutrition, and livelihood resilience [27, 54, 55].

Most commonly used wild edible plants in the study area

Certain wild edible plants (WEPs) are frequently cited by the local community, reflecting their significance in daily diets. Over 60% of respondents identified eight key species C. annuum, S. guineense, F. sur, M. esculenta, C. africana, C. abyssinica, C. esculenta, and A. caudatus as the most commonly used. Their frequent mention by more than half of participants underscores their essential role in nutrition. Notably, C. africana and F. sur are highly valued in other regions of Ethiopia [4, 40, 56] and internationally [50], highlighting their broader cultural and dietary relevance.

The widespread preference for these WEPs can be attributed to their palatability, market demand, multipurpose uses, and consistent availability, facilitated by the unimodal rainfall pattern characteristic of Southwest Ethiopia. These species contribute significantly to dietary diversity in areas where communities rely heavily on cash crops such as coffee. Their resilience and adaptability make them crucial resources for mitigating food shortages and enhancing local food security.

Recognizing the importance of these WEPs underscores the need for conservation strategies to protect both the species and their natural habitats. Safeguarding these plants promotes biodiversity, supports ecosystem health, and encourages sustainable harvesting practices, ensuring long-term availability for future generations.

Marketability of wild edible plants

In addition to their nutritional value, WEPs serve as important sources of income for local communities. Market surveys and interviews revealed that 10 species (15.6%) are commonly sold in local markets and traded to nearby regions, including Dima in the Gambella region and Jimma in Oromia. Comparative analysis of wild edible plants in Guraferda district and other regions

Guraferda district in southwestern Ethiopia is characterized by rich wild edible plant (WEP) diversity, which plays a central role in local diets and cultural traditions. Its heterogeneous ecology, shaped by varying altitudes and climatic conditions, supports species such as Amaranthus spp., valued for their nutrient content, and Solanum americanum Mill., whose bitter leaves are commonly used in cooking. Staple wild tubers including Manihot esculenta Cranz, Dioscorea spp., Coccinia abyssinica (Lam.) Cogn., and Colocasia esculenta L. provide essential carbohydrates, particularly during periods of cultivated crop shortages. The sustainable use of these species reflects traditional ecological knowledge and contributes to local food security.

By contrast, WEP utilization in other Ethiopian regions varies according to environmental conditions and cultural practices. In the highlands of Amhara, for instance, wild grains and species such as Ficus sur Forssk., Dioscorea abyssinica, and Rhamnus abyssinica are occasionally incorporated into traditional foods like injera. However, overharvesting in these areas is becoming a concern due to expanding agriculture [30]. Similarly, in arid lowlands such as Mieso, pastoralist communities rely on drought-resistant species including Acacia spp., Tamarindus indica, and wild legumes, highlighting how geography and climate shape WEP availability and use [51].

Guraferda’s emphasis on sustainable harvesting and culturally informed practices distinguishes it from regions experiencing habitat degradation, such as Tigray and parts of Amhara, where population pressure and land-use change threaten plant diversity [19, 57]. Comparative studies underscore the need for context-specific conservation strategies that integrate both ecological and cultural dimensions to maintain biodiversity and food security [19, 58].

Cultural overlaps in WEP use across Ethiopian regions were assessed using Jaccard’s Similarity Index (JSI) based on 26 ethnobotanical sources (Table 6). The highest similarity was observed between Guraferda and Yeki district (35.3%) followed by Meinit Goldiya District (28.7%). Similarity declined progressively toward northern and eastern regions, reflecting geographic distance, environmental variation, and limited interaction. Shared linguistic and cultural ties likely facilitate the transmission of ethnobotanical knowledge, contributing to regional convergence in WEP use. Implications of utilizing wild edible plants in Guraferda district

The use of wild edible plants (WEPs) in Guraferda District, southwestern Ethiopia, is integral to food security, health, and environmental sustainability. Ethnobotanical research in the region highlights multiple benefits of WEPs. These plants are naturally rich in essential nutrients, including vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants, making them critical in combating nutritional deficiencies and improving overall well-being. For the Meinit community, WEPs serve as supplemental foods during periods of scarcity and provide an affordable dietary alternative.

Many WEPs also possess medicinal properties, enabling communities to address common ailments with locally available resources. Studies in Guraferda emphasize the dual role of these plants in both dietary and therapeutic practices, while acknowledging threats such as habitat loss and overharvesting [16]. Preserving traditional knowledge on WEPs sustains cultural heritage and informs community health initiatives by promoting safe identification, preparation, and consumption practices, thereby maximizing benefits and minimizing potential health risks.

Beyond nutrition and health, WEPs contribute significantly to environmental conservation. Documenting their use supports biodiversity preservation by identifying species vital to local diets and cultural practices. Recognizing their ecological and cultural importance facilitates targeted conservation efforts and encourages sustainable resource management, ensuring long-term availability and ecosystem stability. Similar research in Midakegn District emphasizes the necessity of managing underutilized WEPs to prevent overexploitation and maintain biodiversity [4].

Ethnobotanical knowledge also provides strategies for controlling invasive species that threaten native flora. In Guraferda, invasive plants are considered a major risk, underscoring the importance of effective management measures [16]. Additionally, WEPs enhance cultural ecosystem services by reinforcing local traditions and fostering a stronger connection between communities and their environment. Studies in Dibatie demonstrate that WEPs are integral to local food customs and cultural identity [34].

Certain WEPs demonstrate resilience to climatic variations, providing emergency food sources during droughts and contributing to ecosystem stability. For instance, research in Midakegn shows that underutilized WEPs can serve as critical resources under environmental stress [4]. Sustainable harvesting practices not only preserve biodiversity and ecosystem health but also promote appreciation for indigenous species and encourage environmentally responsible land-use practices. Since WEPs generally require fewer inputs than cultivated crops and avoid harmful chemicals, their promotion can reduce dependence on commercial agriculture and support environmental health. However, establishing clear guidelines for sustainable harvesting is essential to prevent overexploitation and secure the long-term availability of these vital resources.

Newly identified wild edible plants

A comparative analysis of WEPs in Guraferda against previous Ethiopian ethnobotanical studies revealed several novel findings, expanding the national knowledge base. Notably, the herb Solanum americanum Mill., of foreign origin, was identified as a food source, a use not previously reported in Ethiopia. Typically found in forested areas and along riverbanks, S. americanum was recognized for its edible vegetable value, whereas earlier studies focused primarily on its medicinal properties [71].

This study also documents, for the first time in Ethiopia, the consumption of certain plant parts previously unreported as food. For example, the leaves of Cucurbita pepo L. are locally used as a vegetable. Similarly, while prior research reported that ripe fruits of Coffea arabica L. were eaten raw, our findings show that its leaves are also consumed in a traditional dish called “Chemo,” prepared by boiling the leaves with water, garlic (Allium sativum), Capsicum annuum, Ruta chalepensis, salt, and other spices.

Moreover, although earlier studies noted fruits of Keetia gueinzii Schum. as edible in districts such as Metema and Quara [27], this research highlights its medicinal applications, further broadening the understanding of its uses.

Documenting these newly recognized edible plant parts demonstrates the diversity of WEPs in Ethiopia and underscores their potential to enhance local food security. By promoting the sustainable use of these species, communities can achieve dietary diversification, improved nutrition, and strengthened resilience, while preserving the rich ethnobotanical heritage of the region.

A t test conducted using R software revealed a significant difference in WEP knowledge between male and female informants (t = 5.3, P < 0.05). Conversely, other research in Ethiopia has reported greater male knowledge [4, 31], while studies in Ethiopia, Nepal, Bhutan, Brazil, and China found no significant gender differences [19, 27, 45, 72–74].

Literacy level also influenced WEP knowledge. A t test indicated a significant difference (t = 5.7, P < 0.05) between illiterate and literate informants. Illiterate participants exhibited higher mean scores (M = 4.1, SD = 1.9) compared to literate individuals (M = 2.1, SD = 1.3). Moreover, they reported greater diversity of WEPs, potentially due to reduced exposure to modernization and more frequent engagement with natural vegetation. Similar patterns have been observed in previous studies [4, 31], although some research reports no significant differences between literate and illiterate groups [27, 58, 68, 75].

Residency status further influenced WEP knowledge. Long-term residents exhibited significantly greater familiarity with local plants than recent settlers (p < 0.05), reflecting prolonged interaction with the environment and accumulated ethnobotanical knowledge. This aligns with findings from Metema and Quara districts in Ethiopia [27, 67], where indigenous inhabitants displayed higher WEP knowledge compared to resettled individuals.

Thus, these findings underscore that gender, literacy, and length of residence are key determinants of traditional ethnobotanical knowledge in Guraferda. Understanding these sociocultural patterns is essential for targeting conservation initiatives, promoting sustainable use, and preserving indigenous knowledge of WEPs.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Guraferda district in southwestern Ethiopia harbors a rich diversity of wild edible plants (WEPs), reflecting both ecological heterogeneity and strong traditional knowledge systems among local communities. A total of 64 WEP species belonging to 47 genera and 25 families were documented, with fruits, leaves, and tubers representing the most commonly consumed plant parts. Herbs were the dominant life form, and forests served as the primary habitat, highlighting the importance of natural vegetation for sustaining local food systems. Seasonal patterns of availability, particularly during the spring and dry winter months, underscore the integral role of WEPs in supplementing diets throughout the year.

Ethnobotanical knowledge varied significantly among ethnic groups, genders, age cohorts, literacy levels, and residency status. The Meinit and Sheko communities demonstrated the highest knowledge levels, while elderly, female, illiterate, and long-term residents generally exhibited greater familiarity with WEPs. These patterns underscore the vulnerability of traditional knowledge, emphasizing the need for strategies that support intergenerational transmission and community-based knowledge preservation.

The study further highlighted the multifunctional importance of WEPs. Certain species, such as Colocasia esculenta L., Cordia africana Lam., Afromorus mesozygia (Stapf) E.M.Gardner, and Syzygium guineense (Willd.) DC., provide not only nutritional value but also serve as sources of construction material, fuel wood, agricultural tools, medicine, and income. Preference and direct matrix rankings revealed the cultural and economic significance of these species, while priority ranking of threats identified deforestation, overharvesting, and habitat degradation as major pressures affecting their sustainability. Despite these challenges, local communities employ traditional conservation practices, including sacred grove protection, in situ cultivation, and the use of home gardens, underscoring the potential of indigenous knowledge in supporting sustainable resource management.

Comparative analyses with other Ethiopian regions revealed both unique and shared patterns of WEP use, reflecting the influence of ecological, cultural, and geographic factors on plant selection and utilization. Newly documented uses, such as the consumption of Solanum americanum Mill. leaves and Cucurbita pepo L. foliage, highlight the dynamic nature of ethnobotanical knowledge and its potential to enhance local food security and dietary diversity.

Overall, the findings emphasize the critical role of WEPs in promoting food security, nutritional health, and cultural identity, while also contributing to ecosystem services and sustainable livelihoods. Conservation and management strategies should prioritize highly utilized multipurpose species, integrate local ecological knowledge, and encourage community-based initiatives to ensure the long-term availability of WEPs. Additionally, efforts to document and disseminate traditional knowledge can safeguard cultural heritage and strengthen resilience in the face of environmental change and socioeconomic pressures.

Limitations of the study

This study, while providing valuable documentation of wild edible plants (WEPs) and traditional knowledge in Guraferda district, has limitations. Purposive and snowball sampling may have excluded less visible knowledge holders, and reliance on self-reported data could introduce recall bias. Only eight of 32 kebeles were studied, potentially underrepresenting intra-district diversity. Plant identification, though supported by herbarium and digital tools, may have misclassified rare or similar species. Nutritional and phytochemical analyses were not conducted, and sociocultural dynamics were assessed indirectly. Despite these constraints, the findings offer essential baseline data for conservation, sustainable use, and cultural preservation of WEPs.
