Evaluating the psychosocial effects of cancer on sleep quality and mental health in elderly populations
Authors: Yusheng Liu, Zheng Chen, Nanxia Lin, Yu Liu, Xuefeng Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe interplay between chronic illnesses, such as cancer, mental health issues, and sleep disturbances poses significant challenges for older adults. This study aims to explore the relationships among a cancer diagnosis, psychological factors (specifically depression), and sleep quality, utilizing data from the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS).MethodsA case-control study design was employed, analyzing data from 9,302 participants, including 855 individuals diagnosed with cancer and 8,447 healthy controls. Descriptive statistics and multiple logistic regression analyses were utilized to evaluate sleep disturbances, mental health (depression assessed via the CESD-9 scale), and other demographic variables. A Bayesian statistical approach was applied to determine the effect sizes associated with sleep disturbances.ResultsThe results demonstrated significant differences in sleep quality between the two groups. Notably, 54.79% of cancer patients reported difficulty falling asleep, compared to only 45.21% of controls (<0.001p<0.001). Moreover, the frequency of night awakenings was notably higher in the cancer group, with 73.64% reporting frequent awakenings compared to 26.36% in the control group. On the CESD-9 scale, cancer patients had an average score of 5.21 (±± 2.67) versus 4.32 (±± 2.64) for controls (<0.001p<0.001). Odds ratios from logistic regression analysis indicated that individuals with cancer had 3.80 times greater odds of having trouble falling asleep (OR: 3.80) and 1.37 times greater odds of frequent night awakenings (OR: 1.37).ConclusionThese findings underscore the significant adverse effects of cancer on mental health and sleep quality among older adults. Enhanced psychological support and targeted interventions aimed at improving sleep quality are critical for managing the overall well-being of cancer patients. Future research should continue to investigate these intricate relationships and develop comprehensive care strategies to support the aging population facing chronic illnesses.

Introduction

Cancer represents one of the most significant global health challenges of the 21 st century, with the World Health Organization reporting nearly 20 million new cases and 9.7 million cancer-related deaths worldwide in 2022 [1]. This disease burden is projected to increase dramatically, with over 35 million new cancer cases predicted by 2050, representing a 77% increase from current level [2]. Currently, there are more than 54 million cancer survivors worldwide living within five years of diagnosis, with approximately 32 million survivors globally across all time periods. In the United States alone, over 18 million individuals are living as cancer survivors, representing approximately 5.4% of the population [3]. The growing population of cancer survivors reflects both the increasing incidence of cancer and significant improvements in treatment outcomes, with long-term survival rates showing that 70% of cancer survivors have lived five years or more after diagnosis [4, 5].

However, this expanding survivor population faces substantial challenges that extend far beyond the initial cancer diagnosis and treatment [6, 7]. As populations age, an increasing number of individuals face the dual challenges of managing chronic health conditions, such as cancer, alongside mental health issues, which can significantly impair their quality of life [8, 9]. Among the various mental health concerns, depression is particularly prevalent in older adults, often manifesting as a response to the stressors associated with chronic illnesses [10]. Furthermore, sleep disturbances are notably common among this demographic, compounding the challenges faced by individuals with chronic conditions [11, 12]. Given the intricate relationships among chronic illness, mental health, and sleep, it is essential to explore these dynamics comprehensively [13].

Chronic diseases like cancer can profoundly affect a person’s psychological well-being, leading to feelings of anxiety and depression [14]. The emotional turmoil stemming from a cancer diagnosis can not only exacerbate existing psychological conditions but also lead to new mental health challenges [15, 16]. Studies indicate that individuals diagnosed with cancer frequently report higher levels of distress and depressive symptoms, which may hinder their ability to cope effectively with their illness [17, 18]. Furthermore, the disease's physical manifestations can lead to a decline in functioning and an overall reduction in life satisfaction. Such psychological ramifications can create a vicious cycle, where declining mental health further aggravates physical symptoms and impairs individuals' ability to adhere to treatment regimens [19, 20].

Sleep disturbances are another prominent concern within this context, contributing significantly to poorer health outcomes [21]. Numerous studies have highlighted the correlation between sleep patterns and mental health status in older adults, suggesting that poor sleep may exacerbate feelings of depression and anxiety [22, 23]. Sleep disturbances can range from difficulty falling asleep to frequent awakenings during the night, leaving individuals feeling unrefreshed and fatigued during the day [24]. These sleep issues can further complicate the management of chronic illnesses like cancer, as adequate sleep is vital for optimal health and recovery [25]. The bidirectional relationship between sleep quality and mental health underscores the need for an integrative approach when assessing the health needs of older adults with chronic conditions [26].

Understanding the multifaceted relationships between chronic illnesses, mental health, and sleep disturbances is crucial for developing effective intervention strategies. A growing body of literature emphasizes the importance of addressing the psychosocial aspects of chronic disease management to improve overall health outcomes. Interventions targeting mental health—and specifically, sleep quality—can enhance the quality of life for older adults facing these challenges. For instance, cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) has been shown to be an effective approach for treating both depression and insomnia, offering a dual benefit for patients with chronic illnesses [27–29]. Such interventions, when implemented early in the treatment process, may attenuate the impact of mental health and sleep disturbances on chronic disease progression and management.

Moreover, there is a pressing need to consider the social determinants of health when analyzing these interactions. The socio-economic factors influencing health behaviors, access to care, and community support systems can profoundly shape individuals' experiences with chronic disease, mental health, and sleep. Older adults, particularly those from marginalized backgrounds, may face heightened risks as they navigate these interconnected challenges. Educational levels, income disparities, and social support networks contribute to the variations observed in health outcomes among older adults. Understanding these determinants can inform public health initiatives and policy decisions aimed at improving the health trajectories of older populations.

In this study, we aim to delve deeper into the relationships between cancer, mental health, and sleep quality among older adults. By employing advanced statistical methods, we seek to elucidate the mediation effects of psychological factors, such as depression and social engagement, on the relationship between cancer diagnosis and sleep disturbances. Our approach recognizes the influence of various socio-demographic factors and aims to contextualize our findings within the broader framework of aging and health disparities. Through this investigation, we hope to contribute to the existing literature and provide valuable insights that can inform future interventions aimed at improving the health and well-being of older adults facing chronic illnesses.

As we explore these complex interactions, it is essential to consider how various factors—ranging from individual health behaviors to broader societal influences—impact the experiences of older adults with chronic illnesses. Such an understanding is critical for designing holistic, patient-centered care strategies that address not only the medical aspects of chronic disease management but also the associated mental health and sleep-related challenges. By emphasizing the need for comprehensive approaches, we can better support the aging population, ultimately fostering healthier, more resilient communities.

Methodology

Study design and data source

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the 2018 wave of the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS), also known as Estudio Nacional de Salud y Envejecimiento en México (ENASEM) [30–32]. The MHAS is a nationally representative, longitudinal population-based study of community-dwelling adults aged 50 years and older in Mexico, initiated in 2001 and designed to prospectively evaluate the impact of disease on health, function, and mortality of older adults in both urban and rural areas. The study protocols and survey instruments are highly comparable to the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS), enabling cross-national comparisons of aging processes and serving as the first longitudinal study of older adults in Mexico with a broad socioeconomic perspective. This analysis follows the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for reporting cross-sectional studies.

The MHAS employs a complex, multi-stage stratified sampling design based on Mexico's National Employment Survey (Encuesta Nacional de Empleo, ENE) conducted by the Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía (INEGI). The baseline sample (2001) targeted a nationally representative sample of individuals born in 1951 or earlier (aged 50+ in 2001), with the sampling frame consisting of households from the National Employment Survey that had at least one resident aged 50+ years. Geographic coverage included all 32 states of Mexico, encompassing both urban and rural areas, with special consideration given to households in six states accounting for 40% of migrants to the United States, which were over-sampled to ensure adequate representation of this population. When multiple age-eligible persons resided in selected households, one was randomly selected as the primary respondent, and spouses or partners of selected individuals were recruited regardless of age. The 2018 wave included all surviving participants from previous waves (2001, 2003, 2012, 2015) plus refreshment samples to maintain national representativeness, accounting for attrition and demographic changes.

Participants and eligibility

From the 2018 MHAS wave, we included adults aged 50 years and older at the time of interview with complete data on cancer diagnosis status and key demographic and health variables. Specific inclusion criteria were: (1) age 50 years or older at time of 2018 interview, (2) complete data on cancer diagnosis status, and (3) complete data on primary outcome variables. Exclusion criteria included individuals with incomplete data on cancer status, missing data on primary outcome variables, and institutional residents such as those in nursing homes or hospitals. After applying these criteria, our analytical sample comprised 9,302 participants, including 855 individuals with physician-diagnosed cancer (9.2%) and 8,447 controls without cancer diagnosis (90.8%).

While MHAS sample sizes were predetermined based on achieving national representativeness rather than specific hypothesis testing for our research question, we conducted post-hoc power analysis to evaluate our ability to detect meaningful differences between cancer survivors and controls. With our achieved sample size of 9,302 participants (855 cancer cases, 8,447 controls), we had greater than 80% power to detect effect sizes of 0.2 or larger (small to medium effects) for continuous variables and odds ratios of 1.3 or greater for binary outcomes, using a two-sided alpha level of 0.05.

Data collection procedures and instrumentation

Data were collected through computer-assisted personal interviews (CAPI) conducted by trained INEGI interviewers who underwent standardized training protocols. Direct interviews were prioritized, with proxy interviews conducted only when participants were unable to respond due to illness, hospitalization, or temporary absence. Comprehensive quality control measures included supervisor oversight during data collection, standardized interviewer training, and systematic data validation procedures implemented by INEGI.

The 2018 MHAS survey included comprehensive questionnaires covering multiple domains: demographic characteristics and socioeconomic status, health conditions and self-rated health assessment, functional status evaluation including Activities of Daily Living (ADL) and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL), cognitive assessment and mental health screening using the CESD-9 depression scale, social support and family network evaluation, healthcare utilization and insurance coverage, and detailed housing characteristics and wealth indicators. Interview duration typically ranged from 90 to 120 minutes, depending on the participant's health status and complexity of responses.

Variable definitions and measurements

Cancer status, our primary exposure variable, was defined through self-reported physician diagnosis using the question "Has a doctor ever told you that you have cancer?" with responses categorized as binary (Yes/No). While this approach does not distinguish between cancer types, stages, or treatment status, it identifies individuals who have received a formal cancer diagnosis from a healthcare provider, consistent with epidemiological surveys in aging populations.

Primary outcome variables included comprehensive socioeconomic indicators: total household wealth measured as a continuous variable in Mexican pesos, including all financial assets, real estate, business investments, and durable goods; employment status categorized as currently working versus not currently working; detailed labor force status including working, not working, homemaker, student, or looking for work; public pension receipt as a binary indicator; and home ownership status categorized as owned/currently paying, rented, or borrowed/free transfer.

Social support variables encompassed family and friend contact frequency, measured both as a binary indicator (any regular contact vs. none) and as a continuous frequency scale ranging from 1 (never) to 9 (daily); participation in social activities including religious services, community groups, volunteer work, or recreational activities; frequency of social activity participation on a 1-9 scale; and financial assistance received from children as a binary indicator.

Health and functional status measures included self-rated health on a 5-point Likert scale (excellent, very good, good, fair, poor); Body Mass Index calculated from self-reported height and weight; ADL limitations assessed through six standard items (bathing, dressing, eating, getting in/out of bed, walking, using toilet) with responses summed to create a continuous count of limitations [33]; IADL limitations evaluated through four items (managing money, shopping, preparing meals, managing daily activities) similarly summed [34, 35]; depression symptoms measured using the CESD-9 scale, a validated 9-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale with scores ranging from 0-9 [36, 37]; multiple sleep disturbance indicators including difficulty falling asleep, frequent night awakenings, early morning awakening, and feeling rested; pain assessment as a binary indicator of any pain experience; and falls in the past 2 years including both occurrence and frequency.

Additional covariates included age in continuous years, gender as binary (male/female), years of formal education as a continuous variable, smoking status (current/former smoker vs. never smoker), alcohol consumption (any current drinking vs. none), and history of falls in the previous 2 years.

Statistical analysis

The study employed a series of statistical methods to explore the associations between sleep disturbances, mental health, and cancer:

Descriptive statistics

Initial descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics, including means, standard deviations, and frequencies. Differences between cancer and control groups for continuous variables were assessed using independent t-tests, while chi-square tests were employed for categorical variables to highlight demographic disparities.

Multiple Logistic Regression: To evaluate the relationship between sleep disturbances and cancer, multiple logistic regression models were constructed to assess the influence of various covariates. This allowed for a thorough examination of how these diverse influences interacted with sleep disturbances to impact cancer outcomes.

OR were calculated for each model along with 95% CIs to determine the significance and strength of these relationships. Statistical significance was ascertained with a p-value < 0.05.

Bayesian statistics

In this study, we employed Bayesian statistical methods to evaluate the impact of cancer on various dimensions of sleep disorders. The Bayesian approach allows for the incorporation of prior knowledge and updating beliefs based on observed data, providing a comprehensive framework for understanding the complexities of our dataset. Specifically, we utilized Bayesian regression analysis to model the relationships between cancer diagnosis, sleep disturbances, and psychological factors such as depression.

The analysis involved estimating effect sizes and credible intervals to quantify the uncertainty around model parameters. This approach enhances our understanding of the direct effects while facilitating the exploration of indirect relationships through mediation analysis. The Bayesian framework allows for a nuanced interpretation of the data, accommodating the complexities inherent in the relationships between cancer, psychological factors, and sleep outcomes.

Propensity score matching

To control for potential confounding variables in our analysis, we employed propensity score matching (PSM) to create a balanced comparison between cancer patients and healthy controls. PSM is a statistical technique that estimates the effect of a treatment—in this case, cancer diagnosis—by accounting for the covariates that predict receiving the treatment.

Initially, we fitted a logistic regression model to estimate the propensity score for each participant based on demographic characteristics, including age, gender, education levels, and health status indicators. This score represents the probability of being diagnosed with cancer given the observed characteristics and allows for the matching of individuals from the cancer group with comparable individuals in the control group who share similar baseline characteristics.

After matching, we evaluated the balance between the two groups on key variables to ensure that the groups were comparable and that bias from confounders was minimized. This process provided a robust analytical framework, enabling us to focus on causal inference related to the effects of cancer on sleep disturbances while controlling for other influencing factors.

By integrating Bayesian statistics and propensity score matching into our methodology, we aimed to provide a rigorous analytical foundation for understanding the intricate relationships between cancer, mental health, and sleep disturbances among older adults.

Mediation analysis

To investigate potential mediating effects of sleep disturbances on cancer outcomes through psychological factors, two mediation analyses were conducted using SEM. This approach allows for a comprehensive assessment of the direct and indirect relationships between sleep disturbances, psychological factors, and cancer outcomes.

First Mediation Analysis: This analysis aimed to examine whether psychological health (measured with CES-D scores) mediated the relationship between sleep disturbances and cancer. The steps included:

Estimating the direct effect of sleep disturbances (e.g., difficulty falling asleep, frequency of night awakenings) on the diagnosis of cancer.

Assessing the relationship between sleep disturbances and the mediating variable (CES-D scores), allowing for the identification of any significant effects of sleep on psychological well-being.

Evaluating the indirect effect through the mediating pathway by examining how CES-D scores impacted the likelihood of cancer diagnosis.

Bootstrap methods were employed to obtain confidence intervals regarding the indirect effects, enhancing the robustness and accuracy of the mediation analysis results.

Second Mediation Analysis: The second analysis focused on whether life satisfaction and social engagement mediated the relationship between cancer and sleep disturbances. This analysis followed a similar procedure:

Estimating the direct path from cancer to sleep disturbances.

Evaluating the impact of cancer on life satisfaction and social engagement levels.

Examining how changes in life satisfaction and social engagement influenced sleep disturbances.

The evaluation utilized the same bootstrapping technique to establish confidence intervals for the indirect effects of the mediators, thus reinforcing the reliability of the findings.

Software used

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26) and R Statistical Software (version 4.0). SPSS was primarily utilized for descriptive statistics, basic comparative analyses (DESCRIPTIVES, FREQUENCIES, CROSSTABS, T-TEST), and initial data exploration, while R was particularly utilized for advanced statistical modeling, including mediation analysis and multiple logistic regression. Key R packages included survey (version 4.2-1) for complex survey data analysis, mediation (version 4.5.0) for mediation analyses, ggplot2 (version 3.4.2) for data visualization, and tableone (version 0.13.2) for descriptive statistics tables. Graphical representations were created with R to provide visual summaries of results, enhancing the interpretability and communication of statistical findings. The integration of both software platforms ensured comprehensive analytical capabilities while maintaining reproducibility through cross-validation of key results between platforms.

Ethical considerations and data access

The MHAS protocol has received comprehensive ethical approval from relevant institutional review boards in both Mexico and the United States, including the Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía (INEGI), Instituto Nacional de Geriatría (INGer) in Mexico, and the University of Texas Medical Branch Institutional Review Board where applicable. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, with consent procedures conducted in Spanish and following ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki regarding human subjects research.

The study adhered to principles of voluntary participation, with participants informed of their right to withdraw at any time or refuse to answer specific questions. Privacy and confidentiality were maintained through data de-identification procedures, with no personal identifiers retained in the analytical dataset. MHAS data are publicly available through the official study website (www.MHASweb.org) following established data use agreements that require researchers to agree to specific terms regarding data protection, appropriate use, and acknowledgment of the MHAS study team.

Characteristics of participants in the study

Gender distribution showed that approximately 67% of participants were male, with the control group having a slightly higher proportion of females (33%) compared to the cancer group (24%). Years of education averaged 6.17 years, with a median of 6 years spanning from none to 22 years in the control group and none to 21 years in the cancer group.

Self-rated health differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001). Most participants in both groups rated their health as fair, with 61.15% in the control group and 55.32% in the cancer group. However, cancer patients were more likely to rate their health as poor (31.46% vs. 18.89% in controls) and less likely to rate it as good (11.81% vs. 17.50% in controls). Only 0.77% of control participants and 0.47% of cancer patients rated their health as excellent.

The mean BMI for participants was 28.52, with no significant difference between groups. Smoking behavior was similar between groups, with 62.15% of control participants and 61.52% of cancer patients reporting no smoking habits. Alcohol consumption showed that cancer patients were slightly more likely to abstain from drinking (79.06%) compared to controls (75.91%).

Sleep disturbances were significantly more prevalent in the cancer group. Only 21.52% of cancer patients reported no difficulty falling asleep compared to 47.61% in the control group (p < 0.001). Similarly, only 12.40% of cancer patients reported no frequent night awakenings versus 27.77% for controls. Early morning awakening was also more problematic for cancer patients, with only 26.90% reporting no issues compared to 40.11% in controls.

However, the ability to feel rested was relatively preserved in both groups, with 80.12% of cancer patients and 83.55% of controls reporting feeling rested, though this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.01).

On the CESD-9 depression scale, cancer patients scored higher (mean: 5.21) compared to controls (mean: 4.32), indicating greater depressive symptoms. Life satisfaction levels showed cancer patients having slightly lower satisfaction (mean: 1.39) compared to controls (mean: 1.29).

In ADL and IADL, the cancer group reported higher difficulty levels, with mean scores of 0.92 vs. 0.56 for ADL and 0.39 vs. 0.26 for IADL. Specific challenges with daily tasks were prevalent, with cancer patients experiencing significantly more difficulties in personal care activities such as dressing (27.84% vs. 16.63% in controls) and bathing (10.88% vs. 5.97% in controls).

Falls during the previous two years were more common in the cancer group, with 60.12% reporting falls compared to 52.93% in controls (p < 0.001). The mean number of falls was higher in cancer patients (2.05) compared to controls (1.67).

Pain assessment revealed striking differences, with only 25.26% of cancer patients reporting no pain compared to 56.45% of controls, meaning that 74.74% of cancer patients experienced pain versus 43.55% of controls (p < 0.001), highlighting the significant health challenges faced by those with cancer.

Discussion

Key Findings

Our structural equation modeling analysis revealed a complex interplay between cancer diagnosis and various health, psychosocial, and sleep-related outcomes among older Mexican adults. Following the logical flow of our results, we first observed that cancer diagnosis had significant direct positive effects on pain assessment (β = 0.59, p < 0.001) and poorer self-rated health (β = 0.145, p < 0.001). These factors, in turn, strongly predicted higher depression symptoms (CESD scores), with pain showing a larger effect (β = 0.694, p < 0.001) compared to self-rated health (β = 0.317, p < 0.001). Depression symptoms were the strongest predictor of reduced life satisfaction (β = 0.069, p < 0.001), followed by self-rated health (β = 0.032, p < 0.001), ADL limitations (β = 0.026, p < 0.001), and IADL limitations (β = 0.039, p < 0.001), while pain had a non-significant direct effect (β = 0.009, p = 0.279). Finally, regarding sleep disturbances, depression and pain were consistent positive predictors across multiple indicators: difficulty falling asleep (depression β = 0.125, pain β = 0.126, both p < 0.001), frequency of night awakenings (depression β = 0.087, pain β = 0.104, both p < 0.001), and frequency of waking up too early (depression β = 0.109, pain β = 0.098, both p < 0.001). Life satisfaction showed mixed effects on sleep, with a marginal positive association with difficulty falling asleep (β = 0.04, p = 0.07), a significant positive link to waking up too early (β = 0.045, p = 0.043), no effect on night awakenings (β = 0.003, p = 0.891), and a negative association with feeling rested (β = −0.15, p < 0.001). Depression and pain also negatively impacted feeling rested (depression β = −0.046, pain β = −0.191, both p < 0.001).

Comparison with existing literature

These findings align with and extend literature on cancer's impacts in aging populations, especially in low- and middle-income countries like Mexico. Cancer's strong direct effect on pain (β = 0.59) Matches global reviews reporting pain prevalence of 39.77% in cancer survivors (95% CI 31.84-47.70), and cross-sectional studies showing high pain rates (63.5%) associated with elevated distress [38], while its link to poorer self-rated health (β = 0.145) echoes associations with inflammation and fatigue in colorectal cancer cohorts [39].

Pathways from pain and self-rated health to depression (β = 0.694 and 0.317) are supported by meta-analyses: pain correlates with 33.16% depression prevalence globally [40], and depression doubles as a risk factor in cancer patients (OR ≈ 2.0). Mediation analyses show depression partially mediating cancer's effects on life satisfaction, aligning with research where depression, not fatigue or insomnia, directly reduces quality of life [41].

For life satisfaction, negative impacts from depression (β = 0.069), self-rated health (β = 0.032), and functional limitations (ADL β = 0.026; IADL β = 0.039) but not pain (non-significant) diverge from studies linking pain to lower quality of life [39], possibly due to cultural resilience in Latinos, with functional independence aligning with symptom cluster models in prostate cancer survivors [42].

Mechanisms underlying the relationships

Several mechanisms may explain the observed relationships between cancer, mental health, and sleep disturbances.

Psychological distress: One of the primary mechanisms is the role of psychological distress in mediating the relationship between cancer and sleep disturbances [43]. Cancer patients frequently experience heightened anxiety and depression, which can disrupt normal sleep patterns [44]. The stress associated with cancer diagnosis and treatment can lead to hyperarousal and rumination, making it difficult for individuals to unwind and fall asleep [45]. Furthermore, depressed individuals often report changes in sleep architecture, including reduced slow-wave sleep and increased wakefulness during the night, contributing to the perception of poor sleep quality [46, 47].

Physical symptoms and treatment effects (FP): Another mechanism relates to the physical symptoms and side effects of cancer treatments [48]. Cancer can cause various physical complications, including pain, fatigue, and discomfort, which can directly affect sleep quality [49]. Additionally, treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation may lead to insomnia and other sleep disorders as side effects [50]. The combination of physical and emotional challenges can create a cumulative effect on sleep quality, exacerbating the difficulties faced by older adults with cancer [51].

Social isolation and support (FP): The role of social support and isolation is another critical factor influencing these relationships [52]. Older adults with cancer may experience social withdrawal, particularly if their illness limits their ability to engage in social activities [53]. This isolation can heighten feelings of loneliness and despair, further deteriorating mental health and compounding sleep disturbances [54]. Conversely, individuals with strong social networks may have better coping mechanisms and improved mental health outcomes, highlighting the importance of social support in this demographic [55].

Implications for interventions

Given the findings of this study, there are several implications for interventions aimed at improving the health and well-being of older adults with cancer. First, addressing mental health through psychosocial interventions is critical. For instance, implementing cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) may help alleviate sleep disturbances by equipping patients with strategies to manage negative thoughts and behaviors related to sleep [56, 57].

Additionally, enhancing social support networks can play a vital role in mitigating the psychological impact of cancer. Programs that foster community engagement and provide platforms for social interaction can help alleviate feelings of isolation among older adults. Support groups specifically tailored for cancer patients may also offer emotional relief and practical advice, contributing to improved mental health outcomes.

Healthcare providers must also take a comprehensive approach to pain management and symptom relief. By adequately addressing the physical symptoms of cancer, providers can significantly improve patients' sleep quality and overall mental health.

Limitations and future research

While the findings of this study contribute significantly to the understanding of the relationships between cancer, mental health, and sleep disturbances, several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional nature of the analysis limits the ability to establish causal relationships definitively. Longitudinal studies are needed to ascertain the temporal dynamics of these relationships and further clarify the mechanisms involved.

Moreover, relying on self-reported measures for assessing mental health and sleep may introduce bias, as individuals may underreport or overreport their conditions. Future research should consider incorporating objective measures of sleep, such as actigraphy or polysomnography, to gain a more accurate picture of sleep disturbances in this population.

Finally, investigating interventions that specifically target the identified mechanisms—such as psychological support and pain management—would provide valuable insights into effective approaches for improving outcomes among older adults with cancer.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underscores the critical connections between cancer, mental health, and sleep disturbances among older adults. The mechanisms identified provide a deeper understanding of how chronic illness affects psychological well-being and sleep quality. By addressing these interconnected issues through targeted interventions, healthcare providers can enhance the overall quality of life for older adults facing the challenges of cancer. Future research should continue to explore these relationships to inform best practices in geriatric care, ultimately leading to better health outcomes for this vulnerable population.
