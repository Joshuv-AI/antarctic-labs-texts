Human carnivore conflict in and around Borena Sayint Worehimanu National Park, South Wollo, Amhara Region, Ethiopia: A community-based cross-sectional study design
Authors: Indiris Abdu, Nurye Seid Muhie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human carnivore conflict is a global issue for conservationists, especially in protected areas such as national parks. Research on human carnivore conflict around the Borena Saynt Worehimenu National Park has been conducted with the aim of assessing the conflict between communities and carnivores. A cross-sectional study design was used to investigate human carnivore conflict in the community of the study area. Among the six bordering woreda (District), four were selected purposively for the study. From each woreda, the village, which is the immediate boundary to the park, was included. Both open- and close-ended questionnaires were administered to 573 randomly selected respondents. A total of six carnivorous species and one opportunistic omnivorous species were reported as predators of domestic animals, causing conflict. Pantera pardus and Crocuta crocuta are the most reported carnivores, with contributions of 46.9% and 26.5%, respectively, to the conflict. Together, sheep and goats are the most common domestic prey, accounting for approximately 71.6% of the prey species. The majority of predation occurs during the day, when domestic animals graze in the communal land. A total of 45.7% of the respondents reported that the degree of conflict has increased in the last three years. Poisoning the remaining parts (43.6%) is the most common response of farmers, while their domestic animals are killed by carnivores, as a revenge followed by chasing (28.8%) and then direct killing (18.8%) as revenge. The total economic cost of carnivore depredation was substantial 22,968,500 ETB (166438 USD). Farmers use guarding and physical barrier techniques, to protect their domestic herd from predators. The respondents believed that effective government intervention should focus on providing alternative livelihoods and compensation for losses of livestock and property. The conflict was attributed to overgrazing, agricultural expansion, and a lack of compensation. The establishment of livestock insurance programs; community-based conservation and benefit sharing; the creation of alternative livelihoods; and education and implementation of regulations are essential measures for reducing and managing conflicts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40850-026-00261-7.

Introduction

Carnivores are a mammal group under the order carnivore. Carnivores include approximately 26 species, which are characterized predominantly by their dependency on flesh as fodder [1]. They are predators, and some are both predators and scavengers [2–6]. They are intelligent mammals with an enormous diversity of diets, hunting strategies and social structures. To catch and kill other animals, they must be well coordinated and remain one step ahead of their prey [7]. Carnivores are found on every major landmass and in a variety of habitats, ranging from cold Polar Regions of Earth to hyper arid regions of the Sahara Desert and the open seas. They exhibit a wide array of body plans, varying greatly in size and shape [7, 8]. Most carnivores have relatively large brains, high levels of intelligence and less complicated digestive systems than herbivores [8].

Large and small carnivores regulate herbivore populations and prevent overgrazing [5]. In Bale Mountains and Simien Mountains for example Ethiopian wolf controls rodent populations in Afro-alpine habitats [9] and The Lion regulate large herbivores. scavengers like the Spotted hyena remove carcasses, lowering the risk of zoonotic disease transmission and pollution in rural landscapes [10]. On the other hand, Ethiopia is hosts for endemic carnivores such as the Ethiopian wolf, the rarest canid in the world. Protecting carnivores means protecting entire ecosystems because they function as Umbrella species and Indicator species [11] Moreover, Carnivores attract tourists and researchers. Protected areas like Bale Mountains National Park and Awash National Park benefit economically from wildlife tourism. Flagship species such as lions and Ethiopian wolves increase international attention, generating revenue that supports conservation and local livelihoods [12].

Human-carnivore conflict refers to any interaction between humans and wild carnivores, causing harm to wildlife, humans, or their properties [13]. This usually occurs because of the competition between humans and wildlife for shared and limited resources [14]. This conflict has caused significant losses in the economy and human and animal life, and it is mostly severe in and around protected areas [15]. Owing to their obligate carnivorous habits, large carnivores are causing intensive conflicts, which leads them to compete with humans [16].

Wildlife conflict is a worldwide problem [17]. Africa is a continent with many wildlife, and people predominantly live in rural areas. Many people engage in primary production, such as farming, nomadic herding, fishing and mining, resulting in prevalent human‒wildlife conflicts [18]. As several findings have shown, there are significant conflicts between humans and carnivores in different parts of Africa, which face serious problems. Factors such as the seasonal pattern of species distribution, environmental conditions and livestock husbandry practices in the area may increase this problem [19].

Competition for resources between humans and carnivores has been reported in various areas of Ethiopia [20–22]. In many rural areas of Ethiopia, limited access to conservation education, together with broader challenges related to land use policy, governance, and resource management, influences community perceptions toward wildlife coexistence [23]. A lack of such knowledge might be the major reason for their conflict with carnivores; these animals are facing a serious threat to their survival [24]. The problem is more severe in national parks, forests, sanctuaries and other protected areas. This conflict may result from direct threats to human life, destruction of property by wild animals, direct competition for forage and reproduction, and loss of biodiversity [25].

The increasing population of humans and the expansion of agricultural land have forced wildlife into modified areas. People living around BSWNP commonly practice agriculture. As a result, local people are forced to clear nearby forests, damaging wildlife and animals and affecting local people around parks. This activity increases conflict in the area [3]. The problem of conflict is the result of far-reaching economic and social effects, problems associated with wildlife, the loss of human life exchange among earners at the national level, the destruction of endemic wildlife, the displacement of animals to other places and the destruction of crops and livestock [26]. The conservation and management of carnivores are central to the conservation and management of biodiversity [27].

The people living adjacent to this park face livestock depredation by carnivores, resulting in the loss of property and even human life. To solve and design intervention strategies for such conservation challenges, scientific studies on the cause and severity of the problem are needed. Although human carnivore conflict has been reported in several regions of Ethiopia, there is limited site-specific evidence regarding the magnitude, patterns, and underlying drivers of depredation in this particular area. This gap constrains the development of targeted and evidence-based intervention strategies. Despite previous biodiversity studies in parks [26], and study on human wildlife conflict in the area [3], there is no research has specifically examined the intensity or patterns related to human carnivore conflict. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the various dimensions of human carnivore conflict related to the use of in Borena Saynt Worehimenu National Park.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted at Borena Sayint Worehimanu National Park(BSWNP) which is established during 2008 under Regulation No. 68/2017 [26]. The park lays in between 38 ° 48 ’ 0 ” to 39o 12’ 0 ” E and 10o 48 ’ 0 ” to 11 o 4 ’ 0 ” N. The park is located in the South Wollo zone of the Amhara National Regional State, approximately 600 km from Addis Ababa, 300 km from Bahir-Dar, and 200 km from Dessie (zonal city) and 16 km from Mekane Selam town. The Park is shared between Borena, Legambo, Tenta, Mekdela, Sayint and Mehal Sayint districts(woreda). The park is bordered in the west by Mehal Sayint and Saint Woredas, in the east and south by Legambo and Borena woredas and in the north by Tenta and Mekdela woredas (Fig. 1) [28].

Flora and fauna

The park total area covered 15262 hectares. It has different topographical features ranging from low land to highland mountains. The altitude ranges between 1900 and 4280 m(Mount golate) above sea level [29]. BSWNP, consists 174 species of plants. Giant lobelia (Jibera), Bidens pachyloma (Adey-Abeba), Plectocephalus, Euphorbia dumalis, Acanthus sennii (Shekori), Solanaceous gigas (Yeshikoko Gomen), Echinops longisetus and Echinops kebericho are endemic plant species to the park [30]. The park harbors Seven species of small mammals and 23 large and medium sized mammals including the critically endangered Ethiopian wolf (Canis semensis) and the very common Gelada baboon (T.gelada) which are endemic to Ethiopia, carnivores like Leopard, Caracal, Spotted hyaena, Common Jackal are common in the park [31].

Study design and study period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted to evaluate the dynamics of human-carnivore conflict (HCC) within the peripheral zones of Borena Saynt Worehimenu National Park. To establish a foundational understanding of the park’s ecological and administrative context, preliminary qualitative data were gathered through structured discussion with the park head office and field scouts. To quantify the economic impact of livestock depredation, a retrospective cross-sectional approach was employed to reconstruct a three-year trend of conflict incidents and economic impact based on community recall and perception, providing a temporal framework for the intensity of depredation. The data related to human carnivore conflict and conservation challenges were collected from communities living in and around the park during December 2023 to august 2024.

Sample size and sampling techniques

Of the six woredas bordering the BSWNP, four (Legambo, Borena, Mekdela, and Saint) were purposively selected for this study based on two primary criteria. First, these districts represent the historically core areas of the park, encompassing the original demarcation zones where human-wildlife interactions have the longest documented history. Second, these woredas contain the highest density of woodland vegetation cover within the park’s boundaries. This ecological characteristic provides essential habitat for large carnivores, thereby creating a higher probability of livestock predation and human-carnivore conflict compared to the two excluded woredas, which are characterized by more degraded or peripheral habitats and the recent part of the park.”

One village(kebele) from each sampled district(woreda), which shares an immediate boundary with the park was purposively selected to conduct the study. To design a large sample efficiently, a pilot study was conducted prior to the survey to gain information for designing the survey. To determine the study participants, first, the sample proportion (p) was determined via a pilot survey of 20 participants from all kebeles by asking 8 important questions for each participant. From this pilot survey, approximately 72% of the participants answered ‘Yes’ (probability of success), and the remaining 28% participants answered ‘No’ (probability of failure) out of 160 total responses. The total sample size was determined using the formula of Cochran, 1977 [32] with margin of error (d = 0.07) due to the exploratory nature of this study and the significant logistical challenges posed by the rugged topography and dispersed household settlements within the Borena Saynt Worehimenu National Park landscape.

Data collection procedure and quality

A preliminary survey was carried out to collect information about the physical and biological features of the study area. The habitat type and accessibility, climate, vegetation, fauna, topography, infrastructure and other characteristics of the area are identified and located. In addition, discussions with some of the local people were carried out, and information about the major wild fauna and flora of the area was gathered [33]. Furthermore, during this period, with all the available and relevant information for this study, local people’s attitudes toward wild fauna were assessed, and HWCs were also recorded. This preliminary survey is helpful for becoming familiar with the study area [34].

Prior to the actual data collection, permission from the BSNP authorities and local administrative offices had obtained to conduct the research, and the purpose of the study would be explained as intended only to gather genuinely relevant information to help support the wildlife conservation effort of the BSNP through balancing the needs of the people and wildlife. During this time, the boundaries of the park were identified, the number of villages was determined, and a general understanding of the overall situation of the park was obtained. The questionnaires were pretested among some groups of the respondents, which did not include the main sample group. The main purpose of the pilot survey is to evaluate the questionnaire and to check whether it is applicable and suitable in the study area. It is also used to check whether a question is understood by the respondents and is also used to identify the period and occurrence of HCC. Then, on the basis of the results of the pilot survey, the questionnaire was revised and developed.

To facilitate rigorous data collection, a field team comprising eight personnel was assembled, consisting of four park scouts/supervisors and four experienced enumerators recruited from the local study area. The selection of local personnel was strategic, ensuring linguistic competence and geographical familiarity with the Borena Saynt Worehimenu landscape. Prior to the commencement of fieldwork, all team members underwent a comprehensive eight-day technical training program. The curriculum focused on survey administration techniques and Ethical protocols for engaging with community respondents. To achieve the objectives of the study, household questionnaires was used. The questionnaire was prepared in English (S1), and the respondents were farmers and may not have read and written English or even the local language. Therefore, the field collector assisted in the face-to-face reading and translation of the questionnaire into the Amharic language.

Prior to analysis the collected data were screened for entry errors and outliers and managed properly. Quantitative data were processed and analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. The Chi-square test was selected as the primary inferential tool because the study variables were measured on nominal and ordinal scales with assumptions of Each household response was treated as an independent data point and more than 20% of the cells had expected frequencies less than 5, and no cell had an expected frequency less than 1. Descriptive statistics and chi-square tests were used to analyze the data, and the results are presented in tables and charts. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the socio-economic characteristics of the respondents and the frequency of depredation events. To examine the associations between categorical variables, Pearson’s Chi-square (χ2) tests were performed. All point estimates are reported with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) with statistical significance of p < 0.05.

To quantify the economic impact of livestock depredation over the last three years, a multi-stage valuation approach was employed. First, the total number of livestock predated in the last three years was recorded and categorized by species. To determine the financial value of these losses, current market prices were obtained from district-level market and trade offices. To account for market fluctuations and variations in animal size or age, a standardized unit price for each species was established by calculating the mean of the reported “highest,” “medium,” and “lowest” market values. All economic losses were initially calculated in Ethiopian Birr (ETB) and subsequently converted to United States Dollars (USD) based on the exchange rate prevailing on September 26, 2025(equating to 1 USD = 130ETB). Given that the survey utilized a three-year recall period, the data were standardized to reflect the mean annual loss and the annual loss per household (n = 573) to facilitate comparison with regional and global studies.

Causes of human carnivore conflict

The primary cause of the conflict was overgrazing (29.3%), followed by illegal agricultural expansion (17.5%) (Fig. 2). The respondents’ thoughts on the causes of conflict were significantly different (χ2 = 87.287, def. =18, P < 0.05). Overall, conflicts occurred slightly more in the wet season (53.2%) than in the dry season (46.8%). With respect to the specific study site, in Legambo, illegal agricultural expansion was the greatest cause of conflict (36.0%), followed by a lack of compensation for lost property and lives (34.5%). In Sayint, illegal settlement was the main issue (39.5%), coupled with inadequate compensation (36.9%). In Borna, overgrazing is a primary concern of the conflict (33.3%), whereas Mekdela faced illegal hunting as its dominant cause (40.0%) (Table 3).

The most common domestic animals killed by predators were sheep, which were the most frequent victims of carnivorous attacks (48.2%), followed by goats (23.4%). Other animals, such as chickens, donkeys, and cattle, account for the remaining 28.4% of attacks. Sheep consistently rank as the top prey everywhere, but they are particularly targeted at Legambo (56.2%) and Mekdela (55.7%). Goats maintain their second-place position in all Woredas; their share of attacks is 21.7% in Sayint and reaches 26.0% in Legambo. Chickens become a significant target in Sayint, making up 25.6% of all attacks there a much higher percentage than anywhere else. Donkeys are most often killed in Borena, representing 15.3% of attacks (Table 4).

Species of carnivores are mostly responsible for carnivore conflict

Panthera pardus is involved in the most conflicts, accounting for 46.9% of incidents. Crocuta crocuta was second at 26.5%, and a common jackal ranked third, accounting for 17.3%. Together, these three species are responsible for more than 90% of all recorded conflicts. The other species include Canis simensis, Theropithecus gelada, Lycaon pictus, and Caracal, which carry a low percentage of the conflict (Fig. 2).

Predator affinity for each domestic animal

The data indicate a significant relationship between the type of predator and the domestic predator prey (χ2 = 621.764, def. = 25, P = 0.000) (Table 9). Sheep and goats are the primary prey for leopards, hyenas, and baboons. Leopards, for example, attack sheep in 68.0% of their encounters and goats in 29.5%. Hyenas prefer sheep (34.2%) or goats (22.3%). Baboons similarly focus on sheep (68.8%) and goats (31.3%). Common jackals and red foxes, however, mostly target smaller livestock. Common jackals, for example, primarily hunt chickens, which make up 87.3% of their attacks. Red foxes also frequently prey on chickens (55.0%). Farmers’ reactions while their domestic animals are killed by carnivores

When carnivores kill domestic animals, farmers most often poison the remaining parts (43.6%). Chasing the animals away was the next most frequent action (28.8%) followed by killing the predators directly out of revenge (18.8%). In Mekdela, poisoning is the most commonly used technique (52.1%). Chasing is uncommon here (5.7%), but direct killing is also fairly common (32.9%). Sayint and Borena reported that poisoning was the most common response (44.1% and 44.4%, respectively). In Borena, direct killing (17.4%) and chasing (29.9%) are both common. However, chasing ranks second in Sayint (35.7%), whereas Legambo stands out, with chasing (43.2%) and poisoning (34.2%) being the most frequent responses and the lowest rate of direct killing (5.5%). There was a statistically significant association between the districts and their responses to predator attacks or revenge (χ2 = 97.146, def. = 9, P = 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Human carnivore conflict is a primary factor contributing to the worldwide decline in carnivore populations. Addressing these problems is a key priority for conservation efforts [35]. Addressing and preventing conflict is an immediate priority; once this is achieved, people feel that their interests have been recognized and that their livelihoods are respected. The results of the present study revealed that there is a strong human-predator conflict in and around. During the present investigation, a total of seven species of carnivores and one omnivore were recorded as predators of domestic animals, causing the loss of domestic animals and even the occasional attack of people in the area.

The primary root cause and driving factor of human‒carnivore conflict in and around borena Saynt Worehimenu National Park is the overgrazing of domestic herds in and adjacent to the park (29.3%), followed by illegal agricultural expansion (17.5%). This might be because domestic animals frequently move into or near carnivorous habitats [36, 37]. Natural prey becomes scarce, resulting in conflict. Carnivores then have to find other food sources, and domestic animals become easier targets [38, 39]. This aligns with what other studies have shown. For example, a study in Ethiopia’s Bale Mountains National Park identified overgrazing as a major cause of livestock predation [40]. Similarly, research in Tanzania’s Serengeti ecosystem revealed a strong, direct link between livestock presence and predation [41] and a high rate of a direct link between livestock presence and predation events.

Livestock predation usually follows seasonal patterns, although there are some exceptions [42]. In the study area, human carnivore conflict is slightly more severe during the wet season (53.2%) than during the dry season (46.8%). Similar results have been reported from Chebera Churchura National Park, Ethiopia [43], and Tsavo National Park, Kenya [44]. This might be wet season usually results in more plants and water. This often scatters prey animals into new areas. Carnivores, looking for this food, then move closer to human settlements and livestock. Hunting for dispersed prey expands their home ranges significantly to areas where people live (Bauer & De Iongh, 2005; Mbise, 2018) [41, 45].

Sheep are the most common prey of domestic animals (48.2%). This result is in agreement with the findings of different studies. For example, the results of this study agree with those of studies in Bale Mountains National Park [40], in the Guna Mountain Community Conservation Area, Ethiopia [46], and in southern Kashmir, India, which identified sheep as a primary prey species [47]. This may be due to, as the study area is a highland ecosystem, the communities in the current study were focused primarily on raising sheep. This difference might also be related to their size, lack of natural defenses in sheep, and management techniques [48].

Leopards are a major cause of human carnivore conflict (46.9%). This might be the result of the unique biological and behavioral characteristics of leopards. Their biological characteristics cause them to secretly raid livestock, even anyone noticing. Similar situations have been documented in places such as the Community Managed Conservation Forest in Wolaita Sodo, Ethiopia [49], in Nepal Bardia National Park [50] and Western Java, Indonesia [51]. Spotted hyenas are also the second major contributor to human–carnivore conflict. Sociality may make them efficient predators and target livestock, especially at night, when they are less likely to encounter resistance [52]. This allows them to overwhelm and take down domestic animals that might be too large for a solitary predator to handle [52]. The current results agreed with those of studies in northern Ethiopia [53] and in Tanzania [52]. The other listed species, such as the Ethiopian wolf (Canis simensis), gelada baboon (Theropithecus gelada), African wild dog (Lycaon pictus), and caracal (Caracal caracal), contribute a relatively small percentage of the total conflicts because their diet consists mostly of rodents (97.8%) [44].

The affinity of carnivores for prey depends on size, hunting method, and livestock vulnerability. This is true across Africa and other regions, as supported by various studies. Leopards, hyenas, and baboons, for example, mostly predate sheep and goats. These animals are easier to predate than larger prey and are often exposed. In this study, leopards favored sheep (68.0%) and goats (29.5%). These livestock are fairly small, manageable, and often left unguarded in fields. A study in Kenyan revealed that leopards living near people often hunt goats and sheep because they are the most common and vulnerable livestock available (Yirga et al., 2025). While they have the power to hunt larger animals such as donkeys (19.7%), they have a high preference for sheep and goats (34.2% and 22.3%, respectively), possibly because of their hunting strategy. Hyenas are efficient group hunters that are opportunistic and will visit for the easiest available prey. Donkeys are aggressive and larger than sheep or goats are and require more effort and risk to hunt [54]. Baboons often predate sheep (68.8%) and goats (31.3%) and cause serious problems in some areas. These powerful primates kill and eat small livestock as people expand into baboon habitats [55]. Since common jackal and red foxes are relatively small predators, their high preference for chickens (87.3% and 55.0%) is due to their size and efficiency. A chicken food for carnivores with very little risk, making it a highly attractive target [56]. The total number of chickens chosen is 100% of their number of attacks given their size and hunting style [49].

Most frequently, animals are predated during the day while grazing on communal grazing land outside the park (45.5%). This finding aligns with studies in Bale Mountains National Park [40] and in Senkele Swayne’s Hartebeest Sanctuary, Ethiopia [21], and with studies conducted in Kenya [57]. During the day, livestock usually leave the house to graze, which makes them more vulnerable to predators. However, the current findings are different from those reported from Kafta Sheraro National Park [21] and Wolaita, Ethiopia [49], where most livestock depredations occur at night.

Husbandry techniques may have a great impact on livestock predation [58]. Farmers primarily use proactive and direct methods to protect their livestock from carnivores, with the most widely used method being guarding (direct herding by adults (36.5%) followed by physical barriers, such as fences (27.9)). People often value these methods because they are immediate and tangible. The preference agreed with findings from a previous study on human‒wildlife conflict in Borena Saynt National Park [28], Chebera Churchura National Park [43] and bale mountain national park [40], which identified guarding as a key strategy used by local communities. The findings across Ethiopia show a consistent pattern: where modern or expensive solutions are not available, communities turn to direct, low-tech methods, such as guarding, to mitigate issues.

There was an increasing trend of livestock depredation in the study area. The majority of respondents believe that human–carnivore conflict has increased over the last three years (45.7%), indicating that the trend is not a random occurrence and that there has been a genuine change in the level of conflict. This finding is consistent with reports that the wolaita sodo community managed conservation forests [49], Borena sayint [28] and Bale Mountain National Park [40], which reported an increasing trend of conflict. This rise can be attributed to human population growth and encroachment. A study in Namibia reported that habitat fragmentation is a key factor driving an increase in conflict [59].

The most common reactions of farmers while their livestock are killed by carnivores are poisoning, chasing, and direct killing in revenge. This finding on revengeful killing has been reported in different studies across Ethiopia and Africa. A study in Ethiopia’s Bale Mountains National Park [40] reported similar findings, with local communities resorting to retaliatory killings, including poisoning, as a response to livestock losses caused by predators such as African wolves and spotted hyenas. A study in Laikipia County, Kenya, revealed that retaliatory killing was a primary cause of lion mortality [60]. Research on human‒wildlife conflict in Namibia highlighted that farmers use a variety of poisons to kill carnivores as a direct response to livestock depredation, which is a significant threat to carnivore populations [56]. Retaliation is a widespread and difficult part of human‒wildlife conflict. These actions usually result from the large economic and emotional losses that farmers face. Farmers often turn to lethal methods because they feel that there is no other effective way to protect their livelihoods. Studies often show that when conflicts between humans and carnivores run high and farmers see their way of life under threat, killing animals in return is a common, expected reaction.

The majority of the community (66.1%) had a negative attitude toward the conservation of carnivores. This attitude is higher in Legambo (72.6%) and lowest in Borna (39.6%). The variation in attitudes between Woredas (districts) is likely driven by the differing levels of conflict and the specific threats each community faces. This might be a direct and logical response to the high costs they bear from human–carnivore conflict. These negative attitudes may also be caused by a lack of tangible benefits from conservation. The communities that experienced the most frequent and severe livestock losses to lions held the most negative attitudes toward carnivores [42], and community tolerance for carnivores was directly linked to the costs of conflict [56]. Local communities are generally negative about wildlife, especially predators, because of the damage they cause to their livelihoods [43].

This perception greatly varies by district, with one district reporting very high severity (76.4%) and another (Legambo) reporting much lower severity. A review of human‒wildlife conflict in Ethiopia confirmed that the perceived severity of conflict varies significantly across different regions. The review linked this variation to local factors such as the type of wildlife, the type of agricultural activities, and the effectiveness of local mitigation strategies [43]. Research on Tanzania’s Serengeti ecosystem reports that perceptions of conflict severity heavily influence conservation attitudes. For example, communities experiencing significant livestock losses view the conflict as more serious, which makes them less tolerant of carnivores [41]. Moreover, research from Namibia has shown that the severity of conflict is not consistent nationwide. This suggests that human practices can directly shape how severe these conflicts are for people [56].

To ease human‒wildlife conflict, most residents in the study area prefer two government actions: offering alternative livelihoods (44.8%) and setting up compensation (37.9%). This preference comes from the direct financial hardships caused by these conflicts. These steps address the main reason that people dislike carnivore conservation. The study suggested that ecotourism and conservation-based jobs could help communities reduce their reliance on farming and livestock, which would in turn lower conflict [43]. A similar study in Tanzania revealed that offering alternative ways to make a living, such as beekeeping or ecotourism, made local communities less dependent on livestock for income. This also meant that they had less reason to kill carnivores in retaliation [57]. A compensation scheme is a crucial first step in building a positive relationship with local communities and encouraging them to tolerate wildlife [61]. Studies in Namibia also support compensation schemes, which can significantly improve conflict [56]. The low support for community displacement and sharing park land are socially disruptive and can be met with strong resistance, whereas sharing park land is often seen as a threat to the park’s ecological integrity and can increase, rather than decrease, conflict. These do not allow the core issue of coexistence.

Livestock depredation creates a significant economic burden for communities in the study area. Carnivore attacks have led to a total economic loss of 22,968,500 ETB, approximately 166,438 USD. This averages annual loss USD 96.82 per household, revealing considerable financial strain. These figures align with similar studies conducted across Ethiopia and other African regions. For example, a study conducted in Ethiopia’s Kafta Sheraro National Park [21] revealed a five-year economic loss of more than $170,000. According to a study conducted in Bale Mountains National Park, livestock depredation caused a total loss of 347,460.53 USD and a loss of 1040.3 USD per household over a ten-year period [40]. The economic cost of conflict, along with a total lack of compensation, resulted in a high-high conservation problem. This financial loss and conflict constitute not only a conservation problem but also a serious threat to rural livelihoods [62]. Losing livestock can be a great financial problem for communities that depend on it for their main income. This pushes households deeper into poverty, often resulting in resentment among residents.

Conclusion

Human carnivore conflict is a global issue hindering wildlife conservation in areas where human and wildlife needs overlap. The conflict in the current study area is obsessed primarily by livestock predation and perceived as severe by the community which causes great financial losses for rural farmers which become increasing over the past three years. The Community uses farming and livestock rearing as a livelihood income which requires large farms and grazing land. These interns increase the interaction.

The most frequently predated animals were sheep (48.2%) during the daytime, while grazing on communal land outside the park (45.5%). In response to these attacks, farmers most commonly used revengeful actions, such as poisoning (43.6%), chasing away carnivores (28.8%) and even direct killing (18.8%). The economic cost of depredation, combined with a total lack of compensation, forces farmers to protect their livelihoods through such actions. The lack of compensation for livestock losses is a critical driver of negative attitudes and retaliatory killings. Leopards (46.9%) and hyenas (26.5%) were the main carnivores causing these attacks. Most of the community (66.1%) viewed wild carnivore conservation negatively near to community village. To minimize the conflict, respondents suggested offering alternative livelihoods (48.3%) direct compensation for the damage (36.8%).

Limitation of the study

This study limited to the four sample woredas bordering the Borena Saynt Worehimenu National Park in which results cannot be generalized to other protected areas in Ethiopia. The second limitation might be study design. The study used a cross-sectional study design which collect data at once which can inform the prevalence of a problem and shows relation among variables but it cannot inform how things change over time. The other limitation is, the study descriptive statistics with Pearson’s Chi-Square tests. This indicate whether there is a statistically significant association between two categorical variables but it not informs strength of that relationship.
