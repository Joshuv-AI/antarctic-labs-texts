Isolation and characterization of a novel phage belonging to a new genus against Enterococcus faecium
Authors: Lin Yuan, Xuaner Luo, Jianjun Guo, Siyuan Yue, Jing Zeng, Peng Li, Tong Wang, Junhui Nie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Enterococcus faecium is a Gram-positive bacterium commonly found in the intestines of humans and animals. While certain strains exhibit probiotic properties, others have become significant pathogens due to their high level of antibiotic resistance. Bacteriophages, as natural bacterial predators, have emerged as promising alternatives for controlling bacterial infections, especially antibiotic-resistant strains. In this study, we isolated and characterized a novel bacteriophage, Enterococcus phage XWef1, which targets E. faecium, from waste materials of a feed additive production industry. The phage displayed optimal activity at pH levels between 6 and 8, with high stability at temperatures below 40 °C. It exhibited a narrow host range. Genomic analysis revealed 63 open reading frames (ORFs), including genes related to structural proteins, DNA replication, and host lysis. The comparative genomic analysis showed that XWef1 has less than 70% intergenomic similarities with its most similar phages. Thus, we propose that XWef1 is a novel Enterococcus phage belonging to a new genus. The discovery of XWef1 not only enriches our understanding of phage diversity but also offers new perspectives for phage research and development, with promising implications for microbial control and therapeutic applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04428-z.

Introduction

Enterococcus faecium, a Gram-positive bacterium, is commonly found colonizing the intestinal tracts of humans and animals. Morphologically, it appears as spherical or oval cells, existing singly, in pairs, or occasionally forming short chains. As a facultative anaerobe, E. faecium exhibits considerable environmental adaptability, capable of growth under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. Its resilience extends to a wide range of temperatures and pH levels, coupled with tolerance to high salt concentrations and bile presence. These characteristics enable its survival and proliferation not only within the intestinal environment but also under various other demanding conditions [1, 2]. Within the gut microbiome, E. faecium functions as part of the commensal flora, playing a role in digestion and contributing to microbial balance [3]. Intriguingly, research has demonstrated that specific strains possess probiotic properties. These beneficial strains are associated with enhanced immunity in poultry [4], improved livestock production performance [5], and promotion of intestinal health [6]. Consequently, E. faecium finds applications in the pharmaceutical, food (e.g., fermented products), and animal agriculture sectors (e.g., probiotic feed supplements). Certain strains have undergone safety evaluations by regulatory authorities globally and are utilized in intestinal therapeutics and feed additives [7]. However, this bacterium also presents a dual nature. Under specific conditions, particularly in immunocompromised individuals or when the intestinal barrier is disrupted, certain strains can act as opportunistic pathogens, causing serious infections [8, 9]. Furthermore, E. faecium exhibits intrinsic resistance to numerous antimicrobial classes, including vancomycin, aminoglycosides, and specific β-lactam antibiotics [10, 11]. This inherent resistance, compounded by the rising incidence of acquired resistance, poses substantial therapeutic challenges in managing E. faecium infections [12].

Bacteriophages (phages) are viruses characterized by their specificity for infecting and lysing bacterial hosts. They represent the most abundant biological entities on Earth, inhabiting all environments where bacteria exist, including soil, aquatic systems, and the microbiomes of animals and humans. Structurally, a typical phage comprises a protein capsid enclosing its genetic material (DNA or RNA), often complemented by a specialized tail apparatus crucial for host cell recognition, attachment, and genome delivery. These viruses play fundamental ecological roles: regulating bacterial population dynamics, mediating horizontal gene transfer, maintaining microbial community balance, and facilitating nutrient cycling [13]. Significantly, their ability to target specific pathogens positions them as critical natural regulators of infectious disease spread [14]. Given their inherent bacteriolytic activity and specificity, phages have long been investigated as therapeutic agents against bacterial infections. Exploration of phage therapy dates back to the 1920 s [15]. Contemporary research continues to validate this approach. For instance, a 2014 study successfully utilized phage therapy to treat infected central venous catheters in a rabbit model, demonstrating its potential for managing catheter-related infections [16]. Furthermore, phage preparations targeting Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections, including wound and urinary tract infections, have been clinically employed in Georgia and Russia [17, 18]. Most recently, advances have explored engineered phages to combat highly drug-resistant pulmonary infections caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, with promising results [19]. This phage-based approach offers new hope for combating highly resistant pathogens. Collectively, these developments underscore the potential of phage-based strategies as novel therapeutic avenues, particularly against multidrug-resistant pathogens.

The subject of this study originates from a local feed probiotic additive industry ​that utilizes Enterococcus faecium for probiotic production. This industry ​cultivates​ E. faecium probiotic strains with beneficial properties, but their production process has been compromised by ​disruptive​ bacteriophage infection, resulting in significant production losses. From the failed production waste, we isolated a novel bacteriophage and conducted an in-depth investigation. ​The study has two primary objectives:​​ first, to identify methods for ​protecting​ E. faecium cultures ​from bacteriophage contamination during industrial probiotic production; second, given E. faecium’s status as an opportunistic pathogen exhibiting multi-antibiotic resistance, to evaluate this phage’s potential significance for ​controlling​ E. faecium infections. We characterize the phage’s biological properties and genomic sequence, establishing its classification within a novel genus based on these features.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains and culture media

The Enterococcus faecium strains and other bacterial strains used in this study were preserved by the Jiangxi Academy of Sciences, Institute of Microbiology. Enterococcus faecium was cultured in MRS medium (10.0 g/L peptone, 10.0 g/L beef extract, 4.0 g/L yeast extract, 2.0 g/L ammonium citrate, 5.0 g/L sodium acetate, 0.2 g/L magnesium sulfate, 0.05 g/L manganese sulfate, 2.0 g/L dipotassium phosphate, 20.0 g/L glucose, 1.0 g/L Tween 80) (Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China). The culture was conducted in a 5 L fermenter at 37 °C with aeration, and the pH was adjusted and maintained at 6.2 using calcium hydroxide.

Isolation, purification, and transmission electron microscopy observation of bacteriophages

The bacteriophage was isolated from waste material from a feed probiotic additive industry in Nanchang, Jiangxi Province, China. The isolation, purification, concentration, and replication of the bacteriophages were performed according to standard methods as previously described [20]. The double-layer agar plate method was used to detect the presence of the bacteriophage [21]. Enterococcus faecium XW113 in the logarithmic phase, cultured in a 5 L fermenter, was used for the large-scale propagation and preparation of the bacteriophage. After the culture was lysed, it was centrifuged at 4 °C, 8000 ×g for 10 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was then mixed with 10% PEG8000 (Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) and 1 M NaCl, left to stand overnight at 4 °C, and centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in SM buffer (200 mmol/L NaCl; 16 mmol/L MgSO₄; 0.1 mol/L Tris-HCl; 0.02% gelatin; pH 7.4–7.6) (BioSharp, Hefei, China). An equal volume of chloroform was added to the suspension for extraction, resulting in the bacteriophage concentrate. This solution was slowly added to a CsCl gradient and centrifuged. The concentrated bacteriophage solution was collected using a syringe and transferred to PBS for dialysis overnight, yielding purified bacteriophage particles. The obtained samples were stained using phosphotungstic acid negative staining, as previous described [22]. After a 15-minute staining process, the samples were observed and recorded using a JEM-1200EX transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at 100 kV.

Determination of MOI and one-step growth curve

The multiplicity of infection (MOI) of the bacteriophage was determined according to the method described by Chen et al. with some modifications [23]. Briefly, the concentration of the bacteriophage was adjusted to 106 PFU/mL. The bacteriophage was then mixed with the host bacteria at ratios of 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 times the biomass, and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Then the phage titer was measured using the double-layer agar plate method. For the determination of the one-step growth curve, 10 mL of Enterococcus faecium culture in the mid-logarithmic growth phase (OD600 ~ 0.7) was infected with the bacteriophage at the optimal MOI and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The culture was centrifuged at 6000 ×g for 5 min, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in 100 mL of MRS broth. Samples were taken every 20 min, and the phage titer was determined using the double-layer agar plate method, with each time point measured in triplicate. The one-step growth curve of the Enterococcus faecium bacteriophage was plotted with the sampling time on the X-axis and the logarithm of the phage titer on the Y-axis.

PH and thermal stability testing

For the pH stability test, SM buffer was adjusted to pH levels ranging from 2 to 12 using NaOH or HCl solutions. 100 µL of the phage stock solution (1.0 × 107 PFU/mL) was added to 900 µL of the adjusted SM buffer and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. After incubation, phage titers were determined using the double-layer agar plate method. For the thermal stability test, 100 µL of the phage stock solution was incubated at 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, and 70 °C in water baths for 60 min. After incubation, phage titers were measured using the double-layer agar plate method.

Determination of the host range

A total of 26 bacterial strains, including 16 strains of Enterococcus faecium, 4 strains of Enterococcus faecalis, 2 strains of Lactobacillus acidophilus, 2 strains of Lactobacillus plantarum, and 2 strains of Pediococcus acidilactici, were tested to determine the lytic host range of the phage isolated in this study. The bacterial strains were inoculated into MRS liquid medium and incubated at 37 °C. The log-phase bacterial strains were then mixed with the phage and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min to allow for infection. After infection, the phage-host mixtures were added to molten MRS agar medium and poured onto solid agar plates, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C to check for the presence of plaques. All bacterial strains were tested at least three times.

DNA extraction, sequencing, and genome annotation

Phage genomic DNA was extracted using the MiniBEST Viral DNA Extraction Kit (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of the DNA was assessed using Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and Nanodrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Genome sequencing was performed on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, USA) using pair-end technology (PE 150). After obtaining high-quality sequencing data, two methods were combined for assembly: (a) Sequence alignment was performed prior to assembly, extracting mapped reads from clean reads that aligned to the viral/plasmid genome sequence, followed by assembly using these mapped reads. (b) Direct assembly of clean reads was conducted, and contigs potentially originating from the viral/plasmid genome were selected from the assembly results. After determining the assembly strategy and the data to be used, SPAdes was employed for subsequent assembly work. During the assembly process, sequence error correction of the input sequences was performed first, followed by assembly using multiple Kmer values, ultimately combining the results from all Kmer values to obtain the best assembly. Open Reading Frames (ORFs) were predicted using Prokka [24] or the NCBI prokaryotic genome annotation pipeline [25]. Proksee [26] software was used to visualize phage genome.

Comparative genome analysis

The viral genomic tree generated by the ViPTree server [27] was calculated by BIONJ based on the genomic distance matrix. Branch lengths are scaled linearly. Sequence and taxonomic data were based on the Virus-Host DB database, with the host type selected as Prokaryote and the virus nucleotide type selected as dsDNA.

The whole phage sequence was submitted to NCBI BLASTN to search for similar sequences. VIRIDIC [28] was used to calculate the similarity between phage genomes with more distant evolutionary relationships and generate the heatmap. Evolutionary analyses of selected phages based on the protein sequence of major capsid protein and terminase large subunit was inferred using the Neighbor-Joining method [29] conducted in MEGA11 [30, 31].

Results

Morphological characteristics of Enterococcus phage XWef1

A bacteriophage specific to Enterococcus faecium was isolated and purified from process waste of a feed additive production facility, using laboratory-preserved E. faecium XW113 as the host strain. Purification via the double-layer agar method yielded isolated plaques characterized by uniform size, circular morphology, clear transparency, and smooth margins, indicating successful monophage isolation. The purified phage was designated Enterococcus phage XWef1. As depicted in Fig. 1A, phage XWef1 produced distinct, transparent, circular plaques on the agar plate. Transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 1B) revealed that Enterococcus phage XWef1 possesses an icosahedral head structure with a diameter of approximately 20 nm and a long tail measuring approximately 150 nm in length. According to the traditional morphological classification guidelines of the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV, 2005), Enterococcus phage XWef1 belongs to the order Caudovirales and the family Siphoviridae. However, as of October 2022, the ICTV’s final decision was that phage morphology should be used solely for descriptive purposes and not directly as a basis for classification. Instead, the ICTV classification system based on genome sequencing analysis has become the most widely adopted method for phage taxonomy [32, 33]. therefore, comprehensive genomic characterization follows in subsequent sections.

Optimal multiplicity of infection and one-step growth curve of Enterococcus phage XWef1

Enterococcus phage XWef1 was cultured with its host bacterium at multiplicities of infection (MOI) of 100, 10, 1, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001, after which the phage titer was determined using the double-layer agar method (Fig. 2A). A significant inverse relationship between initial MOI and final phage yield was observed. At the highest MOI tested (100), the titer reached only 3.0 × 10⁵ PFU/mL. Conversely, at MOI 0.001, the titer increased by three orders of magnitude to 5.3 × 10⁸ PFU/mL. The maximum titer (2.0 × 10⁹ PFU/mL) occurred at MOI 0.01, identifying this as the optimal MOI for XWef1 propagation. Subsequently, a one-step growth curve analysis was performed at MOI 0.01 (Fig. 2B). The latent period spanned approximately 20 min, during which no progeny phage release occurred. This was followed by a 40-minute burst period characterized by rapid virion release. The eclipse period (not explicitly shown) is inferred to occur within the latent phase. The burst size, calculated as the ratio of final phage titer to initial infected cells, was approximately 57 PFU/cell. Total phage production peaked at 100 min post-infection, after which titers stabilized.

PH and temperature stability of Enterococcus phage XWef1

To determine the temperature stability of Enterococcus phage XWef1, the phage was incubated at various temperatures ranging from 20 °C to 70 °C, followed by an assessment of its infectivity. The results indicated that after 1 h of incubation at different temperature gradients, the phage retained high activity at temperatures below 40 °C. However, a decrease in activity was observed at 50 °C, and a significant reduction in activity occurred at 60 °C. At 70 °C, the phage completely lost its activity and infectivity (Fig. 3A).

Biological characteristics of phage XWef1. Stability of phage XWef1 at various (A) temperatures and (B) pH values

Similarly, to assess the pH stability of the phage, it was incubated at different pH levels ranging from 2 to 12, and its titer was subsequently measured. The results showed that after 60 min of incubation at varying pH levels, the optimal pH range for the phage was between 6 and 8. Within this neutral pH range, the phage maintained high activity and infectivity (Fig. 3B). Under acidic conditions, the phage activity significantly decreased after 1 h at pH 4, and at pH 2, the phage activity dropped to a very low level. In alkaline conditions, a reduction in phage activity was observed at pH 10, and at pH 12, the phage lost all activity and infectivity.

Host range of Enterococcus phage XWef1

Our host range investigation done on lab collected strains of Enterococcus faecium (16 strains), Enterococcus faecalis (4 strains), Lactobacillus acidophilus (2 strains), Lactobacillus plantarum (2 strains), Pediococcus acidilactici (2 strains). Phage XWef1 had lytic activity against 18.75% of tested Enterococcus faecium strains (3 strains out of 16), but none of the other genera in our collection (Table 1).

Genome sequencing and annotation

The complete genome of Enterococcus phage XWef1 was sequenced and subjected to comparative genomic analysis. The results revealed that the genome of phage XWef1 is a circular double-stranded DNA molecule (Fig. 4A), with a size of 43,064 bp and a GC content of 34.93% (Fig. 4B). Annotation and visualization analysis identified 63 putative protein-coding genes within the XWef1 genome (Table 2). The coding sequences (CDS) range in length from 93 to 4,317 bp, collectively encompassing 38,847 bp of the genome, resulting in a gene density of approximately 90.21%. No tRNA or rRNA sequences were detected within the genome.

Summary of phage XWef1 and predicted functions

Among the 63 predicted protein-coding genes, several are involved in phage structure formation (e.g., tail protein, tail length tape-measure protein, head-tail joining protein, head-tail connector protein, capsid protein, portal protein), cell lysis (e.g., holin and lysozyme), nucleotide metabolism, and DNA replication (e.g., terminase, primase/polymerase, helicase, DNA methylase, endonuclease), as well as other functional proteins (e.g., domain protein, motif protein, serine/threonine protein kinase). In contrast, 22 ORFs were identified as hypothetical proteins with unknown functions.

Phylogenetic analysis supports the classification of Enterococcus phage XWef1 as a new genus

From the perspective of whole-genome sequence analysis, XWef1 exhibits low sequence similarity with known phages. BLASTn analysis of the XWef1 whole-genome sequence revealed that its closest relatives are Enterococcus phage vB_Efm_LG62, Enterococcus phage dArtagnan, and Enterococcus phage Aramis (Table 3), with similarity scores (query coverage × percentage identity) of only 63.79%, 60.06%, and 60.88%, respectively. These values fall below the threshold of 70% nucleotide similarity across the whole genome, as defined by the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) for distinguishing different genera. Therefore, XWef1 can be defined as a new genus. Interestingly, VIPtree analysis of the XWef1 genome showed that the phage is closely related to three other phages, EfaS-DELF1, IME-EFm5, and IME-EFm1, which are classified within the same branch (Fig. 5). These three phages represent the newly defined genera Delfunavirus, Efemquintavirus, and Efemunavirus, respectively.

Conclusions

The study successfully isolated a novel bacteriophage, XWef1, from waste materials in the probiotic feed additive industry and conducted a comprehensive investigation into its biological characteristics and genome. XWef1 encodes genes containing holin and lysin, enabling it to infect and lyse the Enterococcus faecium strain XW113. This phage forms clear and distinctive plaques on double-layer agar plates, demonstrating strong lytic activity. In terms of biological properties, XWef1 exhibits good stability at temperatures below 40 °C and optimal activity within a neutral pH range [6–8], making it a promising candidate for applications in human and animal health. However, the narrow host range of this phage somewhat limits its potential for widespread use, yet it also highlights its strong host-specific biological characteristics.

Analysis of the XWef1 gene structure revealed that it shares structural proteins similar to other enterococcal phages, while also containing holin and N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidase genes [34]. This enables it to form transmembrane pores in the host cell membrane, hydrolyze the amide bond between N-acetylmuramic acid and L-alanine in peptidoglycan, disrupt cell wall integrity, and ultimately lead to bacterial lysis. Among the 22 predicted hypothetical proteins with unknown functions—though not yet experimentally validated—their genomic locations and neighboring gene functions suggest potential involvement in various biological processes [35]. For example, hypothetical proteins near the structural gene cluster (e.g., tail protein, capsid protein, head-tail connector) may participate in tail assembly or head-tail connection [36]. Those located within DNA replication and modification modules (e.g., primase/polymerase, DNA methylase) might play roles in nucleic acid metabolism or host transcriptional regulation. Some hypothetical proteins with transmembrane domains could be involved in host recognition, adsorption, or membrane penetration [37]. Additionally, the presence of members with lytic activity, host defense suppression, or ecological advantages (moron genes) cannot be ruled out, as reported in other lysogenic or host-restricted phages. Exploring the functions of these hypothetical proteins will not only help uncover XWef1’s infection mechanisms but may also provide novel molecular tools for phage applications.

Genome-based classification methods have replaced traditional morphological approaches for phage classification [38]. In this study, we thoroughly examined the genetic relationships between XWef1 and known bacteriophages. Comparative genomic analysis revealed that XWef1 maintains conserved modules in genomic synteny only with its closest Enterococcus phages, showing significant divergence in the arrangement and composition of structural gene regions, which may correlate with differences in host specificity and infection mechanisms. BLASTn alignment demonstrated that XWef1 shares less than 70% whole-genome similarity with its nearest relatives—vB_Efm_LG62, dArtagnan, and Aramis—meeting the ICTV criteria for new genus delineation [38]. In the ViPTree conserved protein tree analysis (Fig. 5), XWef1 clustered within a major branch alongside members of novel genera such as Delfunavirus, Efemquintavirus, and Efemunavirus but did not group into any established subclades. VIRIDIC heatmap results (Fig. 6) further confirmed that XWef1 exhibits significantly less than 70% genomic similarity with neighboring Enterococcus phages (e.g., Bp29, vb_GEC_Ef_S_9). Subsequent phylogenetic analysis of major capsid protein and large terminase subunit sequences (Fig. 7) demonstrated clear evolutionary divergence between XWef1 and other branch members, supporting its classification as a novel genus. Given that the diversity of Enterococcus faecium phages remains largely unexplored, XWef1 may represent a distant viral lineage, offering valuable insights into their evolutionary history and enhancing our understanding of their diversity and taxonomy.

Notably, while phage XWef1 was isolated from waste material derived from probiotic feed additives, BLASTn analysis demonstrated high genomic similarity with sequences found in the human gut microbiome. This suggests a potential origin within human or animal intestinal environments, consistent with the predominant distribution of its closest known relatives among gut microbial communities. Consequently, the evolutionary trajectory and ecological characteristics of XWef1 are likely intrinsically linked to host interactions within the gut niche. This distinctiveness is reflected not only in its genomic sequence and phylogenetic placement but may also extend to adaptive mechanisms and ecological functions. These findings provide a significant foundation for further research into Enterococcus faecium phage diversity and their potential applications in infection control.

Experimental observations further revealed unique characteristics of XWef1. Specifically, the phage exhibited limited proliferation and failed to achieve efficient host lysis (strain XW113) under shake-flask culture conditions. In contrast, robust phage proliferation and potent lytic activity were consistently observed in a 5-L stirred-tank bioreactor. We postulate that this disparity is attributable to differences in pH regulation. Environmental pH influences the charge state of amino acid residues within the phage capsid, resulting in conformational changes that impair infectivity [39, 40]. In the bioreactor, pH was rigorously maintained at 6.2, whereas fluctuations likely occurred in the shake flasks concomitant with host growth. This pH instability may have adversely affected XWef1’s capacity for proliferation and host cell invasion. Future investigations are warranted to substantiate this pH-dependent model of infectivity.

In conclusion, Enterococcus faecium phage XWef1 exhibits a high degree of uniqueness in its genomic composition, phylogenetic position, and putative protein functions, supporting its classification as a novel genus of bacteriophages. Future research should explore the potential applications of XWef1, particularly in strategies for treating and preventing Enterococcus faecium infections. The discovery of XWef1 not only enriches our understanding of phage diversity but also provides new perspectives for phage research and development, holding broad prospects for microbial control and therapeutic applications.
