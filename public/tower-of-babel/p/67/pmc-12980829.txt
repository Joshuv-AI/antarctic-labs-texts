Heat during Pregnancy and Reduced Fetal Growth: Critical Windows of Exposure and the Intertwined Role of Air Pollution, Vegetation, and Social Stressors
Authors: Lucie Adélaïde, Maximilien Génard-Walton, Ariane Guilbert, Aurélie Nakamura, Vérène Wagner, Morgane Stempfelet, Ian Hough, Guy Launoy, Ludivine Launay, Marie-Aline Charles, Cécile Chevrier, Christine Monfort, Barbara Heude, Muriel Tafflet, Rémy Slama, Sam Bayat, Itai Kloog, Mathilde Pascal, Johanna Lepeule
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increased ambient heat exposure poses a health risk to pregnant women, which may be amplified by environmental and social determinants, but these interactions have been insufficiently characterized. We examined critical windows for the associations between heat exposure during pregnancy and fetal growth and investigated the role of air pollution, vegetation, and social stressors in these associations. Weekly exposure to ambient temperature and air pollutants (PM2.5, NO2, O3) from highly resolved spatiotemporal models, vegetation, and contextual deprivation were estimated for 20,904 French women (2002–2017). Distributed lag nonlinear models evaluated associations between heat and term birth weight (tBW), tBW Z-score, and small-for-gestational-age. We further adjusted our models for air pollutants and stratified on vegetation and social determinants. Heat exposure during the first two trimesters was associated with reduced fetal growth. A mean temperature of 21.6 °C (95th percentile vs median 13.6 °C) during weeks 2–15 was associated with a reduced tBW (−199 g [95% CI: −268; −131]). These associations differed after adjusting for O3 exposure. Trends for stronger associations were observed in women with low vegetation exposure, low social position, and high contextual deprivation. This study highlights how heat stress during early pregnancy could reduce birth weight.

Introduction

As a result of climate changes, an increasing part of the population is exposed to dangerously high temperatures during heat waves and more generally during warm months. During pregnancy, heat exposure can be harmful to the health of women and children. A growing body of studies suggests that high ambient temperatures can increase adverse pregnancy outcomes such as low birth weight (i.e., weight at birth of less than 2,500 g), preterm birth (i.e., born before 37 weeks of amenorrhea), or stillbirth. Studying the influence of heat on fetal growth is particularly important, as low birth weight is a risk-factor of poor perinatal health, neonatal mortality, and lifelong health issues. In 2015, around 15% of newborns worldwide were affected by low birth weight.

Pregnancy induces anatomical and physiological changes, which can disrupt the thermoregulatory capacities of pregnant women and have adverse effects on the health of the mother and the fetus. ,− Heat stress can reduce placental perfusion in favor of increased blood flow to the skin , and can also trigger harmful biological processes, such as oxidative stress, inflammatory response, or hormonal changes, ,,, all of which can compromise fetal growth. Heat exposure is strongly related to other environmental exposures, including air pollution, vegetation, and social stressors (defined as individual or contextual factors of the social environment that can influence health or well-being), − all known factors influencing pregnancy outcomes. ,,− Vegetation can reduce exposure to heat and is associated with numerous health benefits, but air pollution causes many adverse health effects. , Air pollutant concentrations can be increased by heat and sunlight through O3 formation and by drought through the reduction of pollutant uptake by vegetation and the resuspension of soil mineral dust. Moreover, women who suffer the most from individual social inequalities can experience greater exposure to heat (e.g., higher exposure to heat for outdoor workers or reduced thermal comfort of the housing) and/or greater effects of such exposure (e.g., more pre-existing comorbidities). , Simultaneous exposure to air pollution, vegetation, and social stressors can exacerbate physiological susceptibility to heat, decrease individual adaptive capacity, and thus influence the relationship between heat and fetal growth. − Many studies have documented the effect of a single environmental factor (e.g., temperature, air pollution, vegetation) on birth weight, often controlling only for one additional environmental or socioeconomic factor or not considering any. ,,,,− However, the effect of heat, taking into account the role of all these ambient coexposures to air pollution, vegetation, and social stressors on birth weight, has not yet been investigated.

In addition, understanding the influence of the timing of exposure during pregnancy is critical to better understand the pathways involved and develop targeted public health interventions. However, despite notable advances from recent studies that used distributed lag models to identify windows of vulnerability, the results remain inconsistent, − particularly as heat exposure and population susceptibility vary across different regions. Given that Europe is the continent experiencing the fastest warming in the world, a clearer comprehension of the effects of high ambient temperatures and the different pathways from the exposure to the outcome is needed to identify and support action levers for adaptation and prevention tailored to pregnant women.

Our study aimed (i) to identify critical windows of exposure to heat associated with fetal growth and (ii) to study the role of air pollution, vegetation, and social stressors in these associations. We hypothesized that the effect of heat on birth weight could partly be due to air pollutants concentrations, could be attenuated by vegetation exposure, and increased with higher levels of air pollutants and social deprivation.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We analyzed data from 24,236 participants from four French mother-child cohorts. PELAGIE (Perturbateurs endocriniens: Étude Longitudinale sur les Anomalies de la Grossesse, l′Infertilité, et l′Enfance) enrolled 3,421 women between 2002 and 2006, before 19 weeks of gestational age (GA) in the Brittany region. EDEN (Étude des Déterminants pré et postnatals du développement psychomoteur et de la santé de l′ENfant) enrolled 2,002 women between 2003 and 2006, before 24 weeks GA in the metropolitan areas of Poitiers and Nancy. SEPAGES (Suivi de l′Exposition à la Pollution Atmosphérique durant la Grossesse et Effets sur la Santé) enrolled 484 pregnant women between 2014 and 2017, before 19 weeks GA in the metropolitan area of Grenoble. ELFE (Etude Longitudinale Française depuis l′Enfance) recruited 18,329 infants born in 2011 in 320 maternity wards across continental France.

Medical and sociodemographic data were collected across all cohorts via questionnaires and clinical examinations during and after pregnancy, along with participants’ home addresses (including any changes) which were geocoded. Data from the four cohorts were harmonized and pooled, preserving as many details as possible.

Participants with multiple gestation, nonlive births, or preexisting diabetes and participants lost to follow-up before delivery were excluded. We further excluded preterm births (birth before 37 weeks GA) and participants with missing information in home address history or exposure data (during 1 week of pregnancy or more for temperature and air pollution). In total, 20,904 pregnant women were included in this study (Figure ).

Flowchart of the study population.

Outcome Definition

Reduced fetal growth was studied using term birth weight (tBW), tBW Z-score, and term small-for-gestational age birth (tSGA) outcomes. The tBW Z-scores accounted for gestational age at delivery, newborn sex, and maternal characteristics (weight, height, parity) using the French growth reference chart. tSGA births were defined as weights below the 10th percentile of the French fetal growth reference charts after 37 weeks GA.

Environmental Exposures

Temperature

Weekly averaged minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures were calculated from conception onward, based on daily data estimated at each participant’s home address using a validated multiresolution model. This model has a 200 m spatial resolution for large urban areas (>50,000 inhabitants) and a 1 km spatial resolution across France. It uses a multistage ensemble approach and calibrates meteorological station temperatures with satellite-derived land surface temperature, elevation, and other spatiotemporal predictors.

Air Pollution

Weekly averaged exposure to PM2.5, NO2, and maximum O3 were calculated based on daily data estimated at the participant’s home address using validated models. Daily mean PM2.5 concentrations were estimated using a 1 km resolution multistage ensemble model, while daily mean NO2 and maximum O3 concentrations were estimated from a chemistry transport model (spatial resolution ∼4 km) produced by the French Institute for Industrial Environment and Risks (Ineris).

Vegetation

Vegetation exposure was assessed using Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and a binary indicator of green space exposure based on World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations (presence/absence of a green space of 0.5 ha or more in a 300 m buffer around the home address). The NDVI is a measure of vegetation density ranging from −1 to 1 (higher values indicating denser vegetation). It was calculated as the mean summer NDVI (June 1 to August 31 each year) of the year of pregnancy using Landsat satellite imagery within a 300 m buffer around each home address by Hough et al., providing a reasonable representation of green space in the immediate living environment (5 min walk). NDVI values were marked as missing if satellite data covered less than 50% of the buffer area. The indicator of green space exposure was computed by the National Institute of Geographic and Forest Information (IGN) for hexagonal France based on vegetation cover data (closed and open forests, poplar plantations, hedges or rows of trees, heathland and woodland) from the BD TOPO 2008–2009 (for PELAGIE, EDEN, and ELFE) and 2018 (for SEPAGES).

Social Stressors

Individual Social Position

Individual social position variables, selected based on the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health (CSDH) framework and the availability of data across the four cohorts, included maternal education level (>Baccalaureate +2 years/Baccalaureate +2 years/Baccalaureate or less/No education or primary education), maternal continent of birth (Africa, Americas, Asia, Europe), maternal and paternal socio-professional categories (Agricultural workers/Craft and retail workers and business directors/Employees/Intermediate professions/Laborers/Managers and higher intellectual professions/No profession), working during pregnancy (Yes/No), and relationship status (Lives alone/Married or in a couple). Multiple correspondence analysis (MCA) was applied to these variables. We used the first two components (retained using the “elbow” method and explaining 20% of the total variance) for k-means clustering to construct three social position profiles (lower, intermediate, or higher social position) (Supplementary Figure 1).

Contextual Deprivation

Contextual deprivation was assessed using the European Deprivation Index (EDI 2011) available at the census district of residence level for each participant. The EDI is a composite measure based on a combination of census data created to characterize the socioeconomic environment in a transcultural manner across European countries. The French version combines ten census variables grouped into a material dimension (nonhomeowners, households without a car, households with more than two occupants, overcrowded dwellings with more than one person per room) and a social dimension (unemployment, foreigners, unskilled workers, low education level, single-parent households, unmarried status). Higher EDI indicates greater social deprivation.

Statistical Analysis

Imputation

Missing data for covariates (<1.3%) were imputed using missforest, a tree-based imputation method that predicts and replaces missing values iteratively until no further improvement is observed in the imputed matrix, using the following environmental exposures, contextual variables, health and social stressors variables as predictors: gestational age, season of conception, year of conception, maternal age, education level, relationship status, continent of birth, working status during pregnancy, maternal and paternal socioprofessional category, parity, sex, birth weight, tobacco consumption, alcohol consumption, high blood pressure, body mass index, center, urbanization, mean pregnancy temperature exposure, mean pregnancy PM2.5 concentrations exposure, mean pregnancy NO2 concentrations exposure, mean pregnancy O3 concentrations exposure, relative NDVI, indicator of green spaces, and EDI.

Identification of Critical Windows of Exposure

Associations of term birth weight (tBW) (models 1, Supplementary Table 1) with weekly averaged minimum, mean, and maximum temperatures (Tmin, Tmean, and Tmax, respectively) were studied separately using distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNMs) to account for the time-varying, nonlinear, and delayed effects of the exposures, as well as correlations between exposure windows. Weekly heat exposure was studied from conception to 37 weeks GA (i.e., 35 weeks of pregnancy since conception). The exposure-response and the lag-response relationships were modeled using natural cubic splines. Combinations of degrees of freedom ranging from 2 to 5 for these two relationships were tested. The number of degrees of freedom was set to 4 for the exposure-response relationship and 5 for the lag-response relationship. They were chosen on the basis of the Akaike criterion (AIC), the visual inspection of the curves, and parsimony. Changes in tBW (β and 95% confidence interval (CI)) were estimated for moderate (90th percentile), severe (95th percentile), and extreme (99th percentile) heat exposure relative to median temperature (reference temperature).

The models were adjusted for potential confounders of the relationship between heat exposure and tBW and predictors of the outcome, identified as biologically and epidemiologically relevant and selected a priori using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Supplementary Figure 2). Adjustment variables included: cohort, season (winter/spring/summer/fall) and year of conception, maternal birth continent, NDVI (terciles), green space exposure (yes/no), individual social position (lower/intermediate/higher), EDI (terciles), urbanization level (dense/intermediate/rural, obtained from the French National Institute of Statistics and Economics (INSEE)), newborn sex, maternal age at conception (≤20/>20 and ≤25/>25 and ≤30/>30 and ≤35/>35 and ≤40/>40), prepregnancy weight (≤65 kg/>65 kg), height (continuous), parity (0/1/2/3+), and smoking during pregnancy (yes/no). Pregnancy complications such as hypertension were assumed to be on the causal pathway between temperature and birth weight and were thus not adjusted for.

The same approach was used for tBW Z-score (models 2) and for tSGA (models 3). For tBW Z-score, the number of degrees of freedom was set to 5 for the exposure-response relationship and to 2 for the lag-response relationship (models 2). For tSGA, the number of degrees of freedom was set to 2 for the exposure-response relationship and to 3 for the lag-response relationship (models 3). The models 2 and 3 were not adjusted for newborn sex, maternal age, weight, height, and parity as they are taken into account in the French growth reference chart. Results are presented as average changes in tBW Z-score (β and 95% CI) (models 2) and as relative risks (RR and 95% CI) of tSGA (models 3).

Role of Air Pollution

Air pollution exposure could be on the causal pathway between temperature and fetal growth. Therefore, to assess whether the association between heat exposure during pregnancy and reduced fetal growth might be due to air pollutant concentrations, model 1 for Tmean was adjusted for weekly exposure to PM2.5, NO2, and O3 (models 4) in order to estimate the direct effect of heat. For these pollutants, the exposure-response relationship was modeled using a linear relationship and the lag-response relationship (from conception to 37 weeks GA), using natural cubic splines. The number of degrees of freedom for the lag-response was determined using the same approach as for temperature and set to 4. Considering the high collinearity between ambient temperature and O3, multicollinearity was assessed by decomposing the model week by week and converting exposure into deciles to account for nonlinearity of the dose–response.

Modifying Role of Vegetation and Social Stressors

To assess whether vegetation exposure reduced heat effects on birth weight, we stratified model 1 (for Tmean) by terciles of NDVI (1st and 3rd tercile only, corresponding to low and high exposure) (models 5) and by the binary indicator of green space exposure (Yes/No) (models 6). To evaluate whether social stressors exacerbate the effects of heat exposure on tBW, model 1 (for Tmean) was stratified by social position profiles (low social position and high social position only) (models 7) and by terciles of EDI (1st and 3rd tercile, corresponding to low and high deprivation) (models 8). Due to DLNM methodological limitations, we could not test interactions between heat exposure and these variables, but instead, we statistically tested the differences in estimates and confidence intervals between each stratum for each lag, as recommended in Schenker and Gentleman. Nonetheless, this approach remains exploratory in the absence of formal interaction testing, and results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Sensitivity Analysis

We performed three sensitivity analyses using model 1 for Tmean. The first one explored whether part of the effect of heat on tBW could be explained by a reduction in gestational age by adjusting for it (model 9). The next two models explored the modifying effect of newborn sex (model 10), given that the association may differ by sex, and urbanization (model 11) on the associations between Tmean and tBW.

All models, 1 to 10, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Analyses were performed using R software, version 4.1.2, and the dlnm package.

Identification of Critical Windows of Exposure

Heat exposure (90th, 95th, and 99th percentiles vs median) was associated with reduced or increased tBW, depending on the temperature indicator (weekly Tmean, Tmin, and Tmax) and the exposure window considered (models 1 to 3) (Figure , Supplementary Figures 5 and 6 and Supplementary Table 4).

Change in term birth weight in grams (A), in Z-score (B), and relative risks for term small for gestational age (C) (lines) and 95% CI (errors bars and shaded area) associated with heat exposure (Tmean) during the first 35 weeks after conception (model 1 for tBW, model 2 for tBW Z-score, and model 3 for tSGA), and associated cumulative dose–response effects (D). Moderate heat: 90th percentile; severe heat: 95th percentile; extreme heat: 99th percentile. The reference is the median temperature. Models were adjusted for cohort, urbanization, NDVI, green space, season and year of conception, age (mother), social position, smoking status, weight (mother) (except for models 2 and 3), height (mother) (except for models 2 and 3), sex (except for models 2 and 3), parity (except for models 2 and 3), EDI, and continent of birth.

Based on Tmean, severe heat exposure (95th percentile vs median) during weeks 2 to 15 (i.e., first trimester to early second trimester) and weeks 21 to 26 (i.e., late second trimester) was associated with a cumulated reduction in tBW of −199.4 g [−268.0; −130.8] and −40.7 g [−69.4; −12.0], respectively. The same exposure during weeks 32 to 35 (3rd trimester) was associated with an increase in tBW of 56.5 g [21.1; 92.0]. The results for Tmax were similar with a more pronounced effect size. Associations with Tmin followed a different pattern with, for example, severe heat exposure during weeks 1 to 6 and weeks 24 to 28 being associated with an increase in tBW (Supplementary Figure 5 and Supplementary Table 4).

Unlike tBW, the shapes of the tBW Z-score and tSGA curves were consistent across Tmean (Figure ), Tmin, and Tmax (Supplementary Figure 5). Overall, we observed similar trends between tBW and tBW Z-score for Tmean and Tmax marked by a reduction in tBW Z-score for an exposure to heat in early pregnancy and an increase for an exposure in late pregnancy (Figure , Supplementary Figures 5 and 6, Supplementary Table 5).

Results for tSGA were mostly nonsignificant. However, the observed trends were consistent with the results for tBW and tBW Z-scores, except for a nonsignificant decreased risk observed for an exposure to heat at the beginning of pregnancy (Figure , Supplementary Figures 5 and 6, Supplementary Table 6).

Cumulative dose–response curves (Figure D and Supplemental Figure 5) between heat exposure and all three outcomes show U (SGA) or inverted U curves (tBW and tBW Z-score), indicating consistent reductions in fetal growth for both cold and heat exposures compared to the median temperature.

Role of Air Pollution

The associations between tBW and exposure to severe heat (95th percentile vs median) using Tmean were similar after adjustment for PM2.5 or NO2 (model 4) (Figure , Supplementary Figure 7 and Supplementary Table 7). The associations were different after adjustment for O3 (direct effect of heat). However, if we exclude the extremes of the curves (beginning and end of the pregnancy with larger confidence intervals), the overall directions of the associations were similar between total and direct effect except in the middle of the pregnancy when exposure to heat, adjusted for O3, was associated with an increased tBW. In addition, we did not detect evidence of multicollinearity between ambient temperature, O3, and covariates (data not shown).

Modifying Role of Vegetation, Individual Social Position, and EDI

Overall, heat exposure for women with low NDVI showed a greater reduction in tBW (models 5 and 6, Figure , Supplementary Table 7, and Supplementary Figure 7), with a longer critical window of exposure to heat, compared to women with a high NDVI, especially for an exposure to heat during the first and second trimesters. For example, severe heat exposure during weeks 5 to 7 (1st trimester) was associated with a tBW reduction of −72.5 g [−111.2; −33.8] for women with low NDVI vs −43.4 g [−81.5; −5.4] for women with high NDVI. This result is supported by the cumulative effects observed for the two levels of NDVI exposure (Figure B). This difference seems to be reversed in the third trimester. Nonetheless, we did not find a statistically significant effect modification for any of the weeks (Supplementary Table 8). As for the green space binary indicator, a greater reduction in tBW was suggested for women with a green space of more than 0.5 ha within 300 m of their home, compared with women with none, for an exposure to heat during the first and second trimesters (Supplementary Figure 8). Effect modification by green space was significant for weeks 15 to 20. However, this trend was not supported by the cumulative effect of heat estimated for each category of the green space indicator.

For social position, a greater reduction in tBW was suggested for women with a lower social position for an exposure to heat during the first trimester, compared with subjects with a higher social position (Figure , Supplementary Figure 7, and Supplementary Table 7). This result is supported by the cumulative effects observed for the two levels of social position. This association is no longer observed during the third trimester of pregnancy, and no significant effect modification was found for any of the lags.

Regarding EDI, from the first trimester to the beginning of the second trimester (weeks 5 to 18), exposure to severe heat (Tmean, 95th percentile vs median) was associated with a reduction in tBW of −360.3 g [−491.7; −228.8] for women with a high deprivation (models 8) (Figure , Supplementary Figure 7, and Supplementary Table 7). This reduction was less significant with an earlier and shorter critical window for women with a low deprivation index (weeks 2 to 7: −131.3 g [−222.7; −39.9]). This result is supported by the cumulative effects observed for the two levels of EDI (Figure F). Between the end of the second trimester and the beginning of the third trimester, the curves suggested a greater and more significant reduction in tBW in subjects with a low deprivation. Significant effect modification between EDI levels was found for weeks 10–17 and 24–30.

Sensitivity Analysis

Overall, the shapes of the relationship between tBW and exposure to severe heat (Tmean, 95th percentile vs median) were similar before and after adjustment for gestational age (model 9) (Supplementary Figure 9 and Supplementary Table 8). However, after adjustment for gestational age, the curves suggested a smaller effect size and almost all critical windows were no longer significant. This suggests that part of the effect of temperature on tBW could be explained by a reduction of gestation length.

The associations between tBW and exposure to severe heat (95th percentile vs median), using Tmean, looked quite similar according to sex (model 10) and urbanization (dense vs rural) (model 11) (Supplementary Figure 9 and Supplementary Table 8). The curves suggested a slightly stronger effect of heat in female newborns (for an exposure during early pregnancy) and for women living in rural areas (for an exposure around the end of the second trimester to the beginning of the third trimester).

Discussion

Summary of Findings

Our results highlight critical windows of exposure to moderate, severe, and extreme heat throughout pregnancy regarding fetal growth. Overall, heat exposure during the first two trimesters of pregnancy was associated with reduced fetal growth. Exposure to heat toward the end of pregnancy was associated with increased fetal growth. The associations remained significant toward the beginning of pregnancy when adjusting for gestational age. Results remained consistent after adjusting for PM2.5 and NO2, but critical exposure windows to heat were modified after adjusting for O3. We observed some trends for more pronounced associations of heat with reduced fetal growth at birth for women with a low social position, a high deprivation level, or low NDVI for exposure to heat in early pregnancy. To our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze the association between heat exposure and fetal growth, while identifying the effect of coexposures such as air pollution, vegetation, and individual social position as well as contextual deprivation on this association.

Comparison with the Literature

Comparing our results with previous studies on temperature and birth weight is limited by differences in heat exposure definitions (percentiles, reference temperature) and climatic contexts.

An earlier study on the EDEN and PELAGIE cohorts found a significant association of tBW with temperature variability but did not find any significant association with mean temperature. In the current study, we added the SEPAGES cohort and the national ELFE cohort in our analysis. The ELFE cohort, representing three-quarters of our population, included newborns in 2011, a year without exceptional high temperatures, compared to EDEN and PELAGIE, thus potential reducing associations in our study. Moreover, we used a spatially refined (200 m) exposure model to temperature compared to Jakpor et al. (1 km). Another French study conducted in the Paris region examined the association between pregnancy exposure to the August 2003 heat wave and birth weight in 3,359 women. The critical heat exposure windows identified in our analysis align with those identified in this study, which found significant reductions in tBW associated with exposure to the particularly fatal 2003 heat wave (a year present in EDEN and PELAGIE) during the first (−90 g [−131; −35]) and second trimesters (−126 g [−200; −53]) but not during the third trimester of pregnancy. Consistently, exposure to the 2003 heat wave was associated with an increased risk of 2.70 [1.38; 5.28] of tSGA throughout pregnancy and of 4.18 [1.69; 10.35] when the 2003 heat wave occurred during the first trimester of pregnancy.

In Cyprus, an analysis of 75,207 births (2007–2020) found a decrease in tBW with increased mean temperatures during weeks 1–8 (−57.27 g [−99.62; −14.92] for a 5 °C increase), consistent with our results. Similar associations were observed in weeks 28–37 (−79.20 g [−117.03; −41.52]). A study in Israel on 630,000 full-term births identified critical windows for heat exposure (90th percentile of mean temperature) during weeks 3–9 and 19–35 with the largest effects on tBW observed for exposure in week 3 (−3.8 g [−7. 1; −0.4]) and weeks 26–27 (−2.6 g [−4.6; −0.5] for week 26), respectively. A Massachusetts study on 700,000 births reported a 7.9 g [−11.2; −4.6] reduction in tBW for an increase from 23 to 24 °C during various gestational weeks. In contrast, studies from European cohorts (England, Netherlands, Spain) and Boston, USA, found no association of mean temperature exposure during pregnancy with birth weight or with small for gestational age at birth. Using more than 2 million birth records (1999–2013) from California, a study found lower odds of having a low birth weight in the last 2 weeks before delivery, in line with our positive results regarding the effect of heat exposure on tBW in weeks 32–35 of pregnancy.

Overall, our results align well with previous findings of the literature and provide new insights into the role of coexposures to heat.

Discussion of the Results

Change in birth weight associated with exposure to heat may be indicative of nonoptimal fetal development with clinical consequences for the newborn. The first trimester of pregnancy is crucial for the development of the placenta, while the later stages of pregnancy are marked by the most rapid somatic growth of the fetus. We identified critical windows of heat exposure for these same periods (particularly during the first trimester of pregnancy), which suggest an increased effect of heat on the fetus at these key developmental periods.

The difference in results between the total effect of heat exposure and its direct effect when adjusting for O3 suggests a partial effect of O3 on the association between severe heat exposure and tBW. However, these results need to be interpreted cautiously due to the positive correlation between heat and O3 and the absence of formal methods to investigate mediation effects in DLNM analysis. Indeed, strong correlations between temperature and O3 (Supplementary Figure 4) may increase the variance of regression coefficients, making them unstable and difficult to interpret, and therefore limit the ability to isolate the direct effect of O3.

The results also suggested trends indicating that the association between heat exposure and tBW may vary depending on vegetation indicators, with women living in areas with low NDVI having a greater reduction in tBW, particularly with heat exposure during the first and second trimesters, compared to those in high NDVI areas, a trend that seemed to reverse in the third trimester. Conversely, when using the green space binary indicator, women with a green space larger than 0.5 ha within 300 m of their home showed a significantly greater reduction in tBW following heat exposure in early pregnancy, although this was not observed with cumulative effects curves. Both indicators used in our study may primarily reflect the vegetation’s role in mitigating heat, rather than mechanisms related to capacity restoration (e.g., stress reduction, cognitive enhancement) or strengthening (e.g., physical activity, social bonding). Moreover, associations between tBW and exposure to severe heat according to the green space indicator should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of women without access to a 0.5 ha green space within 300 m of their home (3,314 women, 16% of the population).

We observed a trend suggesting a greater reduction in tBW associated with heat exposure during the first trimester for individuals with lower social position compared to those with higher social position as well as a significant reduction in tBW in the first trimester for those living in highly deprived areas (high EDI) compared to those living in low deprivation contexts. A more detailed characterization of social position, including heat-exposure risk factors (e.g., air conditioning, outdoor work), may help clarify these findings.

In our results, the shape of the relationship between tBW and heat exposure were similar for Tmean and Tmax, while those for Tmin differed. This could be due to a less accurate estimate of real exposure when using Tmin, which mainly represents nocturnal exposure (when the individuals are mainly indoors), compared with Tmean and Tmax (which mainly represents daytime exposure).

Our results further suggested that part of the effect of heat on tBW could be explained by a reduction in gestational age. These findings are supported by several studies that have reported an increased risk of preterm births associated with heat exposure during pregnancy.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study has several strengths and limitations. First, pooling data from four cohorts, collected at different times and regions in France, enabled us to carry out a national study with increased sample size. However, given the extended study period, it could be relevant to examine the temporal variability of the dose–response relationship relative to temperature time trends and to the implementation of preventive measures. For example, women from the PELAGIE, EDEN, and SEPAGES cohorts experienced particularly high temperatures due to exceptionally hot years (e.g., 2003 and 2015). These exceptional temperature levels were not observed in 2011, during ELFE’s observation period. In addition, the level of awareness among pregnant women regarding heat may have evolved due to the implementation of the French heatwave plan in 2004 and by the increasing knowledge on the topic among health professionals. Testing such a hypothesis would require the implementation of cohort-specific models. However, the small number of participants in some cohorts could limit the power of such analyses. Pooling the data might also have led to a loss of precision in certain covariables, such as using continent of birth instead of a more detailed country-based classification, and a lack of information on key social factors such as income and housing quality, which were unavailable for some cohorts. These limitations could have hindered the construction of more detailed social position profiles. Additionally, the most vulnerable populations (e.g., marginalized populations) are underrepresented due to recruitment challenges and attrition. This is particularly the case in the SEPAGES cohort, where 78% of women were managers and 82% had education levels above two years of higher education.

Second, while our analyses used high-resolution spatiotemporal models for temperature, air pollution, and NDVI, we estimated exposures based on residential addresses. It is likely that women spent significant time indoors or in other locations, such as workplaces (particularly during early pregnancy) since 78% of women were working during their pregnancy. This could lead to nondifferential misclassification, reducing statistical power. Note that the last weeks of pregnancy (around 6 weeks) constitute maternity leave in France and women stay mainly at home.

Third, concentrations prior to 2008–2009 for PM2.5 may be underestimated due to air quality monitoring stations not taking the semivolatile fraction into account. , As a result, the PM2.5 exposure of women in PELAGIE and EDEN may be underestimated and could introduce classification bias in the model with adjustment for PM2.5 (model 4).

Fourth, we considered gestational age and maternal characteristics to assess growth anomalies. However, the use of a single French growth reference chart for all cohorts has several limitations. The distributions of birth weight and related factors (e.g., maternal height and weight) may have slightly shifted over time, potentially introducing nondifferential classification bias. Variations in exposure (e.g., reduction in air pollution) over time could influence the distribution of parameters in the reference population, leading to an underestimation of tSGA and potential classification bias. Additionally, the updated calculation methods for the charts offer a more accurate estimate of tSGA compared to older versions.

Fifth, the results highlighted windows of heat exposure associated with an increase in tBW in the later weeks of pregnancy (after 32 weeks of pregnancy), which is consistent with an aforementioned study from Basu et al. This apparent protective effect could occur because it only concerns fetuses who have survived thus far and are past the preterm birth threshold. However, it would be relevant to conduct studies on the effect of heat on macrosomia and large gestational age weights, as the study of these events remains limited in the literature.

Sixth, we applied 2021 urbanization data to all the cohorts studied. This may have led to the misclassification of some women when analyzing the influence of urbanization, particularly those living in peri-urban areas.

Seventh, the use of DLNM helped capture exposure variations over time, delayed effects, and nonlinear relationships and identify critical windows of sensitivity. However, exposures after the 35th week were not considered to ensure comparability of exposure windows between women, limiting the analysis of late-pregnancy exposure effects. Additionally, the focus on term births excluded potential critical windows for premature newborns. Future studies focusing on preterm births or prolonged pregnancies (41 weeks GA) would be relevant to identify potential critical exposure windows in these specific populations. However, such analyses require a sufficiently large study population to ensure adequate statistical power.

Eighth, to the best of our knowledge, there are currently no established methods to formally incorporate mediation or interaction terms within the DLNM in a way that allows rigorous interpretation. Instead, we provided a test for effect modification lag by lag with significant differences emerging for green space and high levels of EDI effect modification at different stages of pregnancy. However, given these methodological limitations, our analyses exploring the role of coexposures should be considered exploratory, and further studies using complementary approaches are needed to confirm these preliminary findings and the suggested hypotheses.

Nineth, in DLNMs, the estimated exposure–response association is characterized by a single smooth function across the entire lag dimension. Because there is strong temporal correlation between lagged exposures corrected by this smooth function, the statistical tests corresponding to individual lags are not independent. Moreover, the objective of exploring several exposure indicators or percentile cutoffs is not to test different hypotheses but to assess the coherence and robustness of associations across alternative exposure definitions and to identify the indicator most strongly associated with the outcome. For these reasons, we chose to report uncorrected p-values while interpreting the results and critical windows of exposure in light of overall consistency and biological plausibility rather than isolated significance thresholds.

Tenth, although missForest uses an iterative random-forest procedure that repeatedly updates predictions until no further improvement is observed in the imputed matrix, it represents a single imputation approach, which may underestimate uncertainty relative to multiple imputation methods. However, given the low proportion of missing data (<1.3%), any resulting impact on inference is expected to be small. In addition, the analyses were not adjusted for factors such as housing quality (insulation, floor, thermal comfort, etc.), protective behaviors against heat or working conditions (air conditioning use, outdoor work, flexible working hours, etc.), or outcome predictors (e.g., diet), potentially leading to nondifferential classification error and thus to decreased associations. Moreover, they did not account for humidity or other meteorological indicators. While humidity can physiologically exacerbate heat stress, integrating it into analyses is challenging due to its weaker influence at high temperatures or failure to account for temperature–humidity interactions. Methods for integrating humidity into exposure metrics remain heterogeneous and may introduce interpretation bias, and some studies have found no differences on the main effect of temperature when adding specific or relative humidity in their models. Note that heat stress indices that consider other meteorological parameters (e.g., humidity, wind speed, radiant temperature, etc.), not always available in our data sets, are also used as indicators of the thermal environment to assess thermal comfort. Further studies should examine the possibility and relevance of creating such indices to characterize heat exposure in pregnant women.

Eleventh, contextual variables of social deprivation are often used as proxies for individual social position in environmental epidemiology. In our analysis, we hypothesized that the mechanisms of heat exposure risk differ at individual and contextual levels. Thus, we examined both census district-level social deprivation and individual social position. While other contextual indicators exist, we used the EDI due to its validated robustness. However, developing a specific index for perinatal and maternal health or incorporating additional relevant contextual variables would also be relevant for future studies.

Lastly, we did not include temperature acclimatization, which could capture the abnormality of exposure based on climate, region, and time of year. Moreover, although supplementary analyses stratified by level of urbanization were conducted to explore potential differences between urban and rural areas, our findings remain specific to the French context and are not generalizable to populations living in warmer climates or under different environmental conditions.

To conclude, our study found that heat exposure during the two first trimesters of pregnancy was associated with reduced fetal growth, which might partly be due to reduced gestational duration. Heat exposure during the third trimester was associated with increased fetal growth. O3 concentrations influenced the association in both directions depending on pregnancy weeks. Trends for stronger associations in women with low vegetation exposure, low social position, and high contextual deprivation were observed. In light of increasing heat exposure due to climate change, these findings underscore the need for targeted measures to protect pregnant women since the first day of pregnancy.
