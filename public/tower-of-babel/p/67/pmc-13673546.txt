Flexible ultrafast spintronic terahertz emitter with exceptional working temperature range
Authors: Piming Gong, Faran Zhou, Liguang Gong, Yu Cheng, Litong Jiang, Peiyan Li, Jian Song, Guoqing Chang, Jing Teng, Xiaojun Wu, Zhiming Wang, Jimin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpintronic terahertz (THz) emitters (TEs) leverage the spin degree of freedom. To address the growing demand for flexible TEs, we demonstrate a soft spintronic ultrafast TE based on a NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3/mica(substrate) heterostructure. It emits picosecond 0–4 THz waves under near-infrared laser excitation, driven by the inverse spin Hall effect. Bendable samples with largely tunable curvatures (−2 cm−1≤ κ ≤ 2 cm−1) are achieved, by which a maximum 82% enhancement in the THz field strength is observed. We attribute it to tensile strain-induced large spin-orbit torque in SrRuO3. Significantly, we demonstrate an 80–500 K working temperature range for this all-inorganic flexible device, enabling applications under unprecedented extreme conditions (e.g., on the Moon’s surface or at the poles). Our work ushers in the dawn of extreme-temperature-environment, all-inorganic, wearable, and broadband ultrafast THz devices, which uncovers an unexplored area for the application of ultrafast THz photonics.Keywords: flexible terahertz device, wide working temperature range, ultrafast terahertz generation, 4d transition metal oxide, ultrafast terahertz spin current

Introduction

Ultrafast light pulse excitation is powerful and critical for generating nonequilibrium states.1,2,3 Ultrafast spectroscopy is a powerful means of controlling such nonequilibrium states and studying the couplings among multiple degrees of freedom4,5,6,7,8,9,10 in quantum materials. In particular, materials with large spin-orbit coupling have been harnessed to generate ultrafast terahertz (THz) emission, particularly in the design of spintronic THz emitters (TEs). There have been many types of spintronic TEs, such as ferromagnet/heavy-metal,4,11,12,13,14 antiferromagnet/heavy-metal,15,16 ferromagnet/topological insulator,17,18 antiferromagnet/topological insulator,19 and antiferromagnet/ferromagnet20,21 heterostructures.

However, to date, most of the TEs reported are non-flexible. Applications of THz waves, such as wearable and soft THz devices, curved-surface THz imaging systems, extreme-condition THz systems, etc., highly demand flexible THz sources with comprehensive high performance. Flexible TEs can withstand bending without breaking, making them adaptable to dynamic complex environments. Moreover, bending and strain in flexible TEs also endow tunable freedom to regulate the THz waves. So far, flexible TEs are rarely reported,13,14,22,23 which are very few and are all fabricated on organic substrates. However, organic substrates cannot sustain high or low temperatures, limiting their applications in extreme-temperature environments. Consequently, flexible spintronic TEs based on inorganic materials have been elusive for decades and are in high demand for next-generation THz devices. As is known, inorganic materials are usually non-flexible, which makes them challenging to realize.

From a materials perspective, materials with large spin-orbit torque (SOT) are essential for spintronic THz radiation. For example, as one of the key mechanisms for THz generation, the inverse spin Hall effect (ISHE) is based on SOT. The 4d materials usually exhibit strong SOT.24,25,26,27,28,29,30 However, to date, there have been few reports of TEs based on 4d materials. Here, we report the first TE made of a 4d transition metal oxide (TMO), a NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3/mica heterostructure that unambiguously exhibits prominent TEs.

In this work, we demonstrate the realization of an inorganic flexible ultrafast spintronic TE. Significantly, this all-inorganic material design enables THz radiation over a wide working temperature range, from 80 to 500 K. Such a wide working temperature range has rarely been explored for TEs and is crucial for devices functioning under extreme temperature environments (e.g., those in factories, on the Moon, or in the polar regions). We show that the soft TE with convex bending exhibits prominently enhanced THz emission (82%). This is because we have designed the TE by, for the first time, employing a 4d TMO layer. Our work enables flexible TEs, especially with an unprecedented working temperature range (80–500 K).

Materials and methods

Sample fabrication

The molecular formula of our sample is Ni80Fe20/SrRuO3/SrTiO3/KMg3(AlSi3O10)F2. First, a 15-nm-thick (001)-oriented SrTiO3 single-crystal film is epitaxially grown on a 1 × 1 cm mica substrate at 800°C under an oxygen partial pressure of 0.1 mbar. Using a KrF excimer laser with a wavelength of 248 nm, a 20-nm-thick (100)-oriented single-crystal SrRuO3 film is deposited on the SrTiO3 film by pulsed laser deposition with a laser energy density of 2.45 J/cm2. After SrRuO3 growth, the film is cooled to room temperature under 1 mbar oxygen pressure with a rate of 10°C/min. Subsequently, a 7-nm-thick Ni80Fe20 layer is deposited in situ on SrRuO3 under high vacuum conditions (2.2 × 10−7 mbar).

THz emission spectroscopy

The THz emission setup is schematically shown in Figure S2. A Ti:sapphire oscillator (with a 800 nm central wavelength, 100 fs pulse duration, and 80 MHz repetition rate) is employed in our THz emission spectroscopy experiment. The ultrafast 800 nm femtosecond laser beam is split into two branches. One is used to pump the sample to generate THz radiation. The other is directed into the THz detector as the detection beam. The THz detector is a low-temperature-grown GaAs photoconductive antenna. The pump laser power varies from 10 to 90 mW. We use a λ/2 waveplate to control the polarization of the pump light pulse. The emitted THz pulse is focused by two parabolic gold mirrors before being collected by the THz receiver. The laser power of the THz detection beam is 23 mW. To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, we use a chopper with 1,600 Hz to chop the pump light pulse, and the modulated signal is sampled by a lock-in amplifier. The whole THz beam path is enclosed in a plastic box purged with dry nitrogen gas to minimize water vapor absorption. The relative humidity is kept below 1.5% during the experiment.

Results and discussion

THz emission from NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3/mica heterostructure

Figure 1A illustrates the schematic diagram of ultrafast THz generation from our flexible sample, whereby the sample structure is shown in Figure S1. The sample consists of a 7-nm-thick ferromagnetic metal NiFe alloy film, a 20-nm-thick (001) SrRuO3 film, and a 15-nm-thick (001) SrTiO3 film, deposited on a 19-μm-thick mica substrate. NiFe is a soft ferromagnet layer with a net in-plane magnetic moment. SrRuO3 is a paramagnetic metal at room temperature and becomes ferromagnetic below its Curie temperature (Tc) of ∼160 K, which originates from the electrons in the Ru 4d orbital.31 The strong spin-orbit coupling in the SrRuO3 film originates from the Ru4+ ion in the octahedral center.31,32 SrTiO3 serves as a buffer layer for depositing the SrRuO3 film with minimal lattice mismatch with mica. A golden mica with the molecular formula KMg3(AlSi3O10)F2 is utilized as the substrate after being thinned down to 19 μm. The sample is excited by laser pulses of 800 nm central wavelength, 100 fs pulse width, and 80 MHz repetition rate at normal incidence. The laser polarization angle θ is controlled by a λ/2 waveplate (Figure S2). The sample azimuth φ is adjusted by rotating the sample about its surface normal direction.

In this work, the THz emission is first studied on flat samples. The THz waveforms from multiple samples are shown in Figure 1B. All samples are grown on identical mica substrates. As expected, the SrRuO3/SrTiO3 sample does not emit THz radiation because there is no spin source present. The NiFe film radiates a THz wave upon optical excitation, as reported in previous studies.33,34,35 The THz radiation in NiFe originates from the magnetic dipole radiation during the ultrafast demagnetization process.33 Under the excitation power of 80 mW, the electric field of the THz waves emitted from the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 heterostructure is 13.6 V/m (Figure S3) (the generation efficiency is 5.9 × 10−5 for an equivalent 1-mm-thickness sample). Although the THz radiation intensity of the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 heterostructure is weaker than that of the NiFe sample (Figure 1B), the central THz frequency is higher (Figure 1C). The central frequency for the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 heterostructure is 1.4 THz. For comparison, the central frequency for NiFe is 0.8 THz. The effective bandwidth of THz radiation from NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 is 0–4 THz, while that from NiFe is 0–2.5 THz. The broadened emission spectrum in NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 is beneficial for THz device applications.11

In the THz emission experiment, a linearly polarized laser pulse illuminates the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3/mica sample at room temperature. A spin current is generated in the NiFe layer and flows toward the NiFe-SrRuO3 interface.4,12,20,21 Due to the strong spin-orbit coupling in the SrRuO3 layer, spin currents are deflected in opposite directions depending on their spin-up or spin-down states, converted into a transverse charge current (i.e., ISHE36). Consequently, the transient charge current radiates THz waves during its decay process.4,36

To test the above ISHE mechanism, we rotate θ to find that both the waveform (Figure 2A) and intensity (Figure 2B; the experimental data are fitted by ETHz = E0, where E0 is the average THz pulse intensity) of the THz radiation from the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 heterostructure remain unchanged. In the control experiment, the THz radiation from the NiFe sample does not vary with θ either (Figures S4A and S4B).

We then rotate the φ of the flat sample while keeping θ fixed. The φ-dependent THz radiation is shown in Figure 2C. At a given φ (i.e., 46°) and φ + π (i.e., 226°), the polarity of the THz signal is reversed, exhibiting a 2-fold rotational symmetry. In the control experiment, similar polarity reversion is also observed in a NiFe sample (Figure S4C), but the time-domain profiles are not fully identical. Figure 2D presents the extracted peak-to-peak intensity of the THz pulses, which exhibits a distinct 2-fold in-plane anisotropy (peanut-shaped lilac curve; the experimental data are fitted by a sinusoidal function ETHz = E0 + E1sin(π(φ-φ0)/90°), where E0 is the average THz pulse intensity, E1 denotes the anisotropy component, and φ0 is the initial azimuth angle). The THz pulse intensity anisotropy of the heterostructure sample is mainly determined by the in-plane anisotropic spin Hall voltage, which exhibits a sinusoidal relationship with a period of 180°.24,26 In the control experiment, that of the NiFe film exhibits a less distinctive 2-fold in-plane anisotropy (elliptical curve, Figure S4D). The difference in amplitude indicates that the THz emission from the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 heterostructure is due to the spin-charge conversion (SCC) process.

The fast Fourier transform results of Figure 2C are shown in Figure 2E, which is almost unchanged with φ. In contrast, that of the NiFe film sample periodically varies with φ (Figure S4E). In Figures 2E and S4E, the purple and white regions together manifest the effective THz emission bandwidth. The bandwidth of the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 sample is broader than that of the NiFe sample across all φ.

Figure 2F shows the positive linear dependence of the THz radiation peak-to-peak intensity on the pump fluence. With increasing fluence, the photo-excited electron density increases, leading to more electrons participating in the SCC process. This generates a higher density of transverse charge current, which radiates stronger THz pulses. There is no saturation observed in Figure 2F, indicating there is a lot of room for improvement in the THz emission intensity.

The above angle dependence results in both θ and φ being consistent with the SCC scenario driven by the ISHE mechanism.11,20,21 Our control experiment demonstrates that the φ dependence of THz generation in the NiFe sample is different from that in the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 sample, which manifests as the two samples having different THz generation mechanisms.

Overall, we expect strain engineering of more 4d materials could greatly enhance spintronic TE performance. For 4d TMOs, by tuning the lattice strain, oxygen octahedral rotation and distortion, oxygen vacancy, ion doping, polar-nonpolar interfaces, effective modulation of their spin-orbit coupling, magnetic order, and electrical conductivity and mobility26,27,28 are allowed, thus enhancing the TE efficiency.

Magnetic-field-enhanced THz emission

In ISHE, the intensity of the generated THz emission is proportional to the magnitude of charge current, which is determined by the cross-product of the spin current and magnetic-moment polarization. Explicitly, it is known4,11,20,36 as ETHz(ω) = eZ(ω)∫dzJc(z,ω) =eZ(ω)∫dzγ(z)Js(z,ω)×M|M|, where -e is the elementary charge of an electron, Z is the impedance of TE, Jc is the charge current, Js is the spin current, and M is the in-plane magnetic moment.

We investigate how the magnetic field affects the THz emission. By using an electromagnet, an in-plane external magnetic field B is applied to the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 heterostructure along the x axis (Figures 3A–3C) in the laboratory frame. The direction of M in the NiFe layer is thus determined by the direction of B. First, we rotate the sample about the z axis by 90° (Figure 3A). The THz waveform remains unchanged (two blue curves, Figure 3D). Note that the isotropic result in Figure 3D is different from that in Figure 2C (with 2-fold rotational symmetry) because here the B field fixes the spin orientation in the NiFe layer (Figure 3A). Second, we rotate the sample around the x axis (up-down flip) or the y axis (left-right flip) by 180° with fixed B. Thus, the pump laser pulse illuminates the back of the sample (Figure 3B), whereby Js reverses direction while M remains unchanged, leading to a polarity reversal of the emitted THz pulse (see the above equation and the purple and red curves in Figure 3D, which have a 180° phase shift relative to the two blue curves). These two rotations yield identical responses due to the soft magnet nature of the NiFe layer. Third, we control the electromagnet current to reverse the direction of B (Figure 3C, relative to Figure 3A), leading to the reversal of M while keeping Js unchanged. Consequently, the polarity of the emitted THz pulses is flipped by flipping the field B (see the above equation and Figure 3E). Last, as a control experiment, we conduct the same B-reversing and sample-flipping experiments on the NiFe film. In Figure S5A, the polarity of the THz pulse remains unchanged, which indicates that spin current is absent in the single NiFe film. We show that the polarity of the THz pulse reverses when we reverse the direction of B (Figure S5B), which is consistent with the magnetic dipole radiation mechanism16 for the NiFe film. Consequently, our control experiments demonstrate that the TE mechanism of the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 sample is ISHE, whereas that of the single NiFe film has a different THz generation mechanism.

Next, we investigate how the B field affects the intensity of the THz emission. When B is increased from 2 to 30 oe, we observe enhanced THz pulses, as shown in Figure 3E. This can be understood as a non-zero B leading to a net finite spin density (i.e., magnetization), causing the difference in the number of spin-up and spin-down electrons to be enhanced. Consequently, the light-induced Js is enhanced by the B field.4,11,36 Interestingly, even a weak B field can enhance the Js in our sample, effectively taking place in the NiFe layer. This manifests that a very weak B field can enhance the THz pulse emission. Saturation is reached at B = ±20 oe (Figure 3F), which is consistent with the fact that the magnetization in the NiFe layer saturates at B = ±20 oe.24 Quantitatively, the B field can enhance the THz pulse intensity by 3.8 times (the THz generation efficiency is enhanced from 5.9 × 10−5 to 2.2 × 10−4), as compared with that without the B field (the star data point in Figure 3F). Further exploration shows that the THz intensity does not change noticeably up to 500 oe (Figure S6). Overall, our result demonstrates that even a very weak magnetic field (for instance, up to 20 oe) can well control the THz radiation using our NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 sample.

Flexible ultrafast TE

We choose mica as the substrate because it has excellent bendability and high compatibility with the epitaxial growth of oxide films.37,38,39 Furthermore, mica remains stable at high temperatures up to 750°C during oxide film growth by using pulsed laser deposition.39 After thinning down mica to tens of microns, the mica substrate becomes both bendable and transparent, enabling flexible optoelectronic device design.

Figure 4A displays photos of the curved heterostructure samples. There is only one sample fabricated on a 19-μm-thick mica substrate, which is attached to 7 different curved copper sample holders in sequential order. The holders possess varying curvatures κ from −2 to 2 cm−1, inducing a surface deformation rate δ (defined as δ = κt/2, where t = 19 μm is the sample thickness) ranging from −0.19% to 0.19%. The flexibility of our all-inorganic spintronic TE on the mica substrate is at the same level as that of TEs on organic substrates.13,22 A 7 × 7-mm2-sized hole is cut in the sample holder to allow the pump and emitting light pulses to pass through. Figure 4B schematically depicts the two cases of bending in the sample: one with tensile strain and the other with compressive strain for the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3 heterostructure. The pump laser always illuminates from the concave side of the sample holder.

As depicted in Figure 4C, the intensity of the generated THz emission is prominently enhanced in the convex curved samples (with κ > 0). As the curvature increases, the strength of the THz emission is initially enhanced, then reduced. The THz intensity reaches its maximum value at κ = 1 cm−1, which is 1.82 times that at κ = 0 cm−1 (the blue-gray dot in Figure 4C; the THz generation efficiency is enhanced from 5.9 × 10−5 to 1.1 × 10−4). As the sample is convex curved, tensile strain exists within the ab plane of the SrRuO3 crystal, leading to an increased lattice constant in the ab plane. It has been demonstrated that SOT of SrRuO3 can be enhanced by tensile strain,29,30 thus leading to higher SCC efficiency. A larger Jc consequently emits stronger THz light pulses. Further, the decreasing strength of THz emission for κ = 1.3 and 2 cm−1 can be understood as follows: a large tensile strain reduces the magnetization of the NiFe layer (for example, spins deviate from the balanced direction, thereby introducing a smaller projection along the direction of the laser beam). This leads to a smaller Js, which reduces the THz emission.

As the sample is concavely curved (κ < 0), the strength of THz emission decreases with increasing curvature (Figure 4C). At the maximum reduction, occurring at κ = −2 cm−1, the intensity was reduced to 70% of the value at κ = 0 cm−1. The reduction in THz intensity can be attributed to the compressive strain within the ab plane of SrRuO3, which leads to a decreased SOT.29,30 Thus, lower SCC efficiency results in smaller Jc and weaker THz emission.

The frequency spectrum remains unchanged for all curved samples, thereby providing a firm foundation for making flexible TEs. The effective bandwidth is 0–4 THz for all curvatures (Figure 4D). Furthermore, bending the sample can affect the THz intensity and orientation while maintaining the 2-fold rotational symmetry (Figure 5). We explicitly show the φ-dependent THz waveform of the curved sample in Figure 5A, which maintains the 2-fold rotational symmetry. The results for the flat and curved sample are compared in Figure 5B. The THz pulse intensity is again fitted by ETHz = E0 + E1sin(π(φ − φ0)/90°). We obtain E0(flat) = 3.71, E1(flat) = 2.28, φ0(flat) = 43.15°, E0(curved) = 3.99, E1(curved) = 2.63, and φ0(curved) = 53.41°. Thus, the convex curved sample can enhance THz emission, consistent with the results in Figure 4C. Note that E1(curved)/E0(curved) = 0.66 > E1(flat)/E0(flat) = 0.61, and φ0(curved) ≠ φ0(flat), which shows that the curved sample can enhance the anisotropy and modify the anisotropy orientation due to the distorted lattice. Consequently, our sample has strong application potential for flexible spintronic THz devices. In particular, we demonstrate that bending the flexible samples provides a new degree of freedom for controlling spin-induced THz generation.

Wide temperature range for the THz emission

To date, there have rarely been reports of ultrafast THz emission at above room temperature221,36,40,41,42,43 and also very few reports of ultrafast THz emission across a wide temperature range. Here, we investigate the THz emission properties of the NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3/mica sample by varying the temperature with an unprecedentedly wide working temperature range from 80 K to 500 K. The results are shown in Figure 6A. In Figure 6B, the intensity of THz emission is the highest at room temperature and, interestingly, remains >90% of the highest intensity in the temperature range 100–400 K. Above 400 K, the intensity of THz emission decreases with increasing temperature. Even up to 500 K, the intensity of THz emission remains at 60% of that at room temperature (Figure 6B). Such a wide range of working temperature has not been reported before for a spintronic TE. Significantly, such a wide working temperature range has rarely been reported for flexible spintronic TEs (Table 1). Throughout the temperature range, the THz emission bandwidth remains robust, ranging from 0 to 4 THz, until a shrinkage trend emerges at 500 K (Figure 6C). In Table 1, we summarize and compare spintronic TEs that are related to this work. Our sample functions with the widest working temperature range among all TEs and exhibits flexibility and other exceptional properties.

Summarized typical spintronics TEs and their performance

Given the demonstrated robust intensity and bandwidth of the ultrafast THz emission at such a wide temperature range, our sample adapts to a wide variety of extreme environmental conditions for THz applications. Devices operating in space environments must possess the capability to adapt to a wide range of temperatures, including on the Moon (100–390 K), outside space stations (116–394 K), on Mars (130–293 K), and near Mercury (100–700 K).46 Our wide-temperature flexible spintronic TE can be employed by a wearable THz communication device in space.47 Furthermore, in industrial application scenarios, our temperature-stable flexible spintronic TE can be employed for non-contact high-temperature measurements48,49 and high-temperature flexible THz sensors for conformal attachment to curved surfaces for defect detection50 and THz imaging.51 Overall, our flexible spintronic TE has achieved stable operation across the full temperature range from 80 K, a deep-space low temperature, to 500 K, an industrial high temperature.

Conclusion

In summary, we present a comprehensive investigation of the laser-induced ultrafast THz radiation from a NiFe/SrRuO3/SrTiO3/mica heterostructure. We demonstrate that ISHE-driven SCC is the dominant mechanism for THz generation. Our sample is the first all-inorganic flexible spintronic TE, enabling wearable soft spintronic THz devices. Significantly, our flexible spintronic TE exhibits an exceptionally broad working temperature range, which endows it with great application potential in a wide variety of extreme-temperature environments. Our study opens a new avenue for the exploration and realization of THz photonics devices.
