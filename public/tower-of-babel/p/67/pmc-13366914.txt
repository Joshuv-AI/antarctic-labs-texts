Antimicrobial resistance patterns of Ureaplasma and Mycoplasma in urethritis: a six-year retrospective study
Authors: Halime Araz, Saliha Kazcı, Yusuf Gökkurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo describe the distribution of Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp. detections and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns among symptomatic patients evaluated for urogenital infection at a tertiary care center in Turkey over six years (2019–2024).MethodsThis retrospective study included adults who underwent laboratory testing for Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp. due to urogenital infection symptoms. Clinical specimens included first-void urine samples and urethral or cervical swabs. Detection was performed using a commercial multiplex Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) assay, with culture-based confirmation and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.ResultsA total of 525 patients with laboratory-detected Ureaplasma spp. and/or Mycoplasma spp. were analyzed. Ureaplasma spp. were detected in 92.6% of cases, Mycoplasma spp. in 1.5%, and dual detection in 5.9% of cases. The median age was 29 years (range: 17–83 years), and 69.0% were male. Urethral discharge (42.9%) and unprotected sexual contact (67.0%) were the most common symptoms. Coexisting sexually transmitted infections (STIs) were recorded, with Human Papilloma Virus (HPV) being the most frequently reported. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing revealed high resistance to fluoroquinolones, particularly ciprofloxacin (79.3% in Ureaplasma spp. and 56.4% in Mycoplasma spp.). Tetracycline susceptibility was observed in 75.2% and 71.8% of isolates, respectively, whereas josamycin showed the highest susceptibility rates (96.1% and 82.1%). Antibiotic therapy was prescribed in 85.1% of patients, mostly doxycycline-based therapy. Follow-up evaluation was performed in 48.2% of cases.ConclusionIn this six-year, single-center cohort of symptomatic patients, Ureaplasma spp. was the predominant genital mycoplasma detected, with resistance to several antimicrobial agents, particularly fluoroquinolones. As species-level identification, clinical outcome assessment, and systematic follow-up data were limited, these findings should be viewed as descriptive local surveillance data rather than direct treatment-guiding evidence.Trial registrationNot applicable.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Background

Genital mycoplasmas, notably Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp., are members of the Mollicutes class and are often found in the urogenital tract of sexually active individuals. Their lack of a cell wall, small genome, and unique metabolism distinguish them from typical bacterial pathogens, causing diagnostic challenges and resistance to several antimicrobials, especially β-lactams. Though often found in asymptomatic people, they are linked to various urogenital issues like non-gonococcal urethritis, cervicitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, and adverse pregnancy outcomes [1–3]. However, their clinical significance in symptomatic patients remains debated, as they may act as opportunistic pathogens or colonizers, thereby complicating causal attribution in the presence of other STIs. The European STI Guidelines Editorial Board advises against routine testing and treatment for Ureaplasma parvum, Ureaplasma urealyticum, and Mycoplasma hominis in asymptomatic individuals and in most symptomatic cases, owing to insufficient evidence of clinical benefit and concerns regarding antimicrobial overuse [4]. Studies on genital mycoplasmas need careful methodological framing, considering diagnostic context and selection bias. Treatment is complicated by rising resistance to antimicrobials like fluoroquinolones and macrolides, linked to mutations in genes encoding DNA gyrase, topoisomerase IV, and 23S rRNA gene [5–8]. Geographic variability in resistance patterns highlights the need for region-specific data. Detection interpretation is affected by frequent co-detection with pathogens like Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Trichomonas vaginalis, Candida albicans, and viral agents, which may independently cause symptoms [1, 7, 9–12]. Routine comprehensive molecular screening is not consistently done, limiting causal inference and emphasizing the observational nature of studies.

In this context, the present study aimed to describe the distribution of Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp. detections, associated clinical features, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns generated through routine diagnostic workflows over a six-year period (2019–2024) in a tertiary-care hospital in Turkey. Rather than establishing causality or directly guiding treatment decisions, the primary objective was to provide descriptive local surveillance data and to interpret observed resistance patterns within the constraints of retrospective, non–species-resolved laboratory testing.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Infectious Diseases and Clinical Microbiology Department of a tertiary-care hospital in Turkey and covered a six-year period between January 2019 and December 2024. The study population consisted of adult patients who underwent routine laboratory testing for Ureaplasma spp. and/or Mycoplasma spp. because of symptoms suggestive of urogenital infection and had laboratory-detected Ureaplasma spp. and/or Mycoplasma spp.; patients with negative test results were not included in the analytic cohort. The study protocol was approved by the local institutional ethics committee, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they presented with at least one symptom compatible with a urogenital infection, including dysuria, urethral or vaginal discharge, pelvic discomfort, or a documented history of unprotected sexual intercourse, and had laboratory detection of Ureaplasma spp. and/or Mycoplasma spp. Patients with incomplete clinical records or documented systemic antibiotic use within two weeks prior to specimen collection were excluded. Information on sexual orientation, including men who have sex with men (MSM) status, was not routinely recorded in medical files and was therefore not available for analysis.

Sample collection

Clinical specimens consisted of first-void urine samples and urethral or cervical swabs, collected according to the primary clinical presentation and at the discretion of the attending physician. First-void urine samples were obtained in sterile containers, while urethral and cervical swabs were collected by trained healthcare personnel using standard aseptic techniques. All samples were transported immediately to the microbiology laboratory and processed according to routine diagnostic protocols. Specimens were processed on the day of receipt whenever possible; if immediate processing was not feasible, samples were kept under routine refrigerated laboratory conditions until testing.

Microbiological detection

Detection of Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp. was performed using a commercial multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay targeting conserved regions of the 16S rRNA gene according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Due to technical limitations of the assay used in routine diagnostics, species-level differentiation (e.g., Ureaplasma parvum, Ureaplasma urealyticum, Mycoplasma hominis, Mycoplasma genitalium) was not performed. According to the manufacturer’s validation data and previously published evaluations of molecular assays for genital mycoplasmas, PCR-based detection provides higher analytical sensitivity than culture-based methods, while specificity depends on the selected target region and assay design. In the present retrospective study, the diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of the PCR assay were not independently re-evaluated against a reference standard; therefore, assay performance was interpreted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and available validation data.

PCR-positive samples were subsequently processed using a commercial culture-based system (Mycoplasma IST2, bioMérieux, France) as part of standard laboratory practice. This system allows semi-quantitative growth assessment and simultaneous antimicrobial susceptibility testing under routine diagnostic conditions. The Mycoplasma IST2 strip is a broth-based colorimetric system designed for detection, semi-quantitative enumeration, and antimicrobial susceptibility assessment of genital mycoplasmas; growth and susceptibility endpoints were read according to the color-change reactions specified by the manufacturer. No changes in diagnostic platforms or laboratory workflows were introduced during the study period.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Mycoplasma IST2 system, which is based on broth microdilution principles and colorimetric growth detection. Inoculation, incubation, and endpoint reading were carried out according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and only samples with interpretable growth and valid susceptibility reactions were included in susceptibility analyses. The following antimicrobial agents were included according to the manufacturer’s panel: fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, levofloxacin), macrolides (erythromycin, clarithromycin, azithromycin, roxithromycin), tetracyclines (tetracycline, doxycycline), lincosamides (clindamycin), and streptogramins (josamycin, pristinamycin).

Susceptibility results were interpreted according to the interpretive criteria provided by the manufacturer and in reference to available international standards where applicable. It is acknowledged that formal Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) or European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) clinical breakpoints are not defined for all antimicrobial agents tested against genital mycoplasmas. Accordingly, antimicrobial susceptibility results were analyzed descriptively and are reported as resistance or reduced susceptibility signals derived from routine diagnostic testing rather than as definitive clinical resistance categorizations. The denominator for each antimicrobial susceptibility analysis was the number of isolates with valid, interpretable Mycoplasma IST2 results for the relevant organism group. Molecular characterization of resistance-associated mutations was not performed.

Coexisting infections and clinical data

Data on coexisting sexually transmitted infections were obtained retrospectively from electronic medical records, based on documented clinical diagnoses and available laboratory results. Comprehensive molecular screening for common STI pathogens (e.g., Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae) was not systematically performed in all patients and therefore could not be uniformly assessed. Demographic characteristics, presenting symptoms, urinalysis findings, antimicrobial treatment regimens, and follow-up attendance were extracted from hospital records. Follow-up attendance was defined as a documented post-treatment outpatient visit recorded in the hospital information system; exact follow-up intervals and standardized clinical cure assessments were not uniformly available in the retrospective records.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviations or medians with ranges, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages. Given the descriptive nature of the study, no inferential statistical comparisons were performed.

Results

During the six-year study period, approximately 1,850 symptomatic patients underwent routine testing for Ureaplasma spp. and/or Mycoplasma spp.; 525 patients with laboratory-detected Ureaplasma spp. and/or Mycoplasma spp. were included in the analytic cohort, corresponding to an approximate positivity rate of 28.4%. Clinical and microbiological data were analyzed to assess the distribution, clinical characteristics, and antimicrobial resistance patterns of genital mycoplasmas.

Among the study population, 69.0% were male and 31.0% were female, with a mean age of 33.2 +/- 11.7 years (median: 29, range: 17–83 years). During the six-year study period (2019–2024), the number of diagnosed cases showed a rising trend, peaking in 2023 (28.2%), followed by a decline in 2024 (17.1%). Most tests were requested by urology clinics (69.0%), while 31.0% were referred by infectious disease specialists. The predominant sample type was urine (98.5%), with urethral discharge samples constituting only 1.5% of the total sample. Pyuria was detected in 40.8% of patients, while nitrite positivity was observed in only 1.1% of patients.

Concurrent urinary tract infections were identified in 62 cases (11.8%), with Escherichia coli (3.4%) being the most frequently isolated uropathogen, followed by Klebsiella spp. (1.1%) and Candida spp. (0.8%). Species-level identification was not available for Candida spp., and therefore Candida albicans could not be analyzed separately. Urethral discharge was present in 42.9% of cases, while dysuria was reported in 77.7% of patients. A history of unprotected sexual contact was documented in 67.0% of individuals, and 45.0% of the study population reported refractory symptoms despite previous treatment. HIV positivity was detected in 1.0% of cases. Coexisting STIs were identified in 24.0% of cases, with HPV being the most common (9.9%), followed by Gardnerella vaginalis (4.2%), Neisseria gonorrhoeae (3.6%), syphilis (1.1%), Herpes Simplex Virus (1.0%), Chlamydia trachomatis (0.4%), and Trichomonas vaginalis (0.2%). Multiple concurrent STIs were present in 3.6% of cases. The most frequently identified pathogen was Ureaplasma spp., which was detected in 92.6% of cases. Mycoplasma spp. was detected in 1.5% of patients, while dual detection of both Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp. was observed in 5.9% of cases.

Antimicrobial susceptibility results were available for all included isolates with valid Mycoplasma IST2 growth and interpretable susceptibility reactions. Among Ureaplasma spp. isolates, ciprofloxacin showed the highest resistance rate (79.3%), followed by ofloxacin (60.7%) and levofloxacin (37.5%). Mycoplasma spp. isolates also showed resistance to ciprofloxacin (56.4%), ofloxacin (23.1%), and levofloxacin (30.8%). Levofloxacin had higher susceptibility rates (57.1% in Ureaplasma spp. and 61.5% in Mycoplasma spp.) than ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin.

Macrolide susceptibility varied. Erythromycin susceptibility was observed in 78.1% of Ureaplasma spp. and 51.3% of Mycoplasma spp. isolates. Clarithromycin susceptibility was 79.9% in Ureaplasma spp., whereas clarithromycin resistance was 56.4% in Mycoplasma spp. isolates. Roxithromycin susceptibility was 83.2% for Ureaplasma spp. and 43.5% for Mycoplasma spp. isolates.

Tetracycline susceptibility was observed in 75.2% of Ureaplasma spp. and 71.8% of Mycoplasma spp. isolates. Tetracycline resistance rates were 18.0% and 15.4%, respectively.

Clindamycin resistance was substantial, with 69.6% of Ureaplasma spp. and 51.3% of Mycoplasma spp. isolates classified as resistant. Susceptibility to clindamycin was observed in only 24.2% of Ureaplasma spp. and 41.0% of Mycoplasma spp. isolates.

Josamycin demonstrated the highest susceptibility rates, with 96.1% of Ureaplasma spp. and 82.1% of Mycoplasma spp. isolates classified as susceptible. Resistance was low in comparison with other tested agents (3.3% and 15.4%, respectively).

Discussion

This study provides a descriptive overview of the distribution, clinical features, and antimicrobial susceptibility patterns of Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp. detected through routine diagnostic workflows over a six-year period in a tertiary-care setting.

Overall, while these findings should primarily be interpreted as local and descriptive surveillance data rather than as direct evidence for treatment decisions, they do contain significant data given the number of patients involved.

The high distribution of Ureaplasma spp. in our cohort aligns with previous epidemiological studies that have consistently reported Ureaplasma spp. as the predominant genital mycoplasma species in urogenital infections. Mycoplasma spp. was detected in only 1.5% of cases, which is consistent with previous reports indicating that M. hominis and other Mycoplasma species are less frequently isolated from symptomatic patients [4, 13–16]. The observation rates of both Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp. suggest a potential interaction between these organisms in the pathogenesis of genitourinary infections, supporting prior studies that highlight their frequent co-detection. The substantially higher detection rate of Ureaplasma spp. compared with Mycoplasma spp. may be attributed to their higher colonization rates in the lower urogenital tract, enhanced adaptation to mucosal surfaces, and greater viability in urine specimens [17]. Additionally, Ureaplasma parvum and Ureaplasm aurealyticum are more commonly found in asymptomatic individuals and may exhibit higher resilience to environmental and host immune pressures, facilitating their detection in clinical samples. These findings are consistent with previous reports from multicenter studies and surveillance analyses conducted in Europe and Asia [18–20]. The higher detection rate of Ureaplasma spp. may also reflect taxonomic and biochemical differences between these organisms, including the urease activity of Ureaplasma species and their relatively easier detection in routine biochemical or culture-based systems compared with some Mycoplasma species.

These findings are consistent with previous reports describing the frequent detection of genital mycoplasmas in patients presenting with urogenital symptoms. Dysuria was the most common symptom, followed by urethral discharge and refractory symptoms despite prior treatment. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that genital mycoplasmas contribute to persistent and recurrent non-gonococcal urethritis (NGU) and other lower urinary tract symptoms [13–16]. Moreover, 67.0% of the patients had unprotected sexual contact, supporting the sexually transmitted nature of these infections.

The study also highlights notable concurrent STIs detected in 24.0% of cases. The most frequent co-infection was human papillomavirus (HPV), followed by Gardnerella vaginalis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and syphilis. These findings align with previous reports indicating that genital mycoplasmas often coexist with other STIs, potentially complicating clinical presentations and influencing treatment outcomes [14–16, 21]. However, the retrospective nature of the study and the lack of systematic molecular screening for common STI pathogens limit the ability to attribute symptoms specifically to genital mycoplasmas.

Antimicrobial susceptibility analysis revealed significant trends in resistance, particularly against fluoroquinolones and macrolides, which are widely used in the empirical treatment of urogenital infections. Ciprofloxacin exhibited the highest resistance rate among fluoroquinolones, with 79.3% of Ureaplasma spp. and 56.4% of Mycoplasma spp. isolates being classified as resistant. Ofloxacin resistance was substantial (60.7% and 23.1% in Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp., respectively), whereas levofloxacin retained higher susceptibility rates (57.1% and 61.5%, respectively). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting increasing fluoroquinolone resistance, attributed primarily to mutations in the gyrA and parC genes encoding DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, respectively [5, 7, 9–12, 22, 23].

Macrolide resistance varied. Erythromycin maintained a relatively high susceptibility to Ureaplasma spp. (78.1% and 79.9%, respectively) and Mycoplasma spp. exhibited considerable resistance to clarithromycin. This pattern is in line with previous findings that mutations in the 23S rRNA gene and alterations in ribosomal proteins have been implicated in macrolide resistance [7, 11, 22, 23]. Roxithromycin showed the highest susceptibility among macrolides (Ureaplasma spp., 43.5% in Mycoplasma spp.), making it a potential alternative when macrolide therapy is considered.

Tetracyclines remain one of the most effective antimicrobial classes, with susceptibility rates of 75.2% and 71.8% for Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp., respectively. These findings reinforce the continued efficacy of doxycycline in the treatment of genital mycoplasma infections, consistent with the global surveillance data [13, 15, 21]. Given its broad availability and favorable resistance profile, doxycycline is the first-line treatment for suspected mycoplasma infections. However, because standardized patient-level clinical cure data were not available, tetracycline-class susceptibility findings and recorded doxycycline use should not be interpreted as direct evidence of doxycycline treatment effectiveness.

Among alternative treatments, streptogramins, particularly josamycin, demonstrated the highest susceptibility rates (96.1% for Ureaplasma spp. and 82.1% for Mycoplasma spp.), further confirming previous studies advocating the use of streptogramins in cases of multidrug-resistant genital mycoplasma infections [5, 10, 11]. Accordingly, the observed susceptibility to josamycin should be interpreted as a laboratory finding from a routine diagnostic platform rather than as a comparative clinical treatment recommendation.

Although most patients had recorded antibiotic treatment, the retrospective data did not allow a reliable patient-level correlation between antimicrobial susceptibility, prescribed therapy, and standardized clinical cure. Therefore, treatment patterns should be interpreted descriptively and should not be used to infer comparative treatment effectiveness.

A significant challenge identified in this study was the low follow-up rate, with only 48.2% of the patients returning for post-treatment evaluation. This finding mirrors previous reports emphasizing poor adherence to follow-up care for STI management [1, 7, 13, 14, 16]. Inadequate follow-up may contribute to persistent infections, reinfection, and ongoing transmission, underscoring the need for patient education and improved follow-up strategies such as automated reminders or integration of follow-up into routine clinical visits.

The susceptibility patterns observed in this cohort emphasize the variability of antimicrobial responses among genital mycoplasmas detected in routine clinical practice. In particular, reduced susceptibility to fluoroquinolones and macrolides supports the value of local surveillance, while the absence of species-level identification and molecular resistance testing limits mechanistic interpretation. Incomplete follow-up in nearly half of the study population also restricts assessment of persistence, recurrence, and true post-treatment outcomes.

Recent studies have emphasized the importance of monitoring Mycoplasma genitalium and Ureaplasma urealyticum, both of which have been implicated in urogenital infections and multidrug resistance. However, these species were not individually assessed in the present study because of the limitations of the routine diagnostic panel. Specifically, Mycoplasma genitalium detection requires highly sensitive, species-specific molecular assays targeting genes such as MgPa or 23S rRNA, which were not included in routine testing. Similarly, species-level discrimination between Ureaplasma parvum and Ureaplasma urealyticum was not feasible using the available PCR workflow. Future research should incorporate molecular typing and resistance mutation profiling to better characterize species-specific pathogenicity and resistance mechanisms.

Study limitations

This retrospective, single-center study had several limitations. First, species-level identification of Ureaplasma spp. and Mycoplasma spp. was not performed, precluding distinction between Ureaplasma parvum, Ureaplasma urealyticum, Mycoplasma hominis, and Mycoplasma genitalium. Second, antimicrobial susceptibility results were derived from a routine commercial diagnostic platform and were analyzed descriptively; molecular resistance determinants were not assessed. Third, treatment records, susceptibility results, and clinical outcomes could not be systematically correlated at the patient level, and standardized clinical cure data were not uniformly available. Fourth, coexisting urogenital infections and the absence of systematic molecular screening for all STI pathogens may have confounded symptom attribution. Finally, sexual behavior data, including MSM status, were unavailable, and the single-center design may limit generalizability beyond the institutional setting.

Conclusion

In this retrospective, single-center study, Ureaplasma spp. constituted the majority of genital mycoplasma detections among symptomatic patients evaluated in routine clinical practice, while Mycoplasma spp. was detected less frequently. Antimicrobial susceptibility data derived from routine diagnostic platforms demonstrated substantial variability, including reduced susceptibility to several commonly used agents. These findings provide region-specific descriptive surveillance data and underscore the need for cautious interpretation of routine susceptibility results. Given the methodological limitations of retrospective, non-species-resolved analyses, the results should be interpreted within the context of diagnostic constraints, incomplete follow-up, lack of standardized clinical cure assessment, and potential confounding by coexisting infections. Prospective studies incorporating standardized molecular diagnostics, species-level identification, resistance mutation analysis, and systematic follow-up are warranted to better characterize the clinical relevance and resistance patterns of genital mycoplasmas.
