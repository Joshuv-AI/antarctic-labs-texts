Explainable convolutional neural network model provides an alternative genome-wide association perspective on mutations in SARS-CoV-2
Authors: Parisa Hatami, Richard Annan, Luis Miranda, Jane Gorman, Mengjun Xie, Letu Qingge, Hong Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identifying informative genomic features in SARS-CoV-2 can help clarify patterns of viral evolution. In this study, we developed an explainable convolutional neural network (CNN) model to classify SARS-CoV-2 genomic sequences into the WHO-designated Variants of Concern (VOCs), Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron. Using a balanced dataset of genomes, the classification CNN achieved 99.96% accuracy on the held-out test set. To interpret the model’s predictions, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to estimate the contribution of each nucleotide position to VOC-label prediction and compared aggregated attributions with a chi-square GWAS baseline applied to the same categorical labels. SHAP prioritized several lineage-associated sites in Spike, including C23525T (S: H655Y) and A21801C (S: D80A), and also highlighted ORF8, ORF9, and intergenic positions that were not detected in the chi-square GWAS baseline. Based on the comparison between the CNN and GWAS, 23.8%–32.4% of top-ranked positions overlapped, with the shared subset enriched in Spike. We interpret these results as evidence that explainable deep learning can complement site-wise association analysis. This work therefore serves as a proof-of-concept that convolutional neural network modeling with post hoc attribution can provide an alternative genome-wide association perspective on mutations in SARS-CoV-2.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53625-x.

Introduction

Deep learning and machine learning have emerged as important tools for connecting genotypes to phenotypes1,2. Explainable artificial intelligence (xAI) methods are increasingly used to interpret these models and to identify which input features most strongly influence their predictions3. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is a widely used xAI framework that quantifies the contribution of individual features to a model output4.

SARS-CoV-2 provides a useful setting to compare model-based and site-wise approaches to genomic interpretation. Conventional genome-wide association study (GWAS) methods have identified mutations associated with fitness-related or viral-load-related outcomes5,6, but those analyses typically rely on site-wise statistical models that do not explicitly represent higher-order interactions among loci. Deep learning models can capture nonlinear relationships among positions7 and can be a complement to traditional GWAS8.

To address the complexity of linking genotypes to phenotypes for SARS-CoV-2, we explored machine learning approaches, including deep learning, as an alternative to genome-wide association studies. Our study leveraged the designation of Variants of Concern (VOCs) by the World Health Organization (WHO)9. These labels summarize lineage-level traits regarding transmissibility, immune escape, treatment response, and growth advantage10,11. We are aware that VOC designations are not direct measures of viral fitness, pathogenicity, or transmissibility. As a proof-of-concept study, these VOC designations are treated as lineage-based proxy labels to compare AI models and GWAS.

Our goal is to test whether an interpretable sequence classifier can recover lineage-associated genomic features from these labels and compare the results with a simple site-wise baseline. At the time of submission, we were aware of a prior CNN-based work on SARS-CoV-2 variant classification12. Relative to this previous study, the specific contribution of this study is the combination of a 2-layer CNN with nucleotide-level Deep SHAP attribution and an explicit comparison against a chi-square GWAS baseline applied to the same categorical VOC labels. We used SHAP to identify which aligned nucleotide positions most strongly supported VOC-label classification in the trained model. We then compared those positions with loci prioritized by a chi-square GWAS baseline.

Results

An overview of this study is presented in Fig. 1. SARS-CoV-2 genomes were obtained from the Global Initiative on Sharing All Influenza Data (GISAID)13,14, filtered to the five WHO VOC categories considered here, deduplicated, and then balanced to a 200,000-genome dataset. The final split used 128,000 genomes for training, 32,000 for validation, and 40,000 for held-out testing. This study focused on genomes of VOCs Alpha, Beta, Delta, Gamma, and Omicron9.

CNN is an effective machine learning model for SARS-CoV-2 classification

We evaluated four machine learning approaches for predicting WHO labels from input sequences: Random Forest15, XGBoost16, CatBoost17, and a 2-layer Conv1D model (CNN) (Fig. 2). The first three are all tree-based models. The CNN architecture included a Dropout layer (rate = 0.4) to reduce overfitting.

All four models underwent optimization procedures. Both the 2-layer Conv1D model and CatBoost exhibited better performance in comparison to Random Forest and XGBoost (Fig. 2b). In the 2-layer Conv1D model, the loss on the training data was 0.0111 and the validation loss was 0.0024, and the accuracy on the test dataset was 99.96%. The lower validation loss relative to training loss reflects the effect of the dropout layer (rate = 0.40), which is active during training but disabled during evaluation. We therefore interpret this behavior as a consequence of dropout regularization rather than evidence of overfitting.

Further SHAP analysis of CatBoost and the 2-layer Conv1D model indicated that the latter was better at identifying regions consistent with current research on SARS-CoV-2, as compared to the tree-based models (SHAP analysis of CatBoost is provided in Supplementary Materials). We repeated model training with different random initializations and subsamples of sequences; the top-ranked SHAP features remained stable across runs. Consequently, we focused subsequent studies on the 2-layer CNN model. To quantify this stability, we computed the pairwise Jaccard similarity of the top-100 ranked genomic positions across three independent random-subset runs (seeds 0, 1, 2) for each VOC (n = 5 sequences per VOC per run). The mean pairwise Jaccard similarity was 0.89 for Alpha, 0.93 for Beta, 0.94 for Delta, 0.89 for Gamma, and 0.76 for Omicron (overall mean 0.88, pairwise range 0.71–0.98), confirming high consistency in the top-ranked positions across independent runs (Supplementary Table S2).

We focused on the specific loci that aligned with previously reported lineage-associated sites, especially in Spike20. Across the studied VOCs, two Spike positions emerged repeatedly as strong discriminative markers: nucleotide 23,525, corresponding to S: H655Y, and nucleotide 21,801, corresponding to S: D80A. These positions corresponded to reported lineage-associated amino acid changes in Gamma21,22, Omicron23, and Beta18. Prior studies have discussed H655Y in the context of variant fitness and transmission24, and D80A in the N-terminal domain related to antigenic and immune-evasion changes11. In our CNN classifier model, these sites provided lineage contrasts: T at 23,525 increased support for Gamma and Omicron labels relative to Alpha, Beta, and Delta, whereas C at 21,801 supported the Beta label relative to non-Beta VOCs. Their high SHAP values, while not evidence of causality, are consistent with prior findings at these loci.

Other variant-specific cases provided a similar picture (Fig. 3; Table 1). For Alpha (Fig. 3a and b), the next-highest SHAP features included G28048T (ORF8:R52I), previously discussed in relation to host-interaction context25, together with the D3L-associated triplet G28280C, A28281T, and T28282A26, which was highly prevalent in Alpha genomes27. For Beta (Fig. 3c and d), SHAP highlighted the reported Spike mutations A21801C (S: D80A)28 and A22206G (S: D215G), both in the N-terminal domain and previously discussed in the same immune-evasion-related context11. Beta also showed a high-ranking synonymous feature at C28253T (ORF8:F120F)29 as a lineage marker. A previous study suggested that ORF8 may be involved in RNA structural and regulatory contexts30.

For Gamma (Fig. 3e and f), the highest-ranking feature G28167A (ORF8:E92K) was a reported Gamma-associated site31, and the accompanying Spike markers G21974T (S: D138Y) and C23525T (S: H655Y) were also known Gamma-associated substitutions32. For Delta (Fig. 3g and h), the most influential feature was C21618G (S: T19R), another reported Delta-associated Spike mutation32 that has been discussed in relation to immune-recognition context33. Another high-ranking Delta feature was A28461G (ORF9/N: D63G), which has been reported in association with more severe clinical outcomes34. For Omicron (Fig. 3i and j), the leading SHAP signals included C23525T (S: H655Y), A28271T, and C28311T (ORF9/N: P13L), all previously reported in Omicron-associated genomes35.

It is interesting that SHAP highlights several synonymous sites and wild-type nucleotides (Table 1). The classifier uses both the presence of variant-associated substitutions and the absence of competing lineage-defining changes, so wild-type nucleotides at positions corresponding to Beta-, Gamma-, or Delta-associated substitutions can still act as positive evidence for the correct alternative VOC label. We therefore interpret the Fig. 3 outputs as relevant markers for VOC-label discrimination within lineage structure.

For comparison, we provided SHAP value plots for the CatBoost approach in the supplementary document (Fig. S1, Table S1). In addition, we further analyzed the scenarios of CNN misclassifications for selected examples provided in the supplementary document (Fig. S2–Fig. S6).

The heatmaps illustrate that the same aligned site can simultaneously support one VOC label (cells in red) while suppressing alternative labels (cells in blue). This cross-variant view shows that SHAP reflects how a position functions within the trained multivariate classifier.

Comparison with the chi-square GWAS baseline

To compare the CNN-SHAP analysis with a conventional site-wise baseline, we applied a chi-square test to the same five categorical VOC labels and summarized the resulting per-position statistics in Fig. 5. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) is a standard method for identifying mutations linked to phenotypic changes8. This chi-square GWAS comparison, though simple, provides a site-wise baseline for this proof-of-concept comparison between CNN-SHAP analysis and GWAS-style site-wise association.

At both the genome-wide and ORF-level illustrations, aggregated SHAP values were more concentrated in Spike and also emphasized selected ORF8, ORF9, and intergenic positions, whereas the chi-square baseline distributed signal more broadly across the genome (Fig. 5).

Next, we presented Venn diagrams to illustrate complementary performance between SHAP and GWAS methods by focusing on the top positions from both methods (Fig. 5e and f, and 5g). To address potential sensitivity of threshold choice, we picked top 1073 (due to ties), 1500, and 2000 positions from both methods. We consistently observed overlapping top features selected by both methods, with an overlap of 23.8%–32.4%. The majority of these overlapping features, 53.3–67.6%, were from the Spike gene. Interestingly, we also observed a large fraction of distinct top features selected by either GWAS or SHAP, suggesting that SHAP offered a different perspective on the link between genotype and phenotype compared with GWAS. In the distinct groups of features, the influence from the Spike gene is not as pronounced as that in the overlapping group.

Discussion

In this study, we used WHO VOC labels as a supervised classification target and then applied SHAP to pinpoint nucleotide positions supporting the predictions. We found that this interpretable sequence classifier could not only recover many lineage-associated loci previously discussed in the SARS-CoV-2 literature but also prioritize additional accessory and intergenic positions for follow-up.

Our results indicate that genomic positions linked to variant-specific mutations have a significant impact on the convolutional neural network model’s performance. For example, top-ranking markers include R52I and D3L for Alpha; D80A, F120F, and D215G for Beta; T19R and D63G for Delta; H655Y for Omicron; and E92K, D138Y, H655Y, and E1264D for Gamma sequences9–11,29,36. Notably, the corresponding wild-type nucleotides in these positions are also important features identified by SHAP analysis, such as 23,525 and 21,801 for Alpha; 23,525 and 28,461 for Beta; 21,801 for Gamma; 23,525, 21,801, and 28,167 for Delta; and 21,618, 21,801, and 28,461 for Omicron.

It is worth noting that two Spike sites, nucleotides 23,525 (S: H655) and 21,801 (S: D80), were found to be top SHAP features across all variants analyzed. Intuitively, the CNN model learned to use these sites like yes/no signals to discern VOCs. At 23,525, the H655Y change (T instead of the wild-type C) is common in Gamma and Omicron. Hence, seeing a T nudges the model toward these lineages, while a C supports Alpha/Beta/Delta. At 21,801, the D80A change (C instead of the wild-type A) is a hallmark of Beta, so C pushes the prediction toward Beta and A toward the non-Beta variants. For comparison, SHAP analysis did not prioritize the well-known mutation A23403G (S: D614G)37 because it is present across all studied VOCs and hence is not an effective feature for classification.

SHAP values quantify each feature’s contribution to model predictions within the context of the observed lineage background, which helps explain why both mutations and their corresponding wild‑type nucleotides can appear as high‑ranking features. Our SHAP analysis is consistent with recent reports on Spike missense patterns and stability-related changes in Omicron and related VOCs9–11,19,23. For the same reason, highly prevalent sites such as D614G contribute little to VOC discrimination in CNN because they are broadly shared across the studied variants9,37,38. Finally, we acknowledge the limitation of using VOC labels as proxies for phenotypic measurements.

Both ORF8 and ORF9 accessory proteins are reported to have key roles in the evolution and pathogenicity of SARS-CoV-2 VOCs39,40. Our results showed that association of mutations in ORF8 and ORF9 with VOCs was not detected by this simplified chi-square baseline but was detected by CNN paired with SHAP analysis. This suggests that the CNN was able to capture complex non‑linear relationships linking ORF8 and ORF9 mutations to VOC classifications.

Phylogenetic analysis41 provides useful context for interpreting lineage structure in SARS-CoV-2 genomes. To compare the sequence patterns captured by the model with evolutionary relationships, we examined a phylogenetic tree derived from aligned VOC genomes together with a hierarchical clustering tree based on SHAP-value profiles (Fig. S9). Most VOC groups showed broad agreement between these views, whereas Gamma differed more noticeably. We interpret this comparison as showing that the classifier captures lineage-associated genomic structure reflected in the sampled sequence background. Because VOC labels are correlated with phylogenetic structure, the top SHAP features are best understood as lineage-discriminative positions within that lineage context. A more formal treatment of phylogenetic confounding remains an important direction for future work.

The primary contribution of this study is the use of nucleotide-level Deep SHAP to characterize which positions the CNN relies upon, and the comparison against a chi-square marginal baseline on the variant labels. A non-circular test of whether explainable CNN can surface genuinely novel associations would require training on a directly measured phenotype, such as in vitro replication fitness, serum neutralization titres, or ACE2 binding affinity, which would require extensive experimental effort and are beyond the scope of this work.

At the theoretical level, SHAP and GWAS reflect different analytical perspectives. SHAP is a local, model-based measure that quantifies how genomic positions contribute to predictions for individual sequences4, whereas GWAS evaluates marginal statistical association at the population level8. Because SHAP operates at the individual-sample level, it can be more sensitive to rare features that are informative for classification in specific lineage backgrounds8. In our study, SHAP highlighted several ORF8 and ORF9 positions that were less prominent in the chi-square baseline42. We interpret these differences as reflecting the complementary strengths of local model-based attribution and population-level association analysis, together with the effects of lineage structure, linkage disequilibrium, and feature frequency43.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) refers to genetic loci that are strongly associated. For loci under strong LD, SHAP values are expected to be re-distributed among the linked loci based on the standard SHAP definition in the Materials and Methods. In other words, LD is expected to cause under-estimation of SHAP for strongly linked loci. The mutation in ORF9(N): D3L can be used as an example to illustrate the effect of LD on SHAP values. In this case, three nucleotide changes caused the D3L mutation, and the SHAP values were about evenly distributed among them. When there are complicated interactions among loci/features, the scenarios would likely become more complicated and would require more in-depth theoretical examination in future studies.

Therefore, when both SHAP and GWAS identify the same features as important, it underscores the consistency between two distinct analytical perspectives. When SHAP and GWAS disagree, the difference may reflect methodological differences and sensitivity to other factors such as LD. This discussion provides another line of reasoning that xAI methods like SHAP can complement GWAS by offering model-based insights at the individual level.

This study is intended as a proof-of-concept for interpretable sequence modeling for SARS-CoV-2. This study is based on a GISAID snapshot from June 2022 and does not include post-Omicron subvariants such as XBB, BQ.1, and JN.1. Accurate and interpretable detection of viral mutations is essential for diagnosis, prognosis, and decision-making, particularly when emerging variants affect transmissibility, immune escape, or drug and vaccine efficacy44. The xAI approaches could provide explanations that support reliable variant classification, inform diagnostic and treatment strategies, and enhance trust and clinical applicability of AI-driven infectious disease management. Machine learning has great potential in public health, such as the prevention of infectious diseases1,45, forecasting46, diagnosis47,48, policy development49, and hospital resource allocation and preparation50. We hope this work will lead to further research into leveraging machine learning to address challenges in public health.

Conclusion

Our study shows that a 2-layer CNN coupled with SHAP can classify five SARS-CoV-2 VOC labels with high accuracy and can identify known mutations associated with viral lineages in the literature. Our analysis links SARS-CoV-2 genomic variation to phenotypic proxies. Our approach highlights key mutations in the Spike gene as well as accessory regions such as ORF8 and ORF9. In addition, our approach prioritized some loci that were not highlighted by the chi-square baseline, consistent with the ability of convolutional neural networks to model nonlinear patterns across genomic positions. Overall, this proof-of-concept study suggests that explainable convolutional neural networks can provide a useful complementary perspective to site-wise association analysis of SARS-CoV-2.

Materials and methods

Data and preprocessing

All data were downloaded from GISAID on 2022-06-16 using the multiple-sequence alignment (MSA) FASTA file “2022-06-16_unmasked.fa” and the accompanying metadata file “variant_surveillance.tsv”. Variant labels were taken from the metadata Variant column and restricted to the five WHO VOC categories used in this study: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron. The GISAID MSA already places sequences on a common aligned coordinate system of 29,891 positions, so no additional realignment was performed. Gap characters (-) were retained.

To reduce train-test leakage, exact duplicate aligned sequence strings were removed before any train/validation/test split. We then constructed a class-balanced dataset by randomly sampling 40,000 genomes per VOC from the deduplicated pool, yielding 200,000 genomes overall. For each VOC, 32,000 genomes were allocated to model development and 8,000 were held out for final testing. 20% of the model-development partition was then reserved for validation, resulting in 25,600 training and 6,400 validation genomes per VOC (128,000 training and 32,000 validation overall). Beyond restricting the dataset to the five WHO VOC labels, we did not add further geographic or temporal balancing, and recombinant lineages were not modeled separately.

Each aligned sequence was encoded position-wise into a 7-channel one-hot tensor with symbol order [‘-’, ‘a’, ‘c’, ‘g’, ‘i’, ‘n’, ‘t’], representing gap, A/C/G/T, ambiguous base N, and an “i” bucket for other characters. This produced input tensors of shape (N, 29,891, 7).

For the tree-based baselines, we randomly sampled 300 genomes per VOC (1,500 total) from the same deduplicated pool used for the CNN. One-hot matrices were flattened to two-dimensional arrays for Random Forest, XGBoost, and CatBoost. We kept these baselines at the smaller input size because the flattened matrices for these methods must be loaded into memory directly. In contrast, the CNN uses batched training on the original one-hot encoded tensors, which made it feasible to train on the full balanced dataset. These models are therefore included as computationally tractable baselines rather than as size-matched competitors to the CNN.

The 2-layer CNN model

The convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture consisted of two one-dimensional convolutional blocks, each composed of a Conv1D layer with 196 filters, kernel size 19, stride 3, and ReLU activation, followed by a MaxPooling1D layer with pool size 5. The convolutional feature extractor was followed by three fully connected layers with 164, 42, and 20 hidden units, respectively, each using ReLU activation, and a dropout layer with rate 0.40. The final output layer used a five-class softmax for VOC classification.

Model optimization used the Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.0014924 and categorical cross-entropy loss. Training was run for 15 epochs with batch size 64, and model selection was based on the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. Random seeds were fixed across Python, NumPy, and TensorFlow, and training was performed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 80 GB VRAM. The dropout rate (0.40) was selected by comparing validation loss at rates of 0.25 and 0.40; 0.40 yielded lower validation loss and was retained. The learning rate (0.0014924) was identified via a systematic grid search over the range 0.0001–0.005 in logarithmic steps and is reported at full precision to support exact reproducibility from the released code.

Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP)

We used Deep SHAP4 to interpret CNN predictions. Because each aligned nucleotide is represented by a 7-dimensional one-hot vector, the raw SHAP outputs were summed across the seven channels to obtain a single attribution per genomic position. The local waterfall plots shown in Fig. 3 use one representative correctly classified test genome for each VOC. The per-VOC beeswarm summaries aggregate SHAP values across held-out test genomes from the corresponding VOC.

The Deep SHAP background consisted of five randomly selected training genomes per VOC (25 sequences total). We retained this small background set as a computationally tractable approximation for a very high-dimensional input. Accordingly, the SHAP outputs are interpreted here as model-relevant contributions to VOC-label classification rather than as direct causal effect estimates.

GWAS on mutations associated with WHO labels

To provide a simple site-wise baseline, we applied chi-square tests to contingency tables relating observed nucleotide states at each aligned position to the five categorical VOC labels (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron). Multi-allelic sites were handled through the observed nucleotide categories present at each position. No covariates for geography, calendar time, recombinant status, or population structure were included. This analysis is intended as a simplified marginal comparison rather than a more sophisticated GWAS implementation8,43.
