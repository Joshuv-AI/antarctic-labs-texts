The impact of emotional management ability on sleep quality of university students: chain mediation of physical exercise and electronic health literacy
Authors: Hao-jie Zuo, Fan-zheng Mu, Ding-you Zhang, Wei-dong Zhu, Ning Zhou, Yao Zhang, Wei Wang, Xiao-yu Wang, Xing-yu Zhang, Chen-xi Li, Mo-han He, Yu-han Li, Wen-hao Zhang, Qi Liu, Jia-qiang Wang, Hao-yu Li, Hu Lou, Bo-chun Lu, Han Li, Yu-peng Ye, Yang-sheng Zhang, Lan-lan Yang, Shan-shan Han, Ya-xing Li, Ling-li Xu, Yu-yan Qian, Lei Ding, Shi-hai Yu, Bo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study investigates the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality in university students, examining the chain-mediating roles of physical exercise and electronic health literacy. The findings provide a theoretical and practical basis for understanding and addressing sleep problems in this population.MethodsUtilizing 2022 data from the China Physical Activity and Health Longitudinal Study - College Students (CPAHLS-CS), this study investigated four applied universities–Nanjing Xiaozhuang University, Yangzhou University, Shangqiu College, and Yangzhou Polytechnic College–through stratified random sampling, ensuring a representative sample from each institution. Emotional Intelligence Scale (EIS), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index Scale (PSQI), Physical Activity Rating Scale (PARS-3), and Electronic Health Literacy Scale (eHEALS) were used for measurement. Descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, regression analyses, and chained mediation effects tests were performed using SPSS 26.0 and process plug-ins, controlling for confounding variables such as gender, grade, smoking, and alcohol use.ResultsEmotional management ability was statistically significantly and negatively correlated with sleep quality (β = -0.051, p < 0.001). Physical exercise and electronic health literacy acted as chain mediators between emotional management ability and sleep quality, with a total effect value of -0.042 (95% CI [-0.059, -0.025]), a direct effect value of -0.051 (95% CI [-0.070, -0.031]), and an indirect effect value of 0.009 (95% CI [-0.003, 0.019]). Emotional management ability indirectly improved sleep quality (β = -0.020, p < 0.001) through enhanced physical exercise (β = 0.430, p < 0.001) and indirectly promoted sleep quality (β = 0.027, p < 0.001) through enhanced electronic health literacy (β = 0.655, p < 0.001). The chain-mediated pathway’ emotional management ability → physical exercise → electronic health literacy → sleep quality’ had an effect value of 0.0002, suggesting a small but non-negligible effect.ConclusionIn several ways, emotional management ability and sleep quality are related among application-oriented university students. There’s a direct negative correlation between them, and it’s also possible that physical exercise and electronic health literacy are mediators in a chain, indirectly affecting sleep quality.

Introduction

Against a backdrop of rapid social development and intensifying competition, sleep quality among Chinese residents has become a salient concern, carrying significant implications for both national health and societal progress [1]. As the prospective cornerstone of the nation, university students’ sleep patterns reflect the lived experiences and psychological pressures facing contemporary youth and presage the calibre of the national talent pool, thereby garnering considerable attention and predicting the quality of the national talent pool [2]. The urgency of this matter is underscored in the ‘2024 White Paper on Sleep Health of Chinese Residents’ published by the China Sleep Research Association. This report reveals that Chinese residents average a mere 6.75 hours of sleep, with 64% experiencing sleep-related difficulties and 59% suffering from insomnia. Specifically, university students, particularly those born in the 2000 s, report notable difficulty initiating sleep [3, 4], highlighting the latent health risks and the subsequent social ramifications. Sleep quality constitutes a crucial index of an individual’s physical and mental well-being [5], but it also strongly correlates with university students’ academic performance. Pertinent studies have substantiated that chronic sleep restriction not only diminishes human life expectancy [6] but also impairs the capacity for genetic repair, thereby accelerating cellular senescence and augmenting the susceptibility to malignant transformations [7]. According to the planning outline of the Healthy China Action (2019–2030), the adult population of China should maintain a daily sleep duration between 7 and 8 hours from 2022 to 2030 [8]. Given their vital role in social advancement, university students’ sleep quality across higher education institutions is attracting escalating scrutiny amidst ongoing social evolution and has become a pivotal research focus across various disciplines.

Numerous studies have indicated a strong association between emotional management ability and sleep quality [9–11]. Students with sound emotional management ability can sustain a positive mental disposition when facing adversity and setbacks. This mitigates the genesis of negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression [12], reducing the disruption of these adverse affective states on sleep. Robust emotional management ability helps university students plan and manage their time more effectively [13], averting sleep curtailment from procrastination or excessive rumination [14]. By scientifically allocating time for study, recreation, and social activities, students can ensure adequate rest while maintaining high academic efficiency, preventing sleep disorders, and improving sleep quality. Longitudinal studies, experimental research, and investigations into neurophysiological mechanisms all corroborate this connection. Longitudinal studies demonstrate that persistent difficulties in emotional regulation lead to a deterioration in sleep quality [15]. Experimental research has revealed that emotion regulation strategies, such as cognitive reappraisal, can effectively enhance sleep [16, 17]. Moreover, further research elucidates that emotional management ability influences sleep by modulating neuroendocrine systems, including cortisol levels, and other neurophysiological mechanisms [18].

Some studies propose that emotion regulation primarily affects sleep initiation and maintenance [19], while others emphasize its impact on sleep architecture and restorative functions [20]. Moreover, variations in how emotion regulation is measured may account for differing findings; some researchers utilize the Emotional Intelligence Scale (EIS) [21], whereas others employ the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) [22], tools that may emphasize distinct aspects of emotion regulation and, consequently, influence study results. Specific investigations have failed to demonstrate a significant effect of emotion regulation on sleep quality [22], a discrepancy potentially attributable to several factors. Cultural nuances in emotional expression and regulation styles may contribute; for instance, Eastern cultures often prioritize implicit emotional regulation, whereas Western cultures tend to favour explicit emotional expression [23]. Furthermore, differences in study populations could play a role, with older adults possibly exhibiting greater vulnerability to emotional fluctuations and their subsequent impact on sleep quality [24].

Prior research indicates that regular physical exercise and enhanced electronic health literacy are associated with better sleep quality [25, 26]. However, the specific ways physical exercise and electronic health literacy combine to affect sleep quality, especially considering the influence of emotional management, are not fully understood. While many scholars acknowledge that improved emotional management benefits sleep in university students [10, 11], few studies have examined potential mediating factors. For example, Chen et al. studied emotion regulation difficulties as mediators of sleep quality in secondary students [27], and Li et al. used emotion regulation as a mediator to enhance sleep [28]. Others have focused on the link between emotion regulation and sleep quality. Although research has explored sleep problems in undergraduates, there’s a paucity of work integrating emotional management ability, physical exercise, electronic health literacy, and sleep quality within a single framework to examine their chain-mediated relationships. Moreover, scant attention has been paid to how these variables affect undergraduates’ sleep quality, particularly when considering the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) and the Health Belief Model (HBM).

To address these lacunae in the current literature, this study integrates physical exercise and electronic health literacy within a unified theoretical framework to explore their serial mediating roles in the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality. The rationale for focusing on these specific variables stems from the interconnected nature of emotion regulation, physical activity, and health knowledge. Guided by the TPB [29], an individual’s emotional state can shape their attitudes towards and engagement in physical exercise, subsequently influencing their health behaviours and, ultimately, their sleep quality. Consequently, physical exercise is proposed to mediate the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality. Building upon the HBM [30], enhanced electronic health literacy empowers individuals to access and interpret reliable health information, promoting healthier lifestyle choices. Accordingly, electronic health literacy is also posited to mediate the association between emotional management ability and sleep quality. Furthermore, prior research has indicated that physical exercise can enhance an individual’s electronic health literacy [31], suggesting a potential serial relationship between these variables. This intricate interplay between emotional management ability, mediating factors, and sleep quality warrants particular attention in the context of university students. This demographic frequently encounters substantial life stressors and psychological challenges, including relatively limited educational resources and uncertain career prospects, which can significantly impact their emotional management ability and sleep quality. Moreover, the collectivist cultural values in China may influence individuals’ emotional expression and regulation strategies [32], potentially affecting sleep outcomes. Therefore, a focused investigation into this group is essential to illuminate the pathways linking these variables, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of sleep health among university students.

The present study leverages cross-regional, large-scale survey data to construct a chained mediation model. This model seeks to rectify existing research limitations and examine the following core questions: Does emotional management ability significantly impact the sleep quality of university students? (Supported by research linking emotional regulation to sleep quality [15]). Do physical exercise and electronic health literacy independently mediate the association between emotional management ability and sleep quality? (Grounded in theories that positive emotions encourage exercise [33], and that electronic health literacy shapes health-related behaviours and cognitions [25, 26]). Do physical exercise and electronic health literacy, in combination, constitute a chained mediation, sequentially influencing sleep quality as a serial process contingent upon emotional management ability? (Inferred from the postulation that physical exercise may bolster electronic health literacy, consequently affecting sleep quality [31, 34]). Critically, this study will deploy advanced statistical techniques to conduct rigorous analyses of the collected data, thereby elucidating the intricate and nuanced relationships amongst these variables. By constructing the aforementioned chained mediation model, this research will furnish a more comprehensive explanatory framework than previous investigations by deeply analysing the direct impact of emotional management ability on sleep quality and accentuating the indirect roles of physical exercise and electronic health literacy within this pathway of influence. Furthermore, this study integrates the TPB and the HBM to facilitate a more profound understanding of the underlying mechanisms. By addressing these inquiries, this research will furnish novel theoretical perspectives and practical evidence for ameliorating sleep problems among university students, whilst concurrently contributing practical significance and scholarly value to promoting the healthy development of students, advancing educational equity, and fostering the holistic development of talent.

Theoretical analysis and hypothesis development

The effect of emotional management ability on the sleep quality of university students

Emotions constitute an individual’s subjective experiences and reactions to the external world, typically categorised into positive and negative dimensions [35]. According to discrete emotion theory, positive emotions include happiness, pleasure, satisfaction, and love. Negative emotions include anger, tension, anxiety, and pain. University students face pressures from academics, employment prospects, and interpersonal relationships. These pressures can easily trigger negative emotions such as anxiety, anger, and tension [36]. Suppose university students lack practical emotional management ability and cannot appropriately cope with these negative emotions. In that case, it may lead to the accumulation of emotional problems, which in turn will hurt sleep quality. For example, long-term anxiety and tension may precipitate sleep disorders, such as difficulty initiating sleep, shallow sleep, or early awakening, which seriously affect university students’ physical and mental health. Conversely, university students with sound emotional management ability can actively regulate and transform negative emotions, maintaining a stable state of positive emotions and thus improving sleep quality because positive emotions can engender psychological pleasure and satisfaction, which helps to alleviate stress and reduce the psychological burden [37], and therefore contribute to the improvement of sleep.

Emotions are not directly caused by external events but are determined by an individual’s perceptions and evaluations of those events. The ABC theory of emotions (also known as rational emotive therapy) posits that the activating event (A), the belief (B), and the consequence (C) are the three key components of emotion generation. By adjusting irrational beliefs (B), individuals can face challenges more rationally, reducing the production of negative emotions [38]. Emotion regulation theory also emphasises individual subjective agency in emotional management, suggesting that individuals can manage emotions through cognitive restructuring and behavioural regulation [39]. Positive and negative emotions exhibit an antagonistic relationship in terms of emotional states. According to research, negative emotions (e.g., depression, anxiety, and stress) not only cause sleep disturbances [40] but also predict future sleep problems [41]. Individuals chronically in an adverse emotional state tend to be characterised by a significant decrease in sleep quality. Research by Chen and Yan confirmed that forgiveness, as a positive emotion, can significantly reduce physiological stress response, ease emotional tension, and reduce cognitive arousal, thus improving sleep quality. International studies have shown positive emotions directly improve sleep quality [29]. Therefore, effective emotional management and enhanced positive emotional experiences may improve university students’ sleep quality. Based on this theoretical framework, Hypothesis 1 proposes that emotional management ability positively correlates with sleep quality among university students.

The mediating role of physical exercise

The TPB provides a systematic framework for understanding health behaviour decisions, encompassing behavioural attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioural control, behavioural intentions, and actual behaviour. The theory posits that individuals do not make arbitrary health-related decisions. Instead, they act after considering multiple factors [42]. Attitude reflects an individual’s positive or negative evaluation of a particular behaviour. University students’ attitudes toward physical exercise depend on their perceptions of the benefits of physical exercise and whether they find the process enjoyable. On the other hand, subjective norms involve the influence of the surrounding environment on an individual’s behavioural intention, including the perceptions and expectations of the social circle of family members, friends, and classmates. If the people around university students favour physical exercise, their willingness to participate will significantly increase. Perceived behavioural control assesses an individual’s perception of the feasibility of performing specific behaviours, including the availability of resources such as time schedules, field facilities, and sports skills. When attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control work together, they shape an individual’s intentions towards healthy behaviours. University students pursuing healthy living are more inclined to explore healthy lifestyles actively [43]. In terms of attitude, they recognise the positive significance of physical exercise for the body and mind; in terms of subjective norms, they are driven by the atmosphere of campus and dormitory peers’ enthusiasm for sports; and in terms of perceived behavioural control, the adequate conditions provided by the university also offer support for their participation in physical exercise. These factors contribute to university students’ high intention to participate in physical exercise [44], and the probability of them performing physical exercise will significantly increase.

Emotions have an organisational and motivational role in human activity [45], and practical emotional management ability may correlate with motivation for physical exercise in university students. Based on the TPB, practical emotional management ability can reshape university students’ attitudes towards physical exercise. When university students can effectively regulate their emotions, they are more likely to experience positive emotional feedback from physical exercise, such as increased vigour and stress release, thus reinforcing positive attitudes towards physical exercise. In a positive emotional state, university students’ sense of perceived behavioural control is enhanced, and they tend to believe that they have the energy and ability to carry out physical exercise [33], which improves their motivation for physical exercise. Conversely, negative emotions inhibit bodily functions and reduce the efficiency of one’s activities, diminishing the human body’s motivation to engage in physical exercise. Studies have shown that emotional state has a key impact on the effect of physical exercise, and the emotional performance during each exercise session directly determines the degree of completion of the exercise task. Failure to properly regulate the emotional response during physical exercise may lead to poor physical exercise habits in the long term [46]. In addition, positive emotions can help stimulate university students’ interest in physical exercise and promote their habit of physical exercise, whilst negative emotions can hinder the formation of university students’ exercise habits. Suppose the critical factor of emotional management ability is neglected in the long term. In that case, it will not only prompt university students to develop adverse physical exercise habits, but also may cause damage to their physical and mental health. Therefore, practical emotional management ability positively affects university students’ motivation to participate in physical exercise. In addition, numerous studies have demonstrated that appropriate physical exercise can benefit sleep quality. Based on this, Hypothesis 2 is proposed: physical exercise mediates the effect of emotional management ability on the sleep quality of university students.

The mediating role of electronic health literacy

Electronic health literacy is accessing, understanding, and evaluating health information through electronic media to make sound health decisions [47]. The HBM posits that individuals can adopt health approaches to promote physical and mental health. Studies show a positive correlation between electronic health literacy and health-related quality of life [34]. Electronic health literacy is also strongly linked to health-promoting behaviours [48], and good sleep quality is one of the most important indicators for assessing quality of life and health behaviours. Therefore, high levels of electronic health literacy may potentially impact the sleep quality of university students. In addition, studies have shown a statistically significant positive correlation between electronic health literacy and emotion regulation (p < 0.01), implying that the higher the electronic health literacy, the stronger university students’ ability in emotion self-regulation [49]; conversely, those with lower electronic health literacy have relatively weaker emotion regulation. Emotion regulation is an integral component of emotional management ability [50]. Based on this, Hypothesis 3 is proposed: electronic health literacy mediates the process of emotional management ability affecting sleep quality among university students.

Chain mediation of physical exercise and electronic health literacy

University students who adhere to physical exercise have statistically significantly higher electronic health literacy levels than those who do not (p < 0.01) [31]. This group exhibits stronger self-health management awareness and ability. They are more inclined to use exercise apps to guide their physical exercise. This promotes the practical application and transformation of electronic health knowledge, further improving health literacy [51]. In addition, studies have demonstrated that positive emotions can stimulate human activity motivation, and improving emotional management ability can influence engagement in physical exercise. Meanwhile, electronic health literacy can positively predict health-related quality of life, and university students with higher electronic health literacy can utilise electronic devices to obtain scientific knowledge about sleep. Thus, they can better adjust their sleep habits and improve their sleep quality effectively. Based on the above analyses, this study proposes Hypothesis 4: Physical exercise and electronic health literacy have a chain-mediating effect between emotional management ability and sleep quality among university students. Methods

Data sources

This study utilised the CPAHLS-CS 2022 data, designed to collect high-quality microdata representing Chinese university students’ physical exercise and health behaviours, analyse the intersection of physical exercise and health in China, and promote interdisciplinary research on university student health. The CPAHLS-CS has been used in multiple studies to examine the health status of Chinese university students [49, 52–61]. The respondents selected for this study were students enrolled in general institutions of higher education in mainland China, and the list of general institutions of higher education was based on the Ministry of Education’s ‘National List of General Institutions of Higher Education (as of 31 May 2022)’. A stratified random sample of students was surveyed at four application-oriented universities: Nanjing Xiaozhuang University, Yangzhou University, Shangqiu University, and Yangzhou Polytechnic College. These universities are committed to constructing disciplinary and professional programs aligned with their respective regions’ economic and social development needs. A shared focus is the integration of practical application within the teaching process, to cultivate highly qualified, application-oriented graduates who possess strong social adaptability and competitiveness, reflecting a crucial aspect of China’s diversified higher education landscape [62, 63]. A minimum sample size of 1107 was determined using the sample size calculator provided by Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (the formula used to calculate the minimum sample size is presented below). Variable measurement

Emotional management ability

The scale used in this study was developed by Scott et al. based on the theory proposed by Mayer and Salovey in 1990 [64], and is a widely recognised measurement tool in this field. The scale comprises 33 items designed to assess an individual’s ability to perceive, understand, express, regulate, and utilise their own and others’ emotions. The scale is divided into four dimensions, and responses are rated on a five-point Likert scale, with participants selecting numbers based on their level of agreement (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Items 5, 28, and 33 are reverse-scored. Higher total scores indicate a higher level of emotional intelligence. The Chinese version of the scale was translated and validated by Wang at South China Normal University, and its internal consistency coefficient was 0.83 [65], indicating good reliability. This scale has been widely used internationally and has demonstrated high reliability and validity [66, 67], therefore, it was selected as the research instrument for this study.

Sleep quality

The PSQI [68], developed by Buysse et al. in 1989, was used to measure the sleep quality of university students. The PSQI consists of 19 self-rated and 5 other-rated items, with the 5 other-rated items and one self-rated item not contributing to the scoring. The 18 self-rated items used in scoring comprise seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored on a scale of 0 to 3, with a total score ranging from 0 to 21. Based on the PSQI scores, sleep quality among university students was categorized as ‘good’ (combining ‘very good’ and ‘fairly good,’ total score ≤ 8), ‘average’ (9 ≤ total score < 16), or ‘poor’ (total score ≥ 17), with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality [60]. Previous studies have demonstrated high reliability for the PSQI when used with Chinese university students, with an overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.87 and a split-half reliability coefficient of 0.87 for scale items [69].

Physical exercise

In this study, we employed the PARS-3, compiled by the Japanese scholar Gongyu Hashimoto and adapted by Liang et al. This instrument comprehensively assesses the level of physical exercise among university students, considering three dimensions: intensity of physical exercise, duration of each exercise session, and frequency of physical exercise. In the questionnaire, each item is rated on a five-point scale, ranging from ‘1’ (never participate in physical exercise) to ‘5’ (often participate in physical exercise). Higher scores indicate a greater amount of physical exercise and reflect physical exercise behaviour, which, to some extent, represents university students’ sports participation at a given time. The scale score is calculated as the product of the intensity of physical exercise, the length of a single exercise session (minus a benchmark value of 1), and the frequency of physical exercise. Physical exercise can be classified into three levels based on the total score: ≤ 19 is considered low physical exercise, between 20 and 42 is considered moderate physical exercise, and a score ≥ 43 is considered high physical exercise [70]. It is worth noting that, in some versions, the threshold for low physical exercise is set at 17 points. The PARS-3 can effectively reflect the sports participation status of university student groups, and its measurement results can be used as an essential basis for assessing the amount of physical exercise. The PARS-3 has been validated for reliability and validity in China, with a test-retest reliability of 0.820 [71].

Electronic health literacy

The eHEALS, developed by Norman et al. [72], was used in this study. The Chinese version of the eHEALS was translated and adapted by Guo’s research team [73]. The eHEALS assesses three dimensions: application, judgment, and decision-making ability related to online health information and services. The instrument contains eight items, which primarily determine university students’ abilities in the following three dimensions: (1) application ability related to online health information and services (items 1–5); (2) judgement ability related to online health information and services (items 6–7); and (3) decision-making ability related to online health information and services (item 8). A five-point Likert scale was used, with responses ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ and scores ranging from 1 to 5. Total scores range from 8 to 40, with higher scores reflecting better electronic health literacy levels. Previous studies have demonstrated high reliability and validity for the eHEALS when used among Chinese university students, with an internal consistency reliability of 0.87 and Cronbach’s α coefficients ranging from 0.81 to 0.86 for the three dimensions [73, 74], indicating good measurement reliability.

Control variables

The control variables for this study included demographic variables, such as smoking and drinking behaviour. The demographic variables were gender (1 = male, 2 = female) and grade level. Following the World Health Organization (WHO) definitions, smoking behaviour was categorized as ‘never smoked,’ ‘occasional smoking,’ and ‘frequent smoking’ for analysis [75]. University students’ drinking behaviour was also analysed according to the frequency of intoxication within the past month, categorized as ‘never drank,’ ‘drank without getting drunk,’ and ‘drank and got drunk’ [76].

Statistical analysis

This study used SPSS 26.0, its PROCESS macro, and Microsoft Excel to process and analyse the data. The analysis steps were as follows: (1) Microsoft Excel was used to carry out initial data processing of the data collected through the Questionnaire Star platform, including the recoding or removal of incomplete or invalid data. (2) Common method bias was assessed. Harman’s single-factor test was used to determine whether statistically significant standard method bias was present. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) included variables such as emotional management ability, sleep quality, physical exercise, and electronic health literacy. The EFA results revealed five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The variance explained by the first factor was 24.146%. Following Podsakoff et al.‘s criterion, this value is statistically significantly lower than the cut-off of 40%, indicating that common method bias was not a statistically significant concern in the measurement data, and that the results have good reliability. (3) Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted. Basic statistics, such as frequencies, means, and standard deviations, were calculated for the demographic characteristics of the study participants and each study variable to characterize the sample. Effect sizes (η2) ranged from 0 to 1, with 0.01 representing a small effect, 0.06 a medium effect, and 0.14 a significant effect, according to Cohen’s criteria [49]. In addition, according to Wei and Hu’s effect size benchmark system, benchmarks for the effect size index r are 0.07, 0.10, 0.14, and 0.30, respectively represent the small, typical (medium), large, and very large cut-offs; the same are also applicable for comparable r-family measures (e.g. Cramer’s V) [77–79]. (4) Correlation analyses between variables were conducted. Pearson correlation analysis examined the relationships between university students’ emotional management ability, sleep quality, physical exercise, and electronic health literacy. (5) Mediation analyses were conducted to test the mediating effects of physical exercise and electronic health literacy in the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality. Specifically, the process macro, Model 6, was used with a 95% confidence interval and 5000 bootstrap samples to analyse the transmission mechanism.

Discussion

This study employed a chain mediation model to comprehensively investigate the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality in college students, simultaneously validating the mediating roles of physical exercise and e-health literacy. The results provided robust support for Hypotheses 1, 2, 3, and 4. The study revealed a statistically significant negative correlation between emotional management ability and sleep quality. This finding warrants careful interpretation: the sleep quality assessment tool used in this research employed a scoring system where higher scores indicated poorer sleep quality. Consequently, the observed negative correlation is entirely consistent and valid, signifying that increases in college students’ emotional management ability are associated with statistically significantly improved sleep quality, as evidenced by lower scores. Moreover, the analysis underscored the crucial mediating roles of physical exercise and electronic health literacy in the pathway through which emotional management ability influences sleep quality. This highlights the significance of individual health behaviours and college students’ capacity to access and utilize health information. By comprehensively examining the interplay between emotional management ability, physical exercise, electronic health literacy, and sleep quality, this study offers a strong scientific basis for developing targeted interventions to enhance the sleep quality of college students.

The effect of emotional management ability on sleep quality of university students

This study’s initial hypothesis (Hypothesis 1) posited a positive association between emotional management ability and the sleep quality of college students; specifically, that stronger emotional management ability would correspond to better sleep. The research findings directly support this hypothesis, revealing a statistically significant negative correlation between these two variables.

Emotional management ability is widely acknowledged as crucial for managing stress, regulating emotions, and maintaining psychological well-being [84]. Prior research has consistently demonstrated the beneficial impact of robust emotional management ability on sleep quality, emphasizing its role in alleviating negative emotions like anxiety and depression, and subsequently, improving sleep [85, 86]. The present study’s results further validate this perspective, indicating a statistically significant link between enhanced emotional management ability and superior sleep quality. This relationship also reflects the internal mechanisms of emotional regulation, as students with well-developed emotional management ability are better equipped to identify and effectively address negative emotions, such as those stemming from academic pressures or interpersonal conflicts [36, 87]. Consequently, this minimizes the disruptive influence of such emotions on sleep. These students are also more likely to adopt proactive coping strategies, including mindfulness and seeking support [87, 88], rather than falling into passive thought patterns that could exacerbate psychological burden and hinder a favourable sleep environment. The findings of this study underscore the importance of cultivating emotional management ability in college students to enhance sleep quality, and lend indirect support to the “ABC Theory of Emotion,” which proposes that by modifying how individuals perceive and evaluate events, they can effectively regulate their emotions and, ultimately, promote psychological health [38]. Furthermore, the results are consistent with research by Chen et al., who found that positive emotional states, like forgiveness, can improve sleep by lowering physiological arousal [86]. Similarly, Gross’s model of emotion regulation supports this notion, as effective emotional management can diminish the interference of pre-sleep emotional fluctuations with the physiological mechanisms governing sleep.

In this analysis, the study revealed statistically significant disparities among university students of varying genders and grades across key variables, including sleep quality, physical exercise, emotional management ability, and electronic health literacy. Sleep quality varied statistically significantly between male and female student populations. Furthermore, students in different years of study demonstrated differing characteristics in emotional management ability and sleep quality, likely influenced by the unique academic stages and challenges they encountered. These findings underscore the importance of considering these group-specific differences when designing interventions to enhance university students’ sleep quality. Interventions tailored to male students may need to address their specific challenges, such as a propensity for late-night activities and increased engagement in outdoor pursuits, necessitating customized approaches. Similarly, targeted strategies may be required for students in different academic years. For instance, interventions for Sophomores, who may be navigating major selection and escalating academic pressures, could benefit from emphasizing emotional support and stress management techniques.

Notably, our findings differ somewhat from specific Western research, a variance potentially stemming from the moderating influence of cultural context [89]. Within China’s collectivist cultural framework, a student facing academic stress might prioritize maintaining composure, avoiding external displays of frustration to preserve group harmony [90]. This could lead them to employ cognitive reappraisal [91], positively reframing the academic challenge, or utilize meditation or mindful breathing to lessen its emotional impact, rather than outwardly expressing frustration. While suppressing negative emotions can be an unhelpful strategy in some instances, within specific Chinese contexts, it may maintain social harmony and be associated with positive outcomes [92]. This inclination toward more internalized emotional management, rooted in Confucian values of self-control, may influence how emotional management affects sleep quality in Chinese college students.

The study’s findings highlight the critical role of emotional management ability in influencing the sleep quality of college students, emphasizing its positive effects. Targeted interventions to improve emotional management ability may better equip university students to cope with the stresses and challenges of academic and social life. This could reduce the disruptive influence of negative emotions on sleep quality, potentially contributing to improvements in their sleep patterns. However, it must be stressed that the cross-sectional design of this study precludes any confirmation of causality. Future longitudinal research will be vital in further elucidating the potential impact of emotional management ability on sleep quality. Furthermore, we recommend carefully considering cultural factors when developing and implementing mental health education for college students. Drawing upon traditional Chinese wisdom—for example, integrating practices such as Tai Chi and Ba duan jin into emotional management training [93]—and simultaneously developing culturally sensitive emotional expression channels can promote individual well-being while respecting collectivist values. Such culturally adapted interventions may prove more effective than simply transplanting Western models.

The mediating role of physical exercise between emotional management ability and sleep quality among university students

This study found that physical exercise mediated the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality among university students, supporting Hypothesis 2 (physical exercise mediates the effect of emotional management ability on sleep quality among university students). This finding is consistent with the TPB, which posits that an individual’s emotional state influences their attitudes and behaviours toward physical exercise, which, in turn, affects their choice of health behaviours [44]. Specifically, emotional management ability was statistically significantly and positively correlated with physical exercise, whereas physical exercise was statistically significantly and negatively correlated with sleep quality. This suggests that university students with weaker emotional management ability may participate in physical exercise less frequently or at a lower intensity, which, in turn, negatively affects their sleep quality. Conversely, individuals with stronger emotional management ability are more likely to participate in physical exercise, indirectly promoting sleep quality by improving physical health, relieving stress, and enhancing emotional states.

Physical exercise is not only related to sleep quality through physiological pathways, such as increasing physical health and improving cardiovascular function, but also may be indirectly related to better sleep through reducing negative emotions such as anxiety and depression [25, 46]. Individuals with stronger emotional management ability are more likely to experience exercise’s mood-boosting effects, including endorphin release and subsequent feelings of well-being and relaxation [94]. This positive reinforcement encourages more consistent exercise, leading to improved sleep. Furthermore, consistent physical exercise helps regulate the circadian rhythm, promoting more consistent sleep-wake cycles and increased sleep efficiency [95]. Thus, through this synergy of physiological and psychological benefits, and by mediating the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality, exercise plays a vital role in improving the overall well-being of university students.

The mediating role of electronic health literacy between emotional management ability and sleep quality among university students

This study found that electronic health literacy mediated the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality, supporting Hypothesis 3 (electronic health literacy mediates the process of emotional management ability affecting sleep quality among university students). The results indicated that emotional management ability was positively correlated with electronic health literacy [49], and electronic health literacy was also positively associated with sleep quality. This suggests that university students with higher emotional management ability are more likely to access health information through electronic media and are better able to understand, evaluate, and apply this information effectively. This, in turn, leads to more informed health decision-making, which positively affects their sleep quality. This finding aligns with the research of Norman and Skinner [72], who found that increased electronic health literacy helps university students acquire scientific knowledge about sleep and indirectly contributes to the optimisation of sleep quality through improved health behaviours.

Furthermore, electronic health literacy promotes healthier sleep patterns among university students by leveraging key constructs from the HBM [48], strengthening both “perceived benefits” and “self-efficacy” to drive positive changes in health behaviours. With increased electronic health literacy, students can more readily access and understand information regarding the negative consequences of poor sleep and the benefits of quality sleep, thereby enhancing their motivation to improve their sleep habits. This increased motivation is further reinforced by the real-time feedback provided through sleep-monitoring apps and other digital health tools, boosting their confidence in regulating their sleep behaviours [96]. Indeed, empirical evidence shows that students who use digital health tools for sleep self-management exhibit significantly lower PSQI scores, indicating better sleep quality than those who do not [97]. As Norman and Skinner highlight [72], electronic health literacy facilitates acquiring and comprehending evidence-based information on sleep hygiene, including optimal sleep schedules, bedtime routines, and environmental factors conducive to restful sleep [98]. It also enhances the ability to critically evaluate the credibility and accuracy of online health information, enabling individuals to differentiate between reliable sources and misinformation about sleep disorders and treatments [93], ultimately improving individual sleep quality. The mediating role of electronic health literacy between emotional management ability and sleep quality highlights its importance in university students’ health management.

Chain mediation between physical exercise and electronic health literacy in emotional management ability and sleep quality among university students

This study found that physical exercise and electronic health literacy played a chain-mediating role in the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality among university students. This chain-mediating effect was statistically significant, supporting Hypothesis 4. Specifically, emotional management ability statistically significantly and positively affected physical exercise, indicating that university students with better emotional management ability are more inclined to participate in physical exercise. This may be because they can better regulate their emotions and reduce anxiety and stress, thus enhancing their motivation and willingness to engage in physical exercise. According to the TPB, an individual’s emotional state influences their attitudes and behaviours towards physical exercise, which, in turn, affects their choice of health behaviours [44]. Therefore, individuals with better emotional management ability are more likely to develop positive attitudes toward physical exercise and translate those attitudes into action.

In addition, physical exercise had a statistically significant positive effect on electronic health literacy. Physical exercise improves physical health and enhances an individual’s awareness of health management. Some studies have shown that university students who engage in physical exercise are more inclined to use electronic health tools, such as exercise apps, to guide their health behaviours [51]. This is consistent with the findings of this study, suggesting that physical exercise can help university students better understand and apply electronic health information, thus enhancing their electronic health literacy. Electronic health literacy, in turn, had a statistically significant positive effect on sleep quality. A high level of electronic health literacy may enables individuals to better acquire, understand, and apply scientific knowledge about sleep, effectively adjusting their sleep habits and improving their sleep quality [72]. This result suggests that the emotional management ability of university students not only directly affects sleep quality but also indirectly affects sleep quality by promoting physical exercise and enhancing electronic health literacy. Specifically, university students with stronger emotional management ability were more inclined to participate in physical exercise, which, in turn, further contributed to the improvement of their sleep quality through enhanced electronic health literacy.

While the effect size of this chain mediation pathway is relatively small, its statistical significance suggests the mechanism’s existence [99, 100]. The modest effect size likely reflects a confluence of factors, including inherent limitations in the study design, the intricate interplay of variables, and the moderating influence of specific cultural emotion-management strategies. Theoretically, this subtle effect validates the potential by which emotional management ability indirectly shapes sleep quality via behaviour change and information acquisition, offering fresh insight into the intricate routes of health behaviours among college students. Practically speaking, this limited effect size implies that interventions relying solely on this pathway might produce modest results, emphasizing the need to incorporate other, more potent influences. Future research could explore how to optimize the translational efficiency of each step along this pathway, and potential differences in this mechanism across various student subgroups.

Limitations

Although this study yielded valuable findings, it is essential to acknowledge its limitations. (1) Regarding data collection, this study employed a cross-sectional survey design, which limits the ability to determine causal relationships between the variables and capture the study indicators’ dynamic characteristics over time. Future studies could consider using a longitudinal study design to explore the causal mechanisms between emotional management ability, physical exercise, electronic health literacy, and sleep quality more thoroughly. (2) Regarding sample representativeness and characteristics, the respondents in this study primarily comprised students from colleges and universities in East and Central China, which limits the geographical diversity of the sample. Furthermore, this study did not analyse the potential impact of gender and grade level on the results. Future research could expand the sampling scope to include more university students from different regions to improve the findings’ external validity and further explore the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality among university students of different genders and grades. (3) Regarding variable selection, this study only examined the mediating effects of physical exercise and electronic health literacy. Subsequent studies could introduce other potential mediating variables, such as mental toughness and social support, to more comprehensively elucidate the mechanisms underlying the relationship between emotional management ability and sleep quality.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study suggests that emotional management ability and sleep quality are directly and indirectly associated among university students in China’s application-oriented institutions. The research highlights a statistically significant correlation between these factors, with potential pathways identified through increased physical exercise and enhanced electronic health literacy. The findings suggest that physical exercise and electronic health literacy may act as essential mediators in the relationship; however, the study’s cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causality.
