Plasma multi-omics and machine learning reveal predictive biomarkers for type 2 diabetes and retinopathy in Qatar biobank cohort

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes (T2D) and its vascular complications, including diabetic retinopathy (DR), are escalating in prevalence globally, with disproportionately high prevalence in Middle Eastern populations, where genetic predispositions and lifestyle factors intersect. Early detection and precise risk stratification remain critical challenges in this region. We hypothesised that an integrated plasma multi-omics profile; comprising microRNA, mRNA, and protein biomarkers, could accurately distinguish individuals with T2D and its complications in a Middle Eastern cohort.MethodsA candidate panel of mRNA and protein biomarkers identified from in vitro hyperglycaemia models, along with a vascular microRNA signature previously defined in an Australian cohort, was evaluated. These multiomic biomarkers were profiled in 962 individuals (492 controls, 434 T2D and 36 T2D with DR) from the Qatar Biobank (QBB). The miR-29c–PROM1 axis and associated proteins represent promising biomarkers for early detection and targeted intervention. Background

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a complex metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance, pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, and chronic hyperglycaemia. Its prevalence has risen dramatically worldwide and is projected to reach 783 million individuals by 2045, posing a substantial burden on healthcare systems and societies alike [1]. Among its complications, diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of preventable blindness and affects approximately one-third of individuals with diabetes [2]. Early identification of individuals at risk for T2D and its vascular complications remains a key challenge in reducing disease burden.

The Middle Eastern region, including countries such as Qatar, faces a disproportionately high prevalence of T2D driven by both genetic susceptibility and rapid lifestyle transitions [3, 4]. Despite this, most genomic and biomarker discovery studies have focused on European ancestry populations, limiting the generalisability of findings to other ethnic groups [5]. The integration of multi-omics profiling offers a powerful approach to uncover molecular pathways and improve disease prediction, particularly when applied to underrepresented populations [6].

Circulating microRNAs (miRNAs) are small, non-coding RNA molecules that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. Their stability in blood and tissue specificity makes them attractive candidates for biomarker discovery. Several miRNAs, including miR-29, miR-126, and miR-122, have been linked to glucose homeostasis, insulin resistance, and vascular dysfunction [7–9]. In parallel, gene expression profiling of circulating mRNAs and plasma proteomics have provided additional insights into the mechanisms of metabolic and microvascular disease progression [10, 11]. Although microRNAs are known to be more stable in circulation than mRNAs, dead, dying, or even stressed endothelial cells lining the vasculature release cellular contents into the blood. Identifying mRNAs linked to endothelial cell damage under high glucose conditions could offer valuable insights into monitoring T2D progression.

Additionally, proteomics offers a broader perspective on the complex interplay of biological interactions and disease progression. Numerous studies have employed proteomics to identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for various diseases, including T2D and DR [12, 13]. For example, differential expression of proteins such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) has been linked to DR, suggesting their roles in disease progression [14].

Machine learning (ML) methods provide a robust framework for integrating complex datasets and identifying patterns that may not be captured by traditional statistical approaches. When applied to multi-omics data, ML can enhance risk stratification and support the development of clinically actionable biomarkers. Importantly, interpretable ML approaches such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) enable biological validation by quantifying the relative contribution of each feature to the model’s predictions [15].

In this study, we combined in vitro discovery and in vivo validation to develop a multi-omics biomarker panel for T2D and DR in a well-characterised cohort from the Qatar Biobank (QBB). Large-scale biobanks, such as the QBB, provide an unprecedented resource for such comprehensive studies wherein clinical parameters, genomic data, and anthropometric measurements, can be leveraged to improve our understanding of T2D progression [16, 17]. We profiled candidate miRNAs, mRNAs, and plasma proteins identified from endothelial cells exposed to sustained hyperglycaemia and validated their predictive value using machine learning in 962 individuals with and without T2D and DR. Our aim was to identify circulating biomarker signatures with predictive potential and mechanistic relevance, tailored to the unique genetic and environmental context of Middle Eastern populations.

Our study is the first multi-omics (circulating miRNA, mRNA, and protein) validation in a Middle Eastern cohort. By leveraging the extensive data available from the QBB, we seek to shed light on the underlying molecular mechanisms of T2D and DR, thereby providing a foundation for developing novel diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. This integrated approach not only enhances our understanding of the disease but also paves the way for personalized medicine tailored to the specific genetic and environmental contexts of the Middle Eastern population.

Methods

Study cohort and phenotyping

This study included 962 Qatari participants (492 controls, 434 T2D patients, and 36 T2D with DR) from the Qatar Biobank (QBB), selected based on clinical, biochemical, and ophthalmological criteria. Diagnosis of T2D was confirmed by self-reported physician diagnosis and HbA1c levels, while DR status was determined through retinal imaging reviewed by a qualified ophthalmologist.

Biomarker discovery and validation strategy

The biomarker panel was hypothesis‑driven. miRNAs were drawn from prior vascular/islet biomarker work; mRNAs (PROM1, HSPB1/2, CKM) were identified from endothelial cells under sustained hyperglycemia; and the 23 proteins were selected from discovery analyses and literature on vascular/retinopathy pathways (see Discovery Analyses & supplementary Methods).

Plasma-enriched microRNAs were measured using TaqMan OpenArray profiling, and the panel of candidate mRNAs (PROM1, HSPB1, HSPB2, CKM) was quantified using qPCR. Protein abundance for 23 analytes was measured using Luminex xMAP multiplex immunoassays. All data underwent quality control, normalization, and batch correction prior to downstream analysis.

Data integration and statistical analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson’s coefficient, and PCA was used for dimensionality reduction.

To integrate multi-omics data, machine learning models were trained using normalized levels of 44 miRNAs, 4 mRNAs, and 23 proteins to predict T2D and DR risk. Model performance was assessed using ROC curve analysis with balanced class accuracies. To minimize overfitting, we used tenfold stratified cross‑validation, feature de‑correlation (Pearson r > 0.3), and bootstrapping. We also evaluated performance on a held‑out test split. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were calculated to interpret feature contributions. Penalized logistic regression (LASSO) with bootstrapping was applied to identify biomarker signatures associated with DR in T2D patients.

Ethics approval

All procedures were approved by the institutional review boards of Qatar Biobank, Qatar Precision Health Institute, and Sidra Medicine. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Supplementary methods

Detailed protocols for cell culture, proteomics, RNA isolation, qPCR, and machine learning model development are provided in Additional file 1.

Results

Discovery analyses

We used a previously established method to isolate and maintain human endothelial cells in vitro [18, 19]. Human endothelial cells were exposed to different concentrations of D-glucose (1, 2, 5 and 10 mg/mL) for 7 days. They showed impaired capacity to populate (heal) an in vitro wound (scratch) with increasing concentrations of glucose (see supplementary Fig. 1, Additional file 2). We then assessed the expression of proteins in these endothelial cells using 2D gel electrophoresis followed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) protein identification (see supplementary Fig. 2, Additional file 3). Identified proteins (their scores) and molecular weights are presented in supplementary Table 1, Additional file 4 and supplementary Table 2, Additional file 5. Key proteins identified through LC–MS/MS (with protein score ≥ 40) were HSPB1, TCF20, TCP, VINC, SMTN, MACF1, NEBL, PRV, VIM, ATM, and CKM. Culture media collected after seven days of in vitro hyperglycemic conditions (2mg/ml and 5mg/ml D-glucose) were taken for the study. The supernatant media of cultured endothelial cells exposed to hyperglycemic conditions had higher transcript abundance of HSPB1, HSPB2, CKM and PROM1 genes and were included as they may serve to be important indicators of sustained (7-day) high-glucose-mediated endothelial cell damage. All microRNAs were selected through previous studies of microRNA biomarkers of vascular damage (supplementary Table 3, Additional file 6). The four mRNAs (PROM1, HSPB1/2, CKM) were identified from endothelial cells under sustained hyperglycemia; and the 23 proteins were selected from discovery analyses and literature on vascular/retinopathy pathways (see supplementary Methods). The multi-omics biomarker signature identified from these discovery analyses were validated in a Middle Eastern cohort of 962 individuals from the QBB.

The expression profiles of miRNAs were compared between T2D patient cohorts with or without retinopathy against the control group in order to elucidate differential expression patterns of the selected miRNAs, ensuring that the calculation only included samples that met the quality filters and showed miRNA expression. Analysis of the miRNA expression unveiled differences in the expression levels of miRNAs in the control cohort and individuals with diabetes, with and without DR, whereby six miRNAs were found to be upregulated and twelve downregulated in patients with diabetes. PROM1 expression shows association with T2D

We assessed the relevance of the chosen set of genes as T2D risk factors in our cohort. Gene Expression patterns of our target gene panel strongly stratify patients from controls. The normalized expression of the four genes was used to check if their gene expression patterns can stratify patients from control samples and could be classified as circulating biomarkers of T2D and T2D-DR. The heatmap (Fig. 1E) provides insights into expression levels of the four genes. It shows a distinct clustering pattern, with CKM and HSPB1 displaying a closer resemblance in their expression. At the same time, PROM1 and HSPB2 show inverse patterns of expression, suggesting that these genes may characterize different conditions of the disease. A specific cluster (outlined in yellow) shows high PROM1 expression while relatively lower expression is observed for other genes in a particular group of samples, which are mostly comprised of T2D individulas. This suggests a consistent difference in gene expression between T2D and control samples, indicating these genes could serve as potential biomarkers for T2D. The distinct expression patterns for these genes between groups suggest that they might play a role in the pathogenesis of T2D or respond to its progression.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to explore patterns in gene expression profiles across diabetic and non-diabetic individuals. The first two principal components—PC1 and PC2—explained a substantial proportion of the total variance, accounting for 40.9% and 30.3%, respectively (Fig. 1F). While the PCA biplot does not show sharply defined clusters, it does reveal a discernible spatial organization: diabetic samples (T2D and T2D-DR, shown in yellow and blue) tend to form a partially overlapping but distinguishable group relative to the healthy controls (grey), which are more tightly clustered and positioned away from the patient samples (Supplementary Fig. 4, Additional file 8).

This distribution suggests a degree of separation in the transcriptional profiles of these four genes for healthy versus diabetic individuals. PC3 and PC4 contribute an additional 17.2% and 11.5% of variance, respectively, indicating that a meaningful proportion of group-specific variation may reside beyond the first two components.

Further, the vector for PROM1 points towards the cluster predominantly composed of yellow points, representing the T2D samples. The direction and proximity of the PROM1 vector to the T2D cluster indicate a strong positive association between PROM1 expression and T2D, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for T2D. The vectors for genes HSPB1, HSPB2, and CKM are associated with different sample groups, indicating that their expression patterns are specific to certain conditions or controls, potentially making them useful in distinguishing between different biological states or conditions of the disease.

Regulatory role of miR-29c in PROM1 Expression and a potential biomarker for T2D

In the pursuit to unravel the molecular intricacies of T2D and T2D-DR, our study has identified a pivotal regulatory element: miR-29c. This microRNA emerges as the most significantly downregulated in patients with T2D and T2D-DR, spotlighting its potential role in the pathogenesis of these conditions. Interestingly, we identified a conserved target site on the PROM1 gene that is predicted to be targeted by miR-29c (Fig. 2A). This conserved site indicates a strong evolutionary pressure to maintain this interaction and suggests that miR-29c may bind to this specific site on PROM1, potentially regulating its expression. To further explore this potential regulatory relationship, we analyzed the expression patterns of miR-29c, PROM1, and two other miRNAs (miR-142-3p and miR30-3p) that were also found to have target sites within the PROM1 gene (Fig. 2B). The heatmap depicts the gene expression correlation across our samples and includes the panel of four genes—HSPB1, HSPB2, CKM, and PROM1- alongside the three miRNAs investigated in this study. Notably, a statistically significant negative correlation exists between miR-29c and PROM1 expression. However, unlike miR-29c, no significant correlations exist between PROM1 and miR-142-3p or miR30-3p expression levels. The significant negative correlation between miR-29c and PROM1 expression strongly indicates the inhibitory effect of miR-29c on PROM1. In contrast, the interactions between miR142-3p and miR-30-3p with PROM1 do not show a significant correlation, suggesting that their regulatory impact, if any, is less pronounced than that of miR-29c. We employed decision tree analysis to gain deeper insights into the regulatory hierarchy for discriminating between T2D and DR progression (Fig. 2C). This analysis focused on miR-29c and the four mRNAs evaluated in this study. The decision tree analysis revealed miR-29c as the key decision maker within this regulatory network. This signifies that miR-29c could play a central role in regulating the expression of PROM1 and other genes investigated. Furthermore, the analysis indicates that downregulation of miR-29c is associated with a higher predisposition to developing the T2D phenotype. This finding is consistent with our observations that miR-29c expression was significantly reduced in T2D and DR patients compared to healthy controls.

Building on our results from differential and co-expression analysis, the decision tree analysis further strengthens the evidence of a pivotal role for miR-29c in the miR-29c-PROM1 regulatory axis, with miR-29c acting upstream of PROM1. Thus, this reinforces the idea of the critical involvement of miR-29c in the regulatory processes underlying T2D pathogenesis. The role of miR-29c as a key decision maker and its upstream regulation of PROM1 highlight its importance as a potential target for therapeutic interventions.

Plasma protein signature in T2D

We assessed the correlations between protein abundances using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (Fig. 2D). This analysis identified blocks of proteins that are positively correlated (r > 0) or negatively correlated (r < 0) in their abundance across our cohort. It also groups similar proteins based on hierarchical clustering. These analyses identified that TNF and EGF had the highest degrees of positive correlation with each other, indicating the co-expression of these proteins and their interconnected roles within the same or similar pathways, which may pertain to T2D and DR pathogenesis (e.g., inflammation pathway).

A total of sixteen different ML models, including a baseline dummy classifier, were trained using the PyCaret library [21], and hyperparameter tuning was applied to optimize their performance. Each model utilized the normalized expression levels of 44 miRNAs, four mRNAs, and 23 proteins as input features. Key evaluation metrics included the Area Under the Curve (AUC), Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and F1 score, providing a comprehensive assessment of model performance (Table 4). To interpret model predictions and identify key molecular contributors, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [15], which quantify the impact of each feature on model output (Fig. 3B). Features with positive SHAP values increase the probability of T2D, serving as risk enhancers, while features with negative SHAP values decrease this probability, functioning as protective factors. Notably, miR-29c consistently showed negative SHAP values, indicating that higher expression reduced T2D risk. This supports its hypothesized regulatory role in mitigating vascular and metabolic stress, potentially via downregulation of PROM1. In contrast, PROM1 tends to exhibit higher positive SHAP values, meaning that its increased expression correlates with a higher risk of T2D, while lower PROM1 levels contribute to a reduced risk. Additional contributors such as LEP and IL6R also showed positive SHAP values, implicating them in pro-inflammatory or metabolic pathways associated with T2D. This SHAP analysis provides valuable insights into the complex roles played by these molecules in T2D, identifying potential protective or harmful biomarkers that may serve as therapeutic targets.

To identify biomarkers distinguishing T2D patients with and without DR, we applied penalized logistic regression (PLR) models using both leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) and fivefold cross-validation strategies. Across 1,000 bootstrap iterations, the LOOCV model selected a 12-marker panel—comprising 8 miRNAs, 3 mRNAs, and ANGPT2—with bootstrap frequencies ranging from 44 to 99.3%. The fivefold model yielded a 16-marker panel, which included all 12 markers from LOOCV and additionally identified CKM and three more miRNAs (Fig. 3C). Both models demonstrated strong predictive performance (AUC > 0.749), underscoring the robustness of the derived signature even with limited DR sample size.

To further validate the importance of key biomarkers, we compared RF model performance with and without miR-29c and PROM1 (Fig. 3D). Exclusion of these features led to a marked decline in multiple metrics—including AUC, recall, precision, MCC, and Cohen’s Kappa—highlighting their central role in model discrimination and stability. Inclusion of both features significantly improved the model’s sensitivity and precision, crucial for clinical utility where minimizing false negatives is vital. The higher Kappa score and preserved class balance (reflected in MCC) further affirm that miR-29c and PROM1 substantially contribute to prediction robustness and biological plausibility.

Together, these analyses establish that integrated multi-omics profiling, combined with interpretable ML, can accurately stratify individuals at risk of T2D and its complications, while also revealing biologically meaningful features for potential clinical translation.

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

This study integrates multi-omics profiling with machine learning to identify and validate circulating biomarkers predictive of T2D and its vascular complication, diabetic retinopathy, in a Middle Eastern population. Our findings demonstrate the power of combining miRNA, mRNA, and protein data to improve disease risk stratification, offering a translational framework for precision diagnostics.

MicroRNAs emerged as critical modulators in our analysis. Among these, miR-29c was the topmost downregulated miRNA in T2D and DR patients. Notably, miR-29c expression was negatively correlated with PROM1 mRNA levels, suggesting a regulatory axis implicated in disease progression. PROM1 is a well-established marker of progenitor and retinal cells and is implicated in retinal degeneration syndromes [22–24]. Our findings suggest that the miR-29c–PROM1 regulatory axis plays a pivotal role in the molecular pathogenesis of T2D and progression to DR, and may offer therapeutic potential. The regulatory association was further substantiated through correlation and decision tree analyses, which placed miR-29c at the top of a gene regulatory hierarchy influencing disease status.

Other differentially expressed miRNAs—including miR-122, miR-126, and miR-223-3p—have also been associated with key metabolic and vascular pathways. miR-122 is known to influence insulin resistance and lipid metabolism [25], while miR-126 was upregulated in both T2D and DR patients, aligning with earlier studies that linked elevated miR-126 to reduced survival rates in T2D patients [26]. Our previous work in zebrafish showed that overexpression of miR-223-3p induces hyperglycemia and retinal abnormalities, reinforcing its candidacy as a conserved biomarker of DR [20].

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that mRNAs encoding HSPB1, HSPB2, CKM, and PROM1 were significantly dysregulated in T2D and DR. Notably, HSPB1 expression was further reduced in DR patients, suggesting its potential role as a stage-specific biomarker. Together with miRNAs, these genes formed a multivariate expression pattern capable of distinguishing between healthy individuals and those with T2D or DR.

The proteomic arm of our study added another layer of insight. Among 23 proteins assayed, SELE, ANGPT2, and ICAM1 were significantly elevated in T2D and DR groups. These proteins are well-established markers of endothelial dysfunction and inflammation [27–31], hallmarks of diabetic vascular pathology. Conversely, PlGF and EGF showed reduced levels in DR, suggesting impaired compensatory angiogenic responses during advanced stages of disease [32–34]. ANGPT2, a known mediator of vascular remodeling and instability, was also significantly upregulated in DR, consistent with prior findings in proliferative diabetic retinopathy [35–39]. Its co-expression with VEGF and association with neovascularization underscores its relevance to DR pathophysiology [36]. Collectively, these protein markers complement the transcriptomic findings and help delineate molecular signatures associated with vascular complications.

Machine learning approaches provided a powerful framework for integrating multi-omics data. Of the 16 models tested, the Random Forest classifier achieved the best performance, with an AUC of 0.83 and F1 score of 0.78. Feature importance analysis using SHAP values revealed that miR-29c and PROM1 were among the top contributors to model performance—confirming their mechanistic relevance. Features with positive SHAP values (e.g., PROM1, IL6R, LEP, SELE, ANGPT2) increase the predicted probability of T2D and align with pathways of metabolic inflammation and endothelial activation. In particular, PROM1—a marker of retinal/progenitor cells implicated in retinal degeneration—shows higher expression in cases and positive SHAP, consistent with vascular/retinal stress seen in diabetes. Conversely, features with negative SHAP values (most notably miR-29c) decrease T2D risk; this is concordant with our results showing miR-29c down-regulation in T2D/DR and its inverse correlation with PROM1, supporting a regulatory axis whereby loss of miR-29c de-represses PROM1. Removing miR-29c and PROM1 led to drop in classification accuracy and AUC, underscoring their role in predictive performance. Penalized logistic regression models applied to stratify DR from T2D identified overlapping and distinct sets of features, with 12–16 biomarkers repeatedly surfacing across bootstrap iterations, further strengthening the robustness of our signature.

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

A major strength of our study is the use of a well-characterised biobank cohort from an underrepresented Middle Eastern population, enabling insights into population-specific disease mechanisms. The integration of three omics layers—circulating miRNAs, mRNAs, and proteins—combined with machine learning, allowed for nuanced biomarker discovery and risk modelling. The use of interpretable ML techniques (e.g. SHAP values) adds transparency to the predictive models and reveals mechanistic relationships among features. Moreover, although our models performed robustly in internal testing, external validation in independent cohorts is imperative for clinical translation. The directions of our top miRNA features are concordant with prior reports already cited in this manuscript, supporting biological plausibility: the miR-29 family in metabolism/metabolic disease [9]; circulating miR-126 alterations associated with vascular risk and mortality in T2D [8, 26]; miR-223 driving hyperglycaemia and retinal changes in our zebrafish work [20]; and miR-122 links to dyslipidaemia, hyperglycaemia, and T2D [25]. Consistent with these signals, our data show miR-29c is down-regulated and inversely correlated with PROM1, while SHAP highlights miR-29c as protective and PROM1 as risk-enhancing. Taken together, these literature-supported associations reinforce the mechanistic relevance of the biomarker panel identified here.

However, the study has limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. A key limitation of this study is the small number of participants with DR (n = 36), which reduces statistical power for DR-specific differential analyses and increases uncertainty around effect-size estimates after multiple-testing correction. As a result, modest associations may have gone undetected (higher risk of type II error), and estimates for DR stratifications should be interpreted with appropriate caution. To mitigate these constraints, we used penalized logistic regression with 1,000 bootstrap iterations and evaluated models under both leave-one-out and fivefold cross-validation schemes, alongside stratified cross-validation for outcome balance; together these approaches repeatedly identified a consistent biomarker panel that achieved AUC > 0.74 for distinguishing T2D-DR from T2D without DR. Even so, DR-focused findings should be considered preliminary pending replication.

Implications for clinicians and policymakers

The study findings have both biological and translational implications. First, the identification of miR-29c as a protective factor and PROM1 as a risk enhancer offers a mechanistic model of vascular and retinal damage in T2D, and could inform development of targeted interventions. For instance, restoring miR-29c levels could mitigate endothelial stress or retinal degeneration.

Second, the predictive utility of our biomarker panel suggests a pathway toward personalised screening and prevention. In Middle Eastern populations, elevated diabetes burden reflects the intersection of genetic susceptibility and rapid lifestyle transitions [3, 4]. Recent regional genomics further supports population-specific risk architecture [17]. Within this context, our proteomic findings align with endothelial/angiogenic mechanisms relevant to diabetic complications: ANGPT2 is higher in T2D-DR (vs. T2D/controls), consistent with Angiopoietin–Tie2 pathway activation and vascular remodeling/instability, whereas PlGF is lower in T2D-DR, suggesting impaired compensatory angiogenesis (Table 3; [35–37, 39]). Notably, a Middle Eastern study found vitreous PlGF correlates with proliferative DR activity [34], supporting the regional relevance of this axis. Together, these data situate our multi-omics signature (ANGPT2↑, PlGF↓ alongside miRNA/mRNA features) within pathophysiological frameworks that may be modulated by regional genetic background and environmental exposures.

In regions with high diabetes burden such as the Middle East, such panels could complement HbA1c testing and enable early identification of individuals at risk of complications. Importantly, the inclusion of easily accessible plasma biomarkers facilitates scalability.

From a policy perspective, integrating such biomarker panels into biobank-linked population health programs could help prioritise high-risk individuals for early intervention, reducing the long-term burden of microvascular complications.

Future research

While this study identifies robust candidate biomarkers, several questions remain. The causal role of the miR-29c–PROM1 axis in T2D pathogenesis warrants experimental validation in functional models. As this cohort was exclusively drawn from Qatar Biobank, findings may not generalize across all Middle Eastern populations. Replication in other MENA cohorts is planned. Future work will prioritize increased DR sample size—via additional recruitment and independent MENA cohorts—and external validation. Additionally, refining machine learning models to include clinical and lifestyle data alongside omics may enhance their predictive power and clinical utility. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses and real-world implementation studies will be necessary to assess feasibility in clinical settings.
