Application of the Newer 2023 Bethesda System for Reporting Cytopathology of Thyroid Nodules and Estimating their Risk of Malignancy
Authors: Sushma Bharti, Renu Singh, Manupriya Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Thyroid swellings are among the most common clinical presentations, yet not all cases necessitate surgical intervention. Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is a widely accepted, minimally invasive diagnostic method that reduces the need for unnecessary surgeries and guides appropriate clinical management. This study is important as it provides population-specific data on the diagnostic accuracy of FNAC and evaluates the risk of malignancy (ROM) and risk of neoplasm (RON) in thyroid nodules (TNs) using the latest Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (2023 edition). By systematically categorizing thyroid lesions, the study offers insights into effective risk stratification and supports evidence-based, cost-efficient approaches to patient care.Materials and Methods:A retro-prospective study was conducted over 2 years, enrolling 111 patients with TNs, after obtaining approval from the institutional ethical committee. FNAC was performed on all cases. Smears were stained with Papanicolaou and Giemsa stains and categorized according to the Bethesda classification. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences statistics (version 21.0), and Fisher’s exact test was applied.Results:This study demonstrated a sensitivity of 83.33%, specificity of 100%, and diagnostic accuracy of 90.24%. The positive predictive value was 100%, and the negative predictive value was 80.95%. ROM was 0% in Category I, 19.4% in Category II, and 100% in Categories IV, V, and VI. The overall RON was 21.62%. Papillary thyroid carcinoma was the most common malignancy observed.Conclusion:FNAC remains a highly effective and accessible tool for diagnosing thyroid lesions, particularly when combined with rapid on-site evaluation and cell block preparation. It enables accurate categorization, risk stratification, and a more individualized, cost-effective approach to the management of TNs.

INTRODUCTION

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) has established itself as one of the most reliable, minimally invasive, and cost-effective diagnostic techniques in modern medicine. It is widely regarded as the first-line investigation for evaluating swellings, particularly those arising in the head and neck region.[1] The technique allows for the rapid sampling of cellular material with minimal discomfort to the patient, while also reducing the need for more invasive diagnostic procedures. FNAC was first applied to thyroid lesions in the early 1950s and, over the subsequent decades, it gained increasing acceptance due to its diagnostic accuracy and simplicity. By the 1980s, FNAC had become globally recognized as the standard preoperative diagnostic modality for thyroid swellings, enabling clinicians to differentiate between benign and malignant nodules with high precision.[2,3]

The relevance of FNAC in thyroid pathology is further underscored by the rising incidence of thyroid neoplasms worldwide. Epidemiological data suggest that thyroid cancer is increasing at an alarming rate, and it is projected to become the fourth-most common cancer by the year 2030.[3] In India, thyroid swellings are observed in approximately 4%–7% of the general population, with women being disproportionately affected, reflecting the known gender predilection of thyroid diseases. These trends emphasize the critical need for accurate, cost-effective, and accessible diagnostic approaches, especially in low-resource healthcare settings. The present study is designed to apply the newly proposed 2023 classification system for thyroid cytopathology, estimate the risk of malignancy (ROM) and cancer risk in thyroid nodules (TNs), and assess the practical applicability of this system in resource-limited environments. This study also evaluates the diagnostic performance of FNAC in TNs, providing crucial data on its accuracy and reliability. The findings help in risk stratification, guide clinical management, reduce the need for unnecessary surgeries, and enhance local diagnostic protocols. By addressing population-specific outcomes, the study contributes to more precise and evidence-based thyroid care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retro-prospective study was conducted in the Department of Pathology over 2 years, from January 2022 to January 2024, following approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (Reference No. 15/23, dated 11/02/2023). Cytological reports were retrieved retrospectively from departmental archives, and corresponding histopathological diagnoses were reviewed and analyzed. Informed written consent was obtained from all patients presenting with either diffuse or solitary thyroid swellings, who were referred from various departments for FNAC. The study included patients of all age groups and both sexes, encompassing both palpable and non-palpable thyroid swellings. FNAC procedures performed directly or under ultrasonographic guidance were considered, while other midline neck swellings and repeated FNACs from the same patient during the study period were excluded to avoid duplication. The procedure utilized a 22–23 gauge needle with a 20-cc disposable syringe. Patients were positioned in a supine posture with neck extension and a pillow placed under the shoulders to better expose the swelling. A nonaspiration or “needling” technique was preferred. Blind FNAC was carried out for palpable lesions, while ultrasound guidance was used for non-palpable nodules. Rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) was performed to assess smear adequacy. For each case, one air-dried smear and two alcohol-fixed smears were prepared. Giemsa stain was used for the air-dried smear, whereas Papanicolaou and hematoxylin and eosin stains were applied to the fixed smears. Cytological interpretation was conducted independently by two pathologists; discrepancies were resolved through a consensus. All cases were categorized based on the newly adopted 2023 Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (TBSRTC). Data were subsequently evaluated to determine the ROM and estimate the diagnostic accuracy and neoplastic potential of the TNs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out to evaluate the sensitivity, specificity, diagnostic accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals. The analysis was performed using the IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, version 21.0 (New York, United States). To determine statistical significance and validate associations, the Wilson scoring method and Fisher’s exact T-test were applied. For analytical purposes, cases categorized as “suspicious” and “malignant” were combined into a single group.

RESULTS

A total of 111 cases of thyroid swellings were included in the study, of which 42 had corresponding histopathological correlations. All patients originated from the Himalayan region. The majority of cases (94.54%) fell within the 21–80-year age group, with a marked female predominance (73.87%). Patients presented with a variety of neck swellings [Figure 1]. All thyroid cases were classified according to the 6-tier system of the 2023 TBSRTC. Category I comprised nondiagnostic or unsatisfactory samples, which included cystic fluid without epithelial cells, acellular smears, or samples compromised by blood, clots, or artifacts [Figure 2]. Category II included benign lesions, such as colloid nodules, adenomatoid nodules, and Hashimoto thyroiditis [Figure 3]. Category III was designated as atypia of undetermined significance (AUS), while Category IV indicated follicular neoplasm (FN) [Figure 4]. Category V comprised cases suspicious for malignancy (SFM), including papillary, medullary, metastatic lesions, or lymphomas. Category VI represented malignant cases, such as papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) [Figure 5], poorly differentiated carcinoma [Figure 6], medullary carcinoma, anaplastic carcinoma, squamous-cell carcinoma, tumors with mixed histological features, metastatic carcinoma, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma. In the present study, non-neoplastic lesions were more prevalent than neoplastic ones. The diagnostic performance of FNAC showed a sensitivity of 83.33%, specificity of 100%, and an overall diagnostic accuracy of 90.24%. The PPV was 100%, while the NPV was 80.95%. Concordance between cytological and histopathological diagnoses was observed at 100% for Categories IV, V, and VI and 80.95% for Category II. In contrast, Category I exhibited a 100% discordance rate. The calculated risk of neoplasms among the TNs was 21.62%. Of the 28 cases diagnosed as malignant on cytology, 20 were confirmed histopathologically. PTC was the most frequently encountered malignancy (18 cases), followed by FN and poorly differentiated carcinoma (one case each) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

Thyroid swellings, particularly solitary TNs, are defined as distinct lesions within the thyroid gland that can be differentiated from the surrounding parenchyma on radiological imaging. These nodules are common and are increasingly encountered in clinical settings due to advancements in diagnostic imaging. The reported prevalence of TNs in the general population varies widely, ranging from 2% to 65%, depending on the method of detection. On physical examination, the prevalence is estimated at 2%–6%, while ultrasonographic evaluations report prevalence rates between 10% and 35%. Autopsy studies suggest an even broader range, from 8% to 65%. Several risk factors contribute to the development of TNs, including increasing age, female sex, previous radiation exposure, and iodine deficiency.[4,5] The widespread use of sensitive imaging modalities has led to a significant increase in the incidental detection of these nodules. Despite their high prevalence, only approximately 10% of TNs are clinically significant due to malignancy, with another 5% associated with compressive symptoms and 5% related to thyroid functional abnormalities.[6]

Thyroid cancer is the seventh most common malignancy among females and ranks fifteenth among males globally.[7,8,9] However, not all TNs are clinically palpable. With the advancement of imaging technologies, many TNs are now detected incidentally during routine health care evaluations or imaging performed for unrelated conditions. This incidental nature makes it increasingly challenging for clinicians to establish a definitive preoperative diagnosis. A variety of diagnostic approaches are available for assessing TNs, including palpation, thyroid function tests, ultrasonography of the anterior neck, FNAC, and occasionally trucut biopsy. Among these, FNAC has long been regarded as the first-line diagnostic tool for TNs due to its accessibility, cost-effectiveness, and high diagnostic accuracy. The utility of FNAC has further improved with the integration of ultrasound guidance, ROSE, and the use of cell block preparation.[3]

Currently, FNAC is widely employed for evaluating all types of thyroid swellings, including both diffuse and solitary nodules.[2] While ultrasonography assists in distinguishing between benign and malignant features, it lacks a definitive diagnostic capability. Trucut biopsy, although more invasive in nature, is generally avoided due to the risk of complications and the absence of a standardized reporting system like TBSRTC.[10] FNAC remains superior as it samples a broader cell population representative of the nodule, often yielding more diagnostic material than a needle biopsy or later well correlated with the histopathological examination of the excised specimen, which is the gold standard for diagnosis.[11] Much like cervical cytology, FNAC benefits from a standardized reporting protocol, which facilitates a clear communication between pathologists and clinicians. Early and accurate distinction between benign and malignant thyroid lesions is essential for guiding clinical decision-making and surgical management, both of which are effectively supported by FNAC.[12] Due to its minimally invasive nature and rapid diagnostic turnaround, the detection rate of thyroid malignancies using FNAC has risen significantly in recent years, now estimated at 30%–35%.[13]

TBSRTC was first introduced in 2007 during the National Cancer Institute’s “Thyroid fine needle aspiration: State of the science” conference held in Bethesda, Maryland. This initiative aimed to resolve the inconsistent and often ambiguous terminology used in thyroid FNAC reporting. Following its initial clinical acceptance, the first editions of TBSRTC, released in 2010 and revised in 2017, not only met their primary objective of standardizing thyroid cytopathology but also significantly enhanced diagnostic clarity and communication among clinicians and pathologists. The most recent edition, TBSRTC 2023, incorporates six well-defined diagnostic categories, each with a single, standardized designation. It further specifies the estimated ROM for each category and offers evidence-based management recommendations. Notably, this edition aligns the diagnostic criteria with the 2022 WHO classification of thyroid neoplasms, ensuring consistency in global reporting standards. One of the key advantages of TBSRTC is its role in minimizing diagnostic uncertainty—a “gray zone” often influenced by the nature of the aspirated material and the expertise of the reporting cytopathologist. TBSRTC is a crucial tool in assessing the “Risk of Malignancy” based on cytological analysis of FNA samples.[14,15] Reported ROM values range as follows: Category I (nondiagnostic) 0%–33%, Category II (benign) 0%–27%, Category III (AUS/FLUS) 11%–54%, Category IV (FN ) 28%–100%, Category V (SFM) 40%–100%, and Category VI (malignant) 86%–100%.[16] In the present study, the ROM observed was 0% for Category I, 19.04% for Category II, and 100% for Categories IV, V, and VI—findings that are consistent with the established international data.

In the present study, nondiagnostic cases accounted for 16 out of 111 thyroid FNAC samples (14.41%). Category I was assigned when the sample was insufficient for a definitive diagnosis—typically due to poor cellularity, the presence of only cyst fluid, or technical artifacts that obscured cellular details. The 2023 edition of TBSRTC no longer uses the term “unsatisfactory” for this category, opting instead for “non-diagnostic.”[16] Although a cyst fluid-only specimen is categorized as nondiagnostic, it may be considered clinically adequate in certain cases, such as when ultrasound reveals a simple unilocular cyst. Standard management for such cases includes repeating FNAC under ultrasound guidance. The estimated ROM for Category I is 13%; however, in this study, the ROM was 0%. This discrepancy is attributed to the fact that all five cases with follow-up were clinically benign and did not necessitate surgical intervention. Only one patient—an elderly female with a significantly enlarged thyroid—underwent surgery following repeated nondiagnostic FNAC results. Histopathological examination in this case revealed extensive xanthogranulomatous inflammation with necrosis. For Bethesda Category II (benign), 67 cases were diagnosed via FNAC, of which histopathological follow-up was available for 21 cases. The limited correlation was due to the benign nature of most lesions, including lymphocytic thyroiditis, colloid goiter, adenomatous goiter, and granulomatous thyroiditis, which typically do not warrant surgical excision. Management for these benign nodules generally involves routine ultrasound monitoring, particularly for asymptomatic lesions. However, surgery may be considered for larger nodules presenting with compressive symptoms such as dysphagia or dyspnea. The ROM for Category II in this study was 19.04%, slightly higher than the standard range. Notably, four cases were found to be malignant upon histopathological evaluation. These were initially misclassified due to overlapping features such as dense lymphocytic infiltrates, oncocytic changes, and, in one instance, the failure to detect a small PTC nodule in the thyroid during FNA, which was detected in the excised specimen.

In the present study, no cases were classified as Category III (AUS). AUS is considered a low-risk indeterminate category. In the third edition of TBSRTC, the term “follicular lesion of undetermined significance (FLUS)” was eliminated, and AUS was subclassified into “AUS–nuclear atypia” and “AUS–other.” This category is intended for cases where the atypia present is insufficient in quantity or quality to permit a definitive diagnosis. To prevent overuse, the second edition of TBSRTC recommends that AUS diagnoses comprise fewer than 10% of all thyroid FNAC cases or that an institution maintains an AUS/malignant ratio of no more than 3:1.[17] The average ROM for AUS is approximately 22% (ranging between 13% and 30%), and standard management includes repeat FNAC, molecular testing, diagnostic lobectomy, or active surveillance.[16]

Category IV, now uniformly termed “Follicular Neoplasm (FN),” includes both follicular adenomas and follicular carcinomas. The term “Suspicious for Follicular Neoplasm” has been removed from the latest TBSRTC classification. In this study, six cases were categorized as FN. Of these, only one had histopathological confirmation of follicular carcinoma, while follow-up data for the remaining cases were unavailable. The ROM for this category is typically around 30% (23%–34%); however, in our study, the ROM was 100%, likely due to the small number of cases with histopathological follow-up. The recommended clinical approach includes molecular testing and diagnostic lobectomy. Categories V and VI correspond to “Suspicious for Malignancy (SFM)” and “malignant (MAL),” respectively. These classifications have not undergone significant changes in the updated TBSRTC. They encompass a range of malignancies, including papillary carcinoma, medullary carcinoma, poorly differentiated carcinoma, metastatic carcinoma, and lymphoma. The distinction between SFM and MAL is primarily dependent on the diagnostic threshold of the cytopathologist. In our study, there were 11 cases in each of these categories, and all showed malignancy upon histopathological evaluation, resulting in an ROM of 100%—higher than the generally reported ROM of 74% (67%–83%). Standard treatment options include molecular testing, lobectomy, or near-total thyroidectomy.

A literature review was conducted for studies published between 2003 and 2024, using databases such as PubMed and non-PubMed sources. Keywords included “cyto-histopathological correlation in thyroid nodules,” “histopathological correlation with cytopathology of thyroid lesions,” and “correlation with cytological and histopathological findings.” Only studies published in English with a sample size of at least 50 cases were included, resulting in 14 relevant studies being incorporated into the comparative analysis. These studies served as a benchmark for evaluating sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic accuracy, thereby placing the current research findings within a broader clinical context [Table 2]. The implied ROM associated with TBSRTC categories II through VI is as follows: Category II—11.7%, Category III—25%, Category IV—40%, Category V—76.6%, and Category VI—96%.[17] These risk stratifications play a pivotal role in guiding clinical decision-making and have been validated in numerous studies, demonstrating wide-ranging diagnostic accuracy.

The reported sensitivity for detecting malignancy in TNs ranges from 96.36%,[26] to 50%[23] with the highest diagnostic accuracy reported at 97%.[24]

Despite the strengths of our research, certain limitations must be acknowledged. This was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, particularly for histopathological correlation, primarily due to loss to follow-up, attributed to geographic distance and financial constraints faced by patients. Further, there is the absence of cases within the AUS category, which precluded any risk stratification analysis for this group. Nevertheless, the study’s strengths lie in its remarkably low rate of nondiagnostic cases (14.41%) and complete absence of Category III (AUS) diagnoses. This was achieved through the implementation of ROSE, ultrasound correlation, and strong clinical communication. Moreover, the study provided valuable diagnostic metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and diagnostic accuracy, all of which were found to be comparable to those reported in the literature. These findings reinforce the ongoing utility of FNAC as a first-line diagnostic tool. FNAC remains widely accessible, cost-effective, and practical, particularly in resource-limited settings. It continues to play a critical role not only in the evaluation and classification of thyroid lesions but also in risk stratification and clinical management planning for patients with TNs.

CONCLUSION

The rising incidence of thyroid cancer highlights the importance of accurately assessing the ROM across all Bethesda categories. Standardized systems like TBSRTC enhance diagnostic consistency and guide effective patient management. FNAC continues to be a highly effective, minimally invasive, and cost-efficient diagnostic tool for the evaluation of TNs. Its accuracy is supported by strong correlations with postoperative histology. Estimating neoplasm risk enables a tailored approach to treatment, helping clinicians decide between surgery and observation. When combined with techniques like ultrasound and ROSE, FNAC further improves diagnostic precision, especially in resource-limited settings.
