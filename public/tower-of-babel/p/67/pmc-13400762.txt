The Impact of Preoperative Weight Loss on 90-Day Complications Following Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: John Paul Lewis, Mary Ziemba-Davis, Leonard T. Buller, Kevin A. Sonn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccess to primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for morbidly obese (MO) patients is often restricted to reduce complications. We compared complication rates in patients who were MO or non-MO (NMO) at initial presentation based on whether their body mass index (BMI) had decreased by ≥5%, remained stable, or increased by ≥5% at the time of surgery, hypothesizing that preoperative weight loss does not reduce complication rates.MethodsProspectively collected data for 2219 unilateral, primary TKAs were retrospectively reviewed. Cases were performed between 2012 and 2023 at the same academic center by 4 fellowship-trained surgeons using standardized protocols. Twenty-eight percent (n = 627) were MO and 72% (n = 1592) were NMO at initial presentation. Outcomes included intraoperative and 90-day complications and reoperations. Outcome covariates included patient demographics and comorbidities, as well as operative details. Independent samples t-tests and chi-squared tests were used for statistical analysis.ResultsIntraoperative complications (P = .999), medical complications (P ≥ .398), death (P = .299), superficial or deep infection (P = .731), and reoperation on the index joint within 90 days of surgery (P = .801) did not differ in MO patients with a ≥5% decrease vs stable BMI before surgery. These outcomes also did not differ between NMO patients with a ≥5% decrease in BMI and those with stable BMI before surgery (P ≥ .250).ConclusionsFindings suggest that withholding surgery from patients who are clinically indicated for TKA pending weight loss may not be necessary to reduce early postoperative complications.

Introduction

Access to primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for morbidly obese (MO) patients with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 40 kg/m2 is commonly restricted in an attempt to minimize complication rates and concomitant reimbursement reductions. In a survey of 674 members of the American Association of Hip and Knee Surgeons, only 13% did not use a BMI cutoff for TKA, and 60% with cutoffs used 40 kg/m2 [1]. However, a small body of existing evidence suggests that requiring weight loss before TKA surgery may not positively impact early clinical outcomes and complication rates [2,3].

Restricting joint replacement for MO patients arises from clear data that demonstrates higher perioperative complication rates, including infection, wound healing issues, venous thromboembolism, and early revisions [4,5]. Due to the elevated risk associated with increased BMI, many orthopaedic surgeons and insurance companies require their patients to lose weight before proceeding with TKA, regardless of clinical severity [6], based on data demonstrating that lower BMIs are associated with improved outcomes and lower complication rates [7]. However, the requirement for preoperative weight loss to qualify for surgery is based on the assumption that weight loss reduces risk. This has not been consistently demonstrated in the literature, and in fact, data exist which demonstrate the opposite impact of preoperative weight loss [2,[8], [9], [10]].

This study evaluated 90-day complications following elective primary TKA in patients who were MO at their initial visit based on whether their BMI decreased by ≥5%, remained stable (<5% decrease or increase), or increased by ≥5% by the time of surgery. The same analyses were performed for non-MO (NMO) patients (BMI < 40 kg/m2 at the initial visit) for comparison. We hypothesized that preoperative weight loss would not result in reduced complication rates compared with preoperative weight stability.

Patient care and surgical protocols

All patients underwent preoperative medical risk assessment by an internal medicine (IM) specialist who followed patients postoperatively, and a multidisciplinary care team reviewed each case preoperatively. Patients and caregivers received comprehensive clinic-based education and attended a hospital-based joint replacement before surgery. All patients received the same rehabilitation protocol, including physical therapy evaluation and treatment with weight-bearing as tolerated and ambulation on the day of surgery.

A medial parapatellar approach was used for all procedures. Standard femoral and tibial bone cuts were made with computer-aided navigation (Stryker Navigation, Kalamazoo, MI). Standard primary implants were utilized. Standardized multimodal perioperative pain control protocols were used for all patients.

Thromboprophylaxis consisted of 81 mg of enteric-coated aspirin twice daily for 6 weeks along with sequential compression devices during postoperative recovery. Patients at higher risk for thromboembolism were treated with additional chemoprophylaxis per IM recommendation. Tranexamic acid was used in all patients. Two grams of prophylactic cefazolin were administered within 60 minutes of skin incision, with the addition of weight-based vancomycin for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus carriers on screening.

Measures

All data for this study were manually extracted from the electronic medical record via chart review by trained study personnel, entered in an Excel spreadsheet, and cleaned and validated prior to statistical analysis. Dependent outcomes included intraoperative complications; the incidence of cardiac, cerebrovascular, pulmonary, renal, sepsis, venous thromboembolism, or any medical complication within 90 days of surgery; mortality within 90 days; the prevalence of superficial or deep infection within 90 days; and reoperation within 90 days.

The independent variable in this study was the change in BMI between the initial and preoperative visits for knee pain (≥5% decrease in BMI vs stable BMI reflecting a <5% decrease or increase in BMI) (Table 2). The initial visit BMI was measured and recorded by clinic nurses at the patient’s first clinic visit for knee pain. The preoperative visit before surgery occurred 2 weeks before surgery when patients saw the IM specialist for preoperative history and physical and risk assessment (including BMI assessment), signed informed consent for surgery, and attended joint replacement education class. Comparisons are also presented for patients with a ≥5% increase in BMI between the initial and preoperative visits. Other potential outcome covariates included patient sex, race, age, American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status classification, cardiac disease, history of stroke, uncontrolled blood glucose, peripheral vascular disease, pulmonary disease, renal disease, untreated obstructive sleep apnea, fentanyl vs morphine spinal opioid, procedure and anesthesia durations, and discharged on oral antibiotic prophylaxis (yes vs no).

Data analysis

Minitab 21.2 (Minitab Inc, State College, PA) was used for data analysis.

Pearson’s chi-squared tests using Fisher’s exact test P values for 2 × 2 tables and Students t-tests were used for bivariate analysis of the relationship between the independent variables and other potential outcome covariates in relation to dependent variables.

Results

The distribution of study cases by BMI at the initial visit and the change in BMI between the initial and preoperative visits for knee pain is presented in Table 2. Overall, 28.3% of the sample was MO, and 71.7% were NMO at the initial visit. At the time of surgery, BMI decreased by ≥5% for 28% of MO patients, increased by ≥5% in 5%, and remained stable in 67%. For NMO patients, BMI decreased by ≥5% in 18%, increased by ≥5% in 8%, and remained stable in 74%.

For MO patients who lost weight vs remained stable, there were no differences in intraoperative complications (0.6 vs 0.7%, P = .999), any medical complication within 90 days of surgery (5.1 vs 5.7%, P = .846), death within 90 days (0.6 vs 0.0%, P = .299), superficial or deep infection within 90 days (1.1 vs 1.9%, P = .731), and reoperation on the index joint within 90 days (2.8 vs 3.6%, P = .801) (Table 3). Odds ratios for patients with a ≥5% decrease in BMI vs stable BMI were 0.87 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.40, 1.92. P = .738) for any medical complication, 0.58 (95% CI: 0.12, 2.77, P = .497) for superficial or deep infection, and 0.78 (95% CI: 0.28, 2.17, P = .629) for reoperation. None of the 15 potential outcome covariates differed among MO patients with a ≥5% decrease in BMI vs stable BMI (P ≥ .002, Table 3). Comparisons including patients with a ≥5% increase in BMI (all 3 groups) between the initial and preoperative visits were like those observed when only the 2 focal groups (decreased vs stable BMI) were compared, except for a significant difference (P = .002) in cardiac disease among the 3 groups highest for patients with a ≥5% increase in BMI and lowest for patients with stable BMI between the initial and preoperative visits (Table 3).

For NMO patients who lost weight vs remained stable, there were no differences in intraoperative complications (0.0 vs 0.1%, P = .999), any medical complication within 90 days of surgery (4.6 vs 4.2%, P = .743), death within 90 days (0.4 vs 0.1%, P = .350), superficial or deep infection within 90 days (0.4 vs 0.3%, invalid chi-square due to low cell counts), and reoperation on the index joint within 90 days (1.8 vs 2.0%, P = .999) (Table 4). Odds ratios for patients with a ≥5% decrease in BMI vs stable BMI were 1.11 (95% CI: 0.59, 2.01, P = .745) for any medical complication, 0.34 (95% CI: 0.05, 2.66, P = .307) for superficial or deep infection, and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.34, 2.40, P = .838) for reoperation. None of the 15 potential outcome covariates differed among NMO patients with a ≥5% decrease in BMI vs stable BMI (P ≥ .002, Table 4). Comparisons among patients with a ≥5% increase in BMI between the initial and preoperative visits were like those observed when only the 2 focal groups were compared.

As shown in Table 5, the prevalence of any medical complication in patients with BMI ≥40 at the initial visit did not differ based on the independent variable (≥5% decrease vs stable BMI, 5.1 vs 5.7%, P = .846) or 15 additional outcome covariates (P ≥ .051). Among patients with BMI < 40 at their initial visit, any medical complication did not vary based on the independent variable (4.6 vs 4.2%, P = .743, Table 6) or 14 of the 15 additional outcome covariates (P ≥ .005). Significantly more of the patients with BMI <40 at their initial visit who had renal disease had an early medical complication (9.0 vs 3.7%, P = .002, Table 6).

Discussion

In our study of 2219 elective primary TKAs, stratified by initial BMI ≥40 kg/m2 vs <40 kg/m2 and preoperative BMI change, we observed no significant differences in intraoperative complications (P = .999), medical complications within 90 days (P ≥ .398), 90-day mortality (P = .299), or reoperations (P = .801) in MO patients with BMI ≥ 40 who achieved a ≥5% preoperative BMI reduction compared to those with a stable preoperative BMI. Similarly, in the NMO cohort, a ≥5% preoperative BMI reduction did not reduce intraoperative complications (P = .999), medical complications within 90 days (P ≥ .250), 90-day mortality (P = .350), or reoperation (P = .999) compared to stable preoperative BMI. Additionally, a BMI increase by ≥ 5% in MO patients preoperatively did not result in increased complications when compared to MO patients with a ≥5% preoperative BMI reduction. These findings suggest that modest preoperative weight loss among MO patients undergoing TKA may not meaningfully alter early perioperative risk.

Our findings support much of the existing literature on this topic. In a cohort of 115 MO TKA patients, 41% achieved a ≥5% preoperative weight loss while 59% remained weight stable or gained weight. The study found no significant differences in intraoperative or postoperative outcomes between those who lost weight and those who did not [11]. Additionally, the authors found that patients who achieved satisfactory weight loss prior to their operation had longer wait times [11]. A retrospective cohort study comprised of 869 TKA patients matched obese patients with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 to nonobese patients and observed no significant difference in postoperative outcomes [8]. Similarly, a prospective cohort study with 1377 patients examined a higher BMI cutoff for obesity, comparing TKA outcomes among patients with a BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 to those with a BMI < 35 kg/m2. A ≥5% preoperative weight loss was not associated with reduced complications or improved patient-reported outcomes 6 months after surgery [2]. Another retrospective cohort study of 90 patients undergoing TKA who underwent medically supervised preoperative weight loss found that despite achieving meaningful weight loss (mean of 10.4%), preoperative weight reduction was not associated with lower complication rates or overall improved postoperative outcomes compared with patients who did not lose weight [9]. Finally, a retrospective study evaluated 1589 TKA patients (BMI < 40 kg/m2: 1387, BMI > 40 kg/m2: 96, weight loss: 106) with morbid obesity who achieved nonsurgical preoperative weight loss from a BMI > 40 kg/m2 to < 40 kg/m2 and compared their postoperative outcomes to those who did not achieve this BMI reduction. They found that despite meaningful reductions in BMI, preoperative weight loss was not associated with lower rates of postoperative complications, improved discharge outcomes, or reduced revision/readmission rates [10].

Although growing evidence supports the current study’s findings, other literature continues to emphasize the increased risks associated with a higher preoperative BMI in patients undergoing TKA. A large retrospective cohort study of TKAs performed between 2011 and 2020 compared outcomes for matched obese (≥30 kg/m2) and nonobese patients, finding that obesity was associated with higher rates of surgical complications and medical complications, including acute kidney injury, deep vein thrombosis, urinary tract infection, and narcotic use but lower rates of anemia, arrhythmia, cardiac arrest, pneumonia, and transfusion [12]. Overall, however, the authors concluded that outcomes were not definitively worse in obese patients and recommend further investigation before using BMI as a barometer of patient safety and success during TKA [12]. Consistent with Aggarwal et al., the current study findings contribute to the growing body of evidence that achieving modest weight loss immediately prior to surgery is not protective in the short term and that the presence of obesity alone should not necessarily mandate weight loss before TKA.

In our study, the lack of statistical difference between the preoperative weight-loss MO group and the preoperative weight-stable MO group indicates that requiring a patient with a BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 to lose weight prior to TKA may impose an unnecessary burden of continued joint pain and decreased quality of life. This conclusion is in line with the findings of a retrospective cohort study of 27,671 primary TKA patients, which estimated the number of patients that would be denied a complication-free procedure if a BMI cutoff of ≥40 kg/m2 was applied to prevent one major complication. They found that roughly 14 patients with BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2 would be denied surgery for every one patient spared a major complication [13]. Additionally, another study examined long-term periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) risk after primary TKA stratified by BMI and other risk factors in 25,160 patients. They found that while patients with BMI ≥40 kg/m2 had a higher relative risk of PJI compared with normal-weight patients, the 15-year absolute risk remained low (4% vs 3%). Therefore, the absolute difference in PJI risk attributable to obesity over 15 years is roughly 1%, implying that the clinical benefit of preoperative weight loss might be modest [14].

We believe our data and the existing literature continue to fail to show risk reduction with preoperative weight loss potentially due to the negative nutritional effects of attempts at rapid weight loss, including a loss of lean body mass, as other authors have suggested [9,15]. The accumulation of the consistent literature on this topic suggests to us that if patients are given a target weight in order to be approved for surgery, the physiologic effects of this forced rapid weight loss may be canceling out the possible benefits of the reduction in weight.

The current study has several important limitations. First, our follow-up period was limited to 90 days, consistent with value-based care initiatives. However, a common reason for surgeons deferring joint replacement due to morbid obesity is infection risk, which most commonly occurs in the acute postoperative period. Second, while we accounted for numerous covariates (age, ASA, comorbidities, etc.), residual confounding is possible. Patients who lost ≥5% BMI may also differ in unmeasured ways (frailty, nutritional status, and muscle mass) from those who remained stable. Third, our measure of weight change (preoperative BMI reduction ≥ 5%) may not represent substantial enough weight loss to alter the pathophysiologic burdens of morbid obesity, some of which include adipose-related wound healing compromise or increased mechanical load, thereby limiting our capacity to detect benefit. However, the Food and Drug Administration suggests 5% BMI change be considered clinically significant weight loss and this is most commonly reported in the arthroplasty literature [16]. Finally, our cohort is from a single academic center with standardized perioperative protocols, which might not generalize to all practice settings or patients with different resources, surgical protocols, and surgeon skill levels. While it is necessary to acknowledge our study’s limitations, it is important to also note that many existing studies have been limited by smaller sample sizes (often fewer than 200 MO patients), shorter enrollment periods, or heterogeneous surgical settings, which may limit statistical power and generalizability [2,[9], [10], [11]]. In contrast, our study leverages over a decade of prospectively collected data from a high-volume academic center with standardized perioperative protocols, allowing for a more robust assessment of early postoperative risk.

In summary, our data demonstrate that in MO TKA candidates (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m2), achieving a modest preoperative BMI reduction did not reduce intraoperative or early postoperative complications compared to patients whose BMI remained stable. This finding adds to the growing body of literature and underscores 2 key messages for clinicians: first, that the use of a strict BMI threshold to deny or delay TKA is not clearly supported by the current data with respect to short-term mitigation of postoperative complications. And second, that individualization of patient care through optimization of comorbidities, functional status, and shared decision-making regarding risks and benefits will improve TKA outcomes more than broad exclusion based on a patient’s BMI.

Therefore, rather than employing a simple one-size-fits-all mandate based on BMI cutoffs, surgeons should consider proceeding with MO patients clinically indicated for TKA and medically optimized for surgery. This will eliminate unnecessary delays in pursuing marginal weight-loss goals with limited short-term efficacy. Ideally, future research would explore whether greater weight loss or interventions targeting adiposity, muscle strength, and metabolic fitness, rather than BMI alone, yield meaningful improvements in risk reduction and long-term TKA outcomes. Our study supports the growing evidence that arbitrary BMI cutoffs do not optimize early TKA outcomes or mitigate risk and highlights the need to shift surgeon focus from a single numeric BMI target toward holistic patient optimization and timely surgical access.
