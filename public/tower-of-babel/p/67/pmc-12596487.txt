Development and validation of the mobility in middle-age questionnaire
Authors: Roee Hayek, Carrie A Karvonen-Gutierrez, Jonathan F Bean, Jack M Guralnik, Kenneth Covinsky, Jay R Hoffman, Michal Azmon, Galit Yogev-Seligmann, Gregory Krautner, Odelyah Saad, Yaniv Nudelman, Rebecca T Brown, Shmuel Springer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMobility decline often begins in midlife, and early identification of individuals at risk of accelerated deterioration can enable timely prevention. However, there is no validated self-report instrument that specifically assesses mobility in the middle-aged population.MethodsThe Mobility in Middle-Age Questionnaire (MMQ) was developed through a 7-step Delphi process, consisting of 10 experts, involving item selection and content validation in both English and Hebrew, comprising 2 factors: (1) Current Mobility Ability and (2) 1-Year Mobility Change. Psychometric properties were assessed in 610 US and 594 Israeli middle-aged adults. Analyses included internal consistency, test–retest reliability, structural and construct validity (using the 10-item Physical Functioning scale [PF-10] from SF-36), and floor/ceiling effect assessments. A “Potential Mobility Risk Zone” was defined as the lowest 20% of MMQ scores.ResultsThe MMQ showed excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = .94 English; .92 Hebrew) and strong test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.89-0.90). Exploratory factor analysis explained 66% of variance; confirmatory factor analysis showed good fit (Comparative Fit Index = 0.99, Tucker–Lewis Index = 0.99, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual = 0.05). Construct validity was supported, with all pre-defined hypotheses confirmed. MMQ showed significantly lower ceiling effects than PF-10 (3.9% vs. 34.5% in United States; 0.17% vs 25.25% in Israel, p < .001, large effect sizes). A score of 50 (20th percentile) was proposed as a preliminary “Potential Mobility Risk” Threshold.ConclusionsThe MMQ is a reliable and valid tool for detecting early mobility decline in midlife. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm its predictive value and responsiveness to change.

Introduction

Middle age (45-64 years) marks a critical phase of aging in which mobility often begins to decline subtly, with notable changes often observed after the age of 50.1 In this life stage, up to 60% of people may report mobility-related difficulties,2 and the risk for falls might increase from 9% at ages 40-44 to 28% at ages 60-64.3 Yet, these difficulties often go unnoticed, underlining the importance of early detection of mobility decline.4 Mobility refers to the ability to move from 1 place to another and includes various physical tasks performed in different environments, such as changing body position, walking, climbing stairs, and carrying objects.5,6 While some definitions of mobility also incorporate instrumental activities, such as driving a car and using public transportation, the physical aspects of mobility are often affected earlier in the aging process. In contrast, the instrumental domains typically remain intact during midlife and may even persist in older age.7 Adequate mobility depends on the integration of various physical capacities, such as aerobic endurance, muscular strength, flexibility, and dynamic balance.5 These capabilities are affected differently by the aging process, with some declining earlier than others.8

Self-report questionnaires are widely used to assess function due to their accessibility and their ability to capture perceived health and psychosocial factors that performance-based assessments may overlook.9 Studies have shown only moderate correlations between performance-based and self-reported measures of physical function, suggesting that these tools are not interchangeable and often offer complementary insights.9,10 Self-perceived mobility measures can thus serve as valuable tools for identifying early or context-specific difficulties that might be missed by objective assessments, providing a more nuanced understanding of mobility. In older adults, self-report mobility measures proved to be a valid measure to predict disability,11 and such tools may be also useful in midlife. To better detect mobility changes, individuals’ perceived change over time should also be assessed. Self-perceived mobility is not merely a cross-sectional state, but a temporal experience, as individuals often evaluate their function by comparing their current abilities with previous levels. Assessing perceived changes over a 1-year period provides a unique insight into subjective trajectories of function and supports the integration of this dimension into mobility assessment tools.12

Most existing functional questionnaires, including those assessing aspects of mobility, exhibit ceiling effects in middle-aged populations, as many were originally developed for older adults.13 Moreover, some omit key mobility components, such as dynamic balance, flexibility, and exertional capacity, that are essential for accurately capturing mobility status in this age group. As a result, these tools may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle changes in midlife.13,14 These limitations highlight the need for a validated instrument specifically designed to assess mobility in middle age.

To address this need, we developed the Mobility in Middle-Age Questionnaire (MMQ) to capture mobility-related changes and support early detection of deterioration. Developing self-report instruments requires a structured process that ensures relevance, clarity, cross-cultural applicability, and psychometric validity.15 Using a structured Delphi process with multidisciplinary experts, the MMQ was developed simultaneously in English and Hebrew. To increase generalizability, its psychometric characteristics were tested with independent US and Israeli samples. This study presents the development and validation of the MMQ to improve the assessment of mobility in midlife and enable targeted interventions.

Methods

Study design

A 2-phase design was used. Phase 1 involved the development of the MMQ in English and Hebrew via a Delphi process focused on item selection and content validation. Phase 2 entailed psychometric evaluation of the MMQ using cross-sectional online survey panels of independent US and Israeli samples. The study design adhered to the COSMIN checklist for patient-reported outcome measures,16 and the methods and results are reported following the Guidelines for Reporting Reliability and Agreement Studies.

The psychometric evaluation of the final MMQ (English and Hebrew) included assessment of internal consistency, test–retest reliability, standard error of measurement (SEM), minimal detectable change (MDC95%), construct validity, floor and ceiling effects, and structural validity using factor analysis. Ethical approval was granted by the institutional review board of the University of Pennsylvania (IRB Protocol #857420) and by the Ariel University Ethics Committee (AU-HEA-SS-20230806).

The development of the MMQ and its content validity assessment

The Delphi process for the MMQ development consisted of 7 steps,17 was conducted between April through September 2024, and involved 3 iterative rounds of feedback and revisions. An overview of the Delphi process is presented in Figure 1 and comprehensively detailed in Supplementary File 1. In step 1, the 3 lead researchers acting as facilitators (ie, RTB, a geriatrician and health services researcher specializing in aging with a focus on middle age, and S.S. and R.H., experienced physical therapists, and researchers specializing in mobility in this population) defined the construct, generated initial items, and defined the criteria for conducting the Delphi process. In April 2024, the facilitators developed the initial MMQ draft, including 24 items across 5 domains, structured in 2 factors. Factor 1—“Current mobility ability,” referring to present status—included items evaluating “mobility functions” that require strength, aerobic capacity, flexibility, stability, and outdoor capabilities and factor 2—“one-year mobility change,” referred to perceived changes in mobility over the past year.

Abbreviations: I-CVI, content validity index for each item; MMQ, Mobility in Middle Age Questionnaire; S-CVI, Scale-Level Content Validity Index..

In step 2, an expert panel was assembled, consisting of 10 leading specialists with academic, clinical, and research experience in the fields of mobility and aging. All panel members held PhD degrees or equivalent advanced training; most had internationally recognized expertise and strong publication records. Invitations were sent to 13 experts, of whom 10 agreed to participate, ensuring a diverse range of expertise. The panel included 3 physical therapists, 2 gerontologists, 3 epidemiologists, 1 psychologist, and 1 physiologist. Eight members were from the United States and 5 from Israel, including the 3 facilitators, enabling parallel development for both languages. The panel received a comprehensive description of the initial MMQ’s construct, items, and context. The initial draft also proposed 2 five-point response scales, which the experts were asked to review, approve, or suggest modifications for the final version of the questionnaire. A five-point Likert scale was used for both factors based on evidence showing it provides an optimal balance of usability, psychometric robustness, and data quality for functional questionnaires compared to broader or narrower scales.18 The questionnaire was developed simultaneously in both English and Hebrew, and a linguist and 2 independent reviewers ­conducted forward/back translations, following standard cross-cultural questionnaire adaptation procedures.19

In step 3, the experts were asked to independently and anonymously evaluate the 24 items based on their relevance, clarity, and importance, using a nine-point Likert scale via Qualtrics (https://www.qualtrics.com/). Items with a content validity index (I-CVI) above 0.78 were retained, those between 0.70 and 0.78 were revised based on expert feedback and re-rated, and items below 0.70 were excluded. Experts were encouraged to suggest revisions for existing items or propose new items. The I-CVI was calculated by dividing the number of experts who rated an item as 7 or higher by the total number of experts.15 The experts confirmed the 2-factor structure and selected the five-point response scale to be used in the final version of the questionnaire.

In step 4, the facilitators analyzed expert responses to determine which items required refinement or elimination based on consensus levels. Steps 5 and 6 involved iterative item revisions, and 2 additional Delphi rounds were conducted. Final consensus was reached on 16 items. In step 7, 50 middle-aged adults from the United States and Israel participated in a pilot study and were interviewed to assess content clarity and relevance. The final questionnaire demonstrated excellent content validity, with a Scale-Level Content Validity Index (S-CVI/Ave) of 0.92, calculated as the average of the I-CVIs, exceeding the accepted threshold of ≥0.90 for excellent content validity.15 The English version, including the response scales, the I-CVI of each item, and the S-CVI of the questionnaire, is shown in Table 1; the English and Hebrew versions intended for practical use are provided in Supplementary File 2—eTable 1 and Supplementary File 3—eTable 2, respectively.

Assessment and analysis of the psychometric properties of the MMQ

To evaluate the psychometric properties of the MMQ, online surveys were conducted in both English and Hebrew. Eligible participants were adults aged 45-64 with self-reported independent mobility (“I move independently without any assistance or assistive device”). Participants self-reported whether a doctor had diagnosed them with any chronic conditions using a multiple-choice checklist, including options for “other” and “none of the above.” Multiple responses were allowed. A minimum sample size of 300 participants per language version was set to ensure very good reliability, per COSMIN guidelines.16 To assess test–retest reliability, participants completed the MMQ at 2 separate time points, 14-16 days apart,20 in either English (for United States participants) or Hebrew (for Israeli participants).

The psychometric evaluation of the English version in the United States was carried out in collaboration with the Geocartography Knowledge Group (https://www.geokg.com/en/) and Dynata panel website (https://www.dynata.com), a nationally representative panel provider with over 62 million users and strict quality control measures. Data were collected online via Qualtrics21 in February 2025, with consent implied by clicking “Continue” after reviewing study details, and anonymity maintained through Participant Serial Identification numbers provided by Dynata for matching responses across time points. The Hebrew version was evaluated using iPanel (https://www.ipanel.co.il/en/), the largest online panel in Israel, in accordance with the European Society for Opinion and Marketing Research, which enables anonymous, large-scale, and geographically diverse data collection with robust privacy guarantees. Data were collected in December 2024 according to facilitators’ guidelines, with anonymity preserved. To address the gendered structure of Hebrew, plural forms were used to minimize bias.22

Structural validity

The structural validity of the MMQ was evaluated using Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) with the Israeli sample, which provided an adequate dataset for identifying the underlying factor structure. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) was then conducted with the US sample to test the replicability of this structure in an independent sample and in the language intended for international use. This approach strengthens the construct validity and enhances the generalizability of the model in both clinical and research contexts. Initially, the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < .05) were used to assess the adequacy of item correlations and the overall coherence of both samples.23 A KMO value ≥ 0.6 was deemed acceptable.23,24

EFA was conducted using Principal Axis Factoring with a polychoric correlation matrix and Promax rotation. Factors were identified based on eigenvalues ≥1, supplemented by a scree plot of parallel analysis. Factor loadings of >0.7 and communalities of >0.5 were considered satisfactory,25 and the extracted factors needed to collectively account for at least 50% of the total variance.26

Confirmatory Factor Analysis was performed using the Diagonally Weighted Least Squares estimation method.27 Model’s goodness of fit was assessed using multiple indices, including non-significant χ2 test (p > .05), Comparative Fit Index (CFI) >0.95, Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) > 0.95, Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) < 0.06, and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) < 0.08.27 Modification indices (MI) were evaluated to test for possible item redundancies and cross-factor loadings.

Construct validity

The 10-item Physical Functioning scale (PF-10), part of the SF-36,28 was used for construct validity. PF-10, a reliable and validated tool for physical assessment, consists of 10 items, with each item scored on a 3-point scale, and higher scores indicating better physical function.29–31

Four hypotheses were formulated to assess the construct validity of the MMQ. First, the MMQ was expected to show a significantly lower ceiling effect than the PF-10, with a large effect size, as reducing ceiling effects for middle-aged individuals is a key aim of the MMQ. The difference was assessed using McNemar’s test, and effect size was quantified using the phi coefficient (φ), where values ≥0.50 indicate a large effect.

Second, a strong positive correlation (>0.60), though not very strong (<0.80),32 was anticipated between MMQ and PF-10 scores, based on the assumption that both tools assess mobility status, but the PF-10 is considered less tailored to a middle-aged population than the MMQ. Third, a positive correlation (0.40 < r < 0.59) was expected between the 2 MMQ factors,32 reflecting related yet distinct aspects of assessment. Fourth, a negative correlation was anticipated between MMQ total scores and the presence of chronic conditions, not expected to exceed −0.40,32 given that evidence reports hazard and odds ratios <4 in studies of multimorbidity and mobility decline.33,34 Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to test the last 3 hypotheses.

Internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and error of measurement

Internal consistency was measured by calculating Cronbach's α; values .8 and above were considered good.35 Test–retest reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) based on a 2-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement and 95% confidence intervals. ICC values were interpreted as follows: <0.20, very weak; 0.20-0.39, weak; 0.40-0.59, moderate; 0.60-0.79, strong; and >0.80, very strong reliability.32 SEM was computed using the pooled standard deviation (SD) formula: ?????? = ???? × √(1 − ICC). Floor and ceiling effects

Floor and ceiling effects were assessed using both the classical method37 and the scale width method.38 The classical method defines a floor or ceiling effect as present when more than 15% of respondents achieve the lowest or highest possible total score on the MMQ. In contrast, the scale width method also applies a 15% threshold but accounts for measurement error by evaluating whether scores are sufficiently distant from the MMQ’s extreme values, using the MDC95%, to ensure that the scale can detect meaningful change over time.38

MMQ scores analysis

The sociodemographic sample's characteristics and questionnaire scores were described using medians, interquartile range (IQR), means, and SDs. To identify individuals potentially at increased risk for mobility-related limitations, a “Potential Mobility Risk Zone” score was defined using the lowest quintile (bottom 20%) of the MMQ total score distribution. We chose this cut-off based on prior research in older adults showing that individuals in the lowest fifth of PF-10 scores performed worse on objective mobility tests, including grip strength, 3-m walk, timed-up-and-go, chair rises, and quadriceps strength,39 supporting the use of percentile-based thresholds to identify mobility risk. The 20th percentile cutoff was calculated in both the English and Hebrew MMQ samples.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), while EFA and CFA were carried out in RStudio with appropriate R packages (RStudio, Inc., Boston, MA).40,41

Results

Descriptive statistics and the “Potential Risk Zone” threshold

Descriptive statistics for the MMQ were based on samples of 610 US middle-aged adults and 594 Israeli adults. The mean ages were 54.4 and 53.6 years, the proportions of females were 45% and 49%, and the mean BMIs were 27.7 and 26.9, respectively. The mean MMQ total scores were 60.4 ± 12.0 and 56.6 ± 10.4, with median (IQR) scores of 64 (54-69) and 59 (50-65), respectively. The full descriptive characteristics of both samples are presented in Table 2. The 20th percentile score was 51 in the English version and 48 in the Hebrew version. Based on these findings, we selected a score of 50 as a pragmatic threshold for identifying individuals within the Potential Mobility Risk Zone.

Structural validity

Exploratory factor analysis

The EFA yielded KMO = 0.90 and a significant Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ2[120] = 8786.77, p < .001). Parallel analysis supported a 2-factor solution, as only the first 2 data extracted eigenvalues exceeded their simulated counterparts (see Supplementary 4). The 2-factor structure accounted for 66% of the total variance. Factor loadings were strong, with items 1-11 loading on factor 1 (0.71-0.83) and items 12-16 on factor 2 (0.76-0.96). Communalities ranged from 0.52 to 0.89, indicating that the factors explained a moderate to high proportion of variance across items. The factors division is found in Table 1.

Confirmatory factor analysis

All factor loadings were statistically significant and strong, ranging from 0.78 to 0.90 for factor 1 and 0.87 to 0.94 for factor 2, indicating that all items contributed meaningfully to their respective constructs.25 The 2 MMQ factors were moderately to largely correlated (r = 0.56). The model demonstrated good overall fit, with CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, and SRMR = 0.05. However, the RMSEA = 0.07 (90% CI, 0.06-0.08) and χ2(103, 610) = 438.89, p < .001 did not meet the defined thresholds.27 Examination of the MI revealed several areas for potential model improvement. The largest suggested residual covariance was between items 7 and 8 (MI = 97.03), suggesting the presence of substantial unexplained shared variance not accounted for by the latent factors, possibly due to overlapping item content in these items. Additional high MI were observed between items 7 and 8 (MI = 52.04) and between items 10 and 11 (MI = 41.79). Cross-loadings were also suggested, particularly for item 8 on factor 2 (MI = 28.94). Item numbers are presented in Table 1.

Construct validity

Construct validity was supported, with all the predefined hypotheses confirmed (see Supplementary 5, eTable 3, for the hypotheses testing results). First, McNemar’s test indicated significantly fewer ceiling effects with a large effect size in the MMQ compared to the PF-10 in both language versions (English: χ2(1, 610) = 180.13, p < .001, φ = 0.54; Hebrew: χ2(1, 594) = 147.01, p < .001, φ = 0.50). Second, the correlation between the MMQ and PF-10 was strong, as expected (ρ = 0.76 and ρ = 0.77, p < .001, in the English and Hebrew versions, respectively). Third, a moderate positive relationship was observed between factors 1 and 2 of the MMQ (English: ρ = 0.48, p < .001; Hebrew: ρ = 0.45, p < .001). Finally, a negative correlation weaker than −0.40 emerged between the number of chronic conditions and the MMQ total score (English: ρ = −0.30, p < .001; Hebrew: ρ = −0.38, p < .001).

Floor and ceiling effects

There were no floor effects observed across any of the measures. No ceiling effects were found for the MMQ total score or for factor 2. However, ceiling effects were present in factor 1 of the English version, with 16.9% of participants reaching the maximum score using the classical method and 39.0% reaching the maximum score with the stricter scale width method. In the Hebrew version, ceiling effects for this factor were lower and observed only when using the scale width method (19.0%). Table 3 presents the floor and ceiling effects of the MMQ.

Internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and measurement error

Internal consistency was good in both language versions. Cronbach’s alpha values for the English version were 0.94 (total score), 0.94 (factor 1—Current Mobility Ability), and 0.94 (factor 2—1-Year Mobility Change), while the Hebrew version showed values of 0.92 (total score), 0.92 (factor 1), and 0.91 (factor 2).

ICCs for the total score and factor 1 indicated nearly excellent reliability in both versions (ICC ∼ 0.9). For factor 2, reliability was moderate in both versions (0.73 and 0.64 for the English and Hebrew versions, respectively). SEM and MDC95% values were comparable across versions, further supporting the scale’s temporal stability. The test–retest reliability and measurement error of the MMQ are presented in Table 4.

Discussion

The MMQ demonstrated strong measurement properties, supporting its use as a self-reported tool to assess mobility in middle-aged adults. It was developed through a structured process including a 3-round Delphi method, resulting in excellent content validity (S-CVI = 0.92). Psychometric evaluation revealed high internal consistency in the English and Hebrew versions (Cronbach’s α > .90), and the hypothesized 2-factor structure (factor 1: “Current mobility ability”; factor 2: “One-year mobility change”) was confirmed by EFA, explaining over 66% of the variance. Test–retest reliability was nearly excellent for the total score and factor 1, while factor 2 showed moderate reliability, possibly reflecting the inherent variability of retrospective assessments of perceived change.

Construct validity was supported, with all hypotheses confirmed. As expected, correlation with the PF-10 was strong, but not very strong, underlining the MMQ’s convergent validity. MMQ showed lower ceiling effects than PF-10, emphasizing its sensitivity in capturing mobility differences in high-functioning middle-aged adults. Factor 1 in the English ersion had more pronounced ceiling effects compared to the Hebrew version (39.0% vs. 19.0%), possibly due to differences in recruitment methods,42 or the underrepresentation of middle-aged adults with mobility limitations. The inclusion criteria focused on community-dwelling adults without reported mobility difficulties, which may have led to higher scores and greater ceiling effects. It is therefore plausible that in a more diverse middle-aged sample, including individuals with mobility limitations, the proportion of ceiling effects would be reduced.34 In contrast, factor 2, focused on perceived change, was less susceptible to ceiling effects, suggesting its value for post-intervention assessment. The MMQ total score also showed fewer ceiling effects, favoring its use in healthier populations.

Confirmatory factor analysis supported the 2-factor structure, with strong item loadings.25 Although the chi-square test was significant as expected, given its sensitivity to large sample sizes,27 the RMSEA value (0.07, 90% CI: 0.06-0.08) slightly exceeded the conventional cutoff. The number of items generally increases this index and therefore indicates possible redundancy of items, further supported by the suggested residual covariances (MI) in factor 1 and the high Cronbach’s alpha.27,43 Given that the MMQ is a health-related self-report tool, item selection was deliberately guided by the need to ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant midlife mobility domains. This approach aligns with established best practices in health questionnaire development, where a broad item pool is essential for establishing content validity, particularly for complex constructs such as mobility.44 Additionally, the MMQ consists of only 16 items in total, which helps mitigate the risk of respondent burden, fatigue, and diminished data quality. The other fit indices met the accepted thresholds for good structural validity. It is worth noting that there is no clear consensus in the literature regarding optimal cutoff values for fit indices in CFA models with ordinal data.45,46 According to COSMIN guidelines, EFA is considered adequate for evaluating the structural validity of newly developed PROMs; therefore, the inclusion of CFA (and the MI) in this study represents a conservative approach that further supports the MMQ’s internal structure and allows for further studies to refine its structure.47

Classifying the lowest quintile of MMQ scores as a “Potential Mobility Risk Zone” offers a pragmatic, distribution-based approach for identifying individuals who may warrant closer monitoring. This method is consistent with previous findings using the PF-10,39 where the bottom 20% of the distribution was associated with significantly poorer performance on objective physical function tests. The MMQ demonstrated a strong correlation with the PF-10, which may reinforce the relevance of the threshold approach in our context. The 20th percentile average of both samples was an MMQ score of 50, leading us to propose it as a provisional threshold.

Although this approach offers a pragmatic method for identifying individuals who may warrant closer attention, it does not imply diagnostic validity or predictive accuracy, and several limitations should be acknowledged before its broader application. The MMQ was validated solely against the PF-10; therefore, future studies are needed to evaluate the predictive validity of this threshold using additional instruments. Ideally, such research would employ a longitudinal design to examine whether individuals scoring at or below this threshold in midlife are more likely to experience future mobility decline or related outcomes, such as increased use of mobility aids, falls, healthcare utilization, hospitalizations, or illness. These studies could use tools like the Late-Life Function and Disability Instrument,48 which is commonly used in older populations. Alongside validation of the proposed risk zone, longitudinal research should further strengthen the construct validity of the MMQ by assessing its responsiveness to change and examining the longitudinal relationships between its factors, as well as investigating its comparability with other self-report and performance-based mobility measures.

Additionally, subgroup-specific normative thresholds based on sex, race/ethnicity, occupation, or socioeconomic status were not applied. These demographic variables may influence score distributions and interpretation, and their omission limits the generalizability of the proposed cut-off. Future studies should account for these factors to enhance the interpretability and clinical utility of the MMQ. It is also important that future research assess the cross-cultural validation of the MMQ in non-Western contexts.

The MMQ is an important innovation in the assessment of midlife mobility, developed through expert consensus and validated across 2 languages and populations. It addresses a critical gap in early detection of mobility decline, a known predictor of later disability and disease.49 The use of such tools may enable more proactive and timely interventions, potentially reducing the burden of chronic disease. Integrating mobility screening, such as the MMQ, into clinical practice, digital health platforms, and public health initiatives may support healthy aging.50 Although the MMQ demonstrated consistent psychometric properties that support its use in midlife populations, some methodological considerations described earlier in this paper should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. Further validation in more diverse groups and comparisons with other relevant instruments could increase its generalizability and improve the interpretability of the proposed cut-off.

Conclusion

The MMQ is a reliable and valid self-report tool specifically designed to assess mobility in middle-aged adults. It demonstrates strong psychometric properties across 2 languages and populations, with minimal ceiling effects and good structural validity. By capturing both current mobility and perceived change, the MMQ offers enhanced sensitivity for early detection of mobility decline in midlife. Future research should focus on its responsiveness, predictive validity, and applicability across more diverse clinical populations.
