Optimizing Pediatric Tracheostomy Care: A Survey of Peri‐Operative Practices and Standardization in ASPO Members
Authors: Tom Ben‐Dov, Kiran R Majeti, Uche C Ezeh, Marie Therese Homsi, Ameen Biadsee, Zahrah M Taufique, Scott Rickert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis ASPO survey investigates pediatric peri‐operative tracheostomy care practices through a nationwide survey. It aims to identify challenges, explore opportunities for standardization across institutions, and recommend strategies that could reduce complications and enhance patient care.MethodsA 19‐question survey was administered online to pediatric otolaryngologists. Descriptive statistics summarized respondent demographics and responses.ResultsOf 138 respondents, 90% practiced in the US, primarily in the Mid‐Atlantic and Great Lakes. Over 60% performed fewer than 10 pediatric tracheostomies annually. A majority (98%) used stay sutures for accidental decannulation, and 25% did not mature the stoma. Notably, 92% adhered to standardized post‐operative protocols. Bivona tracheostomy tubes were most commonly used (72%). First tube changes were typically on postoperative Days 5 and 7, with false passage creation (46%) and bleeding (41%) as primary complications. Routine surveillance bronchoscopy was conducted by 63%, mainly twice in the first year (34%).ConclusionThe survey highlights wide variation in pediatric tracheostomy practices and underscores the importance of standardized protocols. Most respondents perform fewer than 10 procedures each year, yet adherence to institutional guidelines reflects a strong commitment to safety. This study identifies key areas of variability such as the timing of the first tube change, use of surveillance bronchoscopy, and reported complications—and outlines opportunities for further research and standardization aimed at improving consistency and clinical outcomes.Level of Evidence5.Keywords: clinical practice, pediatric airway, tracheostomy

Introduction

Patients in intensive care units (ICU) frequently have a tracheostomy, more often among adults than among children. The reasons why pediatric patients undergo tracheostomies have evolved over the last few decades [1, 2], largely due to the widespread administration of vaccines targeting acute infections such as Haemophilus influenzae and Corynebacterium diphtheriae [3, 4]. These infectious diseases previously led to upper airway obstruction, like epiglottitis and laryngotracheobronchitis, prompting emergent short‐term pediatric tracheostomies. Presently, more prevalent indications for pediatric tracheostomies involve chronic conditions such as congenital airway anomalies or underdevelopment, vocal cord paralysis/paresis, and mechanical ventilation dependence secondary to cardiopulmonary disease and neuromuscular impairment [5, 6].

Compared to adults, pediatric tracheostomy complications are more common [7]. Minor complications include wound breakdown, bleeding, and infection. Major complications, although rare, include tube obstruction and accidental decannulation, requiring immediate intervention [5]. These major events can be fatal. If not managed quickly by parents or practitioners, they may lead to hypoxic brain damage or death. Despite the complex comorbid status of many children, tracheostomy‐related adverse events can often be attributed to inadequate care [8]. Research has demonstrated that with the involvement of multidisciplinary care teams [9, 10, 11] and family‐centered care coordination, these events have decreased [12]. Additionally, there is an ongoing effort to develop assessment tools for pediatric tracheostomy emergency‐readiness to ensure that healthcare practitioners are prepared for inpatient tracheostomy emergencies [13].

With advancements in pediatric intensive care medicine, tracheostomized pediatric patients are now living longer than before [14]. Although there have been some consensus recommendations regarding tracheostomy management [15, 16, 17], significant lack of uniformity in postoperative tracheostomy practices persists [18, 19]. Moreover, despite published consensus statements and quality improvement initiatives, several key aspects of pediatric peri‐operative tracheostomy care remain incompletely standardized or actively debated. These include (1) the optimal timing of the first tracheostomy tube change, particularly in light of emerging evidence supporting earlier change, (2) the role, timing, and frequency of routine surveillance bronchoscopy, for which no formal clinical guidelines exist, (3) technical decisions at the index operation, including the use of stay sutures, stoma maturation, and tube‐securing methods, and (4) peri‐procedural management practices such as feeding cessation and sterilization of custom tracheostomy tubes.

Prior literature supports each of these domains as areas of ongoing uncertainty in pediatric tracheostomy care. The timing of the first tracheostomy tube change remains debated, with consensus statements recommending postoperative Days 5–7 [15, 16]. In contrast, multiple observational studies and a randomized controlled trial have demonstrated the safety of earlier tube changes without increased complication rates [20, 21, 22, 23]. Similarly, no clinical guidelines define the role or frequency of routine surveillance bronchoscopy in pediatric patients with tracheostomies, and prior surveys and institutional studies demonstrate wide variability in practice patterns and timing of airway evaluation [18, 24, 25, 26, 27].

Technical decisions at the index operation, including stay sutures, stoma maturation, and tube‐securing methods, are likewise inconsistently standardized. Surveys of pediatric otolaryngologists and retrospective studies report variation in the use of these techniques and conflicting findings regarding their impact on complication rates, particularly accidental decannulation and stomal morbidity [28, 29, 30, 31, 32].

Finally, peri‐procedural management practices such as feeding cessation surrounding tube changes and the sterilization of custom tracheostomy tubes are largely guided by institutional protocols and expert opinion rather than formal evidence‐based recommendations, further contributing to inter‐institutional variability in care [17, 18, 19]. Therefore, we surveyed members of the American Society of Pediatric Otolaryngology (ASPO) to characterize current perioperative tracheostomy care practice patterns, identify areas of convergence and variability, and highlight opportunities to inform future standardization efforts, with the ultimate objective of reducing the frequency and duration of ICU stays among pediatric tracheostomy patients.

Methods and Materials

A survey was developed by the authors T.B.D. and S.R. and was reviewed and approved by both the ASPO Research Committee and the NYU Langone Medical Center Institutional Review Board—which deemed the study exempt from the need for approval of research involving human subjects—prior to distribution.

Survey questions were designed to reflect key areas of peri‐operative tracheostomy care that have been identified in prior consensus statements and published literature as variable or incompletely standardized, as well as areas of practice variation observed by the authors in routine clinical care. These domains included the first tracheostomy tube change timing, surveillance bronchoscopy practices, peri‐procedural feeding management, and technical aspects of peri‐operative tracheostomy care.

The questionnaire consisted of 19 multiple‐choice items; the full survey instrument is provided in File S1 and was structured to capture current practice patterns. Individual survey items were organized to assess practice patterns within these predefined domains. Response options were selected to reflect commonly used practices and commercially available tracheostomy equipment, as well as approaches described in prior literature and consensus statements, in order to capture real‐world variation in clinical care. Questions and response options were refined through iterative discussion among the study authors to ensure clarity, relevance, and face validity prior to distribution. Individual survey items corresponding to each domain are detailed in File S1.

The survey was administered electronically via SurveyMonkey and initially distributed via email on July 5, 2023, to all active ASPO members with registered email addresses. To improve response capture, a reminder email was sent on September 19, 2023, before being closed to responses on December 5, 2023. Participation was voluntary and responses were collected anonymously.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize respondent demographics and their responses to the survey. Data cleaning and descriptive analysis were performed in Microsoft Excel for Mac (Microsoft 365, Version 16.95, Build 24,063,023; Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) running on macOS Ventura 13.7.6.

Results

The initial survey was distributed to 783 ASPO members and was opened by 478 (61%). Of these, 138 responses were received (17.6%), representing a 28.9% response rate among those who opened the survey. Approximately 90% of respondents reported practicing in the United States, most in the Great Lakes (20.2%) and Mid‐Atlantic (17.2%) regions.

Approximately 42% of ASPO participants (57/135) reported performing 5–10 tracheostomies annually, followed by 38.5% performing more than 10 per year and 19.3% who described performing 0–5 annually. Of the 134 members who responded to question 3, 124 (92.5%) noted using a standardized protocol for post‐operative pediatric tracheostomy care (Figure S1; see Figures S1–S4 for full survey response distributions). Among these protocols, many members mentioned that their institutional checklist was reviewed at least yearly and updated as necessary.

Most (125/128; 97.7%) reported using stay sutures during tracheostomy as a means of facilitating reinsertion of the tracheostomy tube in the event of accidental decannulation. Only 25% (32/128) reported never maturing the stoma. The prevalent tracheostomy type was Bivona (72%), with 81.4% reporting abstention from suturing tracheostomy flanges to the skin and reporting satisfaction with Velcro ties (80%).

The first tracheostomy tube changes were most often performed on postoperative days 5 (70/128; 54.7%) and 7 (35/128; 27.3%). Although 74.4% (96/129 of respondents) performed their first tracheostomy change based on training/experience, 56.6% (73/129) followed their institutional protocol, compared to (26/129; 20.2%) who followed published research. If the tracheostomy change is scheduled to occur on a holiday or weekend, 86.8% of pediatric otolaryngologists (112/129) adjusted the timing accordingly. 58.6% (75/128) of respondents reported not stopping enteral feeding prior to tracheostomy tube change. 70.08% of respondents noted that their institutions sterilized custom tracheostomy tubes that arrive non‐sterile before use.

Most respondents (53/129; 58.9%) reported no complications during the first tracheostomy tube change. However, among those who reported a complication, the most common were creation of a false passage (46%) and bleeding (41%) (Table S1). No deaths were reported. Among respondents, 71.3% (92/129) reported encountering skin breakdown (stage II or higher ulceration) around the stoma and/or neck in less than 10% of cases.

Among the respondents, 63.6% indicated performing routine surveillance bronchoscopy. Of these, 34% noted performing the procedure twice within the initial year. Additionally, 25% reported performing annual surveillance during the first 2 years, whereas 23% described opting for once in the initial year, and 18% chose every 6 months during the first 2 years.

Discussion

To gain insights into the prevailing practices of managing post‐tracheostomy tube care among pediatric otolaryngologists, we conducted a comprehensive survey of ASPO members. This national survey was designed to address previously identified areas of uncertainty in pediatric tracheostomy care, including first‐change timing, surveillance bronchoscopy, peri‐change feeding, and sterilization of custom tubes. It highlights specific domains appropriate for further investigation and potential standardization. These domains were selected based on prior consensus statements, published surveys, and emerging clinical evidence demonstrating variability in practice or absence of definitive guidance.

Notably, the findings revealed that most respondents reported that their institution had a standardized protocol for postoperative tracheostomy care. This is important considering that previous research has found implementing standard protocols leads to reduced rates of skin breakdown, decreased occurrence of total rapid‐response call rates, and fewer acute respiratory emergencies due to mucus plugs [33, 34].

Early post‐operative decannulation is a feared complication of tracheostomies, carrying the risk of hypoxic brain injury and death. Tracheostomy tubes placed postoperatively are commonly secured either with skin sutures or ties alone. A 2008 survey of ASPO members found that 94% preferred stay sutures for tube security [28]. Similarly, our study found that a majority (97.7%) still opts for stay sutures. These sutures are typically removed during the first postoperative tube change.

Our survey results also found that most pediatric otolaryngologists prefer Velcro (79.8%) versus twill (20.2%) ties. Some surgeons advocate for twill ties due to their fixed length and reduced elasticity; others view them unfavorably due to their lack of cushioning and potential for skin‐related complications [29, 30]. On the other hand, some practitioners prefer Velcro ties because they are wider, softer, less moisture‐retentive, and easy to adjust [29]. Ultimately, previous studies have shown that there is no significant difference in accidental decannulation between using twill and Velcro ties [30].

The optimal timing for the initial change of a tracheostomy tube in pediatric patients is uncertain, and opinions regarding the most appropriate day for this procedure vary widely among pediatric otolaryngologists. A 2013 consensus panel established by the American Academy of Otolaryngology‐Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (AAO‐HNSF) suggested waiting until postoperative Day 5–7 to ensure the development of a mature stomal tract in children [16]. Interestingly, our findings reveal that approximately 83% of pediatric otolaryngologists adhere to this recommendation, performing the first tracheostomy change between postoperative Days 5 and 7. Despite research indicating the safety of earlier (Day 3–4) tracheostomy tube changes in children [20, 21, 22, 23], a significant portion of respondents (74.4%) base their timing on training experience and protocol (56.6%), whereas only a few rely on existing research studies (20.2%). Our study highlights a gap between current clinical practice and the most recent findings in the literature, indicating an opportunity for further exploration, and an avenue to provide definitive recommendations for improving patient care.

Currently, there are no clinical guidelines that address the utility of routine airway evaluation in children with tracheostomies. Our survey of ASPO members showed that 63.6% of respondents reported performing routine surveillance bronchoscopy. This is in agreement with a 2011 survey of ASPO members, wherein 59% of respondents had performed surveillance endoscopy at least annually on children younger than 2 years, with indwelling tracheostomy tubes [24]. Practice patterns were diverse, with 34% performing a bronchoscopy every 6 months, 25% annually for the first 2 years, 22.7% once in the first year, and 18.2% every 6 months in the first 2 years. The timing of surveillance bronchoscopies reported in the literature also varies [25]. Liu et al. performed initial surveillance laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy at a median of 1.6 months, with subsequent endoscopies approximately every 6 months [26]. Tom et al. performed surveillance direct laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy at 1 week and then at 6‐month intervals for at least 18 months post‐tracheostomy [27]. Prior research has demonstrated that there is no significant difference in the rate of abnormal airway lesions found in early (< 6 weeks) versus late (6–14 weeks) groups [35]. That being said, given the potential for granulation tissue, stomal narrowing, and tracheostomy size mismatch in a growing pediatric population, consistent surveillance may have a meaningful role. The variability within the literature is reflected in the variability in survey responses and represents a key finding that should be investigated. Further studies are needed to fully explore this issue, especially since growth is a dynamic factor present within the pediatric tracheostomy patient population that is not present in adult care.

The incidence of complications following tracheostomies varies across studies. Common postoperative issues observed in children encompass skin problems, granulomas, accidental tube removal, bleeding, infections, false passages, tube blockages, and localized infections [36]. Among our respondents, the most frequently reported complications were false passages, bleeding, and other occurrences, including skin breakdown, hemodynamic changes, oxygen desaturation, and aspiration. Notably, there were no reports of accidental tube removal or fatalities in this survey. Most respondents noted encountering complications such as skin breakdown (stage II or higher ulceration) around the stoma and/or neck in less than 10% of cases. Controversy exists in the literature regarding the relationship between stoma maturation and rates of tracheostomy‐related complications [31, 32]. Some authors might attribute the low incidence of post‐tracheostomy complications in our survey to factors like stomal maturation. Others might credit the widespread adoption of standardized protocols by most respondents. Further investigation through surveys examining the correlation between stoma maturation and the rate of post‐tracheostomy complications may be warranted.

There are limitations to this study. Considering that it was a survey study, it is important to acknowledge a possible response bias, with physicians with strong opinions toward pediatric perioperative tracheostomy care more likely to complete the survey. We also acknowledge the limitation of recall bias, especially with certain survey questions. Respondents may be more likely to over‐report pediatric tracheostomy experience and under‐report tracheostomy complications observed. Furthermore, demographic data such as age, sex, and ethnicity of respondents were not collected. Lastly, there was a low absolute survey response rate of 17.6% which we acknowledge as a limitation of the study.

Conclusion

This national ASPO survey highlights substantial variability in pediatric tracheostomy practices despite widespread adherence to standardized protocols. Key differences remain in the timing of the first tube change, surveillance bronchoscopy, and reported complications. These findings underscore the need for further research to inform evidence‐based, standardized postoperative care and improve outcomes for tracheostomized children.
