Deep medullary vein abnormalities associated with cognitive function and Alzheimer's disease plasma biomarkers in dementia‐free older adults: A population‐based study
Authors: Dejia Yin, Jiafeng Wang, Chunyan Li, Xiaoyu Wang, Yan Wang, Qianqian Xie, Na Tian, Ziwei Chen, Xuemin Sun, Yongxiang Wang, Tingting Hou, Lin Cong, Li Zhou, Yifeng Du, Lin Song, Chengxuan Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe investigated the associations of deep medullary vein (DMV) score with cognitive phenotypes and blood biomarkers for Alzheimer's disease among older adults.METHODSThis population‐based cross‐sectional study used data from 1206 participants in the Multimodal Interventions to Delay Dementia and Disability in Rural China (MIND‐China) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) substudy; of these, plasma amyloid‐β (Aβ), total tau, neurofilament light chain, phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217), and glial fibrillary acidic protein were measured in subsamples (n = 901∼1133).RESULTSA higher DMV score was significantly associated with increased likelihoods of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and amnestic MCI, and lower z‐scores of verbal fluency, memory, attention, executive function, and global cognition (all p < 0.05); such associations were significant only in males. In the biomarker subsamples, a higher DMV score was significantly associated with a lower plasma Aβ42/40 ratio (p < 0.05), but not with the other examined biomarkers.DISCUSSIONDiscontinuous DMVs might be a biomarker for MCI and poor cognitive function in older men, and the role of Alzheimer's pathology deserves further exploration.

BACKGROUND

The deep medullary veins (DMVs) are the small parenchymal veins run in the periventricular white matter. 1 , 2 In the past decade, susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) has emerged as a sensitive method to visualize small intracranial veins such as DMVs, and discontinuous DMVs seen on SWI could be a manifestation of periventricular venous collagenosis. 3 This structural alteration of the intracranial venous system could cause venous dysfunction. An autopsy‐verified case–control study associated venular collagenosis with Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology. 4

Several clinical‐based studies have shown that patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), amnestic MCI (aMCI), and early AD exhibit higher tortuosity, discontinuity or reduced density of DMVs. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Similarly, discontinuous DMVs were associated with poorer cognitive function in patients with cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) or subcortical infarction. 8 , 9 , 10 To date, the relationship between DMVs and cognition has been examined largely in small‐scale clinical studies of patients with cognitive disorders, where research findings could be generalized only to the selective patient populations. Thus, the potential associations of DMVs with MCI and function of different cognitive domains in the general population settings remain unclear. In addition, glymphatic activities are known to vary by age and sex 11 and DMVs play a pivotal role in glymphatic system function. 12 Furthermore, cognitive phenotypes differ by age and sex and previous research has correlated older age and male sex to lower glymphatic system function. 13 Thus, it is important to explore whether the associations of discontinuous DMVs with cognitive dysfunction vary by age and sex.

The mechanisms underlying the association between discontinuous DMVs and cognitive dysfunction are poorly understood. Research has suggested that the integrity of cerebral venous vessels, particularly the DMVs, may play a critical role in preserving perivascular glymphatic activity. 14 The glymphatic system could promote efficient clearance of plasma amyloid‐β (Aβ) in the central nervous system, 15 whereas dysfunction of the system may decrease Aβ clearance and accelerate its accumulation in the brain, which could contribute to cognitive impairment. 16 Given that DMVs are an important component of glymphatic system, 12 it is plausible to hypothesize that discontinuous DMVs may be associated with plasma Aβ. Furthermore, the relationships of DMVs with peripheral AD‐related biomarkers (e.g., Aβ and phosphorylated tau 217 [p‐tau217]), astrogliosis (e.g., glial fibrillary acidic protein [GFAP]), and neurodegeneration (e.g., neurofilament light chain [NfL]) have yet to be explored. 17 Exploring the associations of DMV measures with AD‐related biomarkers may shed light on the potential mechanisms underlying the association of DMVs with cognitive phenotypes.

Therefore, in this population‐based study of rural‐dwelling Chinese older adults, we sought to examine the associations of discontinuous DMVs with MCI and function of various cognitive domains, while taking into account modifying effect of demographics, and further, to investigate the relationships between DMVs and blood biomarkers of amyloid pathology, astrogliosis, and neurodegeneration. We hypothesized that discontinuous DMVs were associated with MCI, low cognitive function, and plasma AD‐related biomarkers in older adults.

Systematic review: The authors searched PubMed and Web of Science for relevant literature. Several small‐scale clinical‐based studies have examined deep medullary veins (DMVs) in association with cognitive outcomes and plasma Alzheimer's biomarkers among selective clinical samples of patients with cognitive disorders as summarized in our paper, but their relationships have not yet been examined in the general populations. Interpretation: Findings from our population‐based study support the potential that discontinuous DMVs may be a valuable neuroimaging marker for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and poor cognitive function as well as for Alzheimer's pathologies in older adults, which extends research findings from clinical settings to the general older population settings. METHODS

Study design and participants

This population‐based cross‐sectional study used data derived from the Multimodal Interventions to Delay Dementia and Disability in Rural China (MIND‐China), a participating project in the World‐Wide FINGERS Network. 18 The baseline assessments of MIND‐China were reported elsewhere. 19 Briefly, the MIND‐China study targeted people who were aged ≥60 years and living in all the 52 villages from Yanlou Town, Yanggu County, western Shandong Province. In March–September 2018, 5765 participants (74.9% of all eligible people) undertook the baseline examination that consisted of epidemiological, clinical, and neuropsychological assessments. Of these, 1844 participants from 26 villages that were randomly selected from all the 52 villages of Yanlou Town using the cluster (village)‐based sampling approach were invited for the magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) substudy. In September 2018–November 2020, 1304 (70.7%) participants undertook the structural brain MRI scans at either Southwestern Lu Hospital (n = 1178) or Liaocheng People's Hospital (n = 126). The mean time interval between cognitive assessments and MRI acquisition was 1.39 (standard deviation [SD] = 0.62) years. Of them, 98 were excluded due to missing SWI sequence or poor image quality (n = 49), prevalent dementia (n = 8), missing data on the diagnosis of MCI (n = 18), or large brain lesions that affected reliable DMV assessment (n = 23), leaving 1206 participants for the current analysis. Data collection and definitions

In March–September 2018, interdisciplinary assessments for all participants of MIND‐China were conducted following a structured questionnaire by trained medical staff through face‐to‐face interviews, clinical examinations, neuropsychological testing, or laboratory tests, as previously reported. 19 We collected data on demographics (age, sex, and education), lifestyles (e.g., alcohol consumption and smoking), health conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease), use of medications (e.g., antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid‐lowering agents), and apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype. Weight and height were measured in light clothes without shoes and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2). We used an electronic sphygmomanometer to measure arterial blood pressure on the right arm in a seated position after at least a 5‐minute rest. Peripheral blood samples were taken after an overnight fast. Fasting blood glucose and lipids were measured at the clinical laboratory of Yanlou Town Hospital. Smoking and alcohol drinking habits were classified as never and ever smoking or drinking. All medications were classified and coded according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) Classification System, as described previously. 20 Hypertension was defined as arterial blood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg or current use of any antihypertensive drugs (ATC codes C02, C03, and C07–C09). Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥7 mmol/L, self‐reported physician diagnosis of diabetes, or current use of any antidiabetic agents or insulin injection (ATC code A10). Hyperlipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥6.2 mmol/L, triglycerides ≥2.3 mmol/L, low density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥4.1 mmol/L, high density lipoprotein cholesterol ≤1.0 mmol/L, or current use of any hypolipidemic agents (ATC code C10). 21 APOE genotype was performed using the multiple‐polymerase chain reaction amplification, 22 and was dichotomized as carriers vs. noncarriers of the ε4 allele.

MRI data acquisition and processing

All eligible participants were scanned on either the Philips Ingenia 3.0T MR System (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) in Southwestern Lu Hospital or the Philips Archiva 3.0T MR System (Philips Healthcare, Best, The Netherlands) in Liaocheng People's Hospital using 16‐channel head‐neck coil, as described previously. 19 The core MRI sequence was axial SWI, and the SWI parameters were fully described elsewhere. 19 Briefly, the parameters for SWI sequence in Southwestern Lu Hospital were: repetition time (TR) = 18.00 ms, echo time (TE) = 25.00 ms, field of view (FOV) = 221×182 mm2, matrix = 220 × 183, flip angle (FA) = 10°, and thickness = 1.0 mm. In the Liaocheng People's Hospital, the parameters for SWI sequence were: TR = 16.00 ms, TE = 22.00 ms, FOV = 221 × 182 mm2, matrix = 220 × 182, FA = 15°, and thickness = 2.0 mm.

Measurement of DMVs

DMVs were assessed on the SWI sequence, as previously reported. 23 Briefly, we assessed DMVs on five consecutive periventricular slices (10 mm in total thickness, with the interval of 2 mm between consecutive slices) of SWI images from the level of the ventricles immediately above the basal ganglia to the level of the ventricles immediately disappeared, considering that these slices contain most of DMVs. According to the anatomy of medullary venous, the brain regions containing DMVs included six subregions, that is, bilateral frontal, parietal, and occipital regions. Then, we evaluated the characteristics of DMVs in each of the six subregions based on their continuity and visibility. 23

The following 4‐point scale was used for semiquantitative assessment of DMVs 23 : Grade 0—each vein was continuous, clearly visible, and had homogeneous signal; Grade 1—each vein was continuous, clearly visible, but at least one vein had inhomogeneous signal; Grade 2—at least one vein were faintly visible and discontinuous, presented with spotlike hypointensity; and Grade 3—no observed vein was found continuous (Figure S1). The total DMV score is the sum of DMV scores from the six subregions and ranges from 0 to 18. The vein changes in all images were visually assessed by two trained junior neurologists (D.Y. and J.W.) who were blinded to participants’ clinical data. The intra‐ and inter‐rater reliability metrics of DMV assessment of the two raters were checked by reassessing the 50 randomly selected images three months after the initial evaluation, with the intraclass correlation coefficient of intra‐ and inter‐rater reliability being 0.907 and 0.878, respectively.

Neuropsychological assessments

A neuropsychological test battery was used to assess cognitive function, as previously described. 19 , 22 Briefly, subjective cognitive decline was assessed via three questions of memory problems that the participants experienced in the past year, including difficulty remembering, forgetting what had been planned, and worrying about memory decline. 22 , 24 The Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR) was used to assess cognitive changes. We used the Chinese version of the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) to assess global cognitive function, and the Chinese version of the Activities of Daily Living Scale to assess self‐care and instrumental activities of daily living (ADLs). We evaluated function of four cognitive domains 22 : memory, verbal fluency, attention, and executive function. Memory function was assessed using the Auditory Verbal Learning Test, immediate recall, long‐delayed free recall, and long‐delayed recognition; verbal fluency was assessed using the Verbal Fluency Test, including animal, fruit, and vegetable categories; attention function was assessed with the Trail Making Test(Part A) and Digit Span Forward test; and executive function was assessed using the Digit Span Test (backward test) and Trail Making Test (Part B). For the Trail Making Test, we used the score divided by the time taken to complete the test. The raw score of each cognitive test was first standardized into z‐score according to the age‐ and education‐specific mean and SD among all dementia‐free participants in MIND‐China. Because all cognitive domains were assessed using more than one test, we calculated the composite z‐score for each of the cognitive domains by averaging the standard z‐scores of all tests for that domain. A composite z‐score for global cognition was computed by averaging all cognitive z‐scores for individuals with data available in at least two cognitive domains. Objective cognitive impairment was defined as scoring ≥1.0 SD below the age‐ and education‐specific mean in any of the four cognitive domains.

Diagnosis of MCI and subtypes of MCI

We defined MCI following the Petersen's criteria, 25 to which an operational approach that integrated neuropsychological test with clinical consensus was applied 22 : (1) subjective cognitive concern by the subject (according to participant's responses to the three questions of memory problems) or informant (CDR ≥0.5); (2) objective cognitive impairment evidenced in at least one of the four cognitive domains (cognitive z‐score ≥1.0 SD below mean score of the age‐ and education‐specific groups); (3) essentially preserved function of daily activities; and (4) absence of dementia diagnosed according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorder, Fourth Revision (DSM‐IV) criteria. The final judgment about MCI was based on both neuropsychological test scores and a consensus agreement among neurologists specialized in clinical diagnosis, treatment, and care of dementia and cognitive disorders. We further classified MCI into aMCI if the memory domain was impaired, or non‐amnestic MCI (naMCI) if there was no impairment in memory function.

Measurement of plasma biomarkers

After an overnight fast, peripheral blood samples were collected into the ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid citrate vacutainer tubes and centrifuged in a tabletop centrifuge. Plasma samples were then aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Plasma biomarkers were measured using the single‐molecule array (Simoa) platform (Quanterix Corp, MA) according to the manufacturer's instructions (Wayen Biotechnologies Inc., Shanghai). 26 We used the Human Neurology 3‐Plex A assay to measure plasma total tau, the NF‐light® Advantage Kit for plasma NfL, the ACC pTau217 Assay for p‐tau217, and the GFAP single‐molecule array (Simoa) Discovery Assay Kit for GFAP. Two quality control plasma samples were run in duplicate on each plate for each analyte. The intra‐assay and inter‐assay coefficients of variability were both controlled within 15%. 27

Statistical analysis

Characteristics of study participants by cognitive status were compared using Student's t‐test for continuous variables with normal distribution, Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables with skewed distribution, and chi square (χ 2) test for categorical variables. We used binary logistic regression models to examine the relationship of DMV score (as a continuous variable) with MCI and multinomial logistic regression models to estimate the relationship of DMV score with aMCI and naMCI.

General linear regression models were used to examine the associations of DMV scores with cognitive z‐scores and blood biomarkers. We employed the restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis to examine the patterns of relationships between DMV score and cognitive z‐scores. Prior to the data analysis, plasma NfL, p‐tau217 (×1000), and GFAP concentrations were log‐transformed owing to skewed distributions. 27 We presented the main results from two models in which different covariables were included because they were potentially related to both DMVs (Table S1) and cognitive outcomes or plasma biomarkers in the literature 28 , 29 : Model 1 was adjusted for demographics (age, sex, and education), and in Model 2, we additionally adjusted for lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and alcohol drinking), metabolic health conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia), MRI scan center, and APOE genotype to assess their confounding effects. Finally, we entered the independent variables and their cross‐product term into the model simultaneously to test the statistical interactions of DMV scores with age and sex on the likelihoods of MCI, aMCI, and naMCI, cognitive z‐scores, and plasma biomarkers. Further stratified analyses were performed when a statistical interaction was detected at p < 0.05.

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) was used for all statistical analyses. A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Characteristics of study participants

The mean age of the 1206 participants was 69.38 (SD = 4.29) years, 58.5% were women, and the mean years of school education were 3.56 years (SD = 3.58). Of these, MCI was defined in 280 persons (25.1%), including 238 with aMCI and 42 with naMCI. Individuals with MCI (versus normal cognition) were older, less educated, and more likely to be females, less likely to smoke and consume alcohol, and more likely to have hypertension, a higher DMV score, a lower MMSE score, and lower z‐scores of four cognitive domains and global cognition (p < 0.05). The two groups did not differ significantly in mean BMI and the distribution of diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and APOE ϵ4 allele (p > 0.05). In the plasma biomarker subsamples, individuals with MCI had higher plasma GFAP than those without (p < 0.05), but the two groups had no significant differences at p < 0.05 in other plasma biomarkers (Table 1).

Associations of DMV score with cognitive function (n = 1206)

A higher DMV score was significantly associated with lower scores of MMSE, global cognition, and four cognitive domains in both model 1 and model 2 (all p < 0.05) except that the significant association with attention z‐score in model 1 became statistically marginal in model 2 (p = 0.057) (Table 3). The RCS analysis suggested roughly linear relationships of DMV score with MMSE score and z‐scores of the four cognitive domains, and the significant associations of DMV score with MMSE score and z‐scores of global cognition and all cognitive domains except attention (all p for overall < 0.05), although the nonlinear relationships with memory and verbal fluency z‐scores were significant (p for nonlinear < 0.05) (Figure S2).

Interactions of DMV score with age and sex on MCI and cognitive z‐scores

We detected a statistically marginal interaction of a higher DMV score with sex on MCI (p for interaction = 0.052) and a significant interaction with sex on aMCI (p for interaction = 0.048). Stratified analyses by sex revealed that the association of higher DMV score with MCI and aMCI was statistically significant in male participants but not in female participants (Figure 2A). Similarly, we also detected statistically marginal or significant interactions of higher DMV scores with sex on z‐scores of global cognition (p for interaction = 0.042), verbal fluency (p for interaction = 0.060), and executive function (p for interaction = 0.025). Further analysis stratified by sex suggested that a higher DMV score was significantly associated with lower z‐scores of global cognition, verbal fluency, and executive function among male participants, but not in female participants (Figure 2B). We detected no statistical interaction of DMV score with age on MCI, MCI subtypes, and cognitive z‐scores, neither with age or sex on plasma biomarkers (all p for interaction > 0.05).

DMV score and peripheral AD‐related biomarkers

In the biomarker subsample, adjusting for multiple confounders, a higher DMV score was significantly associated with a lower Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, but not with plasma total tau, NfL, p‐tau217, and GFAP in both models (Table 4). There were no statistically significant interactions of DMV score with age or sex on any of the examined biomarkers (all p for interaction > 0.10).

DISCUSSION

In this population‐based study of dementia‐free older adults in China, the main findings can be summarized as follows: (1) a higher DMV score was associated with increased likelihoods of MCI and aMCI and lower scores of MMSE, verbal fluency, memory, attention, executive function, and global cognition, independent of sociodemographic, genetic, behavioral, and clinical factors; (2) the associations of higher DMV scores with MCI and aMCI as well as worse cognitive function varied with sex, such that the associations were evident only in males, and (3) a higher DMV score was correlated with plasma biomarkers for AD (e.g., a lower plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio) but not for astrogliosis (e.g., GFAP) and neurodegeneration (e.g., NfL).

The relationship of DMVs with cognitive phenotypes has been examined in several small‐scale clinical‐based studies of selective patient samples. For instance, a clinical‐based case‐control study (n = 105) showed that a higher DMV score was correlated with MCI and poorer performance in memory, attention, executive function, and global cognition. 7 Similarly, a hospital‐based study of 94 patients with CSVD associated discontinuous DMVs with impaired global cognition, memory, verbal fluency, and attention. 8 To the best of our knowledge, no population‐based studies have so far explored the relationships of DMV parameters with cognitive phenotypes. Our community‐based study revealed for the first time that the higher DMV score was associated with MCI, especially aMCI, and lower function in global cognition and multiple cognitive domains in the general population of dementia‐free older people. The cognitive consequences of discontinuous DMVs in older people should be further characterized in future prospective cohort studies.

Of note, we detected statistical interactions of higher DMV score with sex on MCI and poorer cognition, such that their associations existed only in males. Indeed, population‐based studies suggested that compared to women, men had lower glymphatic activity 13 and were more vulnerable to vascular risk factors, 30 factors that were associated with glymphatic dysfunction. The sex‐dependent association between discontinuous DMVs and cognitive phenotypes is in line with the view that Aβ clearance was more vulnerable to discontinuous DMVs in men than in women probably owing to the lower glymphatic system function in men. 13 In addition, although DMV score was associated with poor function in non‐memory cognitive domains, we found no significant association with naMCI, partly due to limited statistical power because only 42 participants had naMCI. Therefore, future large‐scale population‐based cohort studies are warranted to clarify the relationships between DMVs and naMCI in older people.

Our population‐based study revealed for the first time the association between DMVs and low plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, a validated biomarker for early brain Aβ accumulation. 31 However, we found no association of DMV score with plasma p‐tau217‒a biomarker for both amyloid and tau pathology in the brain, 32 neither with plasma NfL‒a biomarker for neurodegeneration (e.g., synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss) or plasma GFAP‒a biomarker for neuroinflammation. This may be due to the well‐established sequential pathological cascade of AD initiation and progression, 33 that is, Aβ starts to accumulate prior to triggering tau protein deposits in the brain, followed by neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. Our population‐based study consisted of community‐dwelling dementia‐free older adults, with cognitive status ranging from normal cognition and subtle cognitive alterations to preclinical and prodromal dementia, and with some individuals being at an early stage of Aβ deposits, but not yet advanced stages of significant p‐tau accumulations, neuroinflammation, and neurodegeneration. Alternatively, the lack of association of DMV score with plasma NfL and GFAP may indicate that structural DMV impairment might not be a key part of neurodegeneration or astrogliosis in the brain. Further prospective cohort studies are warranted to clarify the relationships between structural DMV markers and plasma biomarkers for various types of brain pathology.

Evidence has suggested that venous narrowing might impair venous drainage, thus leading to Aβ accumulation in the brain. 16 Venous collagenosis, a degenerative sign in the periventricular and subependymal veins, can cause luminal stenosis or even venous occlusion, leading to chronic cerebral venous dysfunction. 2 , 12 , 34 , 35 The glymphatic system facilitated Aβ clearance via perivascular spaces, 36 and venous narrowing impaired the glymphatic system function, thus leading to Aβ accumulation. 16 In addition, research suggested that a higher DMV score was associated with the lower diffusion tensor imaging along the perivascular space (DTI‐ALPS) index, a potential imaging biomarker of glymphatic dysfunction, which supports the link between impaired DMVs and impaired glymphatic function. 14 Furthermore, a clinical‐based study showed that the disruption of DMVs was associated with cognitive impairment in patients with CSVD, partly mediated by the DTI‐ALPS index. 37 Given that DMVs may represent an integral part of the glymphatic system, 12 it is plausible to hypothesize that the stenosis and dysfunction of DMVs due to venous collagenosis may lead to the impedance of the efflux of perivenous glymphatic fluid and inadequate drainage of glymphatic clearance, which further causes Aβ accumulation in the brain and attributes to cognitive impairment. 14 , 35 , 38 , 39

In addition, emerging evidence has linked higher DMV score with CSVD markers such as white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) and enlarged perivascular spaces, which may contribute to cognitive impairment. For instance, previous studies have reported that DMV score is associated with cognitive impairment, partially mediated through WMHs. 8 , 40 This is plausible given that WMHs reflect impaired white matter tracts, which could lead to decreased network connectivity and reduced global network function. 8 Studies have also linked a higher DMV score with enlarged basal ganglia perivascular spaces, a radiological marker of glymphatic function failure. 41 These studies support the associations of discontinuous DMVs, glymphatic dysfunction, and cognitive impairment. Thus, the discontinuation of DMVs in older adults could be a potential neuroimaging marker for MCI and subtle cognitive impairment and for early cerebral Aβ deposits.

The major strengths of our study include the population‐based study design, use of state‐of‐the‐art techniques (e.g., Simoa platform) to assess plasma biomarkers for brain pathologies, and the integration of high‐quality 3.0T MRI data with comprehensive clinical and neurocognitive data. In addition, our study engaged older adults who were living in rural communities in China and who had received no or very limited school education, a sociodemographic group that has been substantially underrepresented in brain aging research. 42 However, our study has some limitations. First, the cross‐sectional nature of this study does not allow us to infer the causal or temporal relationships for any of the observed associations. Second, we assessed the DMVs according to a semiquantitative rating scale, which may be less accurate than the quantitative assessment of DMVs. Third, we used SWI from a 3.0T MRI system to assess DMVs, which was less sensitive than SWI from a 7.0T MRI system that allowed to identify more DMVs, especially smaller and distal branches. Finally, our study sample was derived from only one rural area in China, and participants in the MRI substudy were younger, healthier, and more educated than those in the MIND‐China total sample, 22 which should be kept in mind when generalizing our findings to other rural populations.

In conclusion, our population‐based study showed that the discontinuation of DMVs was associated with MCI, aMCI, and low function in global cognition and four cognitive domains in rural older adults and that such associations were evident only in males. Furthermore, we revealed that a higher DMV score was associated with a lower plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio, the biomarker for brain Aβ pathology. These findings increase our understanding regarding the relationships of discontinuous DMVs with cognitive dysfunction and the potential neuropathological mechanisms underlying these associations. Future prospective cohort studies are warranted to further determine their causal relationships and the underlying biological and neuropathological mechanisms.
