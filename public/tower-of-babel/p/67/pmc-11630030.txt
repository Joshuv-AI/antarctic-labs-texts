Inter-rater variability in multidisciplinary team meetings of oesophageal and gastro-oesophageal junction cancer on staging, resectability and treatment recommendation: national retrospective multicentre study
Authors: Christine Jestin Hannan, Solange León Risso, Mats Lindblad, Louiza Loizou, Eva Szabo, David Edholm, Wolf Claus Bartholomä, Oscar Åkesson, Fredrik Lindberg, Sara Strandberg, Gustav Linder, Jakob Hedberg
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are differences in oesophageal cancer care across Sweden. According to national guidelines, all patients should be offered equal care, planned and administrated by regional multidisciplinary team meetings. The aim of the study was to investigate differences between regional multidisciplinary team meetings in Sweden regarding clinical staging and treatment recommendations for oesophageal cancer patients.MethodsAll six Swedish regional multidisciplinary teams were each invited to retrospectively include ten consecutive oesophageal cancer cases. After anonymization, radiological investigations were presented, along with the original case-specific medical history, anew at the participating regional multidisciplinary team meetings. Estimation of clinical tumour node metastasis (TNM) classification and treatment recommendation (curative, palliative or best supportive care) were compared between multidisciplinary team meetings as well as with original assessments.ResultsFive multidisciplinary teams participated and contributed a total of 50 cases presented to each multidisciplinary team. In estimations of cT-stage, the multidisciplinary teams were in total agreement in only eight of 50 cases (16%). For cN-stage, total agreement was seen in 17 of 50 cases (34%) and for cM-stage there was agreement in 34 cases (68%). For cT-stage, the overall summarized κ value was 0.57. For N-stage and M-stage the κ values were 0.66 and 0.78 respectively. Differences in appraisal were not associated with usage of positron emission tomography-computed tomography. In 15 of 50 cases (30%) the multidisciplinary teams disagreed on curative or palliative treatment.ConclusionThe study shows differences in assessment of clinical TNM classification and treatment recommendations made at regional multidisciplinary team meetings. Increased interrater agreement on clinical TNM classification and management plans are essential to achieve more equal care for oesophageal cancer patients in Sweden.

Introduction

With modern multimodal cancer treatment, survival for oesophageal and gastro-oesophageal junction (GOJ) cancers has recently improved to around 20%1–3. A multidisciplinary approach has proven beneficial for the patient and centres have established multidisciplinary team meetings (MDTs), where different healthcare professionals specialized in oesophageal cancer discuss the best possible management of presented patients4,5. In 2007, less than 60% of all patients with oesophageal cancer in Sweden were presented at an MDT. Ten years later, over 90% of all patients were referred for discussion at one of the six regional MDTs6. Even patients in whom the expectation for cure is low or non-existent can benefit from an MDT since it can disseminate knowledge of new trials (curative or palliative), result in better palliation, and generally facilitate and coordinate the care of each patient7,8. In Sweden, national guidelines therefore recommend that all newly diagnosed oesophageal cancer patients are presented at an MDT9,10. At these conferences, the clinical staging and first treatment recommendation is generally agreed upon.

Swedish healthcare is obliged, by law, to provide equal care for all citizens regardless of sex, place of residence, income and socioeconomic status. Our group has recently identified geographical differences in the proportion of patients with oesophageal cancer being recommended curative care and tumour resection within Sweden11. The reasons behind these differences are likely to be multifactorial, and we hypothesize that the assessment and decisions in MDTs might have a significant impact. Of note, a north European multicentre study on pancreatic cancer suggests that there are differences in staging and recommended treatments at different MDTs12. To date, there is a paucity of studies regarding the mechanisms whereby MDT decision-making can diverge in oesophageal cancer. To address this lack of knowledge, the aim of the present study was to investigate differences, if any, between the outcomes of all regional MDTs in Sweden regarding clinical staging and recommended treatment of oesophageal cancer.

Methods

In this multicentre national study, all six regional MDTs at university hospitals in Sweden (Umeå, Uppsala-Örebro, Stockholm, Linköping, Göteborg, Lund), specializing in oesophageal cancer, were invited to participate. For the MDT to fulfil requirements and be classified as a complete MDT at least one surgeon, one oncologist and one radiologist had to be present. Ten consecutive cases from each participating centre were selected, anonymized and presented at all participating MDTs. All study cases were initially evaluated at MDTs between 13 January 2022 and 9 December 2022. Estimations of clinical stage according to the 8th edition of the AJCC/UICC tumour node metastasis (TNM) classification system and treatment allocation (curative, palliative or best supportive care) were compared between MDTs as well as with the assessment at the original MDT.

The study was approved by the Regional Ethical Committee in Stockholm 2013-05-02, with supplementary application 2020-12-29. Diary numbers: 2013/596-31/3 and 2020-06495.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Each participating centre identified the first ten consecutive cases presented at the MDT starting from 1 January 2021. The inclusion criteria were oesophageal or GOJ adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma, Siewert I and II (diagnosis code according to ICD10; C15, C16.0a, and C16.0b). Only new/primary cancers with a completed preoperative work-up allowing for treatment recommendations to be made were included. Patients under the age of 18 years or with neuroendocrine tumour or sarcoma of the oesophagus were excluded.

Study population and distribution process

The medical charts of included patient cases were reviewed for available information on age, sex, medical history, symptoms, tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption and WHO performance status. This information was presented together with anonymized radiology images anew at the MDTs. Only the information available at the time of the original MDTs was taken into account. Each included case received a unique study identification number and the case-corresponding data was anonymized. The radiological examinations, CT and/or 18F-fluoro-deoxy-glucose (FDG)-positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT), available at the original MDTs were also anonymized. At each participating centre, a radiologist with previous experience within the field reviewed and presented the available images at an MDT.

Evaluation at the MDTs

Cases were presented at all participating centres, at between 1 and 2 years after the original assessment, in identical order from 1 to 50, in groups of 10 from participating centres. A study-specific form for data collection was distributed to all participating centres to be completed during the MDT. The MDT registered its evaluation of cTNM-stage, for T-stage (T0–T4b), N-stage (N0–N3) and M-stage (M0 or M1). The MDT also registered whether cases were considered fit for surgical resection (yes or no) and whether the tumour was considered resectable (yes or no). Additionally, the MDT decided which treatment to recommend, treatment options being curative treatment (perioperative chemotherapy and surgery, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and surgery, surgery, definitive chemoradiotherapy or endoscopic treatment), palliative treatment (chemoradiotherapy, chemotherapy, radiotherapy or stent) or best supportive care. Further, the level of difficulty in assessing the study-specific case (easy, intermediate or hard) and whether or not there was a need for the case to be presented at a national MDT (yes or no) was also registered. Participating centres were blinded to assessments made at the original MDT and at other parallel regional MDTs and clinicians were asked not to discuss study cases outside of the MDT setting.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the cases’ characteristics. Numbers and percentages were used for categorical variables and median with interquartile range for continuous variables. To analyse the discrepancy in T-, N- and M-stage weighted Cohens κ was used between the five new MDTs pairwise and summarized. The κ value was interpreted as follows: < 0.00 = poor agreement, 0.00–0.20 = slight agreement, 0.21–0.40 = fair agreement, 0.41–0.60 = moderate agreement, 0.61–0.80 = substantial agreement, 0.81–1.00 = almost perfect agreement13–15. Visualization of the agreement between centres for T-, N- and M-stage were presented as bar charts. To test if additional examination with PET-CT affected the agreement between the MDTs, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. Agreement on recommended treatment was calculated as percentage and presented visually. Statistical analysis was performed using Stata® version 1516 and R statistical software version 4.3.017.

Results

All six regional MDTs administered by the tertiary oesophageal cancer centres in Sweden were invited to participate in the study, of which five accepted. This resulted in a total of 50 anonymized cases available for presentation to the MDTs. The median age of included patient cases was 73 years; 39 were men. The histological subtype in the majority of cases was adenocarcinoma, 64% (n = 32), while 22% (n = 11) had squamous cell carcinoma and 14% (n = 7) had undifferentiated histology. All cases had a CT of the chest and abdomen and in 37 cases (74%) an FDG-PET-CT was also available (Table 1).

Cases were reviewed anew at all five participating centres, with only a few exceptions due to occasional capacity limitation at participating MDTs (Figs. 1, 2). In estimations of cTNM, the six MDT assessments (including the original assessment and the five subsequent regional MDTs) were in exact agreement on cT-stage in eight (16%) cases, cN-stage in 17 (34%) cases and for cM-stage in 34 (68%). When grouping T-stage as three categories, T0–T1, T2–T4a and T4b, the MDTs were in complete agreement in 34 of 50 cases (68%). For 12 (24%) cases, the MDTs’ cT staging included both cT4b and less advanced T-stages for the same case, and in 10 (20%) cases the different MDTs’ cM staging included both M0 and M1 for the same case (Fig. 1). When comparing the five new assessments, there was a statistically significant difference in the evaluation of cT-stage between the MDTs, while differences in evaluation of cN- and cM-stage were non-significant (Table 2).

To examine if FDG-PET-CT affected the diversity in assessment of T-, N- and M-stage, the interrater difference was calculated for each case. A Mann–Whitney U test showed no difference in range in the assessment of T-stage (P = 0.394), N-stage (P = 0.734) or M-stage (P = 0.750) for the cases where a PET-CT was available compared with the cases without PET-CT.

The results regarding appraisal of resectability and recommended treatment (curative, palliative or best supportive care) are presented in Fig. 2. In 20 cases, one or more MDTs had not provided a treatment recommendation. The six MDT evaluations, five new and one original, were in complete agreement on curative treatment, palliative treatment or best supportive care in 29 (58%) study cases. In 15 (30%) cases, the same case was recommended curative treatment by some MDTs while others recommended palliative treatment. In six cases (12%), the recommendation differed between palliative treatment and best supportive care. In the cases where there was a difference in treatment recommendation between curative and palliative care (n = 15), the MDTs also had different opinions regarding the patient’s ability to tolerate curative treatment (n = 12 of 15, 80%). In some cases, the differences were also/instead due to disagreement on the tumour being considered resectable or not (n = 8 of 15, 53%).

Discussion

This study identified large differences in MDT assessment of clinical T-, N- and M-stage as well as recommended treatment on the same oesophageal and GOJ cancer. The results suggest moderate to substantial agreement between the assessment of five individual MDTs.

There are previous studies supporting a multidisciplinary team approach which leads to better staging and potentially more often treatment recommendations according to guidelines4,18,19. Even though MDTs are recommended for all oesophageal cancer patients in Sweden, this study highlights the difficulty in exact assessment of cTNM even at MDTs with expertise on oesophageal cancer. This may raise initiatives for radiological consensus conferences. It might also inform researchers on the reliability of clinical stage appraisal that might be incorporated into statistical models based on similar data. Additionally, the results support the possibility of integrating machine learning tools when evaluating CT and PET-CT images to increase the accuracy in estimation of cTNM-stage in the future20,21, or investigate the result of more liberal referral for second opinion in difficult cases. Moreover, quantification of the diagnostic inaccuracy in real-life conditions, provided in this article, might aid the evaluation of risk prediction tools based on these variables. It can also add valuable information in discussions with patients and their families.

Differences in evaluations at MDTs have also been demonstrated in oligometastatic disease in a recent study on the assessment of 15 gastro-oesophageal cancer patients with oligometastatic disease at 49 different MDTs in Europe by Kroese et al.22. The authors found more than 75% agreement on whether the patient had oligometastatic disease, although in these cases there was less than 75% agreement on recommended treatment. Even though referring patients to expert MDTs is recommended for numerous malignancies today, differences in the assessment between different MDTs is not unique to gastro-oesophageal cancer. In a study by Kirkegard et al.12 on differences in assessment of resectability in pancreatic cancer, the authors found the agreement on T-, N- and M-stage ranged from moderate to near perfect, although the MDTs were only in complete agreement on treatment recommendation in 1 of 19 (5%) cases. An important difference compared with our study and the study of pancreatic cancer was that in the study by Kirkegard et al., the MDTs were given the option ‘further investigation is needed’, which was commonly used and might have affected the results.

To assess interrater agreements between participating centres regarding clinical T-, N- and M-stage Cohens weighted κ was used. The interpretation is likely to be affected by a different number of categories for the different variables. The κ value is automatically higher with fewer categories, which would affect that there was substantial agreement in M-stages with two categories (M0 and M1) while there was only moderate agreement in T-stages with six categories (T0, T1, T2, T3, T4a and T4b). The result of moderate to substantial agreement may be sufficient in other fields, although as there are no non-invasive biomarkers to diagnose and help predict the spread of oesophageal cancer, the recommended treatment relies greatly on the radiologists cTNM evaluation. Therefore, one could argue that an almost perfect agreement between MDTs is desirable. On the other hand, certain discrepancy in cTNM-stage has little or no value in the decision on recommended treatment. According to European guidelines (European Society for Medical Oncology clinical practice guideline)23 no difference in treatment is made between T2–T4a and N1–N3, while discrepancy between M0 and M1 as well as T2–T4a with T4b has considerable importance on recommended treatment.

When the cases were re-evaluated for the purpose of the study during the MDTs, it was not possible to get the pathologist to re-evaluate the biopsy or show images from endoscopy. This might have increased the difficulty in assessing the T-stage and increased the variability between the MDTs. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) was not used in this study for the assessment of T-stage as EUS does not have widespread use in the routine work-up of oesophageal cancer in Sweden. One reason being that EUS is considered highly user dependent24,25 and is, therefore, only used in selective cases. In Sweden, PET-CT is seen as the standard modality to determine nodal and metastatic disease.

A radiologist with previous experience of oesophageal cancer at each centre was asked to assess the CT and, if available, FTG-PET-CT, before the MDT. In some cases, the radiologist might have been used to different CT and FTG-PET-CT imaging protocols, which might have made the assessment of cTNM-stage more difficult. However, all radiologists participating in the study were given the same material to evaluate in all cases.

In a recent study by van Doesburg et al.26 of diagnostic work-up before MDT in Sweden and the Netherlands almost 20% of all patients were re-evaluated at an MDT at least once due to the need for further investigation. In our study, only the information available at the original MDT was available at the re-evaluation. Some of the MDTs occasionally requested further investigations before making decisions on cTNM and recommended treatment, but this was not possible due to the design of the study. The MDTs might, therefore, in some cases have felt obligated to make a premature decision.

FDG-PET-CT is used for diagnostics and staging in the majority of oesophageal cancer patients presented at specialized MDTs in Sweden as it increases the accuracy in N- and M-staging compared with standalone CT27. In the present study, where 37 of 50 (74%) cases had a PET-CT available, the presence of a PET-CT did not seem to increase the agreement in cTNM-stage between the MDTs. Of the patients who did not undergo PET-CT, nine (69%) were octogenarians, one was endoscopically treated, around half had obvious M1 disease and ten (77%) were deemed as palliative up front (data not shown) and, therefore, did not undergo further examinations. This would explain why the addition of FDG-PET-CT compared with the few non-PET cases did not correlate with a better agreement in staging between MDTs. If instead the patient’s performance status ruled out the option of curative treatment, a PET-CT might not have been of much value for the MDT decision and might, therefore, not have been performed.

When assessing the recommended treatment, the different MDTs were in complete agreement in just above half of the cases (29 of 50 cases) whether to recommend curative treatment, palliative treatment or best supportive care. Even in cases where all MDTs agreed, recommendation for curative treatment might still have differed between perioperative chemotherapy plus surgery, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy plus surgery or upfront surgery. In 15 of 50 cases, the MDTs had different opinions on whether to recommend curative or palliative treatment. In the majority of these cases there were differences in recommended treatment between curative and palliative care, the MDTs also had different opinions regarding the patient’s ability to tolerate curative treatment. Differences in assessment of the patient’s ability to tolerate curative treatment seemed more common in cases with less case-specific history. This supports the importance of having enough information on the patient before presentation at an MDT.

We have studied the correlation between cTNM between MDTs but not pathological TNM (pTNM) among patients who underwent resectional surgery. Nowadays, most patients with oesophageal cancer that are resected also receive neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy or perioperative chemotherapy which likely affects the pTNM. Therefore, the pTNM will reflect the tumour’s response to chemo- and/or radiotherapy rather than validate whether the cTNM decided at the MDT was correct28. Accordingly, it is crucial to emphasize that there is no exact answer to which is the correct cTNM-stage and which treatment recommendation is most beneficial to the patient. Recommending surgical resection to a patient with no possibility for cure could be as harmful as denying curative treatment to a patient with curative potential as oesophagectomy has a substantial impact on short-term quality of life29. We also wish to underline that treatment recommendations are not the same as final treatment as the actually delivered treatment should be a decision made together with the patient and potential relatives after the MDT. Even though the physician’s perception influences the patient’s decision, the treatment recommendation made at the MDT may not be the treatment that the patient receives at the end.

The strengths of the present study include the number of cases assessed is higher compared with previous efforts of evaluating differences at MDT12. All but one of the MDTs with expertise in oesophageal and GOJ cancer in Sweden participated in the study, increasing the broad generalizability of the results. The blinding of the assessments made at other MDTs gives a true picture of the best appraisal possible at each new MDT. The cases were presented at MDTs including at least one surgeon, one oncologist and one radiologist to mirror clinical practise as well as possible.

There are also limitations to the study. The cases were presented with the information available at the original MDT described in the patient’s electronic chart. Possibly, more information on the case was shared between the clinicians during the original MDT without being noted down such as pictures from the endoscopic examinations, information on clinical impression or performance status evaluations from the referring doctor, other healthcare professionals who had seen the patient and might be aware of patient preferences etc. This is a limitation when analysing the differences between the original assessment and the new assessments but should not influence differences between the new assessments as all MDTs were provided with the same information on the cases.

Different routines for certain information being compulsory when assessing a case at their MDT might hamper the new assessments in this study. To limit this impact, we chose to include ten cases from each centre. By including cases consecutively to create anonymized cases we aimed to imitate a real-life MDT, where some cases are considered easy while others are more challenging, giving clinical relevance to our results.

The participating MDTs were not blinded to which cases were study cases and theoretically, they might have been more liberal with suggesting surgical resection, not having to deal with the consequences of real-life postoperative complications. This non-blinding limitation was not technically or ethically possible to avoid, as it would demand creating fictional personal identification numbers, names etc.

The results show differences in cTNM and recommended treatment between the five MDTs as well as the original assessment. A patient recommended curative treatment at one MDT, could be considered palliative at another. The study highlights the complexity of assessing oesophageal and GOJ cancer cases even at expertise oesophageal cancer MDTs. A continuous strive to increase consensus on assessing cTNM and recommended treatment is essential to achieve better and more equal evaluation for oesophageal cancer patients in Sweden.
