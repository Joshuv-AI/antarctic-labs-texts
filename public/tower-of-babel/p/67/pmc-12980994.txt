S100A11 regulates microglial inflammatory response in neuropathic pain via H3K27ac-TFEB-mitochondrial autophagy axis
Authors: Heqing Zheng, Sheng Tian, Lanxiang Wu, Guangyu Jia, Yao Xiao, Xinping Yu, Qi Zuo, Runyu Zhou, Jun Min, Qingping Wu, Hongwu Xie, Qinghua Luo, Wei Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuropathic pain (NP) is tightly linked to spinal microglial overactivation, sustained neuroinflammation; however, the key molecular regulators and epigenetic circuitry driving microglial functional dyshomeostasis remain incompletely defined. S100 calcium-binding protein A11 (S100A11) emerged as one of the most upregulated gene in activated spinal microglia via RNA sequencing. Here, we investigate the functional contribution of S100A11 to microglial dysfunction during NP and delineate the underlying molecular-epigenetic circuitry linking S100A11 to autophagic modulation. Using a chronic constriction injury (CCI)-induced NP mouse model, in vivo transcriptomics combined with in vitro validation demonstrated that S100A11 is markedly upregulated in activated microglia across both in vivo and in vitro NP-relevant contexts. Functionally, microglia-targeted S100A11 knockdown alleviates mechanical or thermal hyperalgesia in CCI mice, mitigates spinal synaptic damage, and concurrently suppresses microglial pyroptosis and proinflammatory cytokine release, while enhance autophagic flux in vitro. Mechanistically, S100A11 silencing promotes nuclear translocation of transcription factor EB (TFEB). To resolve the specific epigenetic modifications driving this effect, we screened key histone acetylation sites and identified histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) as the critical target: S100A11 depletion specifically elevates H3K27ac enrichment at the promoter regions of autophagy-associated genes. This H3K27ac-mediated chromatin relaxation facilitates TFEB binding to these promoters. This interaction directly drives the transcriptional activation of autophagy machinery, restoring microglial homeostasis. Collectively, our study uncovers a novel S100A11-TFEB-H3K27ac-autophagy regulatory axis that dictates microglial functional fate in NP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02328-9.Keywords: Neuropathic pain, S100A11, Microglia, H3K27ac, Autophagy

Introduction

Neuropathic pain (NP) is a chronic, heterogeneous pain syndrome arising from injury or dysfunction in the somatosensory nervous system, including conditions such as postherpetic neuralgia, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, and spinal nerve ligation-induced pain [1, 2]. Clinically, NP manifests as spontaneous pain, hyperalgesia, and allodynia, which severely impacts patients’ quality of life. The pathogenesis of NP involves complex molecular and cellular events, including peripheral and central sensitization, neuroinflammation, and structural remodeling of the pain-signaling pathway [3]. Despite advances in therapeutic strategies, the efficacy of current treatments remains limited, with over 50% of patients failing to achieve sufficient pain relief [4]. This underscores the urgent need to identify novel molecular targets and unravel the underlying mechanisms governing NP progression.

Microglia, the resident innate immune cells of the central nervous system, play a pivotal role in the initiation and maintenance of NP [5]. Following nerve injury, microglia are rapidly activated, undergoing morphological changes, proliferation, and migration to the site of damage [6, 7]. Activated microglia secrete a plethora of pro-inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which act on neurons and astrocytes to promote peripheral and central sensitization [8]. Notably, recent studies have identified microglial pyroptosis, a pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death mediated by gasdermin D (GSDMD), as a pivotal mechanism in the development of NP [9, 10]. This process is initiated by activation of the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, which cleaves pro-caspase-1 into its active form. Active caspase-1 then promotes the maturation of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 and cleaves GSDMD to induce pore formation in the plasma membrane [11]. These pores facilitate the release of inflammatory mediators, thereby exacerbating neuroinflammation [11, 12]. Inhibition of microglial inflammation or pyroptosis has been shown to alleviate NP-like behaviors in animal models, confirming the critical link between microglial dysfunction and NP pathogenesis [13].

Mitophagy, a selective form of autophagy that degrades damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria, is essential for maintaining mitochondrial quality and cellular homeostasis in the central nervous system [14]. Mitochondria are not only the primary source of cellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) but also key regulators of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and calcium buffering, processes tightly linked to neuronal excitability and microglial activation [15]. In NP, nerve injury disrupts mitophagy in microglia, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, excessive ROS production, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, and promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, all of which contribute to neuroinflammation and pain sensitization [16, 17]. Thus, impaired mitophagy plays a crucial role in the pathological process of NP.

S100A11, a member of the S100 family of calcium-binding proteins, plays an important role in regulating cell growth, apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and cytoskeleton remodeling [18–20]. It participates in the pathological processes of various inflammatory diseases through mechanisms such as binding to the receptor RAGE, activating key signaling pathways, and mediating cytoskeleton remodeling [21]. In non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, S100A11 induces hepatocytes to secrete IL-6 and TNF-α via the NF-κB pathway, forming a chronic inflammatory microenvironment that exacerbates insulin resistance and fibrosis [22]. In rheumatoid arthritis, S100A11 is significantly overexpressed in patients’ synovial tissues, synovial fluid, and infiltrating neutrophils, and its levels are positively correlated with disease activity [23]. However, the precise role of S100A11 in regulating neuropathic pain, especially via mitophagy and microglial pyroptosis, remains unclear.

Based on the aforementioned evidence, we hypothesize that S100A11 contributes to NP pathogenesis by regulating microglial inflammatory responses and pyroptosis, with mitophagy serving as a key downstream mediator. Specifically, we propose that nerve injury-induced upregulation of S100A11 impairs mitophagy in microglia, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, increased ROS production, and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, ultimately promoting microglial pyroptosis and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which drive pain sensitization. This study will provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of NP and identify S100A11 as a potential therapeutic target for this debilitating condition.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice (25–30 g) were obtained from SiPeiFu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed under controlled environmental conditions (22–26 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and water. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Nanchang University (Approval No. NCULAE-20221031056).

Chronic constriction injury (CCI) model

A chronic neuropathic pain model was established using the CCI of the sciatic nerve. After anesthesia, mice underwent muscle dissection to fully expose the sciatic nerve; following nerve mobilization, three 4 − 0 medical sutures were applied to ligate the nerve at 1 mm intervals, and the muscle and skin were then sutured. For the sham-operated group, only the sciatic nerve was exposed without ligation.

Behavioral tests

Mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT)

Mice were placed in a custom-made plexiglass compartment with a grid floor and allowed to acclimate to the environment for 30 min. A Von Frey filament was applied vertically to the plantar skin of the mice until the filament bent into a “C” or “S” shape; a positive response was defined as paw withdrawal or paw licking. MWT of mice in each group were measured before modeling and on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 post-modeling. The “up-and-down” method was used for testing, and the 50% MWT was calculated.

Thermal withdrawal threshold (TWT): TWT of mice in each group were measured before modeling and on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 post-modeling. After acclimatizing to a quiet environment for 30 min, the mice were placed on a temperature-controlled metal hot plate (temperature: 52 ± 0.5 °C). The time when the mice first exhibited behaviors such as hind paw licking, hind paw lifting, or jumping was recorded as the thermal pain threshold response latency.

Behavioral tests were conducted and analyzed by experimenters blinded to the treatment groups.

Intrathecal injection

Anesthetized mice were placed on an animal operating platform in a prone position, with a 15 mL centrifuge tube placed under the body for support. The injection site was the bilateral L5-L6 intervertebral space along the iliac midline. After aspirating 10 ul of adeno-associated virus (AAVs) (HBAAV2/9-Cx3cr1-siS100A11, Hanheng Biotechnology (Nanjing, China), with a titer of 1.0 × 1012 viral genomes per milliliter (vg/mL)) using a Hamilton needle, inserting it between the grooves of the L5 and L6 vertebrae in mice, and observing a pronounced sudden tail flick indicates successful intrathecal insertion. The virus was injected slowly, and after injection completion, the needle was kept in place for approximately 1 min before withdrawal.

Tissue and cells immunofluorescent staining

20 μm spinal cord sections were prepared from paraformaldehyde-fixed mouse brains using a freezing-stage sliding microtome, while mouse primary microglial cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Both samples were washed with PBS, blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h, and incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies (NeuN: CST, 24307,1:500; GFAP: CST, 12389, 1:500; IBA-1: Wako, 019-19741, 1:500; S100A11: Proteintech 60024-1-Ig, 1:200; CD68: abcam, ab31630, 1:500; iNOS: SantaCruz, sc7271, 1:200; IL-10: SantaCruz, sc8438, 1:200; CD206: SantaCruz, sc70585, 1:200; IL-1β: SantaCruz, sc-32294, 1:200; NLRP3: AdipoGen, AG-20B-0014, 1:250; iNOS: SantaCruz, sc7271, 1:200; CD68: Proteintech, 28058-1-AP, 1:200; CD206: SantaCruz, sc70585, 1:200; NLRP3: AdipoGen, AG-20B-0014-C100, 1:500; GSDMD-N: Proteintech, 20770-1-AP, 1:500; caspase1: AdipoGen, AG-20B-0042, 1:500; MAP2: CST, 4542, 1:200) in the antibody dilution buffer. After PBS washing, samples were incubated with secondary antibodies (rabbit 488, mouse 555: 1:500; Invitrogen) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by Hoechst (Sigma, B2261) staining for 10 min and PBS washes. The microglial cytoskeleton was labeled using fluorescent phalloidin probes (UElandy, UELandy, YP0114). Brain sections and coverslip-cultured microglia were mounted with antifade medium (Beyotime, P0126) and imaged using a ZEISS LSM 980 confocal microscope (ZEN 3.0 software).

Primary microglia culture

Cerebral cortices from postnatal day 1–3 C57BL/6J mice (meninges removed) were digested with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco), and digestion was quenched with DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). The tissue was gently triturated, filtered through a 40 μm strainer, and centrifuged. The pellet was resuspended in 10% FBS-DMEM/F12 and seeded into poly-L-lysine-coated T-75 flasks. Microglia were collected by gentle shaking after 10–14 days. For LPS stimulation, cells were treated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in serum-free DMEM/F12 for 12–24 h before downstream assays.

Primary microglial si-RNA transfection

Serum-free medium was thoroughly mixed with siRNA and Lipofectamine™ 3000, and the resulting mixture was added to cell culture wells. After 6–8 h, the cells were checked for their status, and the medium was promptly replaced with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS to continue culture. The transfected cells were ready for subsequent experiments 24–48 h post-transfection (Table 1).

Cellular mitochondrial protein isolation

Primary microglial cells were subjected to the separation of cytosolic and mitochondrial proteins in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Scientific™, 89874). The extracted mitochondrial proteins were mixed with 5×SDS-PAGE loading buffer, followed by heating at 95 °C for 5 min. The protein expression profiles were analyzed via Western blot assay, with cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COX IV) serving as the mitochondrial loading control.

Cytosolic and nuclear fraction isolation and cellular protein extraction

The experiment was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions of the nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit (Thermo Scientific™, 78833). Briefly, adherent cells were collected after rinsing with pre-cooled PBS, mixed gently with Reagent A, and incubated on ice to achieve initial lysis of the cytoplasmic membrane. The supernatant (cytosolic fraction) was collected by centrifugation at 4 ℃, and the pellet was retained. Nuclear extraction reagent was added to the pellet, which was then resuspended by vortexing and incubated on ice to lyse the nuclear membrane. Subsequently, the supernatant (nuclear fraction) was collected by centrifugation again at 4 °C. The obtained cytosolic proteins and nuclear proteins were separately mixed with 5×SDS-PAGE loading buffer, denatured by heating at 95 °C for 5 min, and the protein expression levels were analyzed by Western blot assay.

Western blot

Western blot gels were prepared using the PAGE Gel Rapid Preparation Kit (Shanghai Yamei, PG113). Protein samples (30–40 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE at 90 V for 90 min and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) via wet transfer (200 mA, 90 min). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 60 min, rinsed with TBST, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight (β-actin: Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig, 1:10000; S100A11: Abcam, ab169530, 1:1000; NLRP3: AdipoGen, AG-20B-0014, 1:1000; GSDMD-N: Abcam, ab215203, 1:1000; ASC: Wanleibio, WL02462, 1:500; Caspase1: CST, 89332, 1:1000; Bax: Proteintech, 50599-2-Ig, 1:2000; Bcl-2: abclone, A19693, 1:2000; COXIV: Proteintech, 11242-1-AP, 1:1000; LC3: Proteintech, 14600-1-AP, 1:2000; P62: Proteintech, 18420-1-AP, 1:2000; Parkin: Proteintech, 14060-1-AP, 1:1000; Pink1: Proteintech, 23274-1-AP, 1:2000; H3: Proteintech, 17168-1-AP, 1:2000; TFEB: Proteintech, 13372-1-AP, 1:1000; TFEC: Proteintech, 13547-1-AP, 1:1000; TFE3: Proteintech, 14480-1-AP, 1:1000; H3K9ac: CST, 9649, 1:1000; H3K18ac: CST, 13998, 1:1000; H3K27ac: CST, 8173, 1:1000; H4K12ac; CST, 13944, 1:1000; H4K16ac: CST, 13534, 1:1000). The next day, membranes were washed with TBST, incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h, and then developed using ECL chemiluminescent substrate. Signals were captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in mice were quantified using ELISA kits (Shanghai Tongwei, TW158412 and TW10277) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, diluted serum samples and standards were added to pre-equilibrated plates, followed by 100 µL of HRP-conjugated detection antibody per well. After incubation at 37 °C for 60 min, plates were washed five times, and 50 µL each of substrate A and B were added. Following a 15-min incubation in the dark at 37 °C, the reaction was stopped with 50 µL stop solution, and absorbance was read at 450 nm within 15 min. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from the standard curve for subsequent statistical analysis.

Primary neuronal culture

Pregnant C57BL/6J mice at embryonic day 13–16 were euthanized, and fetal cerebral cortices (meninges and blood vessels removed) were dissected. Tissues were digested with 0.25% trypsin (Gibco, USA), and digestion was quenched with DMEM containing 10% FBS and penicillin/streptomycin. After gentle trituration, the suspension was sequentially filtered through 200 μm and 40 μm strainers, centrifuged, and resuspended in neuron culture medium (Neurobasal + 2% B27 + 1% antibiotics). Cells were plated onto poly-L-lysine-coated 6-well plates and used for experiments after 5–7 days in vitro.

Mitochondrial assessment in primary microglia

Primary microglia were seeded into 24-well plates containing coverslips 24 h in advance. After completion of experimental treatments and rinsing with PBS, JC-1 (Invitrogen, T316, 1:3000), MitoSox (Invitrogen, T316, 1:1000), or MitoTracker (Beyotime, 1:1000) staining working solutions were added to the culture wells respectively, followed by incubation in a cell incubator for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. Following rinsing, nuclei were stained with Hoechst. After mounting, fluorescent imaging analysis was performed immediately using a confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. All data are presented as Mean ± SEM. Statistical comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA. All pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

S100A11 are increased in spinal cord of CCI model

NP is a category of chronic pain disorders characterized by complex clinical manifestations and suboptimal treatment outcomes. The mechanisms of pain generation and maintenance at the central nervous system level have always been a core scientific question in the field of pain medicine. To further clarify the central pain-inducing mechanisms of NP, we established a mouse NP model using the CCI method of the sciatic nerve. At 14 days after surgery, spinal cord L4-L6 segment tissues were collected for transcriptome sequencing analysis. A total of 213 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, with 125 significantly upregulated and 88 downregulated in the CCI group compared to the sham group (Fig. 1A). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analyses were performed on these DEGs, revealing predominant enrichment in biological stress, immune response, and multiple biological processes and signaling pathways closely associated with inflammation-including the TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and chemokine signaling pathway (Fig. 1B, C). This suggests that inflammatory response may play a core role in the central pain-generating mechanism of NP.

Given the significant enrichment of inflammation-related pathways and their key role in the pathogenesis of NP, we selected 15 differentially expressed genes closely related to inflammatory response for RT-qPCR validation. We found that compared with the sham group, the expression levels of Nr4a1 and S100a11 in spinal cord tissues of the CCI group were significantly upregulated, with S100a11 showing the most significant upregulation (Fig. 1D). As an important member of the S100 protein family, S100A11 is a key inducer of inflammatory processes and is closely associated with various inflammatory diseases, such as infective endocarditis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, and rheumatoid arthritis [20, 23, 24]. We further verified through WB analysis that the protein expression level of S100A11 in mouse spinal cord tissues was significantly increased in the CCI group compared with the sham group (Fig. 1E, F). The spinal cord tissue is primarily composed of various cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, and microglia. Abnormal activation of different cell types plays distinct roles in the occurrence and development of NP. Therefore, for the above three cell types, we further investigated the expression localization and distribution characteristics of S100A11 using immunohistochemical co-labeling technology to clarify its cellular basis for exerting functions. We found that compared with the sham group, the number of astrocytes and microglia in the spinal dorsal horn (SDH) of the CCI group was increased, indicating the activation of glial cells in the SDH of CCI group. However, there was no significant change in the expression of S100A11 in neurons and astrocytes of the SDH of the CCI group (Fig. 1G-K), but it was highly expressed in microglia (Fig. 1I, L). In conclusion, S100A11 is upregulated in the SDH of the CCI group and is associated with microglial activation, which may play an important role in spinal neuroinflammation and the occurrence and development of NP.

S100A11 knockdown ameliorates neuropathic pain and spinal neuroinflammation in CCI model

To further clarify the effect of S100A11 in spinal microglia on NP, we first downregulated the expression of S100A11 in spinal microglia via intrathecal injection of AAVs carrying the microglia-specific promoter CX3CR1 (Fig. 2A, B). Additionally, we confirmed the AAVs infection efficiency through Western blotting and fluorescence imaging of spinal tissues (Fig. S1A-D). Subsequently, we established a CCI-induced NP model on this basis. We found that S100A11 knockdown significantly increased the MWT and TWL in mice (Fig. 2C, D), suggesting that S100A11 in spinal microglia is involved in the regulation of NP, and downregulating its expression can alleviate CCI-induced mechanical and thermal hyperalgesia. In addition, we evaluated the morphological changes of microglia in the SDH using Imaris 9.0 software. Morphological analysis revealed that microglia in the CCI group displayed characteristic activated changes, manifested as enlarged cell soma, shortened processes, and increased branches, indicating the transition of microglia from a quiescent to an activated state (Fig. 2E). However, after S100A11 knockdown, the aforementioned pathological changes were significantly reversed, and the activation of microglia in the SDH was notably attenuated (Fig. 2F, G).

In the central nervous system, M1-polarized microglia induce synaptic dysfunction, neuronal death, and inhibition of neurogenesis by promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In contrast, M2-polarized microglia contribute to anti-inflammation, tissue repair, and neurogenesis by facilitating the release of neurotrophic factors [25, 26]. Therefore, we used RT-qPCR to detect the expression levels of M1- and M2-phenotype-related inflammatory genes in microglia from mouse spinal cords. The results showed that after S100A11 knockdown, the expression levels of pro-inflammatory M1 markers (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and iNOS) were decreased (Fig. 2H-K), while the expression levels of anti-inflammatory M2 markers (IL-10 and CD206) were increased (Fig. 2L-M). Subsequently, immunofluorescence was used to co-label microglia with the pro-inflammatory M1 markers (CD68, iNOS) and anti-inflammatory M2 markers (CD206, IL-10). Compared with the control group (AAV-NC sham), the CCI group (AAV-NC CCI) showed a significant increase in the expression of the pro-inflammatory M1 markers (CD68, iNOS) in microglia within the SDH (Fig. 2N-Q), and a significant decrease in the expression of the anti-inflammatory M2 markers (CD206, IL-10) (Fig. 2R-U). However, after S100A11 knockdown specifically in microglia, the expression of the pro-inflammatory M1 markers (CD68, iNOS) was significantly reduced (Fig. 2N-Q), and the expression of the anti-inflammatory M2 markers (CD206, IL-10) was significantly elevated (Fig. 2R-U). Collectively, these results indicate that S100A11 exacerbates neuroinflammatory responses in the spinal cord of CCI-induced mice by regulating the polarization of microglia toward the M1 phenotype. In contrast, knockdown of S100A11 drives microglial polarization toward the M2 phenotype, which exerts anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, thereby alleviating neuroinflammatory damage.

S100A11 knockdown decreases NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent spinal microglial pyroptosis

In the complex regulatory network of NP-associated neuroinflammation, neuroinflammation is not the result of a single mechanism involving the inflammatory activation of microglia. Beyond microglial polarization phenotype switching, pyroptosis-a form of programmed inflammatory necrosis mediated by inflammasomes, plays an indispensable role in microglia-driven neuroinflammatory amplification and chronic pain maintenance [12]. Proinflammatory cytokines and damage signals released by activated microglia further activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering pyroptosis in a subset of activated microglia. During pyroptosis, the massive release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, along with damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), further activates surrounding immune cells to form an inflammatory cascade [27, 28]. This, together with the proinflammatory cytokine storm induced by M1 polarization, constitutes a key pathological basis for NP.

Given that S100A11 has been confirmed to regulate microglial polarization phenotypes, we further hypothesize that S100A11 may target the NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated microglial pyroptosis process. To verify this hypothesis, we detected the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome in the SDH of the CCI group. The results showed that compared with the sham group, the expression levels of NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis-related proteins (Caspase-1, ASC, GSDMD-N, and NLRP3) in the SDH of the CCI group were significantly increased (Fig. 3A-E). However, knockdown of S100A11 significantly reduced the expression levels of these proteins, suggesting that S100A11 silencing inhibits the activation of the NLRP3 pathway. Since GSDMD-N-mediated cell membrane pore formation is a critical step for the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18 during pyroptosis, we further detected the serum levels of these two cytokines using ELISA. The results revealed that microglial S100A11 knockdown significantly reduced the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig. 3F, G). In addition, we performed cell-level validation using immunofluorescence colocalization. The results showed that the expression levels of NLRP3 and IL-1β in microglia of the SDH were significantly increased in the CCI group; in contrast, microglia-specific S100A11 knockdown significantly decreased the colocalization signals of these two molecules in microglia (Fig. 3H-K), further confirming the cell specificity of S100A11 in regulating the NLRP3/GSDMD axis. Collectively, these results indicate that knockdown of S100A11 in spinal microglia of the CCI group can inhibit the NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis pathway and reduce proinflammatory cytokine release.

S100A11 knockdown alleviates inflammatory responses in primary microglia

To eliminate interference from the complex in vivo microenvironment and further clarify the regulatory role of S100A11 in microglial inflammation, we isolated primary microglia from mice in vitro and established an in vitro cellular inflammation model using LPS stimulation for ex vivo validation experiments. RT-qPCR and WB results showed that compared with the control (CON) group, the mRNA and protein levels of S100A11 in microglia of the LPS group were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4A-C). These findings suggest that S100A11 plays a crucial role in regulating the inflammatory response of microglia. Subsequently, we further verified the effect of S100A11 knockdown on the expression of inflammatory genes in the LPS-induced cellular inflammation model via RT-qPCR in vitro. The results revealed that compared with the negative control group (si-NC LPS), the S100A11 knockdown group (si-S100A11 LPS) exhibited significantly downregulated expression of proinflammatory M1-type genes, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and iNOS; meanwhile, the expression levels of anti-inflammatory M2-type genes (IL-10 and CD206) were significantly upregulated (Fig. 4D-I). Subsequent cellular immunofluorescence results demonstrated that compared with si-NC LPS, microglia in the S100A11 knockdown group (si-S100A11 LPS) showed a significantly reduced cell soma size, increased cellular branches, downregulated levels of M1-type inflammatory proteins (iNOS and CD68) (Fig. 4J-M), and significantly upregulated expression of M2-type inflammatory proteins (CD206 and Arg1) (Fig. 4N-Q). These results further validated at the subcellular level that S100A11 knockdown can inhibit LPS-induced cellular inflammatory responses. In summary, S100A11 knockdown effectively inhibits the activation of M1-type microglia while promoting their conversion to the M2 type.

S100A11 knockdown inhibits NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in primary microglia

Subsequently, to further confirm the direct regulatory role of S100A11 in microglial pyroptosis, we performed validation in an LPS-stimulated primary microglial inflammation model. WB analysis showed that after S100A11 knockdown, the expression levels of NLRP3, ASC, Caspase-1, and GSDMD-N in primary microglia were significantly decreased (Fig. 5A-E). This trend was consistent with that observed in the in vivo CCI group. Given the diversity of cell death modalities under inflammatory stimulation, we simultaneously detected changes in the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. Following LPS stimulation, the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax was significantly upregulated, while the level of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was decreased, indicating activation of the apoptotic pathway (Fig. 5F-H). Furthermore, we further confirmed the aforementioned regulatory effect at the subcellular level using cellular immunofluorescence. After S100A11 knockdown, the fluorescent signals of pyroptosis-related proteins (including NLRP3, GSDMD, Caspase-1, and ASC) in primary microglia of the LPS group were significantly attenuated (Fig. 5I-P). Concurrently, the proportion of Yo-Pro/PI double-positive cells was significantly reduced (Fig. 5Q, R). Collectively, these results confirm the inhibitory effect of S100A11 knockdown on microglial pyroptosis from both molecular localization and cellular function perspectives, providing ex vivo experimental support for the inflammation regulatory mechanism observed in vivo.

To clarify the mechanism by which S100A11 regulates cellular inflammation, we performed transcriptome sequencing on primary microglia following S100A11 knockdown. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for dimensionality reduction of the data, which revealed a clear clustering trend in the distribution of samples across the 4 groups. Notably, the si-NC LPS and si-S100A11 LPS groups were the farthest apart, indicating significant differences between these two sample groups (Fig. 6A). Subsequent analyses focused on the results from these two groups. A volcano plot was used to visualize the distribution of DEGs. A total of 358 DEGs were identified, among which 219 genes were significantly upregulated and 139 genes were downregulated (Fig. 6B). GO and KEGG functional enrichment analyses were conducted on these DEGs. The results showed that DEGs were significantly enriched in signaling pathways such as apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, autophagy, lysosome function, metabolism, and mitochondrial function (Fig. 6C). The results showed that metabolism-related proteins accounted for a relatively high proportion, particularly those involved in energy production and conversion. Based on previous transcriptome sequencing results, we further investigated the effect of S100A11 knockdown on mitochondrial function to clarify its mechanism of regulating cellular inflammation. A stable mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) is fundamental to the normal operation of mitochondrial energy metabolism and a key factor in maintaining cellular physiological functions. Therefore, we first used JC-1 probes to stain primary microglia for MMP detection. The results showed that S100A11 knockdown reversed LPS-induced mitochondrial damage: specifically, green fluorescence in primary microglia was attenuated, red fluorescence was enhanced, and MMP was increased (Fig. 6E, G). Flow cytometry analysis of mitochondrial JC-1 levels revealed that after S100A11 knockdown, the intensity of JC-1 green fluorescence was significantly reduced, further verifying that S100A11 interference increases MMP levels (Fig. 6F, H). Excess ROS not only exacerbates inflammatory responses but also causes cellular damage. To clarify the effect of S100A11 knockdown on mitochondrial ROS levels, we used MitoSOX probes to stain primary microglia for mitochondrial ROS detection. The results showed that S100A11 interference reduced red fluorescence, indicating a decrease in mitochondrial ROS levels (Fig. 6I, K). Flow cytometry results were consistent with immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6J, L). The dynamic balance of mitochondrial quantity is a prerequisite for efficient cellular metabolism. Finally, to determine the effect of S100A11 knockdown on mitochondrial number, we used the mitochondrial tracking probe MitoTracker for detection. Compared with the si-NC LPS group, S100A11 interference increased intracellular green fluorescence, suggesting a significant increase in mitochondrial number (Fig. 6M, N). Collectively, these results indicate that S100A11 knockdown can increase MMP, reduce mitochondrial ROS levels, and enhance mitochondrial number in LPS-stimulated microglia, thereby maintaining the stability of mitochondrial function.

Knockdown of S100A11 improves inflammatory response and pyroptosis in primary microglia by inhibiting mitophagy

As a degradation mechanism that precisely clears dysfunctional mitochondria in the cytoplasm, mitophagy maintains the homeostatic function of the mitochondrial network, preventing the release of proinflammatory signals or abnormal accumulation of ROS from damaged mitochondria [14, 29]. Dysfunction of the mitophagy pathway may lead to abnormal intracellular accumulation of damaged mitochondria, which in turn triggers mitochondrial respiratory chain dysfunction, reduced ATP synthesis efficiency, and cascading activation of proapoptotic signals, ultimately exacerbating cellular stress injury and inflammatory responses [30, 31]. Based on previous sequencing results, to clarify the effect of S100A11 knockdown on mitophagy levels in LPS-stimulated microglia, we extracted mitochondrial proteins from primary microglia and detected mitophagy-related proteins. The results showed that S100A11 knockdown significantly increased the protein expression of LC3, Parkin, and Pink1, while downregulating P62 expression (Fig. 7A-E). This indicates that S100A11 knockdown enhances mitophagy levels in LPS-stimulated microglia, thereby maintaining mitochondrial quality stability. Subsequently, to assess mitophagic flux in primary microglia, we performed co-immunolabeling of Tom20 and LC3B. In LPS-stimulated primary microglia, LC3B exhibited a diffuse cytoplasmic distribution (green), consistent with impaired mitophagy. In contrast, upon S100A11 knockdown, LC3B reorganized into distinct punctate structures that colocalized with fragmented mitochondria (Fig. 7F, G). These results demonstrate that S100A11 knockdown increases mitophagy levels and promotes the restoration of mitophagic flux.

S100A11 knockdown alleviates microglial inflammation by inhibiting mitophagy through the TFEB/H3K27ac axis

The homeostasis of mitophagy relies on precise transcriptional regulation. As multifunctional transcription factors, the TFE (transcription factor E) family maintains cellular homeostasis by regulating lysosomal biogenesis, autophagy-related gene expression, and metabolic processes [32, 33]. Under basal conditions, members of this family-including TFEB, TFE3, MITF, and TFEC-are primarily localized in the cytoplasm [34]. Upon stimulation, they translocate to the nucleus to activate the expression of downstream target genes. Combined with the “increased expression of nuclear structure-related proteins” observed in previous high-throughput transcriptome sequencing, we hypothesized that S100A11 knockdown may regulate mitophagy by influencing nuclear transcriptional regulatory processes, with the TFE family potentially serving as key mediating molecules. To investigate whether S100A11 modulates autophagy by regulating the nuclear translocation of TFE proteins, we detected proteins in the cytoplasm and nucleus of primary microglia following S100A11 knockdown. The results showed that after S100A11 knockdown, TFEC and TFE3 remained localized in the cytoplasm, while TFEB expression was significantly downregulated in the cytoplasm and markedly upregulated in the nucleus. This indicates that S100A11 interference promotes the translocation of TFEB from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, thereby enabling its role in regulating gene transcription (Fig. 8A, B, D-F). To further validate these results, we used immunofluorescence to detect the nucleocytoplasmic localization of TFEB. We found that S100A11 knockdown significantly increased the nuclear localization of TFEB, confirming that S100A11 knockdown promotes TFEB nuclear translocation (Fig. 8C).

To clarify whether S100A11 knockdown modulates mitophagy via TFEB, we validated whether TFEB knockdown could block the mitophagy-regulating effect of S100A11. We detected mitochondrial proteins in primary microglia. Compared with the S100A11 knockdown group, TFEB knockdown led to decreased expression levels of mitophagy-related proteins (LC3, Parkin, and Pink1) and increased expression of P62 (Fig. 8G-K). This indicated that the S100A11 knockdown-induced enhancement of mitophagy was abrogated. We used Tom20 and LC3B to detect changes in intracellular mitophagic flux. The results showed that S100A11 knockdown significantly increased LC3B expression and promoted the restoration of mitophagic flux. The transcriptional activation of TFEB depends not only on nuclear translocation but also on the opening of chromatin environment and assembly of transcription initiation complexes, a process tightly regulated by epigenetic modifications such as histone acetylation. Histone acetylation, via the addition of acetyl groups to lysine residues on core histones, relaxes chromatin structure and enhances transcription factor transcription factor binding capacity [35]. Combined with the prior high-throughput transcriptome sequencing hint that “changes in chromatin structure and dynamics may affect gene accessibility”, we further examined whether the transcriptional regulatory function of TFEB is influenced by S100A11 knockdown via alterations in histone acetylation. We extracted nuclear proteins from primary microglia after S100A11 knockdown and detected the proteins H3K9ac, H3K18ac, H3K27ac, H4K12ac, and H4K16ac. The results showed that compared with the si-NC LPS group, S100A11 knockdown significantly increased H3K27ac expression, while no significant changes were observed in H3K9ac, H3K18ac, H4K12ac, or H4K16ac expression (Fig. 8N-S). Subsequently, we used the histone acetylation inhibitor A-485 to assess whether A-485 could block the regulation of mitophagy by S100A11. Compared with the S100A11 knockdown group, A-485 administration decreased the expression of mitophagy-related proteins (LC3, Parkin, and Pink1) and increased P62 expression, abrogating the S100A11 knockdown-induced enhancement of mitophagy (Fig. 8T-X). Subsequent immunofluorescence further validated this result (Fig. 8Y, Z). In addition, Our ChIP-qPCR data demonstrated that S100A11 knockout led to a significant increase in H3K27ac enrichment at the promoter regions of LC3, PINK1 and Parkin compared with the control group (Fig. S4 A-C). These findings indicate that S100A11 knockdown promotes mitophagy by increasing histone H3K27 acetylation levels. Integrating the aforementioned results, we hypothesize that S100A11 knockdown increases histone H3K27 acetylation, promotes TFEB nuclear translocation, and thereby enhances the transcription of mitophagy-related genes to alleviate cellular inflammation.

S100A11 knockdown concomitant partial restoration of dendritic morphology in spinal cord neurons of the CCI model

During the transition from acute to chronic pain, dynamic changes in spinal dorsal horn neurons and glial cells represent one of the core pathological mechanisms [36]. Following peripheral nerve injury, microglial activation triggers the release of proinflammatory cytokines, exacerbating neuroinflammation; these inflammatory mediators act directly on neurons, inducing neuronal sensitization and increased excitability, enhancing long-term noxious neural signal transmission. This leads to peripheral and central sensitization, worsening NP [37]. To further investigate the effect of glia-mediated neuroinflammation on neurons after S100A11 knockdown, and to clarify the interaction between microglia and neurons, we collected conditioned medium from primary microglia with S100A11 interference, used it to incubate neurons (Fig. 9A), and detected the protein expression levels of Bax and Bcl-2 in neurons via Western blot. After incubating neurons with the conditioned medium, we labeled neurons using Microtubule-Associated Protein 2 (MAP2) and Calcein AM. MAP2 staining results showed a significant increase in neuronal fluorescence intensity, indicating the restoration of neuronal synaptic structure (Fig. 9E); Calcein AM staining revealed that neurons cultured in conditioned medium from S100A11-interfered microglia exhibited a more intact structure (Fig. 9F).

Nissl bodies are basophilic granules or masses within neurons, composed of parallel arrays of rough endoplasmic reticulum and free ribosomes. They serve as the primary site for the synthesis of neurotransmitters, neural secretions, and structural proteins in neurons. Changes in the morphology and quantity of Nissl bodies reflect neuronal functional status, making them a key indicator for evaluating neuronal health [38]. To further assess the effect of S100A11 on spinal neurons in the chronic constriction injury (CCI) mouse model, we performed Nissl staining on mouse spinal cords. We found an increase in the number of Nissl bodies in the spinal dorsal horn of the S100A11 knockdown group, though the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. S5). This study has not yet directly validated changes in neuronal excitability using in vivo electrophysiology or calcium imaging, approaches that will be critical in our future work. Future studies may require validation of changes in neuronal excitability using electrophysiological or calcium imaging techniques.

Synaptophysin (SYP) is a key presynaptic protein that regulates synaptic plasticity by modulating the packaging, release, and transmission efficiency of excitatory neurotransmitters such as glutamate [39]. Postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95) is a critical postsynaptic density protein primarily localized in mature excitatory glutamatergic synapses. As an important marker of synaptic plasticity, PSD-95 interacts with various glutamatergic receptors, cell adhesion molecules, and cytoskeletal components to influence synaptic development and plasticity [40]. To investigate the mechanism by which S100A11 knockdown alleviates NP in mice, we focused on its impact on synaptic structural remodeling in the spinal cord. Our results showed that S100A11 knockdown significantly reduced the expression levels of both SYP and PSD-95 in the spinal cord of CCI mice (Fig. 9G-M). Collectively, S100A11 knockdown attenuated microglial activation and subsequent neuroinflammation, thereby alleviating neuronal damage induced by inflammatory mediators. Furthermore, it ameliorated maladaptive synaptic plasticity, restored synaptic homeostasis, and reduced sustained postsynaptic excitability, ultimately decreasing nociceptive signal transmission and exerting an analgesic effect. These findings highlight the critical role of microglia–neuron crosstalk in the pathogenesis of NP.

Discussion

In this study, we found that S100A11 was abnormally upregulated in spinal microglia of the CCI groups. This upregulation impeded mitophagy by inhibiting TFEB nuclear translocation, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction. Subsequently, mitochondrial dysfunction triggered M1 polarization and pyroptosis of microglia, ultimately exacerbating neuroinflammation. In contrast, S100A11 knockdown reversed the aforementioned pathological processes by activating the TFEB/H3K27ac-mitophagy pathway, thereby providing a potential therapeutic strategy for the treatment of NP (Fig. 10).

S100A11 regulates microglial inflammatory response in neuropathic pain via H3K27ac-TFEB-mitochondrial autophagy axis

As a member of the S100 protein family, S100A11 plays a key regulatory role in various inflammatory diseases. Rotator cuff tendinopathy is a common condition accompanied by pain and dysfunction. Proteomic analysis of the shoulder joint synovium has revealed that S100A11 is significantly upregulated in rotator cuff injuries and is closely associated with oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [41]. Through RNA sequencing of spinal cord tissues from CCI mice, we found activation of biological stress, immune responses, and multiple inflammatory signaling pathways in these mice, suggesting that neuroinflammation plays an important role in NP. Furthermore, we observed that S100A11 is mainly upregulated in microglia of the SDH, indicating that S100A11 may exert a crucial effect in microglia-mediated neuroinflammation. Subsequently, by specifically knocking down S100A11 expression in microglia, we found that the MWT and TWL of mice were significantly increased, demonstrating that S100A11 plays a key regulatory role in the occurrence and development of NP. These results suggest that S100A11 may be involved in the pathogenesis of NP through microglia-mediated neuroinflammation. Microglia are resident immune cells of the central nervous system, and their activation is one of the core events of neuroinflammation, playing a critical role in NP [42]. When peripheral nerves are injured, nociceptive signals are transmitted to the spinal dorsal horn, leading to rapid activation of microglia and release of various inflammatory mediators, such as cytokines, chemokines, and ROS. These mediators act directly or indirectly on neurons, increasing neuronal excitability and enhancing pain signal transmission, thereby resulting in hyperalgesia and chronic pain [43, 44]. In the present study, we found that microglia in the SDH of CCI model mice were significantly activated. After S100A11 knockdown, the activated state of microglia was markedly attenuated, and their morphology was restored to the resting state. Meanwhile, we detected that S100A11 knockdown significantly inhibited the expression of pro-inflammatory M1-type related genes (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, iNOS) and promoted the expression of anti-inflammatory M2-type genes (IL-10, CD206). This indicates that S100A11 alleviates neuroinflammation by regulating microglial activation, thereby relieving NP. This study was conducted exclusively in male mice; future work will systematically investigate potential sex differences in the role of S100A11 in neuropathic pain.

During the occurrence and development of NP, microglia play a crucial role in the exacerbation of neuroinflammation and the maintenance of chronic pain. Pyroptosis, a type of programmed inflammatory necrosis regulated by inflammasomes, exerts an undeniable effect in this process. When microglia are activated, the pro-inflammatory cytokines they secrete and the damage-associated molecular patterns they release further promote the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome; under this influence, some activated microglia initiate the pyroptosis process [45]. In a sciatic nerve injury model, compared with wild-type (WT) mice, the infiltration of CD68-labeled macrophages was significantly reduced in NLRP3 knockout mice [46]. In addition, overexpression of miR-223 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation by reducing the number of M1-type macrophages and increasing the number of M2-type, thereby achieving a neuroprotective effect [47]. TXNIP/NLRP3 is a key factor regulating oxidative stress and inflammation in diabetes, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and other inflammatory diseases [48, 49]. In prediabetic neuropathy mice fed a high-fat diet, Xu et al. [49] found that the TXNIP/NLRP3 complex can promote the upregulation of IL-1β and inflammatory responses, while the calcium channel inhibitor verapamil improves prediabetic neuropathy in high-fat diet-fed mice by preventing the upregulation of TXNIP. In the CCI-induced NP model, salidroside alleviates CCI-induced hypersensitivity by inhibiting the activation of the TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome axis and reducing spinal neuron loss [50]. In our study, we observed that the expression levels of NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis-related proteins in the spinal cord tissues of CCI model mice were significantly increased. After S100A11 knockdown, the expression levels of these proteins were significantly decreased, indicating that S100A11 can effectively inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis. Further ELISA results showed that S100A11 knockdown significantly reduced the levels of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-18 in mouse serum, which is consistent with the conclusion that pyroptosis inhibition leads to reduced release of inflammatory factors. Moreover, we further verified our in vivo results at the cellular experiment level, and the two were consistent. These results indicate that S100A11 can regulate neuroinflammation through NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, thereby alleviating neuropathic pain.

To clarify the specific mechanism by which S100A11 regulates the inflammatory response of microglia, we performed transcriptome sequencing on primary microglia after S100A11 knockdown. Functional enrichment analysis (GO and KEGG) of DEGs revealed that these DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways such as apoptosis, inflammation-related pathways, oxidative stress, autophagy, lysosome, metabolism, and mitophagy signaling pathways. These results indicate that S100A11 regulates the inflammatory response by modulating mitochondrial function, energy metabolism, and mitophagy. Subsequently, we detected the mitochondrial function of microglia with S100A11 knockdown at the cellular level. Further experiments showed that S100A11 knockdown could increase mitochondrial membrane potential, reduce ROS levels, and increase mitochondrial quantity, thereby alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and maintaining intracellular homeostasis. The above findings suggest that S100A11 may alleviate the inflammatory response by regulating the metabolic state of mitochondria and affecting the level of intracellular oxidative stress.

Mitophagy is an important mechanism for clearing damaged mitochondria, which alleviates neuroinflammation by maintaining mitochondrial quality and cellular homeostasis. In NP, the impairment of mitophagic function leads to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria in cells, the release of harmful substances, the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways, and the exacerbation of pain symptoms. Diphenylamine sulfone, an active component of Gastrodia elata, alleviates mitochondrial damage and ROS production caused by mitochondrial membrane depolarization in microglia stimulated by LPS and nigericin by promoting mitophagy, thereby inhibiting NLRP3 activation and relieving pain [17]. Ginkgolide B exerts a neuroprotective effect. In the CCI rat model, ginkgolide B can activate Pink1-Parkin-mediated mitophagy, reduce intracellular ROS production and the expression of inflammation-related proteins such as Caspase-1, IL-1β and NLRP3 in microglia, alleviate neuroinflammation, and thus relieve NP [16]. In summary, promoting mitophagy can effectively reduce the inflammatory response of microglia and alleviate NP. Our previous transcriptomic results indicated that mitophagy plays an important role in the regulation of inflammatory response by S100A11. Therefore, we detected mitophagy-related proteins and found that after S100A11 knockdown, the expression levels of LC3, Parkin and Pink1 were significantly increased, while the expression level of P62 was significantly decreased, indicating an enhancement of mitophagic activity. The results of immunofluorescence staining showed that after S100A11 knockdown, the colocalization of LC3 and the mitochondrial tracer MitoTracker was significantly increased, suggesting that S100A11 knockdown improves mitophagic level and promotes the recovery of mitophagic flux. In conclusion, by knocking down S100A11, this study reveals its important role in microglial inflammation. S100A11 regulates cellular inflammatory response and pyroptosis by affecting mitochondrial function and autophagic level.

TFEB is a major regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and a member of the TFE family. Members of this family regulate gene expression by binding to specific sequences on DNA and are involved in various biological processes, mainly including the regulation of autophagy, lysosomal biogenesis, lipid metabolism, and energy homeostasis [51]. Nezich et al. [52] found that, under the action of ATG5 and ATG9, TFEB can undergo Parkin-mediated nuclear translocation to activate mitophagy, indicating that TFEB is a transcriptional regulator of mitophagy. A subsequent study revealed that the mitochondrial uncoupler carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) mediates the Pink1-Parkin pathway in neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells, promotes the translocation of TFEB to the nucleus, and enhances mitophagy [53]. The Q311X mutation in Parkin hinders the degradation of PARIS (Parkin-interacting substrate), leading to the massive accumulation of PARIS protein, which in turn inhibits the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1 alpha (PGC-1α)-TFEB nuclear translocation signal and suppresses mitochondrial degradation [54]. These findings indicate that TFEB is closely associated with mitophagy. Intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) is a primary contributor to low back pain and imposes significant socioeconomic costs. The latest research has found that overexpressing TFEB can promote the recovery of lysosomal function, alleviate autophagic flux obstruction in cells, thereby relieving pain [55]. Liu et al. recently reported that Dihydromyricetin ameliorates bortezomib-induced peripheral neuropathy by restoring lysosomal function in Schwann cells via the ERK/TFEB signaling axis [56]. Although their focus is on peripheral glia (Schwann cells), this study importantly underscores the emerging consensus that TFEB-driven lysosomal biogenesis and mitophagy represent a convergent therapeutic node across diverse NP etiologies.

Our high-throughput transcriptome sequencing results showed that S100A11 knockdown affects nuclear signal transduction and the activity of transcription factors through changes in nuclear structure, thereby further regulating gene expression. Based on the sequencing results and previous literature studies, to clarify the effect of S100A11 knockdown on nuclear structure changes, we detected the expression of TFE family-related proteins in the cytoplasm and nucleus. We observed that after S100A11 knockdown, the expression of TFEB in the cytoplasm was significantly downregulated, while its expression in the nucleus was significantly upregulated, indicating that S100A11 knockdown promotes the translocation of TFEB from the cytoplasm to the nucleus. This result was further verified by cellular immunofluorescence. Subsequently, by knocking down TFEB, we found that the expression levels of mitophagy-related proteins LC3, Parkin, and Pink1 were decreased, while the expression level of P62 was increased. That is, the enhancement of mitophagy induced by S100A11 knockdown was abrogated. This indicates that S100A11 knockdown increases mitophagic activity by promoting the nuclear translocation of TFEB.

Histone acetylation is also closely associated with the transcription of autophagy-related genes [57]. As an important epigenetic modification, histone acetylation regulates chromatin structure and gene expression by adding acetyl groups to the lysine residues of histones. Among them, H3K27ac plays a key role in the regulation of gene expression. When enriched at promoter or enhancer regions, H3K27ac promotes a more open chromatin conformation, facilitating the binding of transcription factors and the recruitment of RNA polymerase II, thereby initiating transcription and contributing to various physiological and pathological processes [57]. In our study, high-throughput transcriptome sequencing revealed that S100A11 knockdown alters chromatin accessibility and transcription factor binding dynamics, suggesting a potential impact on epigenetic regulation. Consistently, we observed a significant increase in nuclear H3K27 acetylation following S100A11 depletion. This elevation in H3K27ac was associated with enhanced transcription of mitophagy-related genes. By adding the histone acetylation inhibitor A-485, we found that it could block the enhancement of mitophagy induced by S100A11 knockdown. Together, these findings establish a novel link between S100A11 and epigenetic control of mitophagy. While S100A11 is not a canonical epigenetic regulator, its knockdown may indirectly influence H3K27ac levels through several plausible mechanisms. Although S100A11 is not a canonical epigenetic regulator, its knockdown may indirectly elevate H3K27ac by perturbing cellular processes that influence histone acetylation. For example, S100A11 regulates calcium signaling and mitochondrial function, and its loss could alter acetyl-CoA availability, a critical substrate for acetylation, thereby affecting H3K27ac levels [58]. Additionally, S100A11 is involved in stress responses such as oxidative or ER stress, which can modulate p300/CBP activity [59]; notably, the p300/CBP inhibitor A-485 reversed both the H3K27ac increase and enhanced mitophagy seen upon S100A11 depletion, implicating these HATs in the phenotype. Given its role as a calcium-dependent scaffold protein, S100A11 might also shape the epigenetic landscape through interactions that affect the stability, localization, or modification of chromatin regulators [60]. While the exact mechanism remains unclear, our findings provide a new perspective for understanding the role of epigenetic modifications in autophagy regulation.
