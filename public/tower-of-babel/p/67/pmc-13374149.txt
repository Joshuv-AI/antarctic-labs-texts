Effects of aerobic and resistance exercises on cardiovascular biomarkers in sedentary females: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Niloufar Ghadamyari, Fatma Neşe Şahin, Hamza Küçük
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac and inflammatory biomarkers, including N-terminal pro–brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), Galectin-3, and soluble ST2 (s-ST2), are widely used to assess myocardial stress, fibrosis, and inflammation. Although exercise is known to improve cardiometabolic health, limited evidence exists regarding the effects of different exercise modalities on these biomarkers in healthy sedentary young women. This study aimed to examine the effects of aerobic and resistance exercise on selected cardiac and inflammatory biomarkers in sedentary females.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, 45 sedentary females (mean age: 21.84 ± 2.01 years; body mass index: 25.84 ± 2.65 kg/m²) were randomly assigned to resistance exercise (n = 15), aerobic exercise (n = 15), or control (n = 15) groups. The resistance group performed supervised training three times per week (3 sets of 8–10 repetitions at 75–80% of one-repetition maximum), while the aerobic group completed treadmill exercise at 75% of heart rate reserve for 45–60 min, three times per week, for 8 weeks. The control group maintained their usual sedentary lifestyle. Biomarkers were assessed at baseline and post-intervention.ResultsFollowing the intervention, Galectin-3 and s-ST2 levels were significantly reduced in both exercise groups compared with the control group (p < .001). In contrast, BNP and NT-proBNP levels were significantly increased in the exercise groups (p < .001). NT-proBNP showed a greater increase in the resistance exercise group compared with the aerobic group (p ≤ .05).ConclusionBoth aerobic and resistance exercise induced favorable changes in cardiac and inflammatory biomarkers in sedentary young women. Increases in BNP and NT-proBNP appear to reflect physiological adaptations to exercise rather than pathological cardiac stress, while reductions in s-ST2 and Galectin-3 indicate improved inflammatory status. These findings support the role of structured exercise as an effective non-pharmacological strategy for improving cardiometabolic health in this population.Trial registrationNCT07457047 (Registered date: 03-03-2026). Introduction

Over the past decade, research on sedentary behavior (SB) has expanded substantially, and accumulating evidence has demonstrated a direct association between SB and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1]. Worldwide rates of physical inactivity have increased markedly, and the rising prevalence of sedentary lifestyles among children, adolescents, and young adults has emerged as a major global public health concern [2]. Sedentary behavior has been consistently linked to unfavorable cardiometabolic profiles and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, particularly in young adult populations [3, 4].

Circulating cardiac and inflammatory biomarkers have been widely investigated for their role in the assessment and prognosis of cardiovascular diseases. Among these, natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP), soluble suppression of tumorigenicity-2 (s-ST2), and Galectin-3 have been identified as important indicators of myocardial stress, fibrosis, and inflammation [5]. These biomarkers are widely used in clinical practice, particularly in the diagnosis and management of heart failure [6].

Sedentary behavior has been associated with alterations in cardiac biomarkers, including natriuretic peptides, which may reflect impaired cardiovascular function and increased cardiometabolic risk [7]. Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) and N-terminal pro–brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) are widely used markers of myocardial stress and cardiac function, and their circulating levels are known to increase in response to cardiac load and various pathological conditions [8–10]. These biomarkers are routinely used in clinical practice to evaluate heart failure [11–13].

In addition to natriuretic peptides, emerging biomarkers such as soluble ST2 (s-ST2) and Galectin-3 have gained attention as indicators of myocardial fibrosis [14–17], inflammation, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [18–22]. These biomarkers provide additional prognostic information beyond traditional measures and are increasingly used in cardiovascular risk assessment.

Although sedentary behavior has been linked to unfavorable biomarker profiles, regular physical activity, including aerobic and resistance exercise, has been shown to improve cardiometabolic health and modulate circulating biomarkers [23]. In addition, exercise induces hormonal, metabolic, and hemodynamic adaptations that may influence cardiovascular and inflammatory biomarker responses [24, 25].

However, most previous studies have primarily focused on traditional cardiovascular outcomes or clinical populations, while limited evidence exists regarding the comparative effects of aerobic and resistance exercise on established and emerging cardiovascular biomarkers in healthy sedentary young women. Understanding these responses may provide important insight into early cardiometabolic adaptations associated with different exercise modalities. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the effects of aerobic and resistance exercise on circulating cardiac and inflammatory biomarkers, including BNP, NT-proBNP, s-ST2, and Galectin-3, in sedentary females aged 18–25 years.

Methods

Study design and participants

This randomized controlled study was conducted in 2023 at Ankara University, Turkey, following approval by the Ethics Committee of Ankara University of Medical Sciences, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. A total of 45 sedentary women voluntarily participated in the study after receiving detailed information about the procedures and providing written informed consent. Participants were randomly allocated to one of three groups: aerobic exercise (n = 15), resistance exercise (n = 15), or control (n = 15) using a computer-generated randomization sequence to minimize selection bias.

The inclusion criteria included the absence of diseases related to sedentariness, such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases, inflammatory disorders, or known genetic conditions. None of the participants had prior experience with structured resistance training or weightlifting and were therefore considered untrained individuals.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 for a repeated-measures ANOVA (within-between interaction). Assuming an effect size of f = 0.28 (medium-to-large), an alpha level of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.90, three groups, two measurements, a correlation among repeated measures of 0.50, and a nonsphericity correction of 1, the required total sample size was calculated as 45 participants. Exercise intervention procedures

Aerobic exercise

The aerobic exercise protocol consisted of an 8-week moderate-intensity walking and jogging program performed on a treadmill, including warm-up and cool-down phases. Exercise intensity was set at 60–75% of each participant’s maximal heart rate reserve and progressively increased within the range recommended by the ACSM (2.5–5% per week), with a standardized increment of 5% applied in this study [26]. Each session lasted 60 min, comprising 10 min of warm-up, 40 min of walking or jogging, and 10 min of cool-down. Heart rate was continuously monitored using a heart rate monitor (Impalser CMS50D1, Germany) to ensure adherence to the prescribed intensity. All aerobic exercise sessions were conducted between 09:00 and 10:00 AM under the supervision of an exercise physiologist (corresponding author: N.G.).

Resistance training

Prior to the resistance training program, participants completed a familiarization session to ensure proper technique, during which the exercise equipment and movement patterns were introduced. The resistance exercise protocol included nine exercises: leg extension, leg curl, chest press, leg press, seated cable row, lat pull-down, biceps curl, triceps curl, and cable crunch. Prior to strength testing, participants completed a standardized warm-up using light loads. The 1RM values were calculated using the Brzycki equation [27]:

Each resistance training session consisted of three sets of 8–9 repetitions at 70–75% of 1RM, with approximately 60–90 s of rest between sets and exercises. Sessions began with a 10-minute warm-up using a stationary bicycle, followed by approximately 40 min of resistance exercises, and concluded with stretching exercises for cool-down. The training load was progressively adjusted throughout the intervention period by increasing external resistance while maintaining the target repetition range, in accordance with participants’ strength improvements. Training data were recorded at each session. Participants were instructed to refrain from engaging in additional structured physical activity during the study period [28, 29].

Control group

Participants in the control group did not participate in any exercise program and maintained their habitual sedentary lifestyle and dietary patterns throughout the 8-week study period.

Measurements

Prior to the intervention, all participants underwent cardiovascular fitness assessments using the Harvard Step Test and a modified Bruce treadmill exercise ECG test to evaluate cardiovascular responses to physical stress. The Harvard Step Test was performed using a standardized step height and cadence, and recovery heart rate was recorded following the protocol. The modified Bruce protocol consisted of incremental increases in treadmill speed and incline at regular intervals until the target intensity was reached. These protocols are widely used to assess cardiovascular fitness [ACSM, 2021]. During the ECG test, treadmill speed and incline were progressively increased. Heart rate and blood pressure were measured using a pulse oximeter (CMS50D1, Germany) and a calibrated sphygmomanometer (Riester Exacta, Germany), respectively. All measurements were conducted under standardized conditions. Participants were instructed to avoid caffeine, alcohol, and strenuous physical activity for at least 24 h prior to testing, and all assessments were performed at a similar time of day.

Anthropometric and physiological characteristics

Physiological characteristics, including age and height, were measured using a wall-mounted stadiometer (Beurer, Germany). Body weight, body mass index (BMI), resting heart rate, body fat percentage, waist circumference, hip circumference, and waist-to-hip ratio were assessed using standardized procedures and devices, including a digital scale (Beurer, Germany), pulse oximeter (CMS50-D1), body composition analyzer (BH Body-Control Kit, Spain), and a non-elastic measuring tape. Evaluation of dietary intake

Dietary intake was assessed using 24-hour dietary recall records at baseline and at week 8. Participants were instructed to maintain their habitual dietary patterns throughout the study period. Dietary intake was monitored throughout the intervention; however, participants were not placed on a standardized dietary intervention or controlled nutrition program. No significant changes in dietary intake were observed during the study period.

Blood sampling

After an overnight fast of at least 8 h, blood samples were collected from all participants between 07:30 and 08:30 AM at baseline and after the 8-week intervention. Five milliliters of venous blood were drawn from the brachial vein while participants were seated and in a rested state. Samples were processed according to standard laboratory procedures to determine serum concentrations of BNP, NT-proBNP, s-ST2, and Galectin-3.

Serum analyses and measurements

Serum concentrations of BNP, NT-proBNP, s-ST2, and Galectin-3 were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Sunlong Technology, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All measurements were performed using a microplate reader (Biotech LX 800, USA), and all samples were analyzed under standardized laboratory conditions.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were changes in inflammatory biomarkers (s-ST2 and Galectin-3) and natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) following aerobic and resistance exercise interventions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of the data distribution was assessed prior to analysis, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. A 3 (group) × 2 (time) mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons was used to examine main effects (time and group) and group × time interaction effects. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

BNP and NT-proBNP levels increased significantly from pre- to post-intervention across groups, with significant interaction effects (p < .001), indicating that the magnitude of change differed between groups. Post hoc analyses showed that BNP levels were significantly higher in the aerobic exercise group, whereas NT-proBNP levels were significantly higher in the resistance exercise group compared to the other groups.

In contrast, Galectin-3 and s-ST2 levels decreased significantly over time, with significant group × time interaction effects (p < .001), demonstrating greater reductions in the exercise groups compared with the control group. The interaction effects demonstrated large effect sizes for all biomarkers (η² range = 0.61–0.87), indicating substantial intervention-related changes following both aerobic and resistance exercise interventions.

Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of aerobic and resistance exercise on cardiac and inflammatory biomarkers in sedentary young females. The findings demonstrated that both exercise modalities induced significant changes in circulating biomarkers, with increases in BNP and NT-proBNP and reductions in s-ST2 and Galectin-3, and the magnitude of these responses differed between exercise modalities.

The observed increases in BNP and NT-proBNP are consistent with previous research indicating that these biomarkers respond to increased cardiac workload and hemodynamic stress induced by physical activity [23, 30]. While elevated natriuretic peptide levels are commonly associated with pathological conditions such as heart failure, their increase in response to exercise is considered a physiological adaptation reflecting enhanced cardiac function and metabolic regulation [31]. Previous studies [32] have also suggested that endurance exercise training may reduce circulating NT-proBNP concentrations, reflecting decreased ventricular wall tension and improved myocardial function, whereas resistance exercise may induce transient increases in NT-proBNP levels due to greater hemodynamic load. However, these responses are generally considered physiological and reversible adaptations rather than indicators of myocardial damage in healthy individuals.

In contrast, the reductions in s-ST2 and Galectin-3 observed in the present study suggest favourable modulation of inflammatory and fibrotic processes. These biomarkers have been associated with myocardial fibrosis, inflammation, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [33–35], and their decrease may indicate improvements in cardiometabolic health following regular exercise. Although acute exercise has been shown to transiently influence these biomarkers, long-term exercise interventions may lead to more favourable adaptations [34]. These findings are further supported by evidence from clinical populations, where early exercise in patients with acute heart failure has been shown to induce transient increases in NT-proBNP without affecting fibrosis-related biomarkers such as s-ST2 and Galectin-3, suggesting that exercise does not exacerbate myocardial injury even in compromised conditions [36]. In this context, the reductions observed in the present study may reflect more favourable cardiometabolic adaptations in healthy individuals.

Sedentary behaviour has been associated with reduced natriuretic peptide levels, particularly in young females, potentially due to increased clearance mechanisms and altered receptor activity [37, 38]. The present findings support the notion that structured exercise interventions may counteract these effects and enhance circulating natriuretic peptide responses.

The differential responses observed between aerobic and resistance exercise further suggest that exercise modality plays a role in biomarker adaptations. In the present study, resistance exercise elicited a more pronounced increase in NT-proBNP levels, which may be explained by greater hemodynamic load and transient pressure-related ventricular wall stress during resistance-type contractions. In contrast, aerobic exercise may induce more volume-related cardiovascular adaptations. These findings indicate that different exercise modalities may induce distinct cardiovascular and metabolic responses.

Taken together, the present findings demonstrate that moderate-intensity aerobic and resistance exercise can favorably modulate cardiac and inflammatory biomarkers in sedentary young females. Importantly, the increases in BNP and NT-proBNP should be interpreted as physiological responses to exercise rather than indicators of pathological cardiac stress, whereas the reductions in s-ST2 and Galectin-3 reflect improvements in inflammatory status and cardiovascular health.

Conclusions

In conclusion, both aerobic and resistance exercise resulted in significant increases in circulating natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) and reductions in inflammatory biomarkers (s-ST2 and Galectin-3) in sedentary women aged 18–25 years. These findings suggest that regular exercise may favourably modulate cardiac and inflammatory profiles in this population.

Resistance exercise was associated with a greater increase in NT-proBNP levels than aerobic exercise, suggesting that different exercise modalities elicit distinct physiological responses. Importantly, the observed increases in natriuretic peptides should be interpreted as physiological adaptations to exercise rather than indicators of pathological cardiac stress.

Overall, both aerobic and resistance exercise may serve as effective non-pharmacological strategies for improving cardiometabolic health in young sedentary women. Further studies involving larger sample sizes and detailed cardiovascular assessments are needed to clarify the underlying physiological mechanisms and long-term clinical relevance of exercise-induced biomarker adaptations.

Strength and limitation

A major strength of this study is its focus on the effects of different exercise modalities on both established and emerging cardiovascular biomarkers in a young sedentary female population. The findings provide novel insight into exercise-induced changes in natriuretic peptides and inflammatory markers in a non-clinical population.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 15 per group) may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, detailed cardiac imaging techniques, such as echocardiography, were not performed, which could have provided more comprehensive information on cardiac structure and function. In addition, dietary intake was monitored but not strictly controlled throughout the intervention period, which may have influenced biomarker responses. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more advanced cardiovascular assessments are warranted to further validate these findings.
