A case of exogenous lipoid pneumonia caused by Vaseline to the tracheostomy in a laryngectomy patient
Authors: Miho Wakazono, Hirokazu Kimura, Takatoshi Suzuki, Nozomu Takei, Kenjiro Kato, Nobuyasu Wakazono, Junichi Nakamura, Sho Nakakubo, Emi Takakuwa, Satoshi Konno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 65-year-old man, who had undergone laryngectomy two years earlier, was incidentally found to have nodular shadows and ground-glass opacity (GGO) in both lower lobes, identified nine months ago, which gradually progressed. He was diagnosed with lipoid pneumonia based on a transbronchial lung biopsy. The daily application of Vaseline to the tracheostomy site was considered the cause of exogenous lipoid pneumonia. Although GGO improved after the discontinuation of Vaseline use, it persisted 2 years later. This case is notable for its unusually short onset interval of 1.5 years and the longest reported follow-up of Vaseline-induced lipoid pneumonia after laryngectomy.Keywords: Lipoid pneumonia, Vaseline, Laryngectomy, Tracheostomy, Crazy paving pattern

Introduction

Lipoid pneumonia is a rare respiratory disease caused by the accumulation of lipids in the alveoli. It is classified as either endogenous or exogenous depending on the source of the lipids [1]. Exogenous lipoid pneumonia (ELP) is caused by the aspiration or inhalation of lipids [2]. Chronic ELP is associated with long-term exposure and is often difficult to diagnose because symptoms are absent or nonspecific, and imaging findings are variable [3]. Several cases of ELP caused by Vaseline application to the tracheostomy site have been previously reported, developing 3 to 13 years after laryngectomy [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. We present a rare case of ELP that developed within a short period of 1.5 years after laryngectomy, with the longest follow-up reported to date.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old man underwent a total laryngectomy for laryngeal cancer 2 years ago. He had been undergoing regular Computed tomography (CT) scans postoperatively for surveillance of recurrence. Nine months ago, a CT scan revealed nodular shadows in both lower lobes, which gradually increased in size. He was therefore referred to our hospital and admitted for further examination. The patient had not experienced any symptoms such as fever, cough, or dyspnea. He was taking levothyroxine for hypothyroidism after laryngectomy, benzbromarone for hyperuricemia, and zolpidem for insomnia. He had a remote smoking history of 20 cigarettes per day for 4 years in his 20s and no occupational exposure to toxic fumes, dust, or contact history with animals. Since his laryngectomy, he had used a humidifier, but kept it clean.

On admission, the patient's respiratory rate was 14/min, body temperature was 35.8 °C, and oxygen saturation was 98% on room air. Chest X-ray showed a ground-glass shadow on the mediastinal side of the right lower lung field. Chest CT scan revealed ground glass opacity (GGO) with a crazy paving pattern on the mediastinal side of the bilateral lower lobes, which had been expanding for the past nine months (Fig. 1). Blood tests revealed no elevation in white blood cell count or C-reactive protein, but there was a mild elevation in Krebs von den Lungen-6 of 461 U/mL and surfactant protein A of 70.1 ng/mL. Serum anti granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor antibody was not detected. Respiratory function tests showed diffusion impairment with a percent predicted diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide of 59.7%, with no obstructive nor restrictive impairment. Cell fractionation of the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid obtained from the left lower lobe revealed macrophages (70.3%), lymphocytes (11.7%), neutrophils (10.3%), and eosinophils (7.3%), with a CD4/CD8 ratio of 0.77. The BAL fluid was clear. However, the recovery rate was low (18%), and therefore the accuracy of the cell fractionation was considered limited. Cytological analysis of BAL fluid demonstrated foamy macrophages. Furthermore, a transbronchial lung biopsy was performed from the right lower lobe, which revealed foamy macrophages filling the alveolar space (Fig. 2). Immunohistochemical and Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS)–Alcian blue staining were performed. The foamy cells were CD68-positive and negative for AE1/AE3 and S100 protein, consistent with histiocytes. PAS–Alcian blue staining showed no evidence of mucin, suggesting that the vacuolated structures represented lipid droplets rather than mucin. Based on these findings, a diagnosis of lipoid pneumonia was made.

Additional medical interview revealed that he had been applying Vaseline daily to his tracheostomy site since laryngectomy, which was presumed to be the cause of lipoid pneumonia. The patient was instructed to discontinue using Vaseline. Chest CT findings showed slight improvement at 6 months and further improvement at 2 years after discontinuation (Fig. 1). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report.

Discussion

ELP was first reported by Laughlen in 1925 as pneumonia occurring in patients with a history of oil-based products [9]. A variety of substances have been identified as causes of ELP, including ingested fish oil, excessive use of lip balm, and chronic inhalation of heated cooking oil or furniture polishing oil [3]. These oily substances can easily enter the lower airway, as they do not trigger glottic closure or the cough reflex. Furthermore, they inhibit mucociliary clearance, which prevents their expulsion from the airway [2]. Once in the alveoli, lipids are taken up by macrophages but are not metabolized and are repeatedly released into the alveoli after the macrophages die. These released lipids cause giant cell granulomatous reaction, chronic inflammation, and fibrosis of the alveoli and interstitium, which is thought to be the pathological mechanism of ELP.

In this case, ELP was attributed to the daily use of Vaseline on the tracheostomy site. Vaseline consists of petrolatum, a semi-solid mixture of hydrocarbons derived from petroleum, and is classified as a mineral oil. Oily products such as Vaseline are often used to protect the skin of the tracheostomy after laryngectomy. To date, nine cases of lipoid pneumonia following laryngectomy have been reported in the English literature (Table 1) [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. The duration from laryngectomy to the onset of the disease ranged from 3 to 13 years (median: 10 years), suggesting that the disease typically develops after long-term lipid accumulation. In contrast, the present case developed ELP only 1.5 years after laryngectomy, which is unusually early. Possible contributing factors include the rapid accumulation of lipid due to daily Vaseline use, regular CT follow-up that enabled early detection, and potential individual differences in lipid clearance capacity. ELP is generally associated with chronic exposure to lipid substances; however, it may also develop without a long exposure history, as in the present case.

The disease progress after discontinuation of Vaseline use has varied, with some cases progressing to death, while others have recovered completely. A review of ELP for various causes found that, after withdrawal of the offending agent, radiological findings improved in 33% of cases, worsened in 39%, and remained unchanged in 28% [3]. In this case, the patient demonstrated gradual improvement in CT findings during a two-year follow-up, the longest observation period reported for ELP following laryngectomy. This finding highlighted the importance of long-term follow-up in patients with ELP.

Currently, no established treatment exists for ELP. Previous reports have included whole-lung lavage or bronchoalveolar lavage to remove the aspirated material, and immunosuppressive therapies such as corticosteroids used to suppress inflammation [10]. In this case, the patient remains asymptomatic and has shown gradual radiological improvement. Therefore, careful follow-up continues, with strict avoidance of Vaseline.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the potential for the early onset of exogenous lipoid pneumonia following laryngectomy. Vaseline application to the tracheostomy site should be recognized as a preventable risk factor. Persistent radiological findings highlight the need for careful long-term monitoring.
