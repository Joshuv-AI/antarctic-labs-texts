Identification of Cathepsin L as the Molecular Target of Hydroxychloroquine With Chemical Proteomics
Authors: Jin Shang, Bingjie Hu, Karen Ka-Yan Kung, Jiajun Jiang, Qi Zhang, Man-Kin Wong, Hin Chu, Qian Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) and chloroquine have been utilized as antimalarial drugs for decades. Recently, these compounds were reported to inhibit various viruses utilizing the endosomal entry pathway. However, their direct molecular targets in host cells remain elusive. In this study, we developed a clickable photo-crosslinking probe in combination with proteomic approaches to identified cathepsin L (CTSL) as the binding target of HCQ. Extensive biochemical and in silico analyses were conducted to validate the HCQ–CTSL interactions. HCQ significantly inhibited the protease activity of CTSL and suppressed CTSL-dependent coronavirus entry in cells that support endosomal entry pathway. These findings not only reveal the underlying mechanism of how HCQ inhibits endosomal viral entry but also guide the rational use of HCQ against other emerging infectious agents.

Chloroquine (CQ) has been employed as an antimalarial drug for nearly 8 decades (1). Its analog, hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), has been extensively used as a rheumatism medication and clinically used for treating systemic lupus erythematosus since Food and Drug Administration approval in 1955 (2). Leveraging probe synthesis and chemical proteomics, several targets of lysosomotropic compounds CQ–HCQ have been reported to explain their mechanisms. Notably, a dimeric CQ probe identified PPT1 (palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 1) as a target mediating autophagy inhibition (3), and a CQ-X probe revealed cathepsin L (CTSL) as the target of CQ, leading to the amino acid accumulation phenotype (4). In addition to their traditional applications, CQ and HCQ have demonstrated antiviral effects against a range of viruses, including influenza viruses, flaviviruses, lentiviruses, and coronaviruses (5, 6, 7, 8). However, the reported antiviral effects of HCQ are primarily associated with lysosomal pH regulation, lacking specific molecular targets (9, 10, 11). This attributed to the limited availability of chemical probes of specific molecules, which has hindered the identification and validation of precise mechanisms of the antiviral effects.

Successful infection of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) requires the direct interaction of SARS-CoV-2 spike with its cell surface receptor, angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) (12). In addition to the receptor ACE2, a battery of molecules on the cell surface can serve as attachment or entry factors that facilitate SARS-CoV-2 infection (13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20). Upon physical interaction between SARS-CoV-2 spike and ACE2, proteolytic cleavage of the viral spike protein by host proteases is further required for successful SARS-CoV-2 fusion and entry into the target cells (12). In cells with transmembrane serine protease expression, SARS-CoV-2 predominantly enters through the plasma membrane entry pathway mediated by transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2) or other transmembrane serine proteases (13, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26). In contrast, in cells with low or no TMPRSS2 expression, SARS-CoV-2 alternatively enters through endocytic pathways where spike proteins on virus particles are activated by CTSL in endosomes (27, 28, 29, 30). HCQ has been demonstrated to effectively inhibit SARS-CoV-2 replication in cell types that support endosomal entry, whereas the presence of TMPRSS2 limits its antiviral activity (31, 32, 33). However, the mechanism of action of HCQ as well as the target of HCQ in these cells responsible for its antiviral activity remain unknown.

As a lysosome-targeting small molecules, HCQ raises lysosomal pH and causes amino acid accumulation (4, 11). These effects can introduce background in target identification and complicate validation of target biology. Moreover, whether a probe preserves the antiviral activity of the parent compound is a concern. A quantitative proteomics design based on parent–compound competition can address this issue and reduce technical variation. In a competition experiment, parental compounds serve as competitors to block the binding sites of the probes, and the signal/intensity decrease in the competition group indicates selective binding to the parental compounds.

In the current study, we synthesized a clickable photo-crosslinking probe named HCQ-P. This probe mimics HCQ for binding its target proteins. By leveraging the HCQ-P and proteomic techniques, we identified CTSL as a major binding target of HCQ. Importantly, we found that HCQ significantly inhibited CTSL protease activity and suppressed CTSL-dependent coronavirus entry in cells. Overall, our findings unravel the mechanism of how HCQ inhibits endosomal viral entry and will guide rational use of HCQ against future emerging infectious agents.

Experimental Procedures

Antibodies and Reagents

HCQ (HY-B1370), CQ (HY-17589A), E64 (HY-15282), quinine, mefloquine (HY-17437), promethazine (HY-B0781), azithromycin (HY-17506), and clemastine fumarate (HY-B0298A) are supplied by MCE. CuSO4 (209198), EDTA, Triton X-100, NP-40 (IGEPAL CA-630), formaldehyde solution, SDS, iodoacetamide, protease inhibitor (11873580001), benzonase (70746-4), DTT, and compounds used in probe synthesis, including alkynyl amide and propargyl bromide were ordered from Sigma–Aldrich. Tris(2-chloroethyl) phosphate, fetal bovine serum (FBS, including dialyzed FBS), penicillin, streptomycin, ampicillin, trypsin (EDTA-free), Tris–HCl, imidazole, Hepes, ammonium nitrate, sodium chloride, LB broth powder, PBS, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), acetonitrile (ACN), MgCl2•6H2O, Western chemiluminescent kit (34580), bicinchoninic acid (23227) and cell culture media ,including stable isotope labeling with amino acids in cell culture (SILAC) DME, high- and low-glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) and RPMI1640 were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Cells in the experiments (VeroE6, Huh7, and Calu3) were provided by Dr CHU from HKU, and human embryonic kidney 293T cell line (HEK293T) was ordered from American Type Culture Collection, and competent cells (TOP10 and BL21 [DE3]) as well as Mini-Prep kit (DP103-03) were collected from TianGen. The antibodies used were as follows: CTSL antibody (CST #71298), FLAG-tag antibody (CST #14793), β-actin antibody (AF7018; Affinity), 488-FITC antibody (Thermo; A-11090), and goat anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase (AS014; ABclonal). The CTSL Activity Assay Kit (Fluorometric) (ab65306) was purchased from Abcam. All gel casting reagents and protein markers were purchased from Bio-Rad. TAMRA (tetramethyl rhodamine)-azide, biotin-azide, and Tris((1-benzyl-4-triazolyl) methyl) amine were ordered from Click Chemistry Tools. FPLC, NAP-10 column, and dialysis membrane were purchased from GE. All other chemicals were from Sigma–Aldrich, unless otherwise stated.

Synthesis and Characterization of HCQ-Probe

All solvents and chemicals were purchased from commercial sources and used without further purification. Milli-Q water was deionized using a Milli-Q Gradient A10 system (Millipore). Flash column chromatography was performed using silica gel 60 (230–400 mesh ASTM). 1H and 13C NMR spectra were examined on Bruker Advance-III 400 MHz and 600 MHz spectrometers using TMS or residual solvent as the internal standard. All chemical shifts are quoted on the scale in ppm, and coupling constants (J) are reported in Hertz (Hz) with the following splitting abbreviations: s = singlet, br s = broad singlet, d = doublet, dd = double doublet, t = triplet, and m = multiplet. All low-resolution mass spectrometry (MS) spectra were obtained on an electrospray ionization source of Waters Acquity UPLC H-Class-QDa System in the positive ion mode. All high-resolution MS spectra were obtained on an electrospray ionization source of Agilent 6540 Ultra High Definition Accurate-Mass Q-TOF LC–MS systems in the positive ion mode. Detailed synthesis and structural characterization can be found in the Supporting Information.

Cell Culture

VeroE6, Huh7, Calu3, and HEK293T cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. All media were supplemented with 100 U/ml penicillin and 100 μg/ml streptomycin. All cells were cultured in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2.

Cell Viability Assay

Cells were seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C for complete adherence. HCQ or HCQ-P was added to the cell culture medium at gradient concentrations and incubated for another 24 h. Cell Counting Kit-8 was used to analyze cell viability. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm by (using) a plate reader. Using DMSO-treated group as a positive control, the cell viability rate was determined as Abs490(sample)/Abs490(positive) × 100%. Each gradient has four independent repeats. IC50 values were fitting to dose–response model by using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Inc).

Proteome Labeling and In-Gel Fluorescence Scan

Live cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 80% cell density, after cell attachment overnight, we pretreated DMEM-containing HCQ and then treated cells with HCQ-P for 1 h at 37 °C/5% CO2 incubator. The medium was aspirated, and cells were washed gently with PBS (twice) followed by centrifugation to collect cells. All cell pellets were lysed and subjected to click chemistry reaction with 50 μM TAMRA-azide, 0.1 mM Tris((1-benzyl-4-triazolyl) methyl) amine, 1 mM Tris(2-chloroethyl) phosphate, and 1 mM CuSO4. After incubation for 1.5 h at room temperature, the reaction was terminated by adding prechilled acetone. Precipitated proteins were subsequently collected by centrifugation, loaded onto SDS-PAGE, and visualized by in-gel fluorescence scanning. Gels were scanned by Typhoon 9400 under Cy3 and Cy5 channels for fluorescence bands and protein markers, respectively. All figures with 100-pixel image scanning model, 500 photomultiplier tube laser power were applied for scanning.

SILAC Pull-Down Assay

Before pull-down assay, VeroE6 cells were cultured in SILAC DMEM supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin for more than five generations. 13C6 15N2 l-lysine–HCl– and 13C6 15N4 l-arginine–HCl–labeled cells as heavy cells (H) and l-lysine monohydrate– and l-arginine monohydrochloride–labeled cells as light cells (L). These heavy or light cells with 80% to 90% confluency in 100-mm cell culture dish were treated with or without HCQ followed by HCQ-P treatment. Then, H and L cells were pooled together with the ratio of H/L = 1. After click reaction, samples were mixed with fivefold of cold acetone and stored overnight (−20 °C). Precipitated proteins were washed twice with 5 ml cold methanol and resuspended in 1 ml PBS (pH 7.5) containing 1% SDS and desalted by passing through NAP-10 columns (GE). The labeled proteins were then immobilized with magnetic streptavidin beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After washing with freshly prepared 6 M urea, beads were digested with trypsin overnight. Digestion was stopped by adding 1% formic acid (FA). The resulting peptides were enriched with C18 Zip Tips (Merck) and eluted with 50% ACN and 0.1% FA. The samples were dried down by SpeedVac and then resuspended in 0.1% FA for analysis by LC–MS/MS.

Proteomics and SILAC Data Analysis

Proteomic analyses were performed using an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an UltiMate 3000 UPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with C18 analytical column. Mobile phases A and B consisted of 0.1% FA in water and 0.1% FA in 100% ACN, respectively. A 120 min gradient at a flow rate of 300 nl/min was used. Mobile phase B was increased to 6% at 12 min, 20% at 82 min, 30% at 92 min, 90% at 100 min, and held for 5 min. Data were collected in data-dependent acquisition mode with higher-energy collisional dissociation fragmentation with TopN mode. The resolution was set at 60,000 for MS1 and 15,000 for MS2 with 30 ms maximum injection time. All resulted spectra were searched against the UniProt Homo sapiens database (20,330 entries, accessed September 2019) using MaxQuant 1.5.8.2. Proteins were quantified with the intensities of the heavy (Arg10, Lys8) and light (Arg0, Lys0) isotopes. The parameters for searching: a mass tolerance of 10 ppm for precursor ions; ±0.1 Da for-fragment ions, carbamidomethylation on cysteine was set as a fixed modification, oxidation on methionine, and protein N-terminal acetylation was set as variable modifications. The enzyme was specified as trypsin with two missed cleavages allowed. False discovery rate for peptide spectral matches and proteins was set as 1%. The maximum number of modifications per peptide was three. The results were exported for CRAPome filtering with a cutoff frequency of 0.2. To ensure confident identification of proteins, proteins with fewer than two unique peptides were excluded. NA values of protein intensities were replaced by “100” to calculate SILAC ratios.

Immunofluorescence Colocalization of HCQ-P

According to the reported method with some modifications, live VeroE6 cells were seeded into glass bottom dishes. After reaching 30% confluency, cells were treated with 0.5 ml DMEM with HCQ-P at a final concentration of 5 μM. Cells treated with DMSO served as a negative control. After incubation for 1 h at 37 °C, the medium was removed and cells were washed with 0.5 ml 1× PBS twice, followed by fixation at room temperature with 3.7% formaldehyde in PBS for 1 h, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Then, cells were blocked with blocking buffer for 30 min followed by click reaction for 2 h at room temperature with vigorous shaking. Subsequently, cells were washed with PBS once and 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS three times. CTSL antibody (CST; 1/100 dilution) was incubated with cells at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with secondary antibody at 1:200 dilution. Finally, the cells were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (1:10,000 dilution in 1× PBS) for 10 min at room temperature in dark prior to imaging. Images were collected by using the Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope system equipped with Leica 40×/1.15 oil objective, 405/488/561 nm diode laser, white laser (470–670 nm, with 1 nm increments), and a photomultiplier tube detector ranging from 410 to 700 nm for steady-state fluorescence.

CTSL Knockdown and Overexpression in Live Cells

To knock down CTSL, a complex containing 10 nM CTSL siRNA duplex was transfected into Huh7 cells (8 × 105 cells, 40–50% confluency) grown in 6-well plates at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator by using 6 μl Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. The control siRNA duplex was added to the control group. After 6 h incubation, the medium was replaced by fresh media, and the cells were cultured for another 42 h. Then, cells were treated with HCQ-P or vehicle for 30 min, followed by in-gel fluorescence scanning and Western blotting analysis. For the overexpression assay, 3 μg plasmid (Lenti-CTSL-FLAG) was transferred into HEK293T/Huh7 cells (1 × 106 cells, 60–70% confluency) in a 6-well plate at 37 °C with 5% CO2 by using 9 μl Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 6 h, followed by incubation for another 18 h in fresh medium.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay

According to the reported method with some modifications (34), live Vero E6 cells were seeded 24 h before drug treatment. After treated cells with HCQ or DMSO for 1 h, cell pellets were washed and collected prior to the transient heat by using PCR machine. Cell pellets were then lysed and collected by centrifugation and loaded on SDS-PAGE for Western blot.

Discovery Studio for Molecular Docking Study

The human CTSL was picked up as the docking receptor from its crystal structure (113–333 amino acids) (Protein Data Bank code: 3OF8) (35). All water and its irreversible inhibitor (diazomethylketone) were first removed, and the receptor was autoprepared under macromolecule module. HCQ and CQ were drawn in ChemBioOffice Ultra, version 13 and preformed the autopreparation program for small molecule as docking ligands before discovery docking studies. All invalid charges and broken bonds were fixed, and all hydrogen atoms were added after the preparation. The sulfur atom of Cys138 of human CTSL was selected as the docking atom under current selection model of Discovery Studio, and the radius of spherical site was set to 8. All rotamer libraries of Cys138 and ligands were considered to determine possible combinations of docking poses. The docking was performed under LibDock model of Discovery Studio, and these poses with the highest 50 docking scores were selected. All docking runs and cartoons were performed and drawn by using Discovery Studio 2016, v16 version.

Enzymatic Activity Assay

CTSL activity assays were performed using the Abcam CTSL Activity Assay kit (Fluorometric) following the manufacturer’s instructions; 1 × 106 cells were assayed in each sample.

Pseudovirus Entry and Cell Treatment

Huh7 stable overexpression cells were seeded in 96-well plate 24 h before gradient drug treatment for 1 h. The cells were then transducted with coronavirus spike pseudovirus at 100 tissue culture infectious dose of 50 per milliliter and cultured for another 24 h under drug treatment before firefly luciferase anticavity analysis (Promega). For si-CTSL virus infection, Huh7 cells were plated in 6-well plates and transfected with 10 nM si-CTSL (GenScript). After 24 h of transfection, the cells were lysed to analyze the CTSL mRNA levels to evaluate the efficiency. The si-CTSL cells were seeded in 96-well plates, followed by drug treatment and pseudovirus transduction under the same conditions stated above.

Authentic Virus Infection and Cell Treatment

Huh7 cells with stable overexpression were seeded in 96-well plates 24 h before treatment. A concentration gradient of the drug was applied for 1 h, after which the cells were infected with coronavirus at a multiplicity of infection of 0.5. Cells were maintained under the same drug conditions for an additional 24 h, followed by collection of cell pellets and supernatants for sgE and GAPDH mRNA analysis. Percent inhibition was calculated and normalized to the control group (0 μM HCQ).

Results

Development of a Clickable Photo-Crosslinking Probe to Profile HCQ Targets

We started with the design and chemical synthesis of probes that mimic HCQ for identifying its direct-binding proteins. A clickable HCQ-P was synthesized by coupling the hydroxyl group of HCQ with a tag, which is a bifunctional moiety with a diazirine group and terminal alkyne (Figs. 1A and S1–S11). SARS-CoV-2 Delta and Omicron BA.1 infection assays were performed to test the bioactivity of the probe in VeroE6 cells, in which HCQ exhibited confirmed antiviral activity because of the lack of TMPRSS2 expression (26). We found that the HCQ-P conserved antiviral activity at nontoxic concentrations, with IC50 values 13.01 μM for Delta and 6.95 μM for Omicron BA.1 (Fig. 1, B, C and S12). Based on this decreased antiviral activity of the probe, we performed a competition assay to ensure that the target proteins we identified were potential targets of the parent molecule. Upon UV irradiation, the probe generated carbene efficiently from the diazirine precursor within 2 min (Supplemental Fig. S13). Afterward, to examine whether HCQ-P could specifically label target proteins, we treated live cells with HCQ followed by HCQ-P and observed the competition effects using in-gel fluorescence scan. In the fluorescent images, prominent bands at approximately 30 KDa were labeled by HCQ-P in both VeroE6 and Huh7 cells, and they could be eliminated by HCQ pretreatment (Figs. 1D and S14), suggesting a high specificity and binding affinity to HCQ. The putative target proteins were shared by HCQ and CQ, with HCQ exhibiting higher binding affinity. In contrast, these targets were not shared by the CQ analogs quinine and mefloquine or other lysosomotropic compounds (Supplemental Fig. S15), thereby excluding the competition effects in Figure 1D attributable to nonspecific lysosomal accumulation. HCQ-P Identifies HCQ Targets in Live Cells

In the “Forward” group, VeroE6 cells with heavy isotope labeling (Arg10, Lys8) were treated with HCQ-P, whereas light-labeled cells were pretreated with an excess of HCQ prior to exposure to the same amount of HCQ-P to assess competition. Before the click reaction, heavy- and light-treated cells were combined to form the “Forward” sample. In the reciprocal "Reverse" group, the treatments were swapped. After data searching and contaminant removal, the heavy-to-light fold change for each protein reflected its differential enrichment between the probe-labeled and competition conditions. In Figure 2B, a higher fold change indicates more effective competition by the parent compound, consistent with greater binding specificity. Notably, the fold changes in CTSL, cathepsin D (CTSD), and prosaposin (PSAP) ranked among the top three proteins in both biological replicates (Fig. 2B and Supplemental Table S1). STRING consortium analysis of these top three proteins (CTSL, CTSD, and PSAP) indicated reported physical interactions among them. Furthermore, their probe-enriched MS intensities exceeded 1E8 in both replicates suggesting the strong target engagement (Fig. 2C).

CTSL Binds to HCQ Noncovalently at the Hydrophobic Interface

To exclude HCQ binding to a lysosomal complex rather than directly to certain target, we tested the top-ranked SILAC pull-down candidates. We generated cell lines stably overexpressing FLAG-tagged CTSL, CTSD, PSAP, and 7-dehydrocholesterol reductase (DHCR7) and assessed HCQ-probe labeling. No probe-derived fluorescence was detected for CTSD, PSAP, or DHCR7, excluding their direct interaction with HCQ (data not shown). In contrast, CTSL showed strong probe labeling (Fig. 3A), indicating selective direct binding to HCQ. Using an anti-CTSL antibody, we observed four forms of CTSL at different processing stages (Fig. 3A). Consistent with prior reports, pre-CTSL and pro-CTSL were predominantly cytosolic, whereas intermediate and mature CTSL localized to lysosomes (36). Notably, lysosomal CTSL was robustly labeled by HCQ-P and effectively competed by HCQ, whereas the abundant cytosolic CTSL was not labeled. These findings indicate strong selectivity of HCQ-P for lysosomal-processed CTSL (Fig. 3A). To validate HCQ–CTSL interactions, an alternative assay cellular thermal shift assay was employed. In cellular thermal shift assay, HCQ significantly affected the thermal properties of CTSL, supporting direct interactions between them (Fig. 3B). Immunofluorescence against CTSL further demonstrated that HCQ-P could be UV conjugated to CTSL, specifically in cells (Supplemental Fig. S17). We also performed molecular docking to predict the binding mode of CTSL and HCQ. A potential hydrophobic docking pocket was shown with a convincing score exceeding 100 (Fig. 3C). Cys26, Gly69, and Ser217 of CTSL were involved in the interaction with HCQ (Fig. 3D). CQ docked well into the same binding pocket although it lacked the hydrogen bond with Ser217 (Supplemental Fig. S18), which may account for HCQ’s higher binding affinity. In contrast, when we docked E64, a known CTSL inhibitor, into CTSL, E64 bound to a different pocket, suggesting a distinct inhibitory mechanism (Supplemental Fig. S19). Based on the structural information, we mutated critical residues in CTSL, including Gly69, Asp72, Ala215, and Ser217, and observed that the binding between CTSL and HCQ was abolished (Fig. 3E). In the middle lane of Figure 3E, overexpression of wildtype CTSL allowed the probe to capture and label mature CTSL species. However, when the indicated amino acid residues were mutated, CTSL was no longer labeled by the probe (right lane). These results validate the docking-predicted binding site between CTSL and HCQ.

HCQ Targets Endosomal Virus Entry Mediated by CTSL

As CTSL is a cysteine protease, we performed a substrate reaction assay to evaluate the specific impact of HCQ on CTSL activity. CTSL-mediated hydrolysis of the substrate AC-FR was quantified by monitoring the fluorescence of the released 7-amino-4-trifluoromethylcoumarin. We observed that HCQ inhibited CTSL protease activity by 30% at 50 μM, which has a greater inhibition efficacy than CQ at the same concentration, whereas the HCQ analogs quinine/mefloquine did not inhibit the activity of CTSL (Fig. 4A). Next, after overnight preincubation with CTSL enzyme and HCQ, AC-FR cleavage was inhibited by HCQ with an IC50 of 233.6 μM (Fig. 4B). Then we explored the impact of CTSL inactivation by HCQ on Omicron BA.1 endocytic entry. To this end, we employed vesicular stomatitis virus–based Omicron BA.1 spike pseudoviruses for endocytic entry analysis. To further enhance the CTSL-mediated endosomal entry pathway in Huh7 cells for Omicron BA.1, we constructed Huh7 cells with stable expression of CTSL (Supplemental Fig. S20, A and B). It has been reported that SARS-CoV-2 enters the human liver cancer cell line Huh7 predominantly via an endocytic pathway because of low TMPRSS2 expression (38). We first treated Huh7-CTSL cells with different concentrations of HCQ, followed by transduction with Omicron BA.1 spike pseudoviruses (Fig. 4C). Omicron BA.1 spike pseudovirus entry in Huh7-CTSL cells was robustly decreased in a dose-dependent manner after treatment with HCQ (Fig. 4C). Importantly, the IC50 of HCQ on Huh7-CTSL cells (0.01241 μM) was 408-fold lower than that on the control Huh7 cells (Huh7-EV) (4.963 μM) upon Omicron BA.1 spike pseudovirus transduction (Fig. 4C). To verify the data obtained using pseudoviruses, Huh7-CTSL cells or Huh7-EV cells were treated with different concentrations of HCQ and then challenged with authentic Omicron BA.1. Consistently, HCQ efficiently blocked authentic Omicron BA.1 replication in Huh7-EV cells but with more prominent effects in Huh7-CTSL cells (Figs. 4D and S21). Next, we further evaluated the physiological relevance of HCQ with CTSL by siRNA-mediated gene depletion in Huh7 cells for Omicron BA.1 spike pseudovirus entry (Supplemental Fig. S22). The siRNA-treated Huh7 cells were pretreated with different concentrations of HCQ and then transduced with Omicron BA.1 spike pseudoviruses. The inhibition of Omicron BA.1 spike pseudovirus entry by HCQ was dramatically decreased upon CTSL depletion compared with that of the CTSL intact control group (Fig. 4E). In addition to SARS-CoV-2, SARS-CoV-1 that emerged in 2002 is known to utilize CSTL for efficient entry (39, 40, 41). To evaluate whether HCQ also targets CTSL-mediated SARS-CoV-1 entry, we challenged CSTL or control siRNA–treated Huh7 cells with SARS-CoV-1 spike pseudovirus. Consistent with our observation on SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.1, the inhibition of SARS-CoV-1 spike pseudovirus entry by HCQ was dramatically decreased upon CTSL depletion compared with that in the CTSL intact control group (Fig. 4F).

Discussion

Since the onset of the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic, HCQ–CQ has been extensively investigated because of its antiviral properties. It was reported in 2020 that CQ only inhibits viral entry endosomal pathway, and therefore, CQ does not block SARS-CoV-2 infection in TMPRSS2-expressing human lung cancer cells (26, 33). However, in 2023, the rapidly spreading variant Omicron BA.1 became dominant and was reported to enter primarily through the endosomal pathway. Moreover, several clinical trials and hospitalized statistical analysis updated many positive conclusions of HCQ–CQ curative effects (42, 43). These controversial antiviral effects of CQ–HCQ highlight the importance of elucidating the mechanisms based on the direct binding target.

In this study, we synthesized a clickable photo-crosslinking probe that mimics HCQ to profile its target proteins. By leveraging the chemical probe and proteomic techniques, we identified CTSL as a major binding target of HCQ. Importantly, we found that HCQ significantly inhibited the CTSL protease activity and suppressed CTSL-dependent coronavirus entry into cells. This study establishes CTSL as a direct molecular target of HCQ and a key determinant of HCQ's variant-specific antiviral activity.

HCQ-P maintained the antiviral activity of parental compound at nontoxic concentrations. For target identification, we implemented a competition assay–based quantitative proteomic strategy, which minimized false-positive results and reduced the MS background because of lysosomal accumulation. To further validate the proteomic results, we performed a series of biochemical assays. Only the lysosome-localized intermediate and mature forms of overexpressed CTSL could be labeled with HCQ-P, demonstrating target binding specificity. The suggested binding residues between HCQ and CTSL predicted by molecular docking were subsequently verified by mutagenesis experiments. Function exploration using virus assays reveals two critical determinants of HCQ's antiviral efficacy: (1) higher CTSL expression correlates with significantly enhanced antiviral activity of HCQ, suggesting CTSL-mediated viral entry as the primary target of HCQ inhibition; (2) HCQ exhibits markedly reduced inhibitory potency against Delta variant compared with Omicron BA.1, reflecting differential CTSL dependence in spike protein processing between these variants, and consisting with previous publications (33). These findings established CTSL as a key determinant of HCQ's antiviral activity.

Our study had several limitations. For example, the multiple functions of HCQ suggest that it may engage several targets rather than one, and the mechanisms governed by these targets may work synergistically. These potential synergies were not fully explored in our mechanistic investigations. Accordingly, we conclude that CTSL is a key but not the only target of HCQ.

Overall, these findings not only reveal the underlying mechanism of how HCQ inhibits endosomal viral entry but also guide the rational use of HCQ against other emerging infectious agents.
