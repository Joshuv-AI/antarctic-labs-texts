Suvorexant for Reduction of Delirium in Older Adults After Hospitalization
Authors: Kotaro Hatta, Yasuhiro Kishi, Ken Wada, Takashi Takeuchi, Toshihiro Taira, Keiichi Uemura, Asao Ogawa, Kanae Takahashi, Asako Sato, Masayoshi Shirakawa, W. Joseph Herring, Ichiro Arano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDoes suvorexant, a sleep-promoting orexin receptor antagonist, reduce delirium in older adults at high risk for delirium after hospitalization?FindingsIn this randomized clinical trial of 203 participants, 17% in the suvorexant-treated group had delirium vs 26% in the placebo group; however, this difference was not statistically significant.MeaningFurther studies are needed to determine whether suvorexant may be useful for reducing delirium in older adults at high risk for delirium after hospitalization.

Introduction

Delirium is characterized by acute onset of disturbed consciousness or cognition that is transient and usually triggered by another medical condition, such as fever or alcohol withdrawal. According to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria, delirium is classified into 3 subtypes: hyperactive, hypoactive, and mixed.1 The hyperactive subtype corresponds to the common perception of delirium, with symptoms including agitation and hallucinations leading to the risk of impulsive and potentially harmful action. Hypoactive delirium symptoms include drowsiness or hypersomnia during the day as well as apathy and inappropriate responses, giving the impression of dementia or depressive disorder. Individuals with mixed delirium alternate between the hyperactive and hypoactive states.

Delirium is particularly common in older hospitalized adults.2,3 Preventing and decreasing delirium onset is an important medical issue, particularly for the hyperactive and mixed subtypes, because delirium presents immediate management issues, creates a burden on hospital staff, and results in increased health care costs.4 Hypoactive delirium may be overlooked because it does not interfere with treatment or lead to accidents or injuries and, therefore, does not present an immediate management issue. Although delirium itself is a transient condition, it can result in sustained impairment of cognitive function and contribute to institutionalization and mortality.5,6,7,8,9

Measures to prevent or reduce delirium include multimodal nonpharmacologic approaches that may be time-consuming or difficult to implement in a hospital setting and have limited efficacy.10 Insomnia is a facilitating factor of delirium; consequently, there has been interest in the potential use of sleep-promoting medications for delirium prevention. Benzodiazepine receptor agonist hypnotics, including nonbenzodiazepines such as zolpidem, are not recommended because of the possibility that they may exacerbate delirium.11 Non-GABAergic treatment approaches such as melatonin modulation12 and orexin receptor antagonism have been investigated. Efficacy of the orexin receptor antagonist, suvorexant, for delirium prevention is supported by results from observational studies13,14,15,16,17,18,19,20,21 and several small randomized clinical trials22,23 (eTable 1 in Supplement 2), as well as meta-analyses.24,25

This study aimed to evaluate suvorexant for delirium prevention in a large randomized clinical trial. Older adults at high risk for delirium were evaluated to maximize the occurrence of delirium.

Methods

This double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase 3 randomized clinical trial was conducted at 50 hospitals in Japan between October 22, 2020, and December 23, 2022. The trial was conducted in accordance with principles of good clinical practice and was approved by the institutional review boards of the participating institutions. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant (and their legally acceptable representative as needed). The trial protocol is provided in Supplement 1. The study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline.

Participants

Participants were Japanese men and women aged 65 to 90 years at high risk of delirium who were hospitalized for acute disease (infections, cerebral disorders, heart disorders, respiratory diseases, injuries, or neoplasms) or elective surgery requiring general anesthesia. High risk of delirium was defined as having mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia (as determined by the investigator who could refer to the results of the Nishimura Mental State Scale for the Elderly performed at screening), a history of delirium in any prior hospitalization, or both. Participants with acute disease had a severe disease state (Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II score of 8-3026), decreased daily living function (Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status score of 3 or 4),27 or both at screening. For participants who underwent elective surgery, surgery was scheduled to take place within 2 days after admission. Key exclusion criteria were as follows: development of delirium (assessed with DSM-5 criteria or a Delirium Rating Scale–Revised–98 [DRS-R-98]28,29 total score of ≥14.5) during the screening period, current use of psychotropic drugs (including hypnotics), history of drug or alcohol abuse, excessively severe physical disease for 2 weeks before randomization, history of epilepsy or Parkinson disease, or moderate or severe dementia.

Trial Design

The trial consisted of (1) a screening period of up to 14 days before admission for participants who underwent elective surgery and within 24 hours after admission for those with acute disease, (2) an assessment period of up to 8 days from the first dose of trial medication, and (3) a follow-up period of 14 days after the last dose. On the day of randomization, a qualified rater assessed the DSM-5 criteria and the DRS-R-98 scores of participants. Eligible participants were then randomized to nightly (bedtime) dosing with suvorexant or placebo (described next in “Randomization and Masking”). If the prerandomization assessment was performed more than 3 hours before the scheduled first dose of trial medication (suvorexant or placebo), the qualified rater reassessed participants to confirm that they did not have delirium according to DSM-5 criteria. Per protocol, randomized participants who developed delirium between randomization and first dose of trial medication were not to be administered trial medication; in practice, there were no such occurrences. The rater then repeated the DSM-5 and DRS-R-98 assessments daily (in the morning) during the treatment and assessment period. If the rater also noted symptoms suggestive of delirium at other time points, the DSM-5 assessment was performed at those times. If the participant developed delirium based on DSM-5 criteria, then the trial medication was discontinued. During hospitalization, the investigator evaluated the need for nonpharmacologic approaches to mitigate delirium and an appropriate nursing plan was implemented based on the individual’s condition (eg, this could include measures to address orientation, medical conditions such as dehydration and pain, and inactivity). Use of hypnotics other than trial medication, such as antipsychotics, antidepressants, mood stabilizers, anxiolytics, psychostimulants, and anticonvulsants, was prohibited throughout the assessment period. The use of these prohibited medications, including antipsychotics for treatment of delirium, was allowed if considered necessary by the investigator after completion, or after discontinuation of, trial medication.

Randomization and Masking

Participants were randomized (1:1) to either suvorexant (15-mg tablet) or a visually matching placebo tablet to be taken orally at night before bedtime for 5 to 7 days according to their hospitalization duration. Randomization was stratified according to reason for hospitalization (acute disease or elective surgery) and age category (<75 or ≥75 years). An interactive response system was used to randomly assign participants according to a computer-generated assignment schedule. Suvorexant and placebo were packaged identically and participants, investigators, and other personnel involved in the administration of the trial intervention or the clinical evaluation of participants were unaware of the intervention assignments.

Outcomes

Efficacy was assessed by trained and certified raters using DSM-5 criteria for delirium1 and the Japanese version of the DRS-R-98.28,29 As far as possible, the same raters performed the same evaluations for each participant during the trial. Assessments were performed at least daily and anytime delirium was suspected. Because delirium symptoms tend to fluctuate over the course of a day, DSM-5 criteria and DRS-R-98 scores were assessed on the basis of symptoms observed from the previous assessment until the current assessment. During the assessment period, the qualified rater reviewed the medical record, questioned the participant, and sought information from the participant’s family, site staff, or both as needed. Based on the totality of the available information, the rater assessed whether the participant had developed delirium according to DSM-5 criteria. For those who developed delirium, the subtype (based on DSM-5 criteria and referring to the subtype scale of Meagher et al30) and causes of delirium (using the etiology checklist31) were recorded. Participants completed the Richards-Campbell Sleep Questionnaire32 in regard to their previous night’s sleep once daily in the morning.

Safety and tolerability were assessed throughout the trial by monitoring participants for clinical adverse events. Vital sign assessments (blood pressure, pulse, and temperature) and laboratory safety tests (chemistry and hematology) were also performed routinely.

Statistical Analysis

Efficacy and Safety End Points

The statistical analysis plan is provided in Supplement 1. The primary efficacy end point was the proportion of participants with delirium during the assessment period, as assessed with DSM-5 criteria. The secondary efficacy end points were as follows: (1) delirium severity during the assessment period (DRS-R-98 score), defined as a maximum (ie, worst) total score among those obtained daily during the assessment period; and (2) proportion of participants with delirium during the assessment period assessed with DRS-R-98 scores, defined as the proportion of participants with a maximum total score of 14.5 or greater during the assessment period.

The modified intent-to-treat approach was used for the primary and secondary efficacy end points, in which treated participants with at least 1 assessment of delirium with DSM-5 criteria were included. For the primary end point, treatment differences in the proportion of participants with delirium during the assessment period (as assessed with DSM-5 criteria) are provided, along with corresponding 95% CIs and P values based on the Miettinen and Nurminen method.33 The analysis was stratified by hospitalization reason (acute disease or elective surgery) and age category (<75 or ≥75 years). Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel weight was used to obtain the stratum-adjusted proportion difference. Missing data were considered to indicate no delirium onset. A supportive analysis to assess the robustness of the primary results to missing data was performed using survival analytic methods, in which a missed assessment of delirium with DSM-5 criteria was censored at the previous nonmissing assessment. Kaplan-Meier plots of time to delirium were generated. Hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated based on a Cox proportional hazards regression model with the Efron method of tie handling with treatment as a covariate stratified by hospitalization reason and age category, and a 2-sided P value was calculated based on the log-rank test. A post hoc exploratory analysis of the proportion of participants with hyperactive plus mixed delirium subtypes (ie, delirium with a hyperactive component) was also performed using the same methods as for the primary end point. Delirium with a hyperactive component was considered of particular clinical importance because of the immediate management challenges it presents compared with delirium of the hypoactive subtype.

Regarding the secondary end points, the proportion of participants with delirium as assessed with DRS-R-98 scores was analyzed in the same manner as the primary end point. Delirium severity assessed with DRS-R-98 scores was compared between treatments using an aligned rank test,34 taking into consideration the potential nonnormality of the data.

All treated participants were included in the safety analyses. Safety and tolerability were assessed with clinical review of relevant parameters, including adverse events, laboratory test results, and vital signs. Certain adverse events (eg, cataplexy, suicidal ideation, and complex sleep-related behaviors) were predefined as events of clinical interest based on program-wide conventions for the suvorexant clinical development program.

Sample Size Calculation

With 200 planned randomized participants (100 in each treatment group), the trial had 90% power to demonstrate superiority of suvorexant to placebo in the percentage of participants with delirium, assuming a 2-sided α = .05 and a true treatment difference (placebo minus suvorexant) of approximately 18 percentage points (30.0% for placebo vs 12.0% for suvorexant). This calculation was based on previous research showing that delirium was observed at least in 10% to 20% of older adults hospitalized for surgery or internal diseases.2,3 Because this trial enrolled participants with a higher risk of delirium than the general older hospitalized population, it was anticipated that the incidence of delirium would be greater and, therefore, the true proportion of participants with delirium in the placebo group was assumed to be 30.0%. Previous studies that prospectively assessed suvorexant for delirium prevention22,23 found treatment differences of approximately 18 percentage points vs the control group; hence, a similar treatment effect was assumed for this trial (ie, 12.0% of participants with delirium in the suvorexant group).

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc). Data analysis was performed between January 23 and March 13, 2023.

Results

Participants

Of the 207 participants randomized in this trial, 203 received treatment. There were 101 participants treated with suvorexant (mean [SD] age, 81.5 [4.5]; years; 52 men [51.5%] and 49 women [48.5%]), and 102 received placebo (mean [SD] age, 82.0 [4.9] years; 45 men [44.1%] and 57 women [55.9%]). The trial flowchart is shown in Figure 1. A total of 23 participants (22.8%) in the suvorexant group and 31 (30.4%) in the placebo group discontinued treatment, with the most common reason for discontinuation being development of delirium (per protocol, those who developed delirium were discontinued from treatment). Participant characteristics were generally similar across treatment groups (Table 1). In the overall population, 106 participants (52.2%) were women, 185 (91.1%) were aged 75 years or older, 189 (93.1%) had mild cognitive impairment or dementia, and 38 (18.7%) had a prior history of delirium. The reason for hospitalization was elective surgery for 127 participants (62.6%) and acute disease for 76 (37.4%). The major categories of elective surgery were as follows: bone and joint or nervous system, skull, and spine (68 [53.5%]); hepatobiliary, gastrointestinal, or renal and urinary tract (31 [24.4%]); and respiratory tract or breast (15 [11.8%]). The major categories of acute disease were injuries (24 [31.6%]), cerebral disorders (18 [23.7%]), infections (17 [22.4%]), and heart disorders (15 [19.7%]). The mean duration of treatment was similar between the suvorexant (5.8 days) and placebo (5.5 days) groups, with a range of 1 to 7 days in both groups.

Efficacy

Efficacy findings are summarized in Table 2. For the primary end point of delirium as assessed with DSM-5 criteria, the observed percentage of delirium was lower in the suvorexant group than in the placebo group (17 of 101 [16.8%] vs 27 of 102 [26.5%]), but the suvorexant vs placebo difference was not statistically significant (−8.7% [95% CI, −20.1% to 2.6%]; P = .13). Suvorexant vs placebo differences were generally consistent across subgroups (eTable 2 in Supplement 2), except for some evidence of a stronger benefit of suvorexant in participants classified as having mild dementia (11 of 48 [22.9%] vs 18 of 40 [45.0%]; difference, −22.1% [95% CI, −40.8% to −2.2%]). During the study, 1 participant in the suvorexant group had 1 missed assessment of delirium with DSM-5 criteria. The Kaplan-Meier plot of time to delirium is shown in Figure 2. Event rates per 100 person-days were 2.5 for suvorexant and 4.1 for placebo (HR, 0.62 [95% CI, 0.34 to 1.15]; P = .13).

The results by delirium subtype (Table 2) suggested a lack of treatment effect for the hypoactive subtype (6 of 101 [5.9%] for suvorexant vs 5 of 102 [4.9%] for placebo) and a benefit for the hyperactive plus mixed subtypes (11 [10.9%] for suvorexant vs 22 [21.6%] for placebo). In the post hoc analysis of the hyperactive plus mixed subtypes, the suvorexant vs placebo difference was −10.7 percentage points (95% CI, −21.2% to −0.6%; P = .04). In the Kaplan-Meier analysis of time to hyperactive plus mixed delirium, the event rate per 100 person-days was 1.6 for suvorexant and 3.3 for placebo (HR, 0.48 [95% CI, 0.23 to 0.99]; P = .04). Further evaluation of the hypoactive delirium results suggested differential findings for those hospitalized for acute disease vs elective surgery. In participants hospitalized for acute disease, suvorexant directionally reduced hypoactive delirium (2 of 37 [5.4%] for suvorexant vs 5 of 39 [12.8%] for placebo); in those hospitalized for elective surgery, suvorexant directionally increased hypoactive delirium (4 of 64 [6.3%] for suvorexant vs 0 of 63 [0%] for placebo). The onset times of the hypoactive delirium cases after elective surgery were all in the morning (from 8:30 to 9:30 am).

Results for the secondary end points of delirium and delirium severity assessed with DRS-R-98 scores directionally favored suvorexant, but the differences from placebo were not statistically significant (Table 2).

Safety

Adverse events occurring up to 14 days after the last dose of trial medication are summarized in Table 3. One death (urosepsis) was reported during the follow-up period in the suvorexant group and was not considered drug related by the investigator. The incidences of overall adverse events, drug-related adverse events, serious adverse events, and discontinuations due to adverse events were generally similar between the suvorexant and placebo groups. With the exception of delirium (reported as an adverse event per protocol), the proportions of participants with the most frequently reported (≥10%) adverse events (constipation, vomiting, nausea, and insomnia) were generally similar between the suvorexant and placebo groups. The proportions of participants with events of clinical interest were low and generally similar between treatments; the following occurred more frequently in the suvorexant group than in the placebo group: fall (7 of 101 [6.9%] vs 3 of 102 [2.9%]), hypnagogic or hypnopompic hallucinations (3 [3.0%] vs 1 [1.0%]), and somnolence resulting in treatment discontinuation (1 [1.0%] vs 0 [0%]).

Discussion

In this study of suvorexant for prevention of delirium after hospitalization in older adults at high risk for delirium, the results directionally favored suvorexant (16.8% for suvorexant vs 26.5% placebo) but the difference was not statistically significant. In the post hoc exploratory analysis, delirium with a hyperactive component occurred in 10.9% of participants taking suvorexant vs 21.6% of those taking placebo, whereas no benefit was seen for hypoactive delirium (5.9% for suvorexant vs 4.9% for placebo).

The lack of a statistically significant benefit on the primary end point was unexpected, given the positive findings in previous observational studies and 2 smaller randomized clinical trials (eTable 1 in Supplement 2). However, those studies generally did not distinguish between different subtypes of delirium, apart from one study in which it was stated that hypoactive delirium was excluded due to its relative lack of importance in immediate postoperative management13 and another in which the primary outcome was stated to be delirium of any subtype.21 It is conceivable that the main focus of previous studies was on hyperactive or mixed delirium and that hypoactive delirium was overlooked.

Interestingly, further evaluation of the hypoactive delirium results from our trial suggested differential findings for individuals hospitalized for acute disease vs elective surgery, with the lack of benefit for suvorexant observed among those hospitalized for elective surgery. Although the numbers were small and the finding could be due to chance, suvorexant seemed to be associated with an increase in hypoactive delirium in the elective surgery group (4 for suvorexant vs 0 for placebo), and the onset times of these cases were all in the morning. Except for 1 participant who developed delirium before surgery, the other 3 participants developed delirium 1 to 2 days after surgery. Speculatively, the diagnosis of hypoactive delirium may have been due to a combination of the sustained somnolence effect of the previous night’s suvorexant and the residual effects of anesthetics and sedatives used for the surgery or the weakened condition of the participant after surgery. However, additional studies would be necessary to determine the validity of this speculation.

Another factor that may have contributed to the lack of a statistically significant finding on the primary end point is that the percentage of participants who developed delirium in the placebo group was somewhat lower than what was planned for in the trial design (26.5% observed vs 30.0% planned), and the treatment effect may have been reduced as a result. Even though high-risk participants were recruited, the risk of delirium may have been reduced by provisions in the trial design. Examples of design elements in the present trial that may not have been incorporated in previous studies include prohibition of concomitant use of hypnotics and other centrally acting medications, exclusion of participants unable to communicate or take study medication orally (or both), and implementation of nonpharmacologic approaches to mitigate delirium.

The safety profile of suvorexant in this trial was generally similar to placebo. Not surprisingly, given the setting (ie, hospitalized older adults), the overall incidence of participants with adverse events was high in both groups, but otherwise the overall safety profile of suvorexant was similar to that seen in previous studies of older adults with insomnia or older adults with Alzheimer disease and insomnia.35,36

Strengths and Limitations

This study has a number of strengths. First, it is—to our knowledge—the largest randomized clinical trial performed to date of a sleep-promoting agent for delirium prevention. Second, criterion standard DSM-5 criteria were used to diagnose delirium. Third, we collected potentially informative data on delirium subtypes.

This study also has several limitations. The trial was performed exclusively with participants at hospitals in Japan, so the generalizability of these findings to other populations and hospital settings in other countries is not clear. However, there is no reason to suppose that there would be major differences. The majority of older adults (189 [93.1%]) at high risk for delirium had mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia, which may also limit the generalizability of the findings. The analyses did not account for variables such as medical illness burden, type of surgery, and length of surgery, which may have affected the likelihood of delirium and potentially interacted with suvorexant efficacy. The dose evaluated was 15 mg, which is the maximum approved dose for older adults in Japan but is lower than the maximum dose of 20 mg approved in the US and some other countries. Finally, the analysis by delirium subtype was post hoc and exploratory in nature and should be regarded as hypothesis generating for planning future studies.

Conclusions

In this randomized clinical trial of older adults at high risk for delirium after hospitalization, fewer participants taking suvorexant had delirium compared with placebo but the difference was not statistically significant. Further studies are needed to determine whether suvorexant may be useful for reducing delirium—particularly delirium with a hyperactive component—in this population.
