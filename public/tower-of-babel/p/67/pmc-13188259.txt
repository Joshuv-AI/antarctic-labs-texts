Strengthening pharmacovigilance in emergency care: knowledge, attitudes and barriers to adverse drug reaction reporting among nurses in Ghana
Authors: Stella Sagoe, Isaac Nyarko Kwakye, Thywill Amenuveve Degley, Daniel Adom-Fynn, Adwoa Oforiwaa Antwi, Nuworza Kugbey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdverse drug reactions (ADRs) pose a significant challenge to patient safety, particularly in high-acuity settings such as emergency departments. Despite their critical role in pharmacovigilance, nurses often underreport ADRs due to systemic, knowledge-related, and attitudinal barriers. This study assessed nurses’ knowledge, attitudes and barriers toward ADR reporting in the Accident and Emergency (A&E) Unit of a Teaching Hospital in Ghana.MethodsA cross-sectional survey design was employed, with data collected from 136 nurses using structured questionnaires. Stratified random sampling was used to ensure representation from all three emergency sub-units. Multiple linear regression and parallel mediation analysis (PROCESS Macro, Model 4, bootstrap n = 5,000) were performed.ResultsResults indicated that 76.5% of nurses accurately classified adverse drug reactions (ADRs), and 88% identified antibiotic allergies; yet, only 32.3% were able to articulate institutional reporting protocols. Although 89% recognised the significance of ADR reporting, only 24% consistently recorded ADRs. Significant obstacles included insufficient time (78.6%), poor training (63.2%), fear of retribution (58.1%), convoluted forms (49.3%), and hierarchical conventions (44.1%). The intention to report emerged as the most significant predictor (B = 0.52, β = 0.538, p < .001), whereas attitudes (B = 0.314, p < .001) and perceived behavioural control (B = 0.352, p < .001) somewhat mediated the influence of knowledge (B = 0.198, p = .008) on ADR reporting.ConclusionAlthough the respondents possessed adequate knowledge of ADRs, this knowledge does not consistently translate into reported practices. Again, despite their favorable attitudes toward the value of ADR reporting, systemic and cultural barriers which included workflow constraints, fear of professional repercussions inhibited their active participation. Implementing context-specific training, introducing streamlined electronic reporting tools, establishing feedback mechanisms, and fostering leadership support is critical to enhancing ADR reporting. Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines adverse drug reactions (ADRs) as harmful and unintended effects of medicines that occur at normal therapeutic doses, clearly distinguishing them from medication errors and overdoses [1]. This definition emphasizes that ADRs can occur even when medicines are prescribed and used correctly. ADRs remain a major challenge to healthcare systems worldwide and contribute significantly to avoidable illness and death [2]. ADRs are often under-recognized despite their substantial impact on patient safety and health system performance. In developed countries, ADRs account for approximately 5% of all hospital admissions [3]. In the European Union alone, an estimated 197,000 deaths occur each year as a result of medication-related adverse events [4]. These figures highlight the seriousness of the problem and the need for effective systems to detect, report, and prevent medication-related harm. Despite advances in pharmacovigilance systems, ADR reporting remains consistently low across many healthcare settings. A systematic review by Hazell and Shakir [5], which analyzed 37 studies from both high- and low-income countries, found that only a small proportion of ADRs are reported through formal reporting systems. The review estimated that between 6% and 10% of all ADRs are documented, regardless of the level of health system development. This widespread underreporting limits the ability of healthcare institutions and regulatory authorities to detect early safety signals, monitor medicine risks, and implement timely interventions [1]. As a result, preventable harm continues to occur, and opportunities for learning and system improvement are lost.

The importance of robust ADR monitoring is well illustrated by historical events that shaped modern drug safety practices. The thalidomide tragedy of the late 1950s and early 1960s remains one of the most significant examples. During this period, thalidomide use in pregnant women led to severe congenital malformations in approximately 10,000 infants across 46 countries [6]. This disaster prompted major reforms in drug regulation and highlighted the need for continuous post-marketing surveillance. Since then, pharmacovigilance systems have expanded globally, and healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, have been increasingly recognized as essential contributors to ADR detection. However, major gaps in identification and reporting persist, especially in high-acuity clinical environments where medication use is frequent and complex. The problem of ADR underreporting is particularly pronounced in African healthcare systems. A systematic review by Ogunleye et al. [7], which included 42 studies from 23 African countries, reported ADR reporting rates ranging from 2% to 15%. These rates are substantially lower than global averages, despite increasing medicine use and disease burden across the continent. This situation persists even though many African countries have established national pharmacovigilance centres under the WHO-supported Pharmacovigilance Infrastructure and Post-marketing Surveillance System Development in Africa programme [8]. These findings suggest that structural, institutional, and behavioural barriers continue to undermine ADR reporting in routine clinical practice.

Ghana provides a useful case for examining these challenges. The country established a national pharmacovigilance system in 2001 through the Food and Drugs Authority, positioning Ghana as a leader in medicine safety monitoring in West Africa [9]. Although this system has recorded important successes, implementation challenges remain, particularly at the facility level. A Ghana Health Service report found that while most tertiary hospitals had ADR reporting forms available, many nurses lacked confidence in using them effectively [10]. These findings reveal a gap between national policy frameworks and frontline clinical practice. These challenges are concerning given Ghana’s changing health profile. The country faces a dual burden of infectious diseases and rising non-communicable diseases, leading to increased and prolonged medication use [11]. Expanded access to medicines through the National Health Insurance Scheme has further increased exposure to potential ADRs. However, reporting levels have not increased proportionately, suggesting that systemic barriers continue to limit effective pharmacovigilance.

Emergency nurses play a critical role in ADR detection because of their continuous patient contact and involvement in medication administration. Their position in emergency departments allows them to observe patient responses to medicines in real time and identify early signs of adverse reactions [12]. Despite this advantage, evidence shows that many nurses do not consistently document or report suspected ADRs. A Swedish multi-centre study found that although many emergency nurses could correctly define ADRs, only a minority routinely reported them [12]. Key barriers included heavy workloads, limited training, and uncertainty about reporting responsibilities. Evidence from the United Kingdom demonstrates that targeted interventions can improve ADR reporting among nurses. Lopez-Gonzalez et al. [13] showed that simplified reporting forms, brief training sessions, and regular feedback significantly increased nurse-reported ADRs in emergency departments. These findings suggest that practical, low-cost strategies can strengthen pharmacovigilance when they address real-world constraints.

In Ghana, emergency departments face additional pressures related to staffing shortages and high patient volumes. The operational environment of Korle-Bu’s A&E unit presents unique challenges that compound these issues. At Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, which manages over 75,000 emergency cases annually [14], nurses often prioritize immediate patient care over documentation. Studies in similar Ghanaian settings have shown that many nurses recognize ADRs verbally but fail to document them formally due to time constraints, fear of blame, and limited familiarity with reporting systems [10]. The combination of high patient acuity, frequent use of high-risk medications, and often incomplete medication histories creates conditions where ADRs are both more likely to occur and more likely to be missed or undocumented. Furthermore, the absence of streamlined reporting mechanisms integrated into routine clinical workflows means that nurses must often choose between responding to immediate patient needs and completing time-consuming ADR documentation processes. This dilemma is particularly acute in resource-constrained settings where staffing shortages and equipment limitations already stretch clinical teams to their capacity [1].

Against this background, the present study seeks to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and perceived barriers related to ADR reporting among nurses in the Accident and Emergency (A&E) unit of Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital. By identifying gaps in practice and system-level constraints, the study aims to generate evidence to inform targeted interventions that can strengthen medication safety. Improving nurse participation in pharmacovigilance will be essential for reducing preventable harm and enhancing patient safety in high-risk emergency care settings in Ghana and similar contexts.

Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) and the Knowledge–Attitude–Systems (KAS) Framework to explain nurses’ adverse drug reaction (ADR) reporting behaviour within the Accident and Emergency (A&E) Unit of Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital. The frameworks provide a comprehensive lens for understanding how individual cognitive factors, social influences, and organisational conditions jointly shape nurses’ intentions and actual practices related to ADR reporting. This approach is consistent with contemporary pharmacovigilance research, which emphasizes that ADR reporting is a multidimensional behaviour influenced by both personal and systemic factors [15, 16]. The Theory of Planned Behaviour explains that human behaviour is mainly determined by behavioural intention, which is influenced by attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control [17]. Attitudes reflect nurses’ beliefs about the professional value and consequences of reporting, where those viewing pharmacovigilance as core to patient safety demonstrate higher reporting rates [18]. Subjective norms capture the social and hierarchical pressures within clinical environments, with studies showing that reporting plummets when nurses perceive that colleagues or superiors discourage documentation [19]. Perceived behavioural control addresses nurses’ confidence in their ability to report given contextual constraints, even those with positive attitudes may not report if they believe they lack time or institutional support [20]. This theory has been widely applied to understand health professionals’ reporting behaviours, including ADR reporting. Studies have shown that nurses who perceive ADR reporting as beneficial to patient outcomes are more likely to intend to report suspected reactions [21]. Again, supportive leadership and a positive safety culture significantly enhance pharmacovigilance participation among nurses [15]. A meta-analysis by Hagger et al. [22] found that perceived control explained 38% of the variance in healthcare workers’ reporting behaviours across 27 studies, surpassing the influence of attitudes or norms. This highlights the theory’s relevance for emergency settings where time pressure and clinical acuity routinely undermine nurses’ sense of control over non-urgent tasks like ADR documentation.

The KAS Framework complements TPB by explicitly incorporating the role of knowledge and organisational systems in shaping health-related behaviours. The model posits that optimal reporting requires simultaneous alignment of three elements: adequate knowledge of ADR identification and protocols, positive attitudes toward reporting’s value, and supportive institutional systems [17]. Knowledge encompasses nurses’ understanding of ADRs, their ability to identify reportable reactions, familiarity with reporting procedures, and awareness of national pharmacovigilance guidelines. Attitudes relate to nurses’ beliefs, motivation, and emotional responses toward ADR reporting, including fear of blame or litigation and perceived professional value. Systems, often operationalized as barriers, include institutional and environmental factors such as inadequate training, lack of reporting forms, limited feedback, heavy workload, weak managerial support, and unclear reporting protocols. The framework recognizes that weaknesses in any component can collapse the entire reporting chain - a phenomenon observed in Ghanaian hospitals where nurses demonstrated good ADR knowledge but minimal reporting due to bureaucratic systems and punitive cultures [10]. The KAS Model’s predictive power was validated in a cluster-randomized trial by Kiguba et al. [23], where hospitals implementing interventions across all three domains (training, culture change, and digital tools) achieved 72% higher reporting rates than those addressing isolated factors. García-Abeijon et al. [24] and Geeven et al. [25] indicated that system-level barriers remain a major impediment to ADR reporting even when knowledge and attitudes are adequate.

Conceptual review

The integrated TPB–KAS framework illustrated in Fig. 1 indicates that ADR reporting among nurses is a multifaceted behaviour influenced by knowledge, attitudes, social pressures, perceived control, and structural factors. The concept is fundamentally based on knowledge, particularly ADR awareness and reporting protocols. Knowledge influences nurses’ attitudes by shaping their beliefs, motivation, and perceived value of reporting, while also enhancing perceived behavioural control through increased confidence and procedural competence. Favourable attitudes strengthen subjective norms, reflecting the expectations of peers and supervisors within the therapeutic setting. In accordance with the Theory of Planned Behaviour, attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control collectively influence nurses’ intention to report adverse drug reactions, which subsequently forecasts actual ADR reporting behaviour. Nevertheless, intention alone may not translate into action. The framework also incorporates organisational structures, including institutional support and reporting obstacles. These systems exert both direct and indirect impacts. Organisational systems augment knowledge and social impact through training and institutional support. Conversely, systemic obstacles such as excessive workload, absence of reporting tools, insufficient feedback, and time limitations directly undermine perceived behavioural control and may impede actual reporting behaviour, even when intentions are favourable.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted among nurses at the A&E unit of the Korle Bu Teaching Hospital, from February to May 2025. The design enabled the collection of data at a single point in time.

Study setting

This research was conducted in the Adult Emergency Department of Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, Ghana’s largest tertiary referral institution, which manages over than 75,000 acute cases each year. The unit comprises 206 nurses. The location was deliberately chosen for its significant pharmaceutical exposure, marked by the regular administration of antibiotics, analgesics, and cardiovascular medications, alongside a high-risk milieu for adverse drug reactions (ADRs). In these environments, expedited decision-making and polypharmacy heighten the probability of adverse medication reactions. Moreover, emergency rooms in resource-limited African settings are inadequately researched in pharmacovigilance, despite their essential function in ensuring pharmaceutical safety [9, 23]. The emergency unit at Korle-Bu exemplifies a pertinent case study, illustrating the workflow challenges prevalent in high-demand emergency care, including nursing shortages and elevated patient turnover, as well as the systematic deficiencies in ADR reporting noted in comparable hospitals within the region.

Sample size

The sample size for the study was determined using the formula proposed by Yamane [26]. A margin of error of 5% (0.05) was adopted, and a 95% confidence level was assumed. Based on these parameters, the final sample size for the study was 136 nurses.

Sampling procedure

This study employed a stratified random sampling technique to ensure representative participation from all key subgroups within the A&E nursing population. The nurses were stratified based on their assigned work areas (adult emergency, medical emergency, and surgical emergency units) to account for potential variations in medication administration practices and ADR exposure across different emergency care contexts. From each stratum, participants were then randomly selected using a computer-generated number system until the predetermined sample size of 136 nurses was achieved. To be selected for the study, participants must be a certified registered nurse actively working in the A&E department at the time of data collection and had at least six months of clinical experience. Nurses who were on leave during the data collection period, student nurses, and nurses with less than six months of work experience were excluded from the study.

Data collection instrument

This study used a self-developed structured questionnaire as the main instrument for data collection (a copy is attached as a supplementary file). The instrument was specifically designed to align with the study objectives and the local clinical context, as no single validated tool was found to comprehensively capture nurses’ knowledge, reporting practices, and context-specific barriers related to ADRs within the study setting. Existing validated instruments were reviewed; however, they were either developed for different healthcare systems or focused on limited aspects of pharmacovigilance, making them less suitable without substantial modification. Consequently, a context-adapted tool was developed to ensure relevance, clarity, and completeness of the variables under investigation. The first section collected basic demographic and professional information about the nurses, including age, sex, years of experience, and unit of practice. The second section assessed nurses’ knowledge of ADRs, covering identification, causes, and understanding of pharmacovigilance procedures. The third section focused on ADR reporting practices and behaviours, including frequency of reporting, documentation, and reporting channels used by nurses. The fourth section explored the challenges and barriers nurses encounter when reporting ADRs, such as workload, lack of training, and uncertainty about reporting procedures. The items were measured using a 4-point Likert scale (1 = irrelevant to 4 = highly relevant). The questionnaire was designed to facilitate easy completion and reliable data collection.

Pretesting was conducted among 20 nurses at the Greater Accra Regional Hospital. The necessary revisions were made based on the pilot results before the main data collection. Data was collected by the research team at the study site after obtaining the necessary administrative approvals and written informed consent from the participants. Questionnaires were distributed to eligible nurses during working hours. Participants were given adequate time (24 h) to complete the questionnaire, and completed questionnaires were collected at the agreed time. This procedure ensured a high response rate and the collection of complete and reliable data.

Validity and reliability of the instrument

The study utilised expert review to assess the content validity of the data collection instruments. A panel of three professionals thus, a pharmacovigilance expert, a nursing researcher, and a clinical pharmacologist assessed the questionnaire for relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness. They evaluated whether each item sufficiently assessed the desired constructs of knowledge, attitudes, and reporting procedures. Items with scores below 3.5 were either changed or eliminated. The Content Validity Index (CVI) for the final instruments was 0.88, above the required threshold of 0.80, so affirming that the tools effectively encapsulated the study’s essential concepts without superfluous or deceptive aspects. The internal consistency reliability was assessed via Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability values for the subscale were satisfactory: knowledge (α = 0.81), attitudes (α = 0.88), and ADR reporting conduct (α = 0.84), all surpassing the specified minimal level of 0.70.

Ethical approval

The study adhered to strict ethical guidelines by obtaining approval from the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital’s Research Office (KBTH-ADM/000311/2025). Participants were provided written informed consent after receiving clear explanations about the study’s purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing data (using codes) and securing responses in password-protected files.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the data. Frequencies and percentages summarized respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics. The same measures were applied to assess nurses’ knowledge of ADRs, attitudes toward ADR reporting, and perceived barriers. Knowledge was evaluated by calculating the proportion of correct responses to items on common ADRs and pharmacovigilance procedures. Attitudes were summarized using percentages to describe willingness, motivation, and confidence in reporting ADRs. Barriers to ADR reporting were analyzed using frequencies and percentages. Results were presented in tables and figures to improve clarity and interpretation. Multiple linear regression analysis was used to determine the predictors of ADR reporting behaviour. Mediation analysis was conducted using the PROCESS macro (Model 4) to examine whether attitudes and perceived behavioural control mediated the relationship between knowledge and ADR reporting behaviour. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Results and discussion

Results on demographic characteristics of respondents

The majority of the respondents were females (66.2%), while males constituted 33.8%. This gender distribution reflects the general trend in nursing practice in Ghana, where the profession is female-dominated. Most of the respondents were aged between 30 and 39 years (46.3%), followed by those between 20 and 29 years (39.7%). The distribution of nurses by work area shows that 35.3% were from the Adult Emergency Unit, while 32.4% were from the Medical Emergency Unit and 32.4% from the Surgical Emergency Unit. Regarding shift pattern, 36.8% of the nurses worked the morning shift, 32.4% worked the afternoon shift, and 30.9% worked the night shift. Nurses’ attitudes toward ADR reporting

The attitudes of nurses toward ADR reporting is presented in Table 3. Majority of the nurses (64.7%) indicated that they would be willing to report ADRs if the process were simplified. About 52.9% of the respondents stated that they felt motivated to report ADRs when they saw their reports lead to actual changes in patient care. Only 37.5% of the nurses reported feeling “very confident” in recognizing ADRs.

Barriers to ADR reporting among nurses

Barriers affecting ADR reporting among nurses are shown in Fig. 3. Lack of time was the most frequently cited challenge, with 78.6% of nurses indicating that time constraints hinder their ability to report ADRs. This is unsurprising in the fast-paced environment of an emergency department, where patient care demands are extremely high. Inadequate training affected 63.2% of respondents. Fear of blame was reported by 58.1%, reflecting a culture where error reporting may be associated with personal or professional consequences and the complex reporting forms impacted 49.3% of the nurses. Additionally, hierarchical structures, in which nurses must seek physician approval before reporting, were identified by 44.1%.

Work area, shift pattern, and intention to report ADRs as predictors of ADR reporting behaviour

A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to investigate the predictive relationship of work area, shift pattern, and intention to report ADRs on ADR reporting behaviour among nurses. The comprehensive model shown in Table 4 was statistically significant, F (5, 130) = 19.82, p < .001, accounting for 47.7% of the variation in ADR reporting behaviour. The intention to report adverse drug reactions (ADRs) emerged as the most robust predictor of ADR reporting behaviour (B = 0.52, β = 0.538, p < .001), signifying that nurses with heightened intentions were considerably more inclined to report ADRs. Employment in the Adult Emergency Unit was a notable predictor (B = 0.21, p = .038), indicating increased reporting behaviour relative to nurses in the Surgical Emergency Unit. Nurses on the Morning Shift exhibited much more ADR reporting behaviour compared to their Night Shift counterparts (B = 0.18, p = .046). Nonetheless, employment in the Medical Emergency Unit (p = .314) and the Afternoon Shift (p = .485) did not substantially forecast ADR reporting behaviour.

The relationship between nurses’ knowledge and ADR reporting behaviour is mediated by attitudes and perceived behavioural control

A parallel mediation analysis was performed to investigate whether attitudes and perceived behavioural control mediated the association between nurses’ knowledge and ADR reporting behaviour, with results reported in Tables 5 and 6. Knowledge was a significant predictor of ADR reporting behaviour in the overall effect model (B = 0.482, p < .001). Upon the introduction of attitudes and perceived behavioural control into the model, the direct effect of knowledge diminished however remained significant (B = 0.198, p = .008), suggesting partial mediation. Both attitudes (B = 0.314, p < .001) and perceived behavioural control (B = 0.352, p < .001) were significant predictors of ADR reporting behaviour. The indirect effects of knowledge on ADR reporting conduct via attitudes (B = 0.167, p < .001) and perceived behavioural control (B = 0.157, p < .001) were significant, hence validating mediation. The final model accounted for 47% of the variation in ADR reporting behaviour.

Discussion

The study examined nurses’ knowledge attitudes and barriers toward ADRs in the accident and emergency unit of Korle-Bu teaching hospital. While the study revealed a high level of knowledge about ADR definitions and common reactions, this knowledge did not consistently translate into practice. Thus, the high percentage of nurses who correctly identified the WHO definition of ADR is comparable to findings of a systematic review by Salehi et al. [18] which revealed that the overall, knowledge domain and percentage of nurses who had knowledge of the definitions of ADRs were 74.1%. The high percentage knowledge score of ADR in this current study could be attributed to the high educational level (Bachelor’s and postgraduate degrees) among respondents. This observation is similar to the study findings by Ahsan and Mallick [27]. However, the ability of Ghanaian nurses to identify antibiotic allergies and NSAID-induced gastric bleeding mirrors global trends. Abu Esba et al. [28], in a retrospective analysis of ADRs submitted by healthcare professionals in Saudi Arabia, noted that antibiotics and NSAIDs were among the most frequently implicated drug classes. Thus, the researchers concluded that nurses tend to have higher exposure to these reactions, which likely accounts for increased recognition. Moreover, the relatively lower recognition of opioid-induced respiratory depression highlights an area of concern. This gap may be attributed to lower training emphasis or less frequent use of opioids in some Ghanaian emergency contexts. Geitona et al. [29] observed a similar pattern in a tertiary hospital in the Republic of Cyprus, where 47.6% of ADRs were unreported due to poor understanding of less obvious pharmacodynamic effects. The low level of awareness regarding institutional ADR reporting procedures aligns with the findings of Adu-Gyamfi et al. [10]. Similar challenges were reported in a systematic review by Kunene et al. [30], which covered 35 studies across Sub-Saharan African countries. The review revealed underreporting strongly linked to inadequate knowledge of institutional processes and fear of professional reprisal. The Theory of Planned Behaviour [19] also helps explain these findings. Nurses may have a favourable attitude toward ADR reporting but perceive a lack of control due to unclear protocols, lack of feedback, and unsupportive infrastructure. Hagger et al. [22] in a meta-analysis of 27 studies, found that perceived behavioural control was the strongest predictor of healthcare professionals’ reporting behaviours – more so than knowledge or attitudes.

In terms of attitudes, majority of the nurses indicated that they would be willing to report ADRs if the process were simplified. This high percentage suggests that the complexity of current reporting systems may be a major deterrent. It also reveals a positive underlying attitude towards patient safety and accountability, which could be leveraged through system redesign. The positive willingness observed in this study corresponds with findings from Lopez-Gonzalez et al. [13], who conducted a controlled before-and-after study across 15 UK emergency departments. They found that simplified one-page reporting forms led to a 55% increase in nurse-reported ADRs. Highlighting the need to streamlining and simplifying reporting procedures in order to enhance greater participation and address complexity as a primary barrier. Again, a little over half of the nurses stated that they felt motivated to report ADRs when they saw their reports lead to actual changes in patient care. Their study demonstrated that visibility of impact reinforces intrinsic motivation and normalizes reporting culture. Additionally, findings from Adisa and Omitogun [31] in Nigeria indicated that monthly feedback bulletins resulted in sustained ADR reporting improvements. This indicates that feedback loops and outcome transparency can play a critical role in reinforcing reporting behaviours. Only 37.5% of the nurses reported feeling “very confident” in recognizing ADRs. This relatively low figure reveals a potential barrier to proactive reporting, as uncertainty in clinical judgment may lead to hesitation or non-reporting. It also underscores the need for continuous clinical training and support, particularly in high-acuity environments like the A&E unit.

The association between years of nursing experience and confidence in identifying ADRs is supported by the Theory of Planned Behaviour [17]. According to this theory, perceived behavioural control including confidence in one’s ability to perform a behaviour, strongly influences action. Nurses with more years in clinical settings likely have more exposure to ADRs and thus a higher perceived ability to identify and act upon them. The low confidence levels observed in this study also validate the Knowledge-Attitude-Systems (KAS) Framework, which posits that even when knowledge and attitudes are present, system barriers and lack of internal confidence (particularly among junior staff) can break the reporting chain. In the same vein, Hanafi et al. [32] revealed that nurses’ knowledge and attitude towards ADRs reporting had improved significantly after an educational programme on ADRs.

The findings of this study reveal a range of interconnected barriers that undermine effective ADR reporting among nurses, particularly within the demanding context of emergency care. Time constraints emerged as a dominant challenge, reflecting the realities of emergency departments where high patient acuity, rapid clinical decision-making, and staffing pressures prioritize immediate life-saving interventions over administrative tasks. While some studies from well-resourced settings suggest that experienced clinicians can integrate reporting into routine care, this apparent disagreement may be explained by differences in staffing levels, patient load, and the availability of electronic reporting systems, which are often limited in low- and middle-income contexts [4, 33]. Inadequate training also featured prominently, indicating persistent gaps in nurses’ preparedness to identify and report ADRs. Although pharmacovigilance is increasingly embedded in nursing curricula in some countries, inconsistencies in pre-service education and limited access to continuing professional development may explain why knowledge deficits remain prevalent in many settings [10, 18]. Where training is insufficient, ADR reporting may be perceived as complex or of secondary importance, contributing to disengagement. Fear of blame further constrained reporting practices, pointing to organizational cultures that emphasize individual fault rather than system learning. In contrast to settings with established just-culture frameworks, hierarchical healthcare environments may amplify concerns about professional repercussions, particularly when reporting requires physician endorsement [34, 35]. Such hierarchies can limit nurses’ autonomy and suppress safety advocacy, a challenge widely documented in sub-Saharan Africa [36]. Finally, complex reporting procedures were identified as a practical barrier. Although some health systems have streamlined ADR documentation, reliance on paper-based and poorly integrated systems in resource-constrained settings helps explain continued resistance to reporting [1, 37]. These findings emphasize the need for context-sensitive interventions that strengthen training, simplify reporting processes, and foster supportive, non-punitive leadership to enhance pharmacovigilance practice.

The discovery that the intention to report adverse drug reactions (ADRs) was the most significant predictor of ADR reporting conduct aligns with the TPB, which posits that intention is the primary determinant of action. Recent research indicates that intention is a key predictor of pharmacovigilance behaviours among nurses and other healthcare workers [21, 38]. The findings indicate that when nurses regard ADR reporting as significant and feel a personal commitment, they are more inclined to take action. Nonetheless, several studies indicate diminished intention–behaviour correlations, frequently ascribing this to organisational impediments such as time limitations, insufficient feedback, or intricate reporting mechanisms [16]. Such variances may indicate variations in institutional support and reporting infrastructure. The notable correlation between employment in the Adult Emergency Unit and elevated ADR reporting corresponds with existing data suggesting that engagement with intricate, high-acuity situations enhances alertness and the identification of medication-related incidents [39]. The non-significant effect noted for the Medical Emergency Unit may indicate variations in case mix, task distribution, or supervisory practices. Studies indicating no unit-based variations frequently cite standardised pharmacovigilance training and consistent reporting processes across departments, which may reduce contextual variability. Correspondingly, the observation that Morning Shift nurses documented a higher incidence of ADRs than Night Shift nurses is corroborated by research associating night shifts with exhaustion, less staffing, and restricted management supervision [40]. Nevertheless, the study by Rolfes et al. [41] indicated no variations in settings equipped with computerised, readily available reporting systems. Discrepancies in the literature may consequently arise from differences in workforce levels, digital infrastructure, and organisational culture. These findings underscore the necessity to bolster motivating elements that improve reporting intention, while simultaneously tackling structural impediments, especially during night shifts and in units with lower reporting rates, to guarantee uniform pharmacovigilance across all clinical settings.

Knowledge is a major predictor of ADR reporting behaviour, aligning with previous research indicating that nurses with a deeper comprehension of pharmacovigilance protocols are more inclined to report ADRs [38, 39]. The incorporation of attitudes and perceived behavioural control into the model diminished the direct influence of knowledge, signifying partial mediation. Both attitudes and perceived behavioural control strongly affected ADR reporting conduct, with notable indirect impacts of knowledge through attitudes and perceived behavioural control. The findings indicate that information alone does not adequately facilitate ADR reporting; the impact is enhanced when nurses possess positive attitudes and believe in their reporting capabilities, consistent with the Theory of Planned Behaviour and previous pharmacovigilance studies. Discrepancies in the literature may originate from variations in institutional setting, reporting culture, training, and systemic impediments. Some studies indicate that knowledge alone does not substantially predict reporting due to bureaucratic limitations or fear of retribution [16], whereas research in well-supported units demonstrates more pronounced direct impacts of knowledge. Contextual variables, like workload, supervision, and digital reporting systems, may explain these discrepancies. The results underscore the significance of therapies addressing both cognitive and motivational elements. Improving knowledge via training, cultivating positive attitudes, and bolstering nurses’ confidence and perceived control over reporting might together enhance ADR reporting rates. Moreover, institutional assistance to mitigate obstacles and optimise reporting procedures is essential for converting knowledge and intent into uniform practice.

Strengths of the study

This study makes several methodological and substantive contributions that distinguish it within the pharmacovigilance literature. This study is among the very few conducted in Ghana and sub-Saharan Africa to examine ADR reporting knowledge, attitudes, and barriers specifically within an emergency department setting. Emergency nursing represents a distinct and uniquely high-risk pharmacovigilance context that has been systematically underrepresented in African pharmacovigilance research [9, 23]. Again, the integration of both TPB and the KAS Framework provides a dual theoretical grounding that is rarely applied in pharmacovigilance studies, enabling a more comprehensive account of the individual, social, and systemic drivers of reporting behaviour. Additionally, the application of parallel mediation analysis using bootstrapped confidence intervals (PROCESS Macro, Model 4) adds methodological rigour beyond the descriptive and bivariate approaches that dominate Ghanaian nurse pharmacovigilance studies. Furthermore, the use of stratified random sampling, a piloted context-adapted instrument with satisfactory CVI (0.88) and Cronbach’s alpha values (0.81–0.88), and an adequate sample size (n = 136) ensures both representativeness and measurement credibility.

Recommendation

Improving ADR reporting requires coordinated interventions at the individual, institutional, and system levels. Continuous professional education should be strengthened through regular in-service training and refresher programmes. These activities should focus on recognizing both common and less obvious ADRs, including opioid-induced respiratory depression. Simulation exercises and structured case reviews within the A&E unit may enhance clinical judgement and build confidence, especially among less experienced nurses. ADR reporting systems should also be simplified and digitized. The introduction of short, user-friendly, and electronic reporting tools that align with routine clinical workflows would reduce the perceived reporting burden and encourage consistent participation. In addition, awareness of institutional reporting protocols must be improved. Clear guidelines should be widely disseminated and incorporated into orientation programmes for newly recruited staff. Furthermore, hospital leadership should promote a non-punitive and supportive reporting culture. Framing ADR reporting as a patient safety and learning activity, rather than a fault-finding process, may increase nurses’ willingness to report. Regular feedback on reported ADRs should be provided. Demonstrating how reports lead to improvements in patient care can strengthen motivation and sustain reporting behaviours.

Conclusion

This study concludes that while nurses in the A&E unit of Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital possess generally good knowledge of adverse drug reactions and express positive attitudes toward reporting, substantial system-level and behavioural barriers continue to limit effective pharmacovigilance practice. Gaps in confidence, limited awareness of reporting procedures, time constraints, fear of blame, and complex documentation processes collectively undermine reporting behaviour. Improving perceived behavioural control through training, simplified systems, supportive leadership, and visible feedback mechanisms is critical. Addressing these interrelated factors is essential for strengthening ADR reporting and enhancing patient safety within high-acuity healthcare settings.

Limitation

The cross-sectional design limits causal inferences between knowledge, attitudes, barriers, and ADR reporting behaviour. Data were self-reported and may therefore be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially leading to overestimation of knowledge or positive attitudes. The study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital and focused on the A&E unit, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical departments or healthcare facilities in Ghana. Additionally, actual ADR reporting practices were not objectively verified through institutional records. Self-reported data is susceptible to social desirability and recall bias, likely overestimating knowledge and positive attitudes. Additionally, actual ADR reporting practices were not objectively verified through institutional pharmacovigilance records. The instrument did not differentiate between ADR severity categories (mild, moderate, severe) or ADR types (Type A/B), which may influence reporting thresholds and likelihood; future studies should stratify analyses by ADR type and severity. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable context-specific insights into barriers and opportunities for improving pharmacovigilance among nurses in emergency care settings.
