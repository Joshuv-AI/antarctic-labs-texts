A Nutrient‐Responsive LuxR Regulator Orchestrates Effector Gene Expression Across the Legionella Genus
Authors: Chen Adler, Gil Segal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Legionella pneumophila utilizes the Icm/Dot secretion system to translocate > 330 effectors into host cells, yet the regulatory mechanisms controlling the expression of many effector‐encoding genes (EEGs) remain unknown. Here, we identify and characterize LexR1 (lpg2524), a LuxR‐type transcriptional regulator that controls EEG expression in L. pneumophila and other Legionella species. In L. pneumophila , LexR1 directly activates the expression of four EEGs (lem26, lem16, ravW, and legC1) by binding a conserved inverted repeat located upstream of these genes. We demonstrate that LexR1 activity is stimulated by a nutrient‐derived ligand present in casamino acids, and orthologs from other Legionella species exhibit similar casamino acid dependent activation of their target genes. Genomic analyses identified 29 genes containing the LexR1 regulatory element in the seven Legionella species containing LexR1, most of which encode validated effectors or proteins harboring common effector domains. Many of these genes are shared among the species and appear to have co‐transferred with their regulatory regions via horizontal gene transfer. LexR1 expression is positively regulated by RpoS and repressed by Fis, linking it to the broader effector regulatory network. Together, these findings identify LexR1 as a regulator of EEGs that coordinates nutrient sensing with effector gene expression across the Legionella genus.

Introduction

The intracellular human pathogen Legionella pneumophila utilizes the Icm/Dot type‐IVb secretion system during infection to deliver effector proteins into host cells (Isaac and Isberg 2014; Omotade and Roy 2019). This secretion system is essential for intracellular replication in both protozoa, the natural environmental host of L. pneumophila , and human macrophages (Segal and Shuman 1998, 1999; Vogel et al. 1998). During infection, L. pneumophila utilize translocated Icm/Dot effectors to generate a specialized compartment termed the Legionella‐containing vacuole (LCV) (Tilney et al. 2001; Boamah et al. 2017; Steiner et al. 2018; Swart et al. 2018). In addition to promoting LCV formation, these effectors manipulate diverse host cellular pathways, such as autophagy, signal transduction, translation, transcription, and lipid metabolism (reviewed in (Isaac and Isberg 2014, Sherwood and Roy 2016, Omotade and Roy 2019, Mondino et al. 2020, Lockwood et al. 2022, Romanov and O'Connor 2024)). Both LCV biogenesis and host manipulation rely on an exceptionally large repertoire of effector‐encoding genes (EEGs): approximately 330 in L. pneumophila and likely thousands across the Legionella genus (Burstein et al. 2016; Gomez‐Valero et al. 2019). The enormous number of effectors participating in these processes implies that their expression and translocation are tightly coordinated to ensure successful infection. This coordination is likely achieved at multiple levels, one of which is at the gene expression level.

Studies investigating the regulation of EEGs in L. pneumophila have revealed an intricate and interconnected regulatory network comprising numerous transcriptional regulators that orchestrate the expression of EEGs. These regulators can be classified into two main groups based on their conservation and functional properties.

The first group includes the Legionella core regulators (LCRs), which are conserved across all studied Legionella species. These regulators are typically encoded within conserved operons and control the expression of numerous EEGs, as well as additional (non‐effector) genes. To date, the LCRs comprise: (i) The CpxRA two‐component system (TCS), which directly activates or represses 26 EEGs and four icm/dot genes (Gal‐Mor and Segal 2003; Altman and Segal 2008; Feldheim et al. 2016; Tanner et al. 2016). (ii) The PmrAB TCS, which directly activates 42 EEGs (Zusman et al. 2007; Al‐Khodor et al. 2009). (iii) The stationary‐phase‐associated LetAS‐RsmYZ‐CsrA cascade, which consists of the LetAS TCS, the small RNAs RsmY and RsmZ, and the translational regulator CsrA, and inhibits translation of over 40 EEGs (Hammer et al. 2002; Lynch et al. 2003; Molofsky and Swanson 2003; Forsbach‐Birk et al. 2004; Rasis and Segal 2009; Sahr et al. 2009, 2017; Nevo et al. 2014). (iv) Two Fis‐family regulators that repress ~30 EEGs (Zusman et al. 2014; Linsky et al. 2020; Linsky and Segal 2021; Shapira et al. 2024). These Fis proteins preferentially target EEGs located in genomic regions with reduced GC content, consistent with the silencing of genes that were recently acquired horizontally (Burstein et al. 2009; Degtyar et al. 2009). Finally, the Fur regulator also contributes to EEG regulation; however, unlike the systems described above, it controls only a single effector alongside several genes involved in iron acquisition (Hickey and Cianciotto 1997; Robey and Cianciotto 2002; Chatfield et al. 2012; Isaac et al. 2015). In addition, the ArgR regulator was shown to affect effector gene expression indirectly (Hovel‐Miner et al. 2010).

The second group of regulators consists of the Legionella auxiliary regulators (LARs), which are present only in a subset of Legionella species. These regulators are distributed across diverse genomic loci and appear to have been horizontally acquired, frequently together with their associated EEG targets. The LARs can be further subdivided into two types. The first type includes regulators encoded within “regulator‐effector islands” adjacent to their target EEGs. The genomic organization of genes in these islands is conserved among species carrying them, and regulation is restricted to genes within this island. This type of LARs includes: (i) The copper‐responsive LciRS TCS, which regulates a single EEG (Linsky et al. 2020) and (ii) RegK3, a LuxR‐family regulator controlling two EEGs (Linsky and Segal 2021). The second type of LARs includes regulators that typically control both adjacent and distant EEGs. In these cases, both the local and distal targets vary among species carrying the regulator. This group currently includes three LysR‐type transcriptional regulators (LTTRs): (i) LelA, regulating three EEGs (Shapira et al. 2024), (ii) LelB, also controlling three EEGs (Adler and Segal 2025), and (iii) LelC, which regulates a single EEG that is positioned adjacent in several Legionella species but located distantly in L. pneumophila (Adler and Segal 2025). In addition, the Legionella quorum‐sensing (Lqs)‐LvbR network was shown to affect effector gene expression indirectly (Michaelis et al. 2024).

Together, LCRs and LARs constitute a highly interconnected regulatory network. For example, PmrA controls the expression of the post‐transcriptional regulator csrA (Rasis and Segal 2009), while Fis repressors inhibit the expression of the LARs lciRS and regK3 (Linsky et al. 2020; Linsky and Segal 2021). In addition, the sigma factor RpoS regulates both LCRs and LARs, controlling the expression of rsmY and rsmZ (Sahr et al. 2017; Hovel‐Miner et al. 2009) as well as lelA, lelB, and lelC (Shapira et al. 2024; Adler and Segal 2025). Moreover, additional components, including LetE, LerC, and the nitrogen phosphotransferase system (PTSNtr), modulate LCR activity (Bachman and Swanson 2004; Feldheim et al. 2016, 2018; Speiser et al. 2017).

LuxR‐type regulators participate in the regulation of pathogenesis‐related gene expression in many bacteria (Chen and Xie 2011; Mallery and Paczkowski 2026). The L. pneumophila genome contains five members of the LuxR‐type regulator family, harboring a C‐terminal LuxR helix‐turn‐helix domain. Among these is the well‐characterized LetA regulator described above, which is the only L. pneumophila LuxR regulator containing an N‐terminal receiver domain. The other four LuxR regulators, RegK3 (lpg2557), lpg2524, lpg1946, and lpg1448, have an N‐terminal sensory domain. In RegK3 and lpg1946, the sensory domain harbors a cytoplasmic PAS signal‐sensing domain, whereas lpg2524 and lpg1448 lack recognizable N‐terminal domains.

We hypothesized that additional LuxR‐family regulators in L. pneumophila may function as LARs of EEGs. We identified a LuxR regulator (lpg2524), which is positioned adjacent to an EEG, and found that it has orthologs in multiple Legionella species, located, in most cases, near putative EEGs. Furthermore, bioinformatic analyses indicated that the regulatory element recognized by lpg2524 is conserved in additional EEGs and candidate EEGs in most species harboring this regulator. Experimental results demonstrated that lpg2524 directly regulates EEG expression in L. pneumophila . Under standard growth conditions, its regulatory activity was minimal; however, supplementation with casamino acids strongly induced the expression of four EEGs containing the corresponding regulatory element. Collectively, these findings support the classification of lpg2524 as a casamino acid‐activated LAR that contributes, alongside other LCRs and LARs, to the coordinated regulation of effector gene expression in L. pneumophila .

Results

The Distribution of lpg2524 LuxR Orthologs in the Legionella Genus

Previous studies have shown that several L. pneumophila EEGs controlled by LTTR and LuxR‐type transcriptional regulators are positioned adjacent to their corresponding target genes (Linsky et al. 2020; Linsky and Segal 2021; Shapira et al. 2024; Adler and Segal 2025). Motivated by this genomic organization, we searched for additional regulatory systems of this type and identified the LuxR‐family regulator gene lpg2524 together with its neighboring EEG, lem26 (lpg2523) (Burstein et al. 2009), which are co‐localized in the L. pneumophila genome. To further characterize lpg2524 orthologs and their potential regulatory targets, we examined all fully sequenced Legionella species for closely related homologs. Using thresholds of ≥ 60% amino acid identity, ≥ 75% similarity, and BLAST e‐values ≤ 1e‐132, orthologs were detected in seven Legionella species (Figure 1A). Notably, two distinct paralogs were identified in L. cincinnatiensis and L. sainthelensi . Analysis of genomic regions upstream of genes located adjacent to the lpg2524 orthologs revealed the presence of a conserved putative regulatory element in all cases. This element was consistently located at a similar distance upstream of the predicted −10 promoter (Figure 1). In L. cincinnatiensis and L. feeleii , two genes harboring this motif were identified adjacent to the regulator gene, whereas three such genes were found in L. cincinnatiensis (adjacent to the second ortholog) and L. sainthelensi (Figure 1A). The conserved regulatory sequence comprises an inverted repeat positioned at a fixed distance relative to a putative −10 promoter, a feature typical of LuxR‐type binding sites (Figure 1B). Based on these observations, together with the experimental evidence presented below, we designate lpg2524 as LexR1, for Legionella EEGs (effector‐encoding genes) LuxR regulator.

The LexR1 Orthologs Undergo Horizontal Gene Transfer Within the Legionella Genus

LexR1 orthologs are present in only seven out of the 65 characterized Legionella species sequenced, and no close homologs are found outside the genus. Their patchy distribution within the Legionella genus does not follow the species phylogeny, suggesting possible horizontal gene transfer (HGT). To test this, we reconstructed a phylogenetic tree of the nine LexR1 orthologs and compared it with the Legionella species tree derived from the seven Legionella genomes containing these orthologs (Figure S1A). While the species tree followed the established Legionella phylogeny (Burstein et al. 2016; Gomez‐Valero et al. 2019), the LexR1 tree displayed a markedly different topology (Figure S1A). Notably, the two LexR1 paralogs in L. cincinnatiensis and L. sainthelensi were found in distinct clades of the LexR1 tree, despite being present within the same genome, further supporting the HGT hypothesis. Moreover, the GC content of the lexR1 genes was consistently lower than the average genomic GC content in all examined species (Figure S1B), corroborating the hypothesis of recent HGT events (Ochman et al. 2000; Zhang et al. 2014). Furthermore, in four Legionella species ( L. cincinnatiensis , L. sainthelensi , L. saoudiensis, and L. gratiana ), the lexR1 encoding gene is located adjacent to the same two genes, one of which harbors the putative LexR1 regulatory element, indicating that the entire gene cluster may be co‐transferred during HGT (Figure 1 and Figure S2A). In three of these Legionella species ( L. cincinnatiensis , L. sainthelensi , and L. gratiana ), comparison with closely related Legionella species revealed a homologous genomic locus lacking these three genes (lexR1, its adjacent target gene, and the intervening gene) (Figure S2B–D), providing further evidence that this triplet may be transferred as a single HGT unit. Collectively, the gene distribution, phylogenetic differences, distinct GC content, and co‐localization as a genomic island support the conclusion that the lexR1 locus has undergone multiple HGT events within the Legionella genus.

Examination of the Expression of the L. pneumophila LexR1 Predicted Target EEGs

The identification of the putative inverted repeat regulatory element described above (Figure 1B) enabled a genome‐wide search for similar sequences in L. pneumophila . This analysis revealed four L. pneumophila EEGs (lem26, lem16, ravW, and legC1) that contain a highly similar putative regulatory element (Figure 2A). To investigate whether LexR1 regulates these EEGs, a lexR1 deletion mutant was generated. Examination of this mutant in Acanthamoeba castellanii demonstrated that LexR1 is not required for intracellular replication (Figure S3), consistent with previous observations in other host systems (Li and Faucher 2017; Park et al. 2020). Next, we compared the expression levels of the four EEG lacZ fusions in the L. pneumophila wild‐type strain and in the lexR1 deletion mutant, but no significant differences were detected (Figure 2B). The generally low expression levels of these EEGs suggested possible repression by the Fis regulators, as previously reported for several horizontally transferred EEGs (Zusman et al. 2014; Linsky et al. 2020; Linsky and Segal 2021; Adler and Segal 2025). To test this possibility, we introduced mutations into all predicted Fis‐regulatory elements overlapping the putative LexR1 regulatory region and the −10 promoter, generating “Fis‐less” lacZ fusions (Figure 2A). All the Fis‐less fusions showed higher levels of expression in comparison to the wild‐type fusions (Figure 2B), indicating that they are indeed repressed by Fis; however, no effect by LexR1 was detected even with these mutated fusions. These results prompted us to employ an inducible expression system to further evaluate LexR1‐mediated regulation of the four EEGs. In this system, the lexR1 gene was cloned under the IPTG‐inducible Ptac promoter and introduced into the lexR1 deletion mutant, together with either the four wild‐type EEG‐lacZ fusions (Figure 2C) or the four Fis‐less lacZ fusions (Figure 2D). Upon induction with IPTG, a modest two‐ to three‐fold activation was observed for two of the wild‐type fusions and for all Fis‐less constructs at the highest inducer concentration, correlating with elevated LexR1 expression (Figure 2C,D). This activation level is considerably lower than that typically observed for other LuxR‐type regulators (Rasis and Segal 2009; Linsky and Segal 2021).

Casamino Acids Activates the L. pneumophila LexR1

The consistently low activation levels observed for all four LexR1 predicted target EEGs, even when Fis repression was relieved (Figure 2D), suggested that under the tested conditions (AYE medium), LexR1 might not be active. In an attempt to activate LexR1, we supplemented the AYE medium with various growth supplements, based on the observation that L. pneumophila primarily utilizes amino acids and small peptides as carbon and energy sources during intracellular growth (Chen et al. 2008; Kunze et al. 2021). Accordingly, AYE was supplemented with proteose peptone, peptone, tryptone, casitone, or casamino acids (all 1%). To screen these compounds, we employed the LexR1 overexpression system described above, testing two predicted target EEGs at the maximal IPTG concentration that induces LexR1 expression. The results were striking: while most supplements caused only minor changes in EEGs expression, addition of casamino acids led to a dramatic increase in expression of both EEGs (Figure 3A), suggesting that casamino acids contain an activating compound that stimulates LexR1 activity. To further substantiate these findings, we tested a gradient of casamino acids concentrations to assess their effect on the expression of the two EEGs under the same experimental conditions. The results showed that increasing casamino acids concentrations led to a proportional, linear increase in expression of both EEGs (Figure 3B).

Casamino acids is an acid hydrolysate of casein and does not constitute a defined nutrient source. Casamino acids (Bacto—catalog #223050) contain about 63% total amino acids (which include free amino acids, di and tri‐peptides), while the remainder consists of undefined compounds originating from casein co‐purification and acid hydrolysis products. In an attempt to identify the activating ligand, we added to the AYE medium the most common components of casamino acids: a mixture of the 16 amino acids present in casamino acids at their corresponding concentrations, but no activation was observed (Figure S4). To examine whether di‐ or tri‐peptides serve as the activating ligand, we used NZ‐Amine, an enzymatic digest of casein that contains free amino acids and di‐ and tri‐peptides in proportions similar to those in casamino acids, but no activation was observed. We also tested a mixture of trace metals known to be present in casamino acids, but again, no activation was observed. In addition, another lot of Bacto casamino acids gave a similar result (Figure S4), indicating that the activating ligand is a consistent component of casamino acids.

Together, the strong activation by casamino acids and the concentration‐dependent linear response demonstrate that a component present in casamino acids acts as a bona fide inducer or activator, directly modulating LexR1 activity in proportion to its concentration (Alon 2007; Nevozhay et al. 2009).

LexR1 Regulates the Expression of Its Four Predicted Target EEGs

The identification of growth conditions (AYE medium supplemented with 1% casamino acids) that activate LexR1 enabled examination of how the lexR1 deletion or overexpression affects the expression of its four predicted target EEGs. We used the eight lacZ fusions (four wild‐type and four Fis‐less) and examined them in the lexR1 deletion mutant in comparison to the wild‐type strain (Figure 4A), as well as in the LexR1 overexpression system (Figure 4B,C). Under the inducing growth conditions, the lexR1 deletion mutant still showed no significant change in the expression of the wild‐type EEG‐lacZ fusions, and only modest effects were observed for two of the Fis‐less lacZ fusions, likely reflecting low genomic expression of lexR1 under these conditions (see below). However, LexR1 overexpression caused a dramatic increase in expression of all four EEGs (Figure 4B,C). A gradual, IPTG‐dependent increase in expression indicated that both casamino acids and elevated LexR1 levels are required to achieve maximal activation of the four predicted target EEGs. For instance, lem26 showed minimal expression (about 3 MU) in AYE under Fis repression (Figure 2C), compared to very high expression levels (~14,000 MU) in AYE medium containing casamino acids when Fis repression was relieved (Figure 4C). Comparable activation was observed for all four EEGs. Therefore, we conclude that LexR1 activates the expression of these four L. pneumophila EEGs, suggesting that it might function as their direct positive regulator.

LexR1 Activation Requires a Conserved Regulatory Element Located Upstream of Its Target EEGs

To examine the connection between the LexR1 regulator and the conserved regulatory element we identified upstream of its putative target genes, we introduced two‐point mutations into the consensus sequences identified in each of the four examined EEGs (lem26, lem16, ravW, and legC1) (Figure 5A). The mutant lacZ fusions were cloned into the inducible LexR1 expression system described above and tested in the lexR1 deletion mutant under inducing conditions (AYE medium supplemented with 1% casamino acids). The results showed that these mutations completely abolished LexR1‐dependent activation of the target EEGs (Figure 5B). To further assess the specificity of LexR1 activation, three control EEGs (legK3, legL7, and legA9) were examined, and no activation by LexR1 was observed in AYE medium containing casamino acids at any IPTG concentration (Figure S5). Collectively, these results indicate that the conserved upstream regulatory element plays a critical role in LexR1‐dependent activation of lem26, lem16, ravW, and legC1.

LexR1 Directly Binds to the Regulatory Region of the Effectors It Regulates

The full‐length LexR1 regulator consistently formed inclusion bodies and could not be purified in soluble form. We thus assess DNA‐binding activity using the isolated C‐terminal helix‐turn‐helix (HTH) domain of LexR1 (LexR1C), which contains the predicted DNA‐binding motif of the regulator and is therefore suitable for assessing sequence‐specific DNA interactions in vitro. The L. pneumophila LexR1C protein was His‐tagged, overexpressed, purified (Figure S6), and used for gel mobility shift assays with the regulatory regions of lem26, lem16, ravW, and legC1. The L. pneumophila His6‐LexR1C protein bound to the regulatory region of its target genes, evidenced by a mobility shift of the DNA probes (Figure 6). The amounts of shifted probes positively correlated with increasing amounts of His6‐LexR1C protein (Figure 6A–D, lanes 2–5). Competition with an unlabeled probe reduced the band shift, indicating specific binding (Figure 6A–D, compare lanes 5 and 6). To further assess binding specificity, competition assays were performed using an unlabeled probe containing the mutated regulatory regions described above (Figure 5, Mut‐2), which did not reduce the band shift (Figure 6A–D, compare lanes 6 and 7). Collectively, the mobility shift assays (Figure 6), together with the LexR1 overexpression analysis (Figure 4) and mutational analysis of the LexR1 consensus sequences (Figure 5), establish that LexR1 directly activates lem26, lem16, ravW, and legC1 in L. pneumophila .

The LexR1 Expression Is Regulated by the RpoS Sigma Factor and Fis

We were interested in exploring how the lexR1 gene itself is being regulated. Examination of the regulatory regions of the lexR1 orthologs from different Legionella species revealed putative Fis regulatory elements, as well as predicted extended −10 promoter sequences closely matching the consensus recognized by RpoS (Figure 7A). The RpoS sigma factor was previously shown to act as a direct regulator of three regulators of EEGs (lelA, lelB, and lelC), whereas Fis was found to repress three others (lciR, regK3, and lelB), linking these regulators to the broader effectors regulatory network (Linsky et al. 2020; Linsky and Segal 2021; Shapira et al. 2024; Adler and Segal 2025). Analysis of lexR1 expression revealed that it is expressed at low levels (Figure 7B). However, mutation of its putative Fis regulatory element resulted in a marked increase in expression (Figure 7C), indicating that lexR1 is indeed repressed by Fis. To test whether the RpoS sigma factor regulates lexR1, we examined lexR1 expression in a rpoS deletion mutant and observed a significant reduction in expression for both the wild‐type and Fis‐less lacZ fusions (Figure 7B,C). These results are consistent with previous whole genome transcriptomic analyses comparing wild‐type L. pneumophila and an rpoS mutant (Hovel‐Miner et al. 2009). Furthermore, RpoS overexpression led to increased expression of both the wild‐type and Fis‐less lexR1 lacZ fusions (Figure 7B,C), confirming that RpoS positively regulates lexR1 expression. Collectively, our results show that LexR1 is integrated into the effector regulatory network of L. pneumophila through RpoS and Fis.

Distribution and Properties of the LexR1 Putative Target Genes in the Legionella Genus

The validation of the conserved target regulatory element recognized by LexR1 (Figure 5) enabled us to search for this element in the genomic sequences of the seven Legionella species harboring the regulator. This analysis revealed that the regulatory element recognized by LexR1 is present in the upstream regulatory regions of 29 genes (Figure S7). The number of putative target genes varies between species. In two Legionella species (L. saoudiensis and L. gratiana ), the element was identified only upstream of the gene located adjacent to the regulator‐encoding gene, whereas in five Legionella species ( L. pneumophila , L. cincinnatiensis , L. sainthelensi , L. massiliensis , and L. feeleii ), two to eight genes contained the LexR1 regulatory element (Figure 8). Besides the four validated L. pneumophila EEGs (lem26, lem16, ravW, and legC1), and one validated EEG (lem6) found adjacent to the lexR1 orthologs in L. cincinnatiensis and L. sainthelensi (Figure 1), the putative regulatory element was also found upstream of genes encoding proteins with common effector domains (Cazalet et al. 2004; Burstein et al. 2016; Patel et al. 2025), such as ankyrin, coiled‐coil, methyltransferase, glycosyltransferase, and protein tyrosine kinase domains (Figure 8 and Dataset S1). Overall, 23 of the 29 genes (79%) containing the LexR1 regulatory element encode validated effectors or proteins harboring common effector domains. The remaining genes containing the LexR1 regulatory element encode hypothetical proteins unique to the Legionella genus and lack detectable homology to known proteins or conserved domains (Figure 8 and Dataset S1), suggesting that they may represent previously unidentified effectors (Burstein et al. 2009). Collectively, these observations indicate that LexR1‐regulated genes predominantly encode effectors or proteins containing effector domains.

Genes Containing the LexR1 Regulatory Element Are Shared Among Different Legionella Species

Examination of the five Legionella species containing more than one putative target gene revealed that these genes are often located in close proximity and within genomic regions that also contain additional EEGs (Figure S8). In L. cincinnatiensis and L. sainthelensi , two LexR1 paralogs are present, and four putative target genes were found near one of the paralogs, with two additional targets located near the second paralog in L. cincinnatiensis (Figure S8). In L. pneumophila , two (lpg1312 and lpg1317) of the four target genes were also located adjacent to one another. In L. feeleii , all eight putative target genes are located within a single genomic region alongside the regulator‐encoding gene (Figure S8).

A notable feature of LexR1 putative target genes is their strong overlap among Legionella species containing the regulator. This overlap includes: (i) A gene encoding a coiled‐coil‐containing protein which is found in four species ( L. cincinnatiensis , L. sainthelensi , L. saoudiensis, and L. gratiana ) and is consistently located adjacent to the regulator (Figure 8). In two of these species (L. saoudiensis and L. gratiana ), this is the only LexR1 target gene identified. (ii) lem26 was found in three species ( L. pneumophila, L. cincinnatiensis and L. sainthelensi ), but it is located adjacent to the regulator only in L. pneumophila . (iii) Six additional genes occur in different species pairs. For example, ravW occurs in L. pneumophila and L. massiliensis ; legC1 occurs in L. pneumophila and L. sainthelensi ; and a gene encoding a protein with no known domain is present in L. massiliensis and L. sainthelensi , while three additional pairs occur in L. sainthelensi and L. cincinnatiensis . In two of these gene pairs, one gene is located adjacent to the regulator in one species but distal in the other (Figure 8). L. feeleii represents an exception, as it contains a distinct set of putative target genes compared to the other species (Figure 8).

As described above, the lexR1 locus has undergone multiple HGT events within the Legionella genus (Figure S1). Therefore, given the large overlap between LexR1 putative target gene sets, we examined whether their regulatory regions cluster according to the species in which they are found (indicating duplication of the regulatory region) or according to the genes they regulate (suggesting that HGT events involving the target genes also included their regulatory regions). A phylogenetic tree was generated using the regulatory regions of the 29 genes containing the LexR1 regulatory regions (Figure S9, based on the sequences shown in Figure S7), and it clearly indicated that aside from the LexR1 putative target genes found in L. feeleii , the regulatory regions in all other species cluster according to the genes they regulate (Figure S9). Importantly, the L. feeleii target genes also do not overlap with the target genes found in the six other species harboring LexR1 (Figure 8). This clustering pattern suggests that the regulatory regions were transferred together with their associated genes and subsequently fixed in Legionella species harboring the LexR1 regulator.

Collectively, the genus‐wide distribution of the putative LexR1‐regulated genes shows substantial overlap across Legionella species, likely resulting from HGT events involving both the regulator and its target genes.

The LexR1 Orthologs From Other Legionella Species Are Also Activated by Casamino Acids

To experimentally examine if LexR1 orthologs described above also activate gene expression in a casamino acids dependent manner, we chose three LexR1 orthologs: two from L. cincinnatiensis , representing two of the most common genomic organizations of lexR1 (Figure 1A), and one from L. feeleii , which is predicted to regulate a set of genes different from the other species. The evolutionary distance of these orthologs (together with the L. pneumophila ortholog) provides representative coverage of the LexR1 phylogeny across the genus (Figure 1A). The three lexR1 orthologs selected have a putative target gene containing the LexR1 regulatory element located adjacent to them (Figure 1A), and each regulator was examined for activation of its adjacent gene (Figure 9A). LacZ fusions were constructed for these genes and their expression was examined using the controlled expression system in the L. pneumophila lexR1 deletion mutant. The system was examined in the absence or presence of IPTG (which controls LexR1 ortholog expression) and with different concentrations of casamino acids (0%, 0.5%, and 1%). The results obtained demonstrated that activation of the target genes by the LexR1 orthologs is clearly dependent on casamino acids (Figure 9B). Similarly to what we observed for L. pneumophila (compare Figure 2C and Figure 9B), the three LexR1 orthologs remained almost inactive in the absence of casamino acids even when expressed (1 mM IPTG, no casamino acids), whereas inclusion of casamino acids resulted in strong activation of the three orthologs, with target gene expression increasing proportionally with casamino acid concentration (Figure 9B). The LexR1 orthologs showed activation levels similar to those observed for the L. pneumophila LexR1 on its target genes (compare Figure 4B and Figure 9B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that all LexR1 orthologs examined are almost inactive under standard Legionella growth conditions and are activated by the same ligand present in casamino acids, suggesting that this ligand is commonly present in LCVs formed by these Legionella species.

Discussion

Legionella pneumophila translocates more than 330 effectors into host cells, more than any known bacterium (Isaac and Isberg 2014; Sherwood and Roy 2016; Omotade and Roy 2019; Mondino et al. 2020; Lockwood et al. 2022), and thousands of effectors are estimated to be encoded across the Legionella genus (Burstein et al. 2016; Gomez‐Valero et al. 2019). The study of effector gene regulation has so far identified five Legionella core regulators (LCRs) present in all Legionella species examined. These include four regulators (PmrAB, CpxRA, LetAS‐RsmYZ‐CsrA, and Fis) which control the expression of a large number of EEGs in L. pneumophila and probably also in other Legionella species, and Fur which controls a single effector along with several genes involved in iron acquisition (Figure 10). Another type of regulators are the Legionella auxiliary regulators (LARs), which are present only in a subset of Legionella species and include LexR1 described here (Figure 10). LexR1 is the sixth LAR identified and characterized in the Legionella genus, and shared features of this regulatory group are gradually emerging. The six LARs control the expression of EEGs in L. pneumophila , and in other Legionella species they regulate, or are predicted to regulate, genes encoding proteins that often contain common effector domains. These six regulators are sporadically distributed across the Legionella genus, being present in only five to twelve species, and they were probably acquired by HGT ((Linsky et al. 2020; Linsky and Segal 2021; Shapira et al. 2024; Adler and Segal 2025) and this study). Although absent from most Legionella species, three LARs (LelA, LelB, and LexR1) occur as two paralogs in some species. The LARs belong to three families of regulators: TCS (LciRS), LTTRs (LelA, LelB and LelC) and LuxR‐type regulators (RegK3 and LexR1). The six LARs strongly activate the expression of their target EEGs, and many of these EEGs were shown to be repressed by Fis, suggesting that some of them may also have been acquired by HGT. Two of the LARs (LciRS and RegK3) are located in genomic islands together with their target EEGs, whereas in the other four LARs (LelA, LelB, LelC, and LexR1) the regulator gene is typically adjacent to one target EEG but also controls additional EEGs located elsewhere in the genome. Two of the LAR genes are themselves regulated at the transcriptional level by the RpoS sigma factor (lelA and lelC), two by the Fis repressors (lciR and regK3), and the last two are regulated by both RpoS and Fis (lelB and lexR1) ((Linsky et al. 2020; Linsky and Segal 2021; Shapira et al. 2024; Adler and Segal 2025) and this study). Thus, RpoS and Fis connect the LARs to the EEGs regulatory network, which is integrated with the LCRs (Figure 10).

All six LARs harbor a sensory domain that likely binds a ligand. The LciRS TCS is the only system in which the sensory domain and the DNA‐binding helix‐turn‐helix (HTH) domain are separated, and the LciS periplasmic sensory domain was shown to be activated by copper (Linsky et al. 2020). The five other LARs harbor a cytoplasmic sensory domain, and their activating ligands are not known. However, four of them (LelA, LelB, LelC, and RegK3) were found to be active under standard Legionella growth conditions, that is, in AYE medium. However, LexR1 was almost inactive under these conditions. Addition of casamino acids (CA) to AYE resulted in strong activation of its target genes, a response that was completely dependent on LexR1 (Figure 4) and its target regulatory element (Figure 5). These observations suggest that LexR1 senses a nutrient‐derived ligand that does not activate the other LARs or LCRs.

Although our results establish that casamino acids activate LexR1, the identity of the activating signal remains unknown. The inability of a mixture containing the 16 standard proteinogenic amino acids to reproduce LexR1 activation suggests that the activating signal is unlikely to be one of these amino acids alone. Instead, it may correspond to a minor constituent of the casamino acid preparation, such as a modified amino acid, or another hydrolysis‐derived compound. Alternatively, LexR1 activation may occur indirectly through metabolic changes triggered by the addition of casamino acids rather than through direct ligand binding. Although the linear induction observed following casamino acid addition is consistent with direct sensing, the current data do not distinguish between these possibilities.

Nine LexR1 orthologs were identified across seven Legionella species. All four LexR1 orthologs examined experimentally were found to require the presence of casamino acids for activation (Figure 4 and Figure 9). This conservation in ligand sensing suggests that the same or a similar ligand is also present within the Legionella‐containing vacuole (LCV), thereby activating LexR1 during infection. The absence of an intracellular growth defect in the lexR1 deletion mutant (Figure S3) does not necessarily contradict this hypothesis. In L. pneumophila , LexR1 regulates the expression of only four EEGs, and previous studies have shown that deletion of more than 50 effectors does not result in a detectable intracellular growth phenotype (O'Connor et al. 2011). This apparent lack of phenotype is likely due to extensive functional redundancy within the large repertoire of Legionella effectors (O'Connor et al. 2012).

A bioinformatic analysis identified 29 genes harboring the LexR1 regulatory element across the seven Legionella species that contain the regulator. All four genes identified in L. pneumophila encode effectors, and most of the genes identified (79%) in the other Legionella species encode effectors or proteins harboring common effector domains. The putative target genes identified are shared between the species, probably indicating multiple HGT events (Figure 8). In four of the Legionella species ( L. pneumophila , L. sainthelensi , L. cincinnatiensis , and L. feeleii ), a subset of target genes is clustered together (Figure S8). For three of these Legionella species ( L. pneumophila , L. sainthelensi , and L. feeleii ), several isolates have been completely sequenced, and comparison of genomic regions containing lexR1 orthologs and their target genes among these isolates revealed extensive variability (Figures S10–S12). For example, in L. pneumophila Philadelphia‐1 (used in this study), a single lexR1 ortholog and four target genes are present; however, in strain Paris lexR1 is absent, and only one target gene is retained with its regulatory element. In strain Lens only a degraded remnant of lexR1 is present, and none of the target genes remain (Figure S10). In L. sainthelensi NCTC12450 two paralogs of the regulator are found, but in other isolates (NCTC11988 and LA01‐117), only one of these two paralogs is present, and notably, not the same one in both isolates, and the number of target genes also varies (Figure S11). In two isolates of L. feeleii (NCTC12022 and CDPH‐009), the lexR1 encoding gene is located in the same genomic region together with all of its target genes, several of which are pseudogenized or their regulatory elements disrupted by insertion sequences (Figure S12A). In the L. feeleii isolate NCTC11978, the entire genomic region containing lexR1 and its target genes is absent (Figure S12B). These observations indicate that, in addition to the HGT events introducing lexR1 and its target genes into the genus, both the regulator and its target genes continue to experience substantial genomic instability after acquisition.

In summary, LexR1 is a newly identified regulator of EEGs in L. pneumophila and other Legionella species. It is uniquely activated by a nutrient‐derived ligand that is not required by any other LCR or LAR. LexR1, like other LARs, is integrated into the L. pneumophila EEGs regulatory network through the RpoS sigma factor and Fis. Both LexR1 and its target genes undergo extensive HGT across the genus, and the regulated gene sets show substantial overlap among species harboring the regulator. The finding that LexR1 orchestrates nutrient sensing and effectors gene expression sheds new light on the ways by which Legionella species link their intracellular environment to effectors gene expression.

Experimental Procedures

Bacterial Strains, Plasmids, and Primers

The L. pneumophila wild‐type strain used in this study was JR32, a restriction‐negative, streptomycin‐resistant derivative of L. pneumophila Philadelphia‐1 that retains wild‐type intracellular growth properties (Sadosky et al. 1993). Mutant strains derived from JR32, as well as the L. cincinnatiensis , and L. feeleii species used in this study are listed in Dataset S2. The E. coli strains used in this work are also listed in Dataset S2. Plasmids and primers used in this work are listed in Datasets S3 and S4, respectively.

Plasmid Construction

To construct lacZ translational fusions (Dataset S3), the 300‐bp putative regulatory regions of the L. pneumophila lem26, lem16, legC1, lexR1, L. cincinnatiensis DYH34_RS09810 and DYH34_RS10205, and L. feeleii DT338_RS00495 genes were amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Dataset S4. The PCR products were then digested with BamHI and EcoRI (or BamHI and XmaI for DYH34_RS09810), cloned into pGS‐lac‐02, and sequenced. The resulting plasmids are listed in Dataset S1.

Site‐directed mutagenesis was performed by regular PCR or the PCR overlap extension approach (Ho et al. 1989), as previously described (Zusman et al. 2007), to construct the following substitution mutations: (i) a substitution mutation (GTC → TGA and GAC → TCA) in the putative LexR1 regulatory element in the regulatory region of the lem26, lem16, legC1 and ravW, genes. (ii) a substitution mutation in the putative Fis binding site in the regulatory region of the lexR1, legC1 and ravW, genes; (iii) a double substitution mutation of the two putative Fis sites in the regulatory region of the lem16, and (iv) a triple substitution mutation of the three putative Fis sites in the regulatory region of the lem26 gene (resulting in mutations of all predicted Fis‐regulatory elements overlapping the putative LexR1 regulatory region and the −10 promoter, yielding the ‘Fis‐less’ lacZ fusions). The primers used for the site‐directed mutagenesis are listed in Dataset S4, and the resulting plasmids are listed in Dataset S3.

To construct the IPTG‐inducible lexR1 (lpg2524), the L. pneumophila lexR1 gene was amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Dataset S4. The PCR product was then digested with EcoRI and BamHI and cloned into pMMB207C to generate pSY‐207‐Ptac‐R4. The insert of this plasmid was sequenced and then digested with BamHI, and a Kanamycin resistance cassette (Pharmacia) was cloned into it to generate pCA‐pMMB207c‐Ptac‐lpg2524‐Km. The resulting plasmid was then digested with XbaI or SphI and NsbI, and the resulting fragment, containing P tac ‐lexR1 together with the lacI gene, was cloned into the plasmids containing the lacZ fusions of the lem26, lem16, legC1, ravW, legK3, legL7, and legA9 genes digested with XmnI and XbaI or SphI. This fragment was also cloned into the corresponding plasmids carrying mutations in the LexR1 regulatory element or into the ‘Fis‐less’ lacZ fusion plasmids of these four genes, resulting in the plasmids listed in Dataset S3.

To construct IPTG‐inducible L. cincinnatiensis DYH34_RS09815 and DYH34_RS10215, and L. feeleii DT338_RS00500, the three genes were amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Dataset S4. The PCR product was then digested with EcoRI and BamHI and cloned into pMMB207C to generate pCA‐pMMB207c‐Ptac‐Lcin_9815, pCA‐pMMB207c‐Ptac‐Lcin_10215, and pCA‐pMMB207c‐Ptac‐Lfee_500, and the insert of these plasmids was sequenced. The resulting plasmid was then digested with SphI and NsbI, and the resulting fragments, containing P tac ‐DYH34_RS09815, P tac ‐DYH34_RS10215, or P tac ‐ DT338_RS00500 together with the lacI gene, were cloned into the plasmids containing the lacZ fusion of the L. cincinnatiensis DYH34_RS09810, DYH34_RS10205, and L. feeleii DT338_RS00495 genes, respectively, digested with XmnI and SphI, resulting in the four plasmids listed in Dataset S3.

In addition, the L. pneumophila RpoS sigma factor (lpg1284), cloned under the P tac control in pDT‐pMMB‐Ptac‐rpoS (Shapira et al. 2024), was digested with XbaI and NsbI, and the resulting fragment, containing the P tac ‐RpoS region together with the lacI gene, was cloned into the plasmids containing the lacZ fusions of lexR1 and Fis‐less lexR1 digested with XmnI and XbaI to generate the plasmid listed in Dataset S3.

To construct deletion‐substitution mutants in the L. pneumophila lexR1 gene, a 1‐kb DNA fragment located on each side of the planned deletions was amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Dataset S4, and cloned into pUC18 digested with the suitable enzymes. The resulting plasmids were digested with suitable enzymes, and the inserts were used for a four‐way ligation containing the Kanamycin resistance cassette. The resulting plasmid pSY‐R4‐Km (Dataset S3) was digested with PvuII, and the resulting fragments were cloned into the pLAW344 allelic‐exchange vector digested with EcoRV to generate the plasmid pSY‐R4‐GR (Dataset S3). The allelic‐exchange deletion‐substitution mutants were constructed as previously described (Segal and Shuman 1997). The resulting deletion‐substitution mutants were examined for intracellular growth in A. castellanii as previously described (Hauslein et al. 2016).

For the construction of the plasmid expressing the His6‐LexR1C, the DNA‐binding HTH domain of the regulator (residues 174–287) was used. The HTH domain of the lexR1 gene was amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Dataset S4. The resulting fragments were cloned into pET‐15b and sequenced to generate the plasmid listed in Dataset S3.

β‐Galactosidase Assay

β‐Galactosidase assays were performed as previously described (Zusman et al. 2007). L. pneumophila strains were grown for 48 h on charcoal‐yeast extract (CYE) plates containing chloramphenicol (Cm). The bacteria were scraped off the plate and suspended in ACES‐yeast extract (AYE) broth, and the bacterial OD600 was calibrated to 0.1 in fresh AYE or AYE containing the supplements indicated, containing different concentrations of IPTG (when indicated) and Cm. The resulting cultures were grown on a roller drum for about 18 h, until reaching an OD600 of about 3.2 (early stationary phase) and used for the β‐galactosidase assay. The assays were done for 20, 50, or 100 μL of culture, and the substrate for β‐galactosidase hydrolysis was o‐nitrophenyl‐β‐D‐galactopyranoside.

Protein Purification and Gel Mobility Shift Assay

His6‐LexR1C was purified from E. coli BL21(DE3) using nickel bead columns (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer's instructions. After purification, fractions containing His6‐LexR1C were dialyzed overnight against buffer containing 10 mM Tris (pH −7.5), 50 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 30% glycerol followed by dialysis for several hours against the same buffer containing 50% glycerol. The purified protein was then stored at −20°C. Gel mobility shift assay was performed as previously described (Zusman et al. 2014), with minor modifications. The regulatory region of lem26, lem16, ravW, and legC1 (161 bp, 177 bp, 162 bp, and 170 bp, respectively) were amplified by PCR using the primers listed in Dataset S3 and 3′ end‐labeled with digoxigenin (DIG) by using DIG‐11‐ddUTP (Roche). For His6‐LexR1C binding reactions, increasing amounts of the purified His6‐LexR1C protein (between 2.5 and 56 nM) were incubated with 1 nM DIG‐labeled probe in binding buffer containing 10 mM Tris (pH −7.5), 50 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM DTT, 250 μg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 50 μg/mL herring sperm DNA. For competition assays, a 100‐fold excess of unlabeled probe or the corresponding probe containing mutations in the regulatory region was pre‐incubated with His6‐LexR1C protein for 15 min prior to the addition of the DIG‐labeled probe. Binding reactions were carried out for 30 min at room temperature, after which samples were resolved on a 6% polyacrylamide gel prepared in 0.5× Tris‐boric acid‐EDTA (TBE) gel in 0.5× TBE running buffer. Following electrophoresis, DNA‐protein complexes were transferred to a nylon membrane and fixed by UV cross‐linking. DIG‐labeled DNA fragments were detected using the Amersham ImageQuant‐800 imaging system according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Identification of the LexR1 Target Genes

To search for the LexR1 regulatory element across seven Legionella species containing the regulator, the 300 bp upstream regulatory regions of all the annotated genes in these genomic sequences were collected using the corresponding NCBI RefSeq GCF file of each genome. The resulting sequences were searched using the Regulatory Sequence Analysis Tools (RSAT) (Santana‐Garcia et al. 2022) using the “Pattern matching‐DNA‐pattern” option. The consensus sequence presented in Figure 1B, was used for the search, allowing a single substitution. The results obtained were manually curated to generate the final list of genes harboring the regulatory element.

Reconstruction of Phylogenetic Trees

Trees were reconstructed based on alignments produced using MAFFT (Katoh and Standley 2013) of the proteins indicated for each tree. The trees were reconstructed using RAxML (Stamatakis 2014) under the LG + GAMMA evolutionary model with 100 bootstrap resampling.
