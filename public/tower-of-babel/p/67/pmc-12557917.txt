Impact of a multifaceted continuing education program on occupational stress and professional capabilities in dental hygienists: a longitudinal study
Authors: Yuko Kurawaki, Reiko Aimi, Keiko Misumi, Atsue Matsumoto, Mizuki Mitsui, Mayuka Asaeda, Rumi Nishimura, Toshinobu Takemoto, Mariko Naito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs preventive dentistry gains increasing importance in dental care, the role of dental hygienists has expanded. Alongside this growth, occupational stress has emerged as a significant challenge. We aimed to longitudinally evaluate the impact of a training program on the occupational stress levels of dental hygienists.MethodsA total of 141 dental hygienists participated in a training program between September 2019 and March 2021. Among them, 53 currently employed individuals were included in the analysis. Assessments were conducted at four time points: before training, immediately after completing the training, 3 months post-training, and 1 year post-training. Evaluations included self-assessment of knowledge and skill levels, as well as the Brief Job Stress Questionnaire.ResultsSignificant improvements in levels of “knowledge” and “technical skills” were observed immediately after training and maintained for up to 3 months. These improvements were also associated with increased “job control” and decreased “anxiety” levels. However, at the 1-year follow-up, increases were noted in “anger-irritability,” “fatigue,” “anxiety,” and “depression.” Among participants who completed clinical practice training, “fatigue” and “anxiety” decreased immediately after training. However, “interpersonal conflict” increased after 1 year.ConclusionsWhile the training program effectively reduced occupational stress in the short term, maintaining its long-term effects proved challenging. Addressing occupational stress among dental hygienists requires improvements in working conditions, continuous professional development, and the establishment of regular training and follow-up systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08077-8.

Background

As preventive dentistry has become an increasingly vital aspect of dental healthcare [1], the role of dental hygienists has expanded beyond conventional supportive functions to encompass independent professional responsibilities, including direct patient education, community health promotion, and specialized preventive interventions. This transformation has increased both the professional expectations and accountability placed on dental hygienists. Consequently, occupational stress among dental hygienists has emerged as a significant issue, with multiple contributing factors identified from various perspectives.

Occupational stress, defined as the psychological and physiological tension experienced when job demands exceed an individual’s coping capacity, represents a critical concern in healthcare professions.

Research has revealed that occupational stress among dental hygienists results from multiple interacting factors. Physical factors include musculoskeletal pain (neck, back, shoulder, wrist, etc.) [2, 3], which has been identified as a cause of job turnover [4]. Psychological factors include the significant burden of emotional labor, which is associated with increased burnout risk and decreased job satisfaction [2–4]. Organizational factors include inadequate compensation systems, job instability, lack of organizational support [5–7], and difficulties in maintaining work-life balance [6]. Additionally, less experienced dental hygienists face stress from the workplace environment, interpersonal conflicts [8], and insufficient professional knowledge and skills [5]. Common reasons for leaving the profession include “pregnancy/childcare,” “relationships with management,” and “personal health” [9–11], all of which are closely related to occupational stress factors. Evidence suggests that occupational stress directly impacts professional performance, with stressed dental hygienists showing decreased technical proficiency and quality of care and poor patient communication [12]. Furthermore, chronic occupational stress has been associated with higher absenteeism rates [12], increased medical errors, and lower patient satisfaction scores [13]. These stressors contribute to higher turnover rates and decreased job satisfaction [14–16], which can negatively affect the quality of dental care.

In response, the importance of educational systems for dental hygienists has been emphasized. Completing advanced specialized programs can expand career opportunities beyond traditional clinical areas and improve job satisfaction [17]. Moreover, continuous postgraduate professional education has been shown to reduce occupational stress, burnout, and workplace dissatisfaction [18].

However, improving educational systems alone may not suffice. Workplace environmental improvements, particularly revising compensation systems, are essential to maintaining work motivation and reducing turnover rates [12]. Full-time workers seek workload optimization, clinical duty improvements, better compensation, and enhanced interpersonal relationships, while part-time workers prioritize better working conditions [6]. Furthermore, preventing early career departure requires follow-up support for knowledge and skill deficiencies, while career continuation depends on improving job satisfaction, establishing employment support systems, and clarifying career paths [18].

Despite the importance of these factors, there is insufficient evidence of the impact of training programs on occupational stress. Research on how postgraduate training affects dental hygienists' knowledge, technical skills, and psychological well-being is limited. Reducing occupational stress is thought to enhance job satisfaction and long-term employment retention, which could ultimately enhance oral healthcare management in an aging society.

We aimed to conduct a longitudinal evaluation of dental hygienists' professional capabilities and occupational stress. Specifically, we investigated the impact of training programs on professional capabilities and occupational stress at four time points: before training, immediately after completion, 3 months after completion, and 1 year after completion. Through this evaluation, we aimed to assess the effectiveness of training programs from multiple perspectives and gain insights into improving career support and workplace environments for dental hygienists.

Methods

Training program and data collection

This study included all participants who attended the re-employment support and new employee training program at the Dental Hygienist Education and Training Center, Hiroshima University Faculty of Dentistry, between September 2019 and March 2021. The training program was conducted at Hiroshima University School of Dentistry and Hiroshima University Hospital. All instructors were experienced dental hygienists (> 10 years of clinical experience) who had completed certified training programs for clinical supervisors and practical instructors. The program is subsidized by Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, making it essentially free for participants with occasional requests to cover partial material costs. The curriculum comprised 52 courses divided into four major categories: Knowledge Acquisition Program (18 courses) aimed at strengthening and updating basic dental knowledge; Basic Technical Simulation Program (8 courses) focusing on improving clinical skills such as scaling and root planing using mannequins; Clinical Training Program (10 courses) designed to enhance clinical skills through mutual practice among participants in areas such as intraoral photography and home care visits; and Clinical Practice Program (16 courses) involving observations and practical training at university hospitals, dental clinics, and older individual care facilities. Each course lasted 90 min, and participants were allowed to freely select courses while consulting with the center faculty about desired skills and areas for improvement. Multiple session dates were offered for each course module to accommodate participants’ work schedules, enabling compatibility with ongoing employment.

Clinical practicum components are conducted at university-affiliated hospitals, general dental practices, and elderly care facilities, where participants receive direct guidance from practicing dentists and dental hygienists. Participants were recruited through the distribution of promotional flyers to dental hygienists, dental associations, and announcements on social media platforms. Enrollment in the program occurred via multiple channels, including responses to promotional materials, referrals from employing dentists, and recommendations from previous program graduates. The program incorporated peer support and professional networking opportunities through group discussions and collaborative learning activities, fostering lasting professional connections among participants. These peer support networks naturally develop as participants study in the same classroom environment over the 3-month training period, with ongoing support provided through social gatherings for graduates and continuous career counseling available both during and after the training period.

Before the training began, the purpose and objectives of the study were explained to the participants. Those who provided consent completed self-administered questionnaires at four time points: before training, immediately after completion, 3 months post-completion, and 1-year post-completion. Questionnaires for the first three time points were sent via mail to participants' addresses, while the 1-year follow-up survey was conducted online using Microsoft 365 Forms sent to participants' email addresses.

The self-administered questionnaire included all components of the standardized Brief Job Stress Questionnaire (BJSQ) [19, 20], with the addition of original items developed specifically for this study, which collected data on sex, age, employment status, workplace, years of experience as a dental hygienist, educational background, and self-evaluation scores for “knowledge,” “technical skills,” “motivation,” and “growth mindset.” The scores were calculated relative to a target of 10 points at the start of training initiation, with higher scores indicating higher self-evaluations. The detailed original questionnaire items are listed in Additional File 1. The BJSQ was used to assess occupational stress. This 57-item questionnaire was developed by the Japan Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare’s “Research Group on Mental Health Measures through Workplace Environment Improvement,” and it includes three components: stress-causing factors, stress-induced physical and mental responses, and other factors affecting stress responses. The stress-causing factors comprised 17 items across nine scales: “quantitative job overload,” “qualitative job overload,” “physical demands,” “interpersonal conflict,” “poor physical environment,” “job control,” “skill utilization,” “suitable jobs,” and “meaningfulness of work.” Stress-induced responses included 29 items across six scales: “vigor,” “anger-irritability,” “fatigue,” “anxiety,” “depression,” and “physical complaints.” Buffering factors included 11 items across four scales: “support from supervisors,” “support from coworkers,” “support from family and friends,” and “Job and life satisfaction.” Responses were scored on a four-point scale (true, somewhat true, somewhat false, and false), and scores for each scale were calculated using the BJSQ scoring method. High-stress individuals were identified by comparing the calculated values with established evaluation criteria [21]. Temporal changes in occupational stress were calculated by subtracting baseline scores from the scores at each subsequent time point. In addition, the impact of clinical practice participation on these changes was analyzed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses included Friedman tests for temporal changes in occupational stress scales and self-evaluation measures. The Bonferroni multiple comparisons test was applied to scales showing significant differences. Analysis of covariance was used to examine the effect of clinical practice participation on temporal changes in occupational stress. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 28, with significance set at 5%. This study was approved by the Hiroshima University Epidemiological Research Ethics Committee (approval number: E-1868).

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Questionnaires were administered at four time points (pre-training, immediately post-training, 3 months post-training, and 1 year post-training) to 141 participants between September 25, 2019, and March 15, 2023. Of these, 53 participants who completed all four questionnaires and were employed as dental hygienists during the study period were included in the analysis. Response rates were 99.3% pre-training, 94.3% immediate post-training, 85.1% at 3 months post-training, and 56.0% at 1 year post-training. Discussion

We conducted a longitudinal evaluation of a training program for dental hygienists, focusing on occupational stress. A key strength of the study was its novelty, as no prior research has evaluated dental hygienist training programs from this perspective. The findings demonstrated immediate and sustained benefits of the program. “Knowledge” and “skill” levels improved continuously immediately after training through 3 months, and “motivation” showed improvement immediately after training, suggesting the program's motivational effectiveness. Regarding occupational stress, “anxiety” decreased and “job control” improved 3 months after training, indicating training effectiveness. However, long-term effects showed increased “irritability” and “depression” 1 year after completion, highlighting the need for long-term stress management. Notably, clinical practice participants experienced increased “workplace environmental stress” but decreased “fatigue” and “anxiety” post-training. However, 1 year after completion, decreased “vigor” and increased “interpersonal workplace stress” suggested the need for organizational support in continuously applying acquired skills in practice.

The training program's effectiveness in enhancing knowledge and technical skills was clearly demonstrated. Significant improvements in “knowledge” and “skill” levels post-training, sustained for 3 months, confirmed the program's educational impact. While “motivation” increased immediately after training, it gradually declined at 3 months and 1 year. This decline may be attributed to the practical challenges encountered while implementing learned knowledge and skills and the lack of new learning opportunities in daily work. A previous study [22] reported that skill retention requires repeated training. Additionally, maintaining motivation requires confidence to overcome challenges and managing appropriate levels of anxiety about one's capabilities [23]. In particular, follow-up support for addressing knowledge and skill gaps has also been highlighted as crucial for preventing early career departures [18], underscoring the need for regular training and follow-up systems.

Occupational stress has a complex pattern. The immediate post-training reduction in “anxiety” and 3-month improvement in “job control” suggests increased confidence derived from enhanced professional knowledge and skills. However, the 1-year follow-up revealed increases in “irritability,” “fatigue,” “depression,” and “physical complaints.” The 1-year post-training increase in negative stress indicators warrants deeper examination through multiple theoretical frameworks. From the perspective of Karasek’s Job Demands-Control-Support Model [24], the initial improvement in “job control” observed at the 3-month time point may have generated an overly optimistic outlook that workplace autonomy would be maintained in the long term. When participants persistently encountered high job demands without adequate organizational support systems, the gap between enhanced competence and limited practical control likely contributed to chronic stress accumulation. This phenomenon aligns with the World Health Organization’s definition of burnout as a syndrome resulting from inadequately managed chronic workplace stress [25]. The temporal progression of stress indicators in our study reflects a characteristic pattern commonly observed in interventions for professional development. The observed pattern suggests a transition from an initial “honeymoon period” characterized by post-training confidence to a “reality adjustment phase” wherein participants faced the complexities of implementing new skills in unchanged organizational contexts [26]. Employment changes affected 16 participants (30.2%) at the 1-year mark, often driven by life events such as childbirth, marriage, and workplace transitions. These findings reflect the multirole stress theory [27–29], which indicates that competing demands between professional and personal roles create psychological strain. Among the diverse employment changes observed, the tendency for female workers to shift to part-time roles with a lower mental burden after the first childbirth aligns with the findings in a previous report [30]. This suggests that external life events, including but not limited to transitions to part-time work, significantly moderated training outcomes.

Several interconnected factors likely contributed to the long-term stress increase. First, the lack of continuous learning opportunities may have led to professional stagnation, undermining the initial motivation gains. Second, organizational factors such as insufficient mentorship, limited career advancement opportunities, and inflexible work arrangements may have prevented participants from fully utilizing their enhanced competencies. However, our study did not collect detailed information about participants’ workplace environments or organizational support systems; therefore, these patterns cannot be fully explained. Previous research demonstrates that workplace mentorship and organizational support significantly influence training sustainability and stress reduction among healthcare workers [31–33]. Additionally, the absence of formal follow-up systems for coordination between the training program and participants’ workplaces likely contributed to variability in outcomes, as participants received inconsistent support when implementing new skills in their work environments. Third, individual factors, including coping strategies, self-efficacy beliefs, and social support networks outside the workplace, likely influenced stress resilience.

These results highlight critical limitations in single-phase training interventions. While the immediate educational benefits were clear, the absence of structured follow-up support systems appears to have resulted in an increase in real-world pressures. This pattern is consistent with research indicating that training programs for healthcare workers require sustained reinforcement to maintain effectiveness and prevent burnout [34].

Clinical practice appeared to have both positive and negative effects on occupational stress. While workplace environmental stress increased immediately post-training for the clinical practice group, “fatigue” and “anxiety” decreased, indicating that clinical experience fosters both technical confidence and psychological growth. The 3-month follow-up showed increased |family/friend support,” reflecting strengthened social network developed through training. Previous research [35] highlights the role of “learning communities” and “networks” in professional skill development, leading to enhanced professional identity. However, the long-term deterioration, evidenced by increased “interpersonal conflict” and decreased “vigor” at 1 year, suggests that even beneficial clinical experiences may not sustain stress reduction in the absence of ongoing organizational support. These findings demonstrate that postgraduate education programs for dental hygienists effectively improve short-term knowledge, skills, and stress management but face challenges in sustaining these benefits long-term. The importance of practical education, including clinical practice and continuous follow-up, has been highlighted. Specifically, clinical practice implementation contributes not only to technical improvement but also to psychological support and social network formation. Based on these results, implementing effective postgraduate education requires regular training opportunities, mentorship programs for continuous support, and workplace learning opportunities. Additionally, organizational support systems that consider work-life balance and flexible working arrangements are necessary for long-term occupational stress reduction. Future intervention studies verifying the effectiveness of these support measures are expected to help develop more effective postgraduate training programs.

This study had some limitations. First, the limited sample size and regional focus of the surveys require caution when generalizing the findings. The analysis included only 53 of the 141 participants (37.6%), although no significant differences in participant attributes, except employment status, were found. Additionally, this study is subject to potential selection bias, as participants voluntarily enrolled in the training program and likely represented highly motivated individuals committed to professional development. This may further limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of dental hygienists and could lead to overestimation of the training program’s effectiveness. Second, methodological constraints include the reliance on self-reported data, potentially introducing respondent bias. Despite the longitudinal design, external factors, such as life events and workplace changes during the study period, were not fully controlled. Moreover, we did not collect detailed information about participants’ post-training workplace environments, organizational support systems, or the availability of mentorship programs; this limited our ability to fully interpret the observed stress patterns and training sustainability. Particularly, increases in “irritability,” “fatigue,” “anxiety,” and “depression,” at 1 year post-completion may be confounded by these factors. Third, the lack of a control group made it difficult to determine whether the observed changes resulted directly from the training. Specifically, the simultaneous increase in “workplace environmental stress” and decrease in “fatigue” and “anxiety” complicates the establishment of causal relationships. This limitation directly affects the interpretation of the conclusions of this study. Specifically, decreases in “fatigue” and “anxiety” cannot be definitively attributed to the effects of the program, as they may be due to factors other than the intervention, such as natural progression or changes in the workplace environment. Therefore, the conclusions of this study do not demonstrate causal effects; rather, they should be considered exploratory findings indicating that the program is a promising intervention.

Fourth, the interrelationship between occupational and personal life stress has not been thoroughly investigated, particularly regarding female-specific issues, for which quantitative data alone may not adequately capture psychological changes and stress factors. Fifth, the factors influencing individual differences in training effectiveness, such as years of experience, specialization, and work environment, were not fully addressed. Longer-term follow-up studies are needed to explore these variables. Future studies should incorporate mixed methods, including qualitative research, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the findings.

Conclusions

This longitudinal study evaluated the effects of a dental hygienist training program on occupational stress. While significant improvements in knowledge and skill, along with reduced occupational stress, were confirmed immediately after training and sustained for 3 months, stress symptoms increased after 1 year. These findings underscore the challenges of sustaining long-term benefits. To address these challenges, it is essential to improve working conditions, provide ongoing professional development, establish regular training and follow-up systems, and develop organizational support structures. Future research requires verification with larger samples, qualitative studies, and extended longitudinal investigations. This study contributes to the improvement of human resource development systems aimed at balancing occupational stress reduction and professional development among dental hygienists.
