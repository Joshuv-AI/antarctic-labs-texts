Distinct Neural Representations and Cognitive Behaviors Attributable to Naturally Developed Active Avoidance or Reactive Escape Strategies in the Male Rat
Authors: Liang Jing, Chen Ma, Lin Xu, Gal Richter-Levin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe high individual variability in coping with stress is often attributed to genetic background differences, sustained environmental conditions, or a combination of both. However, the neural mechanisms underlying coping style variability are still poorly understood.MethodsHere we examined the impact of a single extended emotional challenge on coping style variability and the associated involvement of the hippocampus, medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), and periaqueductal gray (PAG). Male Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 170) were trained in an extended 2-way shuttle avoidance (eTWSA) task for 7 days, and daily avoidance rates were measured. Forced swim test, elevated plus maze, or Morris water maze was tested before or after eTWSA exposure. Excitotoxic lesion of the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) was performed by Ibotenic infusion. Transient pharmacological blocking of DG, mPFC, or PAG was performed by muscimol or CNQX+TTX infusion.ResultsExposing rats to eTWSA was found to lead to naturally developing dichotomous, not continuous, coping styles, which we termed active avoidance (AA) or reactive escape (RE). Prior emotional responses did not predict the developing coping style. AA was associated with beneficial outcomes, including reduced behavioral despair and improved spatial learning. RE led to impaired spatial retrieval. AA was abolished by lesioning or pharmacological blocking of the DG. RE was prevented by blocking mPFC or PAG.ConclusionThe results indicate that a single exposure to a significant emotional challenge can lead, in otherwise healthy individuals, to dichotomous development of an active or reactive coping style with distinctive neural correlates and subsequent behavioral significance.Keywords: Two-way shuttle avoidance, controllable stress, individual variability, coping style, stress vulnerability, stress resilience

Significance Statement

The ability to cope with threatening situations is crucial for survival. Coping styles differ, and the differences between passive or reactive coping have been suggested to contribute to greater vulnerability or resilience toward developing stress-related psychiatric conditions such as major depression. The specific neurobiological mechanisms underlying individual differences in coping styles remain to be understood. Here we found that exposure to an extended emotional challenge led to the development of dichotomous (active avoidance [AA] or reactive escape [RE]), rather than continuous, coping styles. Although prior emotional or cognitive traits do not effectively predict coping styles, AA is associated with reduced behavioral despair and improved spatial learning. We provide evidence that the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) is required for demonstrating AA while the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and periaqueductal gray (PAG) are required for RE. Inactivation of the DG impairs the formation and maintenance of AA. Transient inactivation of mPFC or PAG reversibly induces AA behavior in RE coping subjects. These findings pave the way to future work relating to how specific differences in patterns of activity in defined neuronal circuits lead to individual variability in developing coping strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Responses to stressful events and coping styles show tremendous individual variability (Lazaro-Munoz et al., 2010; Ritov et al., 2016), which eventually affect emotional well-being and quality of life (Carver and Connor-Smith, 2010; Carver and Vargas, 2011; Russo et al., 2012), as well as between patients with mood disorder and healthy controls (Murphy et al., 1999; Clark et al., 2009). Research has started to reveal the complexity of neurocognitive traits and associated brain regions contributing to the development of different coping strategies (Carver and Vargas, 2011; Martinez et al., 2013; Ritov et al., 2016; LeDoux et al., 2017), but the neural mechanisms underlying this individual variability remain poorly understood. Stress vulnerability or resilience is often attributed to genetic background differences, sustained environmental conditions, or a combination of both. A well-established critical factor mediating the impact of stress is the level of controllability over the stressor (Lucas et al., 2014; Hadad-Ophir et al., 2015; Jing et al., 2015; Maier, 2015), but the potential of a single emotional event to inflict a long-term impact on coping abilities is yet to be established.

In animal research, the 2-way shuttle avoidance (TWSA) task is a suitable paradigm for studying different responses to stress, because animals can potentially adopt more than 1 strategy when facing the same controllable stress (LeDoux et al., 2017). In TWSA, rats are trained to avoid foot shock by shuttling to the opposite side of the shuttle box chamber when presented with a conditioning stimulus. This active strategy is known as active avoidance (AA). If the rat fails to perform AA, a foot shock will be delivered, but the animal may nevertheless terminate it by shuttling to the opposite side. This reactive strategy is known as escape or reactive escape (RE). The avoidance rate varies (Carballo-Marquez et al., 2011; Jiao et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015), and some studies noted that a fraction of animals fail to acquire effective AA and exhibit mostly RE coping (Lazaro-Munoz et al., 2010). This suggests that AA and RE are distinct behavioral coping responses to the same stressor, and comparing the 2 may reveal mechanisms related to individual differences in coping under stress. TWSA has typically been tested within a 3-day duration. Studies have shown that reduced freezing behavior and anxiolytics treatment facilitates avoidance acquisition (Fernandez-Teruel and Tobena, 2020), suggesting the relationship between affective traits and AA-RE preference. Extended TWSA training enables rats to achieve emotional controllability, which leads to resilience to stress (Lucas et al., 2014). It was also found that the failure of acquiring AA is not rescued by extending the training to 7 days (Martinez et al., 2013; Lucas et al., 2014), suggesting that once developed, AA or RE are not easily modified. Taken together, extended exposure to the TWSA task reveals individual differences in acquired coping styles and is thus suitable for investigating related neural mechanisms.

Multiple brain regions have been implicated in the perception of controllability and in avoidance behavior, such as the amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex (PFC), and periaqueductal gray (PAG) (Ritov et al., 2016; LeDoux et al., 2017; Xia and Kheirbek, 2020). The involvement of the amygdala in AA-RE behavior has been well documented in previous studies. The transition from RE to AA requires activation of basal lateral amygdala neurons and inhibition of the central amygdala (Lazaro-Munozet al., 2010; Vicens-Costa et al., 2011; Martinez et al., 2013; Moscarello and LeDoux, 2013). Determining the roles of the hippocampus, PFC, and PAG in AA or RE behavior awaits further assessment. The hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) have been reported to mediate the impact of stress and are implicated in the development of depression (Covington et al., 2010; Hare and Duman, 2020b). Within the hippocampus, the dentate gyrus (DG) was demonstrated to modulate active avoidance learning (Wang et al., 2014; Lv et al., 2016). The mPFC is functionally connected with emotion-associated regions such as the hippocampus, amygdala, and PAG and may mediate stress responses and related psychopathological symptoms (Franklin et al., 2012; Hare and Duman, 2020b). The PAG, which is modulated by the mPFC, is indicated in detecting threatening signals and mediating escape behavior (Evans et al., 2018; Branco and Redgrave, 2020). These regions are likely to be associated with the transition between AA and RE behaviors and to contribute to individual variability of coping with stress.

Exposing otherwise healthy rats to an extended 2-way shuttle avoidance (eTWSA) task for 7 days was found to lead to naturally developing dichotomous, not continuous, coping styles, which we termed AA or RE. In the present study, we established this finding and examined the impact of the developing coping styles on stress resilience. In addition, we examined the involvement of the DG, mPFC, and PAG in mediating these processes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (8 weeks; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China; Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animals Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China; Envigo, Jerusalem, Israel) were purchased. Housing was in groups of a maximum of 5 per cage in ventilated cages with water and food pellets ad libitum, a 12-hour-light/-dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 am), and a thermo-regulated environment (21°C ± 2°C). After 5 days of acclimation,experimental procedures began and were conducted between 8:30 am and 5:00 pm.

Ethics Statement

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the NIH guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the University of Haifa Ethical Committee (approval no. 627/19) and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kunming Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (approval no. SYDW-20141210-745).

Extended 2-Way Shuttle Avoidance

eTWSA was performed as previously described (Lucas et al., 2014), including 2 habituation and 7 training days. Briefly, a dimly lit, ventilated, sound-attenuated box contained a shuttle box (26 stainless-steel bars formed a grid floor). It was divided into 2 equally sized, side-to-side, cube-shaped compartments by an opaque and automatic guillotine door (9 × 11 cm) (ENV-010, Med Associates Inc., Fairfax, Vermont.). A computer detects the location of the animal and controls the signal presentation (conditioned stimulus [CS; tone of 3.5 kHz, 85 dB]; unconditioned stimulus, electric shock [US; 0.8 mA through the grid floor]). A training session contains 2 minutes of free exploration and 50 trials (trial: 10-second CS followed by maximum of 10-second US; 30 second ± 25% intertrial interval) (Figure 1A). Responses recorded on each trial: (1) avoidance, shuttling to the opposite compartment during CS; (2) escape, shuttling to the opposite compartment during US; (3) failure, no escape.

The reminder test was performed 2 days after the eTWSA, comprising of 10 trials with CS (no shock).

Elevated Plus Maze (EPM)

Anxiety-like behavior was assessed using the EPM (Ritov et al., 2016). The EPM is a cross-shaped apparatus, elevated 74 cm from the floor, with 2 opposite open arms protected only by a 1-cm-high ledge, and 2 opposite enclosed and protected arms (arm size: 52 cm × 11.5 cm). Behavior was recorded by a vertically mounted camera. Each rat was placed on the central platform of the apparatus, facing an open arm, and was allowed to explore freely for 5 minutes. The time spent in the open arms was measured.

Forced Swim Test (FS)

Behavioral despair was assessed using the FS (Porsolt, Le Pichon and Jalfre, 1977; Jing et al., 2015). A glass cylinder, 50 cm high and 26 cm diameter, was filled with water at 25°C ± 1°C (height: 35 cm). Animals were placed in the cylinder for 15 minutes of forced swim. After that, rats were dried before being returned to their home cage. Immobility time (including slight movements of limbs that were necessary for keeping nose above the water) of the first 5 minutes was scored by skilled technicians.

Morris Water Maze (MWM)

The MWM was used to assess spatial acquisition and spatial retrieval in a circular black tank (170 cm diameter; 60 cm deep) filled with water (24°C ± 1°C, 30 cm deep) in a dimly lit room with only extrinsic visual cues. The tank was divided into 4 imaginary quadrants (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4). A hidden square platform (12 × 12 cm) was placed in the center of Q1 (spatial acquisition).

For spatial acquisition, 4 trials (inter-trial interval: 1 minute) were performed daily for 4 consecutive days. A trial started by placing the rats facing the wall of the maze at a different starting point each day. Animals were allowed to search for the platform for a maximum of 1 minute. Upon reaching the platform, rats were left for 10 seconds on the platform before being taken out. If a rat failed to reach the platform, it was gently guided to the platform. Rats were dried and put in a dry cage. For spatial retrieval, a single probe test was performed 1 day after the end of spatial acquisition. The platform was removed, and rats were allowed to swim freely for 1 minute. Swimming tracks were recorded and analyzed with EthoVision XT10 (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands).

In Vivo Electrophysiology

Recording was at the performant path-DG pathway (Vouimba et al, 2020). Anesthetized rats (40% urethane + 5% chloral hydrate, 0.5 mL/100 g, i.p.) were mounted on a stereotaxic frame (Stoelting, USA), and body temperature was maintained at 37°C ± 0.5°C (via a regulated heating pad [FHC, Bowdoin, Maine, USA]). Small holes were drilled in the skull. A reference electrode was affixed to the skull in the area overlapping the nasal sinus. A recording glass electrode (tip diameter, 2–5 μm, filled with 2 M NaCl) was placed in the DG granular cell layer (4.2 mm anteroposterior [AP], 2.5 mm mediolateral [ML], and 3.2–3.7 mm dorsoventral [DV] from dura). A stimulating electrode (bipolar concentric) was inserted in the ipsilateral PP (PP: 8 mm AP, 4.0 mm ML, and 2.5–3.0 mm DV from dura). The dorsal/ventral location of recording and stimulating electrodes was adjusted to maximize the amplitude of evoked field potentials. A second stimulating electrode was inserted to the contralateral DG. Field potentials evoked by a single pulse to the PP (0.1-millisecond rectangular biphasic pulses), amplified (×100) (AM systems, Carlsborg, Washington), and digitized at 10 kHz (Cambridge Electronic Design LTD (CED), Cambridgeת UK). After a 20-minute undisturbed time, input–output response was measured. Stimulation intensity was set to elicit a response of 40%–60% of the maximal amplitude of the evoked-field potential. Field Exitatory Post-synaptic Potential (fEPSP) was recorded at 0.1Hz for at least 15 minutes for baseline, followed by short-term plasticity protocols of frequency-dependent inhibition, paired-pulse inhibition, and commissural inhibition (CI). After that, baseline was recorded for 20 minutes, followed by a theta-burst stimulation (TBS) and further recordings for 60 minutes.

For the frequency-dependent inhibition, 10 pulses were delivered at 1 Hz. The last 9 fEPSP slopes were averaged and compared with the first one, expressed as a percent of it. For the paired-pulse inhibition protocol, 5 pairs of 2 constant stimuli were delivered to the PP (inter-stimuli interval of 15 or 30 milliseconds). The second response’s fEPSP slope was compared with that of the first response, expressed as a percent of it. For the CI protocol, 3 minutes of 0.1 Hz baseline was delivered, followed by 5 pairs of stimuli (15-millisecond inter-stimuli interval), then 5 pairs of stimuli (30-millisecond inter-stimuli intervals) at 0.1 Hz. In each pair, the first pulse was applied to the contralateral DG (intensity: 1.5 mA). The second pulse was applied to the PP at baseline recording intensity. The second response’s fEPSP slope was compared with that of the baseline. For the TBS protocol, 3 sets of 10 trains each (each train: 10 pulses at 100 Hz, at baseline stimulation intensity; inter-train interval: 200 milliseconds; inter-set interval: 1 minute) were delivered. Long-term potentiation (LTP) was measured as the difference in fEPSP slope before and 60 minutes after TBS.

Data were recorded and analyzed by the Micro-1401 system and Spike2 software (Cambridge Electronic Design LTD (CED), Cambridge, UK).

Hippocampal Lesion

Surgery conditions were similar to that of in vivo electrophysiology. Rats were anesthetized with 10% ketamine, 100 mg/kg and 2% medetomidine, 10 mg/kg. Injection needles connected to a syringe pump were positioned in the DG (coordinates: AP, −4.2 mm, ML ± 2.5 mm, DV −3.7 mm) to deliver ibotenic acid (Sigma, 1.5 μL, 3 μg, dissolved in phosphate buffered saline) or phosphate buffered saline (sham lesion) bilaterally at a rate of 100 nL/min. Following infusion, the injection needles were kept at the injection sites for 2 minutes and then slowly withdrawn. Rats were individually housed for 3 days after surgery for recovery and then group housed for the rest of the experiments.

Cannula Implantation and Drug Infusion

Rats were trained in the TWSA for 3 days, then surgery was performed 3–5 days later. Surgery was carried out under pentobarbital sodium (Sigma, 60 mg/kg, i.p.) anesthesia, medical oxygen (95% O2 and 5% CO2) was applied through a mask, and body temperature (37°C ± 0.5°C) was maintained by a regulated heating pad. Stainless-steel guide cannulas were implanted by using a stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). The guide cannula was cut 0.5 mm shorter than the injection needles and placed 0.5 mm above the targeted areas as follows: DG: AP −4.2 mm, ML ± 2.5 mm, DV −4.0 mm DV; mPFC: AP 3.72 mm, ML ± 0.5 mm, DV 4.0 mm; PAG: AP −5.6 mm, ML ± 0.5 mm, DV −5.0 mm. The guide cannulas were plugged with stylets and affixed to the skull (dental cement) with 3 stainless-steel screws as anchors. After surgery, animals were housed individually. Rats were then retested in the TWSA after a 1-week recovery.

Muscimol (Sigma, 4.38 mmol−1) or CNQX (Sigma, 3 mmol−1) + TTX (Sigma, 20 µmol−1) were infused into the DG, mPFC, or PAG, 1 µL per side through the implanted guide cannulas by injection needles connected to a syringe pump (Longer Precision Pump Co., Ltd, Hebei, China) at a rate of 100 nL/min. Following infusion, the injection needles were kept at injection sites for 2 minutes and then slowly withdrawn.

Nissl Staining

Infusion sites were confirmed by Nissl staining. After the behavior test, rats were perfused and brains were sectioned as described in Immunohistochemistry. For the Nissl staining procedure, brain slices were stained with Nissl staining solution (C0117, Beyotime) at 40°C for 8 minutes, washed twice with dd H2O, and then washed with 70%, 90%, 95%, and 100% alcohol for 1 minute each, hyalinized with xylene for 2 minutes, and washed with 100% alcohol. The slices were imaged by microscope (SZX16, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS statistics software (24) (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Most behavioral and electrophysiological data were analyzed using an independent 2-tailed t test or a 1-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction when multiple paired comparisons were required. Fisher exact test was used for comparison of proportion of the AA coping style. All results are presented as mean ± SEM except for Figure 1E and supplementary Figure 1. For examination of time effect, data were analyzed using the mixed model of repeated-measures ANOVA with appropriate Greenhouse-Geisser corrections for sphericity issues when necessary. Paired samples t test was used for compering avoidance rates of the pro-AA and pro-RE groups.

Results

Naturally Developed AA and RE in the eTWSA Paradigm

We examined a large cohort of naïve rats (n = 170) trained on eTWSA without any prior behavioral tests (the post design groups in Figures 1F, 2, 3, 4C, and 4D; supplementary Figure 4 are also included; see supplementary Table 1), and unexpectedly identified naturally developing dichotomous, rather than continuous, distribution of coping styles, which we termed AA or RE (Figure 1A–C). Exposed to 50 trials per day for 7 days of training sessions, animals may develop an AA strategy (moving to the other compartment during the tone and before the shock is presented) or an RE strategy (reducing the duration of the shock by moving to the other compartment after the shock appears) (Figure 1A). Only rarely may an animal display a form of learned helplessness response, expressed as a failure to avoid or escape (supplementary Figure 1).

Over 7 days, rats developed a clear bimodal distribution, falling into either AA or RE category styles. This dissociation was clear already at day 3 and reached a steady level in the last 3 days of training (Figure 1B; supplementary Table 1). Setting the criteria by avoidance rates at ≥60% (AA) or ≤40% (RE) and averaging the avoidance rates of days 5–7, the large majority of rats showed an either AA or RE profile, whereas only 10.1% of the rats exhibited an in-between performance (Figure 1C). The proportion of rats that met the criteria each training day stabilized from day 5 (Figure 1D). The 2 groups exhibited a very different temporal profile of increased avoidance rates over training days; the AA group showed a steady increase in avoidance rate during days 1–4, which reached a plateau at day 5. The RE group exhibited a very limited increase in avoidance rates during the first 3 days, and this low rate plateaued already on day 4 (Figure 1E). An opposite picture was found when measuring the level of freezing to the context at the beginning of each training day, during the 2 minutes of habituation time before the first trial. Both AA and RE animals exhibited increased freezing. However, already from day 2, RE animals showed significantly higher levels of freezing compared with AA animals. Their freezing levels plateaued already on day 3 (Figure 1F), indicating enhanced learned fear in RE animals, which is consistent with previous reports (LeDoux et al., 2017).

Prior Emotional or Cognitive Traits Do Not Predict Coping Styles in the eTWSA

We set out to examine what could lead to such dichotomous distribution of coping styles in otherwise healthy, naïve rats. Prior affective traits are suggested to influence coping with stress (Galla and Wood, 2015; Kansky et al., 2016; Roos et al., 2019). Thus, we examined whether prior emotional or cognitive behavior could predict the development of a specific coping style. Anxiety-like behavior or behavioral despair were evaluated by using the EPM (Figure 2A–D) or FS (Figure 2E–H), respectively. Cognitive performance was evaluated using the MWM (Figure 3). Affective behavior or level of cognitive performance up to 2 days before exposure to the eTWSA did not predict the eventual developing coping style in the eTWSA, and there was no association between the performance in these tests and the later categorization as either AA or RE (Figure 2D and H; Figure 3D and F, pre-AA vs pre-RE, P > .05; supplementary Table 2).

AA Reduces FST Immobility and Enhances Spatial Learning in the MWM

We next examined the potential impact of developing either an AA or RE coping style on subsequent affective and cognitive behaviors. Two days after the end of eTWSA exposure, exposed animals were examined for their performance in the FS, EPM, and MWM tasks, and results were analyzed in accordance with the coping style they had developed. AA and RE rats did not differ in anxiety-like behavior. Comparing the performance of animals tested before eTWSA exposure with that of exposed animals revealed no difference as well (Figure 2C). There was also no difference when AA or RE animals were compared separately (Figure 2D, E). There was no significant difference between AA and RE rats in post eTWSA behavioral despair tested in FS, but comparing performance of animals tested before eTWSA exposure with that of exposed animals revealed a significant difference, with significantly reduced immobility in eTWSA-exposed animals (Figure 2G). Comparing separately AA or RE animals revealed that this reduction in behavioral despair was found only in the AA coping animals (Figure 2H), supporting previous findings (Lucas et al., 2014).

eTWSA-exposed animals showed better spatial acquisition than the animals tested before eTWSA exposure (Figure 3C). Comparing separately AA or RE animals revealed that post exposure, AA animals performed significantly better than preexposed AA animals, However, no such difference was found with RE animals (Figure 3D). Comparing preexposure and postexposure performance of all animals in the Probe test revealed a marginally significant reduction in quality of the spatial search for the platform (Figure 3E). Separating AA and RE animals revealed that this reduction was entirely contributed by RE animals (Figure 3F). The swimming velocity did not differ between groups (supplementary Figure 2B). The reduced depression and improved learning of the AA coping group suggests that AA coping is associated with enhanced resilience, as previously proposed (Lucas et al., 2014).

Hippocampal Dentate Gyrus Is Required for AA Coping

Beyond its role in declarative and spatial memory formation (Squire, 1992; Martin et al., 2000; Neves et al., 2008), the hippocampus—and in particular the DG, which gates major inputs into the hippocampus—is known to be critical for evaluating stressful events, differentiating escapable and inescapable stress, and mediating stress-related behaviors (Shors et al., 1989; Trivedi and Coover, 2004; Fa et al, 2014; Zhang et al., 2014; Jing et al., 2015). We thus tested whether the formation and stabilization of coping style preference are DG dependent. eTWSA was performed 21 days after bilateral excitotoxic lesions induced by ibotenic acid infusion into the DG (Figure 4A; supplementary Figure 3). AA coping was abolished in the lesion group, whereas the sham group was almost identical to naïve group. To further test whether the DG is involved in the stabilization of an AA coping style, we defined pro-AA and pro-RE rats by their avoidance rates at day 3, which was previously found to be a good early predictor of the plateau coping preferences: 86% of rats with an avoidance rate ≥40% at day 3 ended up being AA, and 75% of rats with an avoidance rate <40% at day 3 ended up being RE. Thus, rats with an avoidance rate ≥40% at day 3 were defined as pro-AA, and rats with an avoidance rate <40% at day 3 were defined as pro-RE (Figure 1B, E; Figure 4B naïve; supplementary Figure 3C; supplementary Table 1). Accordingly, rats were first trained in the eTWSA for 3 days, then bilateral cannulation surgery targeting the DG was performed. At 7 days after surgery, an additional 4 days of training in the eTWSA was performed with bilateral CNQX+TTX, DMSO (50% in saline), or saline infusion (Figure 4B; supplementary Figure 3; supplementary Table 3) 30 minutes before training. The saline group was added due to the possibility that DMSO by itself could affect neural function (Tamagnini et al., 2014). In pro-AA rats, the proportion of AA dropped to 8% in the CNQX+TTX treated group (Figure 4B). A floor effect was found with the pro-RE rats, as the percentage of RE subjects was 100% in all 3 treated groups (Figure 4B). Consistent with that, DG activity, as measured by c-Fos expression after a reminder test, changed only in the AA coping group (supplementary Figure 4).

To further test the involvement of the DG in exhibiting different coping styles, we assessed activity-dependent plasticity at the performant path-dentate gyrus synapses, which are the prime input to the hippocampus, and local circuit activity, using in vivo electrophysiological recording. We examined TBS-induced LTP and DG local circuit function in 4 groups: pro-AA, pro-RE, AA, and RE. TBS induced LTP was similar in all 4 groups (Figure 4C). Local circuit activity protocols, which reflect feedforward and feedback inhibition, revealed no differences between groups (supplementary Figure 5). Finally, we examined interhemispheric cross-talk between left and right DG, as reflected by commissure-induced inhibition (CI), in which the contralateral DG stimulus was applied 15/30 milliseconds before perforant path stimulus (Vouimba et al., 2020). No difference was detected in CI between the pro-AA and pro-RE groups. However, CI inhibition was found to be significantly stronger at the 15-millisecond inter-pulse interval in the AA animals than in the RE animals (Figure 4D). Taken together, these results indicate that AA, but not RE, coping style requires the hippocampus, and in particular the DG, as indicated by the developing alteration of CI after the coping style preference was stabilized.

mPFC and PAG Are Required for RE Coping

Besides the hippocampus, the amygdala, mPFC, and PAG are also implicated in avoidance vs escape-associated behaviors (Martinez et al., 2013; LeDoux et al., 2017; Evans et al., 2018). The role of the amygdala has been tested in a similar, though not identical, paradigm (Lazaro-Munoz et al., 2010). The mPFC has been suggested to regulate stress response selection (Gourley and Taylor, 2016), and PAG has been reported to produce types of learned or innate stress-related behaviors (George et al., 2019). However, the role of mPFC and PAG in AA-RE behavior is yet to be revealed. We set out to examine the potential involvement of the mPFC and PAG, by infusion of the GABAa receptor agonist muscimol, or CNQX+TTX (Figure 5A). The infusion sites were prelimbic (PL) and infralimbic (IL) in mPFC or dorsal and dorsal lateral region of PAG, as verified by post hoc Nissl staining. However, considering the diffusion of infused drug, the affected area may not be limited to the exact infusion sites. Four eTWSA training sessions were performed after pro-AA and pro-RE groups were profiled, with the mPFC or PAG being inactivated every other training session, and the avoidance rate was compared between infusion and non-infusion sessions (Figure 5B). Muscimol infusion into the mPFC significantly increased the avoidance rate (i.e., an AA coping response) in pro-RE rats, whereas the pro-AA rats were largely unaffected (Figure 5B). Similarly, PAG infusion of CNQX+TTX increased avoidance in the pro-RE group (Figure 5C) but did not affect pro-AA animals. Importantly, the effects of those manipulations were transient, indicating that the mPFC and PAG are involved in executing a RE coping response but not in acquiring or modifying the already established coping style. These results also suggest that passive coping is modulated more by the mPFC and PAG, whereas AA coping is dependent more on the DG of the hippocampus.

Discussion

Coping with threat is essential for survival across species. An acute dangerous situation can be relieved by either active or reactive actions but with differential implications in the long run. Employing the eTWSA exposure in a large cohort of rats, we clearly identified a developing dichotomous pattern instead of an expected continuous distribution of levels of performance of either the AA or RE coping style. Only very few individuals exhibited an in-between level of performance. These dichotomous developing coping styles, in otherwise healthy individuals, were not associated with prior emotional behaviors nor with prior cognitive performance. However, developing an AA coping style was found to reduce passive behavior in FST and promote spatial learning, whereas the developing a RE coping experience was found to be associated with impaired memory retrieval.

Individual variability in response to stress contributes to vulnerability or resilience to developing stress-related psychiatric disorders. Nevertheless, many preclinical studies still focus on group means rather than on individual differences among the studied subjects (Voelkl et al., 2020). One significance of our finding is that most rats show stabilized coping strategical preference and fall into 1 of 2 main categories, shown in the dichotomous distribution of plateau avoidance rate (Figure 1). This observation provided a convenient window to study mechanisms contributing to individual behavior. Though coping style is expected to be closely related to mood states such as anxiety and depression (Hong, 2007; Kasi et al., 2012), basal measures of symptoms assumed to be related to depression and anxiety did not predict the subsequent developing coping preferences (Figure 2). Furthermore, the low avoidance rates observed in the RE group are unlikely to be attributed to reduced memory capabilities because retrieved fear response and basal spatial memory performance did not differ from those of the AA group (Figure 1; Figure 3). Previous studies suggest an inverse correlation between anxiety and AA acquisition. Various types of anxiolytic drugs, such as benzodiazepines, accelerate avoidance acquisition, whereas anxiogenic drugs have the opposite effect (Fernandez-Teruel and Tobena, 2020). Additionally, studies have shown that context-conditioned freezing, suggested to be a measure of anxiety, is negatively correlated with avoidance acquisition (Vicens-Costa et al., 2011), a phenomenon also observed in this study. Therefore, it is likely that anxiety-related symptoms do influence the AA-RE ration of coping styles, although it was not fully revealed in our experimental design, as assessed by the pre-training EPM test.

Stress resilience and vulnerability may be experience dependent, and previous studies suggest that controllability over the stressor or the lack of it is a critical factor (Maier, 2015). Indeed, uncontrollable stress leads to the development of depressive-like symptoms, whereas controllable stress reduces depressive-like behavior and thus may promote stress resilience (Lucas et al., 2014; Hadad-Ophir et al., 2015; Jing et al., 2015). The current results are in line with this notion. Rats that developed AA coping during the exposure to the eTWSA subsequently showed reduced immobility in the FS and better learning performance in the MWM (Figures 2 and 3), whereas RE coping was found to be associated with impaired spatial retrieval in the MWM.

The eTWSA challenge and the related avoidance or escape behaviors are expected to engage multiple brain regions, including the hippocampus, mPFC, amygdala, and PAG (Lazaro-Munoz et al., 2010; Moscarello and LeDoux, 2013; Horovitz et al., 2017; Daviu et al., 2020). Here we demonstrate that development and stabilization of AA coping is dependent on the DG. Notably, because the difference in commissure inhibition appeared only after the stabilization of the coping style, we assume that the development of AA involves modulation of DG interneurons, supporting previous findings (Hadad-Ophir et al., 2015). The results further indicate that activity in both the mPFC and PAG is necessary for exhibiting RE coping. Silencing either the mPFC or the PAG led to transient increase of avoidance rates (Figure 5), indicating that RE coping is not simply a failure to learn instrumental avoidance. These results agree with previous reports regarding the role of both of mPFC and PAG in encoding emotional valence and in mediating escape responses (Amat et al., 2005; Tovote et al., 2016; Evans et al., 2018). Thus, our results suggest that the DG as well as the mPFC and PAG are differentially involved in developing either AA or RE coping styles. Because the mPFC and PAG encompass distinct subregions with diverse functions, a spatially higher resolution examination is required to understand the contributions of more precisely categorized regions to avoidance-escape behavior.

The mPFC has long been recognized as crucially involved in modulating stress responses, including fear memory and depressive-like behavior. Fear-related freezing is bidirectionally modulated by mPFC, with the PL neurons receiving direct input from a subgroup of basolateral amygdala neurons and being required for the expression of conditioned fear, while the basolateral amygdala–infralimbic projection is vital for fear extinction (Corcoran and Quirk, 2007; Senn et al., 2014; Tovote et al., 2015; Roelofs and Dayan, 2022). Optogenetics stimulation of the ventromedial PFC has been shown to exert antidepressant-like effects, including reduced social avoidance and anhedonia (Covington et al., 2010). In our study, pharmacological inactivation of the mPFC transiently reversed the RE to AA, suggesting that the mPFC contributes to operational responses to stress. This finding aligns with previous reports showing that mPFC lesions improve avoidance performance (Joel et al., 1997). However, other studies have shown that certain specific mPFC regions are necessary for avoidance acquisition. For instance, ventromedial PFC top-down inhibition modulates the sense of stress controllability and is required for the formation of avoidance acquisition (Shah et al., 2004; Moscarello and LeDoux, 2013). Lesions or inactivation of the IL, but not PL, impaired but did not completely block the acquisition of AA learning (Moscarello and LeDoux, 2013). The inconsistencies between these findings may be attributed to differences in experimental design. Previous studies primarily focused on AA acquisition as a learning process, whereas our results revealed a transient and reversible coping preference. In addition, although the infusion sites were located in the PL and IL, it is likely that nearby regions were also affected by diffusion of the administered drug. Taken together, these findings suggest that the mPFC is involved in the switch between AA and RE behavior, but further research is required to investigate the specific roles of subregions within the mPFC.

The PAG, which receives projections from various regions including the PFC and amygdala, is believed to play a pivotal role in stress-induced responses, including avoidance and escape from threats. Similar to the mPFC, the PAG consists of subregions with distinct functions. In our study, pharmacological inactivation of the PAG effectively increased the avoidance rate among RE rats, suggesting a key role in mediating escape behavior. It should be noted that, due to the potential diffusion of the administered drug, regions such as the lateral PAG and ventral lateral PAG may have been affected as well. The PAG is widely recognized for its involvement in passive stress responses, such as freezing and immobility, and operational responses, including escape. The amygdala-PAG pathway plays a critical role in modulating freezing and sets a synaptic threshold mechanism for escape decision (Evans et al., 2018; Roelofs and Dayan, 2022). In this perspective, inhibition of PAG function may lead to reduced freezing and escape behavior and subsequently promote avoidance. However, the PAG is not likely to solely contribute to initiate RE coping and escape. It may also play a critical role in inhibiting RE coping and promoting avoidance. The mPFC-dlPAG projection modulates place avoidance (Vander Weele et al., 2018), and the amygdala-PAG projection is involved in discriminating between threatening and nonthreatening signals in addition to simply producing freezing behavior (Tovote et al., 2016; Ozawa et al., 2017). The transient increase in avoidance rate observed in our study likely involves a combination of these functions, mediated by subregions of PAG.

Limitations

Experiments were conducted during the light phase, which may have some impact on behavior in TWSA. It is important to note that our study included only male rats, which may introduce a sex bias. The choice of testing only males was influenced by the predominance of using male rat in previous TWSA studies. However, in the future, also examining female rats is required because previous work indicates that sex-related differences in mechanisms of individual variability in coping with stress do exist (e.g., Krispil-Alon et al, 2022). Additionally, the interpretation regarding the exact subregions involved in the effects of local inhibitions is limited by the pharmacological inactivation methods employed. Although the infusion sites were located specifically in the PL and IL, or PAG, it is likely that nearby regions were also affected by diffusion of administered drug.

Conclusion

A single exposure to a significant emotional challenge was found to lead to, in otherwise healthy individuals, the development of dichotomous coping styles, with distinctive neural correlates. Cognitive and emotional behaviors before the exposure did not predict the eventual coping style to be developed. However, developing either an AA or RE coping style did have consequences regarding both emotional and cognitive performances later on. It is commonly assumed that such pivotal personality characteristics, as coping strategies, would be the outcome of a specific genetic map or the result of prolonged and repeated exposure to life experiences. The current results present an additional, unexpected possibility that a single, significant, emotional exposure may have long-lasting implications on vulnerability and resilience traits. These findings emphasize the importance of being attentive to the outcome of such single, emotional exposures in a way not assumed to be required before. One can envisage the importance of this in some particular environments, such as kindergartens and schools, which could be expected to be particularly sensitive in that respect.

The dissociation between AA or RE coping styles within a random group of animals is another example for the significant individual variability in emotional responses of otherwise healthy individuals. These findings further emphasize the importance of moving away from working with group averages, which conceal individual variability, and toward more detailed analysis approaches, which take into consideration this individual variability in emotional responses (Richter-Levin and Sandi, 2021).
