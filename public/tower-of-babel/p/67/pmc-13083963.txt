Transmission of Salmonella clones between different animal species in a horse and cattle breeding region in Japan
Authors: Nobuo Arai, Hidekazu Niwa, Eri Uchida-Fujii, Yuiko Sawa, Yukino Tamamura-Andoh, Yuta Kinoshita, Anna Momoki, Ayako Watanabe-Yanai, Taketoshi Iwata, Midori Kubo, Masahiro Kusumoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSequence type 34 (ST34) Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium and its monophasic variant (Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:-) are the most prevalent clones among humans and animals worldwide, including in Japan. Although cross-species transmission may have occurred in the background of global spread, the matter remains unresolved. Here, we conducted high-resolution phylogenetic analysis using whole-genome sequencing data of Salmonella Typhimurium and 4,[5],12:i:- obtained from a horse and cattle breeding district in Japan and identified cases of cross-species transmission of ST34 Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- between horses and cattle. These isolates were classified into five clusters, core genome single-nucleotide polymorphism (cgSNP) clusters 1–5, based on the SNP distance. To elucidate the genetic background of each cgSNP cluster, we also conducted a phylogenetic analysis of 496 ST34 strains obtained from Japan and other countries. Hierarchical clustering using rhierBAPS revealed three clades. The past ST34 epidemic strains in Japan and cgSNP clusters 1–3 were concentrated in clades 1 and 3, which should be referred to as the Japanese epidemic lineages, whereas cgSNP cluster 5 belonged to clade 2, which should be referred to as the global lineage. These results suggest that ST34 Salmonella may have entered Japan through multiple routes and was transmitted between horses and cattle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39311-y.Keywords: Cattle; Cross-species transmission; Horse; Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:-; Sequence type 34Subject terms: Evolution, Genetics, Microbiology

Introduction

Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium (Salmonella Typhimurium) and its monophasic variant (Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:-), are rod shaped, Gram-negative bacteria that are two of the most common pathogens causing gastroenteritis in humans and animals1,2. While S. enterica has several host-restricted serovars, such as Typhi, which infects humans3, Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- have a broad host range that includes humans, mammals, and birds4. A reduction in the contamination levels of these serovars with broad host ranges on food animal farms is necessary to reduce the potential risk of both salmonellosis in animals and food poisoning in humans. Since these serovars infect various kinds of animals and are maintained, globally disseminated clones have occasionally emerged among both humans and animals5,6.

To date, whole-genome sequencing (WGS)-based phylogenetic studies have identified several globally disseminated clones within Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:-. Salmonella Typhimurium definitive phage type 104 (DT104) was among the most widespread clones in both humans and animals during the 1990s6. DT104 is characterized by the presence of Salmonella genomic island 1, which contains antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, such as aadA2, blaPSE−1, floR, sul1, and tet(G)7. In addition, Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- of sequence type 34 (ST34) identified by multilocus sequence typing (MLST) are the most prevalent clones worldwide5. These clones are characterized by harboring a composite transposon containing AMR genes, such as aph(3”)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, blaTEM−1B, sul2, and tet(B), and an integrative conjugative element carrying heavy metal tolerance genes8. ST34 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- were initially reported in European countries8–10; thereafter, these clones were isolated from North America11,12, South America13, Africa14, Oceania15,16, and Asia5,17,18 including Japan19,20. In our previous study, we identified nine distinct clades of Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i- strains through WGS-based phylogenetic analysis19. Among the nine clades, clades 1 and 9 correspond to DT104 and ST34, respectively. We subsequently developed a PCR-based clade-typing method targeting clade-specific single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Furthermore, ST34 is a threat in Western countries, mainly causing salmonellosis in humans and swine11,21–23; however, we revealed that the most prevalent genotype in cattle in Japan since the 2010s is SNP genotype 9, which corresponds to ST3419.

When salmonellosis occurs in farm animals, it is important to determine the route of transmission and prevent Salmonella from entering and spreading to farms and neighboring areas to prevent the spread of infection. The transmission of Salmonella involves the interaction of various factors, including the movement of infected animals, vehicles, feeds, humans, and wild animals24–27. In particular, in the case of infections caused by Salmonella serovars with a broad host range, such as Typhimurium and 4,[5],12:i:-, the presence of farms breeding different animal species in neighboring areas can complicate the estimation of transmission routes due to the diverse factors involved. Molecular epidemiological analysis using pulsed-field gel electrophoresis or sequence-based methods such as MLST and multilocus variable-number tandem repeat analysis (MLVA) is effective for determining the transmission route of bacteria27–30. However, molecular epidemiological studies have typically been conducted on specific animal species, resulting in limited knowledge regarding the transmission of Salmonella between different animal species.

In the present study, we performed high-resolution WGS-based phylogenetic analysis of Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains that were isolated from horses and cattle in ”District A”, a region in Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan, where horse and cattle breeding are actively conducted. The objectives of this study were to estimate the origin of these strains by comparing them with a large amount of WGS data from domestic and global strains and to evaluate the cross-species transmission of Salmonella serovars.

Results

Temporal changes in the prevalence of SNP genotypes in the horse breeding region

To investigate the annual trends in the distribution of Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains isolated from District A, 42 strains were classified using in silico SNP genotyping. As a result, all the 42 strains were classified into four SNP genotypes: 1, 6, 8, and 9 (Table 1). Strains isolated from horses in 1981 and 2004 were classified into SNP genotypes 6 and 1, respectively. Since 2013, strains of SNP genotype 9, which corresponds to ST34, have been continuously isolated from both horses and cattle, and SNP genotype 8 strains were isolated from both animals between 2021 and 2023. Notably, no movement history was confirmed for any individuals or farms investigated in this study between 2019 and 2023.

Phylogenetic relationships among strains of SNP genotypes 8 and 9 isolated in District A

A total of 218 SNPs were detected in 23 strains of ST34/SNP genotype 9 isolated from District A and the reference strain L-3837, and all 24 strains were classified into four clusters using rhierBAPS (Fig. 1A). In this study, we further defined groups consisting of closely related strains within a distance of 25 SNPs as core genome SNP (cgSNP) clusters. The SNP distances within each cgSNP cluster were as follows: cluster 1, ten SNPs; cluster 2, two SNPs; cluster 3, 25 SNPs; cluster 4, three SNPs; and cluster 5, four SNPs (Fig. 1A). All the cgSNP clusters except cluster 4, which consisted exclusively of strains isolated from horses, were composed of strains isolated from both horses and cattle.

From geographical and temporal perspectives, the ST34/SNP genotype 9 strains belonging to cgSNP cluster 1 were distributed separately in the eastern and western parts of District A between 2013 and 2016 (Fig. 1B and C). Only one single-nucleotide variant (SNV) was detected between the sal-k-4 and sal-k-5 strains, which were isolated from cattle at Farm B-2 and horses at Farm B-3 in the same year (Fig. 1A). In cgSNP cluster 2, which included strains isolated from both horses and cattle in Towns III and IV in 2020, the SNP distance was only two (Fig. 1A). Notably, the shortest geographical distance between Towns III and IV is approximately 26 km (Fig. 1B). In cgSNP cluster 3, all but one strain were isolated from Town III, and ten SNVs were detected between the sal-k-26 and sal-k-39 strains that were isolated from cattle in 2021 and horses in 2023 (Fig. 1A). cgSNP cluster 4 consisted solely of strains isolated from horses at Farm D-3 in Town IV (Fig. 1A and C). cgSNP cluster 5 was distributed in both horses and cattle from the central to eastern parts of District A (Fig. 1B), with a maximum SNP distance of four within the cluster (Fig. 1A). The remaining two strains, sal-k-7 and Sal-314, were isolated from horses. We designated these two strains as sporadic isolates because more than 25 SNPs, between 36 and 76, were detected among them and the other strains (Fig. 1A).

A total of 511 SNPs were detected from the core genomes of 31 strains of SNP genotype 8, including six strains isolated from horses and cattle in District A. Among the six strains, the maximum SNP distance was only three in the five strains, except for strain sal-k-33 (Fig. S1).

AMR gene repertoire of ST34/SNP genotype 9 strains

Five AMR genes, aph(3”)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, blaTEM−1B, sul2, and tet(B), were detected at high frequencies in 23 strains of ST34/SNP genotype 9 isolated from District A (Fig. S2). Additionally, all the strains belonging to cgSNP cluster 4 harbor an aminoglycoside resistance gene, aph(3’)-Ia. In cgSNP cluster 5, two strains, sal-k-31 and sal-k-38, harbor dfrA14, floR, qnrS1, and tet(A) in addition to the four AMR genes that are highly prevalent in ST34, with the exception of tet(B).

Phylogenetic position of ST34 Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- isolated from District A in the global population structure

ST34 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- have been globally widespread in both humans and animals5,8,11. To date, phylogenetic studies on ST34 have been reported from various countries, and the genetic diversity within ST34 and the driving forces of its global spread have been elucidated5,15,20. In this study, we evaluated the phylogenetic position of the ST34/SNP genotype 9 strains isolated from District A using WGS data obtained from representative studies on the phylogenetic relationships of ST34. A total of 3,774 SNPs were detected in the core genomes of 496 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains obtained from 28 countries between 1998 and 2023 (Table S3), and three BAPS clades were identified using rhierBAPS clustering (Fig. 2).

BAPS clade 1 included 131 strains isolated from 15 countries, and the largest population was North American strains, accounting for 45%. In particular, the United States strains accounted for 32.1%, followed by Japanese strains, which accounted for 30.5%. BAPS clade 1 included a distinct subclade, designated Japanese epidemic lineage 1, consisting of 32 strains isolated exclusively in Japan (Fig. 2). The strains for the BAPS clade 1 isolated from cattle, swine, and humans accounted for 33.6%, 24.4%, and 24.4%, respectively. In addition, the sal-k-7 strain, which was sporadically isolated as shown in Fig. 1, was also included in this lineage.

BAPS clade 2 consisted of 177 strains isolated from 26 countries, and the largest population was European strains, accounting for 47.5%. Furthermore, British strains accounted for 23.7%, followed by Japanese strains, which accounted for 13.6%. Because BAPS clade 2 was composed of strains isolated from all continents except Antarctica and included the most diverse countries among the three BAPS clades, we designated this clade the global lineage in this study (Fig. 2). The strains belonging to the cgSNP cluster 5 were grouped as part of the global lineage. In addition, the Sal-314 strain belonged to BAPS clade 2 but was located far from cgSNP cluster 5. In BAPS clade 2, strains isolated from humans, swine, and cattle accounted for 54.2%, 22.0%, and 9.6%, respectively.

BAPS clade 3 included 188 strains isolated from nine countries, and the largest population was Japanese strains, accounting for 92.0%, followed by British strains, accounting for 2.7%. A distinctive subclade, designated Japanese epidemic lineage 2, consisted of 170 strains isolated exclusively in Japan, and this lineage was composed of strains isolated from animals, such as cattle, swine, and horses (Fig. 2). The strains in cgSNP clusters 1, 2, and 3 belonged to Japanese epidemic lineage 2, whereas the strains in cgSNP cluster 4 were independently positioned outside this lineage. In BAPS clade 3, strains isolated from cattle, swine, and humans accounted for 65.4%, 22.9%, and 6.4%, respectively.

Temporal analysis of the global lineage and Japanese epidemic lineage 2

To estimate the emergence time of each cgSNP cluster and Japanese epidemic lineage 2, we performed a temporal phylogenetic analysis using BEAST on 146 representative strains from BAPS clades 2 and 3. A total of 2,119 SNPs were detected in the core genome of the 146 strains. As shown in Fig. 3, BAPS clade 3 diverged from BAPS clade 2, the global lineage, in 1997 (95% highest posterior density, HPD: 1992–2001). Subsequently, Japanese epidemic lineage 2, including cgSNP clusters 1, 2, and 3, diverged in 2002 (95% HPD: 1998–2005). The estimated time to the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) was 2009 (95% HPD: 2005–2012), and cgSNP cluster 4 diverged from European strains around the same time (2011, 95% HPD: 2007–2014). The sublineage containing cgSNP cluster 5 (Fig. 3, violet branches) diverged in 1998 (95% HPD: 1994–2002) and consists of 22 strains that were mostly isolated from East Asian countries (twelve strains from Japan, five strains from South Korea, and four strains from China). Furthermore, the estimated time to the MRCA was 2000 (95% HPD: 1996–2003), at which point cgSNP cluster 5 diverged within the violet branch sublineage.

Discussion

In the present study, we elucidated the phylogenetic background and relationships of Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains isolated from horses and cattle in a farming district in Japan through WGS-based phylogenetic analysis. As shown in Table 1, the major SNP genotypes changed from 6 to 1 to 9. In our previous study, we classified 815 strains of Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- isolated from cattle in Japan between 1977 and 2017 into nine SNP genotypes19 and reported that the predominant SNP genotypes changed as follows: genotype 6 in the 1980s, genotype 2 from the mid-1980s to the early 1990s, genotype 1 from the early 1990s to the early 2000s, genotype 7 in the 2000s, and genotype 9 since the early 2010s19. Therefore, the SNP genotype trends in District A were almost the same as the nationwide trend, except for SNP genotypes 2 and 7.

Previous studies have reported that the mutation rates of S. enterica and Escherichia coli are equivalent, ranging from 10− 8 to 10− 7 per nucleotide per year31–34. In a molecular epidemiological study of enterohemorrhagic E. coli in humans, Lee et al.. suggested that when the number of SNP loci was less than five, the strains were highly closely related and likely derived from the same source35. The Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains of SNP genotype 9, the most common genotype in District A, were classified into five cgSNP clusters with SNP distances of less than 25, indicating that they were closely related from a phylogenetic perspective (Fig. 1). In particular, the cross-species transmission of Salmonella clones on the farms was clearly demonstrated for the following cases where the SNP distances were less than five: cgSNP cluster 1, between cattle on Farm B-2 and horses on Farm B-3; cgSNP cluster 2, among horses on Farm C-3, cattle on Farm D-4, cattle on Farm D-5, and cattle on Farm D-6; cgSNP cluster 5, among cattle on Farm B-10, horses on Farm B-11, cattle on Farm B-12, cattle on Farm C-9, and horses on Farm D-3 (Fig. 1). With respect to SNP genotype 8, only two or three SNVs were detected between the sal-k-25 strain isolated from cattle on Farm C-5 and four strains isolated from horses on Farm D-7, and the cross-species transmission of Salmonella clones between cattle on Farm C-5 and horses on Farm D-7 was clearly demonstrated (Fig. S1). In the present study, we were unable to identify the specific transmission routes of Salmonella clones because we investigated the epidemiological relationships among the farms. A previous study indicated that Salmonella spp. detected in Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan, were shared among wild animals, such as raccoons and crows, and cattle27, suggesting that transmission via wild animals may be a possible route.

To date, several studies have investigated the phylogenetic relationships and population structure of ST34 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains isolated globally, including Europe8, North America11, Oceania15,16, and Asia5,20. We estimated the origin of the ST34 Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains isolated from District A by comparing their WGS data with those of numerous domestic and overseas strains that were analyzed in previous studies. As shown in Fig. 2, cgSNP clusters 1, 2, and 3 belong to BAPS clade 2, which is Japanese epidemic lineage 2. This lineage corresponds to clade 9-2, which was suggested in our previous study and dominated among recent strains isolated from food animals in Japan via the emergence of clonal expansion and multiple host-adapted subclades20. These data indicate that the occurrence of salmonellosis caused by cgSNP clusters 1, 2, and 3 is due to the spread of Japanese epidemic lineage 2. From an evolutionary perspective, Japanese epidemic lineage 2 may have formed through divergence from the global lineage around 2002 and adaptation to food animals in Japan (Fig. 3). Furthermore, cgSNP cluster 4 may have been located in a state of genetic drift during phylogenetic evolution from the global lineage to Japanese epidemic lineage 2. ST34 strains typically harbor five AMR genes, aph(3”)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, blaTEM−1B, sul2, and tet(B), within a composite transposon. These AMR genes were detected in almost all the strains isolated from District A (Fig. S2). On the other hand, strains harboring the AMR gene repertoire present in cgSNP cluster 4, including aph(3”)-Ib, aph(6)-Id, blaTEM−1B, sul2, tet(B), and aph(3’)-Ia (Fig. S2), were not detected in ST34 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains isolated from cattle and swine in Japan between 1998 and 201720. Therefore, considering the phylogenetic localization and the repertoire of acquired genes, cgSNP cluster 4 may have a different origin from the Japanese epidemic lineage 2. cgSNP cluster 5 was clearly separated from Japanese epidemic lineages 1 and 2, and this cluster is estimated to have a different origin from the past epidemic lineages in Japan and to represent the spread of a part of the global lineage (Fig. 2). From a geographic perspective, BAPS clade 3, which includes cgSNP clusters 1, 2, and 3, was composed mainly of strains isolated in Japan and Europe (Fig. 3). However, the sublineage within the global lineage, which includes cgSNP cluster 5 (violet branches in Fig. 3), was composed mostly of strains isolated in East Asian countries, such as China and South Korea (Figs. 2 and 3). These results suggest that ST34 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- may have entered Japan through at least two transmission routes from Europe and East Asia. Furthermore, ST34 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- typically show resistance to ampicillin, streptomycin, sulfonamides, and tetracycline8. In contrast, some strains within cgSNP cluster 5 appear to have acquired resistance to trimethoprim and chloramphenicol, derived from dfrA14 and floR, respectively.

Conclusion

Our data clearly demonstrated cross-species transmission of Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- between horses and cattle in a farming district in Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan. We identified five closely related groups, cgSNP clusters 1 to 5, based on pairwise SNP distances within ST34/SNP genotype 9 strains in District A. Interestingly, while cgSNP clusters 1, 2, and 3 were considered part of Japanese epidemic lineage 2, the origins of cgSNP clusters 4 and 5 were suggested to be foreign countries from Europe and East Asia, respectively. This study highlights the importance of considering the cross-species transmission of Salmonella in addition to infection control in specific animal species. Furthermore, our data suggest the possibility of multiple transmission routes for ST34 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- into Japan.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolation, identification, and typing

In this study, 42 strains of Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- were isolated and used for WGS data analysis (Table S1). The 42 strains were obtained from clinical samples (feces or organs) collected from healthy or diseased cattle (n = 12) and horses (n = 30) in the central southern horse-breeding region of Hokkaido Prefecture, Japan, between 1981 and 2023 for diagnostic or monitoring purposes. No anesthesia was administered during the sample collection. Salmonella spp. were identified based on colony morphology on selective media and biochemical properties, as previously reported36. Serotyping of Salmonella was performed using microtiter and slide agglutination methods according to the White–Kaufmann–Le Minor scheme37.

WGS data collection

WGS data from a total of 540 strains of Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- were collected. The data for the 42 strains isolated in Japan were newly obtained (Table S1), whereas those for the other 498 strains obtained from 28 countries, including Japan, were downloaded from public databases (Table S2).

For the 42 strains, genomic DNA was extracted from Salmonella cells cultured on blood agar plates using a DNeasy UltraClean Microbial Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequence libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA), and WGS was performed by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA), generating 150 bp paired-end reads. The WGS data containing the 498 downloaded reads were processed for quality assessment and adaptor trimming by Trim Galore v0.6.7 (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). Drafted genomes for each isolate were constructed by SPAdes genome assembler v3.15.238 with careful options.

In silico MLST and SNP genotyping

MLST was performed using the nucleotide sequences of seven housekeeping genes, aroC, dnaN, hemD, hisD, purE, sucA, and thrA, on the draft genome contigs, according to the protocols available in the MLST database (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/species/senterica/allele_st_search). SNP genotyping was performed using the fastn (nucleotide-to-nucleotide) program in GENETYX version 13 (GENETYX Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) to detect the genotype-specific SNPs. The query sequences were previously reported19, and the nucleotide positions in the reference strain Salmonella Typhimurium LT2 (accession no. NC_003197.1) were as follows: SNP genotype 1, 2,417,663 to 2,417,682; SNP genotype 2, 3,539,824 to 3,539,843; SNP genotype 3, 829,669 to 829,688; SNP genotype 4, 1,439,183 to 1,439,202; SNP genotype 5, 1,074,002 to 1,074,021; SNP genotype 6, 4,377,202 to 4,377,221; SNP genotype 7, 2,279,697 to 2,279,716; SNP genotype 8, 1,514,434 to 1,514,453; and SNP genotype 9, 1,505,716 to 1,505,735.

CgSNP detection and phylogenetic analysis

For the phylogenetic analysis, mapping of short reads to the reference Salmonella complete genome, construction of core genome alignment, and identification of SNPs were performed using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). Salmonella Typhimurium strain 14,028 S (accession no. CP001363.1) and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strain L-3837 (accession no. AP023289.1) were used as reference genomes for ST19 and ST34, respectively. The intact prophage regions of strains 14,028 S and L-3837 were identified using PHASTER39 and masked from the alignment. Recombination regions were detected and removed using Gubbins v.3.1.640. cgSNPs were extracted using SNP-sites v2.5.141. Pairwise SNP distances were calculated using snp-dists v0.7.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snp-dists). A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed from the concatenated cgSNPs using RAxML Next Generation v1.142, with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Population structure was defined using hierarchical clustering of nucleotide sequences using rhierBAPS v1.1.443,44. The ML phylogenetic trees were visualized using iTOL45.

Temporal Bayesian phylogenetic analysis

To perform temporal analysis of 146 ST34 Salmonella Typhimurium and Salmonella 4,[5],12:i:- strains, cgSNPs were extracted as described above. The transversion model (TVM) of nucleotide substitution was selected as the best-fitting model under the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) using jModelTest 2.1.1046,47. We compared twelve combinations of six clock models and two types of tree priors. The isolation year was used to calibrate the time scale of the tree. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis for each parameter combination was run with a chain length of 108. The best fit parameter combination was selected based on the marginal likelihood estimate using the Path Sampler tool packaged in BEAST 2.6.748., resulting in the combination of ”fast relaxed clock log normal (clock model)” and ”coalescent: exponential pollution (tree prior)”. We performed triplicate BEAST runs and confirmed that all effective sample size values were greater than 200 using Tracer v1.7.249. The three tree files were summarized by LogCombiner v2.6.7 and generated into a single maximum clade credibility tree by TreeAnnotator v2.6.7, followed by visualization with FigTree v1.4.4 (https://github.com/rambaut/figtree/).

Detection of antimicrobial resistance genes and replicon typing

Antimicrobial resistance genes, point mutations related to quinolone resistance, and plasmid replicon genes were searched by ResFinder50, PointFinder51, and PlasmidFinder52, respectively. These databases were handled using staramr v0.10.053 with the following thresholds: minimum length coverage > 90% and nucleotide sequence identity > 90%.

Declaration on ethics in animal research

This study did not involve animal experiments. Salmonella strains were isolated from the organs or feces of animals that died from disease and from the feces of live animals.
