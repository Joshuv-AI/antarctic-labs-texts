A Body Shape Index and asthma prevalence in Chinese middle-aged and older adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Manyu Li, Tianqi Qi, Lei Yan, Linmeng Li, Haoyang Li, Zhenyu Hu, Ling Zhou, Xiaorui Wu, Jichao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsthma is a serious global health issue, and obesity is a crucial risk factor for asthma. A Body Shape Index (ABSI) is an innovative metric for evaluating obesity. This study aimed to investigate the association between ABSI and asthma prevalence among middle-aged and older adults in China.MethodsThis cross-sectional study retrieved data from the third wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). We investigated the relationship between ABSI and asthma prevalence using multivariable logistic regressions, stratified analyses, and restricted cubic splines. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of the results.ResultsThis study included 6,138 participants aged 45 years or older, among whom 365 were identified with asthma. A statistically significant association between ABSI Z-score and asthma was observed (adjusted odds ratio (OR) = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.06–1.42). A non-linear dose-response association was identified, with an inflection point at an ABSI Z-score of -0.523. For individuals with an ABSI Z-score ≥ -0.523, each unit increment in ABSI Z-score was associated with a 71% higher odds of asthma prevalence (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.41–2.08). Significant interactions were observed in subgroups stratified by gender, smoking status, and marital status.ConclusionsThis study found a statistically significant association between ABSI and asthma prevalence in middle-aged and older Chinese. The data indicated that higher ABSI values were associated with increased asthma prevalence. Further prospective studies are warranted to elucidate temporal relationships and underlying causal mechanisms.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04200-7.

Background

Asthma is a prevalent pulmonary disease marked by persistent airway inflammation, reversible airway obstruction, and airway hyperresponsiveness [1]. Asthma is a considerable global public health challenge, affecting 260 million people worldwide and claiming over 1000 lives daily [1, 2]. Despite the promotion of asthma management, the health and socioeconomic burden of asthma remains alarming all over the world. The largest representative nationwide survey revealed that 4.2% of Chinese adults aged 20 and above, equivalent to 45.7 million individuals, had asthma [3]. Huang et al. [3] also demonstrated that asthma prevalence increased with age in the Chinese population. Asthma prevalence stood at 6.0% among individuals over 60 years old, rising to 7.4% in those aged over 70. However, asthma was predominantly regarded as a childhood disorder in the past, and little is known about asthma in older people.

Obesity is a formidable health crisis worldwide, distinguished by skyrocketing prevalence and increasing burden on health care systems. From 1990 to 2021, global obesity prevalence has risen from 5.8% to 14.8% among males and from 10.2% to 20.8% among females [4]. Body mass index (BMI) is the most renowned anthropometric measure of obesity, while the BMI-based obesity evaluation system failed to offer comprehensive insights into individual health status [5]. Krakauer et al. [6] developed a composite indicator, A Body Shape Index (ABSI), with waist circumference (WC), height, and weight to assess the degree of visceral obesity. Elevated BMI has been proven to be the leading contributor to universal asthma burden, and previous research has illustrated a U-shaped association between BMI and asthma prevalence in Chinese individuals aged 45 and older [2, 7]. However, few studies spotlight the link between ABSI and asthma. This study explores the relationship between ABSI and asthma prevalence based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database.

Methods

Study population

All participants in this cross-sectional study were obtained from the CHARLS database. CHARLS is a nationally representative cohort study providing high-quality data on Chinese adults aged 45 and above. The database encompasses extensive data on demographics, family structure, health behaviors, clinical assessments, and more [8]. The CHARLS investigation received approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Data of 21,095 participants were retrieved from the third wave (2015) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Participants eligible for the study were those aged 45 or older with available ABSI data and asthma information. Participants were excluded based on the following criteria: (1) being under 45 years of age; (2) missing information on asthma; (3) missing ABSI data; (4) abnormal BMI data (< 12 or > 65 kg/m²), consistent with the protocol established in previous research [9]; (5) missing physical activity (PA) data; (6) missing information on marital status, smoking and drinking status; (7) missing information on hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and heart diseases. Ultimately, 6138 participants were eligible for the analyses. The detailed selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Variable description

The exposure variable was the ABSI, which was calculated using WC, height, and BMI [6]. In the equations, weight was represented in kilograms, while height and WC were represented in centimeters. ABSI was analyzed both as a continuous variable and in quartiles. The outcome variable was the status of asthma, which was assessed by asking the identical questions: “Have you ever been diagnosed with asthma by a doctor?” [7]. Variables concerning socio-demographic characteristics, chronic diseases, and health habits were considered covariates. Socio-demographic characteristics included age, gender (man or woman), place of residence (rural village or urban community), marital status (currently married or not currently married), and education level (middle school or below, high school or above). Chronic diseases were assessed through a series of standardized questions: “Have you ever been diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes, stroke, or heart disease by a doctor?”. Health habits included smoking (never or ever smoked), drinking (never or ever consumed alcohol in the previous year), and PA, which were evaluated through self-reported questionnaires. The questionnaire of PA assesses whether participants engage in vigorous, moderate, or mild physical activity for a minimum of 10 min weekly. PA levels were classified as vigorous physical activity (VPA), moderate physical activity (MPA), and light physical activity (LPA).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations, while group comparisons were performed using the T-test. Categorical variables were displayed as numbers and percentages, while group comparisons were performed via the Chi-square test and Fisher's exact test. Logistic regression analyses were performed in crude and adjusted models to investigate the associations between ABSI and asthma prevalence. In model I, no adjustments for covariates were applied. Model II was adjusted for variables including age, gender, residence, marital status, and education level. Model III was additionally adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, stroke, heart diseases, smoking, drinking, and PA. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to examine the dose-response relationship between ABSI and asthma prevalence. The maximum likelihood method was applied to calculate the threshold value if a non-linear relationship was verified.

Moreover, stratified analyses were performed to identify potential risk factors that might influence the association between ABSI and asthma. The participants were stratified into various subgroups based on age (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years) [10], gender, place of residence, marital status, education level, and history of chronic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and heart diseases, along with health habits such as smoking, drinking, and PA.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings in the presence of potential bias and unmeasured confounding, two complementary analyses were conducted. Firstly, we calculated E-values to quantify the extent to which unmeasured confounding could affect our results; this analysis estimates the minimum magnitude of association an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both ABSI and asthma to negate the observed results [11]. Secondly, to mitigate concerns of reverse causality, the mediation analysis was also conducted as a sensitivity analysis to examine whether physical activity mediates the relationship between ABSI and asthma. Mediation analysis was performed using the quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo simulation method with 100 simulations, implemented via the “mediation” package in R. The outcome variable was asthma, which is a binary variable, and separate mediation models were fitted for each physical activity mediator. The total, direct, and indirect effects were estimated, along with 95% confidence intervals derived from the simulations. The proportion mediated was calculated to quantify the degree of mediation. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and P < 0.05 demonstrated statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population stratified by asthma status. Participants with asthma were significantly older than those without asthma (P < 0.001) and had a higher mean ABSI Z-score (P < 0.001). The distribution across ABSI quartiles differed significantly between groups (P < 0.001), with a greater proportion of asthma participants in the highest quartile (37.0% vs. 24.3%). The asthma group also had a significantly higher proportion of males (P = 0.001), individuals categorized as “Others” for marital status (P < 0.001), those with middle school and below education (P = 0.040), smokers (P < 0.001), and participants with comorbid hypertension (P = 0.011), diabetes (P = 0.028), and heart diseases (P < 0.001). Furthermore, participants with asthma were less likely to engage in vigorous physical activity (VPA) (P < 0.001) and moderate physical activity (MPA) (P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed for residence type (P = 0.051), drinking status (P = 0.156), history of stroke (P = 0.901), or engagement in light physical activity (LPA) (P = 0.532).

Multivariate regression analyses of ABSI with the asthma prevalence

The association between ABSI and asthma prevalence is displayed in Table 2. The results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis for asthma are presented across three models with progressive covariate adjustment. In its continuous form, ABSI was associated with substantially elevated odds of asthma, though the confidence intervals were notably wide. When ABSI was analyzed as a standardized continuous variable (Z-score), each one-standard-deviation (SD) increment was statistically significantly associated with higher odds of asthma across all adjusted models (Model III OR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.06–1.42, P = 0.006). In quartile comparisons, individuals in Q2 did not exhibit statistically significant differences in asthma prevalence compared to Q1 in any model. However, those in Q3 (Model III OR: 1.69, 95% CI: 1.22–2.36, P = 0.002) and Q4 (Model III OR: 2.01, 95% CI: 1.45–2.79, P < 0.001) demonstrated progressively elevated odds ratios relative to Q1. A statistically significant linear trend across ABSI quartiles was observed in all adjusted models (all P for trend < 0.001), indicating a graded association between higher ABSI levels and asthma prevalence.

Non-linear association between ABSI and asthma

To overcome the limitations of quartile-based categorization, RCS regression was employed in Model III to characterize the precise dose-response association between ABSI Z-score and asthma prevalence (Fig. 2). The spline curve revealed a non-linear, threshold-like relationship (P for non-linearity < 0.05), indicating that the asthma prevalence does not increase uniformly across the ABSI Z-score range. Threshold effect analysis identified a critical inflection point at an ABSI Z-score of -0.523 (Table 3), revealing a distinct shift in the association pattern that was obscured in the linear and quartile models. Specifically, below this threshold value, the association remained relatively stable, with no statistically significant change in asthma prevalence (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.81–1.16, P = 0.759). However, above this value, a steeper association was observed, where each unit increase in ABSI Z-score was statistically significantly associated with a 71% higher prevalence of asthma (OR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.41–2.08, P < 0.001). This non-linear modeling approach offers a more comprehensive depiction of the association compared to the quartile-based model, highlighting a specific range of ABSI Z-scores where the strength of the association with asthma prevalence changes markedly.

Stratified analysis

We conducted stratified analyses based on Model III to investigate whether the relationship between ABSI Z-score and asthma prevalence varied among diverse subgroups (Fig. 3). The overall analysis among 6,138 participants demonstrated a statistically significant association between the ABSI Z-score and asthma prevalence, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.35 (95% CI: 1.16–1.56; P < 0.001). Subgroup analyses revealed that this association was statistically significantly modified by gender (P for interaction = 0.005), marital status (P for interaction = 0.025), and smoking status (P for interaction = 0.004). Specifically, statistically significant associations were observed in men (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.41–2.34; P < 0.001) and in smokers (OR = 1.74, 95% CI: 1.38–2.19; P < 0.001). For marital status, a statistically significant association was observed in married individuals (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.21–1.72; P < 0.001) but not in others (P = 0.812). No statistically significant interactions were found for age, residence, education level, drinking, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, heart diseases, or physical activity levels (VPA, MPA, LPA) (all P for interaction > 0.05). Notably, the association remained statistically significant across most subgroups, with the exception of individuals aged ≥ 65 years, those with a high school education and above, those with diabetes, and those engaging in VPA.

Sensitivity analysis

We performed sensitivity analyses using two approaches to substantiate the robustness of our findings. First, we calculated the E-value to estimate the influence of unmeasured confounding factors. The detailed plot is demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. The E-value of 1.76 indicated that the effect of unmeasured confounders is negligible. We also conducted the mediation effect test on vigorous, moderate, and light physical activity, and the results (Supplementary Table 1) showed that the mediation proportion of the three intensity physical activities ranged from 0.0% to 2.8%. The mediating effects of different intensities of physical activity did not meet the statistical significance standard, and the reverse causal path (ABSI——physical activity——asthma) lacks data support.

Discussion

This study characterized the association between ABSI and asthma prevalence among Chinese adults aged 45 and older. The results show that higher ABSI values were statistically significantly associated with higher asthma prevalence, whether ABSI was analyzed as a continuous or categorical variable. This statistically significant relationship persisted after adjusting for multiple confounding factors. The non-linear association between ABSI Z-score and asthma prevalence was identified using RCS analysis and threshold effect analysis, with a critical inflection point at -0.523. No statistically significant interactions were found among most subgroups, while statistically significant interactions were detected in subgroups categorized by gender, smoking status, and marital status. Sensitivity analyses supported the reliability of the observed associations. Our study suggests that ABSI may be a relevant consideration when evaluating factors related to asthma prevalence.

Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease marked by persistent airway inflammation [1]. Despite significant advances in medical care, the incidence of asthma has strikingly increased worldwide in the past decades [12]. It is imperative for us to gain a thorough understanding of asthma pathogenesis and identify potential factors associated with asthma prevalence. BMI, the most established metric for assessing obesity, has been extensively utilized in previous studies to indicate the association between obesity and asthma [13, 14]. However, BMI has several limitations. It can neither discriminate fat and lean mass nor account for metabolic health and fat distribution [5]. Therefore, ABSI, the new indicator integrated based on height, weight, and WC, was developed to evaluate abdominal fat more precisely [6]. Compared with conventional obesity indicators, ABSI has not been affected by the “obesity paradox” [15]. Meanwhile, it also demonstrates a forceful predictive power for cardiovascular and all-cause mortality risks [16, 17]. Hu et al. [18] investigated the association between ABSI and asthma among Americans. Nevertheless, no study has specifically examined the relationship between ABSI and asthma prevalence, particularly in Chinese individuals aged 45 and above. Consistent with previous research, our findings indicate a significant association between visceral adiposity and asthma among middle-aged and older Chinese individuals. Existing meta-analyses have reported a significant association between obesity and asthma; relevant meta-analyses show that obese individuals have a greater likelihood of using short-acting beta-agonists [19]. Obesity related severe asthma is associated with changes in the microbiome [20], and obesity-related factors might contribute to symptom burden via pathways involving fatty acid metabolism, gut microbiota, and cytokines [21]. It is worth noting that only 12.6% of asthma diagnosis and treatment include obesity management [22]. indicating that there is room for improvement in clinical practice.

Several potential mechanisms for the observed association between visceral adiposity and asthma have been proposed. Firstly, central obesity is linked to alterations in lung mechanics. Thoracic and abdominal fat not only compresses the volume of the thoracic cavity but also alters the movement of the diaphragm, which may be associated with constrained lung expansion [23]. Secondly, it is widely acknowledged that obesity is connected with low-grade systemic inflammation, and visceral adipose tissue is a key source of cytokines and adipokines [5, 24]. Park et al. demonstrated that the concentration of cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, and C-reactive protein, is higher in obese subjects compared with non-obese ones [25]. Meanwhile, IL-6 and TNF-α are crucial in the infiltration of inflammatory cells, including eosinophils and activated mast cells, into the bronchial mucosa. It has been identified that TNF-α promotes the secretion of IL-4 and IL-5 in airway epithelium and the pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1b [26]. These findings suggest that the TNF-α pathway may be upregulated in both asthma and obesity, and elevated TNF-α levels are associated with increased airway inflammation. Furthermore, increased adiposity is associated with elevated level of leptin, one of the adipokines secreted by adipose. Leptin can not only promote the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines but also prompt airway remodeling and angiogenesis via vascular endothelial growth factor [27, 28]. Thus, leptin may have a role in the context of asthma in obese individuals. Thirdly, obesity is also related to elevated oxidative stress. Elevated levels of 8-epi-PGF2α, a biomarker of oxidative stress, have been observed in obese participants [29]. Growing evidence demonstrates that the increased oxidative stress in asthma is associated with airway inflammation [30]. Therefore, the elevated oxidative stress may be part of the mechanism underlying the association between adiposity and asthma. Previous literature has shown that white adipose tissue is associated with upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines through macrophage polarization, which is linked to respiratory oxidative stress and airway hyperresponsiveness [31, 32]. In addition, oxidative stress has been associated with amplifying asthma inflammation through pathways such as mitochondrial dysfunction and nicotinamide adenine phosphate oxidases activity imbalance [30].

Our analysis also verifies a prominent non-linear association between ABSI and asthma prevalence. The prevalence of asthma remained relatively stable at lower ABSI levels, while a marked increase was observed once the ABSI Z-score exceeded − 0.523. This finding suggests a potential non-uniform association between visceral adiposity and asthma prevalence. However, given the cross-sectional nature of this study, this association should be viewed as a statistical observation, and no biological threshold can be inferred from these data. The identified inflection point provides clinicians with an explicit indicator that may facilitate consideration of personalized approaches to health management. Individuals with an ABSI Z-score above this value were found to have a higher prevalence of asthma in our study. The observed statistically significant non-linearity (P for non-linearity < 0.05) indicates that a non-linear model provided a better fit to the data than linear or categorical models, as it revealed an inflection point that would be obscured by arbitrary grouping or an assumption of proportional effects. This highlights ABSI as a promising candidate for future studies focused on developing non-invasive risk assessment tools for asthma in Chinese individuals.

Furthermore, our analysis indicates that the association between ABSI and asthma prevalence appears to be more pronounced in certain participant subgroups. For instance, research has reported an association between sex hormone variations and the course of asthma. Fluctuations in female sex hormones, such as those occurring during adolescence, the menstrual cycle, and pregnancy, have been associated with changes in asthma status. The use of oral contraceptives containing acetyl estradiol/levonorgestrel has been linked to airway inflammation through regulation of glucose transporter-1 [33], while progesterone fluctuations have been associated with alter glucocorticoid sensitivity [34]. On the contrary, previous study suggests a potential protective role for male testosterone in downregulating Th17 response [35]. A higher prevalence of asthma has been reported in men over 45 years with lower testosterone levels [36]. In women aged > 45 years, a decline in female hormone levels with age has been associated with a potential reduction in pro-inflammatory effects. Androgen has been identified as a factor associated with a lower risk of asthma, potentially through decreased neutrophil infiltration and IL-17 A production [36]. Besides, it has been identified that the total visceral adipose tissue volume of older males is significantly higher than that of older females [37]. The difference in fat distribution between men and women may help explain the observed discrepancy in the relationship between ABSI and asthma by gender. Moreover, the prominent higher prevalence of smoking in Chinese men than women may contribute to the observed gender differences in the asthmatic population within this study [38]. Our analysis further indicates that the association between ABSI and asthma prevalence is more pronounced among smokers and drinkers compared to non-users. Currently, we do not have direct substantiation to illustrate this observation; the following factors may be involved in elucidating the finding. In addition to the alteration of oxidative stress, sulfites in alcoholic drinks have been associated with asthma attacks [39]. Notably, a higher incidence of alcohol-induced flushing and bronchoconstriction has been reported in Asians compared to other ethnic groups. This may relate to elevated acetaldehyde level, which has been linked to mast cell degranulation and potential asthma symptoms. The isoenzyme alcohol dehydrogenase 2 (ALDH2) is crucial for acetaldehyde degradation; the deficiency of ALDH2, which is more prevalent in Asian populations, may lead to higher acetaldehyde levels, potentially explaining this ethnic difference [40, 41]. It is widely acknowledged that either former or current tobacco use is a risk factor for asthma. Cigarette smoking is associated with increased oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory factor secretion, which is linked to asthma progression [41, 42].

Our study has several strengths. Firstly, our data was retrieved from the nationally representative authoritative CHARLS dataset, with a large sample size and guaranteed quality control. Additionally, we accounted for numerous confounders based on previous literature and performed stratified and sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our observed associations. Furthermore, identifying the ABSI inflection point via RCS analysis, we indicated a statistically significant non-linear association between ABSI Z-score and asthma prevalence, providing a potential reference for risk stratification and personalized risk assessment. Finally, self-reported asthma diagnosis has shown good reliability in population studies consistent with existing evidence. Multiple population-based cohort studies, such as the Finnish Adult Asthma Study and the French Constance Cohort, have shown that self-reported asthma data obtained through structured questionnaires are highly consistent with clinical diagnosis [43, 44].

There are also some limitations to be considered. Firstly, the cross-sectional study design precludes the establishment of a causal relationship between ABSI and asthma. Further prospective studies are required to clarify temporal direction and potential causality. Secondly, this study was limited to middle-aged and elderly Chinese populations, a group with a high comorbidity burden of asthma and metabolic abnormalities. While this reflects an important public health concern, age and ethnic genetic backgrounds may modify the associations between metabolic indices and asthma, necessitating caution in extrapolating findings to younger populations or other ethnicities. Future studies should validate these findings across diverse age groups, ethnicities, and broader populations to clarify external validity.

Conclusions

This study found a statistically significant association between ABSI and asthma prevalence in middle-aged and older Chinese. The data indicated that higher ABSI values were associated with increased asthma prevalence. These results highlight the need to further investigate ABSI as a potential indicator of asthma, particularly among individuals with ABSI exceeding the threshold value. Further prospective studies are warranted to elucidate temporal relationships and underlying causal mechanisms.
