The impact of COVID-19 on physical and mental health: A longitudinal study
Authors: Yaqin (Joyce) Su, Dong Zhou
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we exploit variation in COVID-19 infections and deaths across Chinese cities to identify the health impacts of COVID-19 based on longitudinal data at the individual level. Our paper provides empirical evidence of the immediate impact of COVID-19 on both physical and mental health. Utilizing a difference-in-differences methodology and focusing on changes in within-individual health condition between pre-COVID-19 and the early stages of COVID-19, we find robust evidence that the COVID-19 pandemic has an adverse effect on self-perceived health condition, chronic illness, sleep, and depression. Our findings are robust to alternative constructions of cities’ exposure to COVID-19, to the exclusion of Wuhan city, which was hit the hardest and had experienced the most stringent lockdowns. Furthermore, accounting for the impact of COVID-19 policies, our results indicate that the decline in physical health can be attributed to the shock of the pandemic, while emotional health is mainly affected by anti-contagion policies.Keywords: COVID-19, Health, Physical health, Mental health, A longitudinal study

Introduction

Over the past few years, the world has witnessed an unprecedented public health emergency - the outbreak of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19). From its onset in late December 2019 to March 2020, the COVID-19 outbreak resulted in 693,282 confirmed cases and 33,106 deaths globally. It wreaked havoc around the world, disrupting people's lives and work: limited social contacts, prohibited social gatherings, schools closed, and businesses halted. Governments around the world implemented various mobility restriction policies, for instance, the US, New Zealand, Asian and European countries (Cantor et al., 2022; Grimes, 2022; Manenti et al., 2020; Sibley et al., 2020). People argue that the pandemic has led to a “second pandemic” of anxiety and depression (Agrawal et al., 2021).

As the country where the epidemic first broke out, China first discovered a large number of infection cases, and as a new virus, there were few treatment methods and management tools available. The country's healthcare system was under extreme strain as the government devoted an enormous amount of resources to contain the virus. At the mean time, China implemented large-scale interventions, such as isolation of infected individuals, contact tracing, quarantine of exposed individuals, travel restrictions, school and workplace closures, and prohibition of mass gatherings, to contain the spread of the virus. These COVID-induced policies disrupted people's work and lives, providing a quasi-natural experimental context in which to study various consequences of those policies. However, to our knowledge no studies have examined the impacts of COVID-19 and its related policies on physical and mental health based on nationally representative datasets from China. This study intends to enrich the literature in this important respect.

Research centered on COVID-19 has emerged rapidly. Impacts of COVID-19 have been intensely studied from various perspectives since the outbreak of this global disease, both due to its inextricable link with everyone's life as well as its profound economic ramifications. On one hand, preventing the spread of COVID-19 took an enormous portion of public attention and medical resources, which often delayed the access to health services for people with a tendency of chronic diseases, resulting in a deterioration of their physical health (Abraham et al., 2020; Cantor 2022). On the other hand, the outbreak of COVID-19 incurred social distancing policies of various degrees, which could adversely affect people's mental health due to social isolation or relational impacts. Besides, the lockdown policies during COVID-19 often led to changes in people's diet and physical activity (Giuntella et al., 2021), such as increased sedentary time, decreased physical activity, increased consumption of unhealthy foods and increased snacking, subsequently exerting a negative effect on physical health.

Many suspect that the pandemic also exerted a detrimental effect on anxiety and depression. The literature, however, has generated rather mixed empirical evidence on the effect of COVID-19 and induced policies on mental health. For instance, based on a longitudinal study in UK, Fancourt et al. (2021) found that the COVID-19 appeared to have improved people's mental health condition, likely due to reduced working hours and government subsidies. Using the data on the entire population of Japan, Takanao and Okamoto (2021) found that the suicide rate first declined and then rebounded in Japan, especially for vulnerable populations such as women, children, and adolescents. Meanwhile, numerous studies provide evidence that people's mental health indeed deteriorated in the wake of the COVID-19 outbreak. Grimes (2022) examines the impact of lockdowns in New Zealand and finds that lockdowns were associated both with reduced life satisfaction and greater loneliness. Sibley et al. (2020) uses the longitudinal New Zealand Attitudes and Values Survey (NZAVS) and find that the pandemic/lockdown group reported higher rates of mental distress. When it comes to health care utilization, Cantor et al. (2022) find that the social distancing policies in response to the COVID-19 pandemic led to reductions in healthcare utilization, such as the use of preventive care, elective care, and the number of weekly visits to physician offices, hospitals and other health care-related facilities. Taken together, empirical results vary widely, depending on the country in which the analysis is being performed, the sample used for the estimation (panel or cross-section), and the time window of interest in the study.

In this paper we examine the collective impact of the pandemic and related policies on individuals' physical and mental health, considering it's difficult to completely separate the effect of the pandemic itself as a public health shock from that of the anti-contagion policies. We construct the regional intensity of COVID-19 based on the total number of COVID-19 confirmed cases and deaths for each prefectural-level city as of June 2020. Previous studies have used these data to examine disparity in COVID-19 case rates (Adhikari et al., 2020; Brown & Ravallion, 2020). Our data are from the National Health Commission, the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and public medical and health institutions, which tracks the total number of COVID-19 cases and deaths for each city. Our identification strategy is essentially a difference-in-differences approach based on the reasoning that cities that had more COVID-related cases and deaths experienced larger health shocks and greater strains on assessing health facilities, as well as more aggressive policy interventions. Therefore, we expect to find stronger impacts of COVID-19 outbreaks in these cities. Meanwhile, we rely on individual-level panel data to analyze the changes in the physical and mental health conditions of the same individual observed in both pre- and post-pandemic periods.

Leveraging variation in exposure to the COVID-19 pandemic across cities, we examine the difference in individual physical and mental health conditions before and after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, conditional on individual and year fixed effects. Our results show that the severity of the COVID-19 pandemic is associated with worsened self-perceived health status and a higher incidence of chronic illness. This may be attributable to the fact that utilization of preventive care and visits to hospitals decreased substantially after the onset of the COVID-19 (Cantor et al., 2022). Our results also show that the COVID-19 pandemic has a negative impact on individual mental health; in particular, people are likely to feel depressed following the onset of COVID-19. Our findings are robust to different constructions of COVID-19 intensity measures as well as a battery of falsification tests. Furthermore, heterogeneity analysis shows that people with less education tend to have worsened perceived health, as well as physical health (e.g., insomnia) and mental health (e.g., feeling depressed). Somewhat surprisingly, elderly populations have been less affected by the pandemic compared with their younger counterparts.

This paper contributes to the existing literature in several respects. First, leveraging the variation of COVID-19 infected cases and deaths across cities, we provide plausible causal impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on people's self-rated health condition, as well as physical and mental health. Second, we use difference-in-differences methodology combined with two-way fixed effects to control for unobserved individual heterogeneity as well as secular trends, providing more robust results. Third, we investigate whether the effect is mainly due to the public heath shock itself, or the zero-COVID policies that involved strict containment measures such as lockdowns and mobility restrictions.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 provides the research background. Section 3 describes the data and presents descriptive statistics. Section 4 presents the identification strategy. Section 5 presents main results, performs robustness checks and heterogeneity analysis. Section 6 concludes.

Research background

The massive outbreak of COVID-19 began in Wuhan, Hubei Province in late December 2019. To effectively control the source of infection and curb the spread of the epidemic, Wuhan went into a state of lockdown on Jan 23, 2020. City buses, subways, ferries, and long-distance passenger transport in Wuhan were suspended. Passages from the airport and railway station were temporarily blocked. A few hours after the implementation of travel restrictions in Wuhan, other surrounding cities also implemented travel restrictions one after another. Ultimately, another 15 cities in Hubei implemented travel restrictions. The lockdown was strictly enforced until April 8, 2020, affecting a total of about 57 million people in the province. Subsequently, areas of the country where Covid-19 cases were detected have implemented varying levels of lockdown interventions to curb the spread of the virus.

China's drastic measures to contain the virus proved to be effective (Fang et al., 2020),. Overall, the epidemic surged from December 2019 to February 2020, dropped sharply in March, increased slightly in April, and then gradually subsided. During the first outbreak of the pandemic from December 2019 to March 2020, there were 81,071 confirmed cases and 4,633 deaths, unevenly distributed across 31 provinces, cities and districts. As shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2, the severity of the pandemic varied significantly across different regions. The epidemic was more severe in the eastern region, with Wuhan, Shanghai, Beijing, Yichang and Guangzhou being the most affected cities.

Until the end of 2022, the Chinese government had resolutely enforced a “zero COVID” policy aimed at eliminating the spread of the virus in the country and restoring normal economic and social activities. It was one of the few countries that adhered to this strategy, while many Asian-pacific neighbors had abandoned it. However, the zero-transmission policy may have taken a toll on China's economy and society, though the empirical evidence is still unclear on this regard.

The COVID-19 epidemic was characterized as a major public health shock, and the emergency level was divided into four levels: first-level (especially major), second-level (major), third-level (large) and fourth-level (general). The epidemic response of various provinces and cities was different due to the severity of the epidemic situation. In China, regions were divided into low, medium and high-risk levels according to the differences in confirmed COVID-19 cases, with different prevention and control strategies being implemented based upon those differences. According to the classification criteria, the regions with newly confirmed case within 14 days and cumulative infection of less than 50 cases, or cumulative infection of more than 50 cases while there was no gathering event, were classified as medium-risk areas. If there was outbreak at gathering events and more than 50 cumulative infected cases, the region was classified as high-risk areas. In the severely affected areas, policies of close contact tracing, monitoring, testing and reporting were strictly enforced. Therefore, we expect that individuals in high-risk areas were subject to more stringent policy interventions, therefore were more affected by the outbreak of the pandemic.

We utilize the variation in COVID-19 infection cases and deaths across cities to identify the impact of COVID-19 and the related policies on individuals’ physical and mental health. Although related empirical evidence starts to emerge for the US, UK, Germany, and other Asian countries, the evidence from China, the country that was the first to respond and implemented arguably the most stringent anti-contagion polices, is still lacking. We intend to fill this gap.

Data and descriptive statistics

Our analysis is based on the data from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS), which is a nationally representative, large-scale, longitudinal survey project launched by the Institute of Social Science Survey (ISSS) of Peking University, China. The surveys collected data at the individual, family, and community levels and are designed to track changes in Chinese society, economy, demography, education, and health, etc. The data were officially launched in 2010, and thus far tracked in 2012, 2014, 2016, 2018 and 2020. The original sample covers 25 provinces (municipalities directly under the central government and autonomous regions) in China with a targeted sample size of 16,000 households. It focuses on the economic and non-economic well-being of the Chinese population, and contains rich information on economic activities, educational attainment, family relationships and dynamics, migration, physical and mental health, and so on.

This paper utilizes the 2016, 2018 and 2020 waves of Chinese Family Panel Studies (CFPS). COVID-19 broke out in China at the end of 2019, with infections concentrated in the spring of 2020. The lockdown was lifted on April 8th, 2020 in Wuhan, where the coronavirus pandemic started. To some extent, it represented the end of the first round of the outbreak of COVID-19 in China. The 2020 wave of CFPS was launched shortly after June, mainly during July and August 2020. This is a period of temporary relief from COVID-19, and relaxation of anti-contagion measures. The timing of the surveys covers pre-COVID waves (2016 and 2018) and post-COVID wave (2020), which enables us to explore the short-run impacts of the pandemic on people's health outcomes and behaviors.

Specifically, we construct a balanced panel dataset through matching personal identification numbers from each wave. Meanwhile, we collect coronavirus information (the cumulative number of infections and deaths as of June 2020) released by regional and national health commissions from Tencent news1 and Dingxiang Garden Database2 in 2020. Information is collected from the National Health Commission, the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and public medical and health institutions at provincial and city level. Utilizing the county codes released in the 2010 wave of CFPS, we assign the city-level coronavirus infection cases and deaths to each individual.3 In sum, there are about 12,692 individuals followed over time across 162 counties.

Wuhan has the highest recorded infection cases of 50,340 and deaths of 3,869, constituting an outlier from the remaining cities. Our sample includes individuals aged 16 to 95, 49% are male, and 71% have a rural household registration (referred to as “hukou”). The majority of individuals have an education level below senior high school, and 84% of individuals are married.

Identification strategy

Our identification is essentially a difference-in-differences (DiD) estimation. This strategy compares the health outcomes of individuals before and after the COVID-19 outbreak across cities with varying degrees of COVID-19 exposure. This identification strategy is widely used with observational data in the literature (Duflo, 2001; Lu & Yu, 2015; Qian, 2008). Specifically, we use the 2020 wave to measure outcomes after treatment and compared with outcomes before the COVID-19 for the same person. Meanwhile, with the cumulative numbers of COVID-19 infections and deaths as of June 2020, we can distinguish different intensities of COVID-19 across cities and capture regional variations of treatment.

Intensityc reflects the time-invariant severity of COVID-19 in city c, constructed based on the cumulative number of COVID-19 cases or deaths in the city as of June 2020. Postt is an indicator variable that equals 1 for observations in wave 2020, and 0 for waves 2016 and 2018. δ is the Difference-in-Differences (DiD) coefficient, which shows the integrated impact of COVID-19 on people's physical and mental health. If this coefficient is negative, then we find evidence that individuals in cities that had more COVID-19 infections experienced greater decline in health. X denotes a vector of time-varying individual characteristics, including age, a full set of dummies for educational attainment and marital status. The estimation equation includes individual fixed effects, μi, which capture time invariant differences in individual characteristics that may affect health outcomes. Also, we control for year fixed effects, φt, which capture temporal factors that affect all individuals. Additionally, we control a set of time-varying city attributes (i.e., logarithm of GDP per capita, logarithm of population, share of employment in tertiary industry and unemployment rate) to tease out their potential confounding effects from the treatment effect. Lastly, εict is an idiosyncratic error term. Robust standard errors are clustered at the individual level.

Estimation results

General assessment of health condition

We begin by estimating the baseline model focusing on self-rated health status as the outcome variable. The self-rated health is an ordinal variable that takes values from 1 to 5, corresponding to worst to best ratings, and it reveals a general assessment of personal physical and mental conditions. Intensity reflects cities' exposure to the COVID-19 outbreak, proxied by five quantiles based on the cumulative infection cases as of June 2020. Nevertheless, we will show that our results are not sensitive to alternative constructions of the intensity variable. For example, using the logarithm of infections and deaths generates similar results. Table 2 reports the estimates. Column (1) of Table 2 reports the estimation results based on two-way fixed effects model, where the same individual's health status is compared for pre- and post-COVID-19 periods. The results show that the COVID-19 outbreak has a negative and significant effect on people's self-rated health. Based on three waves of data from 2016 to 2020, the DiD estimate indicates that COVID-19 leads to a 1.6 percentage points reduction in perceived health status, and the effect is statistically significant at the 5% level.

To illustrate whether the results are different had the individual fixed effects not been accounted for, we present the estimates using pooled cross-sectional samples, while adding two sets of dummies variables to control for birth cohorts fixed effects and city fixed effects. These additional controls would not be necessary in an individual fixed effects setting because they are generally time invariant for a given individual. As shown in column (2) Table 2, the cross-sectional estimate is negative and significant at the 1% level, and its magnitude is larger than the two-way fixed effects estimate. Therefore, the results suggest that controlling for individual fixed effects generate more robust estimates.

To lend credibility to our findings, we perform falsification tests by including only pre-2020 waves, before the pandemic actually occurred, and impose the Post dummy equal to 1 for observations from 2018 wave. If the negative effects still show up in these placebo estimations, then our previous findings can be spurious and simply driven by some confounding factors or trends that were not accounted for in the model of estimation. The estimates are shown in column (5) and (6) of Table 2. They are close to zero and highly insignificant, regardless of whether balanced panel or cross-sectional data are used. These results lend support to our baseline findings, indicating that the identified effect indeed captures the impact of COVID-19, which would not have shown up had the pandemic not occurred.

Although the adverse effects are statistically significant, they are not alarming in terms of magnitude. As a standard way to gauge the magnitude of our estimated effects of the COVID-19, we examine the differences in self-assessed health status between the pre- and post-pandemic periods as a percentage of the standard deviation of health variable. Given that our dependent variable is ordinal, the results suggest that for each quintile increase in the severity of cities' COVID-19 infection, people's perceived health condition deteriorates by 0.016 (on a 1–5 scale). Compared with the dependent variable's sample mean and standard deviation of 2.92 and 1.219, this decline in self-evaluated health status is equivalent to 1.3 percent of the standard deviation. To provide some context for the magnitude of our estimates, Grimes (2022) analyze how lockdowns in New Zealand affected people's life satisfaction and loneliness, and find the drop in life satisfaction represents approximately 12 percent of a standard deviation. Similarly, focusing on pandemic policy and life satisfaction in Europe, Clark and Lepinteur (2022) used standardized life satisfaction and find that one standard-deviation higher Stringency Index is estimated to reduce life satisfaction by 5 percent of a standard deviation. Our estimates are smaller than theirs probably due to the efficient containment strategies implemented during the first wave of outbreak in China. Another explanation may be that the outcomes evaluated are different across empirical studies and health status is just one aspect of overall life satisfaction.

Physical health outcomes

Next, we turn to examine the impact of COVID-19 on a set of physical outcomes. Specifically, a group of variables are constructed indicating whether an individual felt sick in the past two weeks (Illness), had chronic diseases within 6 months (Chronic), had sleeping difficulties (Insomnia), including two additional behavior variables that supposedly affect health, namely whether an individual smoked in the past month (Smoking) and drank more than 3 times in the past month (Drinking). The estimates are reported in Table 3. We present the DiD estimates, based on balanced panel data, pooled cross-sectional data, and placebo regressions similar to what has been performed in Table 2. The DiD estimates indicate that the COVID-19 outbreak is positively related to the incidence of having chronic diseases and experiencing sleeping difficulty, while having no significant impacts on getting sick, drinking or smoking behaviors. Cross-sectional estimates are qualitatively similar, although they seem to indicate the COVID-19 pandemic leads to a decrease in smoking. This effect disappears when within-individual comparisons are made, as in the fixed effects model. Note that none of the placebo estimates based on observations restricted to the pre-COVID periods turn out to be significant, indicating that the DiD estimates are not picking up the effects of some confounding factors other than the COVID-19 pandemic.

In sum, we find that residents in areas with more infections are more likely to suffer from chronic disease and insomnia. It is likely due to the fact that containing COVID-19 has taken an enormous portion of public attention and medical resources, which could delay the access to health services for people with a tendency toward chronic diseases, causing their condition to deteriorate. Meanwhile, people may choose to avoid hospitals or delay their doctor visits to avoid getting infected. This avoided or deferred care can cause a lack of preventive treatment of their illness, leading to a diagnosis of chronic disease. On the other hand, the lockdown policies during the COVID-19 could lead to changes in people's diet and physical activity, such as increased sedentary time, decreased physical activity, increased consumption of unhealthy foods and increased snacking (Hu et al., 2020), thereby exerting a negative effect on people's physical health and causing sleeping problems. Perceived risk through media influence, coupled with increased social isolation, may also partly explain sleeping difficulties.

Psychological health outcomes

Up to this point, we have examined the impact of COVID-19 on physical health. Many researchers have shown concerns for the impacts on mental health, due to policies aimed to contain the virus such as social distancing and lockdowns. Although the entire country in China underwent strict regulation, we assume that cities that saw more infections and deaths due to COVID-19 were subject to more stringent monitoring and restraint, thereby exerting a greater impact on people's psychological health.

Table 4 reports the regression results using mental health outcomes as the focal dependent variables. In particular, we construct 5 ordinal variables with values ranging from 1 to 4, corresponding to increasing frequency of emotional manifestations such as: feeling depressed, feeling sad, feeling lonely, having difficulty accomplishing things, and feeling that life cannot go on. As shown in panel A of Table 4, the estimates based on balanced panel data reveal that the COVID-19 pandemic led to an increase in the likelihood of feeling depressed, while the effects on the other measures of mental health are insignificant. The cross-sectional estimates seem to indicate that the effect on feeling sad is significant, as well as difficulty accomplishing things and feeling life cannot continue; however, these effects disappear once individual fixed effects are controlled for. Following a similar reasoning, we perform placebo tests to see whether the identified effects of the pandemic can be attributed to shocks across different time periods or other confounding factors. The panel C of Table 4 shows that none of the effects are showing up when the sample periods are restricted to the pre-COVID-19 periods, supporting the validity of our main findings.

Robustness checks

We perform a series of robustness checks to support the validity of our findings. First, to illustrate that our findings are not sensitive to how the intensity of COVID-19 is constructed, we measure the intensity of COVID-19 exposure by taking the log of the total number of infected cases and deaths, respectively. Panel A of Table 5 shows that our results are very robust to this alternative measurement. The DiD estimate based on the log of infected cases is essentially the same as the baseline estimate. Meanwhile, the DiD estimate based on the log of cumulative deaths indicates that the COVID-19 pandemic reduces the self-rated health status by 3 percentage points, a magnitude larger than the baseline coefficient. Second, to show our results are not sensitive to the ordinal nature of our main dependent variable, we construct a health dummy variable that equals 1 for those who rate themselves as very healthy or in excellent health, and 0 otherwise. Column (3) of Table 5 shows that the effect of COVID-19 remains significant at the 5% level when a dichotomous dependent variable is used. Additionally, we standardize the ordinal health variable to have a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, the estimated coefficient show in column (4) indicates that the COVID-19 pandemic led to a 1.3 percent decline in perceived health condition, identical to the magnitude interpretation we calculated manually. Third, Wuhan city was where the pandemic started and had a substantially higher number of COVID-19-related cases and deaths, accompanied by far more stringent lockdowns and regulation policies. To check the possibility that our results are driven by this outlier city, we exclude Wuhan from the sample and re-estimate the equation. Column (5)–(7) of Table 5 indicate that excluding Wuhan has little impact on the DiD estimates; both balanced panel and cross-sectional estimates are negative and statistically significant, while the placebo test fails to generate a significant effect.

Heterogeneity analysis

Next, we examine the heterogeneous impacts of the pandemic by gender, hukou status, age groups and education levels. The estimates are reported in Table 7. Panel A of Table 7 focuses on perceived health condition. First, the negative impact of COVID-19 is significant only for men but not for women. The rest of Table 7 reports the heterogeneous effects using chronic disease, insomnia and feeling depressed as separate dependent variables; the results are rather mixed. The effects on feelings depressed are similar to those on perceived health, except that women appeared to be more affected. When using insomnia as the dependent variable, the results show that people over 60 years old had greater difficulty sleeping. Furthermore, regressions using chronic diseases as the dependent variables show a quite different pattern: while there is no significant difference in the impact of COVID-19 by people's education levels, the impact on chronic diseases is stronger for women, for those with rural hukou, and for those younger than 60 years of age.

To summarize, the heterogeneity analysis shows that the less educated group generally experienced a decline in perceived health condition, as well as a higher likelihood of having sleeping difficulty and feeling depressed. In line with Wang et al., (2020), women were more likely to be affected in terms of chronic disease, experiencing sleeping difficulties and feeling depressed. Somewhat surprisingly, except having more difficulty falling sleep (insomnia), elderly populations were less impacted by the pandemic in perceived health condition, chronic disease and depression compared with their younger counterparts. Viewing the effects on all health outcomes, there is no consistent evidence suggesting gender differences, or rural-urban COVID-induced disparities. The determinants and disparities of different health outcomes related the COVID-19 pandemic deserve more future research.

Conclusion

This study aims to understand immediate impacts of the pandemic severity and its related policies on health outcomes in China based on longitudinal data at the individual level. Empirically, we exploit regional exposure with the COVID-19 infected cases and deaths across cities and used a difference-in-differences methodology to evaluate within-individual changes in health conditions between pre- and post-COVID periods. We find robust evidence indicating that the COVID-19 pandemic has an adverse effect on self-perceived health condition, chronic disease, sleep, and depression. These results are robust to alternative constructions of cities’ exposure to the COVID-19, to the exclusion of Wuhan city, which was hit the hardest and had the most stringent lockdowns during the pandemic. Furthermore, the heterogeneity results show that adverse impacts of COVID-19 are more pronounced for those who are less educated.

Our findings add new evidence to a growing and insightful literature that examines various consequences of COVID-19. However, we acknowledge a few caveats: First, based on current identification, we cannot completely separate the impact of Covid-19 itself from that of lockdowns or social distancing policies. Therefore, our paper, at best, identifies the overall impact of Covid-19 on health, both direct and derivative. Second, although robustly identified, the estimated effects appear to be small. The costs of the pandemic at the individual level may have been minimized due to effective government crisis management during the first wave of the outbreak. Third, our estimates are based on data from the early stages of the pandemic and can best be interpreted as the immediate health impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic.

China took remarkable measures to contain the transmission of COVID-19, which proved to be quite effective. However, the “zero COVID” policies involve mass testing, contact tracing and mobility restrictions, which come at a price for the economy and society as whole. Our findings indicate that people's physical health and mental health indeed declined as a result of the COVID-19 and related policies in the short run. Future work will evaluate the various impacts on China's economy. Another promising direction is to explore whether the impacts on mental health persist in the longer-run after the abolishment of zero-COVID policies, when newer data provide such opportunities.
