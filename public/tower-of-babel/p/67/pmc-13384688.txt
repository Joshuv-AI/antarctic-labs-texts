Beyond LAMS: Innovative EUS-Guided Needle Drainage With Wall-Apposed Suction Strategy for a Giant Perisplenic Pancreatic Pseudocyst
Authors: Yeshika Thapa, Carson Cornell Creamer, Toufic Dabit, Farah Haneyah, Yaseen Perbtani, Tony S. Brar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Splenic complications occur in approximately 2% of patients with chronic pancreatitis. Standard endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided transmural drainage requires a Doppler-free window and may be unsafe when collaterals traverse the intended tract. We report a 42-year-old woman with alcoholic chronic pancreatitis and splenic vein thrombosis presented with a perisplenic pseudocyst that enlarged from 10.8 to 18.9 cm over 1 month. EUS confirmed intervening vasculature precluding stent placement; a narrow Doppler-free path permitted 19-gauge needle access. Continuous aspiration through a 3-way stopcock system evacuated approximately 1,000 mL with marked decompression. This case highlights EUS-guided needle decompression when vasculature constrains conventional drainage.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic pseudocysts are encapsulated fluid collections that persist beyond 4 weeks and are well-recognized complications of chronic pancreatitis.1,2 The incidence rate of pseudocysts with chronic pancreatitis ranges from 20% to 40%.3 Although most pseudocysts stay peripancreatic, however, can sometimes spread to nearby organs, making treatment much more difficult.

Splenic involvement from pancreatic pseudocysts occurs in approximately 2% of patients with chronic pancreatitis and is associated with risk factors such as splenic vein occlusion and distal pancreatic pseudocysts.4,5 In patients with both risk factors, the splenic complications can reach up to 18%.4

Current American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy guidelines conditionally recommend endoscopic drainage for symptomatic pancreatic pseudocysts, with endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided transmural drainage using plastic stents or lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMS) as the preferred approach.6 However, splenic vein thrombosis with associated vulnerable intervening vasculature can complicate conventional drainage approaches, often necessitating modified or alternative techniques.

We present a challenging instance of rapidly growing giant splenic-adjacent pancreatic pseudocysts, where vasculature intervened to complicate traditional endoscopic procedures and require novel techniques.

CASE REPORT

A 42-year-old woman with alcoholic chronic pancreatitis and chronic splenic vein thrombosis presented with 1 week of progressively worsening left upper quadrant radiating to the flank and shoulder. She was hemodynamically stable and had no systemic signs of infection. One month prior, her computed tomography had revealed a 10.8 cm perisplenic fluid collection and an additional cystic lesion near the pancreatic tail. EUS fine-needle aspiration of the pancreatic cystic lesion was consistent with a pancreatic pseudocyst (amylase >2.6 million U/L and carcinoembryonic antigen 44 ng/mL). Attempted percutaneous drainage yielded only 60 mL of blood, suggesting a hematoma rather than a drainable collection at that time.

During the current admission, repeat computed tomography demonstrated rapid enlargement of the perisplenic pancreatic pseudocyst from 10.8 cm to 18.9 cm, with mediastinal mass effect and associated left pleural effusion as shown in Figure 1. Given the rapid enlargement, symptoms, and prior reaccumulation after percutaneous drainage, endoscopic management was pursued. EUS identified a giant anechoic cystic collection adjacent to the spleen measuring greater than 100 × 100 mm, with a thick mature wall and no solid debris, septations, mass, or clear pancreatic duct communication as shown in Figure 2. Color Doppler revealed multiple intervening vessels between the gastric wall and the collection, precluding safe EUS-guided transmural cystogastrostomy with either LAMS or double-pigtail plastic stents. A narrow Doppler-free trajectory was identified, that was considered suitable for needle access but not for tract dilation or stent deployment.

Under continuous EUS visualization, a 19-gauge needle was advanced into the collection through this avascular window. A closed-circuit suction system was assembled using the 19-gauge needle hub, IV extension tubing, a three-way stopcock, and a 60 mL Luer-lock syringe (Figure 3). This configuration enabled continuous aspiration by alternating between syringe filling and emptying through stopcock rotation, maintaining stable needle position without disconnection and thereby maintaining stable wall apposition throughout the procedure as demonstrated in Video 1. The setup was assembled at the bedside in under 2 minutes without technical difficulty. Approximately 1,000 mL of turbid brown fluid was evacuated in under 10 minutes, with immediate decompression of the collection and no immediate bleeding or hemodynamic instability. Intraprocedural EUS confirmed progressive decompression of the collection during aspiration (Figure 2).

The 19-gauge FNA needle hub is connected to standard IV extension tubing, which attaches to a three-way stopcock. A 60 mL Luer-lock syringe is connected to 1 port for manual aspiration, while the third port allows intermittent venting and syringe exchange without disconnecting the needle. This closed-circuit configuration enables continuous aspiration by alternating between syringe filling and emptying through stopcock rotation, maintaining stable wall apposition throughout the procedure. EUS, endoscopic ultrasound; FNA, fine needle aspiration.

A second, separate pancreatic body pseudocyst measuring 65 × 40 mm had a safe Doppler-free transmural window and was drained using EUS-guided cystogastrostomy with a 15 × 10 mm LAMS, with excellent apposition and brisk drainage. No postprocedure cross-sectional imaging was obtained during this hospitalization. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was discharged the following day with outpatient follow-up and planned interval imaging.

DISCUSSION

Splenic or perisplenic extension of pancreatic pseudocysts is rare and may result from enzymatic erosion, direct extension of peripancreatic fluid, and venous congestion from splenic vein obstruction. This extension can lead to subcapsular collections, intrasplenic pseudocysts, hemorrhage, mass effect, or even rupture.4,7 The development of a gigantic splenic-adjacent pseudocyst, as seen in our patient, is very uncommon. In our patient, the collection enlarged rapidly to nearly 19 cm resulting in a significant mediastinal mass effect, highlighting the potential severity of this presentation.

Management of pancreatic pseudocysts has evolved over time, ranging from surgery toward less invasive endoscopic approaches.2,3 EUS-guided transmural drainage is generally preferred when collections are mature and closely apposed to the gastric or duodenal wall. However, safe stent placement requires a suitable Doppler-free window.6,8 In patients with splenic vein thrombosis, perigastric and perisplenic collaterals may traverse the intended access tract and substantially increase bleeding risk, making standard transmural drainage unsafe.1,8

This case highlights an important technical distinction; a collection may be accessible by needle yet still be unsafe for transmural stent placement. In our patient, Doppler identified only a narrow avascular trajectory, sufficient for passage of a 19-gauge needle under continuous EUS guidance, but not adequate for electrocautery-enhanced access, tract dilation, or deployment of either LAMS or double-pigtail plastic stents. For that reason, needle decompression was selected as a lower-profile approach to relieve mass effect while minimizing the risk of vascular injury. This type of situation is mentioned in current guidelines; however, there is very limited direction on how to proceed when no safe window for standard transmural stenting exists. In such situations, management must be individualized, weighing procedural risk against the morbidity associated with surgical or percutaneous alternatives.

Historically, splenic-associated pseudocysts have needed to be managed surgically or percutaneously, particularly when anatomy limits endoscopic therapy. However, compared with endoscopic therapy, both methods have higher morbidity, longer recovery times, and a higher chance of external fistula.9,10 Large or complicated splenic-adjacent pseudocysts often necessitate splenectomy or partial splenectomy as definitive management.10,11 Although percutaneous sclerotherapy has shown favorable outcomes for primary epithelial splenic cysts,12 its role in pancreatic pseudocysts with perisplenic extension remains uncertain, as ongoing pancreatic ductal pathology and splenic vein thrombosis may predispose to fluid reaccumulation despite local sclerosant therapy. In our patient, the persistent pancreatic inflammatory process and splenic vein thrombosis with collateral formation raised concern that sclerotherapy alone would not address the underlying etiology of fluid reaccumulation.

In this case, EUS-guided needle aspiration with continuous suction through a three-way stopcock provided controlled decompression of the collection while maintaining stable access and avoiding a higher-risk transmural intervention. Anatomy-guided EUS needle decompression of splenic-adjacent pseudocysts is rarely reported due to location and technical complexity.13 This approach should be viewed as an anatomy-driven decompressive or temporizing strategy rather than a replacement for standard pseudocyst drainage. Notably, this technique requires only a standard 19-gauge fine-needle aspiration needle, IV extension tubing, a three-way stopcock, and can be assembled at the bedside in minutes, which may be advantageous in resource-limited settings.

An important limitation of this approach is uncertain long-term durability, as simple aspiration may be followed by recurrence. Ongoing clinical and radiographic follow-up is therefore necessary to assess for reaccumulation and determine whether additional endoscopic, percutaneous, or surgical management is required. Overall, this case emphasizes the importance of careful Doppler-based EUS planning in patients with complex vascular anatomy and suggests that needle decompression may be a reasonable option when conventional transmural drainage is not safely feasible.
