The “good cancer” paradox: Decisional conflict and unmet support needs in patients with thyroid cancer facing surgical decisions—A mixed-methods study
Authors: Juan Deng, Hongyu Xiang, Zhihui Chen, Qin Deng, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess decisional conflict levels and predictors among patients with differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) choosing between open and endoscopic thyroidectomy, and to explore their decision-making experiences and decisional support needs.MethodsA convergent parallel mixed-methods design guided by the Ottawa Decision Support Framework was employed at three tertiary hospitals in China (June 2024–June 2025). The quantitative strand surveyed 696 patients using the Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS) and Control Preferences Scale. The qualitative strand comprised semi-structured interviews with 22 purposively sampled patients, analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis. Data were integrated through joint display and meta-inference.ResultsOver half of participants (57.9%) experienced clinically significant decisional conflict (DCS > 37.5). Lower income (β = −0.150, P < 0.001) and preference for active decision-making (β = 0.117, P = 0.004) independently predicted higher conflict. Qualitative analysis identified five themes: information needs beyond routine clinical disclosure, navigating competing values, barriers to effective decision-making, the “good cancer” paradox: when reassurance becomes invalidation, and unmet decisional support needs. Contrary to intent, the “good cancer” framing served to invalidate rather than alleviate patients’ distress.ConclusionsPatients with DTC experience substantial decisional conflict when choosing between surgical approaches. Decision support interventions, particularly nurse-led decision coaching, should address socioeconomic barriers, assess decisional readiness beyond stated preferences, and validate patients’ concerns rather than inadvertently dismissing them through prognostic reassurance.Keywords: Decisional conflict, Thyroid cancer, Surgical decision-making, Mixed methods, Shared decision-making, Oncology nursing

Introduction

Thyroid cancer incidence has increased by 313% globally over the past four decades,1 with China experiencing particularly rapid growth.2 Differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) accounts for over 95% of thyroid malignancies and is frequently characterized as a “good cancer” due to 5-year survival rates exceeding 98%.3 Concurrently, advances in endoscopic thyroid surgery have expanded treatment options for eligible patients with DTC.4 In China, endoscopic thyroidectomy has been rapidly adopted, with over 85% of surveyed tertiary institutions now offering the procedure. However, endoscopic approaches still account for less than 20% of total thyroid surgeries in the majority of centers,5 indicating that most eligible patients continue to face a genuine choice between approaches. This expanded option set introduces additional complexity to clinical decision-making. When both open and endoscopic approaches yield comparable oncological outcomes,6,7 the decision becomes primarily a preference-sensitive choice shaped by patients’ personal values, rather than a purely clinical judgment.8,9 Patients must weigh trade-offs involving cosmetic outcomes, complications, recovery time, and costs.4 The 2025 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines explicitly recognize surgical approach selection as a shared decision-making process,8 yet patients are often ill-prepared for such preference-sensitive choices.

When patients face preference-sensitive decisions without adequate support, decisional conflict commonly results.10 Decisional conflict refers to the uncertainty that arises when choices involve risk, potential loss, or challenges to personal values.11,12 Clinically significant decisional conflict can delay treatment and is strongly linked to subsequent decision regret.13,14 These patients also report heightened anxiety,15,16 are more likely to discontinue treatment,12,17 and tend to blame physicians when outcomes prove unfavorable.18 For thyroid cancer patients, these consequences may be compounded by the “good cancer” paradox: When a cancer diagnosis is framed as “good”, patients may perceive that their fears are disproportionate and therefore illegitimate, a mismatch that can function as emotional invalidation rather than reassurance.19, 20, 21 Patients who face preference-sensitive surgical choices may therefore be least likely to seek or receive adequate decisional support.

Despite its clinical significance, research on surgical decision-making in DTC remains limited. Existing studies have focused largely on the choice between surgery and active surveillance for low-risk papillary microcarcinoma,22,23 not on the increasingly common decision between surgical approaches. Most evidence originates from Western populations, with few studies examining decision-making among Chinese patients.24 In China, treatment choices are commonly negotiated within families rather than made individually, reflecting Confucian norms of collective deliberation.25, 26, 27 A recent multicenter study of 1422 Chinese cancer patients found that 74.3% expected family members to participate equally in treatment decisions.28 This family-centered model can create tension with shared decision-making approaches that emphasize patient–clinician dyads.26, 27, 28 Additionally, significant socioeconomic disparities in health literacy across China’s urban–rural divide may compound informational barriers for patients from lower-income backgrounds.29,30 How patients experience this choice, including the psychological impact of the “good cancer” label, has not been qualitatively examined.

The Ottawa Decision Support Framework (ODSF) provides a systematic approach for understanding health-related decision-making.10 The framework posits that unmet decisional needs (inadequate knowledge, unclear values, and insufficient support) contribute to decisional conflict, which in turn affects decision quality and health outcomes.9,10 Sociodemographic factors and decision-making preferences predict decisional conflict across surgical populations.12,15 However, DTC patients face a qualitatively different decision: choosing between two approaches with comparable oncological outcomes, rather than deciding whether to undergo surgery at all. Whether the same predictors apply in this context remains untested. A mixed-methods approach guided by this framework lends itself well to this inquiry: quantitative methods can measure the prevalence and predictors of decisional conflict, while qualitative methods can illuminate experiences that standardized instruments may miss.

Guided by the ODSF, we aimed to assess the level and predictors of decisional conflict among patients with DTC choosing between open and endoscopic thyroidectomy, and explore patients’ decision-making experiences, including their information needs, value trade-offs, psychological responses, and decisional support needs. These findings will guide the development of tailored decision support interventions for this population.

Methods

Study design

This study constitutes the needs assessment phase of a larger project aimed at developing a decision aid for surgical approach selection in DTC, conducted within a graduate research program at Chongqing Medical University. We employed a convergent parallel mixed-methods design guided by the ODSF.10,31,32 The quantitative strand examined levels and predictors of decisional conflict, while the qualitative strand explored patients' decision-making experiences and decisional support needs, defined as patients’ perceived needs for information, values clarification, and guidance related to the surgical approach decision. Reporting follows the GRAMMS guidelines for mixed-methods research and COREQ criteria for qualitative components (Supplementary File 1). A study flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Convergent parallel mixed methods design.

Setting

The study was conducted from June 2024 to June 2025 at three tertiary hospitals affiliated with Chongqing Medical University, China. All three sites were qualified to perform both conventional open thyroidectomy and endoscopic approaches, including transoral and transaxillary techniques. Sample sizes across hospitals were: Hospital 1 (n = 494, 71.0%), Hospital 2 (n = 136, 19.5%), and Hospital 3 (n = 66, 9.5%). Baseline characteristics were compared across sites (Supplementary Table S1). Most variables did not differ significantly, including gender (P = 0.431), age (P = 0.767), education (P = 0.092), and marital status (P = 0.381). DCS total scores were also comparable across hospitals (F = 1.097, P = 0.334). Several sociodemographic variables showed significant between-site differences, including monthly income, occupation, medical insurance type, number of children, alcohol use, and BMI (Supplementary Table S1). Of these, monthly income and occupation were also associated with DCS scores in bivariate analysis (Table 2). However, when income was controlled, occupation was no longer significant; the remaining variables were not associated with DCS scores (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2). Only monthly income was therefore retained in the multivariable regression model.

Participants

Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with Bethesda category V (suspicious for malignancy) or VI (malignant) cytology on fine-needle aspiration, deemed clinically suitable by the surgical team for both open and endoscopic thyroidectomy, facing their first thyroid surgery decision, and able to provide informed consent. Patients were excluded if they had distant metastasis, concurrent malignancies, cognitive impairment, or prior thyroid surgery.

For the quantitative strand, sample size was calculated using G∗Power 3.1 for multiple linear regression. Candidate predictor variables included sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, education, income, medical insurance type) and decision-making preference, selected based on the ODSF framework and prior DCS literature12,15 that can inform clinical screening and tailored intervention delivery. As these studies reported small effect sizes for sociodemographic predictors, we anticipated f2 = 0.05 with six candidate predictors (alpha = 0.05, power = 0.80). This yielded a minimum requirement of 395 participants; anticipating 15% invalid responses, we aimed to recruit at least 465 participants. Through consecutive convenience sampling, 802 patients were approached; 696 provided valid responses (response rate: 86.8%).

For the qualitative strand, we employed a two-stage purposive sampling strategy. In Stage 1, maximum variation sampling was used to capture diverse perspectives across sex, age (≤ 40 vs. > 40 years), and surgical preference (open, endoscopic, or undecided), yielding 15 participants (P01–P15). Concurrent preliminary analysis identified underexplored dimensions of the decision-making experience, including concerns about differences between endoscopic techniques (transoral vs. transaxillary), surgeon expertise with each approach, and fertility implications for young unmarried patients. In Stage 2, we purposively recruited 7 additional participants (P16–P22) whose profiles matched these gaps, to ensure comprehensive coverage of diverse decision-making experiences. No new codes emerged after P20; interviews with P21 and P22 confirmed saturation. Of the 25 patients invited, 3 declined due to time constraints, resulting in a final sample of 22.

Data collection

Quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently between June 2024 and June 2025. Survey data were collected via an online platform (Wenjuanxing). Participants completed the 16-item Decisional Conflict Scale (DCS),11,14,33 which measures personal perceptions of uncertainty in choosing options, modifiable factors contributing to uncertainty, and perceived quality of the decision made. Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale (0 = strongly agree to 4 = strongly disagree). Items are summed and divided by 16, then multiplied by 25 to yield a total score ranging from 0 (no decisional conflict) to 100 (extremely high decisional conflict); higher scores indicate greater conflict. The scale comprises five subscales: Informed (3 items), Values Clarity (3 items), Support (3 items), Uncertainty (3 items), and Effective Decision (4 items), each scored on the same 0–100 scale.10 Scores lower than 25 are associated with implementing decisions; scores exceeding 37.5 are associated with decision delay or feeling unsure about implementation.11,12 The Chinese version has demonstrated good internal consistency in cancer populations (e.g., Cronbach’s α = 0.92 in a thyroid cancer sample33). Participants were instructed to respond to DCS items with specific reference to the decision between open and endoscopic thyroidectomy, ensuring that scores reflected conflict related to surgical approach selection rather than general cancer-related distress. Participants also completed the Control Preferences Scale,34 a single-item measure of preferred decision-making role. Following a previous Chinese study,28,35 we adopted a four-category classification (active, collaborative, passive, and family-proxy) to accommodate the prominent role of family in Chinese medical decision-making.25,26

Sociodemographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records. After excluding 106 responses with completion times under 120 seconds or logical inconsistencies, 696 questionnaires remained for analysis.

Face-to-face interviews were conducted exclusively at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University for logistical and methodological reasons: it served as the research team’s base institution, enabling timely recruitment, rapport-building, and member-checking procedures. As all three hospitals were tertiary teaching institutions with comparable resources and clinical protocols, we anticipated that patients' decision-making experiences would be broadly similar across sites. DJ, a female nursing graduate student with training in qualitative methods and clinical experience in thyroid surgery, served as primary interviewer; XHY, also a female nursing graduate student with clinical experience in the same specialty, assisted and recorded field notes. Neither had prior relationships with participants. Participants were informed that interviewers were nursing graduate students conducting research on surgical decision-making, with no involvement in their clinical care. No non-participants were present during interviews. The interview guide, developed around the three ODSF dimensions (decisional needs, decision quality, and decision support), was refined after two pilot interviews (Supplementary File 2). Each participant was interviewed once, with sessions lasting 15–35 minutes (mean: 25.5 minutes). The interview guide was structured around a specific, bounded decision (choosing between two surgical approaches) rather than broad illness experiences, consistent with evidence that focused interview designs yield thematic depth when guided by a clear theoretical framework.36 In our study, thematic saturation for major themes was reached by the 12th interview, with no new codes emerging after P20, suggesting that the interview duration was sufficient for the phenomenon of interest. The two-stage sampling design, which allowed us to identify and fill thematic gaps, further supported data adequacy. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. DJ transcribed recordings verbatim within 48 hours; XHY verified accuracy by reviewing transcripts against recordings. Transcripts were returned to participants for comment.

Data analysis

Quantitative analyses were performed using SPSS 29.0. Among the candidate predictor variables identified a priori, those significantly associated with DCS scores in bivariate analysis (P < 0.10) were entered into the final regression model; age and gender were retained as covariates regardless of significance. Descriptive statistics summarized sample characteristics and DCS scores (means ± SDs). Independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA (with Bonferroni correction) compared DCS scores across groups; effect sizes were reported as eta-squared. Multiple linear regression identified predictors of decisional conflict; multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (all VIF < 2.0). Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Qualitative data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis,37,38 selected for its capacity to accommodate the hybrid deductive-inductive approach required by our ODSF-guided framework and its emphasis on researchers' active role in meaning-making.36 We adopted a hybrid approach: initial codes were informed by ODSF dimensions while remaining open to emergent themes. DJ and XHY independently coded each transcript line-by-line on printed copies, meeting after every 3–5 interviews to compare codes and resolve discrepancies through discussion. A senior researcher audited the coding process throughout. After Stage 1, the team held a comprehensive meeting to review preliminary themes and identify gaps, which informed Stage 2 sampling. Following Stage 2, themes were refined through supplementary coding. Three participants with contrasting profiles reviewed summaries of their interviews and confirmed accuracy. Qualitative themes were compared with quantitative findings to examine consistency and complementarity between the two datasets. Two participants who reported minimal conflict, P13 (a nurse) and P15 (a retired government official), were analyzed as deviant cases to sharpen theme boundaries.

As noted above, the primary interviewer (DJ) and research assistant (XHY) both had extensive clinical backgrounds in breast and thyroid surgery (over 10 years each), which facilitated rapport with participants but may have oriented attention toward biomedical aspects of decision-making. To counterbalance this potential bias, the supervising researcher (ZL), who had no prior research on thyroid cancer, audited coding decisions and challenged clinically grounded assumptions. The team maintained reflexive journals and met regularly to examine how clinical familiarity shaped theme development. The inclusion of deviant cases (P13, a nurse; P15, a retired government official) and member checking with three contrasting participants provided additional interpretive checks.

Mixed-methods integration followed Fetters and colleagues’ framework.39 A joint display was constructed to juxtapose DCS subscale scores and significant predictors with corresponding qualitative themes, examining where findings converged, where qualitative data helped explain quantitative patterns, and where qualitative analysis revealed dimensions beyond standardized measures. Meta-inferences were drawn from patterns across both datasets.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 802 patients approached, 696 completed valid questionnaires (response rate: 86.8%). The sample was predominantly female (74.4%), with a mean age of 44.24 years (SD = 12.33). Educational attainment was relatively high, with nearly half (48.7%) holding college degrees or above. Most participants were married (87.5%) and covered by Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (75.9%). Most participants were non-smokers and non-drinkers (both 90.1%), and few reported hypertension (12.6%), diabetes (4.0%), or other comorbidities (11.5%). For decision-making preference, nearly half (48.9%) favored a collaborative approach; smaller proportions preferred active (23.9%), passive (21.8%), or family-proxy (5.5%) roles (Table 1).

Quantitative findings

Decisional conflict levels

The mean total DCS score was 47.24 (SD = 27.44) on the 0–100 scale, with 57.9% of participants scoring above the 37.5 threshold associated with decision delay and implementation uncertainty.11,12 Subscale scores varied, with Values Clarity showing the highest conflict (55.83 ± 32.71) and Informed subscale the lowest (41.17 ± 25.84), suggesting that patients experienced the greatest difficulty in clarifying personal values and managing uncertainty (Table 1).

Factors associated with decisional conflict

Bivariate analyses identified three variables significantly associated with DCS scores (Table 2). Monthly income showed the strongest association (F = 6.349, P < 0.001, η2 = 0.027); patients earning below 3000 CNY reported higher conflict (M = 53.42) than those earning above 10,000 CNY (M = 41.89). Decision-making preference was also significant (F = 4.008, P = 0.008, η2 = 0.017), with collaborative preference associated with the lowest conflict (M = 43.65) and active preference with the highest (M = 51.69). Occupation showed a smaller but significant association (F = 2.371, P = 0.028, η2 = 0.020). Other sociodemographic and health variables were not significantly associated with decisional conflict (Supplementary Table S2). However, when income was controlled in a general linear model, occupation was no longer significant (F = 1.354, P = 0.231), suggesting its effect operates primarily through economic pathways. Therefore, occupation was not retained in the final regression model to maintain parsimony and avoid multicollinearity.

Multivariable predictors

Multiple linear regression identified two independent predictors (Table 3). Monthly income was inversely associated with conflict (B = −4.271, SE = 1.091, β = −0.150, P < 0.001, 95% CI [−6.41, −2.13]), and preference for active decision-making predicted higher conflict compared to collaborative preference (B = 7.500, SE = 2.564, β = 0.117, P = 0.004, 95% CI [2.47, 12.54]). The model was statistically significant (F = 4.865, P < 0.001, R2 = 0.041).

Theme 1: Information needs beyond routine clinical disclosure. Participants’ information needs extended well beyond the standard information provided during routine preoperative consultations (i.e., diagnosis, treatment plan, major risks, and expected outcomes). They sought additional, specific information about surgical thoroughness of endoscopic approaches, approach-specific complication rates (particularly dysphonia), detailed cost breakdowns and insurance coverage, differences between specific endoscopic techniques (transoral vs. transaxillary; Subtheme 1.4), and individual surgeon experience with each approach (Subtheme 1.5):

“I’m just afraid that the scope can’t see clearly. In quality inspection at our factory, some small defects are invisible to the naked eye—you need a magnifying glass. Isn’t surgery the same? What if they don’t remove it completely? It’s cancer, after all, not a minor problem.”

(P03, female, 35y, quality inspector)

Theme 2: Navigating competing values. Decision-making involved weighing values that specifically differed between surgical approaches: perceived thoroughness of open surgery versus cosmetic advantages of endoscopic approaches, approach-specific recovery times, and fertility-related considerations. Young patients frequently experienced tension between choosing the approach perceived as more effective and the one offering better cosmetic outcomes, often accompanied by shame about prioritizing appearance:

“Honestly, as a man, I shouldn’t care about this, right? But I’m not married yet. When meeting potential partners, will a scar on my neck bother them? My mom says scars make men look tough, but young women today might not think so. I feel pathetic—I have cancer and I’m worrying about this.”

(P08, male, 32y, courier)

Theme 3: Barriers to effective decision-making. Multiple factors complicated the choice between surgical approaches: contradictory online comparisons of open versus endoscopic outcomes, inadequate clinical communication about approach-specific differences, conflicting family opinions about which approach to choose, and time pressure. Social media was a double-edged sword, providing approach comparisons while generating anxiety:

“There are so many posts from people who’ve had surgery on Xiaohongshu. I read tons of them. But everyone says different things. Some say endoscopic is more painful, others say it’s not; some say recovery is faster, others say it’s slower. The more I read, the more anxious I get. I can’t sleep at night.”

(P06, female, 48y, cashier)

Theme 4: The “good cancer” paradox: when reassurance becomes invalidation. This theme captured the central paradox of our findings: the “good cancer” framing, intended to comfort, instead functioned as a barrier to expressing decisional needs. Participants described a gap between being told their condition was “not serious” and experiencing genuine distress about surgical choices. This mismatch manifested in three ways: invalidation of fears by the “good cancer” label (Subtheme 4.1), unspoken doubts about physician motivations for recommending endoscopic surgery (Subtheme 4.2), and anxiety about long-term consequences that felt incongruent with the “good” prognosis (Subtheme 4.3). Despite receiving reassurance about favorable prognosis, many participants found that the “good cancer” framing invalidated rather than comforted:

“The doctor said thyroid cancer is a ‘good cancer,’ nothing to worry too much about. Good cancer … [bitter laugh] But when I hear the word ‘cancer,’ I’m scared. Good or bad, it’s still cancer to me. I feel like none of them understand me.”

(P01, female, 32y, elementary school teacher)

Theme 5: Unmet decisional support needs. Participants articulated clear preferences for decisional support: written materials comparing both surgical approaches for home review, peer connection with survivors of each approach, and emotional validation. Written materials emerged as a consistent request:

“If there were a booklet or video explaining the pros and cons of both surgeries clearly, something I could take home and read slowly—that would be great. In those few minutes at the clinic, I can’t understand anything. Half of what the doctor says, I forget as soon as I leave.”

(P06, female, 48y, cashier)

Deviant case analysis showed that P13 (a nurse) and P15 (a retired government official), both with high health system familiarity, reported minimal distress related to the “good cancer” label, in contrast to other participants.

Discussion

Principal findings

We found that patients with DTC commonly experience decisional conflict when choosing surgical approaches, and that this conflict is associated with socioeconomic status and decision-making preference. Of particular interest, patients who preferred active participation reported higher conflict, a finding with implications for shared decision-making.

The qualitative findings provided explanatory context for the quantitative results. The income–conflict association manifested in interviews as multiple barriers faced by lower-income participants: limited understanding of new technologies, difficulty filtering online information, and inability to afford preferred surgical options. What the DCS measured as “values unclear” appeared in interviews as tension between efficacy and appearance, varying by age, gender, and life stage. The association between active preference and higher conflict was reflected in participants’ accounts of feeling burdened by choice: they wanted control but lacked the conditions to exercise it.

Qualitative analysis also revealed phenomena not captured by standardized instruments, such as distress from the “good cancer” label and latent doubts about physician motivations. These typically emerged later in interviews, after trust had been established. Though invisible to scale-based assessment, these phenomena may affect decision quality and patient–provider relationships.

Both the DCS survey instructions and the interview guide were anchored to the specific decision between open and endoscopic thyroidectomy. Nevertheless, participants' decisional conflict extended beyond the choice between surgical approaches to encompass broader concerns, including the cancer diagnosis itself, long-term medication requirements, and fertility implications. This breadth is not unexpected: the ODSF posits that decisional conflict arises from the totality of unmet needs, not from the technical parameters of a single choice.10 In clinical practice, a patient weighing cosmetic outcomes of different approaches is simultaneously processing a cancer diagnosis; the two sources of distress are difficult to separate. We argue that decision support interventions should address this broader decisional context rather than focusing narrowly on technique comparisons, which risks overlooking the concerns that drive conflict most strongly.

The income–conflict association

The inverse relationship between household income and decisional conflict (Table 3, β = −0.150, P < 0.001) merits further discussion. Health literacy research offers a partial explanation. Gero et al.’s review demonstrated that low-income populations are more likely to experience limited health literacy, constraining their capacity to access, understand, and act upon health information.29 Stormacq et al.’s integrative review reached similar conclusions.30

Yet the mechanism likely extends beyond information access. Several lower-income participants described “constrained choice”, preferences shaped less by values clarification than by economic reality. This pattern echoes Yen et al.’s systematic review: decision support interventions that fail to account for socioeconomic constraints may inadvertently widen health inequalities.40 Additionally, Bazargan et al. found that perceived discrimination based on income and insurance status predicted medical mistrust.41 Decision support interventions designed for populations with baseline physician trust may thus need adaptation for lower-income patients.42

The autonomy paradox

Patients preferring active decision-making roles reported significantly higher conflict (Table 3, β = 0.117, P = 0.004). This finding appears counterintuitive, as one might expect that patients who desire control would experience less conflict when offered it. Qualitative data helped explain this pattern: interview participants who desired active participation described feeling burdened by choice without adequate information or support to exercise it (Theme 3). As P17 expressed: “The doctor says I can choose. But how am I supposed to choose? I’m not a doctor.”

This gap between decisional desire and decisional capacity can be understood through several theoretical lenses. Xiao and colleagues found that health literacy, particularly information acquisition and communication abilities, explained a substantial proportion of variance in perceived shared decision-making among Chinese cancer patients,43 consistent with earlier theoretical work demonstrating the need for functional, communicative, and critical health literacy skills.44 This aligns with the emerging concept of “patient readiness for shared decision-making”, which encompasses not only the desire to participate but also information skills, self-efficacy, and emotional state.45,46 Edwards and colleagues’ Health Literacy Pathway Model further suggests that patients progress through developmental stages in their capacity to participate.47 This aligns with contemporary thinking in shared decision-making: respecting autonomy requires attention not only to whether patients are offered choices, but also to whether they possess the conditions for exercising them.48

An alternative explanation invokes individual differences. Schwartz and colleagues demonstrated that “maximizers” experience more regret than “satisficers”;49 Vargová and colleagues confirmed that maximizing as a strategy was maladaptive.50 Whether our active-preference patients were dispositional maximizers or situationally overwhelmed cannot be determined from cross-sectional data. A third possibility is reverse causality: patients experiencing high conflict may subsequently develop stronger preferences for active involvement. Disentangling this bidirectional relationship requires longitudinal research.

In the Chinese cultural context, this finding may be further shaped by the family-centered decision-making model. Within the Confucian tradition, medical decisions are often negotiated collectively within the family rather than made by the patient alone.25, 26, 27 Patients who express active decision-making preferences may, in effect, be acting against cultural expectations of collective deliberation,25,27 generating interpersonal tension in addition to the intrapersonal decisional conflict measured by the DCS. Our qualitative data support this interpretation: several participants described conflict with family members over surgical choices, and the qualitative sample included patients whose preference for active participation was self-driven as well as others whose families expected them to take the lead. Future research should examine whether the active preference–conflict association is moderated by family decision-making dynamics and specific sociodemographic characteristics such as marital status and parental status.

The “good cancer” paradox

While 57.9% of participants exceeded the clinically significant DCS threshold of 37.511,12 (Table 1), qualitative analysis revealed an additional layer of distress not captured by standardized instruments (Theme 4): the gap between being told one has a “good cancer” and experiencing genuine distress about surgical choices. This phenomenon has been documented in thyroid cancer survivorship research.19,51 Zhang and colleagues demonstrated that patients with low-risk papillary thyroid microcarcinoma also experience significant decisional conflict when choosing between active surveillance and surgery.33 Our data indicate that such conflict also exists during the decision-making phase for surgical approach selection.

This phenomenon may be mediated by emotional invalidation. When clinicians emphasize favorable prognosis, patients may interpret this as a signal that their concerns lack legitimacy. Dionisi-Vici and colleagues’ longitudinal study documented that thyroid cancer survivors frequently report feelings of invalidation despite favorable outcomes.51 A recent meta-analysis by Lelorain and colleagues confirmed that physician empathy is significantly associated with favorable patient outcomes, with stronger effects in emotionally charged consultations.52 Our findings suggest that well-intentioned prognostic reassurance, when delivered without adequate emotional acknowledgment, may inadvertently function as invalidation. Disease label effects are not unique to thyroid cancer. Dixon and colleagues demonstrated experimentally that patients were willing to accept higher recurrence risk to avoid the “cancer” label53; Berlin and colleagues showed similar dynamics in low-grade prostate cancer.54 Comparable effects have been observed in ductal carcinoma in situ.55 Disease framing shapes not only communication but potentially decision quality itself.

Context-specific observations

Several findings may reflect features specific to health decision-making in China. Social media platforms, particularly Xiaohongshu (Little Red Book), emerged prominently in participants’ accounts (Theme 3) as sources of both information and confusion about surgical approach differences. Recent analyses indicate that approximately one-third of popular cancer-related articles on social media contain misinformation, with the majority carrying potential for patient harm.56 Concerningly, misinformation often generates higher engagement than accurate information, amplifying its reach.57 Our participants described information overload, contradictory recommendations, and difficulty distinguishing reliable from unreliable sources. These findings point to the need for platform-specific health literacy interventions and suggest that decision support tools should explicitly address how to evaluate online information.

Family involvement in medical decision-making emerged as pervasive and complex. As noted earlier, the majority of Chinese cancer patients expect family participation in treatment decisions,28 reflecting Confucian cultural values that prioritize collective family welfare over individual autonomy.25, 26, 27 This can create tension with shared decision-making approaches that emphasize patient–clinician dyads.26, 27, 28 In our qualitative data, family participation sometimes generated intergenerational conflict, particularly when younger patients’ preferences diverged from those of parents or spouses. Consistent with prior recommendations,26,27 these findings suggest that decision support interventions in the Chinese context should adopt family-inclusive approaches while remaining sensitive to potential conflicts between individual and collective preferences.

Theoretical considerations

The Ottawa Decision Support Framework identifies three sources of decisional conflict: inadequate knowledge, unclear values, and insufficient support.10,58 Our findings indicate that in favorable-prognosis malignancies, patients may face an expression dilemma: when illness is framed as “good cancer”, they may feel their concerns lack legitimacy and thus express fewer decisional needs. This observation resonates with research on validating communication in cancer care,59 although its specific role in oncology decision support requires further study. Our qualitative findings suggest a gap between the manifest needs patients express and the latent needs they actually experience. This “expression dilemma” may lead to systematic underestimation of decisional support needs in favorable-prognosis malignancies, with implications for needs assessment approaches. The relationship between control preference and decision quality may be non-linear and moderated by support availability,60 and this autonomy paradox warrants longitudinal investigation.

Implications for practice

These results have implications for decision support intervention development. Given the income–conflict association, resource-stratified approaches to decision support may be warranted. Screening for health literacy during preoperative nursing assessments may help identify patients requiring additional support.61 Health literacy models emphasize the importance of addressing social determinants in translating health knowledge into healthy behaviors.62 Randomized trials have demonstrated that culturally tailored decision aids can significantly reduce decisional conflict and improve informed choice among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations.42 Additionally, the autonomy paradox suggests that assessing decisional preference alone is insufficient.45 Clinicians might additionally assess decisional readiness, whether patients feel adequately informed and confident to weigh their options. For patients with high autonomy desire but low readiness, structured decision aids can effectively reduce decisional conflict.63

The “good cancer” paradox has implications for clinical communication. ESMO guidelines emphasize that effective communication requires clinicians to remain present with patients' distress rather than moving quickly to reassurance.64 Acknowledging the emotional weight of a cancer diagnosis before discussing favorable prognosis may help validate patients’ concerns. Interventions should also address information evaluation skills, helping patients navigate social media content critically.

Given the prominence of family involvement in Chinese medical decision-making, interventions should adopt family-inclusive designs. Participants commonly expressed a desire to connect with surgical survivors, reflecting the potential value of peer support. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that peer support interventions can improve psychological outcomes among cancer patients.65 Nurse-led decision coaching may be particularly valuable, as nurses often have more sustained contact with patients and families than physicians.66 Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of structured nurse-led decision coaching protocols tailored to patients’ socioeconomic backgrounds and decisional readiness.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The mixed-methods design enabled both quantification of decisional conflict levels and exploration of experiences that quantitative instruments cannot capture. Purposive sampling achieved diversity in age, gender, income, and surgical preference. The spontaneous emergence of the “good cancer” theme without direct questioning strengthens its validity. Several limitations warrant mention. The cross-sectional design captures conflict at a single time point; how conflict evolves through the decision-making process remains unknown. Qualitative data were collected from a single site, which may limit the transferability of certain context-specific findings. The regression model explained only 4.1% of DCS variance, a limited explanatory power not uncommon in DCS prediction studies,12,15 possibly reflecting the highly individualized nature of decisional conflict; future studies should incorporate psychosocial variables (e.g., anxiety, health literacy) to improve predictive models. Additionally, health literacy and cultural attitudes toward medical authority, while discussed as explanatory mechanisms, were not directly measured in the quantitative strand, limiting our ability to test these pathways empirically. Monthly income differed significantly across the three hospital sites (P < 0.001); although it was controlled for in the regression model, we cannot fully rule out residual site-level clustering effects. Future multicenter studies may benefit from multilevel modeling to formally account for between-site variance. Sensitive topics such as doubts about physician motivations emerged late in interviews, suggesting that even in-depth interviews may not fully overcome social desirability bias.

Conclusions

Patients with differentiated thyroid cancer experience clinically significant decisional conflict when choosing between surgical approaches. Lower income and preference for active decision-making predict higher conflict, findings that complicate straightforward applications of shared decision-making principles. Beyond factors captured by standardized measures, the “good cancer” label creates a paradox whereby patients' legitimate distress may be inadvertently invalidated. Attending to this paradox by reframing reassurance, assessing decisional readiness, and creating space for patients’ unspoken concerns may improve both decision quality and patient experience.
