The Hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenocortical Response to Critical Illness: A Concept in Need of Revision
Authors: Lies Langouche, Arno Téblick, Jan Gunst, Greet Van den Berghe
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBased on insights obtained during the past decade, the classical concept of an activated hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenocortical axis in response to critical illness is in need of revision. After a brief central hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenocortical axis activation, the vital maintenance of increased systemic cortisol availability and action in response to critical illness is predominantly driven by peripheral adaptations rather than by an ongoing centrally activated several-fold increased production and secretion of cortisol. Besides the known reduction of cortisol-binding proteins that increases free cortisol, these peripheral responses comprise suppressed cortisol metabolism in liver and kidney, prolonging cortisol half-life, and local alterations in expression of 11βHSD1, glucocorticoid receptor-α (GRα), and FK506 binding protein 5 (FKBP51) that appear to titrate increased GRα action in vital organs and tissues while reducing GRα action in neutrophils, possibly preventing immune-suppressive off-target effects of increased systemic cortisol availability. Peripherally increased cortisol exerts negative feed-back inhibition at the pituitary level impairing processing of pro-opiomelanocortin into ACTH, thereby reducing ACTH-driven cortisol secretion, whereas ongoing central activation results in increased circulating pro-opiomelanocortin. These alterations seem adaptive and beneficial for the host in the short term. However, as a consequence, patients with prolonged critical illness who require intensive care for weeks or longer may develop a form of central adrenal insufficiency. The new findings supersede earlier concepts such as “relative,” as opposed to “absolute,” adrenal insufficiency and generalized systemic glucocorticoid resistance in the critically ill. The findings also question the scientific basis for broad implementation of stress dose hydrocortisone treatment of patients suffering from acute septic shock solely based on assumption of cortisol insufficiency.

Critical Illness

Critical illness represents any life-threatening condition that requires pharmacological and/or mechanical support of vital organ functions, provided within the specialized environment of an intensive care unit (ICU), to prevent imminent death (1). Common causes of critical illness are sepsis and septic shock, major or complicated surgery, severe trauma, large-scale burn injuries, and deteriorating medical diseases. Such catastrophes uniformly induce the activation of pathogen- and damage-induced pathways that result in multiple physiologic processes that are designed to deal with the homeostasis-disrupting insult and to mediate the return to homeostasis. This constellation of physiological processes is referred to as the “fight-or-flight response” or “stress response.” In the pre-ICU era, the fight-or-flight response was followed by a swift recovery or, when the insult was too severe, by a fast progression to death. Through the development of modern intensive care, survival of acute critical illnesses has dramatically increased. However, an important subset of these survivors go on to require intensive medical care for a prolonged period (2, 3). It is estimated that at least 1 of every 20 patients who are admitted to an ICU eventually suffer from such persisting or prolonged critical illness and remain dependent on vital organ support for several weeks or longer (4, 5). After approximately 10 days of critical illness, the initial diagnosis and initial severity of illness on admission are no longer predictive for in-hospital mortality (5). Indeed, regardless of what the initial insult has been, these long-stay patients uniformly suffer from typical ICU-acquired morbidities, such as lingering organ failure and recurrent bacterial and fungal infections, profound muscle weakness and central nervous system dysfunctions such as encephalopathy and delirium (3). The pathophysiology of these typical ICU-acquired morbidities is not fully understood but likely comprise alterations in endocrine, metabolic, and inflammatory pathways (6). The ICU-acquired morbidities in turn contribute to the risk of in-hospital and late death of patients with prolonged critical illness, and, among survivors, they hamper rehabilitation also in the longer term (7).

The Classical Concept of the Hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal Axis Stress Response

In 1914, Walter Cannon linked the fight-or-flight response with massive release of catecholamines from the adrenal medulla through activation of the sympathetic nervous system (8). However, it was not until the pioneering work of Hans Selye in the 1930s and 1940s that also the adrenal cortex was recognized to play a vital role in this response (9-11). Since then, it has become clear that whenever the brain senses a stressful event, the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is activated. Neuronal networks and inflammation signal the nucleus paraventricularis of the hypothalamus where the parvocellular neurosecretory cells that project to the median eminence release CRH and vasopressin (AVP) into the hypothalamus-pituitary portal system. In the anterior pituitary gland, corticotrope cells express the CRH receptor 1 and activation of the downstream signaling pathways results in transcription of the large precursor hormone pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) and the release of POMC-derived peptides that were stored in secretory granules (Fig. 1). Among these, ACTH is the principal activator of cortisol synthesis and secretion in the zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex. The production of ACTH from the precursor molecule, POMC, is controlled by the pro-hormone convertase-1 (PC1/3) and secretion of the ACTH-containing vesicles is under the negative control of Annexin-A1. In addition, newly synthetized POMC molecules can leak from the corticotrope cells into the systemic circulation, independent of external stimuli, through the constitutive secretory pathway (12, 13). Corticotrope cells also express the AVP receptor-1b and ligand binding by AVP synergizes with CRH to activate production and secretion of ACTH in the systemic circulation. Increased systemic cortisol availability in response to critical illness brings about several vital endocrine, metabolic, and immunological effects. These compose catabolic responses in the liver, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle that foster energy provision for the host, endocrine responses that bring about fluid retention, increased cardiac output and sensitization of the vasculature to catecholamines to prevent hypotension, and suppressive effects on pro-inflammatory cells and cytokines to prevent hyperinflammation, the latter with a concomitant theoretical risk of immune suppression. In addition, cortisol can exert fast, intermediate, and slow feedback inhibition at the pituitary and the hypothalamic level to regulate its own release.

Most critically ill patients admitted to the ICU present with substantially elevated plasma cortisol concentrations that are in proportion to the severity of the illness. Within this classical concept of the adrenocortical stress response to critical illness, 1 of a central top-down activation of the HPA-axis, one expects ACTH-driven cortisol production and secretion to be increased at least 8-fold above normal to bring about the level of hypercortisolism present in these very sick patients. This has been the (empirical) basis for the 200 mg of hydrocortisone as “stress dose” typically used for treatment of adrenal crisis. Until recently, such substantially increased ACTH-driven cortisol production/secretion was assumed to be the main driver of the vital increase in systemic cortisol availability during critical illness, irrespective of the illness duration. However, results from clinical and experimental studies performed over the past decade have challenged this assumption and call for a revision of the classical adrenocortical stress response concept.

The HPA Axis in the Acute Phase of Critical Illness: an Evolutionary Conserved set of Adaptive Responses

The Central HPA Axis Activation in Response to Critical Illness is Short-lasting

Within the classical concept of a central top-down activated HPA axis stress response, one would expect that the severe stress induced by critical illnesses would bring about a rapid rise in the circulating level of ACTH and of cortisol, both sustained sufficiently long to bridge the host to recovery. However, surprisingly few studies have reported such a scenario. A few small studies of patients undergoing surgery have reported high circulating ACTH and high circulating cortisol during and at the end of the surgery (14, 15). However, in the hours following surgery, plasma ACTH concentrations rapidly decreased, whereas plasma cortisol concentrations remained elevated (16). A 1995 study of critically ill patients suffering from sepsis or major trauma documented only transiently increased plasma ACTH, whereas plasma cortisol levels remained elevated for at least 7 days (17). A 2013 study showed that, in a mixed (surgical, medical, trauma) population of patients being treated in a tertiary ICU for at least 1 week, plasma cortisol was always elevated but plasma ACTH concentration was low rather than high, from day 1 after admission and throughout that first week in the ICU (18). Also, studies performed in a clinically relevant mouse model of IV fluid-resuscitated antibiotics- and analgesics-treated polymicrobial sepsis showed absence of elevated plasma ACTH in the face of high plasma corticosterone (19-21). These findings are not quite compatible with the hypothesis of ongoing ACTH-driven increased adrenocortical production of cortisol during critical illness, unless the adrenocortical sensitivity to ACTH would be substantially increased, which was subsequently shown not to be the case (18, 22). A model of ACTH-independent, inflammation-driven cortisol production that feeds into the HPA axis was suggested by studies demonstrating that direct ligation of Toll-like receptor 2 and Toll-like receptor 4 in adrenocortical cells activates cortisol production (23). However, a study of critically ill patients that used state of the art stable isotope tracer technology documented that in the face of several-fold increase in systemic cortisol availability and low plasma ACTH concentrations, the daytime cortisol production rate of patients was either not increased or less than double the cortisol production rate of matched healthy subjects, depending on the degree of hyperinflammation, but never anywhere near the 8-fold increase that was long assumed to drive hypercortisolism in critical illness (18). Extrapolating the quantified daytime production rate to 24 hours, this would come down to a daily cortisol production between 30 and 60 mg. However, a subsequent study that used hormonal time series and deconvolution analysis reported lower than normal nocturnal ACTH-driven cortisol secretion in ICU patients, with a normal ACTH-cortisol dose-response, whereas plasma cortisol was always clearly increased (18, 24). Together, the available evidence suggests that the central top-down activation of the HPA axis in response to critical illnesses, though likely present in the initial, hyperacute phase of critical illness, appears to be very short-lasting and thus other important drivers of hypercortisolism must play a key role in this setting.

Increase in Systemic Cortisol Availability and Action in Response to Critical Illness is Mainly Peripherally Driven

Several ACTH-independent peripheral mechanisms contribute to the sustained increase in systemic cortisol availability and action beyond the hyper-acute phase of critical illness.

A first peripheral mechanism that has long been known to play a key role is the increase in free, unbound cortisol via a reduced binding to the cortisol carrier proteins, corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG) or transcortin, and albumin, something that occurs early after onset of critical illness (Fig. 2) (25, 26). The circulating levels of CBG fall within hours after onset of illness and remain low throughout the ICU stay (16, 18, 27, 28). Although it has been suggested that pro-inflammatory cytokines suppress CBG synthesis in the liver (29, 30), a recent study further scrutinized the mechanism behind the rapid decrease in CBG (31). In a clinically relevant mouse model of sepsis-induced critical illness, low circulating CBG could be partially attributed to a downregulation of the glucocorticoid receptor-α (GRα) in the liver. Indeed, partially suppressing expression and signaling of the hepatic GRα through hepatocyte-specific short hairpin RNA knock-down resulted in a decrease in circulating CBG (31). In critically ill patients, suppressed hepatic CBG synthesis may thus be mediated by cytokines further accentuated by the initially increased binding of cortisol to the hepatic GRα followed by a (transient) GRα downregulation. A decrease in CBG leads to a rise in free cortisol (in humans) or corticosterone (in rodents), which mediates increased systemic glucocorticoid availability even without alterations in total cortisol/corticosterone concentrations (31, 32). Importantly, in the previously mentioned studies of septic mice, elevated free corticosterone levels were present in the absence of elevated ACTH, corroborating the human findings (31). In addition to low levels of CBG in the systemic circulation, its cortisol binding is reduced reversibly by pyrexia and acidemia and is reduced irreversibly by neutrophil elastase cleavage, converting high cortisol-binding affinity CBG to a low-affinity form. These changes may serve to enhance cortisol delivery to the interstitial space in inflammation (25, 33, 34). The decrease in high-affinity CBG has been shown to be proportionate to the severity of septic shock (29). Also, circulating levels of albumin, the other main carrier protein for cortisol, are decreased in critically ill patients. The causes of such low albumin levels comprise losses via bleeding or enteropathy, capillary leakage of albumin into the interstitial space, and dilution from fluid resuscitation (35, 36). Whether albumin synthesis is actually decreased during critical illness remains debated (37, 38).

Although low circulating levels of corticosteroid carrier proteins, CBG and albumin, and the low-binding affinity hereof, explain part of the increase in plasma free cortisol levels during critical illness, a second mechanism contributes to the ACTH-independent increase in systemic cortisol availability (Fig. 2). Indeed, a series of human studies, the results of which were jointly published in 2013, revealed that ICU patients have suppressed cortisol breakdown (18). First, with state-of-the-art stable tracer technology, plasma clearance of cortisol was substantially and uniformly reduced in these patients, a finding corroborated by another study that showed that cortisol half-life was on average 8-fold longer in these patients compared with healthy subjects (18). The suppressed cortisol breakdown was explained by suppressed activity of the hepatic A-ring reductases, 5α-reductase, and 5β-reductase, and of the cortisol-inactivating enzyme 11β-HSD2 in kidney, first suggested by the quantification of urinary cortisol metabolites and subsequently confirmed by analyses of biopsies obtained postmortem from ICU nonsurvivors, which showed substantially reduced gene and protein expression of the A-ring reductases, inversely correlated with plasma cortisol concentrations (18). Hence, suppression of peripheral cortisol metabolism is another important contributor to the vital increase in systemic cortisol availability in critically ill patients. The specific underlying mechanism of this shutdown of cortisol breakdown remains unclear. A role for circulating bile acids has been suggested, given that bile acid levels are typically increased in ICU patients and were found to be inversely correlated with expression and activity of the A-ring reductases (18, 39-42). This is in line with the negative impact of cholestasis on cortisol metabolism (43).

Given the important impact on systemic cortisol availability of these peripheral drivers, the low plasma ACTH concentrations, or the lack of increased plasma ACTH which normally would be expected in case of a central top-down HPA activation, now repeatedly confirmed in patient and animal studies of critical illness, may simply be the result of negative feedback inhibition by free cortisol at the level of the GRα in the anterior pituitary and in the hypothalamus (Fig. 1). This would be comparable to what happens when people are treated with glucocorticoids in doses that exceed the normal endogenous production or in patients suffering from Cushing syndrome because of an adrenal tumor (44). Data from a study performed in ICU patients provided indirect evidence for such feedback inhibition (45). This study revealed a suppressed incremental ACTH response to an IV bolus of 100 µg of a synthetic human CRH analogue, not during the first days of critical illness when this response was normal, but from day 7 in ICU onwards. This is what can be expected in a condition of peripherally driven increase in cortisol availability exerting feedback inhibition at the central level of the HPA axis. In a series of studies performed in septic mice, in which investigation of the hypothalamus, the anterior pituitary gland, and the adrenal cortex could be done at the tissue level in different phases of sepsis-induced critical illness while the mice received intensive care, such negative feedback inhibition was further corroborated (19-21). These studies showed that an initial transient increase in hypothalamic CRH and AVP expression was followed by an increase in pituitary expression of the respective receptors, CRH receptor 1 and AVP receptor-1b. Gene expression of the precursor of ACTH, POMC, was always increased. Together, these data suggest a sustained hypothalamus-pituitary positive forward signaling and central activation of the HPA axis in critical illness. However, pituitary expression of PC1/3, the enzyme controlling POMC to ACTH processing, was always suppressed and expression of Annexin A1, the negative regulator of secretion of mature ACTH, was always increased. Both these findings reflect increased GRα ligand-binding and signaling in the pituitary corticotrope cells (46-49). Ongoing sepsis-induced POMC production together with GRα-mediated suppressed processing into ACTH and suppressed ACTH secretion, explain the observed lack of increased plasma ACTH levels during critical illness. Interestingly, unprocessed POMC appears to leak out of the corticotrope cells into the systemic circulation, resulting in a robust rise in plasma POMC concentrations present in critically ill patients and in the septic mouse model (19, 21). Whether the central GRα-ligand binding is exclusively brought about by the peripherally driven increased free cortisol or also by other GRα ligands, such as bile acids (18, 39, 41), remains to be further studied. It also remains to be investigated in in vivo models whether the rise in circulating POMC plays a role in the ongoing cortisol synthesis/secretion despite low levels of ACTH, though a recent adrenal explant study provided preliminary evidence thereof (21). Such a role of POMC was also suggested earlier by case reports of clinically cushingoid patients, suspected to have ectopic ACTH syndrome but who had low plasma ACTH concentrations, whereas plasma POMC was substantially increased (50, 51).

What is actually “vital” about the critical illness-induced HPA stress response comes down to a lifesaving increase in cortisol action in different organs and tissues. Cortisol action through GRα signaling is highly regulated at various levels, and such regulation is known to be tissue specific (52). These regulators comprise, among others, level of expression of 11β-HSD1, the enzyme responsible for local conversion of inactive cortisone to active cortisol, the expression of the active GRα, relative to the dominant negative GRβ, and the expression of the cytoplasmic chaperones of the GRα, heat shock proteins 70 and 90, and FKBP51, which dissociate from the cytoplasmic GRα on ligand binding thereby allowing translocation of ligand-bound GRα to the nucleus to exert its function by modulating gene expression (52). In particular, FKBP51 is an interesting protein because it reduces the sensitivity of the GRα, whereas its own expression is upregulated by GRα ligand-binding, thus functioning to prevent excessive GRα action. Several studies previously reported that in blood samples from ICU patients suffering from sepsis, and more recently also in patients after cardiac arrest, the expression of the GRα was suppressed (53-58), which was interpreted as a sign of generalized systemic glucocorticoid resistance. However, given the many levels of regulation of GRα action mentioned previously and the possibility of tissue-specific differences, that conclusion appeared premature. In a recent set of studies, performed in human patients and in a validated mouse model of sepsis-induced critical illness, this tissue specificity in GRα expression and action was further investigated (20). These studies revealed that mostly neutrophils are responsible for the earlier observations of a decrease in GRα expression in peripheral blood cells of patients with sepsis and that the expression of the GRα target gene, glucocorticoid-induced leucine zipper (GILZ), in neutrophils was maximally suppressed. However, most other cells and tissues showed a clear upregulation of GILZ expression. GILZ expression in most tissues appeared near-maximal, brought about by several tissue-specific alterations in expression of 11β-HSD1, GRα, and of FKBP51 that prevented a further rise. Only in lung, diaphragm, and adipose tissue, administration of stress doses of hydrocortisone further increased GILZ expression (20).

The results of these studies suggest that such tissue-specific titration of GRα action is the third mechanism, adding on to the 2 peripheral drivers of systemic cortisol availability (Fig. 2). Together these 3 peripheral responses allow vital increase in cortisol action in those cells and tissues that need it most without requiring a continued central “top-down” HPA axis activation. The finding that cortisol action appears highly suppressed selectively in neutrophils, key players in the innate immune system and crucial for microbial killing, could be interpreted as an adaptive response that avoids the theoretical risk of immune suppression by the stress-induced hypercortisolism that was mentioned in the introduction. More research is needed to investigate the validity of this interpretation.

The HPA Axis Response to Acute Critical Illness: Can it Fall Short?

Preexisting diseases of and structural damage to the HPA-axis: “absolute” adrenal insufficiency

Distinct from the ongoing debate regarding any assumed “short falling” of the HPA axis evoked by the critical illness itself, there are patients who indisputably require adjunctive steroid treatment when critically ill. Primarily, these are the patients who, before admission to ICU for whatever reason, were on chronic treatment with glucocorticoids in pharmacological doses to prevent or treat inflammatory diseases as well as those patients on substitution treatment for preexisting primary or central adrenal insufficiency (44) (Table 1). These patients are unable to mount an appropriate activation of the HPA axis in response to stress and should be adequately covered with stress doses of hydrocortisone to prevent adrenal crisis. Although the novel insight of suppressed breakdown of cortisol in ICU patients may perhaps necessitate reconsidering the optimal dose and timing of hydrocortisone coverage for these patients, existing protocols are appropriate and well known to the endocrine and intensive care communities (59). In addition, a small subset of critically ill patients may develop structural damage to 1 of the endocrine glands of the HPA axis as a consequence of the acute illness or insult, for which appropriate hydrocortisone coverage is life-saving. A typical example is fulminant meningococcal, streptococcal, or mycobacterial infections that can cause bilateral adrenal hemorrhage, referred to as Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome. Also, excessive hemorrhage in the peri-/postpartum setting may result in ischemia and necrosis of the anterior pituitary gland known as Sheehan syndrome, and traumatic brain injury can involve the neuroendocrine system (59, 60). In addition, certain drugs can cause adrenal insufficiency, either reversibly such as, etomidate, azoles, prolonged use of opioids or irreversibly such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (61, 62). Recently, concerns were also raised regarding the impact of high doses of opioids used in the ICU (63).

The concept of “relative” adrenal insufficiency as opposed to “absolute” adrenal insufficiency, later renamed as “critical illness related corticosteroid insufficiency”

The concept of “relative adrenal insufficiency” was launched in 2000 by Annane et al, based on an observational study of patients suffering from septic shock. In this study, a subgroup of patients were considered to suffer from a maximally activated adrenal cortex that produces cortisol in large quantities although seemingly not large enough to cope with the severity of the illness (70). These patients were identified as suffering from this condition when the incremental total cortisol response to a 250-µg IV bolus of synthetic ACTH was ≤9 µg/dL, regardless of the baseline cortisol concentration, as this finding best correlated with the risk of death. However, if such a maximally activated adrenal cortex existed, one would expect a very high plasma concentration of ACTH. As described previously, it is now well known that plasma ACTH concentrations in critically ill patients, including those suffering from sepsis or septic shock, are mostly low to low-normal (17, 18, 27). Later, the concept was renamed as critical illness related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI) because the low ACTH concentrations were then interpreted as revealing ischemic or inflammatory damage to the anterior pituitary, and thus it was assumed that the entire HPA axis could suffer from “relative” insufficiency in patients with septic shock (71). However, the more recent study that reported normal incremental ACTH responses to a CRH stimulation test in the acute phase (first 6 days) of sepsis-induced critical illness did not support such an interpretation (45). Subsequently, an additional feature was added to the presumed CIRCI phenotype, namely generalized systemic glucocorticoid resistance, which would render the actions of cortisol insufficient despite an increased systemic cortisol availability (72, 73). As mentioned earlier, the interpretation of systemic glucocorticoid resistance was largely based on small studies revealing a critical illness-induced decrease in the expression of the GRα in peripheral blood cells (53-57). The recent 2022 studies, which confirmed suppressed expression and signaling of the GRα in neutrophils while most other vital organs and tissues showed increased GRα action, did not support that interpretation (20).

The finding of the 2000 Annane et al study, which reported a low total incremental cortisol response to a 250-µg IV bolus of synthetic ACTH as best predicting mortality, can be interpreted differently (27, 45, 74) (Table 1). All critically ill patients have low CBG concentrations, the lowest documented for the sickest patients with the highest risk of death, such as those suffering from septic shock, and such reduced plasma binding of cortisol increases its distribution volume (18, 27). An increased distribution volume explains the low total incremental cortisol responses to a 250-µg IV bolus of synthetic ACTH, whereas the incremental free cortisol responses are mostly normal or at times even higher than normal (27). Hence, in the context of critical illness, the ACTH stimulation test does not provide reliable information on the integrity or functional reserve of the adrenal cortex (Fig. 2).

Vasopressor-refractory shock and treatment with corticosteroids

The assumption of a critical illness-induced state of relative adrenal insufficiency or CIRCI has been the basis for several large randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that subsequently investigated the role of adjunctive glucocorticoid treatment for patients suffering from vasopressor-dependent septic shock. All these trials investigated stress doses of 200 mg of hydrocortisone per day to further increase the systemic cortisol availability and with the intention to overcome the assumed presence of systemic glucocorticoid resistance. Although the first 2002 RCT by Annane et al investigated the impact of 200 mg IV hydrocortisone combined with 50 µg of fludrocortisone administered enterally and revealed a reduction in 28-day mortality in the intervention arm selectively in the subgroup of patients with a low incremental total cortisol response to the ACTH test, this finding was not reproduced in the subsequent RCTs that investigated the impact of 200 mg hydrocortisone without the fludrocortisone (64-66) (Table 1). In fact, only 1 other RCT, by Annane et al, found again reduced mortality, though this time only in the subgroup of patients who did respond adequately to the ACTH stimulation test (67). It is now clear that the ACTH stimulation test is flawed in this context of critical illness because of the low CBG concentrations, and thus treatment with stress doses of hydrocortisone of patients suffering from septic shock should not be seen as treatment for any form of CIRCI, but instead as a pharmacologic anti-inflammatory and blood pressure-augmenting intervention, of which the mortality benefit was not well reproduced. Although the addition of fludrocortisone to hydrocortisone is what distinguished the Annane et al trials from the other large RCTs, a study that compared hydrocortisone alone with the combination of hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone did not find a difference in outcome (68). This is not unexpected given that the used doses of hydrocortisone are known to maximally activate mineralocorticoid receptors (59). Interestingly, although the impact on mortality was inconsistent across the RCTs, a hemodynamic response to the stress doses of hydrocortisone, comprising rise in blood pressure allowing reduction of the dose or duration of vasopressor use, was observed (64-67, 75). Again, these findings likely represent a pharmacological effect of the (high doses of) hydrocortisone on the vasculature, possibly because of increased sensitivity to catecholamines (76). Stress doses of hydrocortisone indeed have previously shown to increase plasma cortisol concentrations to extremely high levels (77), as a result of reduced plasma clearance as was subsequently reported (18, 78). The recent data revealing that sepsis-induced critical illness evokes increased GRα action in most vital organs and tissues, except in neutrophils, where it is suppressed, do not provide a scientific rationale for a broad use of such stress doses of hydrocortisone in sepsis (20). Indeed, this study also showed that further increasing cortisol availability with stress doses of hydrocortisone did not overcome the resistance in the neutrophils, whereas it activated presumably adaptive responses in most tissues that prevented a further rise in GRα action. Only lung, diaphragm and adipocytes showed further increased GRα action with hydrocortisone treatment, but whether that reflects benefit or risk of harm currently remains unclear. Another recent study in a mouse model of sepsis did show that treatment with stress doses of hydrocortisone increased inflammation within the adrenal cortex, which is likely not a beneficial effect (21). Clearly, there are arguments for and against use of hydrocortisone for septic shock (Table 1). However, it is the authors’ opinion that stress doses of hydrocortisone may currently be overused in the ICU, possibly explained by the reassuring effect on the physician because of the rise in blood pressure and reduced need of other vasopressors.

The HPA Axis in Prolonged Critical Illness: no Longer Adapted to the “Unnatural” Condition of Prolonged Intensive Care Dependency?

An estimated 5% of ICU patients suffer from prolonged critical illness and remain dependent on vital organ support for weeks or longer (4, 5). Without the development of intensive care over the past 7 decades, such patients would not have survived and thus alterations typically present in this “unnatural” condition of prolonged intensive care may not all be adaptive. These long-stay patients suffer from newly ICU-acquired problems such as persisting vasopressor-dependency, lingering vital organ dysfunction, profound muscle weakness, and neurocognitive dysfunction (3). A study that investigated the time course of the HPA axis alterations in patients who were being treated in the ICU for weeks or longer has revealed that there is a central suppression of the HPA axis while these patients are in the ICU (Fig. 1, Table 1) (27). A negative impact on the ultrastructure and function of the adrenal cortex selectively in long-stay compared with short-stay patients was revealed by a postmortem study (22). Also, in the mouse model of sepsis-induced critical illness, a with time worsening of the adrenocortical distortion was observed (19). The cause of a central HPA axis suppression seems to disappear when long-stay ICU patients recover (27). Indeed, when long-stay patients were studied on the regular hospital ward, 1 week after discharge from the ICU, plasma ACTH concentrations and plasma total as well as free cortisol concentrations were higher than normal and much higher than those measured when patients were still in the ICU. This suggests a rebound activation of the HPA on recovery and thus a reversible suppression of the HPA axis while patients are in the ICU. It remains unclear which mechanisms underly this rebound HPA activation, though theoretically, a fall in bile acids concentration or the discontinuation of centrally suppressive drugs, such as opioids, could contribute. Another currently unexplored mechanism could be the resolution of inflammation, which may affect pathways such as those controlled by Annexin A1, among others (79). In addition, the role of circulating POMC or of POMC-derived fragments other than ACTH also requires further investigation (46). Evidently, treatment of long-stay ICU patients with synthetic corticosteroids, often in high doses to reduce hyperinflammation for indications such as adult respiratory distress syndrome (80, 81), may further increase the risk of suppressed HPA axis and of central adrenal failure on treatment withdrawal (44).

Whether a sustained suppression of the HPA axis, while patients are being treated in the ICU, plays a causal role in the typical prolonged critically ill phenotype remains hypothetical. These long-stay patients no longer have elevated, at times even abnormally low, plasma free cortisol levels, whereas plasma ACTH is not increased, which seems inappropriate given the high levels of stress in the ICU context (27). These patients also often have symptoms and signs that, albeit atypical and possibly explained by the illness itself, may also reflect an acquired form of central adrenal insufficiency (44) (Table 1). Also, a study that revealed low incremental ACTH responses to an IV CRH bolus suggests a central HPA axis suppression in long-stay ICU patients (45). If a central adrenal insufficiency would develop that can contribute to the prolonged critically ill phenotype, future RCTs should investigate the impact of treatment of this population of long-stay patients (Fig. 1) (Table 1). In the context of intensive care, exact diagnostic tests for central adrenal insufficiency are lacking, necessitating further investigation, though a low plasma free cortisol in the absence of high plasma ACTH in a long-stay ICU patient with clinical suspicion could be suggestive (69) (Table 1). There are several treatment modalities that could be investigated. These comprise hydrocortisone, ACTH, and CRH. If IV hydrocortisone is the therapeutic option, it seems reasonable to study the impact of a daily dose that does not exceed the daily cortisol production that has been determined in ICU patients (approximately 60 mg per day), instead of a 200-mg stress dose of hydrocortisone, to prevent off-target effects because of overdosing (18). Preferentially, a hydrocortisone dose of 40 mg administered in the morning and 20 mg in the evening may be used, followed by tapering to the lowest effective dose. An alternative strategy could be to study the impact of synthetic ACTH bolus injections, though currently the optimal number and dose of such ACTH boluses remain elusive. A continuous infusion of a synthetic CRH analogue would be another intervention to investigate, as in CRH knock-out mice, a continuous infusion of CRH has shown to restore pulsatile ACTH section and normalize adrenocortical steroidogenesis (82). However, in the mouse model of sepsis-induced critical illness, a continuous CRH infusion for 7 days somewhat increased plasma ACTH, though the impact on the adrenal cortex was negligible (21). Of course, these alternative interventions may also have off-target effects; thus, RCTs would require appropriate efficacy as well as safety endpoints (21, 83). While awaiting the evidence from future RCTs, caution must be taken not to neglect individual patient needs for treatment whenever central adrenal insufficiency is suspected (44, 69).

Conclusion and Perspectives

In conclusion, results from studies of human patients and of clinically relevant animal models performed over the past decade have challenged the classical concept of a centrally top-down activation of the HPA axis, with an assumed 8-fold increase in cortisol synthesis and secretion, as the key driver of the vital increase in systemic cortisol action in critically ill patients treated in the ICU. After a brief central HPA axis activation, peripheral responses follow that reduce the need for a sustained increase in cortisol secretion. Besides the known reduction of corticosteroid-binding proteins that increases free cortisol, these peripheral responses comprise suppressed cortisol metabolism in liver and kidney, which substantially prolongs cortisol half-life, and local alterations in expression of 11βHSD1, GRα, and FKBP51 that appear to guide and titrate increased GRα action to vital organs and tissues while reducing GRα action in neutrophils that could prevent immune-suppressive off-target effects of increased systemic cortisol availability. In addition, the peripherally increased cortisol exerts negative feedback inhibition at the pituitary level impairing processing of POMC into ACTH, thereby reducing ACTH-driven cortisol secretion, whereas ongoing central activation results in increased circulating POMC, which has steroidogenesis potential. These alterations can be seen as adaptive and beneficial for the host, at least in the short term. The new findings supersede earlier concepts such as “relative” as opposed to “absolute” adrenal insufficiency and generalized systemic glucocorticoid resistance in the critically ill. These findings call for a critical evaluation of the broad use of stress doses of hydrocortisone for patients suffering from acute septic shock based on assumed cortisol insufficiency. In contrast, the new findings suggest that when patients remain dependent on intensive care for weeks or longer, a form of acquired central (secondary) adrenal insufficiency may develop. The proposed revised concept of the HPA axis response to critical illness is far from final or complete as many aspects regarding underlying mechanisms and clinical implications remain unknown. For example, the complex and time-variable alterations within the HPA axis add substantial difficulty to the diagnosis of both primary and central adrenal insufficiency in the ICU and tests used in the outpatient setting are not valid in this setting (44, 59, 69). It is clear that more in-depth basic research in clinically relevant models and more clinical trials are needed to provide the evidence that then can form the basis for updated clinical practice guidelines. However, while awaiting the evidence, caution must be taken not to neglect treatment needs.
