Induction, amplification, and propagation of diabetic retinopathy-associated inflammatory cytokines between human retinal microvascular endothelial and Müller cells and in the mouse retina
Authors: Dolly Ann Padovani-Claudio, Monica S. Morales, Taylor E. Smith, Cayla D. Ontko, Neeraj S. Namburu, Samuel A. Palmer, Marvarakumari G. Jhala, Carla J. Ramos, Megan E. Capozzi, Gary W. McCollum, John S. Penn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ocular levels of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6 correlate with progression of diabetic retinopathy (DR). Müller cells (MC), which are crucial to maintaining retinal homeostasis, are targets and sources of these cytokines. We explored the relative capacities of these four DR-associated cytokines to amplify inflammatory signal expression both in and between human MC (hMC) and retinal microvascular endothelial cells (hRMEC) and in the mouse retina. Of the four cytokines, IL-1β was the most potent stimulus of transcriptomic alterations in hMC and hRMEC in vitro, as well as in the mouse retina after intravitreal injection in vivo. Stimulation with IL-1β significantly induced expression of all four transcripts in hMC and hRMEC. TNFα significantly induced expression of some, but not all, of the four transcripts in each cell, while neither IL-8 nor IL-6 showed significant induction in either cell. Similarly, conditioned media (CM) derived from hMC or hRMEC treated with IL-1β, but not TNFα, upregulated inflammatory cytokine transcripts in the reciprocal cell type. hRMEC responses to hMC-derived CM were dependent on IL-1R activation. In addition, we observed a correlation between cytokine expression changes following direct and CM stimulation and NFκB-p65 nuclear translocation in both hMC and hRMEC. Finally, in mice, intravitreal injections of IL-1β, but not TNFα, induced retinal expression of Il1b and CXCL8 homologues Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, and Cxcl5, encoding pro-angiogenic chemokines. Our results suggest that expression of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6 may be initiated, propagated, and sustained by autocrine and paracrine signals in hRMEC and hMC through a process involving IL-1β and NFκB. Targeting these signals may help thwart inflammatory amplification, preventing progression to vision-threatening stages and preserving sight.

Introduction

Blood-borne cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors are upregulated in diabetic patients and may activate circulating immune cells, retinal endothelial cells (EC), and perivascular glia, leading to the onset and progression of diabetic retinopathy (DR) [1]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is elevated in ocular fluids of patients with DR and is the main target of current pharmacologic DR interventions, yet many patients fail to respond to VEGF-directed treatment [2]. Elevated levels of interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα), interleukin-8 (IL-8, also known as CXCL8) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) have also been reported in whole blood and ocular fluids of patients with DR, and in mouse models of retinopathy [3-17]. Of these four cytokines, IL-1β, TNFα, and IL-6 activate type 1 cytokine receptors and are not chemotactic, while IL-8 is chemotactic, especially to neutrophils [18]. Upregulation of these four cytokines is tightly correlated with DR progression, suggesting that they may be key contributors to DR pathophysiology [1,9,11,19-22].

While a number of different retinal cells may contribute to cytokine and chemokine upregulation in the retinas of patients with diabetes, our prior work demonstrates that Müller cells (MC) and retinal microvascular endothelial cells (RMEC) are primary sources of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6 [23-32]. We hypothesize that these four cytokines may act through both autocrine and paracrine pathways to initiate, propagate, and sustain inflammatory signals between MC and RMEC, contributing to DR onset and early progression [25,33-36]. Nonetheless, the relative capacity of each of these four DR-associated cytokines to promote an inflammatory phenotype in the retina – particularly in MC and RMEC – and how their levels are amplified and sustained in and between these retinal cells is not well characterized and should be clarified [24,33,37,38].

Our overarching goal was to develop a better understanding of the roles of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6 to induce, propagate, and sustain inflammation in MC and RMEC in vitro and in the retina in vivo. Initially, we aimed to clarify the relative abilities of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6 to modulate expression of their own transcripts in human MC (hMC) and human RMEC (hRMEC). Once we learned which of the four factors elicited inflammatory responses in hMC and hRMEC (IL-1β or TNFα), we investigated their capacities to induce paracrine signals between hMC and hRMEC that could alter cytokine mRNA expression in the reciprocal cell. Expanding our study to an in vivo model of acute inflammation, we used RNA sequencing to determine changes in retinal gene expression in mice receiving intravitreal injections of IL-1β or TNFα. Finally, these datasets of differentially expressed genes, along with their predicted protein-protein interaction networks and enriched pathways, were compared to identify correlations between our in vitro and in vivo results.

Because chronic retinal inflammation contributes to vascular dysfunction, therapeutically targeting cytokines earlier in DR may help preserve retinal structure and function and, consequently, sight. Recently, non-VEGF targeted drugs, including some targeting chemokines, have received more attention as potential DR therapeutic strategies. We believe that our findings will lay the groundwork for a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms driving and sustaining retinal inflammation in DR pathophysiology and thus facilitate the development of targeted and improved therapies.

Methods

Primary human Müller cell (hMC) isolation and culture

Human donor eyecups were received within 24 h postmortem from the National Disease Research Interchange (Philadelphia, PA, USA). Retinas were dissected and dissociated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagles Medium (DMEM; #11885084, Life Technologies; Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing trypsin (#27250018, Life Technologies) and collagenase (#C9722, Sigma Aldrich; Saint Louis, MO, USA). Following incubation in this dissociation medium, hMC were isolated using established methods [39] and cultured in DMEM with 1× antibiotic solution (#97063–708, VWR; Suwanee, GA, USA) and 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; #S11550, R&D Systems; Minneapolis, MN, USA). For hMC cultured under serum-reduced conditions, DMEM with 1× antibiotic solution was supplemented with 2 % FBS. Cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, 20.9 % O2, and 95 % relative humidity. The identity and purity of hMC were confirmed by immunocytochemistry with antibodies against cellular retinaldehyde-binding protein (#sc-59,487, Santa Cruz Biotechnology; Dallas, TX, USA), glutathione synthetase (#sc-166,882, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (#NB30014, Fisher Scientific; Suwanee, GA, USA). Passages 4 to 6 were used for all experiments.

Primary human retinal microvascular endothelial cell (hRMEC) culture

hRMEC (#ACBRI 181, Cell Systems; Kirkland, WA, USA) were grown on plastic dishes coated with attachment factor (#4Z0–210, Cell Systems) in phenol red-free endothelial basal medium (EBM; #4z3–500-R, Cell Systems) supplemented with 10 % FBS and SingleQuots (#CC-4133, Lonza; Walkersville, MD, USA). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C, 5 % CO2, 20.9 % O2, and 95 % relative humidity. Passages 6 to 8 were used for all experiments.

Cytokine stimulation of hMC and hRMEC

hMC were seeded at 3 × 104 cells/well and hRMEC were seeded at 1.5 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates under normal growth conditions. At 70–80 % confluency, hMC were placed in serum-reduced medium for 12 h, followed by treatment for 2, 4, 8, 12, or 24 h with vehicle [0.1 % bovine serum albumin (BSA; #A8806, Sigma Aldrich) in phosphate buffered saline (PBS; #10010031, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Asheville, NC, USA)] or human recombinant IL-1β (#201-LB/CF, R&D Systems), TNFα (#210-TA, R&D Systems), IL-8 (#208-IL/CF, R&D Systems), or IL-6 (#206-IL/CF, R&D Systems) each reconstituted in vehicle at 1 ng/mL. When hRMEC were 75–85 % confluent, they were treated for 4 h with vehicle or human recombinant IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, or IL-6 each reconstituted in vehicle at a concentration of 1 ng/mL.

Conditioned media (CM) generation and stimulation of hMC and hRMEC

hMC and hRMEC were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured under normal growth conditions until 70–80 % confluent. hMC were placed in serum-reduced medium for 8–12 h before stimulation, while hRMEC remained in normal growth media. Cells were treated with vehicle or 1 ng/mL of IL-1β or TNFα in their respective serum-reduced or normal media. After 2 h, media were removed (to remove the exogenous IL-1β or TNFα stimulus) and replaced with fresh serum-reduced EBM. After 6 h, the media was collected and centrifuged at 14,000 xG to remove dead cells and cellular debris, resulting in conditioned media (CM). The CM from donor hMC was used to treat recipient hRMEC, while the CM from donor hRMEC was used to treat recipient hMC. All recipient cells were cultured in 6-well plates under otherwise normal growth conditions and stimulated for 4 h with CM.

IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA) inhibition of CM stimulation in hRMEC

hRMEC were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured under normal growth conditions until they reached 80 % confluency. After 2 h pre-treatment with vehicle (sterile distilled water) or 10 ng/mL of human IL-1RA recombinant protein (#RP-10925, Thermo Fisher Scientific), hRMEC were co-treated for 4 h with CM derived from IL-1β-, TNFα-, or vehicle-stimulated hMC and with IL-1RA or its vehicle.

Intravitreal cytokine injection and tissue extraction

All experiments were approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and were performed in accordance with the ARVO statement for the use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Adult C57BL/6 mice were treated topically with 1 % tropicamide (#17478–102, Arkorn; Gurnee, IL, USA) and 0.5 % proparacaine hydrochloride (#17478–263, Arkorn) and anesthetized with inhalant isoflurane (#029405, Covetrus; Chicago, IL, USA). Intravitreal injections of vehicle (<0.1 % BSA) or 50 ng/mL mouse recombinant IL-1β (#401-ML/CF, R&D Systems) or TNFα (#410-MT/CF, R&D Systems) were performed using custom-made 33-gauge Hamilton syringes with detachable needles (0.375″ long and 19° bevel; Hamilton; Reno, NV, USA). Injections were made temporally, 0.5 mm posterior to the ora, with a 2 μL volume administered to each eye. Eyes were treated with 0.3 % tobramycin antibiotic (#61314–643, Sandoz; Princeton, NJ, USA) immediately after each injection. Mice were euthanized 6 h after intravitreal injections and their retinas were harvested for RNA sequencing.

RNA extraction

After treatment, media was collected, and cells were washed twice with cold PBS. The cells were lysed, and total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (#74104, Qiagen; Germantown, MD, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Retinas were dissected into lysis buffer and homogenized in a TissueLyser LT (#85600, Qiagen). Total RNA was extracted from mouse retinas using a similar method to that used for cells. Total RNA from cells and retinas was quantified using a BioTek Synergy Multimode Plate reader and BioTek Gen5 2.09 software before being processed for RNA sequencing or qRT-PCR.

RNA sequencing

hMC (8 h post-stimulation) and mouse retina (6 h post-intravitreal injection) RNA samples were submitted to the Vanderbilt Technologies for Advanced Genomics (VANTAGE) core (Nashville, TN, USA) for RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq). RNA quality was determined using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 Instrument (Agilent; Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA-Seq libraries were prepared using 200 ng of total RNA and the NEBNext rRNA Depletion Kit (#E6310X, New England Biolabs; Ipswich, MA, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. This kit employs an RNaseH-based method to deplete both cytoplasmic (5S rRNA, 5.8S rRNA, 18S rRNA, and 28S rRNA) and mitochondrial ribosomal RNA (12S rRNA and 16S rRNA) from human and mouse total RNA preparations. The mRNA was enriched via poly-A-selection using oligoDT beads (New England Biolabs) and then the RNA was thermally fragmented and converted to cDNA. The cDNA was adenylated for adaptor ligation and PCR amplified. The libraries were sequenced using the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina; San Diego, CA) with 150 bp paired end reads targeting 50 M reads per sample. Illumina Real Time Analysis NovaSeq Control Software (1.8.0) was used for base calling.

Differential gene expression analysis

Sequence alignments and differential expression of human and mouse mRNAs were performed by the Vanderbilt Technologies for Advanced Genomics Analysis and Research Design (VANGARD) core. TopHat v2.0.9 was used to align sequences to the UCSC human reference genome hg19 using default parameters [40]. Raw counts of mapped reads were generated and then used by the MultiRankSeq program, which utilizes the DeSeq2 algorithm to determine differential expression [41,42]. For mouse retinas, transcript level abundance was estimated using Salmon software (v1.9.0). DESeq2 (v1.36.0) was used to identify significantly differentially expressed genes [42].

Comparisons were made between primary hMC cultures treated with vehicle vs. IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, or IL-6, and between retinas from mice receiving intravitreal injections of vehicle vs. IL-1β or TNFα. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are defined as transcriptome alterations with an adjusted p value less than 0.05 with an absolute fold change larger than 2.0 or smaller than 0.50. Functional protein-protein interaction networks were created from DEGs using Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) [43] with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 threshold for enrichment. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) tool, with a Bonferroni Correction applied using a p < 0.05 threshold [44,45].

Real time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR)

RNA extracted from hMC and hRMEC was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (#4368814, Life Technologies). qRT-PCR was performed by coamplification of IL1B (#4331182 Assay Id Hs015555410_m1, Life Technologies), TNF (#4331182 Assay Id Hs00174128_m1, Life Technologies), CXCL8 (#4331182 Assay Id Hs00174103_m, Life Technologies) or IL6 (#4331182 Assay Id Hs00985639_m1, Life Technologies) vs. TBP (TATA-binding protein, #4331182, Assay Id Hs00427620_m1; Life Technologies) or 18S (#4331182, Assay Id Hs99999901_s1; Life Technologies) as endogenous normalization controls. Each gene expression assay was performed using the TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (#4444556, Life Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3 Real Time PCR System/Machine (#A2813, Life Technologies). Amplicon lengths and gene loci for each assay are available on the vendor’s website (https://www.thermofisher.com/taqman/gene-expression/assay). Expression data were analyzed by the comparative threshold cycle (ΔΔCT) method. PCR data are normalized to the mean of the housekeeping gene and reported as mean ± standard deviations of these normalized values. GraphPad Prism software was used for all statistical analyses. For timecourse qRT-PCR experiments in hMC, a two-way ANOVA with full fit model was performed with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons post hoc test. An ordinary one-way ANOVA was performed for all other qRT-PCR experiments, with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons post hoc test with a single pooled variance used for experiments using direct cytokine stimulation and Tukey’s multiple comparisons post hoc test with a single pooled variance used for experiments involving NFκB signaling inhibitors and those using CM stimulation.

NFκB nuclear translocation in hMC and hRMEC treated with cytokines or CM

Sterile 12-mm circular glass coverslips (#12–545-81, Fisher Scientific) were placed in the bottom of 24-well plates and seeded with 5 × 104 hMC or 1 × 105 hRMEC per coverslip. At 75 % confluence, hMC were switched from 10 % FBS-containing medium to serum-reduced medium for 12 h, then treated for 30 mins with vehicle; 1 ng/mL of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, or IL-6; or with CM from vehicle- or IL-1β-stimulated hRMEC. At 75 % confluence, hRMEC were treated for 30 mins with vehicle; 1 ng/mL of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, or IL-6; or with CM from vehicle- or IL-1β-stimulated hMC. Cells were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 1× PBS with 0.3 % Triton X-100 (#T8787, Sigma Aldrich) for 10 mins and blocked in 2 % nonfat dry milk (#1706404; Bio-Rad Laboratories; Hercules, CA, USA) for 1 h. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with 1:200 primary mouse antibody against NFκB-p65 (#SC-8008, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) diluted in 2 % milk blocking solution. After washing three times with 1× TBS, cells were then incubated for 24 h at 4 °C with 1:750 donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor-647- (#A3157, Thermo Fisher Scientific) conjugated secondary antibody diluted in 2 % milk blocking solution containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, #574810, Fisher Scientific) for nuclear counterstaining. Cells were imaged using an inverted wide field fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX83; Tokyo, Japan) at 10× and 20× magnification for hMC and hRMEC, respectively. Total number of NFκB-p65+ nuclei and total DAPI+ nuclei were counted in four random fields per treatment group, with the ratio of NFκB-p65+ to DAPI+ nuclei presented as mean ± standard deviation for each group. An ordinary one-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s multiple comparisons post hoc test with a single pooled variance were performed using GraphPad Prism software.

NFκB signaling inhibition

hMC or hRMEC were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured under normal growth conditions until 80 % confluent. hMC were then placed in serum-reduced medium for 12 h before treatment while hRMEC were maintained in normal growth medium until treatment. Cells were pre-treated for 30 mins with fresh 2 % media containing vehicle [0.00435 % dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) #D8418, Sigma Aldrich] or 5 μM of the NFκB signaling inhibitor IKK2i-VI (#17276, Cayman; Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Vehicle (0.1 % BSA), 1 ng/mL IL-1β or 1 ng/mL TNFα were then added for an additional 4 h. Cultures were photographed every hour to monitor cell viability. NFκB-p65 immunoreactivity was used to detect nuclear translocation 30 min post-stimulation, and qRT-PCR was used to quantify inflammatory cytokine transcripts 4 h post-stimulation according to methods described above.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

IL-1β (#DLB50, R&D Systems) and TNFα (#DTA00D, R&D Systems) concentrations in CM derived from hMC were determined via ELISA following the company’s protocol. Protein amounts were normalized to CM volume, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Effect of cytokine stimulation on the hMC transcriptome

Müller cells span all neuroretinal layers, are in immediate proximity to other retinal cells, and are a major source of cytokines in the retina [24,46,47]. Several cytokines, including, IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6, are significantly elevated in ocular fluid samples from patients with advanced stages of DR and all are implicated in DR pathophysiology [3,46,47]. Therefore, we investigated the relative potencies of these cytokines to induce inflammatory transcriptome changes in hMC.

First, using qRT-PCR, we investigated the timecourse of IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, and IL6 mRNA expression profiles in hMC in response to stimulation with each of their four respective recombinant proteins for 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h. Only IL-1β stimulation induced expression of all four transcripts (Fig. 1, blue bars). TNFα stimulation (Fig. 1, red bars) elicited significant induction of some transcripts, but these effects were, in general, smaller than those observed after IL-1β stimulation. Neither IL-8 nor IL-6 significantly altered expression of the transcripts encoding any of the four cytokines of interest during the timecourse chosen. IL1B, TNF, and CXCL8 were elevated throughout the timecourse following stimulation with either IL-1β or TNFα, with TNF expression peaking 2–4 h post-treatment and IL1B and CXCL8 expression peaking at 8 h. The upregulation of IL1B and TNF was higher after stimulation with IL-1β than with TNFα, while CXCL8 upregulation by both was similar. The largest fold inductions observed, regardless of stimulant, were of TNF and CXCL8 expression.

Next, in order to characterize differential gene expression across the full genome in an unbiased fashion and using our qRT-PCR findings as a guide for timing, we conducted bulk RNA sequencing of hMC after 8 h of stimulation with IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6. Consistent with our PCR results, only stimulation with IL-1β and TNFα resulted in significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs, an adjusted p value <0.05 and fold change >2.0 or < 0.50) in hMC. These RNA-Seq experiments not only allowed an alternative method to compare the relative induction of our four transcripts of greatest interest, but also provided an unbiased confirmation of lack of hMC induction of our select PCR targets in response to IL-8 and IL-6 stimulation. Finally, it allowed us to explore overlap between IL-1β- and TNFα-induced transcriptome alterations.

Next, to further support our qRT-PCR results, we specifically focused our attention on expression changes in transcripts encoding the four specific inflammatory cytokines used for stimulation. In addition, we assessed expression of a few other DR-relevant inflammatory markers and of VEGF, the main target of most current pharmacologic interventions for DR (Fig. 2). Stimulation with either IL-1β or TNFα significantly upregulated IL1B, CXCL8, and IL6 expression; however, only stimulation with IL-1β also significantly upregulated TNF (Fig. 2). In addition, both IL-1β and TNFα upregulated the expression of NFKB2 (FCIL-1β = 5; FCTNFα = 4) and of the cell adhesion molecules VCAM1 (FCIL-1β = 179; FCTNFα = 83) and ICAM1 (FCIL-1β = 63; FCTNFα = 25). Expression of the adhesion molecule E-selectin (SELE) was only significantly upregulated by IL-1β (FC = 6486, out of range of figure). Of note, expression of transcripts encoding VEGF-A was not significantly altered by stimulation with any of our four cytokines of interest. Overall, and consistent with our qRT-PCR results, IL-1β was shown to elicit the strongest increases in expression of IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, and IL6.

We next looked at all differentially expressed genes. IL-1β significantly altered 907 total transcripts and TNFα significantly altered 157 total transcripts (Fig. 3A). Again, IL-8 and IL-6 stimulation did not significantly alter any transcripts in hMC. This pattern informed our decision to use only IL-1β and TNFα in later experiments. To better understand the relevance of the observed transcriptome alterations in hMC in the context of DR, we performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis for all DEG after either IL-1β or TNFα stimulation (Fig. 3B). While pathways enriched by IL-1β stimulation outnumbered those enriched by TNFα, there was significant overlap between them. Nine of the 16 pathways affected by stimulation with IL-1β were also affected by TNFα, and the same three pathways were identified as the most highly enriched by both stimuli. These included, in descending order, the “TNF signaling pathway”, the “IL-17 signaling pathway”, and the “NF-kappa B signaling pathway” (Fig. 3B). Even though TNFα did not directly autoregulate TNF expression in hMC under the experimental conditions employed, our pathway enrichment data suggest that TNF signaling may play an important role in regulating hMC responses to these diabetesrelevant cytokines.

Among the four cytokines of greatest interest to us, transcripts for IL-1β (FCIL-1β = 467; FCTNFα = 10) and IL-8 (FCIL-1β = 443; FCTNFα = 41), were among the top 15 DEGs after hMC stimulation with IL-1β and TNFα. To explore the potential pathogenic effects of IL-1β and TNFα upregulation in disease, we then specifically focused on the top 15 upregulated and top 10 downregulated transcripts altered in hMC after IL-1β or TNFα stimulation (“top DEGs”, Fig. 4A). A handful of the top upregulated (CCL20, IL1B, CXCL8, CXCL3, and VCAM1) and downregulated (FAM13C, EGR3, HS3ST1, and MYOD1) transcripts (bolded, Fig. 4A) overlapped between the two stimuli. Furthermore, several of the most highly upregulated transcripts encoded cytokines, chemokines, and cell adhesion molecules that have been previously implicated in DR pathology. Specific examples included transcripts encoding the transcription factor NFKB2, the cell adhesion molecules VCAM1, and ICAM1, and the proangiogenic CXCR2 ligands (CXCL1 and CXCL2, in addition to CXCL3 and CXCL8). Using STRING protein-protein association analyses, a large fraction of proteins coded by the top DEGs are predicted to associate in biologically relevant networks (Fig. 4B) with a few (CCL20, CXCL2, and CXCL10) predicted to have high co-expression. The top enriched tissue association was to vascular cells and the top relevant enriched biological categories related to chemotaxis, chemokine signaling, and NFκB complexes (Fig. 4C). This suggests a link between inflammatory amplification in MC and induction of vasculopathy.

Finally, we focused our attention on all DEGs exhibiting overlap after hMC stimulation with IL-1β or TNFα, since both proteins are upregulated in the vitreous in DR and could act synergistically. We examined the predicted protein networks (Fig. 5A) and top enrichments (Fig. 5B) after hMC stimulation. Several top STRING-defined enriched biological categories related to IL-1 processing and to NFκB and CXC chemokine signaling. In addition, the top tissue enrichment by these overlapping DEGs was for endothelial cells. Our focus thus turned to hRMEC and their relationship with hMC in propagating inflammatory stimuli.

Effect of cytokine stimulation on inflammatory cytokine expression in human retinal microvascular endothelial cells

Our RNA-Seq analysis pointed to endothelial cells as a primary downstream target of the hMC transcriptome alterations elicited by IL-1β or TNFα. Notably, retinal microvascular endothelial cells are considered the main effectors of DR progression to vision-threatening stages. Thus, mimicking our strategy in hMC, we investigated expression profiles of IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, and IL6 mRNA following stimulation of hRMEC with the four respective recombinant proteins.

As observed in hMC, IL-1β stimulation significantly induced expression of all four transcripts in hRMEC, namely, IL1B (FC = 198, p < 0.0001), TNF (FC = 186, p < 0.0001), CXCL8 (FC = 28, p < 0.0001), and IL6 (FC = 35, p < 0.0001; Fig. 6). TNFα stimulation of hRMEC resulted in similar expression trends, although only its induction of IL1B reached statistical significance – IL1B (FC =49, p < 0.0001), TNF (FC = 51,p = 0.4284), and CXCL8 (FC = 12, p = 0.0564). TNFα stimulation did not alter expression of IL6. Finally, as seen after hMC stimulation, neither IL-8 nor IL-6 induced significant changes in expression of any of the four main transcripts investigated in hRMEC. Thus, IL-1β was the only stimulus able to upregulate all four cytokine mRNAs in both hMC and hRMEC, suggesting that IL-1β may be a master inducer of inflammation through cytokine induction and amplification.

Effects of cytokine stimulation on NFκB activation in hMC and hRMEC

NFκB signaling is known to be involved in cytokine and chemokine transcriptional regulation and amplification in multiple organ systems, including the retina [48-50]. Our KEGG pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes in hMC revealed NFκB signaling to be among the top enriched pathways involving transcripts altered after IL-1β and TNFα stimulation (Fig. 3B). Likewise, NFκB signaling also dominated the lists of enriched biological processes from WikiPathways, InterPro, and Gene Ontology reference databases for transcripts co-regulated by IL-1β and TNFα in hMC (Fig. 4C). We therefore explored NFκB nuclear translocation in hMC and hRMEC in response to inflammatory cytokine stimulation. Consistent with observed transcriptome alterations, only IL-1β and TNFα (but not IL-8 or IL-6) significantly induced nuclear translocation of the NFκB p65 subunit in both hMC and hRMEC (Fig. 7). This consistent pattern further validated our decision to use only IL-1β and TNFα in later experiments.

Given this overlap, we investigated the contribution of NFκB signaling to the observed amplification of cytokine transcripts elicited by stimulation of hMC and hRMEC with IL-1β and TNFα using IKK2 inhibitor VI (IKK2i-VI) [51]. We co-treated hMC and hRMEC with IL-1β or TNFα in the presence or absence of the NFκB signaling inhibitor for 4 h, then assessed both NFκB p65 nuclear translocation and transcriptional alterations in expression of IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, and IL6.

IKK2i-VI fully blocked IL-1β- and TNFα-induced NFκB p65 subunit nuclear translocation (Fig. 8A, left). IKK2i-VI also significantly reduced hMC upregulation of IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, and IL6 in response to IL-1β stimulation and the hMC upregulation of IL1B and CXCL8 in response to TNFα stimulation (Fig. 8B, top). In hRMEC (Fig. 8A, right), IKK2i-VI blocked (~80 %) IL-1β- and TNFα-induced NFκB p65 subunit nuclear translocation. Additionally, IKK2i-VI significantly inhibited the upregulation of IL1B, TNF, and IL6 (but not CXCL8) in response to IL-1β stimulation, and the upregulation of IL1B and TNF in response to TNFα (Fig. 8B, bottom). IKK2i-VI’s reduction in NFκB p65 nuclear translocation and IL1B upregulation in both hMC and hRMEC in response to IL-1β and TNFα stimulation suggests that cytokine-induced IL1B and TNF amplification in these cells is at least partially NFκB-dependent.

Role of inflammatory cytokines in stimulation of hRMEC by hMC-derived conditioned medium.

Cumulatively, the data described above indicate that direct stimulation of hMC and hRMEC with IL-1β or TNFα, but not IL-8 or IL-6, induces NFκB p65 nuclear translocation and upregulation of the expression of our four inflammatory cytokines of interest. It also suggests that the resulting upregulation of IL1B and CXCL8 in hMC and of IL1B and TNF in hRMEC are at least partially NFκB-dependent.

Müller cells are known to affect retinal EC behaviors through paracrine signals and our hMC RNA-Seq pathway enrichment analyses identified “endothelial cells” as the target most likely to be affected by the transcriptome changes in hMC. Therefore, we sought to determine whether cytokine stimulation of hMC could result in paracrine signals capable of increasing cytokine expression in hRMEC. Since only IL-1β and TNFα upregulated IL1B and TNF in hMC after direct stimulation (Fig. 1, blue and red bars) and only IL-1β and TNFα had direct effects on hRMEC cytokine induction (Fig. 6, blue and red bars), we explored the ability of these two cytokines to induce paracrine signals from hMC to hRMEC using transfer of hMC-conditioned media (CM) to hRMEC.

After treating hMC with IL-1β or TNFα for 2 h, we replaced the media with fresh culture media to remove the exogenous cytokine stimulus. hMC-derived factors then were allowed to accumulate in the fresh culture media for 6 h to generate CM uncontaminated by exogenous stimulatory cytokines. This protocol (Fig. 9A) ensures that only factors secreted by the hMC, and not the exogenous cytokines, which we know have direct effects on the recipient hRMEC, were transferred to them. The hMC CM was used to stimulate hRMEC for 30 min to assess NFκB p65 subunit nuclear translocation and for 4 h to assess cytokine expression by qRT-PCR. Stimulation of hRMEC with CM from IL-1β-treated hMC significantly increased NFκB p65 sub-unit nuclear translocation (Fig. 9B, C) and transcript upregulation of IL1B (FC = 31, p < 0.0001), TNF (FC = 18, p < 0.0001), CXCL8 (FC = 10, p < 0.0001), and IL6 (FC = 9, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 9D) in hRMEC. Conversely, CM from TNFα-treated hMC did not elicit significant NFκB p65 sub-unit nuclear translocation or alterations in expression of any of the four targets in hRMEC.

Role of IL-1β in paracrine signals from hMC to hRMEC in conditioned medium experiments.

We had previously observed that, although direct stimulation with either IL-1β or TNFα increased IL1B, TNF, and CXCL8 expression in hRMEC, only direct stimulation with IL-1β increased IL6 expression. Given that IL6 expression was also increased in hRMEC following treatment with hMC-derived CM, we suspected that IL-1β secreted by hMC, and thus present in the CM, contributed to the inflammatory cytokine transcript induction observed in hRMEC. We therefore used ELISA to assess protein levels of soluble IL-1β and TNFα in the CM generated from IL-1β- or TNFα-stimulated hMC. Soluble IL-1β was increased in the CM of IL-1β-stimulated hMC but not in the CM of TNFα-stimulated hMC (Fig. 10). Soluble TNFα levels were undetectable in either CM (data not shown). These data suggest that IL-1β auto-amplification may be largely responsible for the bioactivity observed in hMC-derived CM resulting in increased cytokine expression in hRMEC and that IL-1β may comprise an important paracrine signal between hMC and hRMEC in vivo.

To corroborate the contribution of IL-1β to the proinflammatory activity of CM, we tested the effects of IL-1RA (IL-1 receptor antagonist) on hRMEC cytokine transcript induction by hMC-derived CM. After a 2 h pre-treatment with 10 ng/mL of IL-1RA, we co-treated hRMEC with a combination of IL-1RA and CM from IL-1β- or TNFα-stimulated hMC (Fig. 11A). As was the case in direct stimulation experiments, CM from IL-1β-stimulated hMC upregulated IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, and IL6 expression in hRMEC. These effects were significantly reduced by IL-1RA co-treatment (Fig. 11B). This suggests that IL-1R-dependent signaling contributes to the cytokine expression changes induced in hRMEC by the secretome of IL-1β-stimulated hMC. Furthermore, the CM of TNFα-stimulated hMC, which had undetectable levels of IL-1β did not cause significant cytokine transcript upregulation in hRMEC.

Role of inflammatory cytokines in stimulation of hMC by hRMEC-derived conditioned medium.

After determining that IL-1β-stimulated, but not TNFα-stimulated, hMC produced CM with sufficient bioactivity to elicit inflammatory transcriptome changes in hRMEC (Figs. 9 and 11), and understanding that the communication between these cells could be bidirectional, we explored the reverse scenario. We used qRT-PCR to assess changes in hMC inflammatory cytokine expression in response to CM generated from IL-1β- or TNFα-stimulated hRMEC (Fig. 12A). Similar to observations in experiments wherein the CM source were hMC and hRMEC were the recipients, only the CM generated from IL-1β-stimulated hRMEC had sufficient bioactivity to induce NFκB p65 sub-unit nuclear translocation (FC = 76, p < 0.0001; (Fig. 12B, C) and to upregulate expression of our four primary targets, IL1B (FC = 12, p < 0.0001), TNF (FC = 27, p = 0.0016), CXCL8 (FC =20, p < 0.0001), and IL6 (FC = 7, p < 0.0001) in hMC (Fig. 12D). Together, these experiments suggest that IL-1β can modulate bidirectional crosstalk between hMC and hRMEC with the capacity to upregulate inflammatory cytokine expression and increase IL-1β protein levels. Our results suggest the existence of a positive feedback mechanism that can sustain IL-1β elevation between these cells, which could potentially lead to and worsen chronic retinal inflammation and retinopathy progression.

Stimulation of retinal inflammation by IL-1β and TNFα

Recognizing that MC are in close contact with cells other than RMEC in the retina – all of which are also potentially susceptible to inflammatory responses after IL-1β and TNFα stimulation – we used RNA-Seq to survey the whole retinal transcriptome in mice receiving intravitreal injections of mouse recombinant IL-1β or TNFα [25,31,52,53] (Fig. 13). We first evaluated the spectrum of differentially expressed genes in the mouse retina after intravitreal injection of IL-1β or TNFα, which resulted in 530 and 125 differentially expressed genes, respectively (Fig. 13A). Next, to better understand the functional relevance of the retinal transcriptome alterations, we performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of all DEG after intravitreal injection of IL-1β or TNFα in mice (Fig. 13B). The TNF signaling pathway demonstrated the highest fold enrichment after intravitreal IL-1β injection (as was seen via RNA-Seq in hMC following direct stimulation with IL-1β). This was also the only pathway enriched by all the top DEGs after intravitreal injection of either cytokine. This, again, suggests a role for TNF signaling in the amplification of cell responses to IL-1β and TNFα stimulation. All of the six pathways enriched in the mouse retina after TNFα intravitreal injection (i.e., allograft rejection, graft versus host disease, Type I diabetes mellitus, TNF signaling, phagosome, and NOD-like receptor signaling) were also enriched following intravitreal injection of IL-1β (Fig. 13B).

The top 15 upregulated and the top 10 downregulated protein coding transcripts (“top DEGs”) in mice after intravitreal injections of mouse recombinant IL-1β or TNFα are illustrated in Fig. 14A. These included Cxcl5, Cxcl10, Ccl7, Icam1, and Vcam1. Of interest, these same transcripts were among the top 15 upregulated transcripts identified by RNA-Seq in IL-1β- or TNFα-stimulated hMC. A large fraction of the proteins coded by these top DEGs are predicted to have strong associations in biologically relevant networks, with Cxcl10 showing the highest predicted co-expression with other proteins encoded by top DEGs in mouse retina (Fig. 14B). The top tissue enrichment was also in endothelial cells and the top enriched categories related to cytokine/chemokine signaling, protein-membrane interactions/cell adhesion, and crystallins (Fig. 14C).

We then turned our attention to the effects of intravitreal injections on transcripts specifically encoding our four inflammatory cytokines of interest (Fig. 15A) as well as on several other transcripts implicated in DR which we previously explored in hMC. Since mice do not express CXCL8, in addition to analyzing changes in Il1b, Tnf, and Il6 expression, we assessed differential expression of the CXCL8 mouse homologues Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, Cxcl5, and Cxcl6. Consistent with findings from RNA-Seq analysis in hMC, intravitreal injection of IL-1β, but not TNFα, altered the expression levels of these targets in retinal tissue.

Specifically, intravitreal IL-1β injection upregulated its own retinal expression (FC = 5.0) and the expression of Cxcl1 (FC = 5.9), Cxcl3 (FC = 4.4), and Cxcl5 (FC = 9.6) but did not affect the expression of Il6 and Tnf. Furthermore, intravitreal injection of both IL-1β and TNFα upregulated retinal expression of Nfkb2 (FCIL-1β = 5.0; FCTNFα = 4.9), Icam (FCIL-1β = 5.8; FCTNFα = 6.4), and Vcam (FCIL-1β = 5.8; FCTNFα = 6.4). As observed in hMC, Vegfa expression in the mouse retina was not significantly altered by intravitreal injection of either IL-1β or TNFα.

Finally, we examined the correlations between DEGs in hMC (which we suspect to be a primary effector and propagator of inflammatory responses in retinopathy) and those in retinal tissue following stimulation with IL-1β and TNFα. Of the ten specific transcripts that were upregulated by both stimuli in both contexts, nine (BIRC3, NFKB2, RELB, ICAM1, VCAM, TLR2, GBP2, PSMB9, and TAP1) formed a predicted protein-protein interaction network (Fig. 15B). KEGG analysis using the DAVID database revealed enrichment of the NFκB signaling pathway by these transcripts. This pathway was also implicated in STRING enrichment analyses using cluster, pathway, cellular compartment, tissue, and biological process databases. The highest tissue enrichment in STRING was for coronary artery endothelial cells. Finally, the top enriched categories in the STRING network analyses reflected membrane processes (guanylate binding/vacuole/docking) and Toll-like receptor signaling (Fig. 15C).

These enriched pathways, networks, tissues, and processes may shed light on important functional consequences, such as the accumulation of inflammatory cytokines in the vitreous of patients with DR, in which IL-1β and TNFα levels are known to rise and to correlate with DR progression.

Discussion

Cytokines as mediators of systemic disease and DR

Blood-borne cytokines and chemokines are elevated in diabetic patients and may activate circulating immune cells, retinal endothelial cells, and perivascular glia. These systemic factors can trigger expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6 in retinal cells, including MC and EC, leading to the onset and progression of retinopathy [26,30,54,55]. Indeed, serum levels of these four cytokines correlate with the development of retinopathy in patients with diabetes, while their elevation in the aqueous and vitreous correlate with its severity [10,21,56-64]. However, how their expression is amplified and sustained in the retina, an immunoprotected organ, is not well established.

Müller cells not only constitute a major source of inflammatory cytokines in the retina, but are also highly sensitive to them [24,38,50,65,66]. MC dysfunction has been suspected to contribute substantially to the chronic low-level inflammation and vascular manifestations of vision-threatening DR [67,68]. MC are positioned to be a major source of pathogenic retinal factors that could influence EC responses resulting in DR’s clinical phenotype. We suspect that both MC and EC contribute to DR in part through inflammatory cytokine induction, amplification, and persistence via autocrine and paracrine signals.

To test this presumption and to better understand the hierarchy of inflammatory cytokine potencies, we examined the capacity of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6 to alter expression of their respective encoding genes (IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, and IL6) in both hMC and hRMEC. Additionally, we performed RNA-seq analysis of hMC stimulated with our four cytokines/chemokines of interest. This step was taken in order to expand the scope of our assessment of the responses elicited in hMC and to begin to define what signaling pathways may drive the inflammatory cascade. Because we observed that, among our four cytokines of interest, only direct stimulation with IL-1β and TNFα induced inflammatory responses in either cell, we subsequently evaluated the capacity of conditioned media (CM) from only IL-1β- and TNFα-stimulated hMC and hRMEC to induce inflammation in the reciprocal cell. CM from IL-1β-, but not TNFα-, stimulated cells elicited changes in gene expression. Finally, the transcriptome alterations to intravitreal injection of IL-1β and TNFα in the mouse retina were correlated with our results in hMC and hRMEC, validating the utility of our in vitro approaches to predict responses in a complex, multiceullular setting. Taken together, our findings suggest that, of the four DR-associated cytokines studied, IL-1β is the most potent stimulus of inflammatory transcriptome alterations in hMC, hRMEC, and the mouse retina. TNFα demonstrating the next most potency. We found that NFκB activation correlated with cytokine induction in hMC and hRMEC and that inflammatory propagation through CM transfer from hMC to hRMEC was dependent upon IL-1R activation.

IL-1β as master regulator

Several studies have identified IL-1β as a master regulator of inflammatory responses [69-71], and it has long been suspected to contribute to DR pathophysiology [56,57,59,72-75]. In addition, IL-1β has been proposed to facilitate the interplay between inflammatory and angiogenic responses in tissues [70,76]. Using rats, Liu et al. reported that IL-1β mRNA was upregulated in rat retinas soon after diabetes induction, and that this upregulation was sustained and correlated with the accumulation of gliosis markers of disease progression [38]. They also reported that rat IL-1β induced its own mRNA in glia [38]. Our findings from in vitro experiments using direct cytokine stimulation of human primary MC support these prior results and recognize IL-1β as the most potent of the four cytokine stimuli evaluated in hMC and hRMEC.

IL-1β significantly upregulated cytokine mRNAs, including its own transcript, in hMC and hRMEC, consistent with the capacity to initiate retinal inflammation. We also demonstrated that IL-1β can further amplify retinal inflammation through our CM experiments, in which CM derived from IL-1β-treated hMC increased cytokine expression in hRMEC, and CM derived from IL-1β-treated hRMEC did the same in hMC. This also suggests that IL-1β can sustain retinal inflammation by auto-amplification and by maintaining elevated levels inflammatory cytokines, including itself, through positive feedback.

We detected IL-1β protein, but not TNFα, in CM from IL-1β-treated hMC. Furthermore, the IL-1β receptor antagonist, IL-1RA, diminished inflammatory cytokine mRNA induction in hRMEC treated with stimulus-depleted CM derived from IL-1β-stimulated hMC. These data suggest that IL-1β comprises a significant portion of the bioactivity in the CM derived from hMC, and that the direct activation of IL-1R is important for hRMEC to respond to this putative paracrine signal. Our in vitro findings incriminating IL-1β were further confirmed in the transition to an in vivo setting, where intravitreal injection of IL-1β caused a significant increase in retinal Il1b expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that the capacity of IL-1β alone to initiate, amplify, and sustain inflammatory signals alone may be sufficient to promote chronic retinal inflammation. This capacity extends beyond that associated with TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6, whose ocular levels also correlate with DR.

When used as a direct stimulus in hMC and hRMEC, TNFα, like IL-1β, also initiated inflammation, as shown by its induction of IL1B, TNF, and CXCL8. However, CM from TNF-stimulated hMC or hRMEC did not alter cytokine expression in the reciprocal cell, thus failing to acutely propagate inflammatory signals between them. Likewise, TNFα also failed to upregulate retinal expression of Il1b, Tnf, CXCL8 homologues (CXCL8 is not expressed in mice), or IL6 after intravitreal injection, suggesting it may also be unable to acutely propagate and sustain inflammatory cytokine signals in vivo.

TNFα supportive roles

Intravitreal injection of TNFα did upregulate the expression of Nfkb2, Vcam1, and Cxcl10 in the retinas of mice, factors which are associated with chronic inflammatory responses and immune cell recruitment. One of these in particular, NFκB, is a transcription factor highly implicated in eliciting inflammatory responses in other cell types, tissues, and organ systems [77-79]. Our experiments detected NFKB2/Nfkb2 upregulation by IL-1β and TNFα in both hMC and mouse retinas. We also observed a correlation between NFκB p65 nuclear translocation and inflammatory transcript upregulation in hMC and hRMEC after both direct stimulation with IL-1β or TNFα and reciprocal cell stimulation with CM from IL-1β-treated cells. In addition, the NFκB inhibitor IKK2-VI reduced inflammatory cytokine induction in hMC and hRMEC, indicating a pivotal role of NFκB in the amplification of inflammatory cytokine expression in these cells.

Of additional relevance to inflammation was the upregulated expression of VCAM-1. Although cell adhesion molecules (VCAM, ICAM, e-Selectin) are often studied in the context of endothelial cell-mediated responses that facilitate leukocyte attachment and trans-endothelial migration, their upregulation has also been reported in glia affected by various pathologies [65,66,80]. ICAM and VCAM upregulation is reported in patients with DR, in retinopathy models, and specifically in MC and EC in DR [80-86]. When elevated in retinal EC, they promote leukocyte adhesion, vaso-occlusion, and downstream capillary dropout, potentially leading to ischemia-induced hypoxia and consequent neovascularization [78]. VCAM expression also affects IL-8 expression [87], potentially influencing both inflammation and angiogenesis in due course.

Finally, CXCL10 is implicated in the activation and migration of immune cells, including monocytes, and is known to stimulate apoptosis and modulate the expression of adhesion molecules [78,88]. Conversely, CXCL10 also displays angiostatic bioactivity and its upregulation may influence progression to PDR [89].

Beyond TNFα’s role as an acute direct stimulus of inflammation, chronically elevated levels of retinal TNFα may act to support other DR associated processes including direct activation of neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation and indirect upregulation of IL-8 (the main neutrophil chemoattractant) through VCAM-1 [7,87,90]. Neutrophil granules contain several proteases (e.g., matrix metalloproteinases) capable of activating inflammatory and/or angiogenic molecules such as IL-8 and VEGF through cleavage of their pro-forms or proteolysis of their extracellular matrix tethers [91-93]. These processes could transition the retina toward an angiogenic phenotype, contributing to the onset and progression of PDR.

Exploring the relevance of IL-1β and TNFα co-regulated transcripts in hMC and the mouse retina.

Some of the top transcripts upregulated by both IL-1β and TNFα in hMC have been previously associated with tissue inflammation and/or retinopathy (i.e., CCL20, IL1B, CXCL8, CXCL3, and VCAM) [9,38,54,55,86,87,94,95]. Although not within the top 15 differentially expressed, several other notable transcripts were also significantly upregulated by both IL-1β and TNFα in hMC (i.e., BIRC3, CCL2, CSF2, PTX3, ICAM, NFKB1, and NFKB2) and in the mouse retina (i.e., Nfkb2, Icam, Vcam, Cxcl10, and Fgf2) [78,96-104]. Substantive evidence suggests significant roles for each of these genes in DR [82,89-97]. In addition, consistent with our observations in hMC, induction of ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and NFKB2 has also been reported in MIO-M1 cells (an immortalized Müller cell line) after TNFα stimulation [105].

In aggregate, these changes in inflammatory mediator expression by Müller cells further support their roles in facilitating retinal inflammation. Additionally, two families of proteins showed significant alterations after IL-1β or TNFα injections into the vitreous of mice. Retinal guanylate binding proteins (Gbp*) were upregulated by both IL-1β and TNFα, and retinal crystallins (Cry*) were downregulated by TNFα. Several guanylate binding proteins, GBP2, 4, and 5, have been recently identified as the products of pyroptosis-related genes associated with DR. [106] On the other hand, the crystallins are chaperone proteins that are reported to inhibit photoreceptor cell apoptosis; thus, their downregulation may have implications for vision loss in DR [107].

Overall, 10 transcripts were upregulated by both IL-1β and TNFα in both human Müller cells and the mouse retina as observed in our RNA-Seq experiments. These were NFKB2, VCAM1, ICAM1, BIRC3, PSMB9, TLR2, TAP1, GBP2, GBP4, and RELB. The relevance of NFKB2, VCAM1, and ICAM1 is discussed above in the narrative, while in Table 1, we briefly discuss known functions and alterations in proteins encoded by the other transcripts in the context of diabetes and ocular disease.

Avenues to new therapies for DR

Our study was undertaken to enhance the understanding of inflammatory cytokine expression and amplification in two specific DR-relevant primary human retinal cells, hMC and hRMEC. It provides insights into the plausible cytokine hierarchy involved in initiating, amplifying, and sustaining inflammatory signals associated with retinopathy progression between these cells. It expands upon what has been reported by others using animal-derived, immortalized, or non-retinal cells, and it goes beyond direct stimulation of monocellular cultures to explore putative paracrine signals between cells using conditioned culture medium. Finally, it correlates cytokine-induced responses in hMC and hRMEC to those in the mouse retina in vivo. The insights derived from this work, and those from studies that will follow, will inform novel strategies for DR prognosis and treatment to protect and preserve ocular health in patients with diabetes.

IL-1β inhibitors have been developed and evidence suggesting their potential to reduce retinopathy is beginning to emerge [123-126]. As such, the repurposing of existing IL-1β inhibitors designed for other clinical indications may have significant clinical value in DR management. However, few clinical trials have tested IL-1β inhibitors in DR, and their potential to initiate or promote adverse retinal effects must still be assessed [127]. Exploring the inhibition of IL-1β, or of IL-1β-dependent signaling intermediates whose levels also correlate with DR progression, may also allow the identification of other downstream targets that may be blocked with novel or repurposed drugs for enhanced efficacy or safety.

For example, NFκB signaling was among the top pathways enriched by the differentially expressed genes in hMC treated with IL-1β or TNFα, and was the top pathway enriched by transcripts significantly altered in both hMC and the mouse retina after stimulation with both IL-1β and TNFα. Notably, the correlation between NFκB activation and cytokine transcript induction was observed for all four of our primary cytokine targets – IL1B, TNF, CXCL8, and IL6 – in hMC, and of three of them – IL1B, TNF, and CXCL8 – in hRMEC. NFκB inhibitors have proven efficacious against glial activation in the retinas of diabetic mice, correlating with reduced neuronal apoptosis [128]. However, because of its pleiotropic and ubiquitous roles, global retinal inhibition of NFκB is likely to negatively impact tissue homeostasis and cell-targeted therapeutic approaches may be necessary.

Limitations and future directions

We fully acknowledge the challenge of modeling chronic disease processes in short-term cell culture experiments, yet also recognize their value for answering specific questions about monocellular mechanisms in isolation from the potentially confounding influences of other cells/tissues. Our in vitro experiments were thus designed to tease out the contributions of hMC and hRMEC to tissue inflammation and its consequences, with the results informing the subsequent translation of our experimental strategies to an in vivo model. This sequence of experiments permitted replication of the inflammatory processes we observed in cells in a much more complex biological system, thus facilitating our understanding of retinopathy pathogenesis and informing potential future therapeutic strategies for retinal vascular inflammation.

Given our findings, and those reported in the existing literature, we believe that IL-1β may act to weave blood-borne inflammatory mediators, retinal inflammation, and angiogenesis into a consolidated cascade that potentiates the progression of DR to vision threatening stages [70,129] and that Müller cells may be key effectors in propagating and sustaining IL-1β induced pathology. Our group is now further investigating how hRMEC behaviors (e.g., EC-leukocyte adhesion/leukostasis, blood retinal barrier function/vascular permeability, angiogenesis) are affected by either direct IL-1β-stimulation or the CM from IL-1β-stimulated retinal cells. Such studies may reveal additional insights into the physiologic consequences of inflammatory cytokine amplification induced by IL-1β in MC and other retinal cells. Finally, while not a point of emphasis in this study, several transcripts, including the proangiogenic CXCR2 ligands, showed consistent upregulation in response to our chosen stimuli in multiple experimental contexts, and thus are candidates for use as biomarkers to predict DR onset and track progression and response to therapeutic intervention.

Conclusions

Diabetes is associated with chronic low-level inflammation and increased circulating and ocular levels of IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8, and IL-6 [3-5,11,130]. The goal of this study was to investigate the relative influences of these four cytokines on DR-relevant molecular events, with special emphasis on transcripts for IL-1β, TNFα, IL-8 (and its mouse homologues), and IL-6 in hMC, hRMEC, and the mouse retina. Overall, our findings suggest that: 1) IL-1β and TNFα may initiate retinal inflammation in part by inducing their own expression; 2) IL-1β may acutely induce itself at levels with sufficient bioactivity to support retinal inflammatory propagation through crosstalk between hMC and hRMEC; 3) IL-1β and TNFα may contribute to retinopathy progression through the induction of the proangiogenic CXCR2 ligands; and 4) TNFα and NFκB may be key transient intermediaries in IL-1β-dependent processes contributing to the interplay between inflammation and angiogenesis.

We have found that IL-1β demonstrated the most potent inflammatory activity, suggesting that neutralizing IL-1β and/or its downstream targets may constitute an effective means of slowing or halting NPDR progression. Accordingly, exploring the initial triggers of IL-1β upregulation and characterizing the downstream consequences of elevated IL-1β may enhance our understanding of the pathophysiology of DR and lead to new therapeutic approaches.
