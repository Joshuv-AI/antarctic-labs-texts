The fetal origins of metabolic health: exploring the association between newborn biological age and metabolism hormones in childhood
Authors: Zhenxian Jia, Feng Qiu, Yujie He, Huan Chen, Chenhui Yang, Hongxiu Liu, Tongzhang Zheng, Shunqing Xu, Shiqiong Wang, Yuanyuan Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTelomere length (TL), mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNAcn), and DNA methylation age (DNAmAge) are common aging biomarkers. However, research on the associations between these three markers at birth and subsequent metabolic status was limited. This study aimed to evaluate the association between TL, mtDNAcn, and DNAmAge in newborns and the variation in metabolic hormones of children at 3 years old.MethodsThis research involved 895 mother–child pairs from a birth cohort in China, with TL and mtDNAcn measured using quantitative real-time PCR, DNA methylation (DNAm) assessed using Infinium MethylationEPIC Beadchip, and DNAm age (DNAmAge) determined using Horvath’s epigenetic clock. Insulin and leptin levels were measured via electrochemiluminescence assay. Multivariable adjusted linear regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis were utilized to examine the association between aging markers and metabolic hormones.ResultsThe linear regression analysis indicated the percentage change of metabolism hormones for per doubling of aging biomarkers alterations and found significant associations between DNAmAge and insulin levels (adjusted percent change (95% CI), − 13.22 (− 23.21 to − 1.94)), TL and leptin levels (adjusted percent change (95% CI), 15.32 (1.32 to 31.24)), and mtDNAcn and leptin levels (adjusted percent change (95% CI), − 14.13 (− 21.59 to − 5.95)). The RCS analysis revealed significant non-linear associations between TL (Ln transformed) and insulin (Ln transformed) (P = 0.024 for nonlinearity), as well as DNAmAge (Ln transformed) and leptin (Ln transformed) (P = 0.043 for nonlinearity). Specifically, for TL and insulin, a positive association was observed when TL (Ln transformed) was less than − 0.05, which transitioned to an inverse association when TL (Ln transformed) was greater than − 0.05. Regarding DNAmAge and leptin, there was a sharp decline when DNAmAge (Ln transformed) was less than − 1.35, followed by a plateau between − 1.35 and − 0.67 and then a further decline when DNAmAge (Ln transformed) was greater than − 0.67.ConclusionsIn this prospective birth cohort study, variation in metabolic hormones of children at 3 years old was associated with TL, mtDNAcn, and DNAmAge at birth. These findings suggested that TL, mtDNAcn, and DNAmAge might play a role in the biological programming of metabolic health from birth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-024-03629-z.Keywords: Telomere length (TL), Mitochondria DNA copy number (mtDNAcn), DNAm age (DNAmAge), Insulin, Leptin

Background

Global prevalence and burden of metabolic syndromes are increasing [1–4]. Dysregulated metabolism in early life usually leads to an increased risk of subsequent metabolic syndrome in adulthood [5–10], underscoring the significance of monitoring the metabolic status in early life and an urgent need to identify risk factors and provide an appealing new tool for risk assessment and intervention. Several studies have found the associations between insulin and leptin in childhood and metabolic disorders in later life [11–15].

Insulin and leptin, as crucial metabolic hormones, play complex roles in the maintenance of energy homeostasis, the dysregulation of which often portends adverse metabolic health [16–21]. Insulin, secreted by the pancreatic beta cells, is essential for regulating blood glucose levels [22]. It facilitates the uptake of glucose into cells, particularly in the liver, muscle, and adipose tissue, promoting its storage as glycogen and inhibiting the breakdown of fats and proteins [23]. The insulin signaling pathway ensures that energy is readily available and stored efficiently, maintaining metabolic balance [24]. On the other hand, leptin, produced primarily by adipose tissue [25], regulates energy balance by signaling the hypothalamus to suppress appetite and increase energy expenditure and serves as a feedback mechanism to prevent overheating and excessive fat accumulation [25].

Biological age, which is a measure reflecting the physiological and functional state of an individual, is often used as an indicator of their health status relative to their chronological age [26]. Biological age is highly variable at birth and strongly projects towards later life [27]. Biological aging tracks different age segments in life [28, 29], showing that health conditions observed later in life may find their origin at birth to some extent. This highlights biological age at birth as an essential molecular explanation in the fetal programming of health and disease hypothesis [30]. Adverse biological aging may signal the onset of deviation of metabolic processes from normal [31–34]. Markers of biological age, such as telomere length (TL), mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNAcn), and DNA methylation age (DNAmAge), encapsulate the compounding effects of genetic predisposition and environmental exposure, providing a window into the process of cellular senescence [35, 36]. Telomeres are protective structures at the ends of chromosomes, and their excessive shortening has been associated with an increased risk of metabolic diseases and insulin resistance [37, 38]. Changes in mtDNAcn and integrity reflect mitochondrial dysfunction, revealing a disruption of bioenergetics, which may have implications for insulin and leptin [39, 40]. Epigenetic clocks (such as DNAmAge), DNA-based methylation patterns, whose deviations are associated with various adverse metabolic outcomes, including obesity and insulin resistance. Several studies have suggested an intrinsic association between methylation patterns and metabolic disorders [41, 42]. In addition, recent studies have also highlighted the significant impact of metabolic hormones such as insulin and leptin on these biomarkers of biological age. A 6-year longitudinal study showed that insulin therapy led to a decrease in mean telomere length in patients with type 2 diabetes after 6 years [43]. mtDNAcn in adults with type 1 diabetes is lower than that in control [44]. High leptin might be involved in short TL and accelerated epigenetic aging [45]. Insulin might influence telomere length (TL) by regulating cellular oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, as increased oxidative stress and chronic inflammation in insulin-resistant states [46–48] are known to accelerate telomere shortening [49, 50]. Additionally, insulin could modulate mitochondrial biogenesis and function, affecting the stability and copy number of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNAcn) [51]. Leptin might impact TL and mtDNAcn through its roles in regulating energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, and inflammatory responses [52–56]. Furthermore, leptin might influence DNA methylation patterns via signaling pathways related to epigenetic modifications, thereby affecting DNA methylation age (DNAmAge) [57].

Biological age, assessed using cord blood, serves as an indication of the life-programming processes that occur during the fetal period, with potential long-term implications for metabolic health throughout childhood and beyond [58, 59]. The identification of biological age at birth may offer insight into the risk of subsequent metabolic disorders, presenting an opportunity for timely intervention to support healthy development and mitigate metabolic diseases. Nevertheless, there is a scarcity of epidemiological research on the association between TL, mtDNAcn, and DNAmAge at birth and, subsequently, metabolic hormones.

This study aimed to assess the relationship between biological age at birth and metabolic health in childhood. Specifically, we hypothesized that key markers of biological age (telomere length, mitochondrial DNA copy number, and DNA methylation age) at birth are associated with metabolic hormone levels (insulin and leptin) in children at approximately 3 years of age. Using data from a prospective longitudinal birth cohort, we conducted a quantitative analysis to determine these associations.

Methods

Participants

The participants were recruited at their first antenatal examination at the Wuhan Medical and Healthcare Center for Women and Children (WMHCWC), Wuhan, China. Our recruitment criteria were (1) residents of Wuhan city, (2) a singleton gestation and less than gestational 16 weeks, (3) willingness to complete assessments, and (4) intention to have intrauterine check-ups and give birth at the study hospital. This work received research ethics approval from the Human Investigation Committee of WMHCWC and Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and proof of approval is available upon request. Each participant provided written informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the study's purposes and study procedures. In the present analysis, the participants were those enrolled and gave birth at the study hospital, along with offspring children provided cord blood at birth (from April 2014 to October 2016) for measuring TL, mtDNAcn, and DNAmAge. During the follow-up visits (from January 2017 to February 2019), peripheral blood of children at approximately 3 years of age was obtained for measuring insulin and leptin in early childhood. After excluding the children with ineligible measurements and those with missing information, the final study population consisted of 895 children. Furthermore, 160 participants were randomly selected to test the epigenome-wide DNA methylation to calculate DNAmAge (Additional file 1: Fig S1).

Blood sampling

Cord blood samples were obtained by practiced obstetric nurses under sterile conditions immediately following placental expulsion. Later, two hemostatic forceps were utilized to clamp and cut off the umbilical cord, followed by the collection of cord blood. After 2-h standing under 4℃ and 10-min centrifugation at 3000 rpm, the cord serum and cells were respectively separated in the Eppendorf tubes (2 mL) and preserved under − 80 ℃ for subsequent analysis of aging markers. Peripheral blood samples were collected from study children about 3 years old in health clinics in the morning in a fasting state. The collected blood specimens were sent to the laboratory each day, refrigerated in an insulated box with ice packs, centrifuged at 3000 rpm/min for 10 min in a low-speed centrifuge within 2 h, and after centrifugation, the plasma was separated into microcentrifuge tubes and preserved under − 80 ℃ in the subsequent measurement of leptin and insulin.

DNA extraction

Total genomic DNA was isolated from the cord blood and peripheral blood samples using a Blood DNA Extraction kit (BioTeke, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For DNA extraction, the cryogenic blood sample is placed in a 37°C water bath and turned several times to quickly return to room temperature. The plastic film of the pre-packaged deep-well plate was carefully removed, and 200 µL of the sample was added to deep-well plate 1, supplemented with 1 × PBS if necessary. Then, 20 µL of proteinase K and 120 µL of isopropanol were added. The deep-well plate was placed into the Automated Nucleic Acid Extraction System, and the stirring sleeve was inserted into the card slot. The system was set with a lysis temperature of 80°C and an elution temperature of 65°C and run as the following steps: step 1 involved a 25-min mix at speed IV with a temperature of 80°C; step 2 involved a 1-min mix at speed IV with 30 s of magnetic absorption; step 3 involved a 10-min mix at speed III with a temperature of 80°C and 60 s of magnetic absorption; step 4 involved a 5-min mix at speed IV with 30 s of magnetic absorption; step 5 involved a 4-min mix at speed IV with 30 s of magnetic absorption; step 6 involved a 3-min mix at speed IV with 30 s of magnetic absorption; step 7 involved a 15-min mix at speed IV with a temperature of 65°C and 60 s of magnetic absorption; and step 8 removed magnetic beads. During the run, after step 1, the stirring sleeve and deep-well plate were removed, and 150 µL of isopropanol was added to row 1 and row 7. At the end of the program, transfer the DNA solution to a clean nuclease-free centrifuge tube and store it at − 20°C. For the test, DNA samples were normalized to 10 ng/μL, and concentrations were confirmed using NanoDrop™ 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) quantification prior to amplification.

Telomere length measurement in cord blood

By utilizing Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega Corporation, USA), DNA was extracted from cord blood leukocytes. Later, a quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assay was conducted to measure TL according to the previous description [60]. In this study, we defined relative TL as the repeat copy number ratio (T/S ratio) of telomere (T) to single-copy gene (S). Altogether, 50 samples chosen at random in the present work were blended in the standard reference genomic DNA sample. Every sample was run within the 384-well plates, with 3 replicate wells being set to guarantee the quality. Samples whose standard deviations were ≥ 0.30 were retested. Notably, the inter- and intra-run coefficients of cord blood TL were 4.1% and 3.0%, respectively. Details were presented in the supporting materials (Additional file 1: Method S1).

Mitochondrial DNA copy number measurements in cord blood

The qPCR assay was performed to measure the relative mtDNAcn in line with the previous description [61], in the meantime of measuring abundances of mitochondrial gene copy numbers (mtND1) and single-copy nuclear control gene (human (beta) globin: hbg), and their abundance ratio was determined to be the relative mtDNAcn. qPCR assay was carried out with ViiA™7 Dx Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Every measurement was conducted thrice in the 384-well plate to take the average. More detailed information can be obtained from Supplemental Methods. In the assay, both inter- and intra-run coefficients of variation were < 4%. Information about details was presented in the supporting materials (Additional file 1: Method S2).

DNAm determination in the cord blood and methylation clocks

We analyzed DNA methylation (DNAm) in DNA isolated from the neonates-derived whole umbilical cord blood. DNAm was quantified using Infinium MethylationEPIC Beadchip (EPIC, Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA). We later randomized samples and a portion of technical replicates according to bisulfite conversion plate, chip, as well as position in a chip. In this experiment, certain quality controls were considered, which were assessed by ChAMP R Package. Horvath’s publicly available online calculator (https://dnamage.genetics.ucla.edu/home) was employed to determine DNAm age (DNAmAge).

Quantitative detection of plasma insulin and leptin in child's peripheral blood

The insulin and leptin were measured by multi-factor electrochemiluminescence (ECL) method, using a Human U-PLEX Metabolic Group 1 Assay kit (catalog #K151ACL-1) from Meso Scale Discovery (MSD, Mesoscale, Maryland, USA), according to manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed using an MSD QuickPlex SQ 120 plate reader (Mesoscale, Maryland, USA) and the DISCOVERY WORKBENCH® 4.0 software.

Covariates

Interview-based questionnaires were distributed to pregnant women by well-trained nurses, including questions regarding socioeconomic status (such as maternal age, education, occupation, household income, and parental education status) and lifestyle factors in the process of pregnancy and childhood (like passive smoking during pregnancy, source of drinking water). In addition, clinical data concerning gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), gestational weight gain, child sex, and neonatal birth outcomes were obtained in medical records. The self-reported pre-pregnancy weight (kg, kilograms) and height (m, meters) were obtained to determine pre-pregnancy BMI (PBMI) before classification. Sample collection season (warm: May–October; cold: November–April) and storage time were accurately recorded during follow-up.

Statistical analysis

With regard to general characteristics of participants, categorical data were indicated by N (%), whereas continuous data by mean (SD). The distributions of aging biomarkers (TL, mtDNAcn, and DNAmAge) and metabolism hormones (insulin and leptin) were presented as medians and interquartile ranges, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed for evaluating the difference of medians between boys and girls. The values for aging biomarkers and metabolism hormones were processed through natural logarithm (Ln) transformation to approximate the normal distribution. The associations of aging biomarkers with metabolism hormones were evaluated by the general linear regression and restricted cubic spline (RCS). While linear models provide a straightforward and interpretable approach, they might not adequately characterize the complex associations between variables, especially when these associations exhibit nonlinearity. RCS, a more flexible nonlinear model, could accurately represent interactions and varying rates of change, leading to improved predictive performance and a deeper understanding of underlying patterns. The present study combined these two models that facilitate more profound insights and enhance decision-making capacities. Model selection for RCS was conducted using a combination of Akaike information criterion (AIC) to determine the optimal number and position of the knots (Additional file 1: Table S1, Table S2). This approach helps to balance the trade-off between model complexity and goodness of fit. AIC values for both nonlinear and linear models were reported in Additional file 1: Table S3. For potential confounders, if the factor was found to be a confounder in the association between biological age and metabolic hormones in previous studies [15, 39, 40, 62], or if its main effect estimate changed by more than 10% (Additional file 1: Table S4), this was used as adjusted covariate. Child sex (boys or girls), birth weight (continuous), season of delivery (warm or cold), gestational age at birth (continuous), passive smoking during pregnancy (yes or no), gestational weight gain (inadequate, recommended, excessive), children’s age, season of peripheral blood sampling (warm or cold), storage time of peripheral blood, maternal education status (≥ or < bachelor’s degree), paternal education status (≥ or < bachelor’s degree), and annual family income (≥ 100,000 yuan, 50,000–99,000 yuan, < 50,000 yuan) were selected as covariates in the final models. In addition, to improve the interpretability of the results, the percentage change of metabolism hormones for per doubling of aging biomarkers alterations could be determined by [e (ln2× β) – 1] × 100%. For evaluating the possible modification effects, the infant sex-based subgroup analysis was conducted. The present study has also performed two sensitivity analyses to test the robustness. First, maternal metabolic health phenotypes during pregnancy (GDM and PBMI) were additionally adjusted. Second, household drinking water status (source of drinking water) was additionally adjusted. All database management and statistical analysis were completed with SAS 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). P < 0.05 (two-tailed) stood for statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of participants

This longitudinal study involved 895 children and focused on examining the enduring impact of biological aging on metabolic hormones (Table 1, Fig. 1A and Additional file 1: Table S5). The demographic characteristics of the 895 participants analyzed are detailed in Table 1 and Additional file 1: Table S5. Of the newborns, 476 (53.2%) were boys; the mean (SD) birth weight and gestational age of newborns were 3.32 (0.40) kg and 39.37 (1.14) weeks, respectively. Mothers had a mean age of 28.62 (SD = 3.20) years at the time of delivery, and most of them were first-time births (777 [86.8%]). The majority of mothers had received a bachelor’s degree or higher (732 [81.8%]) and reported no exposure to passive smoking during pregnancy (629 [70.3%]). Similarly, most fathers had received a bachelor’s degree or higher (703 [78.5%]). The mean follow-up period after birth was 3.09 (SD, 0.33) years.

Linear associations between biological age and metabolism hormones

The linear regression analysis indicated the percentage change of metabolism hormones (insulin and leptin) for per doubling of aging biomarkers (TL, mtDNAcn, and DNAmAge) alterations. Generally, DNAmAge was negatively associated with insulin (crude percent change (95% CI), − 12.05 (− 21.81 to − 1.06); adjusted percent change (95% CI), − 13.22 (− 23.21 to − 1.94)), TL was positively associated with leptin (crude percent change (95% CI), 14.94 (1.17 to 30.59); adjusted percent change (95% CI), 15.32 (1.32 to 31.24)), and mtDNAcn were negatively associated with leptin (crude percent change (95% CI), − 14.23 (− 21.71 to − 6.04); adjusted percent change (95% CI), − 14.13 (− 21.59 to − 5.95)) (Table 2). However, TL and insulin, mtDNAcn and insulin, DNAmAge and leptin have not shown a significant trend of linear association (P > 0.05) (Table 2). Further sex stratification showed that mtDNAcn was negatively associated with leptin in both boys (crude percent change (95% CI), − 13.80 (− 24.44 to − 1.67); adjusted percent change (95% CI), − 12.71 (− 23.69 to − 0.14)) and girls (crude percent change (95% CI), − 14.00 (− 23.94 to − 2.76); adjusted percent change (95% CI), − 14.09 (− 24.16 to − 2.68)). Conversely, while mtDNAcn initially showed a positive association with insulin in girls (crude percent change (95% CI), 8.12 (0.36 to 16.47)), this association was no longer significant after adjusting for confounders (Table 3).

RCS analysis for the associations between biological age and metabolism hormones

The nonlinear associations between biological age and metabolism hormones were estimated by RCS analysis (Fig. 3). A nonlinear association was observed between TL (Ln-transformed) and insulin (Ln-transformed) (P = 0.024 for nonlinearity) (Fig. 3A), DNAmAge (Ln-transformed), and leptin (Ln-transformed) (P = 0.043 for nonlinearity) (Fig. 3F). As TL increases, we observed a positive association in the interval of TL (Ln-transformed) less than − 0.05, followed by an inverse association in the interval of TL (Ln-transformed) greater than − 0.05, indicating that moderate TL exhibited the highest insulin concentrations. The relationship between DNAmAge and leptin levels exhibited a fluctuating downward trend, there was a sharp decline in the interval of DNAmAge (Ln-transformed) less than − 1.35, reaching a plateau in the interval of DNAmAge (Ln-transformed) between − 1.35 and − 0.67, and then a further decline in the interval of DNAmAge (Ln-transformed) greater than − 0.67. For TL (Ln-transformed) and leptin (Fig. 3B), mtDNAcn (Ln-transformed) and insulin (Ln-transformed) (Fig. 3C), mtDNAcn (Ln-transformed) and leptin (Ln-transformed) (Fig. 3D), DNAmAge (Ln-transformed), and insulin (Ln-transformed) (Fig. 3E), no significant nonlinear relationships were observed (P for nonlinearity > 0.05).

Results of sensitivity analysis

The results were mainly robust in sensitivity analyses when further adjusted for maternal metabolic health phenotypes during pregnancy (GDM and PBMI) and household drinking water status (source of drinking water). For these two sensitivity analyses, the linear associations and nonlinear associations between aging biomarkers and metabolism hormones have not yet materially changed. Percent change obtained by linear regression analysis (Additional file 1: Table S6, Table S7) and nonlinear dose–response trends obtained by RCS analysis (Additional file 1: Fig S3, Fig S4) were still robust.

Discussion

This study revealed that the biological age of newborns was associated with the metabolism hormones of 3-year-old children. Specifically, children born with longer TL displayed higher leptin levels in early childhood, while those with higher mtDNAcn displayed lower leptin levels. Additionally, children born with higher DNAmAge displayed lower insulin levels in early childhood. These findings suggested that biological age at birth may serve as a valuable indicator for monitoring metabolic health and offered new perspectives on preventive strategies for metabolic disorders.

This study found a weak inverse correlation between TL and mtDNAcn. Both telomeres and mitochondria are key regulators of the aging process and are susceptible to structural damage due to various environmental factors [63] and oxidative stress [64, 65]. In mtDNA damaged by oxidative stress, healthy mitochondria increase their copy number as a feedback mechanism to balance the metabolic defects of damaged mitochondria [66]. Increased mitochondrial copy number leads to excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [67], which stimulates excessive oxidative stress and increases telomere shortening [50].

A positive association between newborn TL and leptin levels in childhood has also been observed, providing evidence for the impact of fetal origins TL on metabolic health. Our finding was consistent with previous study in the elderly [68]. Telomeres are structures that protect the ends of chromosomes, and they progressively shorten with cell division, thus serving as a marker of cellular senescence [69–71]. Previous studies have linked shorter telomeres to a range of age-related diseases [72, 73], while elevated leptin levels have been associated with childhood obesity and metabolic syndrome [74, 75]. The association between TL and leptin indicated that the process of cellular senescence, which begins as early as intrauterine, profoundly affects adipocyte function and fat storage regulation [76, 77]. The present study also revealed a particularly negative association between mtDNAcn at birth and subsequent leptin levels. Mitochondria are key to central energy production and metabolic processes; thus, mtDNAcn reflected the reprogramming of energy metabolism [78, 79]. Disturbed mitochondrial dynamics have been linked to metabolic disorders [80]. The association with leptin implies that early mitochondrial dysregulation may lead to disturbances in the energy homeostatic framework in developing children. In addition, the negative association of the epigenetic age of newborns with insulin and leptin levels in childhood was observed. It emphasized the role of epigenetics in the regulation of insulin and leptin. DNAmAge, a typical epigenetic age indicator based on DNA methylation sites, responded to the effects of genetic and environmental exposures [81–83], providing valuable insights into the developmental history of children and consequent health trajectories [84].

Several nonlinear relationships between newborn biological age and metabolism hormones in childhood were further revealed by RCS analyses, which implied the dose–response relationships between TL and insulin. As TL increases, insulin levels first rise, then fall, indicating that moderate TL exhibited the highest insulin concentrations. A moderate TL may symbolize an optimal balance, indicating adequate protection against cellular senescence [85], while metabolic overcompensation is induced by excessive cell proliferation viability in individuals with long telomeres [86, 87]. Cellular senescence and overcompensation could ultimately precipitate a decrease in insulin secretion functionality [62, 88–90]. Moderate TL might facilitate better insulin regulation and metabolic function, highlighting the complex relationship between TL and metabolic health. However, previous studies have usually ignored the non-linear dose–response relationship between TL and insulin. The dose–response relationship between DNAmAge and leptin showed a fluctuating downward trend, which showed that as epigenetic age increases, leptin levels first decline rapidly, then enter a period of stabilization, and then decline further when epigenetic age continues to increase. This fluctuating downward trend in the nonlinear dose–response relationship between DNAmAge and leptin might be due to complex leptin regulation and compensatory mechanisms [91, 92]. The inconsistencies between the general linear regression model and the RCS are mainly due to the different focus of these two methods. Linear regression modeling offers a straightforward explanation of the relationships between variables; however, it may oversimplify intricate associations [93]. Conversely, the restricted cubic spline (RCS) model more accurately captures nonlinear relationships, providing a more adaptable fit to the data [94]. Consequently, the concurrent application of both models enables a synergistic analysis that leverages the simplicity of linear regression and the flexibility of RCS, thereby facilitating a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying data relationships. In this study, both linear regression and RCS analyses did not reveal a significant dose–response relationship between mtDNAcn and insulin levels. Previous research has predominantly focused on the relationship between mtDNAcn and insulin resistance [95, 96], but lacking studies explored the direct relationship between mtDNAcn and insulin levels.

Strengths and limitations

This study used multiple biological age markers covering telomeres, mitochondria, and DNA methylation. Newborn TL, mtDNAcn, and DNAmAge may be set molecular mediators containing information on genetic and prenatal environmental exposures, contributing to an integrated understanding of intrauterine development in children. This cohort study included a relatively large number of mother–child pairs and a long-term follow-up, which allowed us to prospectively explore the association between newborn biological age and metabolism hormones in childhood. In the present study, we utilized validated instruments for data collection, such as surveys, but the potential for measurement errors still persists. Specifically, self-reported data may be susceptible to recall bias. To address this issue, we cross-validated self-reported data with objective measures, including medical records and physical measurements, wherever feasible. While the sample size of our study was sufficient for the initial design, further validation in diverse regions and with larger sample sizes is warranted. Some limitations also exist. First, as an observational study, we cannot make definitive conclusions about causality. Second, children’s recent lifestyle factors and meals were not well taken into account, which could bring about potential confounding effects. Additionally, changes over time need to be tracked to elucidate better whether biological aging has lasting effects on metabolic health.

Conclusions

The present findings on the association between newborn biological age and metabolic hormones in childhood offered novel evidence suggesting that biological age may serve as a predictor of future metabolic health. Biological aging tracks over different ages, showing that metabolic health observed later in life may find their origin at birth to some extent. Future studies should focus on unraveling the mechanistic pathways regarding these associations to provide valuable biomarkers for early preventive and therapeutic strategies in pediatric healthcare.
