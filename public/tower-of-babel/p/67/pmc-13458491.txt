Warming climate has lengthened global intense tropical cyclone seasons
Authors: Jimin Liu, Jeremy Cheuk-Hin Leung, Dingchi Zhao, Daosheng Xu, Hong Huang, Zengliang Zang, Wenshou Tian, Yaoming Ma, Yi Li, Banglin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntense tropical cyclones (TCs), which pose serious threats to human life and property, often occur within a short period of time each year, known as the intense TC season. Changes in the lengths of intense TC seasons under climate change are critical scientific and socioeconomic issues. While trends in overall TC seasons have been widely studied, the response of intense TC seasons to climate change remains underexplored. Here, we show that intense TC seasons have been lengthening globally since 1980, with statistically significant increasing trends ranging from 9.9–13.8 days/decade across all basins, equivalent to 7.4–21.9% increase in intense TC season lengths per decade. This is primarily due to the enhancing probability of off-season TCs experiencing rapid intensification, which is partly driven by oceanic warming. Meanwhile, changes in background atmospheric circulation play a role in the complexity of intense TC seasonality change. As a result, off-season TCs are more likely to develop into intense TCs. The findings in this study indicate an increasing exposure of human societies to intense TC risks outside historical seasonal norms. This suggests the urgent need for preparation and mitigation measures for the potential risks of intense TCs under future climate change.Subject terms: Climate sciences, Climate change, Atmospheric science

Introduction

Intense tropical cyclones (TCs), or Saffir-Simpson Category 4–5 storms that have lifetime maximum intensities (LMI) exceeding 113 knots, are responsible for the majority of TC-related disasters each year, causing extensive damage and loss of life due to their strong winds, heavy rainfall, and storm surges1–3. These intense TCs require substantial potential energy from the atmosphere and ocean, typically occurring during a relatively short period each year, known as the intense TC season. Despite their infrequent occurrence, the consequences of intense TCs for human society and the environment are profound. For instance, in 2024, the recent record-breaking Hurricane Beryl in the North Atlantic (NA) and Cyclone Chido in the South Indian Ocean (SI) caused substantial destruction4,5. Given the huge impact of intense TCs, it is crucial to study how climate change may influence their activity1,6,7.

Over the past decades, anthropogenic warming has altered the seasonal cycle of the climate system. Research indicates that the length of summer has been extended, whereas winter has shortened, owing to the altered timing of seasonal transitions under greenhouse warming8–10. Furthermore, the seasonality of sea surface temperature (SST) and oceanic warm pool has also been affected11,12. Notably, anthropogenic forcing has been found to enhance the amplitude of the global SST seasonal cycle by 3.9 ± 1.6% from 1983–202213. These changes in Earth’s seasonal cycle are hypothesized to influence the activity of weather systems and extreme events that are seasonally dependent14–18. In particular, the shift in the seasonal distribution of atmospheric and oceanic conditions favorable for TC development may modulate the likelihood of TCs occurring during specific months and seasons, hence influencing the behavior of TC seasons.

The above hypothesis is evident in numerous studies19,20, especially those documenting the effects of climate change on the onset and withdrawal timing21–25, as well as the lengths20,26–28 of overall TC seasons (see Methods for definitions). However, long-term changes in intense TC activities may differ from those of overall TC seasons because of their different evolution patterns and controlling factors, especially the substantial energy required to form intense TCs. Research has reported that anthropogenic forcings have resulted in more favorable environments for TC rapid intensification, consequently leading to a higher occurrence of intense TCs globally over the past four decades29–33. Climate simulations also indicate a notable increase in the frequency of intense TCs34–36. In particular, a recent study has identified a shift in the peak occurrence of global intense TCs from autumn to summer and attributed it to the seasonal heterogeneity of oceanic warming37.

Given the profound risks associated with intense TC disasters, even minor changes in the timing of intense TC seasons can have substantial consequences. However, to date, while plenty of work have discussed the impacts of climate change on the seasonality of TC activity, very few have focused on that of intense TCs37. It remains unclear to what extent the altering atmospheric and oceanic seasonal cycles have influenced the lengths of intense TC seasons under past anthropogenic warming.

In this study, we thoroughly address this particular issue by investigating changes in intense TC season lengths over the NA, eastern North Pacific (ENP), western North Pacific (WNP), South Pacific (SP), and SI basins, as well as their underlying drivers. Our results indicate statistically significant lengthening trends of intense TC seasons globally, despite the relatively stable overall TC seasons. This is primarily attributed to the increased efficiency of off-season TCs developing into intense TCs, driven by the enhancing probability of off-season TCs experiencing rapid intensification under the warming climate. The findings imply that intense TCs have become more common during periods traditionally considered off-season, potentially leading to a higher risk of intense TC-related disasters and associated societal and economic consequences.

Results

Lengthening intense TC seasons

Intense TC seasons, defined as the period in which 97% of intense TCs occur in a year (see Methods), typically last for a shorter period compared to overall TC seasons. Climatologically, the globally average intense TC season length is 97.8 days, with the shortest observed in the NA (45.8 days) and the longest in the WNP (187.7 days). For most basins, the intense TC seasons generally occur in late summer and early autumn (Supplementary Note S1, Figs. S1–S2 and Table S1), when the ocean and atmosphere accumulate sufficient potential energy to allow a TC to intensify to a Category 4–5 storms.

As the seasonality of the climate system changes under global warming8,12,13, the climatology of intense TC seasons is also affected. Based on historical TC observations, we find that the durations of intense TC seasons have increased significantly for both hemispheres and all basins since 1980. Specifically, from 1980–2023, the intense TC seasons in the Northern Hemisphere (NH) and Southern Hemisphere (SH) significantly lengthened by 12.9 ± 7.1 (p < 0.01; Fig. 1a) and 12.6 ± 7.5 (p < 0.05; Fig. 1b) days/decade, respectively. For specific basins, in the NH, the lengths of intense TC seasons in the NA, ENP, and WNP increased by 10.0 ± 6.9 (p < 0.01), 10.4 ± 7.7 (p < 0.05), and 13.8 ± 8.6 (p < 0.05) days/decade, respectively. Meanwhile, in the SH, the intense TC season lengths in the SP and SI increased by 11.8 ± 9.8 (p < 0.05) and 9.9 ± 7.9 (p < 0.10) days/decade, respectively (Fig. 1c).

(1) The first type of lengthening (TYPE I) is characterized by an earlier start of the intense TC season, but without obvious change in the season end date. The lengthening trends of ENP, SP, and SI intense TC seasons belong to TYPE I (Fig. 2b, d, e). (2) TYPE II lengthening is also primarily due to an earlier start of the intense TC season, but is partly offset by the earlier shift of the end of the season. The lengthening intense TC season in the WNP belongs to TYPE II (Fig. 2c). (3) TYPE III lengthening, in contrast, is characterized by a later end of intense TC season. The lengthening NA intense TC season belongs to this type (Fig. 2a). This suggests the complexity of the changes in the intense TC seasons across different basins (Supplementary Note S2, Fig. S3 and Table S2).

The above results indicate that the intense TC seasons have become 1–2 weeks longer every 10 years, equivalent to a relative change of 7.4–21.9%/decade. These lengthening trends of intense TC seasons are insensitive to the definitions of intense TC seasons (Supplementary Fig. S4 and Table S3), confirming the robustness of the above conclusions (Supplementary Note S3).

Comparison between IBTrACS and ADT-HURSAT

The lengthening trends of intense TC seasons presented above are derived from the official best‑track dataset, IBTrACS. Despite its widespread use as an authoritative observational product, the temporal inhomogeneity issue of the IBTrACS has been documented because of the continuous improvements in observation techniques. Some studies suggested the use of other reconstructed TC datasets that may provide more temporally homogenous data, such as the advanced Dvorak Technique–Hurricane Satellite (ADT-HURSAT)29. Even so, no consensus has yet been reached on which dataset is more reliable for particular TC metrics29. In order to provide a more comprehensive understanding of long-term variability of intense TC seasonality, we further compare the similarities and discrepancies in intense TC season lengthening trends between IBTrACS and ADT-HURSAT.

Supplementary Fig. S5 presents the long-term linear trends of intense TC seasons derived from ADT-HURSAT v7b, using the same methodology applied to our IBTrACS results. The results show that from 1980–2023 the intense TC seasons in the NA, ENP, and WNP, increased by 13.2 ± 7.9 (p < 0.01), 5.1 ± 8.6 (p = 0.52), and 0.6 ± 9.6 (p = 0.96) days/decade, respectively. Meanwhile, in the SH, the intense TC season lengths in the SP and SI increased by 6.1 ± 7.2 (p < 0.10) and 3.9 ± 8.7 (p = 0.39) days/decade, respectively. Overall, the intense TC season lengthening trend in the SH is statistically significant, while that in the NH is not significant. The above results, based on ADT-HURSAT v7b data, suggest that lengthening trends in intense TC season are observed in all basins during 1980–2023, but these trends are statistically significant only in NA and SP.

It is noted that there is currently no consensus on which dataset is more reliable for trend analyses, including for the intense TC seasonality change results in this study. Nevertheless, we identify physical distinctions that may partly explain the differences between the two datasets. Namely, we find that the TC intensity estimates in IBTrACS show comparatively stronger correlations with surface variables, such as sea level pressure and surface wind speed. Conversely, those in ADT-HURSAT are more closely linked to satellite‑retrieved cloud top features, such as brightness temperature (Supplementary Figs. S6–S10). This may be because ADT-HURSAT is purely derived from satellite observations, whereas IBTrACS combines multiple independent sources, including in-situ surface measurements and operational track records. These suggest that IBTrACS‑derived intensity records, as well as the derived intense TC seasonality change, better reflect TC-related disasters at the surface. Thus, the following discussion will be based on results derived from the IBTrACS.

Off-season storms are more likely to develop into intense TCs

The increasing trends in the lengths of intense TC seasons imply a higher probability of intense TCs occurring outside of traditional intense TC seasons. However, it is important to note that the number of TC genesis (TCG) does not necessarily increase during the pre-season and post-season periods25,38,39. This is reflected in the inconsistent lengthening trends between intense TC season and overall TC seasons (Fig. 1c), of which the latter is defined as the period when the majority of TCs occur (see Methods). Using the same dataset and study period, we find that the trends in overall TC season lengths across the ENP, WNP, SP, and SI are statistically insignificant (Fig. 1c). Meanwhile, although a significant lengthening trend is observed in NA’s overall TC season (+7.2 ± 4.8 days/decade, p < 0.05; Fig. 1c), the trend magnitude is much smaller than that of intense TC seasons. These observations contrast with the significant increase in intense TC season lengths, suggesting that changes in off-season TCG may not be the main reason driving the lengthening of intense TC seasons (Supplementary Note S4 and Fig. S11).

Instead, we find that the efficiency of TCs developing into intense TCs (α), which indicates how likely a TC reaches Category 4–5, is the primary factor contributing to the lengthening of intense TC seasons. By decomposing the trends in pre-season and post-season intense TC occurring probability (△r/△t) into changes in α (△α/△t) and TCG (△TCG/△t), we can quantify to what extent △α/△t and △TCG/△t respectively contribute to the trends in intense TC season lengths (Eqs. 2–3, see Methods). Results show that, for all basins, the distribution of △r/△t primarily follows the contributions made by △α/△t. For instance, for TYPE I basins (ENP, SP, SI) which are featured with earlier starts of intense TC season, we find that the rise in pre-season r is almost attributed to the increase in pre-season α (Fig. 2g, i, j). Similarly, the earlier start of intense TC season in TYPE II basin (WNP) is also predominantly due to the increase in pre-season α (Fig. 2h). The NA (Type III), where the lengthening intense TC season is due to a later end of the season, is associated with enhancing post-season α (Fig. 2f). In comparison, △TCG/△t contributes negligibly to the increase in off-season r, except that a decrease in pre-season TCG offsets the α increasing trend over SP and a decrease in post-season TCG results in the early shift of WNP intense TC end date. (Supplementary Note S5 and Fig. S12).

The above results suggest that, under the past climate change, TCs are more likely to intensify and become intense TCs during pre-season (for WNP, ENP, SI, and SP) and post-season (for NA) periods. This is the primary reason leading to the lengthening of intense TC seasons over all basins. No matter for which lengthening type, changes in the number of TCG play a minor role.

Major roles of △α/△t and TC rapid intensifications

The above results indicate that, no matter for which basins, the lengthening trends of intense TC seasons are primarily attributed to the increase in off-season α. That is, a growing proportion of TCs have been intensifying to Category 4–5 during pre-season periods for the ENP, WNP, SI, and SP, as well as post-season for the NA. A key question is, what is the underlying mechanism that favors off-season storms developing into intense TCs more easily?

It has been widely documented that the occurrence of intense TCs is governed primarily by rapid intensification (RI) processes. These works suggest that climate change has resulted in more favorable environments for TC rapid intensification, consequently leading to a higher occurrence of intense TCs globally. Thus, it is hypothesized that the changes in efficiency with which off-season TCs develop into intense TCs (i.e., △α/△t), as well as the resulting intense TC season lengthening trends, are related to changes in RI processes. To verify this, we further analyze rRI, the probability of TCs experiencing RI processes, and αRI, which represents how likely a TC will experience RI (see Methods). If the above hypothesis is valid, then rRI and αRI should also exhibit trends similar to r and α. The RI is defined as an increase in maximum sustained wind of at least 45 kt/24 h (See Methods).

The results reveal that the distributions of both ΔrRIΔt and △αRI△t are consistent with those of △r△t and △α△t. Specifically, for NA where the lengthening intense TC season is dominated by a later ending, ΔrRI/Δt increases during the late post-season, and ΔαRI/Δt increases throughout nearly the entire post-season, being consistent with △r△t and △α△t. This indicates that TCs during the NA post-season experience RI more easily during their lifetime, enabling them to develop into intense TCs. For the three TYPE I basins, namely ENP, SP, and SI, the shift in the intense TC season is driven mainly by an earlier onset. We find that these earlier onsets are associated with pronounced enhancement of ΔrRI/Δt and ΔαRI/Δt RI during the pre-season. Similarly, increased RI probability during the pre-season dominates ΔrRI/Δt in the WNP, leading to an earlier onset of intense TC season (Fig. 3). In addition, the correlations between α and αRI are statistically significant at the 99% confidence level for all basins over the period 1980–2023, except for the ENP where significance also reaches above the 95% confidence level (Supplementary Fig. S13).

These results provide direct evidence that during some of the pre- and post-intense TC seasons, TCs more easily experience RIs hence being more likely to develop into intense TCs. The consistency between ΔrRIΔt, △αRI△t and △r△t, △α△t confirms that the lengthening of intense TC season is closely related to changes in RI activity. Therefore, the lengthening of intense TC seasons across the globe is primarily attributed to the higher chance for an off-season TC experiencing RI processes.

Complexity of ITC seasonality response to oceanic and atmospheric changes

The likelihood of TC rapid intensification and intense TC development is partly related to its maximum potential intensity (MPI) by considering a TC as a Carnot engine30,31 (see Methods). Results show significant increasing trends in the average pre-season MPI over the ENP (1.56 ± 0.66 m s−1 decade−1, p < 0.01), WNP (3.00 ± 0.54 m s−1 decade−1, p < 0.01), SP (2.99 ± 0.71 m s−1 decade−1, p < 0.01), and SI (2.27 ± 0.50 m s−1 decade−1, p < 0.01), as well as the post-season MPI over the NA (+1.70 ± 0.41 m s−1 decade−1, p < 0.01) from 1980–2023 (Fig. 4a). MPI can be decomposed into the thermodynamic efficiency term (η) and the thermodynamic disequilibrium between the sea surface and troposphere (L) of the TC’s surrounding environment. While the former is more sensitive to upper tropospheric temperature, the latter is generally governed by the SST variability40–42. To investigate the cause of the increasing off-season α and αRI, which leads to the lengthening of global intense TC seasons, we quantitatively estimate the contributions of changes in η (△η/△t) and L (△L/△t) to trends in MPI (△MPI/△t) (see Methods).

Results show that the increasing MPIs of off-season storms are predominantly driven by the enhancing background thermodynamic disequilibrium. In Type I basins, where the pre-season △α/△t is responsible for the lengthening of intense TC seasons, changes in L contribute to pre-season △MPI/△t basins by 72.2% for the ENP (Fig. 4b–e), 81.1% for the SP (Fig. 4j–m), and 65.7% for the SI (Fig. 4n–q), respectively. In the WNP (TYPE II), where the lengthening intense TC season is also attributed to the increase in pre-season α, △L/△t accounts for 81.7% of the pre-season MPI increase (Fig. 4f–i). For the NA (TYPE III), where the post-season △α/△t explains the extension of intense TC season duration, 63.7% of the post-season △MPI/△t is contributed by (△L/△t) (Fig. 4r–u). On the contrary, the increasing η has limited effects on the rising trends of MPI, with contributions ranging from 16.2%–35.4% (Fig. 4a) (Supplementary Note S6).

These findings suggest that the thermodynamic disequilibrium term plays a role in inducing more RI events and causing more off-season TCs to develop into intense TCs, thereby extending the duration of intense TC seasons. This aligns with previous studies that identified L as the main driver of MPI seasonality40. Since L is typically controlled by the sea surface temperature40,42, it is the warming sea surface that amplifies the gradient of saturation moist static energy between the sea surface and the free troposphere, which then contributes to the lengthening of intense TC seasons under climate change.

The basin-dependent lengthening patterns of intense TC seasons (Fig. 1d) also imply the complexity of how intense TC seasonality responds to climate change. Especially, MPI increases uniformly throughout the year for all basins due to the relatively uniform oceanic warming (Supplementary Fig. S14), which is inconsistent with the complex lengthening patterns of intense TC seasons. This suggests that other atmospheric factors in addition to MPI may also play a role.

MPI determines the theoretical upper limit of maximum sustained wind speed that a TC can achieve. However, other atmospheric conditions can also modulate intense TC activity30,31,43. By comparing the changes in seasonality of key atmospheric factors, we examine the possible reasons why insignificant trends in intense TC seasons onset/withdrawal dates are observed in particular basins. In Type I basins, insignificant changes are observed in the intense TC season end dates. We find that the insignificant intense TC season end date trend in the ENP is associated with the relative weak increase in post-season low-level vorticity compared to that in in-season vorticity, which leads to a reduction of post-season r; that in SP is due to the weakening environmental low-level atmospheric vorticity; that in SI is associated with the relative weak increase in post-season vertical wind shear (Fig. 5c, d, g, j). For WNP (Type II), the combined result of weakening low-level vorticity and strengthening vertical wind shear causes the earlier end of intense TC season, offsetting the season lengthening trend (Fig. 5e, f). For NA (Type III), the insignificant trend in intense TC season onset date is a result of the strengthening atmospheric vertical downdraft, which is not observed during the post-intense TC season (Fig. 5a, b).

Discussion

The lengths of intense TC seasons directly influence the number of days human society is exposed to the potential threat of Category 4–5 storms. In this study, we discover that, since 1980, the lengths of intense TC seasons have been extending across all basins, with statistically significant trends ranging from 9.9–13.8 days/decade. This corresponds to 7.4–21.9% increase in intense TC season lengths per decade. The lengthening trends are mainly characterized by an early onset of season (for the ENP, WNP, SI, and SP), whereas the NA exhibits a late withdrawal of season. This highlights the complexity of how intense TC seasonality responds to climate change.

Analyses indicate that change in TC RI activity is a major driver for this trend. The enhancing probability of an off-season TC experiencing RI increases the likelihood of off-season TCs developing into intense TCs30,31. This is partly driven by the increasing MPI, which aligns with previous research indicating that oceanic warming leads to an earlier shift in TC activity22,28,37. On the other hand, changes in background atmospheric conditions contribute to the complexity of lengthening patterns of intense TC seasons. In this paper, only changes in atmospheric conditions that may offset lengthening trends of intense TC seasons are presented. While our study identifies off-season RI probability as the key driver of the lengthening of intense TC seasons, the underlying causes of this off-season RI increase remain an open question that warrants further research.

While the response of overall TC seasons to climate change may vary and even contrast across basins25,38,39, our findings indicate a consistent lengthening of intense TC seasons on a global scale due to past anthropogenic warming. This suggests a higher probability of intense TC disasters occurring outside traditional intense TC seasons. The more frequent off-season intense TCs further imply a greater chance of human society facing compound extreme weather events resulting from the intersection of intense TCs with other weather systems during off seasons, such as winter extratropical cyclones. This could lead to more extreme rainfall and flooding, resulting in more severe infrastructure damage and heightened risks to public safety during periods traditionally considered safe from intense TC threats37,44,45. Therefore, this study emphasizes the urgent need to reevaluate and strengthen disaster resilience and recovery strategies and risk mitigation efforts to address the increasing risks associated with these rarely occurring weather patterns under future climate scenarios.

Methods

Data

All TC data utilized in this study were obtained from version 4 of the International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS v4r01)46. The IBTrACS collects historical TC observation records from official meteorological agencies. The standards, or units, of measurement vary across regions in the IBTrACS. In this study, to ensure a consistent global standard, we use the 1-minute maximum sustained wind speed records reported by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) (USA_WIND) for TCs over all basins.

However, IBTrACS suffers from well‑documented temporal inhomogeneities arising from the continuous improvements in TC observation technologies. Thus, some studies suggested the use of other reconstructed TC datasets that may provide more temporally homogenous data, such as the advanced Dvorak Technique–Hurricane Satellite (ADT-HURSAT) which is produced by processing the globally homogenized satellite imagery and has been widely applied to TC trend studies29. However, the limitations of the ADT-HURSAT should also be noted. For example, it is also suggested that the ADT-HURSAT, which is constructed based on coarse (8 km) resolution satellite data, may not be able to accurately detect small TC eyes, hence inducing biases in estimating intensities of strong TCs29,47. On the other hand, given that the data inhomogeneity issue of the IBTrACS stems from interannual advancements in observation techniques, it mostly affects the time series of annual TC counts and does not alter observational consistency within a single year. Thus, this issue may exert relatively small influence on the seasonal cycle of TCs within a given year. However, it should also be noted that the use of a fixed absolute intensity threshold to define intense TCs may potentially introduce spurious trends in the derived intense TC seasons. In short, no consensus has yet been reached on which dataset is more reliable for TC trend analyses, so we also compare the similarities and discrepancies in intense TC season lengthening trends between IBTrACS and ADT-HURSAT in this study.

The SST data analyzed in this study were provided by the NOAA High-resolution Blended Analysis of Daily SST (OISSTv2) with a 0.25°x0.25° grid48. The data of atmospheric variables analyzed were obtained from the NOAA NCEP/DOE Reanalysis 2 (NCEP2)49, which provides data of vertical velocity, u and v components of horizontal wind speed, and air temperature. Note that because the OISSTv2 only provides data since September 1981, the analyses in this study that involved these data were limited to the period of 1982–2023. Nevertheless, this does not affect the conclusions of this study.

Identification of intense TCs

In this study, a TC is defined if its LMI is greater than 34 knots. And, a TC is considered intense when its LMI exceeds 113 knots, which is equivalent to Category 4–5 hurricanes in the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale. The occurrence time of a TC is taken as the time when the TC first reaches its LMI. As in previous studies37,50, only intense TCs in the NA, ENP, WNP, SP, and SI were analyzed in the study. The North Indian Ocean and South Atlantic basins were not studied because intense TCs rarely occurred in these regions.

Definitions of intense TC seasons and overall TC seasons

The intense TC season is defined as the period in which 97% of intense TCs occur in a year20,23. The start and end dates of intense TC seasons and overall TC seasons are estimated for each hemisphere and each basin based on the TC occurrence time20,23, based on the IBTrACS data at 00/06/12/18 UTC. In our calculations, we first determine the occurrence time of each detected intense TC, which is defined as the first time when the TC reaches its LMI. Then, for each year, the start and end dates are defined as the 1.5th and 98.5th percentile values of the intense TC occurrence time within a 3-year period. The use of 3-year data points helps reduce uncertainties caused by the small number of intense TC cases each year29. Note that because of the opposite seasonal cycle between the NH and SH, we set a year of the NH and SH beginning on 16 February and 16 August, respectively. Following the same procedure, the start and end dates of the overall TC season are defined as the 1.5th and 98.5th percentiles of the occurrence time of all TCs with LMI greater than 34 knots within a 3-year window.

Linear interpolation is a common way for calculating percentile values. However, it may introduce biases if the data sample size is small. In our analysis, the number of intense TCs (n) in each year is rather small, which requires a more refined approach. We applied a weighted interpolation method, assume that the TC occurrence time in each year follows a distribution shape similar to the climatology derived from 1980–2023. In the main text, in order to explain variations in the lengths of intense TC seasons, we analyze changes in TC activity during the pre-season and post-season periods. Technically, the pre-season period for a basin is defined as the interval between the start date of the intense TC season and the occurrence data of the first recorded intense TC in history for that basin. Similarly, the post-season period is defined as the interval between the end date of the intense TC season and the occurrence data of the last recorded intense TC in history for that basin.

It is noted that different percentile values have been used to define TC seasons20,23. Although some studies may adopt stricter definitions, the definition employed in this study is the least sensitive to outlier data points, which ensures more reliable and robust results in the time series analysis of intense TC season lengths. Additional analyses have confirmed that the conclusions of this study are not affected by the choice of definitions (more details in Supplementary Note S3).

Definitions of rapid intensification (RI)

RI is commonly defined as an increase in the maximum sustained wind exceeding a specified threshold within 24 hours. In this study, following the definition of Li et al.34, we define RI using a threshold of 45 kt/24 h.

Trend analysis and significance test

All trend values were calculated using linear regression, with their uncertainty ranges denoting the 95% confidence intervals based on the Student’s t-test. The statistical significance (p values) of all linear trends presented in this study was tested by the Mann-Kendall test. The Student’s t-test was also employed and yielded the same conclusions as those by the Mann-Kendall test. All trend values presented in the main context are statistically significant at a confidence level of at least 90%, unless otherwise specified.
