Getting on the Nerve: A Report of a Rare Case of Herpes Zoster Reactivation Following Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy and Review of the Literature
Authors: Kawaljit S Kaura, Manish Kumar, Abhishek Chhabra, Varun Gowda, Abhay S Gaur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Varicella-zoster virus (VZV) causes herpes zoster, reactivation of which is usually characterized by painful, vesicular rashes in a dermatomal location. Though extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL) is a common procedure for the management of renal stone disease, only a few cases of its association with VZV reactivation are reported. We report about a 42-year-old male patient who was admitted with herpes zoster localized to the T11 dermatome, which occurred one week after ESWL. The given case indicates a rare ESWL complication and indicates that the mechanical damage to nerve roots could be an activator of VZV. This potential complication should be raised so as to be recognized and managed at an early stage.

Introduction

The incidence of herpes zoster in the general population is estimated as 10%-20%, and it rises to about 50% in persons aged above 80 years [1]. In North America, the incidence varies between 3.4 and 5 per 1,000 person-years every year, and the incidence rates rise significantly with age [2]. The risk factors associated with reactivation of varicella-zoster virus (VZV) are known to be old age (>50 years), immunocompromised conditions (HIV infection, hematologic malignancies, and immunosuppressive therapy), diabetes mellitus, psychological stress, major surgery, and mechanical nerve tissue trauma [3]. Procedural trauma and herpes zoster are under-identified triggers of herpes zoster in urology. In extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), although considered a minimally invasive treatment, where a localized mechanical trauma is the expected outcome, shock waves can be sufficient to trigger latent VZV in certain dermatomes. This effect seems to be independent of the larger effect of immunosuppression of major surgery itself, and therefore, the effect of focal nerve damage could trigger viral reactivation in vulnerable persons.

It manifests as painful vesicular rashes along one or more contiguous dermatomes. This condition is usually unilateral and never crosses the midline. The thoracic region is the most common site of occurrence, but it can appear anywhere on the body. After the initial infection, the VZV remains in an inactive state in the dorsal root ganglia. Various mitigating factors, like immunocompromised state, aging, stress, and mechanical trauma, can trigger reactivation of VZV [4].

ESWL is a well-established, non-invasive treatment modality for renal stone disease. High-energy shock waves are transmitted through the skin and deeper tissues to fragment kidney stones. Although it is considered safe, potential complications include hematoma formation, adjacent organ contusion, localized inflammation, and varying degrees of nerve irritation or trauma [5].

We present a rare case of VZV reactivation in the right flank region (T11 dermatome) following ESWL in a patient treated for a right-sided renal stone. Post-treatment, the patient developed characteristic multiple painful, vesicular rashes in a T11 dermatomal distribution clinically suggestive of herpes zoster. This case suggests that mechanical trauma to nerve roots following ESWL may contribute to VZV reactivation [6].

Prompt clinical diagnosis and early initiation of antiviral therapy can help attenuate complications. Further research is needed to establish a link between ESWL-induced nerve trauma and VZV reactivation, as well as to identify preventive measures for at-risk individuals.

Case presentation

A 42-year-old gentleman with complaints of right flank pain was diagnosed with 15 x 14 cm lower pole calculi and 9 x 8 mm upper ureteric calculus in the right solitary functional kidney and was planned for ESWL (Figure 1). On the seventh post-procedure day, the patient presented with painful vesicular rashes and blisters along the T11 dermatomal distribution (Figure 2). He had a history of chickenpox at the age of 16 years. The diagnosis of herpes zoster was confirmed by a dermatologist using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing of cells from the lesion base (Table 1). The patient was then started on oral acyclovir. For remnant calculus on post-ESWL X-ray, the patient was planned for another session of ESWL after one month of resolution of herpes zoster.

Discussion

The herpes zoster reactivation rate is approximately 15%-20%, and incidence rises significantly with age, nearly doubling every decade beyond the age of 50. Multinucleated keratinocyte, acantholysis, ballooning degeneration, intranuclear inclusions, multinucleation, necrosis, and vesicles or ulcers can be observed after histological examination of vesicular lesions [7]. After primary infection, VZV remains quiescent in the dorsal root ganglia. Its reactivation is often attributed to a decline in cellular immunity, triggered by factors such as aging, immunosuppression, emotional distress, malignancies, major surgical procedures, and mechanical trauma. Minor urological procedures do not typically act as a trigger for reactivation of VZV; localized trauma and resultant inflammation may contribute to it. VZV reinfection after ESWL is rare, and only a few reported cases are in the literature. Therefore, healthcare providers should be aware of clinical issues and be willing to address this complication.

Other than cutaneous eruptions, VZV infection has urological manifestations too. The most common of them is voiding dysfunction, seen in approximately 4% of herpes zoster patients, with up to 29% of these patients presenting with lumbosacral dermatome involvement. The three pathophysiological mechanisms causing voiding dysfunction are (i) direct viral cystitis with detectable mucosal lesions on cystoscopy, (ii) neuritis spreading from dorsal root ganglia to the sacral motor neurons with resultant bladder atony, and (iii) myelitis causing spastic bladder [8]. ESWL causing reactivation of herpes zoster is an extremely rare event with only sparse documentation in the literature. This association was first documented in the Indian Journal of Urology (2016), where the patient presented on the 10th day with vesicular eruption in the T11 dermatome [9].

Similar to this observation, our case also presented with unilateral T11 dermatomal involvement one week after the procedure and without any systemic immunosuppression. The hypothesis that shock wave-induced localized mechanical or neural trauma can induce reactivation of VZV in the dorsal root ganglia is associated with a close temporal relationship and anatomical overlap between the treatment field and the site of affected dermatome. Herpes zoster has also been reported following other urological procedures like endourological and pelvic surgeries, presumably due to regional tissue trauma and procedural stress. This presentation in a young, immunocompetent patient highlights that this complication is not restricted to high-risk groups. Despite its rarity, heightened clinical awareness of such a clinical picture is critical, as early diagnosis and prompt antiviral therapy are essential to minimize morbidity and post-herpetic complications.

Conclusions

Herpes zoster reactivation after ESWL is a clinically uncommon yet important complication that must be noted among urolithiasis patients and medical professionals who work with this group of patients. As our case has shown, local mechanical trauma due to shock waves, even in young, immunocompetent people who have no classic risk factors, can lead to VZV reactivation of the dorsal root ganglia of the treatment field. The temporal correlation between ESWL and herpes zoster development in the few reported cases - usually occurring between seven and 10 days after the treatment procedure - and the topographical distribution of the area of treatment similar to the location of nerve root irritation and focal tissue injury are strong pointers to the existence of a causal association linking the two phenomena mediated by nerve root irritation and focal tissue trauma. There are some crucial clinical implications noted in regard to this case. To begin with, urologists are advised to be more alert about this possible complication, and herpes zoster should be included in the list of possible diagnoses when patients report dermatomal pain or vesicular eruption after ESWL, in the thorax or lumbar area. Second, a discussion of this uncommon but potentially dangerous complication is to be included when counseling the patient before ESWL is done, particularly in older patients or those who have a history of herpes zoster previously. Third, it is essential to note that early identification and prompt administration of antiviral treatment within 72 hours of the onset of the rash will help reduce acute morbidity, hasten recovery, and decrease the risk of post-herpetic neuralgia, the most debilitating long-term effect.

Considering the increased incidence of urolithiasis all over the world and the extensive use of ESWL as a minimally invasive intervention method, awareness of infrequent complications, including VZV reactivation, is likely to provide a full understanding of the management of a patient. Further prospective research is required to determine the actual occurrence of this complication and define the particular risk factor, including shock wave energy levels and the number of shock waves delivered, and measures of the effect of prophylactic antiviral treatment in high-risk groups receiving ESWL. Such a preventive measure as the promotion of herpes zoster vaccination in those patients who are due to undergo any urological operation can also be a worthwhile topic of study. Reporting this case will raise clinical awareness and add to the scarcely existing, yet increasing literature base, on this significant association.
