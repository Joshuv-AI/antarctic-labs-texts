“You’ve made it if you can, not have to live to survive, but live to live”: Beliefs about socioeconomic success and mobility among youth and parents in public housing
Authors: Lindsay Lanteri, Jane Leer, Gabriella Valencia, Rebekah Levine Coley, Samantha Teixeira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBeliefs about success and social mobility are important contributors to young people’s wellbeing and motivation, yet understanding of youths’ and parents’ views on these factors is limited. Prior work is mostly quantitative, using researcher-defined definitions that prioritize financial success, educational attainment, and occupational prestige. To expand this perspective, we conducted semi-structured interviews with a racially diverse sample of 25 youth ages 12 to 24 (M = 17) and 32 parents living in a large public housing development (39 % Hispanic or Latino, 19 % Black, 16 % Asian, 16 % White, 11 % multiracial). We asked participants about their goals for their own and their children’s future, how they would define success, and the processes that support or impede said success. We coded and analyzed transcripts using Reflexive Thematic Analysis, centering participants’ experiences. Most parents (81 %) and youth (92 %) expressed traditional views of success, emphasizing educational attainment, well-paying careers, and homeownership. These views align closely with the American Dream. Close to half of participants described alternative definitions of success, emphasizing non-socioeconomic and communal values, such as job satisfaction, morals, and familial relationships. Most youth and parents emphasized a reliance on personal characteristics, like hard work, as key to achieving success. Far fewer acknowledged structural barriers that made “getting ahead” difficult, including financial stressors, neighborhood disadvantage, discrimination, and stigma. Study findings extend prior work highlighting how perspectives of success are nuanced and encompass both traditional and alternative views, with implications for policies and programs that aim to support social mobility among youth and families living in poverty.Keywords: Adolescent development, Parent perception, Social mobility, Economic inequality, Public housing, Reflexive thematic analysis

Introduction

Young people today are growing up in a strikingly unequal world. Income and wealth inequality in the U.S. have risen markedly since 1980 (Horowitz et al., 2020), with similar trends globally (Chancel et al., 2022). Against this backdrop, a growing body of research aims to understand how youth make sense of inequality and reason about socioeconomic success or mobility (i.e., improvements in social status) (Browman et al., 2019; Ruck et al., 2019). Beliefs about success and mobility are especially influential during adolescence and young adulthood, when young people are uniquely attuned to social status and actively constructing their own identities (Branje et al., 2021; Erickson, 1968). Beliefs about the attainability of upward social mobility have been linked to improved mental and behavioral health and greater persistence in academically challenging environments, especially among youth from economically disadvantaged backgrounds (e.g., Browman et al., 2017; Browman et al., 2019; Ritterman Weintraub et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2022).

Yet the extant literature has numerous limitations. Youths’ voices are largely absent in prior work on social mobility. Qualitative research on mobility beliefs also is limited, and existing studies suggest a potential mismatch between academic notions of mobility and definitions of success among those from economically disadvantaged backgrounds who are often the targets of interventions aimed to support social mobility (Folkes, 2022; Hoskins and Barker, 2017). Further, little is known about the socialization processes through which youth acquire mobility beliefs. Parents are theorized to play a central role in youths’ learning about social class (Mistry et al., 2021), yet research is only just beginning to compare and contrast beliefs about mobility among youth and their parents and to understand the role of cultural scripts, such as the American Dream, in shaping parents’ and youths’ mobility beliefs (Griffin et al., 2024; Thompson et al., 2018).

To address these gaps, this paper uses qualitative interviews with a racially diverse sample of 25 youth (ages 12 through 24) and 32 parents living in public housing. We examine how youth and parents define success (or “making it”) and explain the processes through which success is achieved. Our focus on youth and parents in public housing is an important contribution to the literature on beliefs about social mobility, which is thus far comprised of studies with mostly middle and upper middle-class samples (with some exceptions; see Browman et al., 2019; Hoskins and Barker, 2017). Further, by asking youth and parents to describe in their own words what success and social mobility looks like and how mobility is achieved, we center participants’ lived experiences for a more holistic and inductive approach to examining how youth make sense of the socioeconomic hierarchy.

Adolescence as a pivotal moment for beliefs about socioeconomic success and mobility

Beliefs about success and mobility are likely to be especially consequential during adolescence due to changes in cognitive complexity, increased attention to the social hierarchy, and life-course transitions occurring during this period, roughly spanning ages 12 through 24 (National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine, 2019). During adolescence, young people are increasingly capable of abstract and complex thinking about social categories like “working class” and “upper class,” while also being uniquely sensitive to social comparisons and status differentials (Crosnoe, 2011; Twenge and Campbell, 2002). Additionally, a key developmental task during adolescence is identity development (Branje et al., 2021; Erickson, 1968), which includes goals and beliefs about one’s future identity (Oyserman and James, 2011). Identity-based decisions about educational attainment, career trajectories, and other behaviors relevant for socioeconomic attainment also surface during this developmental period.

Existing research on mobility beliefs spans from early to late adolescence, but has not examined developmental differences within this relatively large age range (Browman et al., 2017; Browman et al., 2019; Ritterman Weintraub et al., 2015). The executive function and metacognition skills necessary for long term planning and goal setting emerge in mid through late adolescence (Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023; Sari et al., 2025), and young adolescents are still in the early stages of identity exploration (Branje et al., 2021). This suggests that definitions of mobility may be less concrete in early versus late adolescence. Younger adolescents also are just beginning to conceptually integrate abstract concepts like poverty and wealth, and there is some evidence that complex thinking about inequality increases from early to mid-adolescence (Mistry et al., 2012, Wray-Lake et al., 2023). A better understanding of changes in mobility beliefs over time, particularly among youth from economically disadvantaged backgrounds, can inform interventions that aim to support youths’ upward mobility trajectories.

Youths’ views of success and perceived pathways to mobility

The existing research makes clear that beliefs about success and mobility may relate to numerous domains of adolescent development. However, young people growing up in public housing may hold views of success that differ from traditional views of success centering money, educational attainment, and occupational prestige (Browman et al., 2019). Qualitative research with youth has revealed definitions of success that emphasize relationships, health, and values (Hoskins and Barker, 2017; Martin-Storey et al., 2018). For example, working class parents and adolescents in the U.K. defined success in terms of finding fulfillment and happiness through familial bonds and ties to community (Folkes, 2022; Hoskins and Barker, 2017). Job satisfaction was important to youth, but they discussed a desire for challenging and stimulating work as opposed to prestigious or well-paying jobs (Hoskins and Barker, 2017). Further, whereas upward social mobility is typically understood as achieving a higher status from one’s family of origin, working class parents emphasized the value of continuity from generation to generation (Folkes, 2022; Hoskins and Barker, 2017). These findings demonstrate the complexities of beliefs about success and highlight the need for additional research to understand beliefs about success among youth and parents from economically disadvantaged backgrounds in distinct cultural contexts.

The U.S. is an important context to study youths’ social mobility beliefs given that beliefs about success are deeply ingrained in the cultural ethos via the American Dream. Here, we refer to the American Dream as a master narrative in which success is defined by socioeconomic attainment and is reflected in intergenerational mobility. A core element of the American Dream is that success is universally accessible, so long as one puts in the effort. A growing body of research, however, has cast doubt on the accessibility of the American Dream, highlighting how rates of upward mobility have declined dramatically since the 1940s (Chetty et al., 2014). A recent Pew Research poll shows that only about four-in-ten adults under the age of 50 think it is still possible to achieve the American Dream, with higher-income Americans saying it is more likely than lower-income Americans (Borelli, 2024).

Despite declining rates of social mobility nationally, the ubiquity of the American Dream is evident in research on beliefs about how success is achieved and causal attributions for wealth and poverty. Evidence across age groups shows that wealth and poverty are most often attributed to individual factors (e.g., hard work), followed by societal or structural factors (e.g., inequitable education systems), and/or fatalistic explanations (e.g., luck) (Bullock, 2017; Davidai and Wienk, 2021). Adolescents are more likely than younger children to cite structural factors (Diaz et al., 2023; Flanagan et al., 2014; Mistry et al., 2012; Mistry and Yassine, 2022), but individual factors are the most common explanation given by both youth and adults (Clemente et al., 2017; Davidai and Wienk, 2021; Dickinson et al., 2023). However, there are contextual differences. For example, Finnish youth were more likely to endorse structural explanations for inequality rather than individual factors, perhaps due to growing up in a strong welfare state where poverty is viewed as a societal, rather than personal problem (Hakovirta and Kallio, 2016). Additionally, a recent study of adolescents of color attending progressive U.S. schools that emphasized civic engagement found that structural explanations dominated (Diaz et al., 2023).

Theoretical framework

Where these beliefs come from, or how young people are socialized to develop beliefs about success and attributions for wealth and poverty, is less well understood. Ecological theories of child and adolescent development emphasize how the broader sociopolitical macrosystem—including societal norms and cultural values—shape development by influencing interactions and behaviors within the proximal settings where youth spend their time (Bronfenbrenner, 1979; García Coll et al., 1996). Newer models which place sociopolitical history and culture at the center of the ecological model rather than on the periphery (see Vélez-Agosto et al., 2017) allow for cultural norms, narratives, and values to be seen as inseparable from human development (Syed and McLean, 2021).

This study draws on the concept of “master narratives” to consider how youth come to understand success and mobility (McLean and Syed, 2015; Syed and McLean, 2021). Master narratives describe how macrosystem cultural scripts (e.g., the American Dream) shape the development of personal narratives, identities, and beliefs about the social world. Master narratives are a reflection of dominant power structures within society, and are perpetuated by institutions and by people, often passively (e.g., through cultural norms and behavior). Pushback against master narratives can emerge through the adoption of alternative narratives, which actively resist the master narrative or at a minimum provide a degree of difference from the dominant view (Andrews, 2002; McLean and Syed, 2015).

Master narrative cultural scripts are likely passed on in part through parents and other primary caregivers, who are important socializers, transmitting beliefs, values, and social norms through both explicit conversations as well as implicit, non-verbal cues (Abaied and Perry, 2021). However, research is just beginning to theorize (Jones et al., 2018) and empirically examine (Griffin et al., 2024; Thompson et al., 2018) parental socialization about class and inequality. Studies show that explicit socialization about social class (via parent–child conversations) is infrequent, often perceived as taboo, and wrought with tension and inconsistencies (e.g., parents emphasized the importance of finding a career that would pay well, while at the same time avoiding or limiting direct conversations about their own family’s socioeconomic circumstances) (Griffin et al., 2024; Thompson et al., 2018). Less is known about how parents themselves describe success and pathways to success, which is key to understanding the types of implicit messages about success and mobility that parents may convey.

The current study

To address these gaps in the literature, this qualitative study investigates social mobility beliefs and perceptions of success in a sample of youth (ages 12–24) and parents living in a public housing community. Developmental research on social mobility beliefs among youth and parents living in low-income neighborhoods is limited (Folkes, 2022; Hoskins and Barker, 2017); as such, we chose a qualitative design to further explore both commonalities and differences in these beliefs. We were guided by two primary research questions: 1) How do youth and parents living in a public housing community think about and define success and mobility? and 2) What are the processes that youth and parents describe as promoting or impeding social mobility? As an exploratory aim, we asked how do social mobility beliefs vary over the span of adolescence?

Methods

Study setting

This paper drew on data collected as part of the Housing Opportunity and Mobility Experiment (HOME) Study, a longitudinal, mixed-method study of residents of one of the oldest and largest public housing developments in New England which is slated for redevelopment into a mixed-income community. The development includes approximately 1,000 households and 2,000 residents. Of those 1,000 households, 54 % consisted of families, and children under 18 represented 29 % of the development’s overall population, while young adults (ages 18–24) represented 10 %. The broader neighborhood where this study is situated has become increasingly gentrified; median rents have risen drastically, the share of adults with a bachelor’s degree has grown, and household incomes have increased (Hermann et al., 2019), such changes may have implications for youths’ mobility beliefs.

HOME data collected at Wave 1 (prior to the redevelopment) include (1) adult surveys, (2) youth surveys (open to all youth ages 12–24), (3) physiological and environmental assessments, and (4) in-depth, semi-structured interviews, which are the source of data for this manuscript. A more detailed description of the overall study is available upon request including the in-depth interview protocols.

Participants

The in-depth interview component of the study consisted of one-on-one, semi-structured interviews with a sub-sample of 75 youth and adult HOME Study participants between August 2023 and January 2024. Due to this paper’s focus on youth and parents’ perceptions of intergenerational beliefs on social mobility, we excluded interviews with non-parental adults, which resulted in a final sample of 57 participants, including 25 youth and 32 parents. The youth sample was fairly evenly distributed in terms of gender (44 % female) and ranged in age from 12 to 24 years (M = 17, SD = 3.85), capturing the period from early to late adolescence. The large age range of our sample facilitated an exploratory analysis of developmental differences in definitions of success. Parents were predominantly female (94 %) and ranged from 26 to 72 years (M = 44, SD = 11.23). The sample was racially and ethnically diverse (44 % of parents and 32 % of youth were Hispanic or Latino, 19 % of parents and 20 % of youth were Non-Hispanic Black, 16 % of parents and youth were Non-Hispanic White, 13 % of parents and 20 % of youth were Non-Hispanic Asian, and 9% of parents and 12 % of youth were multiracial). Furthermore, the sample was linguistically diverse: while all youth interviews were conducted in English, 53 % of parents completed an interview in English, 34 % in Spanish, and 13 % in Cantonese or Mandarin. Procedures

In-depth interviews were conducted by a diverse team of 11 trained interviewers, including 3 of the 5 authors of this manuscript. Interviewers were matched to participants based on language and, where possible, race and ethnicity. Interviewers recruited participants by phone and text message. Prior to initiating the interview, researchers obtained consent from participants aged 18 and above, and parental consent and youth assent for youth under age 18. Interviews were guided by a semi-structured instrument comprised of open-ended questions that aimed to elucidate resident perceptions of neighborhood dynamics, housing-related issues, parenting and family life, school and work, health concerns, as well as social and economic mobility. The interview protocol was designed to gain a holistic view of participants’ experiences. It included several questions aimed explicitly at capturing participants’ thoughts on success and social mobility (see Table 2); social mobility related content also came up in response to other questions focused on domains including neighborhood and family dynamics. Interviews were conducted in-person in participants’ homes or in a private community space (except for one Zoom interview), averaging 55 min for parents and 44 min for youth. Participants were compensated with a Target gift card ($40 for youth, $60 for parents). Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim; Chinese and Spanish transcripts were translated into English for analysis. All procedures were approved by the Boston College Institutional Review Board.

Analytic approach

We used Braun and Clarke’s (2021) six phase Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) approach to analyze interview transcripts. This approach is well-suited due to its utility in identifying and interrogating patterns in qualitative data, producing layperson-friendly results, and informing policy (Braun and Clarke, 2021). These six phases included 1) familiarization; 2) coding; 3) generating initial themes; 4) developing and reviewing themes; 5) defining and naming themes; and 6) writing the findings.

To familiarize ourselves with the data, we engaged in a process of data immersion. This began with several members of the authorship team conducting interviews and continued as the entire team listened to recordings of interviews and read transcripts. We wrote memos to capture early insights which were used in developing the initial codebook. We then engaged in a multi-cycle coding process using NVivo 14 software. Beginning deductively, our initial set of codes were broad (e.g., aspirations, definitions of success, examples of success) and were derived from theory and a priori ideas from the literature related to master narratives like the American Dream which emphasize personal characteristics for achieving success over structural impediments. We then worked inductively, adding codes that came directly from participants themselves. For example, “better future” was not an initial concept in the codebook, but because of its salience in interviews it was added to represent parents’ desires to provide a better future for their children. A team of 11 students and faculty, representing the linguistic and cultural diversity of the participants, worked in pairs to code all transcripts. In RTA, though a multi-coder process is not essential, when employed, the purpose is to collaborate and gain insights from the data, not necessarily to achieve inter-coder agreement (Braun and Clarke, 2021). Therefore, coding teams met weekly to discuss the coded transcripts and used a consensus-based approach to determine final codes. This consensus-based approach led to richer insights because different coders noticed unique nuances in the data, spurring thoughtful conversations that led to a deeper second cycle coding process. A smaller team of three then conducted second cycle coding focused on the research questions explicit to this manuscript. Second cycle codes were axial in nature, which allowed similarly coded data to be grouped together and relabeled into codes that were more representative of the core ideas participants raised related to social mobility (Saldaña, 2015).

Next, we generated initial themes. In an iterative process, beginning during second cycle coding, we developed themes by collating codes into broader thematic categories. Through memo writing and continued review of the literature on perceptions of mobility influenced by master narratives like the American Dream, as well as theoretical notions of collectivistic versus individualistic norms, we developed an initial set of themes. We then iteratively reviewed our candidate themes and assessed their fit with the data. Upon conclusion of the review process, themes were defined and named (Braun and Clarke, 2021).

We used NVivo tools such as queries and conceptual diagrams to analyze themes separately for different periods of adolescence, as well as to compare parent and youth responses. Debrief meetings were used to discuss differences and similarities between older and younger youth, and between parents’ and youths’ beliefs. While there were 15 parent–child dyads in our sample, we did not analyze the data in a dyadic manner because the interview protocol was not designed to examine dyadic interactions or within-family belief congruence, and many participants were not in dyadic pairs. Rather we aimed to understand how views were similar or different between parents and youth broadly, within our unique neighborhood-specific setting, and to exploratively examine variation in social mobility views by adolescent developmental stage. Finally, we wrote the results of our analysis, selecting illustrative examples that logically told the story of the data. Developmental differences were only described in the results section, if the majority of youth in an age band endorsed a particular theme. Most qualitative quotes were presented verbatim, however, in some instances quotes were “tidied up” to assist with readability, succinctness, and to maintain grammatical integrity of sentences (Lingard, 2019, p. 361). For example, excessive use of filler words such as “uh/um/like” were removed for readability. Ellipses were used to signal where tangential portions of quotes were removed, while still maintaining the authenticity of participant’s words, and brackets were used to reflect when quotes were revised for grammatic clarity.

Reflexivity

A key aspect of RTA is the “reflexive” element, which involves an acknowledgement that the themes developed cannot exist separately from us as researchers. Rather, they are mediated through our values, skills, and experiences, and thus we recognize that our coding process is inherently subjective (Braun and Clarke, 2021). As part of our reflexive process and to establish our credibility for interpreting the data, we share our positionalities here. Our authorship team is multicultural and multilingual. All five authors identify as White women, and one as Hispanic/Latina. Most members of the authorship team were raised in low-income households and have experienced significant upward mobility from their family of origin, including being the first in their family to go to college, while others grew up middle class and have stayed middle class. Authors collectively have decades of professional experience working with urban communities of concentrated poverty to understand the developmental consequences of socioeconomic inequality.

Throughout our research we engaged in continual reflection on how our personal and professional identities as well as our privileges may influence our interpretation of the data and generation of themes. We interrogated these interpretations and checked the trustworthiness and rigor of our analysis by using multiple coders to triangulate the data and by debriefing with peers. Furthermore, authors have been engaged with the community for over seven years, which has led to strong community relationships and allowed us to seek community feedback on our survey instruments, interview protocols, and baseline findings.

Results

We begin with findings for research question one: How do youth and parents living in a public housing community define success? We identified two overarching definitions of success, traditional views associated with socioeconomic success factors and alternative views emphasizing happiness and other well-being factors, as well as several sub-themes within each domain (Table 3). Most parents (81 %) and youth (92 %) in our sample endorsed traditional definitions of success – that is, definitions of success emphasizing income, education, and economic mobility in line with the American Dream master narrative. Approximately half of parents and youth recognized alternative definitions of success that differed from, but did not necessarily resist the master narrative. Many participants incorporated both themes in their definitions of success, highlighting the multifaceted nature of these views.

Traditional views of success

Traditional views of success, endorsed by more than 80 % of youth and parents, focused on educational goals, attaining financial stability, and/or a well-paying career. Some participants also incorporated the importance of a home in providing stability and financial and physical security. Through exploratory analysis we found that goals related to this theme were discussed differently by younger versus older youth (generally categorized into < 15; 15–19; 20–24) and by youth versus parents. Additionally, parents often defined their success vis-à-vis their children’s success with an intense desire to provide their children with a better future. Each traditional view sub-theme is discussed below.

Education

Over half of youth and parents associated success with educational attainment or achievement. Younger youth (less than 15 years old) talked about educational success in terms of earning good grades, while youth between the ages of 15 and 19 discussed aspirations pertaining to college enrollment and completion. As one 17-year-old female said, “I mainly hope to go to college, graduate in a major that I’m interested in.” An 18-year-old male replied similarly when asked about his future goals, “Um, I’m going to apply to several colleges … pursue further education.” This desire to attend college and achieve success through graduating and receiving a diploma was a common sentiment, particularly among the high-school aged youth. External pressures and messaging around the importance of college from parents, school, and society is likely amplified for this age group. In contrast, few of the older youth (ages 20–24) mentioned attending college. One youth (age 22) specifically indicated that a college trajectory was not in her future, “College isn’t for me. I feel like I just wanna get my GED.” This shift in mentality from high-school aged students who have strong aspirations to attend and graduate from college, to those who are college-aged but often facing educational challenges (e.g., in GED classes or still trying to complete alternate high school programs) may indicate developmental shifts in perceptions of educational mobility when faced with disrupted educational paths.

Parents similarly emphasized education as a marker of success and stressed the importance of attending college. As one mother said, “it is my goal that both kids gonna go to college … regardless of what you doing you need at least two years of college.” Parents in turn measured their own success via their ability to support their children in navigating educational trajectories. For some parents this included providing their children with greater opportunities like enrolling them in high-performing charter schools or providing educationally supportive activities from an early age. For example, one mother shared her success in supporting literacy activities with her children:

You know, one thing about me, I educated my kids from the time they were little … with every tool, hooked on phonics … Anything they had to educate you, my kids [had it].

Another mother similarly valued the importance of education and described her own dedication to her child’s learning opportunities:

Um, education has always been very important to me … So when my kids was little, I always read to them … My son started reading at four, like fully reading, you know?

Other parents’ narratives focused on providing financial and emotional support for their children’s educational and life success. For example, one mother described her success vis-à-vis financially supporting her children’s educational endeavors: “If I can financially get them through college, then I am successful.” In addition to the importance of financing their children’s education, parents also recognized that support goes beyond the material, such as teaching children how to live independently, as reflected in this mother’s description:

I hope he can finish college, find a good job, and then move out on his own. That’s it. He can be independent without us having to worry about him. Teach him some simple cooking and he won’t explode the house …

Finally, parents described a strong sense of pride when sharing their children’s educational success stories, despite the challenges they faced growing up in public housing:

Like I said, my baby just made it to college. That’s right. Living in this development raised by a single mom. Yes, he is right on over at [prestigious university] … For him, that’s a great accomplishment … but for me, parental-wise, it’s just like … whew. One kid down … I was happy that my words and prepping him, you know, he was able to prevail.

Careers, material goods, and financial stability

Youth and parents acknowledged that good jobs and making money were important measures of success, but there were differences between younger and older youth, and between parents and youth. Younger youth mentioned goals related to material goods like “having a lot of new tech products” while older youth clearly articulated how earning money affords a certain level of status. An 18-year-old male described, “I think money is a good measure of your contribution to society, you know? And, how much you’re earning also is, kind of your status in society.” Some youth spoke about making money in idealistic terms, as a 21-year-old female said, “Um, long-term, it’s like everybody has big goals, and like obviously, everybody wanna be a millionaire. [laughs] … It’s just one day, I guess I hope to hit the lottery.” Other youth were more prudent, focusing on basic economic stability when thinking about the role that money can play in their lives. For example, a 19-year-old female reflected on what success and financial comfort looked like to her, “you don’t have to save up half your paycheck to pay the rent … you don’t have to be like … if I want to eat … I have to save money.” Similarly, a 17-year-old female said, “I feel like when you have a stable income, you have food on the table every day.”

These definitions of career and monetary success, emphasizing earning sufficient income to meet basic needs, align strongly with parents’ definitions and views. In fact, the mother of the 17-year-old quoted above reported a very similar definition as her daughter, “having a stable home, always food on your table … your essential needs, what you need. Having savings put aside.” Parents in our sample typically focused on jobs and money as a way to provide financial stability, which for them was an indicator of achieving success. This was emphasized in another mother’s description of her career hopes for her children:

Well, I want them to have a profession that pays good money, that they live well, that they don’t have to live in a place like this, but a place, that is, a house they can afford, that they don’t have scarcity … that they live a life without precariousness …

Her aspiration was for her children to have a good profession as a pathway to a better, more stable life. A better life equated to moving out of public housing and living a life where shortages of essentials were not a constant worry. Other parents similarly talked about how employment mattered for this level of stability, as this father described:

You have to be working, you just, you need a good job. You, you’d have to have something stable … the world’s changing so fast with something that you can transition into if things change up.

This father not only reflected on how a job could provide financial stability but also references the need for transferable skills so that one can be more flexible in the face of external changes, indicating a pragmatic grounding in the world of work.

Home

Home ownership is a quintessential symbol of the American Dream which was central to both youths’ and parents’ visions of success, though in different ways. Youth tended to describe homes as a material marker of success or status, using visceral imagery in their descriptions of how homes were relevant to success, including descriptions of physical features like a porch and triangular roofs (juxtaposed with the flat roofs of the low-rise urban housing projects in which they live). For example, an 18-year-old male used descriptive language to paint a picture of how he saw having a home as a key marker of success in American society:

I feel like in America, a house is a staple of a modern American family. It’s like, oh, you have a house. House with a motherf***ing porch … I’m sorry for the cursing. Um, a house with a porch or I don’t know, a yard or something. Or like you come home to like a pie [laughs] or something … just stuff that stamps your wealth or your financial class.

A younger youth (12-years-old) similarly described attributes of suburban homes as signaling success and wealth:

… a good house. Maybe like a suburban house, something like that… Maybe two stories. Three stories? Which is including the basement … a triangle roof … suburban type houses … garages, and a pool or something, like a nicely set up backyard.

Parents, in contrast, were more focused on how homes provided stability and safety. This theme appeared uniquely relevant in the public housing development that is the context for this study, where there are two types of residences: multistory apartment buildings and single-family attached townhomes. Townhomes are larger with private entrances and have small front yards, while apartment buildings have concrete courtyards and shared stairwells, which were described by many residents as unsafe. A mother who had moved from another public housing development in the city, which had only multistory apartment buildings, to a townhome in the current study community reflected on how she achieved her dream of living in the type of house she always desired, highlighting safety features:

My dream was to have a house with the bedrooms upstairs and a basement. That was my dream. At that time … I was living on the sixth floor. There were drugs and all that. The elevator was always out of service because they kept doing bad things there. And something happened with my daughter, and they gave me a transfer to come here, so I was able to achieve my dream, a house with bedrooms and a basement.

Home ownership also came up when residents referenced moving out of public housing. One of our interview questions asked residents if they knew anyone in their neighborhood who had achieved the types of success they desired for themselves. Over forty percent of parents cited neighbors who had relocated as examples of success precisely because of their ability to move out of public housing, whether through renting or purchasing a home elsewhere. In many cases self-comparison with neighbors who were able to move out of the projects led to thoughts of despair, as this one parent reflected, “Oh, it sucks. Sitting there like, oh sh**, you just used to live right next to me, now look at you. You got a house … It sucks, but can’t let it bring me down.” Others remained hopeful, as one mother described her desire for “a house with a beautiful car and a backyard” leveraging her own mother as an example for what could be possible in terms of “getting out of the projects”:

Like, I’ve always said I do not wanna live off the system my whole life. I refuse to be one of those people. I’ve seen my mom get out of it. So, I’m, like, I definitely wanna be one of those people … Make a better life for myself.

Better future

Providing a better future for one’s children was also a common sub-theme interwoven into parents’ narratives on living a successful life. Promoting intergenerational mobility and ensuring access to opportunities that parents themselves did not have is a core component of master narratives around the American Dream. This idea of providing a better future came up when parents discussed each sub-theme within the traditional view of success: educational opportunities, career trajectories, and home ownership. For example, one mother described how she saw education serving as a pathway out of the housing projects for her children:

I might have been one of those kids that grew up here and is kind of still here, but at the end of the day, they have a chance to just be gone and go to college and get out as soon as possible. So, I’m working hard for them to be like that.

Another mother echoed a similar sentiment about how education opened doors and possibilities that she herself was not able to pursue:

Yeah, I want them to go to college. Even if they don’t want to do it like right after finishing high school. But I do want both of ‘em to do some type of career that I couldn’t do. I want them to have something.

Both parents’ descriptions highlight the desire for their children to succeed in ways that they themselves could not, whether it was graduating from college, having a specific career, or moving out of the neighborhood. Some parents thought about this idea of a “better future” as the ability to have options and agency in life. This mother reflected on her own desires to prevent her children from ending up in challenging, low-wage work like she found herself in:

Well, I want my daughter, I always tell her, “you have to go to college,” … I say “if you don’t go to school, you’ll end up like me, serving food in school.” My hand hurts from so much scrubbing and mopping, putting up with it … And it’s not that I’m ashamed of the job. No … but if you don’t study, opportunities become limited, and you have to put up with things you maybe shouldn’t have to.

Other parents spoke about the need to accomplish their own goals and be a role model to their children, to show them that having a better future was possible. For this mother, a better future entailed home ownership specifically:

There’s a reason why I have all these goals and want to do better and want a property, so they actually have something I want to pass on, I wanna show them how things are supposed to be done. And I feel like owning our property specifically like a rental property is probably the only way to get the hell out of poverty, you know what I mean?

When parents considered ways in which they could support their children to have a better future they reflected primarily on traditional views of success (e.g., education, career, home) and how these could be used as pathways out of poverty, menial work, and public housing.

Alternative views of success

In contrast to traditional views, alternative views of success focused on emotional sentiments related to finding fulfillment and happiness through job satisfaction, strong morals, and relationships. Communal (as opposed to individualist) definitions of success were also emphasized in each of these sub-themes and serve as a cross-cutting theme within alternative views of success. Differences between parents and youth were identified in all three sub-themes, but developmental differences between older and younger youth were only observed for youths’ description of career satisfaction.

Career satisfaction and happiness

The most common alternative definition of success that youth mentioned was related to career and job satisfaction, with close to half (46 %) expressing the importance of having a job that brings joy as opposed to prestige or wealth. For example, a 22-year-old female described success as finding happiness and fulfillment in one’s career:

I feel like someone who has a sense of self … doing what they want, doing what they love and you know, doing what makes them happy instead of doing what [they] have to do to just keep getting by. I feel like if you’ve made it, you’re able to, you know, like if you’re an artist, you’re able to just paint and that’s … what you make your living off of and that’s what you love to do. I feel like you’ve made it if you can, not have to live to survive, but live to live.

Her definition reflects a belief that success is more than financial stability and survival, it’s about thriving and well-being that goes beyond meeting basic needs. A 21-year-old female in our sample echoed the importance of finding joy in one’s career path, but also integrated more traditional views associating jobs with financial stability:

… if you’re successful, I think you’re doing something that you like truly like or love, or if not … [at least] you feel comfortable. You don’t have to be rich, but at least you’re comfortable and you know, you’re not like, struggling.

Both young women’s definitions of success include a desire for the freedom to pursue a path that will lead to happiness, rather than merely working a job to make ends meet or to gain prestige and wealth.

Younger youth in our sample also touched upon the idea of fulfillment in their definitions of success, but in much shorter and less elaborate ways. When asked what makes someone successful, they said things like, “being happy with what they’re doing” (13-year-old) or “finally doing something … like a dream” (12-year-old). This comparison between younger versus older youth reflect youths’ developing capacity for goal setting and future orientation.

Parents similarly emphasized happiness and job-satisfaction when defining success, but often via hybrid definitions, acknowledging the importance of finding happiness in one’s career (alternative view), but also the practicality of needing to earn a stable income (traditional view). A mother of multiple children said, “I want them all to do something that is gonna make them happy, but also make them money.” She went on to describe her role as a parent in ensuring their career success by instilling certain values early on, “… So I’m like trying to get that in their heads now and I’m trying to get them to be responsible, like to show up early, don’t be late ‘cause those are the things that employers look for.” Another parent also endorsed the importance of traditional views of earning money and alternative views of finding happiness in one’s career, which she described as doing meaningful work and having a larger purpose:

So, I actually wanna do something meaningful and be part of the community. To me success is when you find something like that, that you can do that either makes you an income or helps you bond with your community and keep ties.

Purpose beyond the self is connected to the cross-cutting theme of communal values.

Moral values

Almost a quarter of parents measured their own success in terms of raising their children to have strong morals and behave in prosocial ways. In line with this sub-theme’s relevance to raising children, moral values were not discussed by youth in our sample. Moral values discussed by parents were imbued with concepts such as selflessness and working to better the community rather than solely focusing on self-improvement. The cross-cutting theme of communal values is showcased in this mother’s definition of success centered around ensuring success for the entire community:

Everybody’s made it. Everybody is comfortable … if we are going to be successful as a [public housing] development together, we all need to have the same level of comfort … I mean generally as a baseline, same level.

Another mother defined success in terms of living a life where she could help others and give back to her community and explicitly rejected a status and money centric notion of success:

So my level of what I think success is, is not a Lamborghini, is not having a house in the Hamptons. It’s not having lots of money … a lot of, um, consumerism. Like, I’m not with that. I feel like a person that had a well lived life, that helped people, that was selfless, that was, you know, honest and truthful … in a sense of accomplishing personal victories. Like for me, a personal victory was to help a church build a homeless shelter, reconstruct someone’s home.

This mother went on to explain that she teaches her children these same values, “That is the goal as a parent, is to be able to instill good morals, good values, good spiritual stuff.” Morals also emerged in discussions of raising children to be good citizens and neighbors, specifically within the context of public housing, as one mother explained:

How do I define achieving success? For me, my success was educating my three kids honorably in a project. And saying I’ve never had trouble with a child of mine. I have two girls, they already have their lives. I have this young man, he’ll leave soon. And that’s my success. Because I’ve heard people who discriminate or undervalue a project. And I don’t agree with that because you can live in a mansion and have bad kids too, raised in an awful way. Once my son came [home] with that belief … And I tell him, “Yes, you live in a project. But don’t let anyone make you look down because you live in a project.”

This statement shows an awareness of negative stereotypes about residents of public housing, defining success through active defiance of these stereotypes and emphasizing that despite their circumstances she has still been able to raise “good” children.

Familial relationships

While relatively less frequent, some participants connected success and happiness to the strength of their familial relationships. For example, an 18-year-old male stated, “If I’m still in a healthy relationship. My family is healthy. I’m healthy … Yep. I’d be pretty happy.” A 15-year-old male described success in his own words as “A good job. A loving family. And that’s basically it.” Parents similarly reflected on the importance of family, and one mother in particular showcased how familial relationships are often prioritized above individualistic goals stating, “When your family is happy … that for me is success … You have to put your family first, always. Because without family, you don’t get no success.”

The cross-cutting theme of communal values, that is, defining one’s own success through the success of one’s family and community, was further defined when parents talked about maintaining relationships and providing for their families, as one father said:

I define success as follows. That each person wakes up every morning to look for sustenance for his family, and when they find it, that’s success. Because you have a family to support, and that’s it … keep going, and support your children. To me, that’s success.

Not only did participants define success in terms of having a family and acknowledging that their ability to support their family was a crucial indicator of success, but participants also shared the importance of family as a mechanism for helping them achieve their goals. One mother expressed her views around how she accomplished her goals related to successfully raising her children and keeping them safe via familial support, “You have a supportive family behind you. Like I said, I’m a single mom by myself, but we do have a big extended family, which is there, you know, [for] support, emotionally and stuff like that.”

Processes associated with social mobility and achieving success

Our second research question examined the processes that youth and parents described as promoting or hindering success. Responses were categorized around two major themes: personal factors and structural factors, with associated sub-themes (Table 4).

Personal factors driving success

Over half of our sample (58 % of youth and parents) emphasized personal factors contributing to success. Personal factors fell into two distinct sub-themes: 1) individual effort, motivation and hard work and 2) limiting self-doubt, with important nuances in youths’ versus parents’ descriptions. Participants also acknowledged that family support was crucial for staying motivated to complete one’s goals, especially in the face of barriers.

Individual effort, motivation, and hard work.

Working hard and putting in effort were commonly mentioned by both youth and adults when asked to describe how they planned to achieve their definition of success. Youth tended to name hard work and effort as key, but rarely provided concrete examples even when probed. For example, an 18-year-old male described how failing to put in effort might serve as a barrier to accomplishing one’s goals, “I think the hardest part is, probably keeping up the hard work.” A lack of hard work was also recognized by participants as a means for hampering success as this 19-year-old male noted.

At least from what I see, if you work hard, focus on that. Eventually, you might not get exactly what you want, but you at least be closer to that goal of what you want. As long as you focus, work hard, and do what you need to do. No slacking, no being lazy or any stuff like that …

Compared to youth, parents provided more contextualized and personal stories of why and how hard work led to success. For example, hard work and effort were frequently highlighted as key to overcoming obstacles through the portrayal of redemptive self-narratives. A mother described the difficulties she faced along her journey coming to the U.S. and how she persevered, overcoming adversity through her determination and hard work:

I came to this country, I grew up almost alone and I have moved forward with what I see in myself. No, I don’t have a lot, but I have been able to get ahead, I don’t know much English, like others … but I became a citizen, I work, I make an effort, I try to give my children the best education, and I understand that that is worth a lot.

In some instances, parents seemingly disregarded the idea of structural barriers and described success as dependent solely on individual effort. For example, this mother recounted a message she told her son, which stressed that achieving success was solely up to the individual and not dependent on life circumstances:

Just like I told him, if you work harder and spend more time now, you will live a very comfortable life in the future … This is a very fair world. Especially for us, we are new immigrants, we rely on [our] own efforts …

Participants who had immigrated to the US frequently discussed overcoming difficult circumstances and persevering in the face of adversity, which is reminiscent of the dominant, master narrative of meritocracy in which hard work leads to success.

Limiting self-doubt.

In a similar vein, numerous youth and parents shared a belief that one must combat self-limiting beliefs in order to achieve success. An 18-year-old male emphasized how self-doubt served as a barrier to accomplishing one’s goals

Not trusting yourself … or not trusting the process or not committing to something … You like, lose trust [in] yourself or you just fill your head up with negative thoughts and then you actually do end up believing that you can’t do it.

A parent similarly noted that the only thing getting in her child’s way was their own self-doubt:

Honestly, biggest obstacle is themselves. Not having enough faith within yourself will be your biggest obstacle. Nothing around you can control what you do to yourself … nothing else controls you putting one foot in front of the other ‘cause at the end of the day, you’re the only one who can control yourself.

However, self-doubt could be offset by family support, as this one young mother described in reflecting on her non-traditional educational trajectory and how she was able to achieve success because of her mother’s unwavering support:

Family support, it’s a big support because when I dropped out of high school, it was because of a few personal things that had happened, and I got into depression and I just didn’t want to go back to school … I had a baby and it was just a lot. But I did have my mom, she supported me all the way. I took a few afternoon classes and then I did online classes to finish high school. And I graduated … and my mom was there the whole way.

Structural factors affecting success

While many youth and parents in our sample reported very strong views that personal factors were the primary driving mechanism for achieving success and social mobility, approximately one third of participants (39 % of parents and 29 % of youth) recognized that both personal factors and structural barriers may impact one’s chances of getting ahead, with some differences observed between youth and parents in the types of structural factors they emphasized. In contrast, only a handful of participants in our sample solely endorsed structural factors and dismissed individual characteristics as pertinent to achieving success. For example, a 24-year-old female described how both personal and structural views could be true simultaneously and shared her depiction of what she thinks “making it” looks like for people born into different social classes:

I believe in both factors. Like, obviously, people could be born with it handed to them, like if you grew up, I don’t know, just say your family owns some hotels, business, or something like that, like you’re born rich. But then if you’re born here, for example, like you have to work way harder than someone who wasn’t born here to achieve your goals. But I do believe it’s possible. You just have to work harder than the average person.

She acknowledged the importance of hard work (i.e., personal characteristics), but also had an understanding that in American society there is a class hierarchy which makes it easier for some people to remain at the top, while others must work harder to get ahead. Her younger mid-adolescent sister (17-years-old) also acknowledged that working harder is a requirement for people who come from neighborhoods and backgrounds like hers versus those who come from more privilege:

Not so much whatever circumstances you’re born in, you have to stay in, but it’s harder for you to get out of the situation that you’re in. Like, there are some people who grew up poor and … have to work extra harder to get to like, let’s just say … a billionaire status, like, it’s way harder, opposed to somebody who was born in a … middle-class home.

In addition to acknowledging how social mobility prospects vary across social classes, many participants also spoke about specific structural barriers that make it particularly challenging to attain social mobility, including financial barriers, their neighborhood context, and experiences of stigma.

Financial barriers.

Financial struggles such as an inability to afford rent or costs associated with caregiving, higher education, or college loan debt were frequently mentioned, mostly by parents, as barriers to social mobility and success. In contrast, relatively few youth discussed financial barriers, and when they did, they were often less specific and detailed than parents. For example, one 12-year-old male, when asked about the types of things that can get in the way of success, replied, “Maybe rent. Or like, tax.” An older 22-year-old youth provided a more detailed example of economic structural barriers acknowledging the phenomena known as the benefits cliff, which occurs when a small increase in one’s income can result in a reduction or the loss of public assistance benefits, making it difficult to get ahead and move beyond living paycheck to paycheck.

… if you get the job then you know … you gotta tell housing and then they’re just gonna raise your rent and then you don’t even have a chance to start building up money before it’s like, okay. Now your rent’s gonna be like almost three grand.

Many parents described financial barriers in conjunction with personal factors. For example, one parent mentioned how even if young people have the motivation and skills necessary to achieve success in higher education, opportunities are limited due to financial constraints:

Well, I think that schooling should be cheaper … it should be easier to get into a good university without having to pay so much money … Because there are many people out there who have the ability to study, that is, they are intelligent, super intelligent, but it’s not easy for them to enter a good university because of their finances.

While many parents talked about barriers to college for their children, some also described how their own educational aspirations were thwarted due to financial expenses and debt:

There is a system in place … like, me personally, even though I went to three different colleges, I didn’t really finish to get my degree. I was halfway there, but I’m still in a situation where I can’t go, I can’t grow. There’s no forgiveness for a class. Like for instance, I owe a thousand dollars to [college redacted] because I didn’t finish the class and I didn’t withdraw in time … Now I can’t go back to the school.

Neighborhood context.

The neighborhood environment was also mentioned as a barrier to success by both youth and parents. Youth described how concentrated poverty could lead to social disorder, negative peer influence, and limited community and institutional resources. An 18-year-old male described how people living in the neighborhood could get swept up into problematic behavior, through no fault of their own, but simply due to the circumstances they were born into.

… I guess the area or the people around you, you know there’s people [with] really good hearts who are born into not the best situation. Or who are forced into certain, like gang violence or … when you see little kids with their parents and you can tell they use certain substances. And, I don’t know, sh** like that just makes me sad … It’s like that kid’s gonna probably go into the system … Stuff like that is upsetting.

A 12-year-old also talked about the neighborhood environment as an influential factor in determining whether one can get ahead and how negative peer influence can be detrimental to future life chances:

I think you could probably … become successful if you try hard enough. But it’ll also be harder if other people are trying to bring you down. And if you’re in a bad environment, it’ll be harder … Like, people doing a bunch of bad things like stealing, just doing a bunch of bad stuff … That’ll get you in trouble … ‘cause you’ll probably start doing that too.

Parents echoed similar fears that their children would be negatively influenced by their neighborhood, as the mother of the 12-year-old stated:

I think the neighborhood you’re in really makes an impact. Because then if you are doing good and people see that, people are so quick to tear people down … I’ve seen people verbally attack and destroy people that are trying to do better. You know, like, “oh, you think you’re better?” like just giving them a hard time. When it’s like, no, I’m just not on drugs anymore or no, I’m just like working and I feel like if you get caught up in hanging out with people and that kind of juvenile sh** that you get stuck.

Furthermore, both youth and parents also discussed the lack of resources in the neighborhood, which they viewed as inhibiting opportunities, as described by one mother:

It’s, it’s literally where problems and problematic people congregate … Not only that, it’s a set up for failure, literally in the community, there is no resources … and if there are any, they’re very limited. And, there’s nothing but you know, corner stores, liquor stores … there’s not many opportunities for bettering.

While some reported positive perceptions and highlighted the role of school and community resources that they (or their children) had personally utilized to achieve mobility goals (e.g., career counseling, English language programs, exam schools), others were concerned that despite availability of resources in the neighborhood there were barriers to access and limited information sharing. One parent described the situation as follows:

What I realized, there’s so many services out here in terms of food pantries, like get help with your taxes. Like take a class on this, that, or the other thing to improve your career … but I feel like there’s just not one central place for people to go. So, you kind of hear about these things by chance or word of mouth. But I feel like if there was one place for everybody to go to say, “I need help with food and here’s a food pantry list, these are the days that they’re open around here” and then somebody says, “um, I would like to learn coding.” And people say, “well, here’s a great college … ” They have these programs from the government for people like us, but they’re just not all in one place where people can take advantage of ‘em or use them.

Experiences of stigma.

Another barrier to success mentioned by a handful of parents, yet no youth, was experiences of social class and race-related stigma. Parents’ discussions of stigma provide striking examples of how they contextualize and understand the structural and systemic barriers they and their children face.

One mother described how people from outside the public housing development treated her in discriminatory ways based on her status as a Spanish-speaking public housing resident:

You know, people look at me when I go to the nice parts of [neighborhood redacted] … homeowners and stuff like that. And they look at me like I’m in the wrong side of the river. It’s just anywhere and everywhere about everything that I’m broke, that I’m Spanish that I’m poor … You know? Uh, I don’t belong here. I don’t have the right class. I’m a low class, so I should be treated like a low-class person because that’s where I am. So you’re telling me that I’m supposed to rise to my occasion, but I can’t be nothing but a project mom that’s never gonna go nowhere.

Her story reflects the disconnect between the American Dream (i.e., anyone can succeed if they are willing to work hard and put in the effort) versus the reality of how people in public housing—particularly those from racially minoritized or immigrant backgrounds—are often treated. As this mother noted, “rising to the occasion” is not enough; she believes society will always view her as a “mom from the projects.”

Another mother talked about how society expected her to stay on her side of town:

… The three projects have you cornered … To the white people, you’re the poor, the mediocre, the ones that come here to take up space, they see you as, “Okay, stay there, don’t go past there.”

This mother was aware of how both her race and her social class affects how she is viewed by others. She went on to describe how the stigma associated with living in public housing specifically has been passed down to her children through messaging that they will not be able to achieve success given the neighborhood they grew up in:

Because society views children differently when they live in public housing, they see them as kids who may not be able to achieve much, they place obstacles in their way, and often perceive them as “oh, you live in public housing? … Oh, there’s a lot of crime. That’s a delinquent too.” Sometimes even the teachers themselves … say, “Oh no, this child won’t amount to anything because they are growing up here.” … I mean, you’re already placing a barrier in front of a child. It’s even more challenging for you as a mom who is raising two kids, three kids … when they tell you these things, you get stressed, anxious because as a mom, you want your daughter to grow up, you want her to go to college, you want her to be someone, but they’re already putting obstacles in her way.

Stories related to discrimination and stigma surfaced among Hispanic, Black, and multiracial parents; in contrast, White and Asian parents in our sample did not mention having these experiences. While parents’ experiences clearly showcase that they feel looked down upon due to their low-income and public housing status, their stories also allude to their racial and ethnic identities, highlighting the intersectionality of race and poverty which can create a multi-layered experience of stigma for parents and families.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to investigate how parents and youth living in a public housing development define success and mobility and explain the processes through which success is attained and mobility achieved (or thwarted). Our qualitative approach began deductively leveraging the concept of master narratives (e.g., the American Dream), and then inductively incorporating parents’ and youths’ lived experiences of the opportunities that supported them in achieving their goals along with barriers that impeded their success.

Traditional and alternative views of success

Our findings add to an understanding of how both traditional and alternative views of success can co-exist. Some parents and youth fully embraced traditional views of success (i.e., what prior literature has used to quantitatively measure mobility), focusing on educational attainment, a good job and financial stability, stable housing, and better opportunities for the next generation. Others constructed alternative definitions for what living a successful life meant-highlighting factors such as career satisfaction, moral values, and strong families- and many endorsed both (44 %). We use the term alternative views instead of counter narratives which have been used by others in the literature (Andrews, 2002) because alternative views are broader and do not necessarily imply resistance to the master narrative (McLean and Syed, 2015). We found limited evidence of explicit resistant or antagonistic views towards traditional views of success, which could be related to several factors. First, educational attainment and income remain strong predictors of life course wellbeing in the United States, thus desires to further one’s education or earn decent money are rational. Second, our finding that most participants ascribed to traditional views of success and emphasized personal factors as key for achieving said success demonstrates the strength of the American Dream master narrative and the broader socio-political macro system (e.g., social norms, economic and education policies) that serve to maintain this narrative (McLean and Syed, 2015).

Education was an important component of traditional views of success. However, the way that education goals were described among our sample of parents and youth from low-income backgrounds differs from past research examining education-related goals and behaviors employed by more economically advantaged parents (Friedman, 2013; Galloway and Conner, 2015). Parents and youth in our sample discussed success in terms of college attendance and completion as opposed to aspirations of attending an elite college or attaining a prestigious and lucrative occupation (e.g., doctor, lawyer). Only a handful of youth named such careers as future goals. Further, we found limited evidence that parents and youth weighed the pros and cons of different colleges or universities in terms of future job or earnings prospects, rather highlighting the broad goal of attaining a college degree. In contrast, research on middle- and upper-middle class families shows how parents attempt to transmit status to their children by defining success in much narrower terms – prioritizing elite colleges and specific high-earning and prestigious career paths (Friedman, 2013; Galloway and Conner, 2015). Thus, while both high and low-SES parents have been shown to place a high degree of importance on higher education, they display different strategies and specific targets to support these goals.

Parents in our sample defined their own success in part through their children’s success, seeking to provide a better future for their children by creating access to educational opportunities which they themselves did not have. Opening doors to opportunities for the next generation is a central tenet of the American Dream, and parents conveyed this message in both explicit and implicit ways. For example, parents directly relayed the value of education to their children through explicit verbal messaging like “you have to go to college,” and describing a college degree as a ticket out of poverty. Parents also used implicit messaging via their behaviors such as returning to school themselves as adults and modeling for their kids how education was an important pathway to achieving other goals in their lives related to career and financial stability. The perceived importance of education was reflected in youths’ descriptions of their educational goals and desires to attend college, potentially resulting from explicit and implicit parental socialization in addition to other socializing agents and broader macrolevel master narratives like the American Dream (McLean and Syed, 2015). The tempered aspirations and expectations of educational attainment for older youth in our sample may reflect structural barriers related to educational opportunities and ease of access to higher education.

Traditional views of success also centered around careers, financial stability, and home ownership, though there was heterogeneity across youth and parents in terms of how these themes were discussed. For example, youth who held more traditional views of success around jobs and career focused on the status it would afford them (e.g., becoming a millionaire), while parents were more practical and focused on how jobs provided financial security. Some youth also had definitions that veered away from traditional views of career and monetary success and adopted alternative views emphasizing the importance of career happiness and satisfaction. These findings add to prior qualitative research with youth where social mobility was determined by stimulating and challenging work as opposed to prestigious jobs (Hoskins and Barker, 2017). This is not to say that money and earnings were unimportant; rather, money was of interest to these “career satisfaction” driven youth in so far as it provided them with sufficient funds to do the things they enjoyed doing. Youth described wanting to avoid jobs where they had to work paycheck to paycheck just to survive and instead wanted the freedom to pursue what they loved. In contrast, parents’ descriptions of their goals for their children were primarily focused on ensuring they found stable employment to avoid replicating the financial stressors parents themselves had faced.

A similar dynamic unfolded with respect to home ownership. Youths’ aspirations often revolved around material components of homes (e.g., having yards, porches, a garage) which in their minds signaled a degree of wealth and status. Parents’ narratives around homeownership, on the other hand, emphasized stability: having a roof over one’s head meant not having to worry about the stressors associated with sub-standard housing.

Our findings from youths’ narratives complement prior research and reflect their developing identities, which are often influenced by a desire to differentiate themselves from their parents and other youth while simultaneously balancing a need to belong to a social group and position themselves within the social hierarchy (Milner, 2004). In fact, studies of low-income adolescents have found that young people may obscure their social class and present an image of higher socioeconomic status than they truly possess (Deutsch and Theodorou, 2010; Pattillo-McCoy, 1999). This may explain the more aspirational definitions of success we observed among younger adolescents. An alternative explanation could be that youths’ optimism is particularly strong because their aspirational goals have yet to be tested (Arnett, 2001), as suggested by the tempered educational aspirations of older youth in our sample.

There were differences between younger versus older youth’s descriptions of education and career-related definitions of success. Younger youth (<15) emphasized doing well in school (getting good grades) while mid adolescents (15–19) emphasized getting into, attending, and completing college; thus, both groups’ goals aligned with the specific education-related tasks of their developmental stage. In contrast, older youth faced greater educational challenges and barriers to success, and many appeared to have tempered their expectations to more modest goals. In terms of financial and career goals, younger youth focused on material goods (e.g., “having a lot of new tech products”), while older youth explicitly noted the connections between different types of careers, financial status, and jobs as a means of providing financial stability. In line with past work (Heck et al., 2022), this suggests that early understandings of social status center around tangible, visible differences between rich and poor, whereas with age, youth develop more complex notions around the meaning of work and status.

While traditional views of success tied to education, money, careers and home ownership were central to both youths’ and parents’ narratives, alternative non-socioeconomic components of success related to communal goals were also mentioned across age groups. For example, many parents defined success in terms of raising their children to have strong morals, emphasizing responsibility and respect. Parents also described success in terms of developing personal relationships within their community, and staying connected with family. Thinking about success through a communal lens has also been found in prior literature, emphasizing “rising with your class” versus “rising out of [your class]” (Bradley, 2018; Reay, 2018, as cited in Folkes, 2022, p. 149). This finding is aligned with relational narratives constructed by participants from working class and low-income backgrounds that deemphasize dominant views of individual success and competition and instead prioritize family ties and social support systems (Folkes, 2022; Hoskins and Barker, 2017). Others have also suggested that conceptualizing a life consisting of meaningful connections to others from marginalized backgrounds can serve as an alternative narrative of what it means to live a good life (Syed and McLean, 2022).

Prioritizing family networks over individual success was more common among parents than youth in our sample. It could be that lower income parents are less optimistic about their own chances to move up in life and therefore they focus on intergenerational mobility for their children (Davidai and Wienk, 2021), as was reflected in stories of wanting to provide a better future for their children. Alternatively, the focus on family could reflect participants’ developmental stages, with parents seeing family as a source of deep connection, stability, and core to their identity, whereas youth are in the process of personal exploration and defining their identities, resulting in more individually focused future goals and aspirations.

Perceptions of personal and structural pathways to success

While youths’ and parents’ views around the ultimate purpose of education and careers differed in nuanced ways, their thoughts regarding the essential processes required to achieve success in these domains were similar. Consistent with past research (Clemente et al., 2017; Davidai and Wienk, 2021; Dickinson et al., 2023), participants strongly emphasized individual factors like hard work and personal effort as key to success. Parents explicitly stated and implicitly modeled the importance of hard work for their children through working multiple jobs, and via redemptive narratives like overcoming language and immigration barriers and returning to school as an adult. It appeared youth internalized these messages, as more than half also endorsed the importance of hard work in attaining success. However, while youth reiterated personal factors such as hard work and motivation as being important for success, their stories were far less detailed, and most did not include specific examples of times they had worked hard and what resulted from the hard work. This finding comports with a developmental perspective, considering that parents have more cumulative personal life experience to reflect on, whereas youth, particularly younger adolescents, are still developing the executive functioning and meta-cognitive skills necessary to reflect on the types of behaviors and values that are necessary to achieve their goals (Tervo-Clemmens et al., 2023; Sari et al., 2025).

A focus on hard work and personal responsibility is indicative of the American Dream mentality and shows how deeply ingrained these beliefs are in US society. While hard work and effort are reasonable and logical traits to support goal attainment, the master narrative overemphasizes meritocracy and individual effort, which can lead to deficit-based thinking (see Davis and Museus, 2019). Such thinking can end up suggesting that a lack of hard work and effort is the sole cause of failing to succeed. Often this results in narratives that place blame entirely on individuals, their families, or their communities while ignoring biases embedded within ecological contexts and institutions which contribute to disparities across groups (Stephens et al., 2014).

Youth may internalize messaging around the American Dream and meritocracy via two pathways, 1) through their parents, who have internalized American Dream messages and transmit these beliefs to their children and 2) through the American culture that conveys these messages via everyday interactions in institutional and social spaces. Prior literature has suggested that individuals from marginalized backgrounds may focus more on individual (versus external) pathways and barriers to success because doing so facilitates an internal locus of control (Godfrey and Wolf, 2016). An emphasis on personal over structural factors is also consistent with research on systems justification theory (Jost and van der Toorn, 2012), which describes a tendency to believe in the fairness of systems and to blame individuals, rather than structural barriers, for disadvantage. This sentiment was reflected in several participants’ narratives describing the importance of hard work and avoiding “laziness.” Research has shown both negative implications of system-justifying beliefs (i.e., validating economic disparities) and positive implications—via the promotion of individual agency (i.e., if I work hard enough, I can succeed). Thus, systems justification may provide a sense of hope for marginalized groups.

Structural factors and barriers to mobility were mentioned less by both youth and parents, and when they were discussed, they were often accompanied by individual-focused attributions. This dual view has been found in prior research with marginalized populations (Godfrey and Wolf, 2016). Of the one-third of participants who acknowledged structural barriers to mobility, the most common barriers mentioned were financial, such as the high cost of college and the social capital and networking privileges relayed through intergenerational wealth. These participants acknowledged intergenerational transmission of opportunity and how it is harder for those coming from less advantaged backgrounds to achieve success, in comparison to those who are born into greater privilege and already have a leg up. Neighborhood disadvantage and “bad influences” from within the neighborhood were also cited as structural barriers by both youth and parents, with more limited endorsement, solely among parents, of the role of racist and classist stigmas as obstacles.

Study strengths and limitations

This study explored the experiences of a sample of youth and parents from a public housing community in New England. While not generalizable to a broader context, the findings from this study likely are transferable to other public housing populations and low-income communities. Further, a major strength of our study is the diversity of our sample; participants were racially, ethnically, and linguistically diverse and covered ages of early adolescence through late adolescence, as well as adult parents. While many public housing developments are located in redeveloping and gentrifying neighborhoods (like the one in our study), others are in areas of concentrated poverty or less urban environments with limited access to institutional resources. The broader neighborhood in which a public housing development is situated may play an important role in how residents define success and think about their own opportunities for social mobility. Thus, further research is warranted exploring the concepts of success and social mobility in other public housing neighborhoods with different macro-level contexts.

It is important to note that the qualitative interview protocol covered a broad range of topics including neighborhood perceptions, housing issues, family, work and school life, as well as health and well-being, and was not focused solely on beliefs around social mobility. Future studies could explore in greater depth the concept of social mobility in understudied populations (like public housing residents) and build on these initial findings which highlight the nuances between traditional and alternative views of success and person-oriented processes versus structural factors that support and/or impede social mobility.

In addition, the interview protocol was not specifically designed to inquire about parent socialization around social mobility beliefs and definitions of success or to conduct dyadic analyses. Rather, we extrapolated from parent narratives both implicit and explicit messaging about social class and mobility and compared and contrasted parents’ versus youths’ views. Future studies should leverage dyadic designs to more adequately explore view congruence and specific socialization practices.

Finally, our wide age range for youth (12 to 24) was chosen based on the developmental significance of adolescence at a time when social mobility beliefs are thought to be uniquely influential and to facilitate an initial exploration of developmental differences in definitions of success and mobility. We found some differences between older versus younger youth’s definitions of success, but future studies employing longitudinal methods will be better able to identify how beliefs about mobility shift over time in response to changes in social cognition and executive function, contextual factors, and experiences.

Policy implications

The results of this study have implications for policies that aim to support social mobility and well-being among low-income communities. Findings highlight that while traditional views of success are dominant, many parents and youth also endorse alternative definitions of success that are not tied to socioeconomic status motives. Understanding various views of success is important for designing appropriate social mobility focused programs. For example, for some residents in the community, educational and career initiatives to help reach socioeconomic goals were desired and viewed as beneficial; for others there was a much stronger emphasis on family and improving the community one grew up in. These different viewpoints could inform the types of careers emphasized by programs such as the Jobs Plus Initiative, which aims to increase earnings and employment among public housing residents (MDRC, n.d.). Housing authorities could also leverage community members’ interest in collective (versus individual) values by providing multiple ways for residents to be involved in community governance.

Conclusion

This study furthers understanding of the psychological and developmental mechanisms underpinning beliefs about mobility and success. Participants in our sample commonly identified personal attributes like hard work and effort indicative of the master narrative of the American Dream, with fewer mentioning structural attributions associated with success or lack thereof. These views may stem from psychological processes to protect one’s sense of agency and justify the system rather than challenge the status quo. Exploring the transmission of these views between parents and youth will be a valuable next step for research and potential intervention.
