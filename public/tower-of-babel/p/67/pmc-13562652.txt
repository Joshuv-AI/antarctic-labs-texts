Design and evaluation of an intent-based web of things query framework for smart device discovery
Authors: Salman Tahir, Najia Saher, Muhammad Rehan Faheem, Muhammad Nabeel Asghar, Abid Iqbal, Saad Arif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid growth of Internet of Things (IoT) deployments has made smart device discovery difficult in heterogeneous environments where devices differ in type, location, operational status, metadata structure, and communication interface. Conventional IoT search approaches often rely on keyword matching, static indexing, or predefined metadata fields, which limit their ability to interpret user intent in Web of Things (WoT) environments. This paper presents an intent-based WoT query framework for smart device discovery. The framework converts natural-language queries into structured search constraints by extracting device type, operational status, and geographic location. The prototype uses Flask, TextBlob for lightweight preprocessing and tokenization, Google Maps Geocoding API, and Apache Solr, with modular components for user interaction, robust intent extraction, geospatial resolution, query construction, retrieval, and multi-factor ranking. The evaluation was extended using a 380-query intent-robustness benchmark, a 10,000-record enriched WoT-like metadata corpus with controlled noise conditions, multi-factor ranking ablation, stronger lexical and semantic baselines, and external standards-backed validation on combined W3C Thing Description 1.1-style and OGC SensorThings-style metadata. Results show overall intent-extraction accuracy of 0.968, normalized noisy-metadata retrieval F1 of 0.978, NDCG@10 of 0.950 for full multi-factor ranking versus 0.446 for timestamp-only sorting, and clear superiority over keyword, BM25, fuzzy lexical, and TF-IDF baselines. External validation reached query success rate 0.946 and NDCG@10 0.905 on 55 labelled queries over a reproducible standards-backed corpus. Remaining limitations include spelling-error sensitivity, synthetic data constraints, the modest size of the external standards corpus, and the need to integrate the full ranking module into the live Flask/Solr interface.

Introduction

The Internet of Things (IoT) has emerged as a key enabling technology for smart cities, health systems, industrial automation, environmental monitoring, transportation, agriculture, and smart buildings. The physical world is being increasingly linked to digital services via sensors, actuators, gateways, cloud services, and edge devices, and is becoming accessible for monitoring, control, and decision support. Recent research on the WoT and IoT systems reveals a growing and diversified array of connected devices, generating huge amounts of data on the state, context, location, and conditions surrounding them1,2.

This same growth also poses a practical discovery problem. Different IoT devices are usually installed on different platforms, communication protocols, formats of data, descriptions of metadata, and application areas. Data from a temperature sensor, an air-quality monitor, a motion sensor, or a humidity sensor can be exposed in a variety of ways, with various naming schemes, and in various models of description. This fragmentation makes it harder to identify the right device or data source to meet the requirements for a particular operation. In this regard, interoperability is one of the major issues that arises throughout IoT research, ranging from device to network, syntactic to semantic, and platform-level interoperability3,4. Semantic interoperability is especially significant as it enables the interpretation of data and device descriptions based on meaning rather than format.

The Web of Things addresses part of this problem by applying web principles to IoT devices. The integration of physical devices in the web, called “Networked Things” (NWT), is described in Fig. 1 by 4 layers: Access (connectivity), Find (discovery), Share (security), and Compose (application mashups)1. The model is based on standard web protocols that supports interoperability and scalability across heterogeneous IoT devices. It is difficult, however, to discover when users state their needs in natural language, rather than in a set of pre-designed query fields. The current discovery methods for IoT and WoT are mostly based on keyword matching, static indexing, semantic annotations, or metadata matching2,5,6. Though these techniques are helpful, they do not necessarily reflect the intent of the user’s query. For instance, a user may only require a list of all temperature sensors, rather than wanting the list of sensors within a monitored area that are currently active, or of those currently operating in the city that are faulty.Fig. 1Conceptual Web of Things (WoT) architecture stack showing how physical networked things are exposed through access, discovery, sharing, and composition layers.

Context-aware IoT search systems attempt to improve this situation by considering contextual attributes such as user location, device availability, operational state, and environmental conditions7,8. In healthcare, context-aware monitoring can help locate relevant patient-monitoring devices or adapt retrieval according to patient state8. In smart-city scenarios, such systems can support search over traffic, parking, air-quality, weather, and environmental sensing devices, where the relevance of a result depends not only on the device type but also on its location, status, and recency9. Earlier work on matching objects to user queries in WoT applications also shows the value of aligning device descriptions with user requirements, although dynamic and scalable discovery remains challenging10.

Another challenge is the security and privacy issues involved in discovering IoT entities. Therefore, the challenge for the public IoT search engines is to ensure they can be found while also providing responsible access, privacy protection, and secure handling of device information11,12. While the current effort does not deploy a full security layer, such issues argue for a structured and controlled context-rich discovery process, rather than the unchecked exposure of devices.

Although significant advances have been made in IoT search engines, WoT discovery, and IoT semantic descriptions, an important gap still exists. The present systems lack a joint support for lightweight intent extraction in natural language, operational state filtering, geospatial query formation, timestamp-aware ranking, and a reproducible software architecture for the smart device discovery. Searching for keywords can return devices that have the same keywords, but are not functioning devices. If recency is not a part of the criteria, static indexing can retrieve older records. Semantic discovery approaches offer a more detailed description in a machine-readable format, but they can be more complex and demanding in terms of the amount of metadata, the maintenance of ontologies, and reasoning mechanisms, which may not be feasible in lightweight and rapidly deployed IoT environments.

To overcome the aforementioned limitation, this paper introduces a new query model for discovering smart devices based on intention. A query in natural language is treated as a conjunction of structured query elements in the proposed framework. Three intent elements are used in this study: device type, operational state, and geographic location. The above elements have been chosen since they are common and practically useful search dimensions in smart city and environmental sensing applications. Device type represents the category of requested object, operational status specifies whether the device is active, inactive or faulty, and location places a restriction on the search in a relevant geographic area. The intent extracted is then transformed into structured queries using the Apache Solr. A structured query using the Apache Solr is then generated for the extracted intent for indexing, retrieval, and ranking.

The solution is realized in a modular way as a software prototype, with the Web application part built using Flask, TextBlob used only for preprocessing and tokenization, Google Maps Geocoding API for resolving location, and an indexing and retrieval component built using Apache Solr. The evaluation combines controlled benchmarksincluding a 380-query intent-robustness set, enriched WoT-like metadata with noise conditions, ranking ablation, stronger lexical and semantic baselines, and external standards-backed W3C TD/SensorThings validationwith prototype-level indexing, latency, and cache measurements.

The main contributions of this study are as follows:An intent-based WoT query framework which translates natural language query requests from user to structured search constraints for discovery of smart devices.A modular software architecture that combines query processing, intent extraction, geospatial resolution, Solr-based indexing, retrieval, and result ranking.A powerful lightweight intent extraction technique that supports synonym, phrase, fuzzy, multi-intent and ambiguity handling to extract device type, operational status and location from user queries and generate executable Solr queries.An extended experimental analysis using a 380-query benchmark, enriched WoT-like metadata with noise evaluation, multi-factor ranking ablation, stronger baseline comparison, and external standards-backed validation, together with prototype-level indexing and latency measurements.A discussion of the benefits, limitations, assumptions and future extensions of the framework, such as real-time stream ingestion, semantic query interpretation, adaptive ranking, and validation on real-world IoT deployments.The experimental scenario in this paper is the smart city sensing, which is equipped with all the natural tools and requirements of distributed devices, geospatial search needs, dynamic operational states, and heterogeneous sensor categories. However, the proposed framework could be applicable to any smart city. The same design could be applied to other scenarios where devices need to be located by intent, such as healthcare monitoring, industrial IoT, agricultural sensor networks, smart buildings, and emergency response networks.

Related work

Initial research efforts in discovery for IoT and WoT focused on sensor search, middleware interoperability, and description of devices, while more recent studies shifted to semantic sensor discovery, context-aware discovery, scalable IoT search engines, and intelligent ranking. In this section, the major research streams related to the proposed framework are reviewed, and a gap is identified to be addressed by this study.

IoT and web of things device discovery

The WoT extends IoT systems by exposing connected devices through web-based principles, standard interfaces, and machine-readable descriptions. This enables easier access to, discovery of, composition of, and integration of the devices in heterogeneous environments. Initial WoT research showed how physical devices could be described as web resources and linked together to create applications using web technologies, and subsequent research concentrated on discovery, description, composition, interoperability, and deployment patterns1,13.

Findability also has been identified as a central challenge in WoT environments. Faheem et al.14 presented a taxonomy of WoT findability and key challenges of finding Things in diverse IoT-based environments over the Web. While their work offers a significant conceptual basis for discovery within WoT systems, it does not use a natural-language query framework based on intents for translating a user request into a structured search constraint with the device.

Sciullo et al.1 provided a general overview of the WoT field, including architectural paradigms, enabling technologies, discovery mechanisms, and open challenges. Among the other findings of their review is the need for standardization, interoperability, security, and scalability in WoT-based systems. As a survey, however, it does not offer empirical implementation of the intent-aware discovery mechanism. Sciullo et al.15 proposed WoT store as a prototype platform for discovering and managing Things and applications that are compliant with the W3C. It serves as an illustration of the usefulness of semantic metadata for discovering WoT and does not thoroughly measure query latency, ranking quality, or scalability under different query scenarios.

The integration of WoT with with edge computing has also received attention. These paradigms, namely edge, cloudlet, multi-access edge computing, and others, are analyzed by Anees et al.16 with respect to their ability to decrease latency and increase data locality in WoT applications. While their study helps in understanding deployment-level issues, it does not propose an actual query-processing model to transform the user’s intent into executable constraints on device searches. In a similar way, conceptual modeling and semantic-ontology approaches have been suggested to enhance the representation and reasoning of IoT systems17,18. These approaches enhance machine interpretability but may be burdensome in terms of the depth of semantic descriptions, and can make lightweight deployments more complex in terms of modeling.

Previous work that involves WoT object matching or the discovery of sensors still continues to be a core piece of work. Xu et al.10 expressed Things and requirements of users as property-action graphs and semantic labels, and demonstrated that the richness of the matching will provide better retrieval quality than a keyword search. CASSARAM, a context-aware sensor search, selection and ranking model based on semantic querying and weighted ranking was proposed by Perera et al.19. While these studies highlight the importance of context and semantic matching, they also rely on relatively rich metadata and are not fully adequate for practical smart devices discovery with lightweight natural-language intent extraction.

IoT search engines and scalable discovery

The vision of IoT search engines is to search, find, and retrieve connected devices and their metadata from distributed environments. Liang et al.5 talked about some of the lessons that can be learned from the traditional web search for IoT search, such as crawling, indexing, ranking, and resource discovery. While their work offers a conceptual foundation, it doesn’t apply or test an intent-based query pipeline. Pattar et al.2 surveyed the techniques used in searching IoT resources, and categorized the available techniques into keyword-based, semantic, and context-aware techniques. The survey highlights the capabilities that are important for next-generation IoT search engines, such as scalability, heterogeneity and context awareness, but lacks a working prototype and a recent empirical benchmarking.

Later generation IoT search-engine architectures have tackled scalability and intelligent query management. Hatcher et al.6 introduced an intelligent and efficient IoT search-engine architecture that comprises names, indexes and a pre-emptive caching solution based on LSTM model for predicting query volume. Gao et al.7 studied the scalability issues and presented a cluster-based search system, which distributes query load according to the Lowest Utilization First approach. These studies are valuable to large-scale search infrastructure, but have a narrower focus on interpreting user queries in natural language and converting the intent of the search to structured device filters.

In addition, indexing strategies can enhance the findability of Things in WoT environments. Faheem et al.20 suggested an indexing approach to finding the indoor Things in a WoT application and emphasized the importance of structured indexing for efficient search of device in WoT. Their work involves indexing and indoor location of Things, whereas the current study is focused on translating natural language user intent to structured search constraints that can be performed over an indexed backend.

IoTCrawler21 provides a system-of-systems architecture for crawling, indexing, and searching heterogeneous IoT data sources. It helps in the actual discovery of IoT by providing a layered discovery architecture that merges multiple discovery tools. However, the primary emphasis is on broad-scale crawling and indexing, not lightweight device type, status, and location user-intent extraction. Vulnerability oriented IoT search engines like I-RECON22 show that internet facing IoT services can be discovered via scanning and protocol analysis. Although relevant to the larger IoT search picture, these systems aim to discover vulnerabilities, not smart devices, but from a user’s point of view.

Security is also a crucial issue in IoT search. Hatcher et al.12 have conducted a survey of security issues in IoT search engines, such as malformed queries, data manipulation, denial of service, privacy leakage, adversarial machine learning, etc. Zhao et al.11 demonstrated that the deployed IoT devices still have practical security issues in terms of firmware, protocols, and maintenance. The findings of these studies suggest the importance of carefully designed device discovery: revealing device metadata can lead to security and privacy concerns. The present work does not implement a complete security layer, but it treats structured and controlled device discovery as a step towards more accountable search interfaces.

Semantic, context-aware, and metadata-based IoT search

Semantic and context-aware approaches are attempts to achieve a better IoT discovery that goes beyond basic keyword matching. Elsaleh et al.23 proposed IoT-Stream, a lightweight ontology to annotate IoT data streams with temporal, spatial, and contextual information. Rhayem et al.4 discussed the semantic web technologies for IoT and emphasized on the need for ontology alignment, semantic annotation, and dynamic context management. From the works, it is found that semantic description can enhance the interoperability of the system, but some problems of metadata quality and ontology maintenance, reason overhead, and scalability can also be observed. Anees et al.16 reviewed the integration of WoT and edge computing, showing how edge-based architectures can reduce latency and improve data locality in smart applications. However, their work focuses on architectural and deployment-level issues rather than intent-based natural-language query processing for smart device discovery.

Context-aware IoT search has also been explored in domain-specific environments. Rhayem et al.8 proposed a semantic-enabled and context-aware monitoring system for the Internet of Medical Things, combining ontology-based modeling with context-sensitive monitoring. Their work demonstrates the usefulness of semantic context in healthcare-oriented IoT environments, but it relies on a specialized infrastructure and is not designed as a general-purpose natural-language device discovery framework.

Metadata-based prioritization and quality-aware discovery are especially relevant to the present study. Kiourtis et al.24 proposed prioritizing IoT healthcare data using attribute scoring and metadata annotation, showing how metadata can help distinguish more critical or useful device data. Mavrogiorgou et al.25 addressed the reliability of unknown IoT devices, which is relevant when discovery systems retrieve devices whose trustworthiness, availability, or quality is uncertain. Loulaki et al.26 talked about complex scenarios for complex data integration and harmonization, thus raising the general issue of how to harmonize and integrate heterogeneous data sources. These studies help explain how quality control and bias can affect IoT search results, since retrieved devices may differ in metadata completeness, reliability, freshness, and contextual relevance. The framework proposed to solve part of this problem includes operational status, location, and time stamp in query formation and ranking, but for the time being, it is limited to these elements only and cannot be extended to reliability scoring or advanced data harmonization.

Machine learning and intelligent query processing in IoT

Machine learning has been widely explored in IoT for anomaly detection, localization, prediction, classification, and resource optimization. Khan et al.27 combined Cuckoo Search with support vector machines for indoor localization. Panda et al.28 used feature-selection methods for IoT botnet detection. Thakur et al.29 reviewed deep-learning approaches for IoT applications, including anomaly detection, predictive maintenance, and optimization. These studies show that learning-based methods can improve prediction and classification in IoT environments, but their main focus is not on user-facing device discovery.

For IoT search, machine learning can support query expansion, semantic matching, ranking, caching, and adaptive retrieval. Hatcher et al.6, for example, incorporated LSTM-based query-volume prediction to improve search efficiency through pre-emptive caching. However, learning-based methods often require large query logs, labeled training data, and computational resources that may not be available in lightweight prototypes or early-stage deployments. For this reason, the present work adopts a lightweight intent-extraction approach based on tokenization and domain vocabularies. This design is simpler than transformer-based semantic parsing, but it is interpretable, reproducible, and suitable for evaluating the basic value of intent-aware query construction before introducing heavier models.

Summary of research gaps

The reviewed literature shows that IoT and WoT discovery has been studied from several directions, including semantic descriptions, context-aware search, scalable indexing, IoT search-engine architectures, security, and machine-learning-based optimization. However, several gaps remain. First, many IoT search systems focus on indexing, crawling, or metadata discovery rather than interpreting natural-language user intent. Second, semantic approaches often require rich ontologies and complete metadata, which may not be available in lightweight or heterogeneous deployments. Third, previous research rarely integrates device type, device operation, location, and recency into a common query processing pipeline. Fourth, software architecture descriptions are not always adequate to replicate the implemented solution, particularly if it is a working search prototype. Methodology

This study employed an applied design and evaluation approach. The goal is to develop, prototype, and test a solution that enables intent-based smart device discovery in a WoT context. The methodology can be divided into five stages: requirement identification, software architecture design, prototype implementation, dataset preparation, and experimental evaluation. The choice of this structure was made due to the fact that the main result of the work, besides the search formula, is a working software framework that transforms the user’s query, which is entered in natural language, into a structured device search constraint.

The proposed framework is designed around a common search scenario in smart cities and environmental sensing systems. A user submits a natural-language query, such as “active air quality sensors in Lahore” or “faulty motion sensors near Karachi”. The system then extracts the intended device type, operational status, and location, converts these elements into structured filters, executes the query over an indexed device repository, and returns ranked device records. The workflow is intentionally lightweight so that the design can be reproduced and extended without requiring a full ontology-based reasoning stack or large-scale training data.

Research design

The research design combines prototype development with controlled experimental evaluation. First, the functional requirements of an intent-based WoT device discovery system were identified from the limitations of keyword-based IoT search and context-aware retrieval discussed in the previous sections. Second, a modular software architecture was designed to separate user interaction, query processing, geospatial resolution, indexing, retrieval, and ranking. Third, a prototype was implemented using widely available software tools. Finally, the prototype was evaluated using a synthetic but realistic IoT device dataset and a controlled set of user queries.

The study uses a synthetic dataset rather than a live IoT deployment because the primary goal is to evaluate the behavior of the proposed query-processing pipeline under controlled conditions. This allows device type, status, location, and timestamp attributes to be varied systematically. However, the use of synthetic data is also a limitation, because it cannot fully capture the incompleteness, noise, failures, metadata inconsistency, and temporal variability of real-world IoT deployments. This limitation is discussed further in Section Discussion and Limitations.

System architecture

The proposed framework is described using complementary architectural views similar to software architecture description for original software publications to elucidate the structure, runtime behavior, and data objects of the framework30.

This paper distinguishes between two architectural views. The conceptual WoT architecture stack in Fig. 1 illustrates the steps to expose, discover, share, and compose physical networked things via a web-based approach. The figure serves as an introduction to the study concept. Figure 2, on the other hand, shows the architecture of the implemented proposed intent-based WoT query framework. The presentation layer receives natural-language user queries through the Flask interface. The application layer coordinates query control, intent extraction, geocoding and caching, Solr query construction, and result ranking. The data layer uses Apache Solr to store and retrieve indexed IoT device records before the ranked results are returned to the user.Fig. 2Proposed architecture of the intent-based WoT query framework.

Proposed architecture of the intent-based WoT query framework.

The implemented architecture follows a modular layered design composed of the presentation layer, application layer, and data layer. The presentation layer provides the Flask-based web interface through which users submit natural-language queries and view search results. The application layer contains the main processing components, including the query controller, intent extraction module, geocoding and cache module, Solr query builder, and ranking/result formatting module. The data layer contains the Apache Solr index, where IoT device records are stored and retrieved using structured field-based and geospatial filters.

The architecture can also be described through three software views.

Logical view The logical view specifies the primary software components and what they do. The query is passed to the user interface. It is passed by the query controller to the intent extraction module. The intent extraction module recognizes the type of device, its operational status, and location. The geocoding and cache module is responsible for converting location names to coordinates. The intent is extracted to be passed to the Solr query builder to create search filters that can be executed. The search backend is used to fetch matching records from Apache Solr. The ranking and result formatting module sorts the results and formats them for display.

Runtime view This view contains information about the runtime sequence. A user enters a query into the Flask user interface. The query is standardized and broken down into terms. The tokens are compared to the pre-defined dictionary for the device type, device status, and the city names. The city is found, and its coordinates are either obtained from the local cache or from the Google Maps Geocoding API. These intent elements are then converted into a Solr query. The application ranks the retrieved device records by their time and returns the top N records to the user. The application collects the records of matching devices from Apache Solr, ranks the records by their time, and returns the top N devices to the user.

Data view The data view defines the main data objects processed by the system. These encompass the original user query, an intent object with structured intent, geospatial coordinates, Solr query filters, indexed device documents, retrieved records, ranking scores, and evaluation labels. This perspective is crucial for reproducibility as it helps understand how the user’s unstructured input is transformed into machine-actionable search constraints.

Architecture and evaluation boundary conditions

The architectural views are accompanied by specific evaluation boundary conditions to support the reproducibility of the prototype and to prevent overgeneralization of the prototype results. The logical view specifies the actual software elements and what they do. The runtime view is the view of the processes that are executed from the user’s query until the ranked results are returned. The data view shows the primary objects that are passed through the system, such as the raw query, the normalized tokens, the structured intent object, Solr filters, device documents being indexed, records retrieved, ranking scores, and evaluation labels. These views give an idea of what was developed and what was tested in the prototype used in this study.

The assessment is a blend of controlled benchmark tasks and prototype-level assessment measures. The intent extractor is robust and rule- and vocabulary-guided, meaning that no train/test split was needed for training the model. The primary controlled evidence is provided by the 380-query benchmark, metadata-noise evaluation, ranking ablation, baseline comparison, and external standards-backed validation, with confusion matrices and latency measurements as n prototype diagnostics. Query processing workflow

Figure 3 depicts the query processing process. The system converts a natural language query into a structured intent object, processes location data, generates Solr filters, fetches device records that match the intent, and returns ranked results. The workflow begins with a user request for a natural language query. The query is normalized by first converting it to lowercase and stripping the query of any extraneous punctuation. This is then broken down into individual words. Each token is checked against a set of pre-defined device and operational type dictionaries and a set of supported city names for each device. If a match is found, the element of the structured intent object is filled.Fig. 3Workflow of the proposed intent-based query processing pipeline.

Workflow of the proposed intent-based query processing pipeline.

If the query includes a location, the system determines if the location is present in the local cache. Cached coordinates are used if available. If not, the Google Maps Geocoding API is invoked to get the latitude/longitude. The coordinates are then used to create a geographic bounding box that encloses the target location. Lastly, an intent object and a geospatial constraint are converted to a Solr query, and then the query is passed to a device repository that has been indexed.

Intent extraction model

The intent extraction module transforms an unstructured query into a structured representation. The intent object contains three fields:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} I = \{D, S, L\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where D denotes device type, S denotes operational status, and L denotes location. A user query is represented as a sequence of tokens:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Q = \{w_1, w_2, \ldots , w_n\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The extraction function maps the query tokens to the structured intent object:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} I = f(Q) = \{D, S, L\} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}TextBlob is used only for lightweight preprocessing and tokenization; semantic interpretation is handled by subsequent structured extraction layers rather than by TextBlob itself. Ranking strategy

The implemented ranking model combines three relevance dimensions: recency, operational status, and location proximity. For a retrieved device \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_i$$\end{document}, the ranking score is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R(d_i) = \alpha T_i + \beta S_i + \gamma L_i \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_i$$\end{document} is the normalized timestamp recency score, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_i$$\end{document} is the status relevance score, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_i$$\end{document} is the location proximity score computed using Haversine distance between the query location and the device coordinates, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1$$\end{document}. In the revised experimental ranking module, the default weights are α = 0.4, β = 0.3, and γ = 0.3. Status relevance increases when a retrieved device matches the requested operational state, and location proximity increases as geographic distance decreases.

The original Flask/Solr prototype can still sort results by timestamp for compatibility, but the controlled ranking ablation reported in this paper evaluates the full multi-factor model. Timestamp-only ranking corresponds to α = 1, β = 0, and γ = 0, and serves as the baseline against which status-aware and location-aware ranking variants are compared.

Experimental setup and evaluation configuration.

The experimental setup consists of a lightweight web application, query-processing module, geolocation service, and a search backend based on Solr, as illustrated in Table 6. To ensure consistency across intent extraction, retrieval, ranking, and baseline comparison experiments, the same setup was adopted for all the evaluation tasks.

Experimental evaluation protocol

The evaluation was organized into controlled benchmark tasks and prototype-level performance measurements. The controlled tasks were: (1) intent robustness on a 380-query labelled benchmark; (2) metadata-noise evaluation on clean, noisy unnormalized, and noisy normalized enriched metadata; (3) ranking ablation on 160 labelled queries comparing timestamp-only and multi-factor ranking; (4) baseline comparison on 184 labelled queries against keyword, BM25, field-boosted lexical, fuzzy lexical, TF-IDF, and proposed intent-based methods; and (5) external standards-backed validation on 55 labelled queries over a combined W3C TD 1.1 and OGC SensorThings corpus31,32.

The 380-query intent benchmark was generated under controlled category quotas: explicit (60), synonym (60), spelling (60), paraphrase (60), ambiguous (40), multi-intent (60), and unsupported (40). Each query was manually annotated for expected device type, status, and location labels, including unsupported cases that should not map to known labels. Query generation followed template-based expansion with manual review to ensure category balance and label consistency.

Prototype-level measurements were kept for indexing scalability, retrieval latency, and cache effectiveness. These experiments are reported separately from the benchmark tasks, as they do not form a large-scale user-query benchmark, but are observed under controlled conditions. The performance of indexing was evaluated for 1,000 to 10,000 records, the latency for retrieval was evaluated for simple, status-based, location-based, timestamp-related, and complex queries, and the effectiveness of the cache was evaluated for repeated location resolution.

Intent robustness evaluation Extracted device type, status, and location values were compared against manually defined expected labels. Metrics included full-query accuracy, macro-F1, and per-field F1 for device, status, and location extraction. Unsupported-query accuracy and false-positive rates for unsupported device and location terms were also reported.

Metadata-noise evaluation Retrieval was evaluated under clean enriched metadata, noisy unnormalized metadata, and noisy normalized metadata using success rate, precision, recall, and F1-score.

Ranking ablation Ranking variants were compared using Precision@10, NDCG@10, MRR, and average relevance@10. Because Precision@10 and MRR were saturated across methods in the controlled setting, NDCG@10 and average relevance@10 were emphasized as the primary discriminative metrics.

Baseline comparison The proposed intent filter and proposed intent + full ranking methods were compared against keyword search, field BM25, field-boosted lexical, fuzzy lexical, and semantic TF-IDF. Recall@10 and F1@10 were not emphasized because many queries match thousands of relevant records in the 10,000-record corpus while only the top 10 results are evaluated.

External standards-backed validation External standards-backed validation was performed using a combined WoT/SensorThings metadata corpus. The corpus includes W3C Thing Description 1.1-style records and OGC SensorThings-style records normalized into the same canonical schema used by the framework. Raw FROST demo entity JSON was cached for reproducibility; live SensorThings endpoints were attempted but were not used because stable responses were unavailable during collection. Validation reported query success rate, Precision@10, NDCG@10, MRR, source coverage, and unsupported-query behavior on the external corpus.

Controlled benchmark results cover intent robustness, metadata-noise robustness, ranking ablation, baseline comparison, and external standards-backed validation. Prototype-level measurements for indexing, retrieval latency, and cache effectiveness are reported separately.

Indexing performance

The second experiment is the time taken to index IoT device records into Apache Solr. The indexing time for various repository sizes is shown in Fig. 7. The number of indexed records varied from 1,000 to 10,000 device records. The indexing time for 1,000 records was around 1.8 seconds and for 10,000 records, it was around 11.2 seconds. As the size of the data set increases, so does the indexing time. This is a normal behavior as larger repositories need more parsing, field construction and indexing operations to be able to retrieve them.

The result shows that the prototype is able to index small to medium-sized synthetic IoT repositories within a reasonable offline preparation time. This is not to be taken as an indication of production scale indexing performance. In real deployments, incremental indexing, streaming updates and repeated measurements under various hardware and workload conditions would be required.Fig. 7Indexing time for increasing numbers of IoT device records. In the prototype run observed, the indexing time rose from about 1.8 seconds for 1, 000 records to about 11.2 seconds for 10, 000 records. This means that the more devices there are in the repository, the more expensive the indexing process will be. This cost is not as important as the interactive retrieval latency, because indexing is typically done before a query is executed or as part of periodic updates. For larger deployments, however, incremental indexing or streaming update mechanisms would be needed.

Retrieval latency

The third experiment is a comparison of the latency of query retrieval for different query complexities. The number of intent elements in the query was used to define the query complexity. Simple queries have only one intent element, like device type. Moderate queries have two components, like device type and status. There are three components to a complex query: device type, status, and location.

The retrieval latency results show that the system is able to provide interactive search in the evaluated prototype setup. More optimization would be needed for deployment in a larger or real-time environment, including having persistent Solr connections, caching queries, incremental indexing, asynchronous API requests, and more efficient spatial filtering.

Figure 8 displays the observed query latency for different types of queries. The simple queries took approximately 0.52 seconds, the status-only queries took approximately 0.74 seconds, and the location-based queries took approximately 0.81 seconds. Time taken for timestamp based queries was approximately 0.93 seconds and complex queries with multiple constraints were approximately 1.28 seconds.

The results show that the query latency increases with the number of constraints and processing steps in the query. Location based queries need to be geocoded or retrieved from the cache, and complex queries need to have multiple field filters that are combined before they can be executed. The results validate the architectural choice to split intent extraction, geocoding/cache handling, Solr query construction, and ranking into separate components.Fig. 8Observed query latency by query type. Simple queries show the lowest latency, while complex queries involving multiple constraints require higher response time because more query-processing and filtering steps are applied.

Observed query latency by query type. Simple queries show the lowest latency, while complex queries involving multiple constraints require higher response time because more query-processing and filtering steps are applied.

The range should be understood as prototype evidence, not as a performance indicator for the city. For this implementation, location-based queries include location detection, potential cache hit, geocode, bounding-box construction and execution of Solr filters. Thus, the differences in response time can be due to the cache state, distribution of the candidate records, and the overhead of query processing. Only a representative subset of supported cities is displayed, future work should test all supported cities with repeated runs and report variance.

Cache effectiveness

The response time for location related operations is compared in Fig. 9 with and without caching. With cached execution, latency is always lower for all operations tested. For instance, location search time drops from around 1.36 seconds without cache to 0.61 seconds with cache, repeated queries drop from around 1.12 seconds to 0.48 seconds, coordinate lookup drops from around 1.28 seconds to 0.52 seconds, and device retrieval drops from around 1.41 seconds to 0.67 seconds.

The results agree with the use of a geocoding/cache module in the proposed architecture. Location-based queries can be executed multiple times and if the same cities are involved in multiple queries, the coordinates can be cached to avoid repeated external geocoding calls and to save query-processing overhead. The comparison should be taken as a performance observation at the implementation level, and not as a complete ablation study, as repeated runs, confidence intervals, and hardware-specific measurements were not reported.

Discussion and limitations

The results show that the proposed intent-based WoT query framework can enhance smart device discovery by translating natural language user queries to structured search constraints. The framework not only matches keywords directly, but it also gets information about the device type, operational status, and location, and then it builds a query in Solr that can be executed. This way, it enhances the match between the query and the devices it returns, particularly when the query explicitly mentions the type of sensor, its status, and the city where it is located.

Interpretation of findings

The results of the controlled benchmark demonstrate that the revised intent extractor significantly outperforms the dictionary matching approach in handling queries. The 380-query benchmark achieved an overall accuracy of 0.968 and macro-F1 of 0.990, with perfect accuracy on the synonym, paraphrase, multi-intent, ambiguous, and unsupported categories. The most persistent problem is spelling, particularly for more difficult misspellings.

The retrieval performance on noisy enriched metadata was improved from F1 = 0.946 to F1 = 0.978, which shows the significance of schema harmonization when the metadata in the WoT variants td_v1, sensorthings, and legacy_csv differ. The full multi-factor ranking outperformed the timestamp-only ranking with NDCG@10 of 0.446 to 0.950, and the proposed intent-based methods outperformed the keyword, BM25, fuzzy lexical, and TF-IDF retrieval methods.

The external standards-backed validation corpus is more robust than the previous single-format sample, as it includes both W3C TD and OGC SensorThings structures in a single normalized schema. The small size (26 normalized records, 55 queries) and the lack of location metadata (76.9% missing location) make it difficult to draw conclusions about large-scale live WoT performance. Prototype-level indexing, latency and cache measurements are supportive but not part of the controlled benchmarks.

Advantages of the proposed framework

The main advantage of the proposed approach is that the natural language query issued by the user is mapped to the structured query of the IoT devices. Users need not select device type, device status, and position separately in the respective fields. Instead, they can provide the system with a request in very basic language, and the system will be able to obtain the appropriate search constraints.

The second advantage is modularity. The framework splits the UI, intent extraction, geocoding, Solr query construction, search backend, and ranking module. TextBlob is used only for preprocessing and tokenization, while synonym, phrase, fuzzy, and structured extraction layers handle semantic interpretation. The ranking module can evaluate the full multi-factor model independently of the Flask/Solr UI, which may still use timestamp sorting for compatibility.

The third advantage is that it is interpretable. The current intent extraction method depends on the explicit intent vocabulary, and the behavior of the system can be easily checked. This is helpful for troubleshooting and to see why a specific query yielded a specific result. Many operational IoT environments find this transparency useful as it helps system administrators to understand how the search constraints are being created.

The fourth advantage is deployability. The prototype uses widely used tools like Flask, Apache Solr, TextBlob, and Google Maps Geocoding API. Many of the developers are familiar with these technologies and can be utilized without any complex ontology stack, machine learning pipeline, etc.

Limitations

The existing system is advantageous, but several limitations remain important when interpreting the results.

First, although the enriched WoT-like dataset is more realistic than the original basic synthetic corpus, it is still synthetic and not a substitute for large-scale live WoT data. Real deployments can include unavailable statuses, incorrect coordinates, repeated device data, late timestamps, variant naming conventions, and varying device availability.

Second, spelling errors remain the main intent-extraction weakness. The 380-query benchmark reached only 0.800 full accuracy on the spelling category, with residual failures on harder misspellings such as moiton and air qality.

Third, the external standards-backed validation corpus is stronger than a single-format sample, but it remains modest in size and is not a live deployment benchmark. Missing or unknown status appeared in 38.5% of normalized records and missing location in 76.9%, so location-aware retrieval on standards metadata remains difficult.

Fourth, the Flask/Solr UI may still require integration with the full ranking module. The controlled ranking ablation evaluates the implemented multi-factor model, but the live prototype can still sort by timestamp for compatibility.

Fifth, transformer-based retrieval and ontology-aware semantic baselines were not evaluated because of dependency and runtime constraints in the current prototype environment.

Sixth, the current prototype does not include live IoT stream ingestion or security and privacy mechanisms. Access control, authentication, query authorization, and privacy-preserving search remain necessary before deployment in sensitive environments.

Seventh, location filtering in the Flask/Solr prototype still uses bounding-box approximation in some paths, although the ranking module uses Haversine-based proximity scoring in the controlled evaluation.

Assumptions

The framework assumes the following: It assumes that there is metadata about devices and that this metadata is structured and is sufficiently accurate to be indexed. In addition, it assumes that the attributes of the target discovery scenario have significance as device type, status, location, and timestamp. The current implementation is based on the assumption that a user query is explicit and has terms that are able to be matched to the supported vocabularies. It also assumes that the geocoding is of the city level, and that the more recent the device records are, the more useful they are for the experimental scenario.

These assumptions are valid for a controlled prototype but might not be true in every deployments. For instance, device information could be incomplete, device status might be out-of-date, and users might be vague about what they intend to do. Thus, in order to deploy it in such a practical situation, it is necessary to include more validation of metadata, support multiple languages, and have adaptive ranking.

Considerations for quality control and bias

Quality control of IoT discovery of devices is crucial since the results retrieved are heavily dependent on the quality of the metadata indexed. The search results will be impacted if the device records have been wrongly marked, the statuses are not up to date, the location is not precise, or the time stamp is inconsistent. This can pose a problem because the system may get the relevant devices, but use only partial or biased metadata.

One more source of bias is the use of synthetic data. Synthetic data can be used for controlled evaluation, but it can also have the disadvantage of representing only clean data and not real operational issues. In real IoT environments, various issues such as noisy device names, missing fields, communication failures, duplicate records, uneven geographic coverage, and inconsistent reporting intervals are present. A query benchmark based on a set of pre-defined terms can also advantage the proposed dictionary-based extraction method since the test queries can be based on the same words found in the system.

All these dangers should be mitigated in future evaluation by employing real device information, realistic query logs, noisy query variants, class imbalance analysis, and repeated measurements with varying loads. Also, the reliability of the devices should be taken into account, and the scores given to the unknown and poor-quality devices should not be as high as the scores given to the verified and active ones in a recent period.

Applicability to other domains

The experimental scenario is for smart city sensing, but the framework can be applied to other domains of IoT and WoT. The system could be used in healthcare and Internet of Medical Things systems to find active patient-monitoring devices, wearable sensors, or room medical equipment, while meeting privacy and access-control requirements. In industrial IoT, it could facilitate the detection of misbehaving machinery sensors, working vibration monitors, or temperature sensors in manufacturing spaces. In the agricultural sector, it could be applied to locate soil moisture sensors or weather sensors within certain fields or to identify irrigation devices. For smart buildings, it can be used for searching HVAC sensors, occupancy detectors, energy meters, and indoor air-quality devices.

This same design can also be used for emergency response and environmental monitoring. For instance, an active air quality sensor can be searched near a contaminated area, or a recent flood level sensor can be searched in a region, or a faulty environmental sensor can be searched in a region that needs maintenance. Examples display that the framework is not specific to any particular application domain, but that each domain would need its own vocabulary, metadata schema, access policy, and evaluation data set.

Lessons learned

The study offers a number of implications for the design of intent-based search systems for IoT and WoT. First of all, the lightweight intent extraction can help the device discovery of the structured search domain and the known main dimensions of the query. Second, the clarity of the architecture is crucial since the usefulness of the framework depends not only on the extraction algorithm but also on the interaction between software components. Thirdly, ranking should be considered as a different design issue. The filtering method returns matching devices, and the ranking method is used to rank the most relevant devices to the user. Fourth, although they can assist in the controlled evaluation, synthetic data is not a replacement for real-world validation, but should be used as a starting point.

The study also demonstrates that simple and easy-to-understand techniques can constitute a useful benchmark before the introduction of more complicated techniques. The dictionary-based approach is not a perfect solution to the natural-language WoT discovery problem, but it is useful to set up the process, determine the necessary data structures, and define evaluation metrics. This baseline can then be compared with models that are semantic parsers, transformer-based, conversational search interfaces, and adaptive ranking methods.

Beneficiaries and practical use

There are potentially multiple groups that can benefit from the proposed framework. The architecture could serve as a starting point for implementing intent-aware search in device management systems by IoT platform developers. The approach can be applied to locate relevant sensors more efficiently for smart-city operators for monitoring, maintenance, and planning activities. The framework can be reused by researchers exploring WoT discovery as a starting point to test more advanced query interpretation and ranking techniques. A user-friendly search user interface helps system administrators by streamlining the process of manually reading registries on devices. More natural access to device information is useful for end users when they are not familiar with the exact field names and/or metadata structure of the backend system.

The framework additionally offers a template for future dissemination via open-source release, technical documentation, reusable query benchmarks, demonstration videos, and integration into IoT/WoT teaching labs. The measures would help facilitate researchers and practitioners who wish to replicate, validate, or extend the proposed approach.

Future improvements

Future work should prioritize live public WoT validation, larger-scale real metadata corpora, integration of the full ranking module into the Flask/Solr interface, and transformer-based or ontology-aware baselines under controlled runtime budgets. The evaluation should also add formal significance testing, repeated runs, and multilingual query support. Real-time ingestion through MQTT, CoAP, and REST APIs, together with security and privacy controls, remains essential for operational deployment.

Conclusion and future work

This paper presented an intent-based Web of Things query framework for smart device discovery and evaluated it. The framework is not just keyword matching, but it is able to extract structured intent elements from natural-language user queries and generate executable Apache Solr queries. The updated implementation includes a powerful intent extractor that supports synonym, phrase, fuzzy, multi-intent, and ambiguity handling; a 380-query benchmark; a 10,000-record enhanced WoT-like metadata corpus with controlled noise evaluation and normalization; a multi-factor ranking model; and more comprehensive baseline comparisons with lexical, BM25, fuzzy, and TF-IDF approaches, along with external standards-backed validation on combined W3C TD 1.1 and OGC SensorThings metadata.

The controlled evaluation results indicate that the overall accuracy of intent extraction is 0.968, the normalized accuracy of noisy-metadata is 0.978, the NDCG@10 is 0.950 for full ranking and 0.446 for timestamp-only sorting, and the Precision@10 and NDCG@10 are above 0.992 for the proposed intent-based retrieval methods. Prototype-level indexing, latency, and cache measurements validate the system’s usability for interactive search in small to medium-sized repositories.

The framework can be used by IoT platform developers, system administrators, and researchers in the field of WoT discovery. The architecture can be reused by the developers as a starting point for intent-aware device search, and by the operators and administrators for monitoring and maintenance tasks to find relevant sensors. The framework can be extended to include semantic search, adaptive ranking, and natural language access to WoT resources.

There are still some limitations, such as spelling-error sensitivity, synthetic data constraints, the small size of the external standards corpus, the requirement for large-scale live WoT validation, the lack of transformer-based baselines, the lack of formal statistical significance testing, and the need to integrate the full ranking module into the live Flask/Solr interface. These gaps should be addressed in future work by using larger real-world corpora, operational deployment, real-time ingestion, and by carefully evaluating the semantic retrieval models.
