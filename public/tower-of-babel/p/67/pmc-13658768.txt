Development and validation of a multidimensional scale for AI tutoring acceptance in higher education
Authors: Zhuoran Zhang, Mehdinezhadnouri Katayoun, Lihuan Tan, Guannan Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) is being increasingly applied in higher education. This has given rise to the issue that, within the published literature, there is still a lack of widely established and psychometrically validated tools for assessing the actual perceived acceptance quality of AI tutoring systems in a comprehensive manner. Although previous studies have examined cognitive patterns in educational technology, few have adequately captured the multidimensional nature of learners’ experiences with AI tutoring systems. Existing evaluation models mainly emphasize cognitive outcomes, with less attention given to motivation, relational experience, and trust in AI-assisted teaching contexts. This study aimed to develop and validate a learner-centered scale of AI tutoring acceptance quality. The scale was constructed through literature review, expert consultation, and pre-testing, and comprised four theoretically grounded dimensions: Feedback Quality, Instructional Effectiveness, Interaction Experience, and User Trust. Data were collected from undergraduate students at five universities in Shanxi Province, China. ESEM, bifactor modeling, and reliability analysis indicated a robust bifactor structure, good internal consistency, and acceptable construct validity. The validated scale may serve as a practical tool for AI-supported educational design and future intervention research.Subject terms: Mathematics and computing, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) tutoring systems have become increasingly visible in higher education as tools that can support personalized guidance, provide immediate feedback, and assist students in managing complex learning tasks. Their growing use is especially notable in domains that require sustained practice and iterative improvement, such as vocal training, where learners often need timely feedback, adaptive support, and repeated opportunities for refinement1,2. In this sense, AI tutoring systems may perform certain instructional functions traditionally associated with human tutors, including explanation, feedback, and individualized guidance. Yet they are not equivalent to human teachers. Unlike human instructors, AI systems operate through computational processes rather than pedagogical judgment shaped by lived experience, contextual interpretation, and socio-emotional understanding. This distinction is important because it suggests that the value of AI tutoring should not be inferred solely from its technological capabilities, but should instead be examined through learners’ actual educational experiences.

For this reason, robust measurement is essential. The perceived acceptance quality of AI tutoring systems cannot be adequately understood through academic performance alone, nor can it be reduced to general indicators of technology adoption. In educational settings, students’ judgments about whether an AI tutor is helpful, pedagogically meaningful, responsive, and trustworthy may substantially shape their engagement with the system and the extent to which it functions as a credible source of learning support. A valid survey instrument is therefore necessary to capture these dimensions in a systematic manner. Such an instrument can provide a structured basis for comparing learner experiences across systems and contexts, while also strengthening the empirical basis for evaluating AI-supported teaching and learning.

Existing research on AI-supported learning has generated important evidence on learners’ acceptance, trust, engagement, usability, and perceived usefulness of educational AI tools3–5. At the same time, recent reviews suggest that evaluation criteria in this field remain conceptually dispersed, with studies often focusing on selected outcomes or isolated perceptions rather than assessing AI tutoring acceptance quality through an integrated, multidimensional framework6,7. In addition, although acceptance-oriented models such as TAM continue to inform much of the literature on educational technology, these models do not fully capture the instructional, interactional, and relational qualities that shape learners’ experiences with AI tutoring systems8,9. A more context-specific measurement tool is therefore needed to evaluate AI tutoring acceptance quality from a learner-centered perspective.

To address this gap, the present study develops and validates a multidimensional scale for assessing students’ perceptions of AI tutoring acceptance quality in higher education. The proposed instrument comprises four dimensions: Feedback Quality, referring to learners’ perceived evaluation of AI-generated feedback in terms of its timing, target, depth, clarity, accuracy, and usefulness for subsequent learning adjustment; Instructional Effectiveness, referring to learners’ perceptions of the extent to which AI tutoring supports understanding and skill development; Interaction Experience, referring to the responsiveness, clarity, and richness of the interaction between learner and system; and User Trust, referring to learners’ confidence in the reliability, competence, and educational intent of the AI tutor. Together, these dimensions are intended to capture central aspects of how learners experience AI tutoring beyond mere usability or acceptance.

The scale is grounded in an integrated theoretical framework that brings together several complementary perspectives. TAM helps explain how learners evaluate the usefulness of AI-supported instructional tools. Self-Determination Theory (SDT) highlights the importance of feedback and guidance in supporting learners’ needs for competence and autonomy10, thereby informing the conceptualization of instructional support and feedback quality. Media Richness Theory (MRT) offers a useful lens for understanding how immediacy, responsiveness, and communicative richness shape the perceived quality of interaction in AI-mediated tutoring. In addition, the Model of Organizational Trust11 provides a foundation for examining how perceptions of ability, reliability, and intention influence learners’ trust in autonomous systems. Rather than functioning as isolated theoretical references, these perspectives collectively support a broader understanding of AI tutoring acceptance quality that includes instructional, interactional, and relational dimensions.

Methodologically, this study adopts a multi-stage scale development procedure involving item generation, expert review, pilot testing, and psychometric validation using Exploratory Structural Equation Modeling (ESEM). The study pursues three objectives: first, to develop a theory-informed instrument for assessing AI tutoring acceptance quality; second, to evaluate its factorial validity and internal consistency; and third, to provide a measurement tool that may support future empirical research on AI-supported learning in higher education. The primary contribution of this study is the development of a theory-informed and psychometrically validated instrument for assessing students’ perceptions of AI tutoring acceptance quality. By providing a more context-sensitive measurement framework, the study aims to support future empirical research and more rigorous evaluation of AI-supported learning in higher education.

Artificial intelligence has become increasingly prominent in higher education, where it is no longer treated solely as a general digital tool, but increasingly as part of broader teaching, learning, and support processes3,12,13. Within this evolving landscape, AI Tutoring can be understood as a more pedagogically specific form of AI integration. Rather than merely assisting with information retrieval, writing support, or automation, AI Tutoring refers to AI-enabled systems that perform functions commonly associated with tutoring, such as providing guidance, delivering feedback, supporting practice, and structuring learner interaction in ways that contribute to learning processes1,14,15. This understanding is also consistent with recent work on intelligent tutoring systems, pedagogical AI conversational agents, and AI-enabled intelligent assistants, all of which highlight the growing role of adaptive, personalized, and instructional support in higher education1,2,15.

This distinction is necessary because AI Tutoring should not be treated as equivalent to general AI use in education. Recent reviews have shown that AI chatbots, generative AI tools, educational conversational agents, and intelligent tutoring systems are often discussed within overlapping research spaces, yet they are not conceptually identical4,6,7. A general-purpose AI tool may help students draft text, search for information, or complete routine tasks, whereas an AI Tutoring is expected to contribute more directly to learning by organizing feedback, guiding understanding, supporting skill development, and sustaining meaningful interaction during study15–17. In this sense, AI Tutoring is more appropriately conceptualized as a pedagogically meaningful form of AI-supported learning assistance than as a purely technical application.

Based on this conceptualization, the construct of AI tutoring acceptance quality also requires explicit clarification. In the present study, AI tutoring acceptance quality does not refer to a single external outcome such as examination scores, nor is it reducible to a general expression of satisfaction, usability, or technology acceptance. Instead, it refers to learners’ perceived evaluation of how effectively an AI Tutoring supports meaningful learning through useful feedback, instructional guidance, productive interaction, and dependable assistance. This clarification is important because the educational AI literature has tended to examine adjacent yet partial constructs rather than a unified effectiveness construct. More specifically, technology acceptance measures mainly assess whether learners perceive an AI system as useful, easy to use, and worth adopting. Satisfaction measures focus on learners’ overall affective or evaluative response after using a learning technology. Trust measures examine whether users regard AI as competent, reliable, transparent, or safe enough to rely on. Chatbot and conversational-agent measures usually emphasize interaction quality, usability, engagement, or communication experience. Intelligent tutoring system measures are closer to the present study because they address adaptive support, scaffolding, feedback, and learning assistance. Yet these traditions usually measure one part of the learner-AI tutoring experience rather than the integrated quality of AI tutoring as an instructional support process. Therefore, they provide important foundations but do not fully capture AI tutoring acceptance quality as conceptualized in this study.

For example, technology acceptance research typically focuses on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and related adoption beliefs8,9,18,19, while satisfaction-oriented studies emphasize broader evaluative responses to educational technology use20–22. Other work has examined perceived usability, interaction quality, or learner experience in digital environments23,24, yet these perspectives do not in themselves define what it means for AI Tutoring to be educationally effective.

The same issue applies to trust and chatbot-related research. Trust in AI has emerged as a critical issue in educational settings, especially where learners rely on AI-generated guidance, feedback, or recommendations5,25–28. Likewise, educational chatbot studies have examined interaction, engagement, and perceived benefits in increasingly sophisticated ways4,6,29,30. Even so, these lines of research generally treat trust, chatbot experience, or adoption as focal constructs in their own right. They do not by themselves provide a dedicated learner-centered framework for conceptualizing AI tutoring acceptance quality as a broader educational construct.

For this reason, AI tutoring acceptance quality is conceptualized here as a learner-perceived multidimensional construct. This position is consistent with construct development scholarship, which emphasizes that a new construct should be clearly bounded, theoretically specified, and represented through dimensions that are conceptually related yet not interchangeable31,32. In the present context, the educational value of AI Tutoring cannot be adequately captured by any single criterion alone. A system may be easy to use, but still provide weak instructional guidance. It may deliver rapid feedback, but it fails to establish the level of confidence required for learners to rely on its suggestions. It may appear engaging, yet offer limited educational value. For this reason, AI tutoring acceptance quality in this study is defined through four constitutive dimensions: Feedback Quality, Instructional Effectiveness, Interaction Experience, and User Trust.

This multidimensional definition also helps clarify the construct boundary of the present study. The proposed scale is not intended to measure downstream outcomes such as actual academic achievement, nor does it directly assess learner motivation, behavioral intention, or global satisfaction as independent end states. These variables remain important in the broader literature on AI and educational technology, but they are conceptually distinct from the present construct. The focus here is narrower and more specific: how learners perceived the acceptance quality of AI Tutoring itself as an instructional support process. In this respect, the proposed construct is designed to capture the pedagogical, interactional, and relational qualities of AI Tutoring that together shape whether learners experience such systems as educationally effective.

Although AI Tutoring has become increasingly visible in higher education, the existing measurement landscape remains conceptually fragmented. Current studies assess a range of related constructs, including technology acceptance, learner satisfaction, usability, trust in AI, chatbot experience, engagement, and learning support. These strands of work have made important contributions to understanding students’ experiences with AI-supported learning, but they do not provide a unified, AI-tutoring-specific measure of effectiveness. This fragmentation is also reflected in recent systematic reviews and state-of-the-field analyses, which show that educational AI research spans multiple technologies, purposes, and evaluation criteria rather than converging on a single learner-centered construct framework3,13,33,34. Comparison of Existing Measurement Approaches Adjacent to AI Tutoring Acceptance Quality

As shown in Table 1, existing measures provide valuable but partial insight into AI-supported learning. The following sections, therefore, examine these approaches in greater detail and explain why they do not yet offer a sufficiently integrated, learner-centered, and AI-tutoring-specific instrument for higher education.

One major group of related measures comes from the technology acceptance tradition. Drawing on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and related extensions, this line of work has emphasized perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, adoption intentions, and acceptance-related beliefs when evaluating educational technologies and AI systems8,9,18,19,35. Such measures are useful because they capture how learners judge the practical utility and usability of AI-enabled systems. However, acceptance-focused instruments are not sufficient for the present purpose. A system may be accepted because it is convenient, novel, or easy to access, yet this does not necessarily mean that it functions effectively as a tutor. More importantly, acceptance measures do not usually ask whether the system can diagnose learner difficulty, provide feedback at an appropriate level, scaffold understanding, or maintain a pedagogically meaningful tutoring exchange. In other words, acceptance helps explain adoption-related judgments, but it does not directly operationalize AI tutoring acceptance quality as a pedagogically grounded construct.

A second cluster of related measures centers on learner satisfaction and perceived effectiveness in technology-enhanced learning. Research in this area has examined how students evaluate their overall learning experiences, including online learner satisfaction, microlearning effectiveness, and perceived effectiveness in self-directed or app-based learning environments13,21–23,36. These studies are relevant because they foreground learner perceptions and recognize that educational value cannot be inferred from system design alone. Nonetheless, satisfaction and general perceived effectiveness remain broader constructs. They may capture whether students feel satisfied with a learning environment, but they do not specify which tutoring mechanism produces that evaluation. For example, a learner may report high satisfaction because the system is convenient or enjoyable, even when its feedback is shallow, its instructional guidance is weak, or its suggestions are not sufficiently trustworthy.

A third line of work focuses on trust in AI and reliance on AI-generated support. This literature is highly relevant to AI Tutoring because learners are often required to interpret, evaluate, and decide whether to follow AI-generated suggestions. Existing studies have explored trust in AI from conceptual, social, and educational perspectives, including the role of trust in AI-powered educational technology adoption and reliance on generative AI in learning contexts5,25–28,37–39. These contributions confirm that trust is a core dimension of learner engagement with AI-mediated support. Yet trust-oriented measures usually treat trust as a discrete focal variable rather than situating it within a broader multidimensional account of AI tutoring acceptance quality. As a result, trust measures capture whether learners are willing to rely on AI, but they do not assess the pedagogical substance of that reliance. They do not fully examine whether the AI tutor provides feedback that learners can act on, explanations that support understanding, or interaction that sustains the tutoring process.

A fourth group of measures emerges from research on chatbots, conversational agents, and AI-mediated interaction. Recent reviews have documented the expansion of chatbot research in education and have compared objectives, technological foundations, evaluation criteria, and observed impacts across multiple contexts4,6,30. Other studies have examined virtual teaching assistants, chatbot adoption, and learner engagement with generative AI tutoring systems16,29,40. This literature is particularly useful for understanding interactional aspects of AI-supported learning, including responsiveness, engagement, and communication quality. However, most of these instruments assess chatbot use, user experience, or system interaction in relatively broad terms. They are informative, but they often evaluate the conversational interface rather than the pedagogical tutoring process. In other words, they can show whether an AI agent communicates smoothly, but they may not show whether the agent provides instructionally useful feedback, supports skill development, or becomes a dependable source of learning guidance.

The literature on intelligent tutoring systems and AI-assisted instructional support provides the closest contextual background to the present study. Reviews of intelligent tutoring systems, AI in education, and AI-based feedback have highlighted the importance of adaptive support, personalized guidance, instructional dialog, and feedback processes in digitally mediated learning1,13,41–43. Empirical studies have also examined AI Tutoring in concrete settings, including personal AI Tutoring, generative AI Tutoring systems, and AI Tutoring versus expert instruction14,16,44. These studies reinforce the contextual relevance of AI Tutoring and show that tutoring-oriented AI differs from more general educational technologies. Even so, much of this literature emphasizes system design, implementation, instructional outcomes, or contextual application rather than the development of a concise learner-perceived multidimensional scale. This is a measurement-level gap rather than a claim that previous ITS research lacks pedagogical value. The issue is that existing ITS studies do not yet provide a compact instrument designed to capture how higher education learners evaluate the quality of AI tutoring across feedback, instruction, interaction, and trust at the same time. Thus, even the most directly relevant literature does not yet provide a sufficiently integrated instrument for assessing AI tutoring acceptance quality in the way proposed here.

Taken together, the existing literature suggests that prior measures have captured important but isolated aspects of AI-supported learning. Technology acceptance studies explain adoption-related beliefs; satisfaction studies assess overall evaluative responses; trust research examines confidence and reliance; chatbot studies focus on interaction and user experience; and intelligent tutoring system research emphasizes adaptive support, feedback, and instructional assistance. However, none of these strands, considered individually, offers a learner-centered and AI-tutoring-specific scale that simultaneously captures Feedback Quality, Instructional Effectiveness, Interaction Experience, and User Trust. In line with construct development guidance, this gap justifies the development of a new multidimensional instrument with clearer construct boundaries and stronger contextual specificity31,32,45. The present study, therefore, seeks to extend prior work by integrating these four dimensions into a single framework for assessing AI tutoring acceptance quality in higher education. Specifically, the present study contributes in three ways. Conceptually, it defines AI tutoring acceptance quality as a higher-order learner-perceived construct rather than as technology acceptance, satisfaction, trust, or chatbot experience alone. In measurement terms, it develops a multidimensional instrument that integrates feedback, instruction, interaction, and trust within one scale. Contextually, it validates this construct in higher education, where AI tutoring is increasingly used to support complex learning tasks that require guidance, feedback, and sustained learner engagement.

The development of the present scale is grounded in four complementary theoretical perspectives: the TAM, SDT, MRT, and the Model of Organizational Trust. These frameworks were not selected as parallel or competing explanations, but as mutually reinforcing lenses for specifying the construct domain of AI tutoring acceptance quality. This distinction is important because AI tutoring acceptance quality, as conceptualized in this study, is not reducible to a single form of technology adoption, instructional satisfaction, or interpersonal trust. Rather, it reflects a broader learner-perceived judgment of how well an AI Tutoring functions as an educational support system through feedback, instruction, interaction, and dependable guidance. In line with construct development scholarship, a multidimensional construct should be supported by a coherent theoretical rationale that explains why its dimensions belong together while remaining conceptually distinguishable31,32. The present study, therefore, integrates these four theories to explain the pedagogical, motivational, interactional, and relational foundations of AI tutoring acceptance quality.

SDT provides the motivational and pedagogical basis for understanding why feedback and instructional support are central to effective AI Tutoring. According to SDT, learners are more likely to engage deeply and persist in learning when educational environments support their psychological needs, especially competence and autonomy10. In AI Tutoring contexts, this theoretical perspective is particularly relevant because learners depend on the system not only for information, but also for scaffolding, guidance, and evaluative input that can strengthen their understanding and support progress toward learning goals. From this perspective, Feedback Quality and Instructional Effectiveness are not peripheral features of the system; they are core indicators of whether AI Tutoring supports meaningful learning in ways that align with learners’ need for competence and self-directed progress. Prior research on AI-enhanced learning has likewise emphasized the role of well-designed AI support in facilitating motivation, cognitive support, and learning improvement46. SDT, therefore, helps explain why AI tutoring acceptance quality must include the educational quality of feedback and the perceived instructional value of the system, rather than being confined to technical usability or surface-level convenience.

The TAM contributes a different, but complementary, perspective. TAM explains how users evaluate technology through perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, both of which shape user attitudes and continued engagement with a system18,47. In the present framework, TAM is not used to define AI tutoring acceptance quality as a mere acceptance construct. Instead, it helps clarify why learners’ perceptions of usefulness and usability remain theoretically relevant to the tutoring experience. Instructional Effectiveness is closely aligned with perceived usefulness because learners are unlikely to regard an AI Tutoring as effective if they do not perceive it as genuinely helpful for understanding, task completion, or skill development. At the same time, Interaction Experience is partly informed by perceived ease of use, since tutoring interactions that are confusing, effortful, or inaccessible may reduce the educational value of the system even when its content is potentially useful. Recent educational technology research has also shown that acceptance-related evaluations remain important in understanding learners’ engagement with AI-supported systems, although they do not by themselves capture the full pedagogical complexity of educational effectiveness8,9,19,35. Accordingly, TAM contributes to the present framework not as a complete explanation, but as a lens for understanding the practical and functional conditions under which AI Tutoring is experienced as valuable and usable.

MRT provides the interactional foundation of the proposed construct. MRT suggests that communication media differ in their capacity to convey rich, immediate, and meaningful exchanges, particularly through prompt feedback, multiple cues, personalization, and natural language48. This perspective is highly relevant in AI Tutoring contexts because tutoring is inherently interactive. The educational value of an AI Tutoring depends not only on what information it provides, but also on how effectively it communicates, responds, and adapts within the learner-system exchange. In this framework, Interaction Experience is therefore not treated as a secondary design issue, but as a constitutive component of AI tutoring acceptance quality. An AI Tutoring that communicates in a responsive, intuitive, and contextually meaningful way is more likely to support learner engagement and sustained use than one that is rigid or interactionally impoverished. Recent work on online learning, conversational agents, and AI Tutoring supports this view by showing that richer, more natural, and more responsive interactions are associated with stronger learner experience and more meaningful participation49–51. MRT thus explains why the interactional richness of AI Tutoring should be treated as part of its educational effectiveness rather than as a purely technical or aesthetic feature.

The Model of Organizational Trust adds the relational and dependence-based dimension of the framework. Mayer et al.11 conceptualized trust in terms of beliefs about ability, benevolence, and integrity. Although originally developed outside educational AI, this framework is highly applicable to AI Tutoring because learners are often required to rely on system-generated feedback, explanations, and recommendations without being able to fully verify the logic behind them. In such situations, User Trust becomes an essential component of effective AI Tutoring. Learners must perceive the AI Tutoring as capable enough to provide useful guidance, sufficiently reliable to be taken seriously, and appropriately aligned with educational purposes. This does not mean that trust is treated here as a downstream outcome of AI Tutoring; rather, it is conceptualized as part of the learner’s evaluation of whether the tutoring support is dependable enough to function educationally. Recent research has reinforced the importance of trust in AI-enhanced educational settings, particularly when learners decide whether to adopt, rely on, or resist AI-mediated support5,25–28,38. The trust perspective, therefore, helps explain why effective AI Tutoring cannot be conceptualized solely in terms of instructional utility or interface quality; it must also include whether learners regard the system as trustworthy enough to rely on in practice.

Taken together, these four theories define a coherent multidimensional rationale for the proposed scale. SDT explains why effective AI Tutoring should support competence-related learning needs through high-quality feedback and instructional guidance. TAM clarifies why learners’ perceptions of usefulness and ease remain relevant to whether AI Tutoring is experienced as educationally valuable and accessible. MRT explains why interactional richness and responsiveness are essential because tutoring unfolds through communicative exchange. The Model of Organizational Trust shows why learners’ confidence in the system’s ability, reliability, and intent is indispensable when tutoring involves advice, feedback, and guidance. Importantly, these theories do not operate at the same conceptual level in a redundant way. Instead, they illuminate different but interdependent domains of AI tutoring acceptance quality: the pedagogical domain, the functional domain, the interactional domain, and the relational domain.

This integrated perspective also addresses the concern that AI tutoring acceptance quality might otherwise become a vague umbrella term combining antecedents, process features, and outcomes without sufficient conceptual discipline. In the present study, Feedback Quality, Instructional Effectiveness, Interaction Experience, and User Trust are treated as constitutive dimensions of a higher-order learner-perceived construct rather than as independent predictors or distal consequences. Their inclusion is justified not because they are merely correlated, but because each captures a necessary aspect of what it means for AI Tutoring to function effectively in higher education. Together, they specify the conceptual boundaries of AI tutoring acceptance quality in a way that is theoretically grounded, educationally meaningful, and appropriate for multidimensional scale development31,32,52. On this basis, the proposed framework offers a more coherent alternative to theory stacking by showing how TAM, SDT, MRT, and the Model of Organizational Trust collectively support the four-dimensional structure of the scale.

Based on the conceptual and theoretical arguments presented above, the present study specifies Feedback Quality, Instructional Effectiveness, Interaction Experience, and User Trust as the four constitutive dimensions of AI tutoring acceptance quality. These dimensions were selected not simply because they appear frequently in the literature on educational technology, but because together they represent the core pedagogical, functional, interactional, and relational qualities through which learners evaluate whether AI Tutoring is educationally effective. In other words, the proposed dimensions are intended to capture the essential domain of AI tutoring acceptance quality rather than a collection of loosely related perceptions. This approach is consistent with scale-development scholarship, which emphasizes that dimensions should be selected on the basis of conceptual centrality, theoretical defensibility, and construct-domain relevance31,32,52.

Feedback Quality is included because feedback is one of the most fundamental functions of tutoring. In educational settings, tutoring is not limited to delivering content; it also involves diagnosing learner needs, identifying errors, guiding improvement, and helping learners regulate subsequent action. When AI is used in a tutoring role, the quality of feedback becomes a primary basis on which learners judge its educational value. Yet feedback quality should not be understood only as whether the AI response is fast, accurate, or clear. Recent feedback research suggests that feedback effectiveness also depends on when feedback is provided, what aspect of learning it addresses, and the level at which it supports correction, explanation, reflection, or regulation. Ning et al.53, for example, proposed the Time-Object-Level framework and showed that feedback effectiveness is shaped by the interaction of these dimensions. From this perspective, AI-generated feedback should be evaluated not only as an output from the system but also as a pedagogical mechanism that helps learners interpret performance, adjust strategies, and continue learning.

The inclusion of Feedback Quality is also supported by prior work showing that AI-generated feedback can influence learning processes in meaningful ways. Research on educational chatbots has indicated that metacognitive feedback can improve science learning and support learners’ reflective engagement with tasks54. Related work on AI-generated feedback in writing contexts also suggests that learners respond not only to the availability of feedback, but to its perceived usefulness and efficacy55. In intelligent tutoring systems, the role of feedback has been recognized as central enough to warrant systematic review, with recent evidence highlighting how feedback use shapes the instructional value of such systems43. More recent work further suggests that feedback should be examined through multiple interacting dimensions rather than through a single quality indicator. Ning et al.53 distinguished the timing of feedback, the object to which feedback is directed, and the level at which feedback operates. This distinction is useful for the present scale because learners may evaluate AI feedback differently depending on whether it arrives immediately or after reflection, whether it targets errors, strategies, emotions, or learning processes, and whether it offers surface correction, process explanation, or higher-order regulation. For these reasons, Feedback Quality is not treated here as a peripheral design feature. Rather, it is conceptualized as a core dimension of AI tutoring acceptance quality because tutoring without high-quality feedback would fail to fulfill one of its central educational functions.

Instructional Effectiveness is included because AI Tutoring must ultimately be judged by whether it is perceived to support learning in a substantively educational sense. Whereas feedback quality focuses on the characteristics of the evaluative information provided by the system, instructional effectiveness refers more broadly to the extent to which the AI Tutoring helps learners understand content, improve skills, organize learning tasks, and make progress toward learning goals. This distinction is important because a system may provide polished or responsive feedback while still offering limited instructional value overall. Thus, AI tutoring acceptance quality must include learners’ judgments about whether the system actually functions as a meaningful source of instructional support.

This dimension is supported by both conceptual and empirical literature. Reviews of intelligent tutoring systems have emphasized the role of AI in delivering adaptive and personalized support intended to facilitate learning processes rather than merely automate content delivery1,42. Empirical studies of AI Tutoring have likewise shown that AI-based tutoring can meaningfully influence learning performance and engagement in specific educational settings16,44,56. Related research on learning effectiveness in digital environments further suggests that learners’ perceptions of instructional support are closely tied to whether educational technologies are experienced as genuinely useful for progress and understanding23,36. In addition, work on AI-enhanced learning has indicated that AI can contribute to students’ motivation and learning improvement when it functions as meaningful educational support rather than as a passive tool46. Accordingly, Instructional Effectiveness is treated here as a distinct and indispensable dimension of AI tutoring acceptance quality.

Interaction Experience is included because tutoring is inherently interactive. Unlike one-way content delivery systems, tutoring depends on exchange, responsiveness, and the learner’s ongoing engagement with guidance. In AI Tutoring contexts, this means that the quality of learner-AI interaction is not a superficial usability issue but part of the core educational experience. Interaction Experience refers to the degree to which communication with the AI Tutoring is perceived as natural, responsive, rich, intuitive, and engaging enough to support continued learning activity. If interaction is rigid, confusing, delayed, or difficult to sustain, the tutoring process may be disrupted even if the system’s content is otherwise strong.

This dimension is strongly supported by the literature on communication and AI-mediated learning. MRT suggests that richer and more responsive communication channels better support complex and ambiguous tasks, which is particularly relevant to tutoring situations that require explanation, clarification, and adaptive response48,49. Research on AI in online learning has also shown that AI can influence learner-instructor interaction and the broader interactional structure of learning environments51. Studies of AI Tutoring and chatbot-based educational systems further indicate that responsiveness, naturalness, and interaction quality are closely associated with learner engagement and overall experience16,20,50. Reviews of educational chatbots likewise confirm that interaction is one of the most consistently evaluated aspects of AI-supported educational systems6,30. For this reason, Interaction Experience is included as a constitutive dimension of AI tutoring acceptance quality rather than as an external correlate.

User Trust is included because learners’ willingness to rely on AI-generated guidance is central to whether AI Tutoring can function effectively in practice. In many AI-supported learning situations, students cannot fully inspect or verify the reasoning behind the system’s suggestions, feedback, or recommendations. As a result, the perceived acceptance quality of AI Tutoring depends not only on what the system provides, but also on whether learners regard it as sufficiently capable, reliable, and appropriate to rely on. User Trust, therefore, refers to learners’ confidence in the AI Tutoring ability, dependability, and educational intent. Without such trust, even technically accurate or pedagogically valuable support may be ignored, questioned, or underused.

The inclusion of User Trust is well supported by recent research. The trust literature has consistently emphasized the importance of beliefs about ability, benevolence, and integrity when users decide whether to depend on a system or agent11,27,37. In educational AI specifically, trust has been identified as a critical factor shaping adoption, reliance, and resistance to AI-powered learning support5,25,26,28,38. This body of work makes clear that trust is not merely a downstream outcome of effective tutoring. Rather, it is part of the learner’s judgment of whether AI Tutoring is dependable enough to support learning in the first place. Therefore, User Trust is conceptualized here as an internal dimension of AI tutoring acceptance quality rather than as a separate external variable.

Taken together, these four dimensions provide a coherent and educationally meaningful specification of AI tutoring acceptance quality. Feedback Quality captures the evaluative and corrective function of tutoring. Instructional Effectiveness reflects the broader educational value of the AI Tutoring in supporting understanding and skill development. Interaction Experience captures the communicative conditions through which tutoring is enacted. User Trust reflects the learner’s confidence in the system as a dependable educational partner. Each dimension represents a distinct aspect of the construct, yet none is sufficient on its own. A system may be easy to interact with but instructionally weak; it may be instructionally promising but poorly trusted; or it may be trusted but provide vague and unhelpful feedback. The present scale, therefore, treats these four dimensions as complementary components that jointly define AI tutoring acceptance quality in higher education.

This specification also helps avoid conceptual overlap with adjacent constructs. The four dimensions are not intended to measure learner motivation, behavioral intention, overall satisfaction, or actual academic achievement as such. Nor are they treated as independent predictors of a separate effectiveness outcome. Instead, they represent the internal content domain of AI tutoring acceptance quality itself. In this respect, the proposed framework offers a more construct-specific and multidimensional alternative to existing approaches that focus only on acceptance, usability, trust, or single aspects of instructional support. On this basis, the four-dimensional structure of AI Tutoring Acceptance Quality Scale (AITAQS) is theoretically justified and appropriate for subsequent empirical validation.

Building on the preceding conceptual and theoretical discussion, the present study proposes a four-dimensional framework for assessing AI tutoring acceptance quality in higher education. This framework conceptualizes AI tutoring acceptance quality as a higher-order learner-perceived construct reflected through Feedback Quality, Instructional Effectiveness, Interaction Experience, and User Trust. The framework is grounded in the view that AI Tutoring should be evaluated not as a purely technical system feature, nor as a single outcome variable, but as a multidimensional educational support construct that captures how learners judge the pedagogical, interactional, functional, and relational quality of AI-mediated tutoring.

The proposed framework also clarifies how the four theoretical perspectives used in this study complement one another. SDT explains why effective AI Tutoring must support learners’ competence and self-directed progress through meaningful feedback and instructional guidance10. The TAM helps account for why perceived usefulness and ease of engagement remain relevant to learners’ judgments of instructional value and interactional accessibility18,47. MRT explains why the quality of communicative exchange matters, particularly in tutoring contexts that depend on responsiveness, clarity, and adaptive interaction48,49. The Model of Organizational Trust contributes the relational logic needed to explain why learners must perceive the AI Tutoring as capable, dependable, and appropriate to rely on when using its feedback and recommendations11,25,27.

Taken together, these theories do not function as disconnected justifications for separate variables. Rather, they jointly define the construct domain of AI tutoring acceptance quality. In this integrated perspective, Feedback Quality represents the diagnostic, corrective, formative, and regulatory quality of AI-generated support; Instructional Effectiveness captures the perceived educational value of AI Tutoring for learning and skill development; Interaction Experience reflects the communicative and experiential conditions through which tutoring is enacted; and User Trust captures the learner’s confidence in the AI Tutoring as a dependable educational partner. Each dimension is conceptually distinct, yet all four are necessary to explain how learners form an overall judgment of AI tutoring acceptance quality.

This framework is intended to address a limitation in the existing literature, where related constructs such as usefulness, satisfaction, trust, or chatbot experience are often examined separately rather than integrated into a tutoring-specific measurement model20,21,25,49. By contrast, the present framework treats the four dimensions as constitutive components of the same higher-order construct rather than as antecedents, correlates, or distal outcomes. This conceptualization is consistent with multidimensional construct development principles, which emphasize that a well-defined scale should be based on a clearly specified construct domain and theoretically defensible dimensions31,32,52.

Accordingly, the proposed theoretical framework provides the conceptual basis for the development of the AITAQS. It explains why the scale is structured around four dimensions and why these dimensions should be understood as jointly reflecting the broader construct of AI tutoring acceptance quality in higher education. Discussion

The results of sequence analysis have provided a strong empirical basis for the structural validity of AITAQS. Across the sequential analytic stages, including PCA-based exploratory item reduction, CFA, ESEM, and bifactor ESEM, the results consistently supported the four-factor configuration of the AITAQS. It specifically covers feedback quality, instructional effectiveness, interaction experience and user trust. The above-mentioned configuration is highly consistent with the theoretical framework constructed in the Literature Review, fully demonstrating that although learners’ experience of using the AI Tutoring system has multi-dimensional characteristics, there are still effective correlations among each dimension.

During the process of confirmatory analysis, although the traditional CFA model demonstrated good fitting results, this model fixed all cross-loads at 0, and this constraint condition was overly strict. As Hu and Bentler71 proposed, such rigid models often ignore the shared variance among related dimensions, thereby misrepresenting complex psychological constructs. When cross-loads are allowed in ESEM, the model fit will be significantly improved. The decrease in RMSEA and SRMR values, as well as the increase in CFI and TLI values, all indicate this change. The emergence of this change indicates that flexible models can precisely capture data structures.

The bifactor ESEM, which incorporated a general AITAQS factor together with four specific latent dimensions, achieved the best overall performance. Dunn and McCray72 proposed that the bifactor model is particularly effective in clarifying whether the multi-dimensional structure still has common latent features. In the present study, this general factor may be interpreted as the shared variance underlying learners’ overall perceived acceptance quality of AI tutoring.

From a theoretical perspective, the research results emphasize that learners who have received AI Tutoring are involved in multiple psychological processes during the tutoring process. In this process, Feedback Quality reflects learners’ evaluation of whether the AI tutor provides clear, timely, and useful performance-related feedback. Instructional Effectiveness captures students’ perceptions of whether the AI tutor supports understanding, practice, and skill improvement in a pedagogically meaningful way. These dimensions should be interpreted as constitutive components of perceived AI tutoring acceptance quality rather than as direct indicators of broader outcomes such as learning achievement, motivation, or participation. Instead, they represent how students appraise the educational usefulness and acceptability of the AI tutoring experience73.

During the research process of this study, a strong universal participation factor emerged in the Bifactor ESEM model. This phenomenon indicates that learners, in addition to the aforementioned different aspects, can also regard the AI Tutoring system as a unified whole with experiential attributes. This view is the same as that of Gegenfurtner74 research. This view is that the Bifactor ESEM model can effectively capture the coexistence of general and specific sources of variance, and its performance is particularly prominent in multi-dimensional structures such as relational, technology adoption, and motivation. In scenarios where AI-assisted systems are used for learning, emotional and cognitive relationships, as well as relationship components, do not exist independently; they work in coordination with each other. Therefore, AITAQS can effectively identify different sub-dimensions and also elaborate on the overall structure of AI learning participation.

This study aims to bring about a change in people’s understanding of the operation mechanism of AI Tutoring. Previous studies often only focused on one dimension, but now people should establish a multi-dimensional cognitive perspective. The attributes of this mechanism cover multiple factors such as cognition, motivation, and human-machine levels. This mechanism establishes a more comprehensive analytical framework for how students view AI teaching. The final results of this study support the TAM : The main reason why AI is easily accepted by students is still the practicality of the system itself. However, there are two key factors within this study: user trust and interaction experience, which indicate that the use of AI Tutoring systems by students is not solely determined by students’ rational cognition. Emotional and relational reasons have a significant impact on whether students are willing to use AI Tutoring systems.

The interaction between the AI Tutoring system and students mainly lies in two aspects: emotional comfort and trust building. According to the SDT, the abilities of students’ psychological needs specifically cover autonomy, competence and relatedness. These traits can have an impact on the perceived acceptance quality of AI counseling. Therefore, high-quality feedback and guidance can promote students’ perceptual competence. At the same time, personalized interaction methods can also enhance the connection between interpersonal relationships. As long as the above requirements are followed, it can promote students’ intrinsic motivation.

This study also adds to MRT. It gives real evidence that AI systems using many modes, such as text, voice, adaptive feedback, and pictures, help create fuller and better learning experiences. The superior fit of the ESEM and bifactor models suggests that learners simultaneously process multiple channels of information, combining cognitive understanding with affective responses during AI-mediated instruction. In conclusion, the AITAQS has established a theoretical framework for how motivation, cognition, and related factors influence students’ participation in intelligent tutoring systems.

Practical experience has shown that the AI Tutoring system has multi-dimensional value in education. The role of AI systems in assisting students can be fully exerted among educators, curriculum designers, policymakers, and developers of AI Tutoring systems. For teachers and designers, AITAQS can be used as a diagnostic reflection tool: high grades indicate that the AI Tutoring system can effectively support adaptation, while poor grades suggest that the AI Tutoring system has content or feedback deviations. Teachers can optimize and improve the use of AI based on this. Strike a balance between AI Tutoring and human teaching to maintain students’ participation in the AI Tutoring system. For AI developers, research emphasizes the importance of human-centered design training. AI developers need to integrate teaching intentions and effective interaction during the development process. Use multimodal feedback to enhance and promote engagement, genuine communication, and transparency features. For managers and policymakers, AITAQS serves as a powerful basis for AI-assisted system projects: institutions can use it to evaluate the practical efficiency of AI-assisted systems, guide teacher training, and ensure the technical alignment of teaching objectives. At the policy level, it can first assist in the formulation of usage standards, and secondly ensure that technological innovation is merely supplementary rather than a complete replacement for human teaching. In essence, the AITAQS is an evidence-based diagnostic tool for assessing students’ perceived acceptance quality of AI tutoring across four dimensions: feedback quality, instructional effectiveness, interaction experience, and user trust. It is intended to support the evaluation and improvement of AI-supported learning environments, rather than to serve as a direct measure of broader constructs such as learning motivation, teacher-student relationship, or overall educational effectiveness.

Although this research is of great significance, it still has the following limitations: In terms of geographical scope, data collection was only carried out in universities within specific regions and cultural backgrounds. The research conclusions of the AITAQS cannot be generalized to regions with other cultural backgrounds. Therefore, if subsequent research is conducted in different cultural and language environments, the scale needs to be revalidated. The research method used in this study was a one-off self-reported survey, which did not clarify the causal relationship between the perceived acceptance quality of AI tutoring and broader academic achievement. In terms of group analysis, although relatively advanced testing methods were used, the adaptability of this scale among different groups was not verified. Establishing the invariance of measurement can first enhance the credibility of the scale, and secondly provide support for the fairness of student feedback interpretation. In terms of the number of items, although the scale is currently usable, there are relatively few items in each dimension, with only three items per dimension. It is recommended that future research increase the number of items appropriately to enhance the comprehensiveness of the content. This would also ensure that there is a sufficient pool of items available for flexible selection during subsequent sample validation. Future research needs to integrate multiple perspectives and conduct a comprehensive exploration of the practicality of AI Tutoring to design an AI Tutoring system that is precise in both teaching and technology.

The integration of AI and educational technologies is by no means merely a technological upgrade, but rather requires transformative, practical teaching centered on learning and interpersonal interaction. Grounded in an integrated theoretical framework, this study develops and validates a four-dimensional model of AI Tutoring Acceptance Quality, comprising feedback quality, instructional effectiveness, interaction experience, and user trust. The AITAQS provides a structured framework for assessing learner-perceived acceptance quality of AI tutoring and offers a practical basis for the evaluation and improvement of learner-centered AI-supported educational environments.

Methods

Research design

This study adopted a five-stage scale development and validation design to develop and validate a multi-dimensional practical tool for evaluating the perceived effect of learning on AI-enabled tutoring systems in the field of higher education. Based on the established guidelines for the development of psychometric scales52,75,76, the study was conducted through a sequential process comprising five stages: (1) concept establishment, (2) project generation, (3) expert validation and review, (4) testing, and (5) an empirical validation phase involving multiple rounds of survey-based data collection. In this final phase, independent samples were used for exploratory, confirmatory, and structural model-testing analyses, including PCA-based item reduction, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), ESEM, and bifactor model comparisons.

The main reason for using ESEM to replace the traditional CFA is that it allows cross-loadings between hidden variables. This phenomenon is relatively common in fields such as AI-assisted systems, but its conceptual boundaries remain rather ambiguous77. Furthermore, compared to EFA, ESEM integrates rotation techniques to achieve confirmatory objectives with structural equation algorithms, using more precise model fit indices to determine the underlying structure of the data78. Thus, this study adopted a stepwise validation approach, beginning with an initial screening of items, followed by an exploration of the factor structure, and finally testing the model through CFA and ESEM analyses, in order to comprehensively assess the construct validity of the AITAQS.

Participants and sampling

The research subjects of this study cover undergraduate students majoring in vocal music from five universities in Shanxi Province, China. To ensure the comparability and relevance among the selected samples, only students who have used the AITAQS in vocal music or vocal performance guidance scenarios will be selected during the selection process. This study intends to employ a two-stage cluster sampling method, striving to achieve representativeness and balance among samples from various institutions and grades. In the first stage of the research, a purposeful sampling method will be adopted to make a reasonable selection of five universities to ensure that all of them offer vocal music programs and to verify the technology of the AI Tutoring system. In the second stage, students from each institution were stratified by academic year (freshman to senior), and the selected personnel will be grouped. It is necessary to ensure that the number of samples randomly drawn for each group is basically the same. This strategy will enhance the representativeness and balance of the sample. It can not only reflect the educational background of the research sample, but also their learning situation after receiving the tutoring system and the richness of their learning.

Data for the CFA and ESEM analyses were collected separately. The CFA study yielded a total of 248 valid data points and 23 invalid data points, while the ESEM study yielded 252 valid data points and 33 invalid data points. This sample size exceeded the recommended sample size threshold for the ESEM design of the 4 latent variables and 12 observed items included in this study. Among them, A sample size of ≥200 can ensure the interpretability and stability of the estimation results79. Three criteria defined an invalid questionnaire. The team excluded surveys completed in less than 2 minutes or more than 4 min. Researchers removed responses with highly uniform or repetitive patterns. Any surveys with missing or insufficient data were deleted. Before starting, all participants knew the survey would take approximately 2 to 4 min to complete. Prior to the two formal surveys, exploratory studies were conducted on the scales. Through two rounds of reliability analysis, items were preliminarily screened in each round; the number of valid participants for the two surveys was 172 and 132, respectively. Additionally, data collection for PCA-based exploratory item reduction analysis was conducted separately following the reliability analysis, ultimately yielding 137 valid data points. Although the sample sizes for the reliability analysis and exploratory factor analysis were relatively small, they met the analytical requirements.

The data used in all analyses of the pilot and formal test were collected from participants at different schools, ensuring the independence of the samples across all analytical methods.

After conducting a reasonable analysis of the results through quantitative analysis and qualitative feedback, four items were deleted due to weak item relevance and concept redundancy. So the final formed item scale covers a total of twelve items and is evenly distributed across four dimensions. The distribution results are as follows: feedback quality (3 items), teaching effect (3 items), interaction experience (3 items), and user trust (3 items). Such uniform distribution results are very helpful for the subsequent analysis of the factorial stability and interpretability of ESEM81. Although standard practice tends to involve 4 to 6 items per dimension, this study did not aim for brevity at all costs. In fact, five representative items were initially provided for each dimension, selected from a pool of 20 items in total. The current configuration of three items per dimension is the final outcome of a combination of theoretical screening and psychometric optimization. The selected items are superior to those that were omitted in terms of both conceptual and operational quality. Of course, the coverage provided by three items is indeed somewhat narrower than that of a longer scale. Therefore, future research may consider expanding the number of items within each dimension and re-validating the factor stability and generalizability of this scale across a wider variety of higher education settings.

Data collection and analysis procedures

This research has received ethical permission from each university involved in the project. The investigation environment is not limited to the classroom; it can also be conducted through a secure online network. Participation strictly adheres to the principles of voluntariness and anonymity, and is highly consistent with the research ethics guidelines of the institution.

Outliers, missing values and distribution hypothesis tests were conducted on the data. If the missing response rate is less than 5%, the method of maximizing expectations is adopted for estimation. The skewness index (<2) and kurtosis index (<7) show acceptable univariate normality82. All analyses were completed with the aid of IBM SPSS 24 and Mplus 8.0. In the exploratory phase of this study, PCA was first employed to perform preliminary item reduction and structural identification, using Varimax rotation. Subsequently, to further evaluate the latent structure, ESEM analysis was introduced; this allows for more flexible handling of cross-loadings between items, making the evaluation more realistic. Subsequently, the ESEM model was estimated to confirm the latent structure, using Geomin rotation (ε = 0.5) and the MLR estimator, which took into account the non-normality and allowed for cross-loading. Model adequacy was judged by χ²/df, CFI, TLI, RMSEA (with 90% CI) and SRMR. Fit criteria refer to Hu and Bentler71: CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95, RMSEA ≤ 0.06, SRMR ≤ 0.08.
