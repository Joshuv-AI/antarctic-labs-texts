Bioprocessing of monomethyl ether from Alternaria alternata, a multi-target antiproliferative compound as emphasized by in silico analysis
Authors: Suhaila R Alsaid, Manar M Abdel Gwad, Gamal Abdel-Fattah, Mohamed A Marawan, Yehia Osman, Samar A Khashana, Ashraf S A El-Sayed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of drug-resistant cancer cells driven by mutations, proteins tertiary structure alterations, and overexpression of drug efflux pumps, particularly P-glycoprotein (P-gp) system is the major challenge of cancer chemotherapy. Consequently, the search for affordable, stable, and multi-targeted lead compounds has become a critical objective. Alternariol monomethyl ether (AME) is known for its cytotoxic activity; nevertheless, its bioavailability and in vivo efficacy remains equivocal, which limits its further therapeutic application. Alternaria alternata LSR PV576354.1, inhabiting stored barely seeds, was isolated with the highest yields of AOH and AME as quantified by HPLC. Upon nutritional bioprocessing, the yield of AOH and AME by A. alternata was increased to 8.65 µg/ml and 10.05 µg/ml, respectively, at C:N ratio 14.2:1, of pH 5.0 after 18 days. The purified AME of A. alternata was chemically resolved from the HPLC, LC–MS and MS/MS analyses, with 272.2 m/z, and consistent fragmentation pattern of authentic AME. The maximum antiproliferative activity of AME was reported for HCT-116 (0.61 μg/ml), HepG-2 (1.72 μg/ml), MCF-7 cells (2.41 μg/ml), with selectivity indices 17.1, 6.4, 4.3 folds, compared to normal OEC cells. AME of A. alternata had a strong anti-tubulin polymerizing activity (IC50 value 3.9 μg/ml), anti- topoisomerase I (IC50 value 40.9 μg/ml) and II (IC50 value 35.6 μg/ml) activities. The AME of A. alternata strongly induces the total, early apoptosis, late apoptosis and necrosis of the HCT-116 cells by 6.7, 19.5, 17.2 and 1.8 folds, compared to the control cells. From the molecular docking analysis, the AME of A. alternata had a conceivable binding energies with topoisomerase I, II and β-tubulin (-7.0—7.3 kcal/mol), with RMSD values 1.5 and 1.9Å, respectively. Consequently, from the experimental and in silico analyses, A. alternata AME could be a promising multi-target antiproliferative lead compound, and with further structure–activity relationship, transcriptomics and proteomics analyses, this compound could be a novel platform of cancer chemotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-60529-3.Keywords: Alternaria alternata, Alternariol monomethyl ether, Anticancer activity, Topoisomerases inhibitionSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cancer, Chemical biology, Drug discovery

Introduction

Chemotherapy is the one common approach for cancer therapy, implementing various natural or synthetic compounds to halt the multiplication and proliferation of cancer cells, however, the development of cellular biological drug-resistance is the challenge that limits the efficiency of this approach1. The invasion and metastasis of cancer cells are the responsible for 90% of resistance to chemotherapies2. Several mechanisms were reported to be the crucial in tumor cells drug-resistance, such as alterations in drug metabolism, epigenetic modulation, mutations, gene transposition, translocations and microRNA alteration3. The genomic instability, miRNA, transcriptomic proteomic heterogeneity of tumor cells that directly influence on the cell cycle stage, stochastic alterations of the cell are the main reasons of drug resistance3. As well as, releasing of the drugs outside the cells by the ABC membrane transporting system, via the development of P-glycoprotein, multi-drug Resistance-associated Protein 1 and Breast Cancer Resistance Protein, were the most reported drug-resistance mechanisms2,4. Chemotherapies have a wide range of targets including angiogenesis, histone deacetylase, tyrosine kinase, topoisomerases, tubulin polymerization and proteasome inhibitors1,3–5. Topoisomerases inhibition is one of the common chemotherapies, that stopping the vital biological activities of DNA replication, transcription, and repair3. So, the topoisomerase inhibitors are one of the potential anti-cancer therapies for stopping the cellular DNA replications, with a subsequent cell death. Camptothecin derivatives and podophyllotoxin derivatives are the common types of topoisomerase I and II inhibitors, respectively5. However, the price and availability of these compounds are the major hurdles that limit their usage, especially in the developing countries. So, searching for a novel compounds with affordable availability and efficiency is the challenge.

Among the promising natural compounds, mycotoxins were recently received much attention as a promising anticancer compounds6, of superior cytotoxicity with diverse mechanisms against mammalian cells7. The promising anticancer activity and applicability elaborates from their higher heat-resistant, and resilient chemical stability which add to their value as possible anticancer medications8. Mycotoxins are usually associated with the presence of fungal contaminants in stored food and feed commodities, especially in grains, grain-based products, vegetables, fruits, fruit juices, oil seeds, coffee, and wine10,12. Alternariol (AOH), alternariol monomethyl-ether (AME), tenuazonic acid, tentoxin, altertoxin II and altenuene (ALT) as “emerging mycotoxins” are usually associated with the Alternaria spp, inhabiting food and feed materials12,14,15. Among the secondary metabolites produced by Alternaria, AOH and AME are particularly important polyketides that have been widely studied for their toxicity, exhibiting a strong mutagenic, cytotoxic, and anticancer properties18. The mode of action of AOH and AME usually by inhibiting topoisomerases, inducing DNA strand breakage and gene mutations in cultured human and animal cells, disrupting redox balance15,19. As well as, AOH mediates the rupture of the plasma membrane, inducing the cellular apoptosis and necrosis7,20,21, interacting with the DNA, inhibiting the cellular proliferation, and induces cell cycle arrest22–25. AOH and AME promote the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing oxidative DNA damage, inhibiting topoisomerase II, stabilizing the cleavable DNA complex, causing final cell arrest26–29. However, the availability, and selective cytotoxicity of Alternaria mycotoxins AOH and AME, towards different cell lines, compared to normal ones remains equivocal. So, the objective of this study was to chemically characterize the AME and AOH from the potential species of Alternaria, and to assess their cytotoxic activities towards the tumor cells compared to normal ones.

Materials and methods

Samples collection, fungal isolation and identification

Wheat, barely, and corn grains samples were collected from storage facilities in Misurata city, Libya, on June /2023, according to the guidelines of the European Commission International Organization for Standardization30. Three replicates of each sample were aggregated in 1 kg composite sample, in sealed sterile polyethylene bag, for isolation of their mycoflora31. Fungi were isolated by plating 0.5 g (10 grains) of each sample into Potato Dextrose agar medium (PDA) (200 g potato extract, 20 g glucose, 20 g agar in 1 L distilled water) of 0.5 mg chloramphenicol32. The plate cultures were incubated for 10 days at 30 °C, and the emerged colonies of Alternaria species were purified on the same medium. The Alternaria isolates were identified based on their morphological features of conidial ontology, and mycelial anastomosis as described by Barnett and Hunter33. The samples of Alternaria isolates were prepared for the scanning electron microscope (JEOL JSM 6510lv, at 20.0 kv)34.

The potent AOH and AME producing fungi were molecularly identified based on their ITS sequences with the primers ITS4 5′-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3′, and ITS5 5′-TCC-TCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′ (Rex). The fungal genomic DNA was extracted by CTAB reagent35, as a PCR template, and the PCR reaction was prepared using the 2xPCR master mixture (Cat.# 25,027) according to the manufacturer’s instructions36. The amplicons were analyzed by 1.5% agarose gel in TAE buffer with 1 kb DNA ladder (Cat.# PG010-55DI), purified and sequenced. The sequences were BLAST searched on NCBI, aligned by ClustalW muscle algorithm by MEGAX37, and the phylogenetic analysis was performed by neighbor joining method of 1000 bootstrap replication38.

Screening, bioprocessing, and chemical validation of the extracted AOH and AME

The isolates of Alternaria were screened for AOH and AME production by growing on potato dextrose broth (PDB) medium39. The cultures were incubated for 10 days at 28 °C, filtered and the filtrate was acidified with 1N HCl to pH 2.0, extracted by dichloromethane for three times39. The extract was dried in a rotary evaporator at 35 °C, dissolved in 1 ml of methanol, detected by TLC (Silica gel 60 F254, Merck KGaA, Darm, Germany), developed by water and methanol (2:8 v/v)40. The plat was visulaized at λ254 nm, and the putative spots of AOH and AME were considered, and their concentrations were semi-quantitatively determined by Image J (https://imagej.net/welcome), compared to authentic AOH and AME (Cat#0.1.05715.0001) concentrations. The AOH and AME were extracted from the silica gel spots and dissolved in methanol40, and their purity and concentration were assessed by HPLC (YOUNG In, Chromass) of RP-C18 column (Cat.#959,963–902) with mobile phase water: acetonitrile: methanol (65:5:30 v/v/v) at 1.0 ml/min flow rate, at λ365 nm, compared to known concentrations of AOH and AME. The structure of the extracted AOH and AME was committed by LC–MS/MS (Thermo Scientific LCQ Deca mass spectrometer equipped with electrospray source, Hypersil Gold aQ (C18) column), in positive-ion mode. The sample was eluted from the column by a gradient of 2–98% of acetonitrile in 0.1% formic acid for 30 min, at flow rate 0.2 ml/min. The ion trap was scanned from 300–2000 m/z, and the mass was recorded at 300 to 2000 Da. The structural identity of the compounds was assessed based on their mass spectrum fragmentation pattern (https://cfmid.wishartlab.com/queries/7fb03f547c6d0f7e4c2749bc3718e244f41e8b07).

The impact of various carbon and nitrogen sources (glucose, sucrose, yeast extract, peptone, beef extract, ammonium sulfate, and tryptophan), incubation period, and pH on the AOH and AME productivity by the selected fungus was screened by Plackett–Burman design41,42 at the two variables (high + 1 and low -1 values). The productivity of AOH and AME were expressed by the means ± SD, the p-value, and F-values were determined by the Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). The concentration of AOH and AME was determined, as described above.

Cytotoxicity, cell cycle and apoptosis analyses of the extracted AME

The cytotoxicity of the purified AME for various cell lines, liver (HepG-2) (ATCC, HB-8065), breast (MCF-7) (ATCC, HTB-22), colon (HCT-116) (ATCC, CCL-247) carcinoma, and olfactory ensheathing cells (OEC), was assessed by the MTT assay43. The cells were grown on 96-well microtiter plate of RPMI medium, till the density 104 cells per/well, different AME concentrations were amended, and the plate was incubated for 48 h, then the MTT reagent was added for 6 h, and washed by solubilizing solution. The resulting formazan complex was measured at λ570 nm, the IC50 (for tumor cells) and CC50 (for normal cells), as stated by the compounds concentrations reducing the initial growth of cells by 50%, compared to controls. The selectivity index was expressed by the CC50 value of OEC cells to the IC50 value of tested tumor cell lines40.

The effect of purified AME on the apoptosis of HCT-116 cells was assessed, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat. #: K101-25). The cells were seeded into a 96-well plate culture (2 × 106 cells/well), incubated overnight at 37 °C, amended with AME different concentrations, incubated for 48 h, then the cells were harvested and washed with phosphate buffered saline. The cells were amended with 1X Annexin-binding buffer, Annexin VFITC and PI, incubated in dark for 15 min at room temperature, and the Annexin-PS complex intensity was measured by FITC signal detector at Ex λ488 nm and Em λ530 nm44.

Kinetics of DNA Topoisomerases inhibition and antitubulin polymerizing activity of AME

The kinetics of inhibition of topoisomerase I and II in response to the AME were assessed45. The activity of topoisomerase I (Cat.#0.1015–1), and topoisomerase II (Cat.#. TG1001-1) was measured, according to the Kits manufacturer’s instructions, by converting the supercoiled circular DNA into relaxed DNA. The IC50 value was expressed by the AME dose reducing the half-activity of initial activity of the enzymes activity, compared to control.

The antitubulin polymerizing activity of extracted AME was assessed by the fluorescence-based assay (Cat.# BK011P)46 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The tubulin proteins (4.0 mg/ml) was suspended in 80 mM PIPES (pH 6.9) buffer of 2 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 1.0 mM GTP, in 96-well plate with different AME concentrations, normalized to 10 μM Combretastatin as positive control. After samples gently mixing, the tubulin polymerization was measured by monitoring the turbidity at λ340nm.

Molecular docking analysis

Protein and ligand preparation

The interaction of AME with the predicted proteins namely; Topoisomerase I, II, and β-tubulin were assessed by the molecular docking simulation tools PyRx and AutoDock Vina Software47 and Discovery Studio48, and Chimera X software, Cytoscope Software, using Avogadro Software49–51, to clean up the structures before docking simulations. The 3D structure of AME was built by Avogadro software and prepared for the molecular docking analysis by minimizing the energy, and their interaction was evaluated with Topoisomerase I (PDB ID:1K4T), Topoisomerase 2A (PDB ID:4FM9) and β-tubulin (PDB ID:1JFF). The Protein- protein interaction network pharmacological analysis was assessed. The hub genes controlling the tumor progression and cell cycle, these genes were categorized into proteins of DNA damage responses (TOP1, TOP2, TDP1, PARP1, ATM, ATR, BRCA1, CHEK1, and CHEK2), and of mitotic arrest and cell survival pathways (TP53, AKT1, BCL2, CASP3, MYC, and EGFR)52,55. The docking results were ranked by predicted binding energy (kcal/mol), and the top-ranked proteins with the lowest root mean square deviation (RMSD) were selected as the optimal binding mode. The pattern of interaction (hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, and π-π stacking), was analyzed by the Discovery Studio Visualizer v21.1.0.20298 and UCSF Chimera49.

Fungal deposition

The ITS sequence of Alternaria alternata isolate LSR was deposited on Genbank with accession number PV608083.1 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/PV608083.1).

Statistical analysis

The experiments were committed in three biological replicates, and the results were expressed by the means ± SD. The significance and F-test were calculated using one-way ANOVA.

Results

Isolation and identification of the most potent AOH and AME producing fungal isolates inhabiting the stored seeds

Seventeen fungal isolates were recovered from the stored wheat and barley seeds, and identified to their species level according to their morphological features as described in our previous study. The AOH and AME productivity by the recovered fungal isolates were assessed by growing on PDB media, incubated for 15 days, extraction with methylene chloride, and their concentrations were checked and determined by TLC and HPLC. From the TLC and HPLC analyses, Alternaria species was reported as the highest AOH and AME (3.91–4.26 μg/ml) producer, compared to the corresponding authentic ones. The most potent AOH and AME producer “Alternaria sp” was comprehensively identified according to their microscopical features. Based macroscopical features, the isolate on PDA had colony size of 3 to 9 cm after 7 days at 25 °C, with flat colony of downy to woolly greyish short aerial hyphae that becomes greenish black or olive brown with light borders (Fig. 1). The plate reverse side was typically brown to black for pigment production. The microscopical features, the fungal isolate had septated, dark hyphae, short-septated conidiophores, brown in color with zigzag-like conidiophores (Fig. 1). The branched conidophores bear large ovoid or ellipsoid conidia, with transverse and longitudinal septations, conidia may produce germ tubes, ovoid to obclavate, darkly pigmented, muriform, smooth or roughened. From the macroscopical and microscopical analyses, the features of the current isolate “Alternaria sp LSR” were typically follows those of Alternaria alternata as described by56.

The identity of the potent fungal isolate was molecularly confirmed based on their ITS sequence. Using genomic DNA as PCR template, the ITS amplicon was ~ 700 bp, and sequenced. The obtained ITS sequence was non-redundantly BLAST searched on the NCBI database, giving 100% similarity with those of A. alternata LSR, and deposited to genbank with accession # PV576354.1. From the phylogenetic analysis, the ITS sequence of A. alternata LSR gave were grouped into two clusters (I, II). The ITS sequence of Alternaria alternate LSR had a 99% similarity with A. alternata with accession numbers MT481800.1, MZ046167.1, OQ591984.1, LC663172.1, MZ351370.1, MZ546173.1, and with A. tenuissima JQ417902.1, MW881066.1, ON962751.1, MZ160961.1, MZ151368.1, KP231179.1, and KF887142.1, with Zero value and query coverage 99.4% (Fig. 1). Thus, from the morphological features and molecular barcoding, the isolate was confirmed as Alternaria alternata.

Bioprocessing of A. alternata to maximize their yields of AOH and AME

The preliminary variables affecting AOH and AME productivity by A. alternata were screened by Plackett–Burman design. The tested parameters of carbon, nitrogen sources, incubation time, temperature and pH were assessed on AOH and AME productivities by A. alternata, with the lower and higher values of each variable as reported in Materials and Methods. After cultural incubation, the cultures were extracted, and the yield of AOH and AME were determined by the TLC and HPLC. The actual and predicted yield of AOH and AME by A. alternata was fluctuated from 1.01 to 10.05 µg/ml, ensures the efficiency of the Plackett–Burman design in screening of the parameters. The actual and predicted responses of AOH and AME productivity by A. alternata with the tested variables were summarized in the matrix of Plackett–Burman Design (Table 1). From the obtained results, the highest actual and predicted AME yield by A. alternata was 10.05 and 9.35 µg/ml, respectively at run# 13, followed by run #19 (6.78 and 6.5 µg/ml). As well as, at run # 13, the maximum actual and predicted productivity of AOH by A. alternata was 8.65 and 8.39 µg/ml, respectively, followed by run # 19 (6.01 and 6.86 µg/ml). At run # 13, the highest yield of AOH and AME was recovered by A. alternata, followed by run # 19. Practically, the AME productivity by A. alternata was slightly higher than AOH yield, for all the tested runs of Plackett–Burman design. The responses were analyzed by ANOVA by Minitab 16 software, to detect the most significant factors affecting AOH and AME at 90% level of confidence and α 0.1 (Table 2). The tested variables were considered to be significant when p-value less than or equal 0.05. The determination coefficients of AME and AOH (R2 = 90.45% and 90.83%) indicating the goodness-of-fit of the linear regression models. From the statistical analyses (Table 2), highest significant parameters affecting AME and AOH productivity by A. alternata was the temperature (X3), followed by pH (X1), incubation time (X2), glucose (X5), peptone (X8) and tryptophan (X11). The Pareto Charts, and normal probability plots of the standardized effects were illustrated as Fig. S1. From the Pareto- Charts, the highest statistically significant variables affecting on AME and AOH productivities by A. alternata were the pH, temperature, glucose, incubation time and peptone, as revealed from the p-value of the ANOVA statistical analyses. So, glucose, tryptophan, peptone, temperature, pH, and incubation time had a mathematically significant effect on AME production by Alternaria alternate. From the ANOVA analyses, the constructed model was highly significant as shown from the values of Fisher’s f-test 5.52 & 5.78 and probability p-value 0.016 and 0.014 for AME and AOH, respectively. The actual yields of AME and AOH from the runs of Plackett Burman design showed an obvious fluctuations and variations by A. alternata, confirming the significance of the tested variables. Based on the Plackett–Burman design, the optimal medium components for the maximum AME and AOH production by A. alternata contains glucose (15 g/L), peptone (2 g/L), tryptophan (5 g/L) at temperature 28 °C, pH 5.0, and incubated 18 days. Thus, for the higher productivity of AME than AOH by A. alternata, AME was further chemically and biologically characterized.

Chemical identity of the putative AME from Alternaria alternata

The chemical identity of the putative AME of A. alternata was verified by LC–MS/MS analyses. The spots of TLC silica gel with the same color and mobility of authentic AME, illuminated at λ258 nm were considered as AME (Fig. 2). The putative AME of A. alternata was extracted and eluted from the silica gel, and checked by HPLC, gave the same retention time authentic AME, at 3.7 min, under the same conditions, ensuring the identity of sample as AME. The structure of the purified sample was verified by LC–MS/MS analysis. From the total ion chromatogram, the major fraction of the parent molecule was recorded at 15.8 min, with 272.2 m/z that was identical to the authentic AME (Fig. 2). The parent AME molecule of A. alternata of 272.2 m/z was fragmented by collision energy 35 eV, giving molecular mass fragments 154.9 and 214.147 m/z, that was identical to the molecular fragmentation pattern of the authentic AME. From the LC–MS, and MS–MS, the molecular mass of the parent AME molecule was 272.2 m/z, with the typical pattern of molecular fragmentation of the authentic AME, ensuring the molecular identity of the target compound of A. alternata as AME. Prior to the cytotoxic analyses, the purity of AME was rechecked by HPLC, as the area of the main target peak/ sum of area all peaks × 100. The actual yield of purified AME for the further analysis was 7.6 μg/ml, with total purity 83.8%.

Biological activities of the purified A. alternata AME

The antiproliferative activity of the purified AME of A. alternata was assessed towards various cell lines; HepG-2, MCF-7, and HCT-116, compared to the normal OEC cells. From the cellular viability, the cellular growth was suppressed in a concentration-dependent manner with AME concentration. From the IC50 values (Fig. 3A), the highest cytotoxic activity of the purified AME was reported for the HCT-116 cells (0.61 μg/ml), followed by HepG-2 cells (1.72 μg/ml) and MCF-7 cells (2.41 μg/ml), compared to OEC cells (10.4 μg/ml). Overall, the purified AME had a marked higher cytotoxic activity toward the tested cancer cell lines than staurosporine, used as authentic anticancer drug. The purified AME had the maximum selectivity indices towards HCT-116 (17.1 folds), followed by HepG-2 (6.4 folds) and MCF-7 (4.3 folds), compared to the normal OEC cells (Fig. 3B). However, the selectivity indices of staurosporine towards HCT-116, MCF-7 and HepG-2 were 26.4, 8.5 and 3.6 folds, compared to the normal cells. Overall, the antiproliferative activity and selectivity of the purified AME was apparently quietly similar for the HepG-2 and MCF-7 cells. The activity of purified AME of A. alternata to inhibit tubulin polymerization was assessed. The tubulin polymerization reaction was amended with different AME concentrations, incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, compared to combretastatin A-4, and the tubulin polymerization was assessed. From the results (Fig. 3C), the IC50 values of the anti-tubulin polymerizing activity of AME was 3.9 μg/ml, compared to combretastatin A-4 (CA4) (1.96 μg/ml). Thus from the IC50 value, the anti-tubulin activity of combretastatin A-4 was approximately two folds higher than A. alternata AME.

The inhibitory effect of AME of A. alternata to topoisomerase I, II was assessed, as highly active enzymes in tumor cells for relaxing the supercoiled DNA during the multiple divisions. The reaction of Topoisomerase I and II with supercoiled DNA were amended with the AME concentrations, and the relaxed DNA was estimated by agarose-gel electrophoresis. From the kinetics of the enzymes inhibition (Fig. 3D), the IC50 values of A. alternata AME for inhibition of topoisomerase I and II were 40.9 μg/ml and 35.6 μg/ml, compared to camptothecin (26.8 μg/ml) and Etoposide (60.1 μg/ml), respectively. Thus, the affinity of A. alternata AME for binding with topoisomerase II was about 1.2 folds higher than topoisomerase I. Practically, the A. alternata AME has a higher binding affinity to topoisomerase II by about 1.7 folds higher than Etoposide (60.1 μg/ml), as a reference drug. As well as, the affinity of A. alternata AME to bind with topoisomerase II was slightly higher than topoisomerase I by about 1.2 folds, based on their corresponding IC50 values.

The apoptotic response of the HCT-116 cells for the purified A. alternata AME was assessed by Annexin V-PI flow cytometry assay. From the results (Fig. 3E), the purified AME of A. alternata significantly induces the total apoptosis and early apoptosis of the HCT-116 cells by about 6.7 and 19.5 folds, compared to control cells, respectively. The late apoptosis and necrosis of the HCT-116 cells in response to AME was 3.25 and 3.02%, compared to 0.19% and 1.8% of the control cells, respectively. The late apoptosis and necrosis of the HCT-116 cells were induced by about 17.2 and 1.8 folds, compared to control cells (without treatment).

Molecular docking analyses

The molecular interactions of the AME with the predicted target proteins namely topoisomerase I, II and beta-tubulin were analyzed. The molecular docking analysis was conducted to inspect the binding, orientation and thermodynamic stability of AME in the catalytic pocket of topoisomerases. AME had a considerable binding affinity with the active sites of topoisomerase I by -7.3 kcal/ mol, by strong conventional hydrogen bonds. To confirm that the reliability of binding affinity, the Root Mean Deviation (RMSD) was determined to be 1.5 Å, that falls well within the established threshold of < 2.0 Å confirming the accuracy of the docking algorithm20. The molecular interactions of AME with topoisomerase I was detected at ARG508, by strong conventional hydrogen bonds of bond distance of 2.44 Å, which anchors the ligand in the binding cleft. The aromatic scaffold of AME showed an electrostatic interaction, including pi-cation and pi-anion interactions with residues ASP500 and GLU495, respectively.

The binding scores of AME with the topoisomerase II was -7.1kcal/mol, with its RMSD 1.9Å. The interaction of AME with the active sites of topoisomerase II was reported with strong conventional hydrogen bonds between the oxygen atom of AME and the LYS 676 residues, at bond distance 2.31Å. The binding interactions were stabilized by an extensive network of van der Waals with the cluster of hydrophobic and polar residues including ARG673, VAL1006, GLY1007, GLU839, ARG727, PRO724, GLU712 and PHE1003. The combination of validated energetic scores and precise bond distances provides a good evidence for the potency of AME as a lead scaffold for targeting topoisomerase 2A mediated pathway.

AME was molecularly docked within the β tubulin binding domain to examine its structural affinity and potentiality as tubulin polymerization inhibitor. A stable and thermodynamically advantageous ligand protein interaction was predicted with a binding affinity -7.0kcal/mol, and its RMSD 1.8Å. This result confirms that high geometric accuracy of docking algorithm. The scaffold is primarily anchored by a diverse set of hydrophobic interactions, particularly pi-pi stacked and pi-pi T shaped with the residue HIS229. Moreover, the ligand is sequestered within the hydrophobic pocket through several Pi-Alkyl interactions with residues VAL23, ALA233, PHE272 and PRO360, and Van der Waals forces with residues ASP26, GLU27, ARG320, SER236 and ARG369. The integration of these energetic scores and multi-type interaction patterns confirm the strong theoretical activities of AME to effectively target β tubulin, as a new platform for further experimental confirmation (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The biological drug-resistance of the tumor cells that usually emerged by genetic mutations, epigenetic alterations, and overexpression of the ATP binding cassette (ABC) transport system, especially permeability glycoprotein (p-gp) that pumps the drugs out of cells, is the major challenge of cancer chemotherapy in clinical applications57,58. So, the emergence of multidrug resistance (MDR) of tumor cells remains the major obstacle for cancer therapy57. Among the multi-factors associated with the MDR of tumor cells, the overexpression of ABC transporters, that is ubiquitous superfamily of integral membrane proteins, of seven subfamilies (ABCA to ABCG)59,60. Majority of the cancer chemotherapeutic drugs are usually targets topoisomerases (I, II) (camptothecin, and podophyllotoxin derivatives), and β-tubulin (Taxol and Epothilone, vincristine, and colchicine derivatives)57,61. In an extension to our studies on exploring and development of the chemotherapeutics derived from fungi, AME has been recognized as a multi-target agent for its higher affinity to inhibit topoisomerases and β-tubulin40. So, the objective of this study was to screening for a higher AME producing fungal isolates, bioprocess their productivity, chemical and biological characterization of this compound with the in silico molecular docking analyses.

Among the recovered the isolates from the stored wheat and barley seeds, Alternaria alternata LSR PV576354.1 was the highest AOH (3.91 μg/ml) and AME producers (4.26 μg/ml), as quantified by TLC and HPLC. The nutritional requirements of A. alternata to maximize its productivity of AOH and AME were assessed by Plackett–Burman design. The maximum actual (10.05 µg/ml) and predicted (9.35 µg/ml) yields of AME by A. alternata was reported at run# 13. Also, at run # 13, the highest actual and predicted productivity of AOH was 8.65 and 8.39 µg/ml, respectively. Noticeably, the yield of AME by A. alternata was slightly higher than AOH, for all the tested runs. The maximum yield of AME and AOH by A. alternata was obtained by growing on medium of glucose (15 g/l), peptone (2 g/L), tryptophan (5 g/l) at temperature 28 °C, pH 5.0, and 18 days incubation. Thus, upon nutritional bioprocessing by Plackett–Burman design, the yield of AME and AOH by A. alternata was increased by about 2.4 folds, compared to control medium. Practically, the yield of AME by A. alternata was higher than those of Cladosporium cladosporioides (0.7 μg/ml)40, by about 14.3 folds. Consistently, the highest AOH and AME productivity by A. alternata DSM12633 was reported at pH 5.562. The yield of AME by A. alternata LSR PV576354.1, was 10.2 μg/ml (10.2 mg/l), that practically higher than those of A. alternata EFBL-025 (550 μg/L), i.e. by about 18.5 increment folds64. The yield of AOH and AME by A. alternata LSR was comparable to those reported by the different isolates of A. alternata63–65. The highest yield of AOH and AME by A. alternata was obtained C:N ratio 14.2:1, that collectively from the total carbon and nitrogen at the optimum conditions, that was consistently with those reported for AOH and AME production by A. alternata62. Similarly, the highest AOH and AME production by A. alternata was reported at pH 5.0 and incubation temperature 28 °C63. The higher yield of AOH and AME by A. alternata at neutral pH, could be due to the activation the transcriptional factor PacC, as the pH-activated transcription factor that regulates the expression of various secondary metabolites encoding genes. Similarly, the biosynthesis of fumonisin by F. verticillioides 66 and sterigmacystin of A. nidulans 67 were maximally produced at neutral pH, with the relation to the PacC expression. The species of Alternaria were reported to produce a plethora of host-specific and non-host-specific mycotoxins40.

For the higher productivity of AME by A. alternata, AME was selected for further chemical verification by the LC–MS/MS analysis. The AME spots of TLC silica gel, corresponding to the authentic AME were considered. From the LC–MS/MS chromatogram, the parent molecule had 272.2 m/z, with molecular mass fragments of 154.9, and 214.147 m/z, that identical to the molecular mass and fragmentation pattern of authentic, as consistent with those reported for AME by Alternaria species63,64. Thus, from the molecular mass of the parent molecule and fragmentation pattern, the identity of the compound was verified as AME.

The antiproliferative activity of A. alternata AME as assessed for the HepG-2, MCF-7, and HCT-116, compared to the normal OEC cell lines. The highest cytotoxic activity of AME was reported for HCT-116 (0.61 μg/ml), HepG-2 (1.72 μg/ml), MCF-7 cells (2.41 μg/ml), with selectivity indices 17.1, 6.4, 4.3 folds, compared to normal OEC cells. The cytotoxic activity of the purified A. alternata AME was consistent with AME of C. cladosporioides40, ensuring the proximity of the chemical structure. The purified AME of A. alternata had a strong anti-tubulin polymerizing activity (IC50 value 3.9 μg/ml), significant inhibitory effect for topoisomerase I (IC50 value 40.9 μg/ml) and II (IC50 value 35.6 μg/ml). So, the affinity of AME to binds with topoisomerase II was 1.2 folds higher than topoisomerase I. Topoisomerases has been recognized as crucial target enzymes in cancer therapy for their essential role in controlling of the topological relaxation, transcription and replication of DNA, and the inhibition of these enzymes leads to DNA strand breaks, cell cycle arrest, and cell death68. However, conventional topoisomerase inhibitors such as camptothecin and etoposide are often associated with toxicity and drug resistance. Consequently, exploring of novel compounds such as AOH and AME may provide safer and more effective therapeutic alternatives69,70. Similar results of antitubulin polymerizing activity and inhibition to topoisomerase II by AME was reported for C. cladosporioides40. The AME of A. alternata strongly induces the total, early apoptosis, late apoptosis and necrosis of the HCT-116 cells by 6.7, 19.5, 17.2 and 1.8 folds, compared to the control cells. The cytotoxic activity of the AOH and AME could be attributed to their simplicity to hydroxylation by CYP450 enzymes, as substrate phase 1 transformation, generating monohydroxylated AOH and AME catecholic structures, inducing ROS formation70–72. The cytotoxic activity of AOH was apparently higher than AME due to the formation of four monohydroxylated AOH products, generating more ROS, intercalating with the DNA topoisomerases73–76, however, AME for methylation of OH groups, is chemically stable, with lower susceptibility to oxidation and photo-degradation and pH stability70, that gave this compound a more privilege in clinical trials. The O-methylation of AME protects the compound from the degradation at acidic pH, with better photochemical stability, since the extracellular microenvironment of the tumor cells are usually pH acidic77,78. Also, the cytotoxicity of AOH and AME has been associated with the dys-regulation of the mitochondrial pathway inducing the apoptotic cell death, arresting the cell cycle at G2/M-phase74,75. Consistently, the cytotoxicity and mutagenicity of AME causing DNA stranded break and cell cycle arrest were frequently reported40,71. AOH and AME have a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect on HepG2 and Caco-2 cell lines72,74, inducing the apoptosis via mitochondria-dependent mechanism by p53 activation and mitochondrial permeability transition pore75.

The experimental results of AME interactions with the topoisomerase I, II and β-tubulin were confirmed from the in silico molecular docking analyses. As revealed from the binding energies, AME had a substantial affinity to bind with the active sites of topoisomerase I (-7.3 kcal/ mol) and II (-7.1kcal/mol) by strong conventional hydrogen bonds, with RMSD values 1.5 Å and 1.9Å, respectively. Experimentally, the affinity of AME for topoisomerase II is higher than I, that partially consistent with the molecular docking analyses. However, the slight inconsistent of the experimental and in silico analyses, were not significant, ensuring the higher affinity of AME to bind with topoisomerase I and II. The dual activity of AME to bind with topoisomerase I and II being a feasible therapeutic criterion that enhance the activity of the compound in vivo towards both of the enzymes simultaneously. As well as, the molecular interaction of AME with the β-tubulin was assessed by the docking analysis, with binding scores -7.0kcal/mol, and RMSD 1.8Å, ensuring the affinity of AME to inhibits the tubulin polymerization. Coincident results were reported for AME of C. cladosporioides40. Similar results were reported for pomalidomide-based PROTACs for targeting EGFRWT and EGFRT790M80–86. Thus, from the experimental results and in silico analyses, AME obviously has a substantial affinity to inhibit topoisomerases I, II, in addition to tubulin polymerization, that reinforce practical efficiency as a multi-target compound. This multi-target strategy advocate the simultaneous dual inhibition of the microtubule dynamics and DNA replication, thereby overcoming limitations associated with single-target drugs79–81. The multi-target properties AOH and AME, affecting several key cellular pathways involved in cancer development. Their combined actions on tubulin interaction, apoptosis induction, topoisomerase inhibition, with higher selective cytotoxicity enhance their potential as anticancer agents.

In conclusion, A. alternata LSR PV576354.1 isolated from stored wheat seeds was the highest AOH (3.91 μg/ml) and AME producers (4.26 μg/ml), and upon nutritional bioprocessing, their yields were increased by ~ 2.4 folds. The chemical structure of the purified AME was resolved from the LC–MS/MS analysis, with molecule mass 272.2 m/z, and same fragments of the authentic one. The highest cytotoxic activity of AME was reported for HCT-116, HepG-2, MCF-7 cells, with selectivity indices 17.1, 6.4, 4.3 folds, compared to normal OEC cells. The purified AME had a strong inhibitory effect to topoisomerase II by 1.2 folds higher than topoisomerase I, with a considerable anti-tubulin polymerizing activity. The AME of A. alternata induces the total, early apoptosis, late apoptosis and necrosis of the HCT-116 cells by 6.7, 19.5, 17.2 and 1.8 folds, compared to the control cells. From the molecular docking analysis, the purified AME has lower binding energies to the active sites of topoisomerase I, II and β-tubulin, ensuring the experimental results for inhibiting the activities of these enzymes. Thus, from the experimental and in silico analyses, AME has a substantial affinity to inhibit topoisomerases I, II, in addition to anti-tubulin polymerization, that reinforce their efficiency as platform for a multi-target compound for further optimization and preclinical evaluation.
