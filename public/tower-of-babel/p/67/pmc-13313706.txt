Unveiling Sunderland Zero Injury in a Patient with Brachial Plexus Trauma
Authors: Shay N. Nguyen, Aditya N. Joshi, Leslie H. Lee, Jaime R. López, Catherine M. Curtin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Peripheral nerve injuries can profoundly impact patients’ functionality and quality of life. Classification systems such as those proposed by Seddon and Sunderland have provided valuable frameworks for understanding the nature and severity of nerve injuries. However, recent emerging evidence has shed light on an underrecognized category of nerve injury. This article provides a review of nerve injury classification systems and presents information on the Sunderland zero injury, a milder category of nerve injury characterized by immediate recovery upon decompression. We present a focused Sunderland zero literature review and a case report of a 26-year-old with a brachial plexus injury who developed secondary proximal median nerve compression. Intraoperative somatosensory-evoked potentials and motor-evoked potentials were obtained immediately before and after surgical decompression to dynamically assess nerve function. Preoperatively, the patient demonstrated median nerve motor deficits despite interval recovery from the brachial plexus injury. Immediately following decompression at the level of the lacertus fibrosus, increased motor-evoked potential amplitude and improved somatosensory-evoked potential waveform quality were demonstrated. Early postoperative examination confirmed functional improvement without the expected temporal profile of axonal regeneration. Sunderland zero represents a reversible ischemic conduction block without demyelination or axonal loss. Recognition of this nerve injury category is critical, as timely intervention may result in immediate clinical improvement and meaningful functional recovery and improved patient outcomes. Further research is needed to refine diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to identify Sunderland zero injuries.

Peripheral nerve injuries (PNIs) are common and impact patients’ quality of life. Seddon and Sunderland established the frameworks for assessing nerve injury severity and prognosis and guiding treatment.1,2 These systems delineate various grades of injury, from neurapraxia to axonal damage.

Recent observations have identified a subset of patients with focal nerve-related pain and weakness with immediate recovery following decompression, findings not adequately explained by traditional neurapraxia or axonal injury.3 This phenomenon has been termed Sunderland zero and is thought to result from nerve compression-induced ischemia without structural nerve damage. It poses unique diagnostic challenges because standard electrodiagnostic findings are normal. Diagnosis requires other modalities such as detailed examination and intraoperative neurophysiologic monitoring. Recognition allows for appropriate treatment and recovery following restoration of the neural blood flow.

This report aimed to contribute to our understanding of Sunderland zero injury by presenting a clinical case of brachial plexus injury complicated by proximal median nerve compression, a double-crush syndrome previously described by Upton and McComas.4 This article highlights the importance of recognizing and addressing Sunderland zero injuries for optimized patient care.

CASE REPORT

Patient 1 is a 26-year-old man with a brachial plexus injury from a gunshot wound. Electrodiagnostic studies at 3 months postinjury revealed right lower trunk brachial plexopathy with ongoing motor denervation. By 6 months, the patient exhibited partial recovery but persistent tenderness over the proximal forearm and weakness localized to median nerve–innervated muscles, indicating a potential secondary crush injury due to swelling.

Release of proximal median nerve at the level of the lacertus fibrosis was indicated. Intraoperative electrodiagnostic tests were performed using a Cadwell Cascade Elite system.

For motor-evoked potential (MEP) recording, 13-mm subdermal needles were placed intramuscularly. Using standard scalp electrode montages, transcranial electrical stimulation was performed at 180–360 V, with a train of 7–9 pulses, interstimulus intervals of 1.5 or 2.5 ms, and a pulse width of 75 μs. Somatosensory-evoked potentials (SSEPs) were recorded using a standard montage (Erb’s point, inion, and C3′), averaged over 200 sweeps. Square wave pulses with a width of 0.2 ms were delivered at a rate of 4.13 Hz, and stimulation intensity was increased until reproducible waveforms were obtained (50 mA).

Two months postoperatively, the patient reported improved grip strength and was able to flex his thumb interphalangeal joint against gravity (grade 3 out of 5).

DISCUSSION

Seddon categorized nerve injuries into neurapraxia, axonotmesis, and neurotmesis.1 Sunderland introduced a more comprehensive classification system, consisting of 5 categories and recently a sixth category encompassing mixed axonal and conduction losses.2 Recent clinical observations have led to the recognition of another category of nerve injury.

Lundborg recognized the importance of low pressure on nerves, which reduces venular intraneural flow, causing nerve dysfunction. The authors described subtypes of neurapraxia, with a milder form being associated with a transient ischemia without focal demyelination.5 This subclassification, although initially overlooked, has gained recognition, with patients having immediate recovery after nerve decompression. This milder dynamic nerve injury is classified as Sunderland zero.

Sunderland zero is characterized by (1) focal nerve-related weakness, numbness or point tenderness at the compression site; (2) transient ischemia in the absence of nerve demyelination or axonal disruption; (3) normal electrodiagnostic studies; and (4) immediate physiologic and/or clinical recovery following decompression.

Standard nerve conduction studies, including compound muscle action potential and nerve conduction velocity, are often used to aid in the diagnosis of PNI.6 They primarily detect demyelination or axonal loss. Thus, low grade peripheral nerve ischemia without demyelination or axonal injury (Sunderland zero) may fall below the threshold of detectability of compound muscle action potential and nerve conduction velocity.4 In contrast, SSEPs and MEPs provide dynamic, real-time assessment and demonstrated immediate improvement after decompression.

In this case, immediate postoperative improvements of SSEPs and MEPs were observed immediately after decompression. This immediate nerve improvement is consistent with emerging literature on these dynamic compressions. Peters et al3 reported a case of complete foot drop with normal electrodiagnostic studies and symptoms immediately resolved after decompression. Azócar et al assessed 24 patients with lacertus syndrome. Decompression at the elbow resulted in an immediate increase strength of flexor digitorum profundus of the index finger and FPL.7 Hagert observed 44 patients with nerve entrapments at the lacertus fibrosus who reported perioperative return of strength in median innervated muscles after decompression.8 Salengros et al also reported two cases of immediate intraoperative SSEP changes after a brachial plexus and a femoral cutaneous nerve decompression.9 These immediate nerve and functional recoveries are not expected for Sunderland grade I–IV injuries and point to a milder nerve injury.

This case shares conceptual overlap with posttraumatic distal nerve compression and the double-crush hypothesis described by Upton and McComas.4 Similarly, Nassif and Steiger demonstrated the “double crush syndrome” hypothesis with rare cases of immediate recovery after distal nerve decompressions.10 This case of a patient with a brachial plexus injury with immediate nerve function improvement after release suggests that the second site of compression (proximal median nerve) would be a Sunderland zero injury, a distinct dynamic pathophysiologic mechanism with reversible ischemia and the hallmark feature of immediate recovery, distinguishing it from classic neurapraxia or axonal injury.

Prolonged and persistent nerve compression can progress to epineural and perineural thickening, leading to demyelination and even axonal injury. An international survey on PNIs reported that the overall nerve injury outcomes were linked to Sunderland classification.5 Recognition of Sunderland zero and early intervention can alleviate patient discomfort and prevent potential progression to more severe nerve damage.

CONCLUSIONS

Sunderland zero injury represents a paradigm shift in our understanding of nerve injuries, emphasizing the dynamic nature of nerve pathology. Further research is needed to develop better diagnostic testing for optimal management. Clinicians should remain vigilant for Sunderland zero injury, as timely intervention can lead to favorable outcomes and improved quality of life.
