Urbanization and Suspended Sediment Transport Dynamics: A Comparative Study of Watersheds with Varying Degree of Urbanization Using Concentration-Discharge Hysteresis
Authors: Suffiyan Safdar, Anne J Jefferson, David M Costello, Andrew Blinn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuspended sediment is a critical water quality parameter and an indicator of geomorphic processes, but suspended sediment dynamics in urban streams may not conform to the first-flush model widely used for other pollutants. We analyzed discharge and turbidity data for 367 events from three urban watersheds (impervious cover 16–45%) in Cleveland, Ohio (USA). Less intensely urbanized watersheds exhibit higher turbidity compared to that of the most highly urbanized watershed. Proportionally, more counterclockwise hysteresis is observed in the two less urbanized watersheds, and more clockwise hysteresis occurs in the highly urbanized watershed. However, hysteresis patterns are driven by different mechanisms in each watershed, and geomorphic analysis was critical to identifying the underlying mechanisms. In the least urbanized watershed, spatial rainfall variability controls sediment hysteresis. In the intermediate watershed, the erosion of upstream weathered shale banks during dry periods plays a significant role in the sediment supply and shaping hysteresis. In the most urbanized watershed, high eroding banks in downstream reaches lead to more frequent clockwise hysteresis. Overall, we suggest that as the impervious surfaces increase, the availability of instream sediments (bed and banks) plays an increased role in suspended sediment dynamics, and geomorphology remains essential for guiding management decisions.Keywords: hysteresis, suspended sediment, first-flush, urbanization, geomorphology, erosion

Introduction

Urbanization transforms the landscape through soil sealing by impervious surfaces and the creation of efficient urban drainage systems, which in turn alters the hydrology, geomorphology, and ecology of urban streams.1−3 Consequently, elevated levels of suspended sediment and pollutants have become a challenge for urban watershed managers, with the scientific community recognizing the degradation of urban stream health as “urban stream syndrome”.4

Suspended sediment is a critical parameter in the evaluation of water quality in rivers and other connected waterbodies. Excessive flux of suspended sediments has deleterious effects on river ecosystems, including degradation of stream habitat.5,6 In contrast, a lack of suspended sediment flux results in “hungry water”, which can lead to bank erosion, channel widening and incision, and modification of the riverine habitat.7−9 Although alteration in sediment regime and geomorphology are among the primary symptoms of urban stream syndrome10 and have been documented in earlier studies,11,12 research on sediment transport in urban watersheds remains limited. A significant portion of the limited studies of sediment in urban watersheds13−16 has investigated the issue of increased suspended sediment yield, first documented by Wolman.13 The understanding of the sources, timing, mechanism, and pathways of fine sediment delivery in built-out urban watersheds remains underexplored.

Identifying sources, pathways, and drivers of temporal and spatial variability in suspended sediment is challenging due to complex nonlinear transport dynamics.17 Sediment fingerprinting has been used to determine sources of sediments in different watersheds, including mountainous,18−20 agricultural,21−24 and urban environments.25−28 Sediment fingerprinting in a small urban catchment demonstrated that both paved and unpaved areas, as well as stream channel, can make substantial contributions to the total sediment flux.27 However, application of sediment fingerprinting in urban area is challenging due to the diverging methodologies and uncertainty in tracer conservation because of human interference and diverse land use.29 A review of existing literature and public data repositories (e.g., US Geological Survey) revealed a scarcity of urban stream suspended sediment data. Therefore, the use of high frequency discharge and turbidity data can be valuable in overcoming the challenges associated with nonlinear transport dynamics to advance our understanding of the underlying mechanism of suspended sediment supply and transport.

Turbidity is a commonly used proxy for suspended sediment concentration.29−33 The relationship between turbidity and discharge is usually asynchronous in time, leading to the formation of hysteresis patterns.34 Size and direction of the hysteresis loop have been used to interpret potential sources and pathways of suspended sediments and mechanism of suspended sediment transport.35−37 The classification of turbidity-discharge hysteresis was first proposed by Williams,38 who identified five patterns. A recent study,39 has expanded this classification to 14 patterns. Despite the increased number of hysteresis patterns, the most commonly used are (i) clockwise, (ii) counterclockwise, (iii) figure-8, and (iv) complex or no hysteresis (Figure S1). Clockwise hysteresis is observed when the peak turbidity occurs before the peak discharge, with the turbidity remain lower during the falling limb of hydrograph, indicating proximal sediment sources.40−42 Conversely, counterclockwise hysteresis indicates distal sediment sourcing, with turbidity peak occurring after the discharge peak.43,44 Figure-8 hysteresis represents contribution from both distal and proximal sources.45 Finally, complex or no hysteresis pattern is difficult to interpret in terms of system dynamics, as it may indicate the interplay of several factors.

While many studies45,46 have investigated turbidity-discharge hysteresis and made conclusions based on comparison between urban and nonurban watersheds, to our knowledge, there has been no work to understand how the hysteresis patterns and key controls of sediment transport vary between urban streams within a region. The first-flush model has been a commonly used conceptual model, which suggests that sediment is transported during the rising limb of hydrograph, resulting in a clockwise hysteresis.47,48 The first-flush model was confirmed in a Polish urban catchment, where all 12 events measured showed a clockwise hysteresis pattern.49 However, a competing model was proposed in a recent study along the gradient from rural to urban watersheds,45 where a shift from proximal to distal watershed sediment sources due to increased hydrological and structural connectivity is inferred a change from clockwise to counterclockwise hysteresis. Structural connectivity is a representation of watershed sediment source distribution and the transport potential from these sources to the river channel,50,51 while hydrological connectivity is governed by the linkage between runoff and receiving water.52 However, it remains unclear whether the patterns of sediment transport proposed by previous studies are generalizable across urbanized watersheds and importantly what role geomorphology plays in driving the turbidity-discharge hysteresis and suspended sediment transport in urban watersheds.

In this study, we examine the controls on urban stream suspended sediment transport by analyzing high frequency turbidity-discharge data from three urban watersheds with varying levels of imperviousness within the same geographical region. The main objectives of this study were (i) to investigate how turbidity-discharge hysteresis patterns change with impervious cover and sediment source configuration, (ii) to understand the hydrometeorological factors that influence suspended sediment response and hysteresis pattern across space (regional, watershed) and time (event), and (iii) to propose a conceptual framework of suspended sediment transport in urban watersheds, with an aim of informing management strategies and future research.

Methods

Study Area and Data

The three study watersheds are located within the Cleveland, Ohio (USA) metropolitan area along a gradient of impervious cover (Table 1, Figure 1). The climate is humid continental, with an average annual temperature of 9.35 °C53 and an average annual precipitation of 1248 mm during the 2018–2021 water years. All three watersheds have a mixed land use, incorporating both residential and commercial.

The highly urbanized Mill Creek watershed (44.8 km2) is predominantly covered with till 77% and has alluvial streambeds (Table 1). The low urbanized Chippewa Creek and moderately urbanized West Creek exhibit mixed bedrock-alluvial streambeds and have multiple small knickpoints formed in shale and sandstone.

We analyzed turbidity, discharge, and precipitation data over a period of three years from September 2018 to 2021. Turbidity data were collected using in situ turbidimeters (Turner Designs) integrated with a Cyclops-7 logger (Precision Measurement Engineering, Inc.) and recording data every 10 min. Sensors were deployed within 1000 m of USGS gages, with locations selected to ensure stable cross section and homogeneous flow conditions.

Instantaneous discharge from USGS stream gages was initially retrieved at 5 min intervals using dataRetrieval package v.2.7.1256 in R 4.2.2.57 The timestamp of instantaneous discharge was harmonized with the timestamp of turbidity data, leading to down sampling of discharge data to 10 min. Precipitation data at a frequency of 5 min were obtained from eight rain gauges operated by NEORSD (Figure 1).

Event Identification and Hysteresis Classification

Storm event identification was carried out using a semiautomated approach (See Supporting Information section 1.1 for details). Multipeak events were split at the local minimum when the maximum discharge of first peak dropped by >70% and the subsequent peak rose at least 300% from local minimum (Figure S2a). This step was deemed necessary as it allowed for the analysis of hysteresis patterns and calculation of hysteresis index while avoiding the misclassification of multipeak events as complex hysteresis.

Manual and index-based classification methods were used to classify the hysteresis pattern for each delineated event. We used tidyverse package v2.0.058 in R to plot turbidity-discharge loops to visually classify hysteresis patterns (Figure S1). The hysteresis index (HI),59 was used for index-based classification, with event HI calculated as the mean of the differences in normalized turbidity at every 1% increment of normalized discharge i.e., i = 0.01, 0.02, 0.03...,1 (eq 1). HI ranged from −1 to +1, where negative values indicated counterclockwise loop and positive values indicated a clockwise loop. The index magnitude represented the strength of the hysteresis pattern (i.e., a more pronounced loop).

Statistical Analysis

We conducted statistical analysis at the regional, watershed, and event-hysteresis scales (i.e., events of the same manually classified hysteresis shape) to explore the scale dependency of potential explanatory variables on suspended sediment transport. Partial least-squares regression (PLSR) and classification and regression trees (CART) were performed using JMP Pro 15 statistical software.60

We used flow weighted total turbidity (TQ) as the response variable to analyze sediment supply and the hysteresis index to understand variability in hysteresis patterns. TQ was selected to remove the potential autocorrelation with hydrological parameters,61 while HI served as a quantitative proxy of hysteresis patterns. A total of 19 explanatory variables were computed to encompass runoff, precipitation, and antecedent characteristics (Table 2).

PLSR was used to reduce dimensionality and to overcome the issue of multicollinearity and overfitting.62,63 We selected the most significant component that explained the highest variance in the response variable. Potential explanatory variables were determined based on variable importance for the projection (VIP) value (VIP ≥ 0.8).64 The assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals for linear model were satisfied.

CART is a nonparametric supervised recursive partitioning technique that creates a decision tree based on the relationship between explanatory variables and response variable.65−68 We used CART to build trees explaining categorical hysteresis classification from the response variables (Table 2). We built trees both at the regional scale and separately for each watershed. Data sets including all hysteresis types often generated poorly fit trees, and thus, we also built trees that discriminated between single loop and figure-8/complex hysteresis. Additionally, we divided the data among single loop, complex, and figure-8 events and built separate trees for each pairwise combination of hysteresis types. After the initial exploration, we adopted the assumption that a model explaining at least 70% of the variance indicated a significant separation between hysteresis classes.

Assessment of Sediment Source Areas

We performed a qualitative assessment of the streams’ geomorphic characteristics at 22 discrete 100 m reaches (Figure 5). Characteristics of bank slope, height, material, and armoring were assessed to indicate the presence and severity of bank erosion. For each reach, grain size distribution of the bed material was characterized via the Wolman pebble count69 using existing data sets where available.70 For each stream, 1500 m upstream of the sensor location was mapped by walking in the stream to estimate the percentage of bedrock exposed on the streambed, while the cumulative bank height was calculated using a 2020 DEM (1 m × 1 m) from Ohio geographically referenced information program71 and by applying a 30 m buffer from the stream center. The average channel widening was calculated using orthorectified aerial imagery from the GIS portal of Cuyahoga County72 to support in field bank erosion assessments conducted in early 2023. At surveyed reaches, the difference in channel width between an aerial image from the start (Fall 2019) and one from the end (Fall 2022) of the turbidity record was computed using the measure tool in QGIS v3.28.7.73 At each reach, the width was measured at five cross sections and then averaged.

To guide the reaches selection, we estimated stream power at the watershed scale using the ArcGIS toolbox Stream Power Index for Networks (SPIN).74 With inputs of a digital elevation model (DEM), land cover and imperviousness, the toolbox incorporates a modified discharge relationship Qu2 = cAdIe to account for the increase in discharge due to urbanization, where A is the drainage area (km2). The land cover and impervious percentage cover (I) is extracted from 2019 NLCD,54 while c, d, and e are the coefficient derived from Bledsoe & Watson.75

Results

Suspended Sediment and Hysteresis Variability

During the study period, highly urbanized Mill Creek had 10% more impervious cover but produced a similar mean specific discharge (0.07 m3/s/km2) as moderately West Creek (0.08 m3/s/km2), likely due to the presence of a combined sewer system in the Mill Creek watershed that attenuated the hydrological response. Chippewa Creek had the lowest mean specific discharge (0.05 m3/s/km2), possibly due to greater evapotranspiration in the less urbanized watershed. Based on multiyear USGS records, the highest peak flow recorded in Chippewa, West, and Mill in this study’s storm data set had an annual exceedance probability of 80, 28, and 4.5%, respectively.

The relationship between turbidity and specific discharge is similar for Chippewa and West Creek with regression slopes of 0.87 and 0.95, respectively, while Mill has a slope of 0.62 (Figure S3). In general, Chippewa and West showed higher turbidity in high flows compared to Mill. This is supported by the normalized peak turbidity per unit peak specific discharge, which yielded values of 1117 and 1071 NTU/m3/s/km2 for Chippewa and West Creek, respectively, and a lower value of 628 NTU/m3/s/km2 for Mill Creek.

A total of 367 storm events were delineated, with 92, 180, and 95 events in Chippewa, West, and Mill Creek, respectively. In all three watersheds, more than 50% of the events produced complex and figure-8 hysteresis patterns (Figure 2). West Creek produced the highest percentage of complex hysteresis events (31.1%, n = 56), and Mill Creek had the highest percent of figure-8 hysteresis events (38.9%, n = 37). There were 4 events in Chippewa Creek and 6 events in West Creek that showed a linear turbidity response without a hysteresis effect, while no such events occurred in Mill Creek.

Percentage distribution of each hysteresis pattern (top) and frequency distribution of the hysteresis index (bottom). The blue vertical dashed line represents the median HI value. CW: clockwise (+ve HI) and C–CW: counterclockwise (−ve HI).

Single loop hysteresis (i.e., clockwise and counterclockwise) for Chippewa and West Creek showed a similar distribution with a higher frequency of counterclockwise hysteresis, while Mill Creek produced a higher frequency of clockwise hysteresis. Overall, there was a decrease in the percentage of counterclockwise hysteresis with urbanization (Figure 2). The median HI values for each stream reflected the differences in the occurrence of single loop hysteresis, with Chippewa and West Creek having negative values of −0.06 and −0.02, respectively, while Mill Creek had a positive value of 0.07 (Figure 2).

Temporal sequencing of hysteresis showed no significant patterns, indicating that there is no consistent influence of preceding hysteresis on the occurrence of subsequent hysteresis. Similarly, no strong impact of seasonality was observed on the occurrence of hysteresis in any of the watersheds (Figure S13). There were some shifts in the distribution of single loop versus complex or figure-8 hysteresis patterns following the largest flow events during the study period for West and Mill Creek (Figure S14).

Partial Least-Squares Regression (PLSR)

Regional scale

At regional scale, PLSR explained 21.3% of variance in TQ but showed a poor statistical relationship with HI (Figure 3a&d). Rainfall duration, intensity, and spatial variability in upstream and downstream rainfall were important features in the TQ model, and a higher contribution of upstream convective rainfall increases the total suspended sediment response. Discharge, rainfall intensity, and lag time between rainfall centroid and peak turbidity/discharge were important features in HI model but collectively explained only 6.8% of the variance.

Watershed Scale

At watershed scale, the regression models for TQ explained 16.4, 24.5, and 27.4% of the variance in Chippewa, West, and Mill Creek, respectively, but with different explanatory variables and directionalities (Figure 3b). In Chippewa Creek, high rainfall intensity with a lower response time to peak discharge increased the suspended sediment response. In West and Mill Creek, rainfall in the preceding 3–7 days leads to a decrease in the suspended sediment response; however, short duration intense rainfall results in an increase in suspended sediment response in West, unlike Mill. The percentage of variance explained for TQ for each watershed remained within ±6% to the regional scale.

The statistical power of predicting hysteresis pattern increased at the watershed scale relative to the regional scale, but differences in model parameters and performance across watersheds underscored the dynamic nature of the hysteresis. In Chippewa Creek, rainfall spatial variability and intensity explained 18% of the variance, while in Mill Creek, discharge and rainfall intensity explained 15.6% of the variance in hysteresis index (Figure 3e). The regression model performed poorly in predicting HI for West (7.9%) compared to Chippewa and Mill Creek.

Event-Hysteresis Scale

For events with clockwise hysteresis, models were primarily loaded with rainfall characteristics, which explained 35 and 48.7% of the variance in TQ in West and Mill Creek, respectively, but with different directionalities (Figure 3c). In Mill Creek, sediment load increases with lower antecedent rainfall, but this surge is attenuated by increased runoff, suggesting supply limitation, unlike West Creek where sediment load increases with rainfall intensity. In Chippewa Creek, higher upstream rainfall increases suspended sediment response.

For counterclockwise events, the model explained 72% of the variance in TQ in Mill Creek but <28% of variation in other streams, with the weakest model for West Creek (14.5%) (Figure 3c). As with clockwise events, for counterclockwise events in Mill Creek, the sediment load decreases with increased runoff and rainfall intensity, in contrast to West Creek. In Chippewa Creek, a short duration upstream rainfall increases suspended sediment load.

For figure-8 and complex hysteresis model in West Creek, a higher variance in TQ is explained compared to the West Creek model for counterclockwise hysteresis (Figure 3c). The model indicates an increase in the suspended sediment load in West Creek with an increase in rainfall intensity and less antecedent precipitation. However, figure-8 and complex hysteresis events in Mill Creek exhibited decreased TQ with increased runoff (>40% variance explained), which was similar to single loop events.

Predictive Modeling: Classification and Regression Trees (CART)

At a regional scale, the CART produced weak classification (R2 ≤ 0.14) of hysteresis patterns (Figures S4–S5), which supports our finding that the occurrence of hysteresis pattern is influenced by the specific characteristics of each watershed. However, at the watershed scale, predictive models were still not strong (R2 ≤ 0.4) when all four hysteresis patterns were considered simultaneously (Figures S6–S8). Examination of event types pairwise revealed weak models (R2 ≤ 0.36) when complex or figure-8 hysteresis was included (Figures S9–S11).

Strong classifications (R2 > 0.7) were identified for single loop hysteresis (clockwise vs counterclockwise) in Chippewa and Mill Creek (Figure 4). The occurrence of clockwise and counterclockwise hysteresis in Chippewa Creek is related to the upstream–downstream variability of rainfall in the watershed (Figure 4a), and in Mill Creek, it is related to rainfall intensity (I5) and preceding three-day rainfall (P3day) (Figure 4b). For Chippewa Creek, the thresholds for intensity and depth were both near 0 and misclassified events were all close to the origin (Figure 4a), further supporting this strong model. Similarly, in Mill Creek, misclassified events were all near the thresholds (Figure 4b). The best model for West Creek (R2 = 0.4) was weaker than those identified for Chippewa and Mill Creek, similar to the PLSR HI models at the watershed scale.

Stream Geomorphic Mapping

The shape and slope of the watershed significantly influenced the contributing drainage area and location of the highest stream power within each watershed (Figure 5). In Chippewa Creek, the highest stream power values were in the downstream-middle parts of the watershed (1500 to 6400 m; Figure 5a). A maximum value of 19,000 W/m occurred in an area with a narrow gorge and several small waterfalls. In contrast, stream power was more uniform in West Creek, with a high stream power zone throughout the middle and upstream sections of the watershed (6500 to 9500 m) (Figure 5b). The maximum stream power estimated in West Creek is 8000 W/m. In Mill Creek, the highest stream power values were concentrated in the downstream section (0 to 3500 m), below the waterfall where slope abruptly increased (Figure 5c), with a maximum estimated value of 16,000 W/m.

In Chippewa Creek, geomorphic mapping revealed a stable bed and banks in the middle section, while the upstream section exhibited high banks with evident failures compared to the most downstream section (0–1500 m) with low banks and moderate failures (Table S1). Upstream reaches were mixed bedrock-alluvial streambeds, with patches of fine sediment (sand, silt, and clay), comprising 22% of the bankfull area, primarily along the toe of banks and in pools. The upstream-most reach exhibited high bank erosion with exposed tree roots. Downstream reaches were mixed gravel and cobble bed materials, with fine sediments covering 14% of the channel. In these reaches, banks were weathered shale overlain by alluvium, and bank undercutting was observed. No bedrock was exposed at or between downstream reaches. In contrast, the middle section of Chippewa Creek had highly stable bedrock banks, with 12% fine sediments on the streambed. Chippewa Creek banks were relatively low for 2 km upstream from the monitoring station, whereas the stream had higher banks in its middle and upstream sections. Approximately 70% of the cumulative bank height was from 2 to 8 km upstream from the outlet. The aerial imagery showed no significant (≤0.2 m) widening of the channel in reaches with stable and moderate bank failure from 2019 to 2022 (Table S4).

In West Creek, geomorphic mapping indicated that upstream banks are likely a significant contributor of suspended sediments, whereas the downstream section is stable, with the streambed predominantly composed of bedrock and banks experiencing periodic failures (Table S2). The upstream three reaches in West Creek exhibited prominent undercutting and erosion of shale banks. There were very few fine sediments (3.7%) on the streambed in these upstream reaches. In contrast, the middle two reaches had naturally stable banks composed of shale and alluvium, with toe protection by boulders and logs, and higher bed coverage by fine sediments (18%). Fine deposits were primarily silt and clay found upstream of instream wood. In the downstream three reaches of West Creek, high shale banks were overlain by alluvium. The alluvium was potentially undergoing episodic slope failure due to vertical slopes and significant height. Cobble-gravel bars with patches of fine sediment were present on the otherwise bedrock streambed. Fine sediment covered 10.4% of the channel in the lower section. Cumulative bank height increased uniformly moving upstream in West Creek. Approximately 70% of the 2 km section upstream of the sensor is mapped as exposed bedrock. The aerial imagery showed no significant (<0.2 m) widening of the channel in reaches with stable and moderate bank failure from 2019 to 2022 (Table S4).

Geomorphic mapping of Mill Creek indicates that the stream bank in the lower 3.2 km is a potential source of suspended sediment, while the middle and upstream reaches are naturally stable or have been stabilized (Table S3). The upstream section of the stream exhibited stable alluvium banks, with some of the reaches buried and replaced by concrete conduits. Due to limited accessibility in the middle section as a result of urban development, only one reach (4.3 km from the monitoring station) was assessed, where we observed stable banks protected by a combination of logs and construction debris. No bedrock was exposed in either the bed or banks in the upstream and middle sections, with the upper three reaches having 15% of the bed covered with fine sediment, while the middle reach had 7% fine sediments. Immediately downstream of the knickpoint, the erosion potential was attenuated due to the presence of bedrock and banks protected by boulders. However, continuous high bank erosion was observed in the 2 km upstream of the monitoring location. Here, the banks were predominantly composed of alluvium. Approximately 50% of the cumulative bank height is found in the downstream section 3.2 km of Mill Creek. In addition, the downstream section of Mill Creek had an average of 11% of the channel covered in fine sediments, predominately silt and clay. Only 5% of the 2 km section upstream of the sensor is exposed bedrock. The aerial imagery showed significant (1.5–3.5 m) widening of the channel in downstream surveyed reaches from 2019 to 2022.

Discussion

Variability in Factors Influencing Sediment Supply in Urban Watersheds

Previous studies in urban watersheds have identified a range of potential hydrometeorological variables that contribute to dynamics of suspended sediment transport, including peak discharge, antecedent conditions, average rainfall intensity, base flow, time to peak flow, and runoff duration.45,76 In our study, the factors exhibit variability at regional, watershed, and event-hysteresis scales. However, interpreting the effects of hydrometeorology on the amount of suspended sediment transport (TQ) is difficult at the regional and watershed scales, given differences in watershed characteristics, the wide array of variables involved, and potential interactions between variables. Inability to generate a strong model at the regional scale nor consistent models at the watershed scale signifies that the mechanisms controlling sediment transport vary significantly between and within watersheds, even when subjected to similar climate forcing. Therefore, analysis at the event-hysteresis scale, similar to the approach taken in recent studies,61,77 can be valuable to understand the underlying mechanism of sediment transport in urban streams.

Our results show that high discharge produced by intense rainfall modulates a substantial amount of sediment transport in urbanized watersheds, especially if dry conditions precede the storm. Suspended sediment transport during clockwise hysteresis in the highly urbanized Mill Creek watershed is driven by rainfall intensity and peak discharge. The sediment load decreases when flow increases, potentially due to sediment exhaustion and limited sediment supply.78−80 When counterclockwise hysteresis occurred in Mill Creek, we observed that peak discharge and rainfall intensity remained explanatory variables. However, unlike clockwise hysteresis, the sediment exhaustion effect is not prominent due to the relatively low peak discharge during these events. Sediment transport during figure-8 and complex hysteresis in the moderately urbanized West Creek watershed were also largely controlled by rainfall intensity and antecedent conditions. This finding suggests that low soil moisture coupled with high rainfall intensity increases suspended sediment supply in a moderately urbanized watershed.

In the less urbanized Chippewa Creek watershed, rainfall intensity and spatial variability emerged as the primary controlling factors for the suspended sediment response. During complex hysteresis, a small duration high intensity rainfall increases suspended sediment load. This suggests that convective storms produce higher suspended sediments supply, which is similar to the observation in a semidesert watershed in northern Negev, Israel, and an urban watershed in West Yorkshire, England.81,82 For events with counterclockwise, clockwise, and figure-8 hysteresis, spatial rainfall variability with intense upstream rainfall increases suspended sediment response. This indicates that the upstream part of the watershed is active in the supply of suspended sediments, emphasizing the importance of understanding sediment source configuration.

Sediment Sources: Influence of Urbanization and Stream Geomorphology

The dominant hysteresis pattern in our urban watersheds varied, which contradicts previous studies that posit simple frameworks for explaining sediment hysteresis in urban streams. Contemplating a traditional first-flush framework, runoff from impervious surfaces produces a sediment-rich first-flush and clockwise hysteresis in an urban watershed in Warsaw.49 However, for two of the three studied watersheds, we observed a higher frequency of counterclockwise hysteresis. Recently, a study45 suggested that urban watersheds tend toward counterclockwise hysteresis due to better connectivity of distal sediment sources by headwater tributaries, road infrastructure, and drainage networks. However, in our study, the most highly urbanized watershed had the highest frequency of clockwise hysteresis, while less intensely urbanized watersheds had more frequent counterclockwise hysteresis. Therefore, our results contradict the notion that urbanization directly or uniformly modulates the hysteresis pattern and suggest that interpreting urban stream hysteresis requires consideration of geomorphology.

In the highly urbanized Mill Creek watershed, we observed the first-flush effect similar to an urban watershed in Warsaw49 but with different underlying mechanisms. Our result suggests that unstable banks exert a greater influence on hysteresis patterns than sediment connectivity or impervious cover. Combined sewers decrease the upland sediment supply from the downstream region. Despite this, clockwise hysteresis is frequently observed, indicating that sediments primarily originate from downstream banks. Our stream mapping observations support this interpretation, as we found a higher frequency of unstable banks with active erosion in the downstream reaches. In addition, the high stream power zone in the downstream section suggests an increased probability of downstream bank and bed erosion. In contrast, the upstream reaches had greater bank stability, both naturally and through restoration and channelization, leading to infrequent counterclockwise hysteresis with less sediment transport.

In the moderately urbanized West Creek watershed, despite a higher frequency of counterclockwise hysteresis, the geomorphological characteristics indicate that the stream bank and bed influence the sediment supply. The downstream streambed in West Creek is dominated by bedrock with a steep slope, which substantially reduces the sediment deposition and supply. However, much of the stream banks in the watershed are composed of shale, which undergoes active erosion and creates a potential sediment source. Dry shale is more susceptible to erosion due to decreased tensile strength, leading to a drying and wetting cycle that modulates shale erosion rate.83 Consequently, we observed a significant influence of the antecedent conditions in West Creek. We suggest that when there is no rainfall for at least 7 days, dry and weathered materials from the upper banks or bluffs sluff onto the lower banks, resulting in increased sediment supply in the next storm. In addition, high stream power extended farther upstream in West Creek than in Mill Creek, which could potentially increase upstream bank erosion, leading to counterclockwise hysteresis. However, the occurrence of high stream power does not necessarily indicate active erosion, as the presence of large roughness elements like bedrock, boulders, and logs can stabilize the streambed and banks in high stream power reaches,84 as we observed in some areas during field mapping.

In the least urbanized watershed (Chippewa Creek), rainfall spatial variability was a potential driver of a single loop hysteresis. Specifically, we found that higher rainfall intensity and depth upstream compared to downstream resulted in counterclockwise hysteresis. This suggests that elevated rainfall in the upstream area triggers a significant amount of sediment from distal sources, which is then transported at a slower speed than the flood wave celerity, leading to a delay in sediment response at the monitoring station.85 Similarly, when there is increased rainfall in the downstream area, it mobilizes substantial sediments in the downstream sources, causing earlier transport and resulting in a clockwise hysteresis loop. This pronounced separation using spatial rainfall variability further supports previous interpretations of sediment hysteresis, indicating that proximal sediments generate the clockwise pattern, while distal sources contribute to the counterclockwise hysteresis.35,38,86,87 We suggest that less surface sealing by impervious cover allows sediment sources from throughout the watershed to be eroded and that channel sediments play a secondary role in this watershed. Hence, our result shows that sediment source configuration in urban watersheds is influenced by the degree of urbanization, as well as by stream geomorphology.

Conceptualizing Urban Suspended Sediment Dynamics: Integrating Hydrology and Geomorphology

Synthesizing our findings and previous studies, we propose a conceptual framework to understand suspended sediment dynamics in urban watersheds (Figure 6). Urbanization has undeniably increased the velocity and volume of overland flow in urban watersheds, resulting in amplified erosivity and transport capacity of sediments.88−90 However, the quantity and sources of suspended sediment produced within the watershed depend on the availability of erodible material. In predevelopment watershed with spatially uniform sediment sources (Figure 6a), local meteorological characteristics shape hysteresis. As watersheds undergo active construction during urbanization (Figure 6b), a significant increase in sediment supply occurs.16,91 Despite an increase in suspended sediment supply, the hysteresis pattern depends on the local hydrometeorology, the spatial distribution of construction activity within the watershed, and its connectivity with the stream channel.

Conceptual model of suspended sediment dynamics in urban watersheds. In the event time series, the blue line represents normalized discharge (Q) and the brown line displays turbidity (Conc.). Tan color represents exposed sediment sources within the watershed.

In watersheds with relatively low amounts of impervious cover (<30%) and abundant unsealed soil surfaces with connectivity to the stream, the sediment contribution from the watershed itself can be a significant fraction of the total suspended sediment load (Figure 6c). However, the dominant hysteresis patterns still depend on the hydrometeorological characteristic, the spatial distribution of sediment sources within the watershed, and the hydrologic connectivity, all of which can be mediated by urbanization. For example, spatial variability in rainfall can alter the sediment supply from upstream (resulting in counterclockwise hysteresis) to downstream (resulting in clockwise hysteresis) when sediment sources are uniformly distributed and mostly unsealed by impervious surfaces, as observed in Chippewa Creek. In moderately urbanized watersheds (Figure 6d), surface sealing limits watershed sediment supply in certain areas, but we suggest that comparable sediment contributions between the watershed and stream results in dominance (>50%) of figure-8 and complex hysteresis patterns.

In urbanized watersheds, road dust and atmospheric pollution from industrial and transportation sources can contribute to sediment supply.27,92 In built-out urban watersheds, sediment supply is often thought to be lower than under predevelopment conditions,16,91 and studies have identified the stream channel itself as a major contributor of suspended sediments in urban watersheds.26,93 Despite previous studies that have proposed that the connectivity imposed by urban drainage systems as the most important driver of suspended sediment supply and hysteresis,16,45 we emphasize that stream geomorphology plays significant role in determining the volume and location of suspended sediment supply. Therefore, as the watershed reaches full development and surface sealing substantially reduces sediment supply from the watershed, sediment contribution from the stream becomes significant (Figure 6e). These contributions can be significantly influenced by bank material through erosion and/or by streambed material through bed incision or resuspension of fine sediment when the coarse armored layer is disrupted. Thus, stream geomorphology plays a critical role in driving hysteresis in these developed highly urbanized watersheds. For instance, counterclockwise hysteresis proportionally increases for an urban watershed characterized by unstable upstream reaches and stable downstream reaches, as observed in West Creek. In the opposite case, with unstable downstream reaches and stable upstream reaches, the dominant hysteresis pattern would be clockwise, as observed in Mill Creek.

Conclusions

Improving our understanding of the impact of urbanization on suspended sediment transport is essential to effectively address the consequences of urban stream syndrome. In this work, we used field geomorphic mapping to support the analysis of high frequency turbidity data, which was effective in understanding the sources and transport of suspended sediments in urban watersheds. In the mostly highly urbanized watershed, we observed comparatively lower suspended sediment response to flow than in less intensely urbanized watersheds. However, the underlying mechanism of the suspended sediment response varied between watersheds. Short duration, high intensity rainfall increased suspended sediment load in the least urbanized watershed, while the presence of dry conditions with intense rainfall and rapid discharge increased suspended sediment load in the moderately urbanized watershed. In contrast to the other two watersheds, we observed a prominent sediment exhaustion and limited sediment supply phenomena during clockwise hysteresis in highly urbanized watershed.

Inconsistent hysteresis patterns along the gradient of urbanization were observed, with more counterclockwise hysteresis in low and moderately urbanized watersheds and more clockwise hysteresis in highly urbanized watershed. This observation rejects simplistic conceptual models like first-flush or delayed response in urban watersheds. We propose a conceptual framework to evaluate the likely impact of urban development on suspended sediment transport and hysteresis responses. In predevelopment homogeneous sediment source watersheds, sediment supply remains moderate and meteorological factors shape hysteresis. As watersheds become developed and surface sealing substantially reduces watershed sediment supply, the role of the stream channel becomes significant in controlling suspended sediment transport and the hysteresis pattern. We therefore emphasize the importance of considering stream geomorphology in urban watershed management and stream restoration efforts. We suggest that the proposed framework needs to be tested in more urban watersheds across climate conditions using long-term observations, where they are available. The proposed framework can potentially assist in the improvement of suspended sediment transport models for urban watersheds, particularly by incorporating concepts beyond the first-flush model.
