Longitudinal urinary LAM measurements in pulmonary tuberculosis patients: preliminary observations
Authors: Vasiliki Kolokotroni, Irini Gerogianni, Konstantinos I. Gourgoulianis, Garyfallia Perlepe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMonitoring treatment response in pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) is critical, but non-invasive biomarkers are limited for patients unable to produce sputum.ObjectiveTo evaluate the utility of the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay in urine for diagnosis and treatment monitoring of pulmonary TB.MethodsNine patients with confirmed TB at the University Hospital of Larissa, Greece, were followed longitudinally. Urine samples were collected at treatment initiation and at weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24. Lipoarabinomannan (LAM) concentrations were measured using the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay; values ≥ 10 pg/mL were considered positive.ResultsUrinary LAM positivity peaked between weeks 4 and 12. Three kinetic patterns emerged: (i) early pronounced decline in patients with high baseline LAM; (ii) modest decreases in intermediate baseline patients; and (iii) variable or rising trajectories in low-baseline patients. Patterns mirrored previously reported sputum LAM kinetics. One patient treated on clinical criteria alone showed baseline positivity, and two patients with non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection also tested positive.ConclusionsUrinary LAM kinetics closely mirror sputum-derived patterns, supporting urine as a non-invasive specimen for serial TB monitoring. Interpretation of low-baseline results requires caution, and further studies with larger cohorts are warranted.

Main text

Subject line: Urinary lipoarabinomannan (LAM) kinetics during treatment of pulmonary tuberculosis: preliminary observations using the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay

We read with interest the recent article by Orina et al. entitled “Evaluation of the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay as a treatment monitoring tool for pulmonary tuberculosis in Nairobi, Kenya” [1], published in Tropical Medicine and Health. Their study highlights the potential of the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay as a rapid biomarker for treatment monitoring, showing correlations between sputum LAM concentrations, smear grade, culture results, and early therapeutic response. Their longitudinal dataset contributes to the growing evidence supporting pathogen-derived biomarkers for tuberculosis treatment monitoring.

Motivated by these findings, we applied the same assay to urine samples from patients with confirmed tuberculosis, with the aim of evaluating whether urinary LAM measurements could similarly assist in the diagnosis and monitoring of active disease. To complement and expand upon the observations of Orina et al., we report our experience using serial urinary LAM determinations throughout the treatment course. Although the biological matrices differ (sputum vs. urine), the underlying analyte—lipoarabinomannan released from Mycobacterium tuberculosis—may reflect comparable dynamics of bacillary burden.

We focused on urine because it is easy to collect, non-invasive, and suitable for repeated sampling—features that facilitate longitudinal monitoring, especially in patients unable to expectorate sputum. Given the promising performance of the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay in sputum, investigating its utility in urine represents a logical and clinically relevant extension, particularly as systematic studies on this application remain limited.

Patients with confirmed tuberculosis who were hospitalized or followed at the Respiratory Medicine Department of the University Hospital of Larissa since December 2024 were included in our study. Exclusion criteria were HIV infection, non-compliance with the follow-up protocol and refusal to participate. The study population consisted of nine patients with pulmonary tuberculosis (mean age of 52.6 years, seven male and two females). Microbiological confirmation was obtained via culture and molecular techniques for drug susceptibility testing. One patient was diagnosed empirically due to negative culture but with characteristic clinical and imaging findings. Disease severity varied, with cavitary lesions identified in four patients. Two patients (Patients 4 and 9) were diagnosed with non-tuberculous mycobacterial [NTM] infection with species identification confirming Mycobacterium avium in both cases.

Urine samples (50 mL) were collected by clean-catch (first morning or spot urine) at treatment initiation and in weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24. The sputum pretreatment protocol described in the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay manual was intentionally not applied to urine samples. Owing to the low viscosity and relatively low bioburden of urine, samples underwent a simplified pretreatment consisting of heat treatment followed by centrifugation. Urine samples were heated (e.g., at 100 °C) and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min to remove particulate material. The resulting supernatant was collected and used directly for analysis in the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay. No NaOH-based decontamination or chemical neutralization steps were performed. Nevertheless, the urine specimen was chosen to evaluate the feasibility of using the assay with a minimally processed, clinically practical specimen. For each measurement, 100 μL of urine supernatant was directly loaded into the PATHFAST cartridge. The remaining urine was aliquoted into 1.5 mL microtubes and stored at − 20 °C until analysis. LAM concentrations were measured with the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay, and values ≥ 10 pg/mL were considered positive [2]. As the assay has not been validated for urine samples, results should be interpreted with caution.

Among the nine patients enrolled, urinary LAM positivity was most frequently observed between weeks 4 and 12, with follow-up still ongoing. In most cases, LAM levels gradually rose until reaching the positivity threshold, followed by a progressive decline. Patient 2, who initiated anti-tuberculosis therapy based solely on clinical criteria demonstrated a marked peak at week 12, which may reflect individual differences in bacillary burden or early LAM release after treatment initiation. Moreover, a pronounced peak in urinary lipoarabinomannan (LAM) concentration was observed in patient 3 during follow-up. Notably, this patient had extensive pulmonary tuberculosis with cavitary disease on chest computed tomography, which may explain the markedly elevated LAM levels. The longitudinal urinary LAM trajectories revealed three kinetic patterns similar to those observed by Orina et al.: (i) patients with high baseline LAM showed a pronounced early decline; (ii) patients with intermediate baseline concentrations demonstrated modest but consistent decreases; and (iii) patients with very low baseline values displayed non-linear or rising trajectories during follow-up reflecting the variability described by Orina et al. in their low-baseline sputum LAM group. These kinetics are illustrated in Fig. 1A and B, with the full-scale figure highlighting a marked week-12 spike in one patient, while the zoomed version shows early and mid-treatment variability among the remaining patients. In addition to Panel A showing urinary LAM concentrations, Panel C presents the same data on a log scale, illustrating the fluctuations in LAM levels more clearly across the treatment period.

Our findings indicate that urinary LAM measurements follow patterns broadly consistent with sputum-derived LAM kinetics, particularly in patients with higher baseline concentrations. The non-invasive nature of urine collection makes it a practical option for serial monitoring, especially in patients unable to produce sputum. However, interpretation of low-baseline results requires caution, and further research is needed.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small (n = 9), which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, follow-up is still ongoing, so full longitudinal patterns are not yet available for all patients. Finally, the PATHFAST TB LAM Ag assay was used off-label in urine, and its performance in this sample has not been fully validated. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Nevertheless, the preliminary results support urine as a promising specimen type for future investigation. As a future research consideration, urinary LAM levels, in contrast to sputum-derived LAM, may decline rapidly after treatment initiation. Given that urinary LAM levels may decline after treatment initiation, more frequent sampling at earlier time points (e.g., weekly measurements) may be necessary to better characterize the early kinetics of urinary LAM.
