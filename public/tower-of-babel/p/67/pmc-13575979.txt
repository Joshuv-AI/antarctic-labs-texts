ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONFrontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD) often affects younger patients, making time to diagnosis especially consequential. BACKGROUND

The concept of dementia refers to a deterioration of cognitive function substantial enough to impair everyday functioning. Often it is linked to two persistent misconceptions: “dementia occurs in old age” and “cognition is equal to memory.” 1 Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a clinical entity that defies these preconceptions, posing a challenge to those who must diagnose the disease. FTD predominantly affects individuals younger than 65 years of age, where it is a leading cause of dementia, and presentations primarily involve behavior, executive function, and/or language abnormalities. 2 , 3 , 4

FTD comprises three main clinical phenotypes: the behavioral variant of FTD (bvFTD), and both the semantic variant (svPPA) and the non‐fluent variant (nfvPPA) of primary progressive aphasia (PPA). 4 , 5 From a neuropathological perspective, FTD is part of a broader group of disorders defined as frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD), characterized by the abnormal aggregation of proteins such as transactive response (TAR) DNA‐binding protein 43 kDa (TDP‐43), tau, or FUS, EWSR1 and TAF15 (FET) family proteins. 2 , 6 , 7 Other clinical syndromes also fall under the FTLD umbrella and share pathological mechanisms and genetic overlap with FTD, such as progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal syndrome (CBS), and FTD‐associated motor neuron disease (FTD‐MND). 8

The six clinical syndromes (bvFTD, svPPA, nfvPPA, PSP, CBS, and FTD‐MND) pose unique diagnostic challenges, making early detection difficult. For example, more than 50% of patients with bvFTD are diagnosed with a primary psychiatric disorder, including depression, bipolar disorder, and schizophrenia as the most common diagnoses. 9 This situation is exacerbated in psychiatric centers, where the likelihood of receiving a misdiagnosis may be even higher. In a recent study, 71.4% of patients with bvFTD in a psychiatric center in Colombia were diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder as the cause of their symptoms, with an even higher frequency in women (73.7% vs 66.7% in men). 10 In the case of PPAs, diagnostic difficulties have been reported. 11 , 12 In one study, patients with PPA received their diagnosis between 1.5 (svPPA) and 3.7 years (nfvPPA) after symptom onset. 3 Furthermore, the subclassification of PPA variants poses an additional challenge. More than 30% of PPAs are not classified under any of the proposed variants or present mixed features. 13 , 14

Patients with FTD are often misdiagnosed not only with psychiatric disorders, but also with Alzheimer's disease or vascular dementia. 9 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Parkinsonism may emerge during the course of disease and contribute to misdiagnosis as Parkinson's disease—particularly in PSP and CBS—and, although it is present in ≈20%30% of cases across the FTLD spectrum in both sporadic and familial forms, it has historically received limited attention, further increasing the risk of diagnostic confusion in FTD. 20 , 21 , 22 For FTD‐MND, the differential diagnosis is frequently made with pure forms of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). 23 Delayed diagnosis and misdiagnosis have important clinical, social, and economic consequences for patients and families. 24

There is considerable evidence regarding diagnostic challenges in FTD, with most data originating from high‐income countries, particularly the United States, Europe, and Asia. 3 , 11 , 12 This is particularly relevant in Latin America, where dementia prevalence is already estimated at 8.5% and is projected to reach 19.3% by 2050, representing one of the sharpest increases worldwide. 25 This poses limitations for the extrapolation to low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) such as those in Latin America. Sociodemographic profiles, healthcare systems, access to specialized services, and provider training vary widely across Latin America, limiting the generalizability of findings described in high‐income settings. To address this gap, the present study evaluates time to diagnosis across FTLD syndromes in the Multi‐Partner Consortium to Expand Dementia Research in Latin America (ReDLat) cohort, with special emphasis on differences among clinical phenotypes. Understanding these delays is clinically relevant, as late recognition may limit timely access to appropriate management, family counseling, and participation in research, while also reflecting broader structural barriers to diagnosis in Latin America.

METHODS

Participants

The ReDLat consortium includes sites across Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico, Peru, and the United States and aims to investigate how social, economic, and cultural determinants shape the clinical presentation, diagnostic process, and progression of dementia in Latin America. This study included 415 participants from the ReDLat cohort recruited between 2019 and 2024, across 12 sites in six Latin American countries (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Mexico, and Peru) following a standardized protocol implemented at each center (Figure 1, Table S1). Eligible participants were individuals 40 to 80 years of age with a clinical diagnosis within the FTLD spectrum established by an interdisciplinary team of neurologists, psychiatrists, geriatricians, and neuropsychologists at each site according to current diagnostic criteria for each phenotype. Diagnoses were established by the local multidisciplinary teams as part of routine clinical care prior to enrollment in ReDLat. Clinical syndromes included bvFTD, svPPA, nfvPPA, non‐specified primary progressive aphasia (nsPPA), PSP, CBS, FTD‐MND, and non‐specified FTD (nsFTD). Additional inclusion criteria were sufficient fluency in Spanish, Portuguese, or English for clinical and neuropsychological assessment, adequate visual and auditory abilities for testing, availability of a study partner or informant with frequent contact with the participant, and the ability to provide informed consent directly or through a legally authorized representative. Participants subsequently underwent a harmonized assessment protocol across participating ReDLat sites, including the Uniform Data Set version 3 protocol, Tablet‐based Cognitive Assessment Tool (TabCAT) Brain Health Assessment, TabCAT Examiner, mini‐Social Cognition & Emotional Assessment, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Exclusion criteria included severe dementia (FTLD‐CDR [Clinical Dementia Rating] = 3), inability to undergo MRI, major neurological disorders or structural brain lesions that could affect cognition or confound imaging interpretation, clinically significant cerebrovascular disease, and major systemic medical conditions considered by the treating neurologist to substantially affect cognition or behavior.

Geographic distribution of Multi‐Partner Consortium to Expand Dementia Research in Latin America (ReDLat) participants across Latin America (2019–2024).

In addition, 29 participants were identified as carriers of pathogenic variants associated with the FTLD spectrum, including variants in TAR DNA‐binding protein (TARDBP), granuline (GRN), chromosome 9 open reading frame 72 (C9ORF72), microtubule associated protein tau (MAPT), superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), and TANK binding kinase 1 (TBK1). These cases were included in the cohort and analyzed separately in exploratory comparisons between genetic and non‐genetic cases. Genetic analysis had been completed for 323 of 415 enrolled participants (77.8%) at the time of manuscript preparation. Inclusion in genetic testing was determined by sample availability and informed consent, as systematic testing was not mandated across all participating sites. Participants underwent whole genome sequencing, whole exome sequencing, or both. Variant pathogenicity was classified according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, as described in Acosta‐Uribe et al. (2026). 26 Of the 323 samples analyzed, 294 (91.0%) carried no pathogenic variants, whereas 29 (8.9%) were found to harbor pathogenic variants in GRN, MAPT, TARDBP, SOD1, or TBK1, or a pathogenic C9ORF72 repeat expansion.

Definition of time to diagnosis

The primary outcome was time to diagnosis, defined as the interval in years between symptom onset and the year of clinical diagnosis. Because symptom onset was retrospectively determined and could vary according to clinician interpretation across sites, we used two complementary definitions. First, T‐Reported was calculated using the age at symptom onset as originally recorded by the local investigating team. Second, T‐Criteria was calculated using the age at which the first symptom consistent with current diagnostic criteria for the corresponding FTLD phenotype was documented after detailed review of the clinical history. This review was performed to reduce misclassification of nonspecific early symptoms, such as depressive episodes later considered unrelated to the syndromic onset of FTLD. Based on this review, onset age was corrected in four cases (1 svPPA, 1 nfvPPA, and 2 bvFTD). Ten cases (7 bvFTD, 2 svPPA, and 1 PSP) were excluded from T‐Criteria analyses because the available retrospective clinical information was insufficient to establish a reliable corrected onset age. This dual approach was designed to capture both real‐world clinical assessment and a more standardized approximation of syndromic onset.

Social determinants of health

The analysis of social determinants of health (SDHs) and their association with time to diagnosis was based on the multidimensional framework of the social exposome proposed by Migeot et al. (2025). 27 Due to the complexity of the model and the large number of variables involved, a simplified approach was implemented focusing on the Social Vulnerability Index, where eight dimensions of social vulnerability were analyzed: education, financial status, material assets, access to healthcare, occupation, social relationships, traumatic experiences, and childhood experiences. Correlation analyses were conducted to assess the association between each of these variables and time to diagnosis.

Clinical variables

To examine whether symptom presentation influenced diagnostic delay, we conducted separate linear regression analyses using the first and second symptoms reported for each participant across FTLD phenotypes. Symptom domains were categorized into eight broad clinical areas: memory, language, attention, executive function, visuospatial skills, behavior, motor, and constitutional symptoms. This classification reflects general symptom domains and does not capture specific subtypes within each category. For instance, behavioral symptoms were not subdivided into apathy, disinhibition, or compulsions, and language impairments were not further classified into specific features such as anomia or agrammatism.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted in RStudio 2025.05.0 (Build 496). Time to diagnosis was calculated as the interval, in years, between symptom onset and clinical diagnosis. Negative values were recoded to zero to reflect the absence of diagnostic delay. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics across FTLD phenotypes. For descriptive comparisons across phenotypes in Table 1, effect sizes were reported in addition to p‐values, using omega squared (ω2) for continuous variables and Cramér's V for categorical variables. Owing to small cell counts, the p‐value for sex was calculated using Fisher's exact test with Monte Carlo simulation. Differences in time to diagnosis across phenotypes were examined using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by pairwise comparisons using estimated marginal means. Associations of diagnostic delay with age at symptom onset and years of education were evaluated using ordinary least squares linear regression models, both in the overall sample and stratified by phenotype. To examine whether early clinical presentation influenced diagnostic delay, first and second symptoms were grouped into broad clinical domains and analyzed in relation to time to diagnosis across phenotypes.

Missing data

Because the study relied in part on retrospective clinical information, missingness was expected to be greater for variables related to symptom onset. Cases with insufficient information to determine a reliable corrected onset age were excluded from T‐Criteria analyses, as described. All other analyses were conducted using available data for each model.

RESULTS

Among the 415 individuals with FTLD, the mean age (SD) was 66.5 ± 8.2 years, and 50.8% were female. Distribution by clinical phenotype included 232 bvFTD, 68 svPPA, 33 nfvPPA, 13 nsPPA, 32 PSP, 22 CBS, 7 FTD‐MND, and 8 nsFTD. No differences were observed in age, sex, or years of education across phenotypes (Table 1)

Time to diagnosis: T‐Reported vs T‐Criteria

The overall mean time to diagnosis, calculated from the clinician's first reported symptom (T‐Reported), was 3.33 ± 3.4 years. Across phenotypes, the longest durations since symptom onset were observed among individuals with bvFTD, followed by those with PPA variants (svPPA and nfvPPA), whereas patients with FTD‐MND exhibited the shortest delays (Table 2).

When using the age at onset corresponding to the first symptom that fulfilled diagnostic criteria for FTLD (T‐Criteria), the time to diagnosis was slightly reduced across most clinical syndromes, highlighting the influence of nonspecific early symptoms. The reduction in time to diagnosis was particularly pronounced in bvFTD and svPPA. However, no statistically significant differences were found between clinical phenotypes in either the T‐Reported or T‐Criteria groups (p > 0.05, Table 2).

Determinants of time to diagnosis

No statistically significant associations were found between the SDH composite model and diagnostic delay (p > 0.05). We subsequently examined the effect of years of education separately. Education was not associated with diagnostic delay for any individual phenotype in separate linear regression models. In the global model, the education × phenotype interaction was not statistically significant (p = 0.2516). In subgroup analysis restricted to svPPA, higher education showed a positive but non‐significant association with longer diagnostic delay (β = 0.149, p = 0.0736).

In contrast, age at symptom onset was inversely associated with time to diagnosis, with younger age at onset predicting longer diagnostic delays (β = −0.108, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.145 to −0.070, p = 2.99×10− 8), explaining 7.2% of the variance (R2 = 0.072). When stratified by clinical phenotype, this association remained significant only for bvFTD (β = −0.122, 95% CI: −0.173 to −0.072, p = 2.82×10− 6), explaining 9.1% of the variance in time to diagnosis (R2 = 0.091). No significant associations were observed for the remaining phenotypes (Figure 2).

We also examined the influence of sex on diagnostic delay. When modeled separately, sex did not predict time to diagnosis (p = 0.836). However, when analyzed in interaction with age at onset, a significant interaction emerged (p = 0.029). Specifically, the effect of age at onset on diagnostic delay was more pronounced in males, suggesting that younger males experienced longer delays than younger females.

Regarding the first symptoms of disease, exploratory analyses using initial linear regression models suggested that certain symptom presentations may influence diagnostic delay. Specifically, language symptoms as a first clinical feature appeared to be associated with shorter diagnostic delays in PPA variants, whereas executive and memory symptoms also showed potential associations in selected phenotypes. In bvFTD, some second symptom domains appeared to be associated with longer diagnostic delays. However, these findings were derived from exploratory models and require further validation.

To assess the robustness of these observations, we applied global testing approaches with correction for multiple comparisons. A significant association between first symptom and diagnostic delay was observed only in the nfvPPA group (ANOVA p = 0.000502). Post hoc pairwise comparisons using Wilcoxon tests with false discovery rate (FDR) correction showed that patients presenting with language symptoms had shorter diagnostic delays compared to those with behavioral symptoms (p = 0.0283). No significant associations were identified in the remaining phenotypes, including svPPA. Furthermore, no significant associations were observed between second symptom presentation and diagnostic delay across any clinical phenotype (all ANOVA p’s > 0.05; all pairwise comparisons were non‐significant after FDR correction).

Genetic cases

Compared to non‐carriers, carriers of pathogenic variants exhibited a significantly younger age at symptom onset (56.9 ± 7.3 vs 61.9 ± 8.4 years; p = 0.0011; 95% CI: −7.92 to −2.16). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in time to diagnosis between genetic and non‐genetic cases (3.72 ± 3.72 vs 3.31 ± 3.38 years; p = 0.439; 95% CI: −0.95 to 2.13).

Among genetic forms of FTLD, no differences were observed across clinical phenotypes in either age at onset (p = 0.697) or time to diagnosis (p = 0.545). Notably, GRN variant carriers had the shortest mean time to diagnosis among all genes, a finding that could reflect distinct clinical or familial features (Table 3). However, due to the small sample size, these comparisons lacked statistical power and prevented definitive conclusions about whether carriers of pathogenic variants vary by gene or mutation type.

DISCUSSION

This study analyzes time to diagnosis in a multicenter cohort of 415 patients with FTLD‐associated syndromes, recruited from 12 sites across six Latin American countries, and emphasizes the value of regionally generated data for understanding diagnostic trajectories in historically underrepresented populations. Analyses of time to diagnosis by clinician report (T‐Reported), revealed a mean delay of 3.33 years, consistent with international estimates. A recent meta‐analysis found an average diagnostic delay of 4.2 years for FTD and 3.5 years across all dementia types. 18 Although no differences were observed across phenotypes in our study, it is notable that the three canonical FTD presentations (bvFTD, svPPA, and nfvPPA) showed the longest delays (Table 2, Figure 3). These findings underscore the challenge of timely diagnosis in FTLD‐associated syndromes and the value of regionally generated data from Latin America.

On closer review of the clinical histories, we found that in some cases the first reported symptom was not compatible with the current diagnostic criteria for the corresponding clinical phenotype. This occurred mainly in bvFTD and PPA phenotypes, where differentiating symptomatically similar conditions (e.g., depression vs apathy) makes true disease onset difficult to define. We then performed a second analysis redefining onset based on the first symptom meeting FTLD‐associated diagnostic criteria (T‐Criteria). Under this adjusted approach, the average time to diagnosis was reduced to 2.97 ± 2.67 years. Although no statistically significant differences across phenotypes were observed for either T‐Reported or T‐Criteria, the reductions in diagnostic delay—5.4 months for bvFTD, 4.7 months for svPPA, and 3.7 months for nfvPPA—may still be clinically meaningful.

These findings further underscore the intrinsic complexity of recognizing the initial symptoms of FTLD syndromes. This difficulty is especially pronounced in domains such as language and, even more prominently, behavior—core features of PPA and bvFTD, respectively. Our cohort mirrors patterns previously described in the literature, where symptomatic overlap between primary psychiatric disorders and FTD, as well as the subtle onset of language impairments, contributes to misdiagnosis or diagnostic delay. 2 , 9 , 11 , 12 , 15

The analysis of factors contributing to diagnostic delay revealed that age at symptom onset plays a significant role: the earlier the disease begins, the longer it takes to reach a correct diagnosis. This finding aligns with previous studies on early‐onset dementias, in which FTD is a predominant clinical presentation. 18 , 19 A widely accepted explanation for this phenomenon is that the concept of dementia is not typically associated with younger individuals 1 ; as a result, neurodegenerative conditions such as FTD are frequently overlooked in this age group. This underscores a challenge for specialized centers and clinicians trained in dementia: ensuring accurate and timely diagnoses for younger patients. It also highlights the importance of implementing updated educational programs to equip healthcare professionals—including neurologists, psychiatrists, psychologists, and general practitioners—with the tools to recognize these syndromes. A recent Latin American training initiative on FTD for psychiatrists reported that 85.9% of participants acknowledged, upon completing the course, that they had likely evaluated patients with FTD in the past but failed to recognize the diagnosis. 28

Although no differences in time to diagnosis were observed based on sex alone, a combined analysis with age at onset revealed that younger male patients experienced longer diagnostic delays. Multiple studies show that, in general, men—and particularly younger men—are less likely to seek medical attention, or tend to do so later than women. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 Given that most FTD cases in this cohort correspond to the bvFTD phenotype, which typically presents with prominent behavioral symptoms, this pattern may help explain the longer delays observed in younger male patients.

Initial symptoms are likely to influence how quickly patients seek care or are referred for specialist evaluation. In our cohort, after global testing and correction for multiple comparisons, the only symptom‐related association that remained significant was observed in nfvPPA, where language symptoms as the first clinical manifestation were associated with shorter diagnostic delay compared with behavioral symptoms. This finding is consistent with the clinical profile of nfvPPA, as early language difficulties may more readily prompt referral for cognitive or speech‐language evaluation, whereas behavioral presentations may initially be attributed to psychiatric or nonspecific causes. Differences in symptom presentation may also influence when medical attention is first sought. Language and motor symptoms are often perceived as signs of a neurological disorder and may therefore prompt earlier consultation, whereas behavioral changes may be attributed to personality traits or life circumstances, or initially managed within psychiatric settings. These differences in symptom interpretation could contribute to variation in the interval between symptom onset and first medical evaluation, ultimately affecting diagnostic timing. By contrast, other symptom‐related patterns identified in exploratory analyses, including second‐symptom associations in bvFTD, were not confirmed after correction and should not be overinterpreted.

When examining the factors that influence clinical aspects of neurodegenerative diseases, SDHs are important to consider, particularly in LMICs, where the greatest increase in dementia cases is projected. 25 In this cohort, the SDH analysis did not reveal significant associations with diagnostic delay, including in analyses adjusted by country. Although no clear signal emerged from this approach, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Participants were recruited from specialized academic centers, potentially limiting variability in access to care among individuals who had already reached expert evaluation. Moreover, previous work suggests that social and environmental determinants play an important role in brain health outcomes across diverse populations. 34 Therefore, the absence of significant associations in the present study should not be interpreted as evidence that these factors are unrelated to diagnostic processes in FTLD. However, variables related to the initial clinical presentation appeared to be more closely linked to diagnostic delay in our sample, highlighting the relevance of early symptom recognition in FTLD‐associated syndromes.

We did not find a significant global interaction between education and phenotype. Still, in svPPA, higher education showed a positive trend with longer diagnostic delay, although the association was not statistically significant. Although exploratory, this pattern may still be of clinical interest. In svPPA, greater educational attainment could allow compensation for early semantic deficits, making the syndrome less evident in its initial stages and delaying diagnosis. 35 , 36 This interpretation remains speculative and should be tested in larger samples.

In line with previous reports indicating that age at onset in genetic FTLD‐associated syndromes is highly variable, 37 our cohort showed a statistically significant difference: carriers of pathogenic variants presented symptoms ≈5 years earlier than non‐carriers. Despite the earlier onset, no differences in time to diagnosis were found between genetic and non‐genetic cases. This is unexpected considering that a known family history of neurodegenerative disease—expected in many of these cases—should ideally serve as a diagnostic red flag, prompting earlier referral and recognition. These findings reinforce the notion that individuals with FTD, even those with strong genetic risk, remain a diagnostically vulnerable population.

Although the small sample size limits statistical comparisons between variation in different genes, some patterns are worth noting. Cases with C9ORF72 expansions—all presenting as bvFTD—exhibited the longest time to diagnosis, consistent with international reports highlighting the often prolonged and clinically complex neuropsychiatric course of C9ORF72‐associated presentations. Conversely, cases with GRN variants showed the shortest time to diagnosis. Given that GRN is currently the focus of several experimental therapeutic trials, this observation underscores the relevance of timely diagnosis for enabling access to clinical research opportunities. In summary, this study offers a comprehensive characterization of time to diagnosis in FTLD‐associated syndromes across Latin America, revealing both shared global patterns and distinctive diagnostic challenges shaped by the regional context. Although the identification of initial symptoms relied on retrospective records, potentially influenced by clinical judgment and informant accuracy, and symptom categorization did not differentiate specific subtypes, the main findings remain clinically informative. In addition, the cohort was composed of participants recruited from specialized academic centers, which may limit the generalizability of results to community or primary care settings where access to expert evaluation is more limited. Most participating centers were public institutions operating within heterogeneous healthcare systems across Latin America. Differences in access to specialized care, referral pathways, availability of dementia specialists, diagnostic resources, and waiting times for specialist evaluation may have influenced time to diagnosis. Although these factors were not directly measured in the present study, they likely contribute to variability in diagnostic trajectories and should be examined in future multicenter studies. Another limitation relates to the assessment of disease severity. Functional severity at study enrollment, as measured by the FTLD‐CDR, did not differ significantly across phenotypes (Table 1). However, FTLD‐CDR scores were obtained at study entry rather than at the time of diagnosis. Because participants were recruited across multiple sites and clinical cohorts, comparable measures of disease severity at diagnosis were not consistently available. Future studies incorporating standardized assessments closer to the time of diagnosis may help clarify whether disease stage influences diagnostic delay. Despite these limitations, this work addresses a major gap in dementia research by generating evidence from Latin American populations underrepresented in the literature. It underscores the urgent need for context‐sensitive clinical tools, targeted educational initiatives, and inclusive public health strategies. Reducing the time to diagnosis in Latin America is not only key to improving clinical outcomes—it is a matter of equity, recognition, and global representation in the field of neurodegenerative diseases.
