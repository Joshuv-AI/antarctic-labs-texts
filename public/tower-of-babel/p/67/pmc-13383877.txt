Ex vivo partial nephrectomy and renal autotransplantation for an ultra-long ureteral stricture with incidental papillary renal cell carcinoma: A 4-year follow-up case report
Authors: Wei Shi, Guilin Wang, Junsheng Bao, Li Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a 44-year-old male with a 10-cm iatrogenic ureteral stricture and severely impaired renal function. Incomplete preoperative imaging resulted in the unexpected intraoperative discovery of a 2.5-cm papillary renal cell carcinoma. To salvage the organ, we performed an ex vivo partial nephrectomy followed by renal autotransplantation. At 4-year follow-up, the patient demonstrated significant renal functional recovery with no evidence of tumor or stricture recurrence. This case demonstrates that ex vivo tumor excision combined with autotransplantation is an effective, nephron-sparing strategy for complex strictures, while underscoring the critical necessity of comprehensive preoperative imaging.Keywords: Ureteral stricture, Renal cell carcinoma, Renal autotransplantation, Partial nephrectomy

Introduction

The prevalence and incidence of nephrolithiasis are increasing globally. Ureteroscopic lithotripsy (URSL) is a commonly utilized surgical modality due to its wide applicability and satisfactory stone clearance rates.1 However, with the widespread application of URSL, the incidence of iatrogenic ureteral strictures has concurrently risen, occurring in nearly 3% of patients undergoing ureteroscopic treatment for nephrolithiasis.2 Ureteral strictures can lead to severe complications, including hydronephrosis, recurrent urinary tract infections, and progressive deterioration of renal function.

For patients with a single, short (<2 cm) stricture of the upper ureter, resection of the stricture segment followed by end-to-end ureteral anastomosis (ureteroureterostomy) can be considered, provided that the blood supply is adequate and a tension-free anastomosis can be achieved.3 For strictures >2 cm, reconstructive techniques such as lingual mucosal ureteroplasty or appendiceal onlay ureteroplasty are typically preferred.4,5 However, for ultra-long or multifocal strictures characterized by severe fibrosis and loss of tissue viability, reconstructive options are starkly limited. Ileal ureter replacement is often regarded as the last nephron-sparing resort before an inevitable nephrectomy. Renal autotransplantation (RAT) is a relatively rare and surgically challenging procedure that places high demands on the surgeon's technical skills. In recent years, there have been increasing reports of RAT being utilized to treat complex upper ureteral strictures, serving as an alternative reconstructive option.6, 7, 8 Nevertheless, encountering an asymptomatic, concurrent renal malignancy during RAT is exceptionally rare.

Herein, we report a unique case where inadequate preoperative imaging led to unexpected intraoperative findings: an ultra-long ureteral stricture (extending 10 cm) and an incidental papillary renal cell carcinoma (pRCC). This report details the successful execution of an ex vivo partial nephrectomy combined with RAT, while candidly discussing the diagnostic pitfalls and clinical lessons learned.

Case presentation

Patient information and preoperative evaluation

A 44-year-old male presented to our department in April 2022 with progressive left flank pain. He had a history of two left URSL procedures with holmium laser lithotripsy (April 2017 and October 2021), after which he developed worsening ureteral strictures. Preoperative non-contrast computed tomography (CT) revealed a 8 mm stone in the proximal left ureter, significant left hydronephrosis, and a stricture of the upper ureter distal to the stone (Fig. 1A and B). Diuretic renal scintigraphy (SPECT-DTPA) showed severely impaired left renal function, with a split glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of 12.9 ml/min (right kidney: 54.9 ml/min) (Fig. 1C). Unfortunately, definitive preoperative antegrade or retrograde pyelography, as well as contrast-enhanced CT, were omitted—a diagnostic oversight that subsequently altered our surgical strategy.

Surgical intervention

The patient initially underwent robotic-assisted exploration for planned ureteral reconstruction. Intraoperatively, retrograde stenting failed as the guidewire encountered impassable resistance at 12 cm. A ureteral catheter was placed, followed by a Foley catheter, and the ureteral catheter was secured for standby use. Robotic exploration revealed dense peripelvic and proximal ureteral adhesions. After meticulous dissection, a severe stricture was identified approximately 2 cm distal to the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ). A longitudinal ureterotomy at this site exposed a 8 mm stone, which was extracted (Fig. 2A). Subsequent distal exploration and incision revealed severe ureteral fibrosis and complete luminal obliteration distal to the stone. Exploration continued approximately 6 cm distally, where the ureter remained rigid and strictured. Consequently, indocyanine green (ICG) was retrogradely injected via the indwelling ureteral catheter (Fig. 2B). Switching to fluorescence imaging, an approximately 10-cm-long ischemic, scarred stricture extending below the level of the iliac vessels was clearly visualized.

(C) During kidney mobilization, an unexpected mass measuring approximately 2 cm was discovered on the lateral aspect of the lower renal pole (black arrow). (D) The renal artery and vein of the transplanted kidney were anastomosed to the internal iliac artery and external iliac vein, respectively.

Given the significantly impaired split function of the left kidney, the decision to salvage the organ had to be carefully weighed. If renal preservation is attempted, given the extreme length and severely fibrotic nature of the stricture, lingual mucosa or appendiceal onlay grafts are considered to have a low success rate. In this setting, bowel substitution and RAT are viable reconstructive options. Following repeated intraoperative consultations with the patient's family, they firmly insisted on renal preservation but explicitly refused ileal ureter replacement, opting instead for RAT. After obtaining urgent intraoperative consent, we proceeded with RAT to salvage the residual renal function.

However, during further mobilization of the kidney, an unexpected mass measuring approximately 2 cm was discovered on the dorsal side of the lower renal pole (Fig. 2C). The family was immediately informed of this incidental lower-pole tumor and consented to an ex vivo partial nephrectomy combined with RAT. After robot-assisted laparoscopic perirenal mobilization, the ureter was transected at the dilated proximal ureter, and the strictured segment was excised. The renal artery and vein were mobilized to an adequate length, clipped with Hem-o-lok clips, and divided. The kidney was then extracted and immediately covered in ice slush.

Bench surgery and autotransplantation

On the backtable, the kidney was perfused with 4°C cold preservation solution via the renal artery and maintained under cold ischemic conditions. The warm ischemia time, defined as the interval from renal artery clamping to initiation of cold perfusion, was approximately 3–4 minutes. The cold ischemia time, defined as the interval from initiation of cold perfusion to graft reperfusion, was approximately 60–70 minutes. An ex vivo partial nephrectomy was concurrently performed to excise the tumor, and the parenchymal defect was reconstructed using barbed sutures. The surgical margins of the renal artery and vein were meticulously trimmed and prepared.

Simultaneously, a second surgical team repositioned the patient, re-prepped the surgical field, and made a 15-cm left Gibson incision to expose the left iliac fossa. The left external iliac vein and internal iliac artery were mobilized. The internal iliac artery was ligated distally and temporarily clamped proximally, preserving adequate length. After the external iliac vein was temporarily clamped both proximally and distally, a longitudinal venotomy of approximately 1.5 cm was made. An end-to-side anastomosis between the renal vein and the external iliac vein was performed using a 5-0 continuous suture. Subsequently, an end-to-end anastomosis between the renal artery and the internal iliac artery was performed using a 5-0 interrupted suture (Fig. 2D). Upon reperfusion, the kidney demonstrated excellent perfusion, and immediate urine production was observed. The healthy distal ureter and the renal pelvis were spatulated and reconnected via an end-to-end anastomosis over a 5 Fr, 14 cm double-J stent. Estimated blood loss was 150 ml, and no transfusions were required.

Pathology and follow-up

Postoperative pathology of the excised mass revealed a 2.5 × 1.4 × 1.2 cm papillary renal cell carcinoma (WHO/ISUP Grade 2) with negative surgical margins and no lymphovascular invasion (Fig. 3A). The patient experienced a smooth postoperative recovery. At three months postoperatively, follow-up SPECT-DTPA showed significant functional recovery, with the left kidney GFR improving from 12.9 ml/min to 22.6 ml/min, although blood perfusion remained relatively low (Fig. 3B and C). Postoperative left renal blood perfusion continued to rise over time; at the 4-year follow-up, Doppler ultrasound demonstrated a left renal artery blood flow of 422 ml/min (right renal artery: 655 ml/min) (Fig. 3D and E). To date, no tumor recurrence or stricture relapse has been observed.

Discussion

The management of complex ureteral strictures is frequently fraught with diagnostic and reconstructive challenges. The instructive and uncommon elements of this case are the intraoperative decision to perform ex vivo partial nephrectomy when an unexpected renal mass was encountered during renal autotransplantation, and the subsequent recovery of a renal unit with markedly impaired preoperative GFR. A related lesson derived from this case is the importance of incomplete preoperative imaging. In our case, relying solely on non-contrast CT and surgical experience represented an important limitation in preoperative planning. First, without antegrade or retrograde pyelography, we were unable to preoperatively quantify the exact stricture length (which intraoperatively proved to be approximately 10 cm), leading to the failure of the initial mucosal graft repair plan. Second, the omission of contrast-enhanced CT (or MRI) resulted in the failure to detect the concurrent lower-pole renal tumor. Had a comprehensive imaging workup been performed, a more thorough preoperative surgical plan could have been formulated, potentially reducing intraoperative stress and operative time. Therefore, this case suggests that, for complex or recurrent ureteral strictures, antegrade/retrograde pyelography combined with cross-sectional enhanced imaging should be carefully considered as part of individualized preoperative planning. Furthermore, clinicians must remain highly vigilant regarding the possibility of concurrent urological pathologies.

In this case, the patient's split GFR was remarkably low (12.9 ml/min), approaching the traditional threshold where nephrectomy is often favored to avoid the morbidities of a complex reconstruction. However, we hypothesized that this GFR decline was largely functional—secondary to chronic obstruction—rather than representing irreversible parenchymal loss. When the patient refused both nephrectomy and ileal ureter substitution, RAT emerged as the ultimate nephron-sparing alternative, provided the institution possesses the necessary microvascular expertise and surgical infrastructure. Novick et al. reported the largest literature cohort to date of patients undergoing RAT; although the majority were for vascular indications, excellent outcomes were achieved in the 27 patients (25 patients, 92%) who underwent the procedure for ureteral replacement.9 With advancements in endovascular treatments for renal vascular anomalies, complex ureteral strictures are once again becoming a primary indication for RAT.10,11 Currently, the long-term outcomes of RAT are favorable, and while surgical complications can occur, they are generally mild.8 After relieving the obstruction via partial nephrectomy and autotransplantation, the patient's left kidney GFR nearly doubled to 22.6 ml/min at three months, and left renal perfusion continued to increase over a 4-year period. This functional improvement suggests that even a renal unit with severely impaired split function may retain meaningful recovery potential after effective relief of obstruction and reconstruction. This strongly demonstrates that RAT is a safe and highly viable surgical option for patients with complex ureteral strictures when institutional facilities permit.

The most compelling aspect of this case was the rare intraoperative discovery of a small incidental renal tumor during renal mobilization. Partial nephrectomy (PN) remains the gold standard for managing cT1a renal tumors. Even for T1b and T2 renal tumors, PN demonstrates equivalent oncological control to radical nephrectomy.12 Assessing the tumor in this case as cT1a (localized within the kidney, diameter ≤4 cm), we advocated for an ex vivo PN followed by RAT. This intraoperative conversion allowed the surgical team to avoid nephrectomy while simultaneously achieving tumor excision and ureteral reconstruction. Once the kidney is explanted and perfused with cold preservation solution on the backtable, the surgeon gains distinct advantages. Bench surgery provides a bloodless, highly illuminated operative field, allowing for precise tumor excision with guaranteed negative margins and meticulous renorrhaphy, all under the protective umbrella of profound hypothermia. Ultimately, this approach achieved definitive oncological control (negative margins for pRCC) and successfully salvaged the previously obstructed kidney. For surgeons encountering similar unexpected, localized malignancies during reconstructive or transplant surgeries, this case offers a useful clinical reference.

Conclusion

Renal autotransplantation combined with ex vivo tumor resection is a feasible and highly effective salvage strategy for ultra-long ureteral strictures complicated by concurrent localized renal tumors. This complex case underscores the tremendous recovery potential of renal function following the relief of obstruction and highlights the imperative need for rigorous preoperative imaging to avoid diagnostic pitfalls and optimize surgical planning.
