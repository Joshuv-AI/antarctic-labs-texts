Vaginal‐Derived Potential Probiotics and Their Postbiotics Alleviate Aerobic Vaginitis via Suppressing TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB Signalling Pathway and Potentially Enhancing Vaginal Barrier
Authors: Meiyun Tian, Xinyi Zhang, Xuan Xu, Xia He, Xiaoyun Wu, Jing Wei, Buzhen Tan, Tingtao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAntibiotic therapy is currently challenged by drug resistance and high recurrence in aerobic vaginitis (AV), making it urgent to seek novel strategies. In this study, we selected two potential probiotic strains Lactobacillus crispatus YBR‐01 ( L. crispatus YBR‐01) and Lactiplantibacillus plantarum YBR‐01 ( L. plantarum YBR‐01) from our proprietary bacterial library, and comprehensively evaluated the protective potential against AV in mouse model induced by a pathogen cocktail. Supplementation with L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01, either individually or in combination, attenuated vaginal edema, reduced proinflammatory mediators, and restored anti‐inflammatory cytokines, with the combination therapy yielding the best results. Mechanistically, the combination of strains significantly inhibited the TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB cascade (p < 0.05), restored vaginal mucosal integrity (p < 0.01), and rebalanced the Th17/Treg immunity (p < 0.05). Moreover, given that postbiotics represent a safe and highly stable alternative strategy with emerging translational potential, the research investigated their impact derived from L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 on the phenotypic characteristics and molecular profile of the AV mouse model. While postbiotics could synergistically alleviate the AV‐like murine phenotype and improve inflammatory markers, the impacts were quantitatively similar as those observed with the live strain combination (p < 0.05). More importantly, the postbiotics effectively mirrored the mechanisms of their live counterparts by downregulating the TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB pathway (p < 0.01), demonstrating the potential to enhance vaginal barrier (p < 0.01) and restoring Th17/Treg balance (p < 0.05), with the combination of postbiotics exhibiting superior advantages over monotherapy. Our study unveiled the therapeutic benefits of both potential probiotic Lactobacillus spp. and their postbiotics in AV, presenting an ideal prospect for clinical translation.

Introduction

The primary form of vaginal inflammation known as aerobic vaginitis (AV) arises from diminished H2O2‐yielding Lactobacillus spp. and subsequent over‐colonisation of aerobic bacteria, especially Staphylococcus aureus , Group B Streptococcus, and Escherichia coli (Das et al. 2023). The global burden of AV is approximately 7.9%–23.7%, while most affected individuals notably present with inflammatory burning, vulvar swelling, and vaginal yellow discharge, threatening the reproductive health and pregnancy outcome of women (Ma et al. 2022). Antibiotics including Nitroimidazole, Penicillin, and Quinolone are classified as first‐line medications for AV in clinic (Serretiello et al. 2021), whose application is however restricted due to frequent drug resistance, recurrence after treatment, and related adverse reactions (Gui et al. 2024). Therefore, novel therapeutic approaches capable of effectively managing AV while mitigating the limitations inherent to antibiotic regimens are urgently warranted.

The predominance of symbiotic Lactobacillus spp. in the vagina has long been recognised as a cornerstone of vaginal ecology, which protects the reproductive health of women by antimicrobial defence, immune regulation, and mucosal maintenance (Sanozky‐Dawes and Barrangou 2022; Wang et al. 2024). Extensive research has demonstrated that vaginal microbiota dysbiosis is closely associated with the onset and progression of various reproductive tract diseases, including non–sexually transmitted vaginitis such as AV and bacterial vaginosis (BV), as well as sexually transmitted infections (Pagar et al. 2024). Thus, probiotics, especially Lactobacillus spp., are particularly successful in restoring vaginal microbial balance and addressing the underlying aetiology of these disorders. Research has reported a randomised controlled trial (RCT) referring to 45 women that adopted L. crispatus CTV‐05 as a live biotherapeutic through the vagina to modulate the vaginal microbiome and reproductive tract inflammation in women with a high risk of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection (Hemmerling et al. 2025). Zhu et al. decoded the mechanism by which fatty acid responses of L. crispatus ameliorate BV, supporting the application strengths of Lactobacillus spp. in vaginal inflammation (Zhu et al. 2024). Therefore, evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of Lactobacillus spp., administered either individually or in combination, represents a promising strategy for AV treatment.

Postbiotics are non‐living forms or components of probiotics that confer health benefits to the host (Salminen et al. 2021). Beyond live probiotics, postbiotics with higher stability and stricter standardisation have recently become a hotspot in microbiome‐based therapeutics. As bioactive fragments derived from probiotics, postbiotics not only retain the key probiotic properties observed in Lactobacillus spp., but also overcome the inherent defects of live bacteria such as colonisation obstacles and host incompatibility, making them a feasible alternative in gynaecological treatment (Pagar et al. 2024). For instance, injection of cell‐free supernatant (CFS) derived from L. reuteri 29A has been shown to effectively defend against vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC), with findings indicating CFS capable of rehabilitating the integrity of infected vaginal tissue (Boahen et al. 2023). Additionally, exopolysaccharides (EPS) derived from L. plantarum C88 demonstrated pronounced antioxidant effects in a murine model of bacterial vaginitis, significantly reducing oxidative stress and restoring redox homeostasis (Zhang et al. 2013). Santana et al. (2024) investigated that Lactobacillus spp. postbiotics inhibited the in vitro chemical traits of Streptococcus mutans , with the latter being an unignorable pathogenic bacterium of AV. Banakar et al. (2023) also observed the inhibitory effect of postbiotics sourced from L. rhamnosus GG and L. reuteri on S. mutans . However, it remains uncertain whether the postbiotics obtained from LAB can exert long‐lasting mucosal immune repair in AV, and their capability to fully match the workflow of original probiotics still requires further verification.

This study screened for potential probiotics L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 in vaginal secretions from healthy women. Postbiotic derivatives of the strains were also preliminarily obtained by heat inactivation. Through rigorous performance testing in vitro, the ideal growth, metabolic, and potential probiotic properties of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 were validated. In AV mice model of vaginal microbiota disruption constructed with pathogen cocktail, we confirmed the therapeutic effects of potential probiotics and their postbiotic derivatives in treating AV, and elucidated the potential mechanisms in alleviating inflammation, repairing epithelial integrity, and restoring immune balance. The capture of vaginal probiotic Lactobacillus spp. and derived postbiotics, along with their application in gynaecological diseases represented by AV, paves the way for novel therapeutic strategies against vaginal dysbiosis.

Materials and Methods

Strain Screening and Potential Probiotic Characterisation

L. crispatus YBR‐01 (CGMCC No. 26680) and L. plantarum YBR‐01 (CGMCC No. 26679) were originally extracted from the vaginal discharges of healthy women. Both potential probiotics were stored at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC) and were provided by Jiangxi Yibairun Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Prior to formal intervention, the properties of both potential probiotics were comprehensively characterised. Growth kinetics were analysed by incubating strains in MRS broth at 37°C for 24 h, with optical density (OD) readings at 600 nm taken every 2 h to create growth curves (Qin et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2025).

To evaluate the acid tolerance of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01, bacterial pellets obtained by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min) were resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) adjusted to pH 2.0–7.0 (37°C, 4 h), with survival rates being determined through plate counting. Similarly, bile salt resistance was assessed by culturing strains in MRS broth supplemented with 0.0%–0.3% (w/w) bile salts for 24–36 h, followed by CFU enumeration (Qin et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2025).

Bacterial supernatants were utilised to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of potential probiotics. After mixing the supernatant (1 mL) with FeSO4 (2 mM), H2O2 (6 mM), salicylic acid (6 mM), and co‐incubating for 1 h, hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was identified through absorbance at 510 nm, whereas the superoxide radical scavenging activity was measured by incubating 0.5 mL of supernatant with 150 mM Tris–HCl buffer at a pH of 8.0 and 1.2 mM pyrogallol for a duration of 10 min, with absorbance recorded at 330 nm (He et al. 2025).

The Oxford cup method was employed to test antimicrobial activity against pathogens such as Escherichia coli , Staphylococcus aureus , and Candida albicans (Zhang et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2023). Bacterial supernatants were loaded onto agar plates with target pathogens, and inhibition zones were detected after 8 h of incubation at 37°C. Susceptibility to antibiotics was tested using the disk diffusion method for 10 different antibiotics, such as Cephalosporin, Penicillin, and Ciprofloxacin, with the pathogen‐suppressive bands being recorded after 24 h.

The supernatants were spotted onto 5% sheep blood agar plates, with determination of potential probiotic hemolytic activity being performed after 24 h of incubation at 37°C (You et al. 2025). Hemolysis was observed visually, with Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 as a positive control.

Postbiotic Preparation

In 10 mL of MRS broth at 37°C, L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 were incubated aerobically until reaching exponential phase concentrations of 1 × 107 CFU/mL for L. crispatus YBR‐01 and 1 × 108 CFU/mL for L. plantarum YBR‐01. Bacterial suspensions were prepared in sterile PBS (pH 7.4), followed by thermal inactivation at 80°C for 20 min with constant agitation.

Animal Model and Experimental Design

Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, weight 20–22 g, specific pathogen‐free) were obtained from Sibeifu Biotechnology Co. Ltd. (Beijing). The animals were maintained in ventilated polycarbonate cages under group‐housing conditions, with rodent chow and water provided ad libitum. The vivarium maintained standardised environmental parameters, including ambient temperature at 23°C ± 2°C, relative humidity of 50% ± 15%, and controlled photoperiod of 12/12 light–dark cycle, with lights on at 7 AM. For the construction of AV mice model, the method of Estradiol Benzoate (2 mg/mL, 0.05 mL) pretreatment plus aerobic pathogenic bacteria cocktail of Staphylococcus aureus : Group B Streptococcus: Escherichia coli (1:1:2, 1 × 109 CFU/mL) were adopted (Donders et al. 2002; Chen et al. 2021; Gui et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2025). Estradiol Benzoate pretreatment induces immunosuppression to disrupt the vaginal microenvironment in mice, while the pathogen cocktail primarily mimics the vaginal aerobic bacterial infection spectrum commonly observed in clinical AV patients. Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus are the most frequently detected strains, with some studies incorporating Group B Streptococcus to simulate the diversity of clinical AV pathogens (Donders et al. 2002; Gui et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2025).

Following acclimation for 7 days, mice assigned to the potential probiotic treatment were randomised into 5 groups of 8 mice each as follows. Control (C) group: subcutaneous injection of Estradiol Benzoate (2 mg/mL, 0.05 mL) every 48 h for 3 times, vaginally implanting saline‐soaked absorbable gelatin sponge (0.5 × 0.5 cm, 20 μL) on Day 13. Model (M) group: Estradiol priming followed by daily vaginal inoculation of pathogen cocktail (1 × 109 CFU/mL, Staphylococcus aureus : Group B Streptococcus: Escherichia coli = 1:1:2) within gelatin sponge in Day 13–19. L. plantarum YBR‐01 (Lp) group: modelled mice daily treated with vaginal instillation of L. plantarum YBR‐01 (1 × 108 CFU in 20 μL PBS) for 14 consecutive days. L. crispatus YBR‐01 (Lc) group: modelled mice administered with L. crispatus YBR‐01 (1 × 107 CFU in 20 μL PBS) daily for 14 consecutive days. L. plantarum YBR‐01 and L. crispatus YBR‐01 (LL) group: modelled mice receiving 1 × 108 CFU of L. plantarum YBR‐01 and 1 × 107 CFU of L. crispatus YBR‐01 via vaginal instillation once daily for 14 consecutive days.

In the experiments of postbiotics, 48 BALB/c mice were assigned to the potential probiotic treatment and randomised into 6 groups of 8 mice each. Control (C) group: subcutaneous injection of Estradiol Benzoate (2 mg/mL, 0.05 mL) every 48 h for totally 3 times, followed by vaginal implantation of saline‐soaked absorbable gelatin sponge (0.5 × 0.5 cm, 20 μL) on Day 13. Model (M) group: Estradiol priming followed by daily vaginal inoculation of pathogen cocktail (1 × 109 CFU/mL, Staphylococcus aureus : Group B Streptococcus: Escherichia coli = 1:1:2) using a gelatin sponge from Day 13 to 19. L. plantarum YBR‐01 and L. crispatus YBR‐01 (LL) group: modelled mice receiving 1 × 108 CFU of L. plantarum YBR‐01 and 1 × 107 CFU of L. crispatus YBR‐01 via vaginal instillation once daily for 14 consecutive days. Postbiotics of L. plantarum YBR‐01 (p‐Lp) group: modelled mice daily administered with vaginal instillation of postbiotics derived from L. plantarum YBR‐01 (1 × 108 CFU in 20 μL PBS) for 14 consecutive days. Postbiotics of L. crispatus YBR‐01 (p‐Lc) group: modelled mice daily treated with postbiotics originated from L. crispatus YBR‐01 (1 × 107 CFU in 20 μL PBS) for 14 consecutive days. Postbiotics of L. plantarum YBR‐01 and L. crispatus YBR‐01 (p‐LL) group: modelled mice receiving 1 × 108 CFU of postbiotics from L. plantarum YBR‐01 and 1 × 107 CFU of L. crispatus YBR‐01 via vaginal instillation once daily for 14 consecutive days.

After a 2‐week period of treatment, mice were euthanized, and the serum, uterine, and vaginal samples of mice were harvested for subsequent analyses. All experimental procedures were performed in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) while sanctioned by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanchang University (NCULAE, Protocol No. 20221031114).

Haematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining

For H&E staining, 2 μm sections were prepared from paraffin‐embedded vaginal tissue samples following fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin. Paraffin slices were dehydrated through ethanol and then immersed in xylene twice, with 10 min each. Sections were stained with haematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in water for 1 min, and differentiated using hydrochloric acid‐ethanol and eosin solutions. Following dehydration in ethanol and clearing with xylene (two changes, 10 min each), the slices were mounted with neutral gum and morphologically examined under an optical microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

To evaluate target protein expression across study groups, paraformaldehyde‐fixed tissue sections (5 μm) were deparaffinised and hydrated. The slices were then incubated with 3% H2O2 for 10 min and blocked in 5% goat serum at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies [Myeloperoxidase (MPO): Solarbio, GB15224‐100; Tumour Necrosis Factor‐α (TNF‐α): Solarbio, GB115701‐100] were introduced and incubated at 4°C overnight, while the secondary antibody was utilised for IHC detection. Subsequently, the target protein expression was observed and documented using a bright‐field microscope.

Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was reverse‐transcribed and amplified by quantitative PCR following the manufacturer's protocol (Prime Script RT Master Mix; TaKaRa Bio). The transcriptional level of β‐actin (ACTB) was designated as endogenous control, while the primer information of Interleukin (IL)‐1β, IL‐10, TNF‐α, and IL‐17 was summarised in Table S1. Calculation was conducted via the 2−∆∆Ct formula.

Western Blotting

Under reductive conditions, protein lysates were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) with gel concentrations from 8% to 15%. Proteins migrating during electrophoresis were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Immobilon‐P, Millipore, IPVH00010) using a wet transfer apparatus. The membrane was blocked at ambient temperature for 2 h with TBS‐T buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl, 0.1% Tween‐20, pH adjusted to 7.6) containing 5% (w/v) non‐fat dry milk. After TBS‐T washing three times with 5 min each, membranes were probed overnight with primary antibodies (detailed in Table S2) at 4°C with gentle agitation. After 10‐min TBS‐T washes three times, secondary antibodies that were horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated and species‐matched were maintained at ambient temperature for 1 h. The post‐incubation washes (3 × 10 min in TBS‐T) were carried out preceding the chemiluminescent detection using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS substrate (Thermo Scientific, 34,577). Ultimately, signals were captured using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio‐Rad) under optimised exposure settings.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was conducted in GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), with statistical methods selected according to data distribution characteristics, including normality and variance homogeneity. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was adopted when homoscedasticity is satisfied among groups; otherwise, the methods of Brown‐Forsythe/Welch's ANOVA were chosen. Tukey's or Dunn's tests were applied for post hoc comparisons. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Results

Potential Probiotic Features of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01

The strains L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 were isolated from the vaginal samples of healthy women and obtained from our bacterial bank. Identification was performed by amplifying and sequencing the 16S rRNA gene using primers 27F and 1492R, followed by comparison and repeated validation against the NCBI database. Both strains have been deposited in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center under accession numbers CGMCC No. 26680 ( L. crispatus YBR‐01) and CGMCC No. 26679 ( L. plantarum YBR‐01). In Figure 1A, both L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 exhibited distinct growth patterns during 24 h cultivation. After inoculation, L. crispatus YBR‐01 initiated logarithmic growth at 6 h and reached stationary phase after 14 h, while L. plantarum YBR‐01 exhibited faster proliferation with log phase starting at 2 h and stabilisation occurring by 8 h. Morphological examination revealed characteristic differences of the obtained L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01, with the former presenting as elongated curved rods, whereas the latter displayed straight or curved rod‐shapes that were frequently arranged in pairs or chains. As shown in Figure 1B,C, the strains demonstrated concurrent adaptation to both vaginal and gastrointestinal stressors of pH and bile salts. Under vaginal pH conditions (4.0) for 4 h, survival rates of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 still remained at a high level, while simultaneously exhibiting bile salt tolerance (0.2%) comparable to intestinal isolates.

Notably, cell‐free supernatants from both strains of LAB presented evident growth inhibition against multiple pathogens including Candida albicans , Salmonella enteritidis , Escherichia coli , Listeria monocytogenes , Shigella flexneri , Sh. dysenteriae, Staphylococcus aureus , S. typhimurium , and Beta hemolytic streptococcus, as evidenced by the suppressive zones in Figure 1D. Compared with the blank control, both potential probiotics and postbiotics exhibited statistically significant antibacterial effects, with no significant difference being observed between the two. Detailed data could be found in Tables S3.1 and S3.2, the spectrum and potency of antimicrobial inhibition by the postbiotics mirrored those of their parental live strains across all tested pathogens, as quantified by the similar diameters of suppression zones. Quantitative radical scavenging assays also revealed substantial antioxidant potential in both strain: L. crispatus YBR‐01 displayed ideal scavenging activities of superoxide (60.83% ± 2.02%) and hydroxyl radical (30.66% ± 2.75%), while L. plantarum YBR‐01 showed enhanced activities at 64.16% ± 1.22% and 37.23% ± 2.24%, respectively (Figure 1E). Hemolytic assessment confirmed the safety profile of both strains, with no significant hemolysis being observed compared with β‐hemolytic S. aureus controls (1–2 cm clearance zones; Figure 1F). Furthermore, antibiogram analysis indicated the similarly reduced susceptibilities of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 to Ciprofloxacin (CIP), Gentamicin (GEN), and Mycocin (MY), with inhibition zones below their clinical breakpoints specified by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (Table S4).

Therapeutic Effects of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 in AV

The therapeutic impact of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 in reducing cervicovaginal damage was determined by provoking AV via the intravaginal administration of aerobic pathogen cocktail (Figure 2A). During the procedure, mice in all groups were monitored, with no evident systemic adverse reactions, such as significant weight loss, abnormal activity, or spread of local infection, being observed. The M group exhibited marked vaginal shortening and edema compared to the control mice. Monotherapy with either L. crispatus YBR‐01 or L. plantarum YBR‐01 significantly improved the anatomical alterations mentioned above, while combined administration (LL) showed synergistic therapeutic efficacy. By contrast, in group M, characteristic vaginal erythema and purulent discharge were developed, both of which were attenuated by potential probiotic interventions, and the treatment of LL achieved nearly normal vaginal secretion clarity (Figure 2B,C and Table S5). As merged in Figure 2D, HE staining displayed significant epithelial disruption in M group, including disorganised basal columnar cells, discarded nuclei, and indistinct epithelial‐dermal boundaries. Live strain treatment progressively restored normal epithelial architecture, with the LL group exhibiting histological features most comparable to the healthy controls. Then, the expression of vital inflammatory mediator myeloperoxidase (MPO) was assessed with biochemical analysis and the results showed that in AV model, the inflammatory system exhibited marked overactivation (p < 0.001) compared with the control group. Notably, both single and combined therapies were effective, with the latter showing superior MPO suppression (M vs. LL = 4.11 U/g vs. 0.64 U/g, p < 0.05; Figure 2E). Furthermore, the qRT‐PCR profiling of inflammatory cytokines was also calculated and visualised in Figure 2F–I. All treatment regimens effectively reversed the above expression patterns, with dual‐strain administration achieving maximum cytokine normalisation by downregulating pro‐inflammatory markers TNF‐α (M vs. LL = 9.09 vs. 1.79, p < 0.001, C group: 1.00, the same below), IL‐1β (M vs. LL = 7.88 vs. 2.31; p < 0.001), and IL‐17 (M vs. LL = 6.17 vs. 1.70, p < 0.001), while enhancing anti‐inflammatory factor IL‐10 (M vs. LL = 0.20 vs. 0.97, p < 0.01) expression. These findings emphasised that L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 attenuated histopathological changes and reduced murine AV‐related inflammation.

L. crispatus YBR‐01 Combined With L. plantarum YBR‐01 Alleviates AV by Regulating Inflammatory Signalling

Vaginal dysbiosis triggered the vaginal mucosal inflammatory response, evidenced by overactivation of the TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB signalling pathway and elevation of inflammatory factors. The results indicated the elevated expressions of Toll‐like Receptor 4 (TLR4, C vs. M = 0.60 vs. 1.37, p < 0.001), Myeloid Differentiation Primary Response Protein 88 (MyD88, C vs. M = 0.13 vs. 1.23, p < 0.01), and Phosphorylated (p)‐p65/p65 (C vs. M = 0.46 vs. 1.11, p < 0.001) in M group. All potential probiotic interventions significantly suppressed these crucial components in the TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB axis. Among them, the combined LL treatment demonstrated the most potent inhibitory effect (TLR4: M vs. LL = 1.37 vs. 0.58, p < 0.001; MyD88: 1.23 vs. 0.53, p < 0.05; p‐p65/p65: 1.11 vs. 0.66, p < 0.001, Figure 3A–D). As illustrated in Figure 3A,E,F, Inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase (iNOS, C vs. M = 0.30 vs. 1.20, p < 0.001) and Cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2, C vs. M = 0.35 vs. 1.26, p < 0.001) exhibited a marked increase in expression in the vaginas of model mice compared to healthy individuals. Vaginal co‐delivery of both bacterial strains most dominantly downregulated iNOS (M vs. LL = 1.20 vs. 0.31, p < 0.001) and COX‐2 (M vs. LL = 1.26 vs. 0.47, p < 0.001), which corroborates the synergistic effect of the combination therapy.

Potential Probiotics Combination Potentially Modulated the Vaginal Mucosal Barrier to Mitigate AV

Impairment of vaginal mucosal barrier function and immune competence is commonly secondary to vaginal microbiota dysbiosis, which primarily manifests as disrupted tight junction and immune hyperactivation. Figure 4A–D illustrated the upregulation of Matrix Metalloproteinase 2 (MMP2) and MMP9 that were associated with mucosal damage in group M, as well as the downregulation of tight junction protein Occludin, which were significantly improved after potential probiotic treatment, with maximal restoration observed in the LL group (MMP2: M vs. LL = 1.23 vs. 0.51, p < 0.001; MMP9: 1.13 vs. 0.66, p < 0.01; Occludin: 0.50 vs. 1.05, p < 0.01). For live strain‐mediated immune polarisation in Figure 4E,F, Vaginal transcriptomic analysis further revealed significant differences between the AV mice and healthy controls, single or combined administration of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 downregulated Th17‐associated Retinoid‐related Orphan Receptor γt (RORγt, M vs. LL = 6.41 vs. 1.78, p < 0.01), while upregulating Treg‐associated Fork‐head Box Protein 3 (Foxp3, M vs. LL = 0.20 vs. 0.94, p < 0.05).

Postbiotics Derived From L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 Involve in the Amelioration of AV

A sufficient number of studies supports the use of non‐viable forms of probiotics in the treatment of gynecologic conditions (Wahid et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023). Accordingly, we generated postbiotics from L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 via high‐temperature inactivation and evaluated the resulting beneficial effects (Figure 5A). Gross morphological analysis revealed that the monotherapy of postbiotic (p‐Lc or p‐Lp) significantly alleviated vaginal shortening and edema in AV model mice (M group), with combined treatment of postbiotics as p‐LL achieving nearly complete restoration of vaginal architecture, comparable to the effects of LL group (Figure 5B). Clinical assessments revealed that postbiotic treatments effectively resolved vaginal erythema and purulent discharge, and the p‐LL group exhibited vaginal secretion profiles indistinguishable from the healthy controls (Figure 5C and Table S6). Histochemical evaluation through IHC in Figure 5D showed markedly reduced expressions of TNF‐α and MPO in postbiotic‐treated groups, particularly in p‐LL group, where the levels of inflammatory markers approximated those in C group. Through quantification, Figure 5F confirmed the suppression of MPO activity by postbiotics, with the synergetic p‐LL achieving a reduction of 84.34% compared to the M group (M vs. p‐LL = 4.31 U/g vs. 0.67 U/g, p < 0.01). Furthermore, qPCR analysis also concluded that postbiotics exerted potent immunomodulatory effects, which manifested as suppressed pro‐inflammatory cytokines of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐17 (82.0%, 80.6%, and 82.8% vs. group M, respectively), as well as restored anti‐inflammatory IL‐10 to 94.3% of the baseline level. It was worth noting that postbiotics modulated the cytokines in parity with live potential probiotics, as evidenced by statistically equivalent efficacy between p‐LL and their viable counterparts LL (p > 0.05 across all cytokine targets, Figure 5E,G–I).

Postbiotics Derived From L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 Suppress Inflammatory Responses in AV Through TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB Axis

Further experiments were performed to investigate whether the postbiotics originated from these strains function via the same mechanisms as their viable counterparts. Western blotting (Figure 6A–F) revealed that postbiotic treatment prominently inhibited the activation of TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB axis in the model mice (p < 0.05), concurrently downregulating the levels of pro‐inflammatory mediators COX‐2 (M vs. p‐LL = 1.40 vs. 0.57, p < 0.05) and iNOS (M vs. p‐LL = 1.43 vs. 0.49, p < 0.05). Consistent with the characterisation, no significant differences were observed between the LL group and derived postbiotic p‐LL group for the above indicators (p > 0.05). As shown in Figure 6G–J, postbiotic treatment was associated with an inhibition of MMP2 and MMP9 activity (p < 0.05) and an upregulation of Occludin levels (p < 0.05). These molecular changes may be related to the observed improvements in epithelial integrity, which paralleled the effects of potential probiotic interventions. In addition, postbiotics originated from L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 induced significant immune rebalancing, decreasing Th17/Treg ratio through RORγt downregulation and Foxp3 upregulation, whose effects were also quantitatively similar to those of live potential probiotics (RORγt: p‐LL vs. LL = 1.18 vs. 1.17, p > 0.05; Foxp3: 0.93 vs. 0.92, p > 0.05; Figure 6K,L). The above indicated that postbiotics may mechanistically recapitulated the anti‐inflammatory effects of live potential probiotics through coordinated regulation of mucosal homeostasis.

Discussion

The female vagina is typically a Lactobacillus‐dominated microecological system in many populations, and its dysbiosis is a key trigger for diseases such as vaginitis and cervicitis (Gui et al. 2024). When driven by an overgrowth of aerobic bacteria, the vagina may present with inflammation, redness, edema, increased yellow discharge, and even erosions or ulcerations, characteristic of AV (Donders et al. 2017). Since antibiotic treatment confronts the risks of drug‐resistant infections and vaginal candidiasis, research has gradually shifted toward exploring the potential of live Lactobacilli as AV medications (Wang et al. 2023), while the application of corresponding postbiotic formulations remains relatively limited. Herein, we simultaneously evaluated the efficacy of live bacteria and their postbiotics in alleviating AV, aiming to contribute data promoting the diversification of probiotic market.

L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 were isolated and screened by our laboratory from the vaginas of healthy women, ensuring a clear origin that aligns with the natural adaptability and safety requirements within the vaginal environment. The selection of L. crispatus and L. plantarum was based on both biological relevance and translational considerations. As a dominant member of the vaginal microecology of healthy women, L. crispatus has been extensively documented for its protective role against various vaginal dysbiosis and inflammation (Cohen et al. 2020; Farr Zuend et al. 2023). Studies have demonstrated a significant depletion of L. crispatus in patients with AV, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic candidate (Wang et al. 2022; Zeng et al. 2023). In contrast, L. plantarum , although less dominant than L. crispatus in the vaginal niche, has been reported to exert antimicrobial and anti‐inflammatory effects and is occasionally detected within the vaginal Lactobacillus community (Ouarabi et al. 2019; Bae et al. 2024). Importantly, it generally achieves higher biomass yields during fermentation, offering practical advantages for large‐scale production and future clinical‐grade development (Zhang et al. 2025), despite its specific efficacy in AV not yet being fully elucidated. Our in vitro evaluation demonstrated that both strains possessed key potential probiotic properties, including acid tolerance, bile salt tolerance, antioxidant capacity, inhibitory activity against pathogenic bacteria, and resistance to certain antimicrobial agents (Figure 1). These results highlight the potential of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 as promising candidates for novel therapeutic applications.

To better recapitulate the clinical and microbiological characteristics of aerobic vaginitis, we established a polymicrobial AV mouse model using a pathogen cocktail composed of Escherichia coli , Group B Streptococcus, and Staphylococcus aureus following estradiol pretreatment. AV is clinically characterised by replacement of Lactobacillus‐dominated microbiota with aerobic opportunistic bacteria and pronounced inflammatory responses (Donders et al. 2017). The selected pathogens are frequently detected aerobic bacteria in AV patients and are known to possess virulence traits such as epithelial adhesion and induction of inflammatory responses (Ncib et al. 2022; Salmanov et al. 2023), which are consistent with the pathological features of AV. The use of a mixed aerobic infection model enables simulation of the polymicrobial nature of AV‐associated dysbiosis rather than a single‐strain infection, thereby more closely mimicking the inflammatory microenvironment observed in clinical settings. Moreover, estradiol pretreatment facilitates stable vaginal colonisation and enhances susceptibility to infection, which has been widely adopted in experimental models of vaginal inflammation (Chen et al. 2021). Histopathological changes and cytokine profiles observed in our model further confirmed successful induction of AV‐like inflammatory phenotypes (Figure 2). Subsequently, to investigate and compare the therapeutic effects of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 on AV induced by vaginal dysbiosis, we administered the two strains separately or in combination via intravaginal intake into AV mice models over a 2‐week period (Figure 2A). The dominance of Lactobacillus serves as a key marker of healthy vaginal microbiota (Witkin et al. 2013), which metabolises lactic acid while yielding bacteriocins and hydrogen peroxide, whose collaboration effectively inhibits the invasion and proliferation of pathogenic organisms (Tachedjian et al. 2017; Berard et al. 2023). Our data indicated that both L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 significantly ameliorated the vaginal pathological changes in AV mice. Notably, the combined usage of potential probiotics presented superior efficacy compared to monotherapy (Figure 2B–D). Moreover, treatment with these strains evidently reduced the levels of MPO and pro‐inflammatory cytokines including TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐17, while also restoring the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 (Figure 2E–I). Studies conclude that MPO and inflammatory cytokines are commonly utilised biomarkers for vaginal inflammatory conditions (Kwak et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2024). Thus, the above indicated that both L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 regulate immune responses and alleviate AV‐related symptoms by downregulating inflammatory mediators linked to vaginal dysbiosis, at the same time demonstrating a significant synergy.

Given the preliminary confirmation on the efficacy of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 in managing AV, we next explored the underlying molecular mechanisms. Notably, vaginal inflammation is closely linked to overactivation of the NF‐κB signalling pathway (Decout et al. 2024). When vaginal dysbiosis disrupts the local microenvironment, the binding of ligands to Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs) such as TLRs can stimulate downstream NF‐κB or Mitogen‐Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signalling pathways in MyD88‐dependent or independent ways. This stimulation promotes the transcription of various pro‐inflammatory factors, leading to significant infiltration of inflammatory cells (France et al. 2022). In our mouse model of AV, the expressions of crucial components in the NF‐κB signalling cascade, such as TLR4, MyD88, and p‐p65, were significantly upregulated, while being effectively reversed after intervening by L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 (Figure 3A–D). In line with these, Glick et al. unveiled that vaginal‐derived Lactobacilli, particularly the L. crispatus , can inhibit NF‐κB and Interferon‐related signal transduction by producing anti‐inflammatory β‐carboline compounds (Glick et al. 2024). Excessive production of nitric oxide (NO) by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) is a well‐documented contributor to inflammatory pathophysiological processes (Cinelli et al. 2020). Likewise, COX2 not only serves as a key downstream regulator of NF‐κB intracellularly but also is critically involved in promoting the development of apoptosis resistance, angiogenesis, proliferation, inflammation, and invasion (Cai et al. 2022). Administration of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 significantly suppressed the expression of iNOS and COX2, with the combined treatment showing superior efficacy compared to the single usage (Figure 3E,F). Summarising what is mentioned above, L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 effectively alleviated the inflammatory responses in AV by inhibiting the TLR4/NF‐κB signalling pathway and down‐regulating the expression of COX2 and iNOS.

Subsequently, whether the potential probiotic strains L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 could improve mucosal integrity and reshape immune homeostasis was explored. It is known that AV is often accompanied by the disruption of barrier function, leading to increased membrane permeability, which exacerbates inflammatory responses and exposes epithelial cells and resident immune cells to detrimental microbial byproducts (Wang et al. 2024). Previous studies have demonstrated that lactic acid synthesized by Lactobacillus species serves as a primary mediator in enhancing the cervicovaginal epithelial barrier, potentially through upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins (Delgado‐Diaz et al. 2022). Therefore, Occludin was selected as an indicator to evaluate epithelial barrier function (Kuo et al. 2022). MMP2/9 are two zinc‐dependent MMPs that mediate the degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and mucosal damage, with their overactivation being closely associated with epithelial barrier dysfunction (Steck et al. 2011). Consistent with the aforementioned, our results demonstrated that the expression of Occludin in the AV mice model was markedly suppressed, with the expression of MMP2 and MMP9 being the opposite. However, L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 effectively reversed this trend. This suggests that potential probiotics may enhance the synthesis of cell junction proteins by upregulating Occludin, while simultaneously inhibiting MMP2 and MMP9, thereby reducing their degradative effects on the ECM (Figure 4A–D). Moreover, the imbalance between Th17 and Treg cells exerts a critical effect in the pathogenesis of inflammation (Zhang et al. 2024). Specifically, overactivated Th17 cells secrete excessive amounts of pro‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐17, which further promotes the generation of TNF‐α and IL‐6, ultimately leading to tissue damage (Lee et al. 2015). Simultaneously, functional defects of Treg cells result in significantly reduced release of anti‐inflammatory mediators, such as TGF‐β and IL‐10, impairing the ability to suppress excessive immune responses (Leung et al. 2010). Previous studies have demonstrated the modulation of Lactobacillus toward Th17/Treg balance, thereby offering therapeutic potential for inflammatory diseases like colitis (Jang et al. 2019). Therefore, the alterations in Th17 and Treg cells within the vagina of model mice were analysed in this study. Compared to the M group, L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 significantly increased the proportion of Treg cells, exhibiting a more pronounced effect when applied in combination (Figure 4E,F). Thus, we speculated that L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 may alleviate the progression of AV by regulating the Th17/Treg balance.

To evaluate the efficacy of postbiotics, heat‐inactivated preparations of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 were prepared and delivered vaginally to AV mice. Our results demonstrated that the combined postbiotic treatment from L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 showed superior efficacy to monotherapy in ameliorating the AV inflammatory phenotype, as evidenced by a more pronounced reduction in pro‐inflammatory mediators (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, IL‐17, MPO) and a greater increase in the anti‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐10 (Figure 5). We then investigated whether these postbiotics act through mechanisms in line with their live counterparts. The activity of key components in TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB signalling axis were assessed, together with the levels of iNOS and COX2. Similar to potential probiotics, both postbiotics significantly alleviated inflammatory responses, with combined application being more satisfying. Likewise, the postbiotics upregulated the expression of Occludin, while reducing the levels of MMP2 and MMP9, thereby potentially maintaining the integrity of vaginal mucosal barriers. The balance of Th17/Treg cell differentiation was also restored by postbiotic treatment, indicating the reversion of excessive inflammatory damage (Figure 6). This systematic comparison illustrated parallel efficacy of potential probiotics and postbiotics, indicating that postbiotics are not merely metabolic byproducts but functionally active derivatives capable of recapitulating pleiotropic therapeutic effects of their progenitor probiotics.

Although our study verified the therapeutic efficacy of potential probiotics ( L. crispatus YBR‐01, L. plantarum YBR‐01) and their postbiotic derivatives in AV, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the absence of clinical trials restricts the applicability and generalizability of our findings. Existing research suggests that probiotics may pose potential risks to host health through transfer of antibiotic resistance genes, translocation into the bloodstream, and harmful metabolic activities (Liang et al. 2024). Although the two strains in this study demonstrated promising properties and preliminary safety in vitro, further validation through whole‐genome safety assessment, long‐term toxicity studies in animal models, and clinical trials is necessary to ultimately confirm their suitability. Also, our preliminary genomic sequencing revealed no critical resistance or virulence genes; this constitutes only an initial screen. Large‐scale safety validation and adverse event monitoring remain indispensable before any clinical application. Secondly, though the postbiotics we obtained meet the functional definition by the International Scientific Association of Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) consensus statement (Salminen et al. 2021), identifying specific bioactive components remains paramount. Moreover, given that the efficacy of potential probiotic strains may be dose‐dependent, the doses selected for this study were primarily based on our prior experimental data. While this approach allowed us to demonstrate initial biological activity, it precluded the establishment of a systematic dose–response relationship. Therefore, establishing the optimal therapeutic dose and the most effective inter‐strain dosing ratio will be pivotal for maximising therapeutic efficacy. Additionally, our preliminary experiments indicated that L. crispatus YBR‐01, L. plantarum YBR‐01, and their postbiotics play a role in vaginal remodelling, which hints at their potential efficacy against cervicitis, with the underlying mechanisms awaiting exploration. Thus, future research should integrate multi‐omics to better elucidate the molecular mechanisms whilst evaluating the long‐term efficacy of potential probiotics and derived postbiotics under various pathological conditions. Regarding clinical translation, our preclinical model demonstrates the feasibility of intravaginal delivery for potential probiotics and postbiotics. However, for future therapeutic development, expanding the repertoire of stable, patient‐compliant vaginal dosage forms that ensure adequate local delivery will be highly advantageous.

Conclusion

In the present investigation, L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 were isolated and identified from vaginal samples obtained from healthy donors. The potential probiotic properties of these strains were guaranteed through in vitro evaluations including growth kinetics, morphological analysis, acid and bile salt tolerance, antimicrobial activity, antioxidant capacity, and hemolytic and antibiotic susceptibility profiles. The effects of L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 in improving AV were observed in related mice model, which manifested as alleviated vaginal shortening and edema. Mechanically, potential probiotics alleviated AV inflammation through the TLR4/MyD88/NF‐κB axis, potentially maintained mucosal barrier by downregulating MMP2/9 while upregulating Occludin, and moderated immune homeostasis by rebalancing Th17/Treg. Moreover, the postbiotics obtained from L. crispatus YBR‐01 and L. plantarum YBR‐01 also replicated the above effects observed in AV following treatment with the corresponding strains. The above results established a data foundation for the launch of live LAB and their postbiotics as microecological agents in regulating vaginal dysbiosis and thereby mitigating gynaecological diseases.
