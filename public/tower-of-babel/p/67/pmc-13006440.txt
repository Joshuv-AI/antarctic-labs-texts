Delayed viral rebound post-ART interruption in infant macaques given SIV-specific neutralizing antibodies
Authors: Jenna S Powers, Katherine M Bricker-Holt, Brianna Williams, Veronica Obregon-Perko, Dormarie E Rivera Rodriguez, Sophia Sukkestad, Lakshita Lopez Lopez, Amir Dashti, Kaleaha Davis, Vinh Dinh, Jairo A Fonseca, George Cotsonis, Kirk A Easley, Kate S Levine, Lindsay R McManus, Ross Blanc, Ashley Heimann, Renguang Du, Ryan McNamara, Christine M Fennessey, Brandon F Keele, Rosemarie D Mason, Mario Roederer, Ann Chahroudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInterventions to prevent HIV rebound in the absence of antiretroviral therapy (ART) are needed. Here, we use a rhesus macaque model of pediatric HIV to assess the impact of simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) Env-specific rhesus monoclonal antibodies (RhmAbs) on viral rebound. We find that RhmAb-treated infants experience delayed rebound compared to controls (median of 64 versus 7.5 days, p = 0.0007). Time to rebound very strongly correlates with both ITS102.01-LS concentrations (r = 0.9, p = 0.002) and anti-drug antibodies against ITS103.01-LS (r = −0.95, p = 0.008). SIVgag-specific CD8+ T cell levels increase after RhmAb administration and negatively correlate with rebound viremia (r = −0.67, p = 0.0415). Treatment also expands neutralization breadth and potency against tier 2 and tier 3 viruses in 5/8 and 3/8 RhmAb-receiving animals, respectively. Thus, giving SIV Env RhmAbs prior to ART cessation leads to prolonged time to viral rebound, enhanced antiviral T cell responses, and broadening of neutralization capacity, thereby supporting use of neutralizing Abs to promote post-ART viral control.Keywords: HIV, SIV, neutralizing antibodies, ART interruption, viral control, CD8+ T cells, infants, perinatal

Introduction

Of the estimated 40 million people currently living with HIV-1, 1.4 million are children.1 Despite the significant progress that has been made to eliminate perinatal HIV, more than 120,000 children were newly diagnosed in 2024, emphasizing the continued need for sustainable HIV treatment strategies and ideally a cure for children. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has markedly improved disease outcomes; nevertheless, it falls short of eliminating the persistent HIV reservoir, creating a substantial barrier in the search for a cure. Additionally, children who acquire HIV perinatally must adhere to lifelong ART to prevent rebound, thereby exposing them to potential toxicities, the risk of developing resistance, and ongoing external and internalized stigma.2 Interventions to prevent viral rebound in the absence of ART would be highly beneficial for this vulnerable population.3

The use of broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) that target conserved epitopes on the HIV envelope (Env) glycoprotein has been the subject of intense study in recent years.4 These bNAbs can exert antiviral activity to neutralize virions released from infected cells following ART interruption. There have now been numerous clinical trials in adults that demonstrated a delay in viral rebound following treatment with bNAbs with breakthrough viremia associated with bNAb resistance.5,6,7,8,9,10,11 Although comparable strategies are frequently underexplored in pediatric populations, there are ongoing and completed phase 1 and 2 clinical trials focused on the use of bNAbs for treatment or prophylaxis of HIV in children.12 The recently published Tatelo study demonstrated safety and efficacy of two bNAbs (VRC01-LS and 10-1074, specific for the CD4-binding site and V3 loop of HIV Env, respectively) in children with HIV on suppressive ART started in the newborn period.13 Eleven out of 25 (44%) enrolled children maintained HIV-1 RNA <400 copies/mL through 24 weeks of monthly bNAb-only treatment. bNAb interventions offer several advantages to standard ART that are particularly beneficial for the pediatric population, including reduced toxicity, less-frequent administration, and the potential for post-treatment viral control through enhancement of host immune responses.14

Pediatric HIV infection is characterized by unique features such as a developing and dynamic immune system during initial reservoir establishment, heightened peak and set-point viral loads, swifter disease progression in the absence of treatment, and the necessity for lifelong ART across various developmental stages.15 Recognizing these specificities is imperative for tailoring cure strategies and addressing the complex dynamics of HIV in infants and children. Utilization of newborn and infant rhesus macaques (RMs) permits us to evaluate the safety and efficacy of novel interventions such as bNAb therapy in the pediatric setting and to gain insights into the mechanisms of protection.16 Here, we investigated the ability of a single dose of combination Env-specific rhesus-derived monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to delay viral rebound after ART cessation using a robust simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) RM model for pediatric HIV infection.

Results

Study design and interventions

Fourteen infant RMs were orally challenged with SIVmac251 at 4 weeks of age to simulate post-natal infection via breastfeeding (Figure S1A). At 4 weeks post-infection, all 14 infants were treated with a triple ART regimen for at least 16 months, with viral suppression achieved in all RMs by 26 weeks of ART (Figure S1B). Control and experimental groups were balanced for sex, Mamu A∗01 status, peak viral loads, and SIV DNA levels in blood and lymph node CD4+ T cells (Table S1). One week prior to ART interruption, eight RMs were given a subcutaneous (s.c.) injection of 4 different rhesus-derived SIV Env-specific IgG1 mAbs (RhmAbs) with the LS-encoding mutation (M428L/N434S) to extend their half-lives.17,18,19 The cocktail of SIV RhmAbs (ITS09.01-LS, ITS102.01-LS, ITS103.01-LS, and ITS113.01-LS) was designed to target different epitopes on the SIV Env protein as illustrated in Figure S1C. ITS09.01-LS targets the V1/V2 loop; ITS102.01-LS and ITS103.01-LS target the CD4-binding site proximal and distal regions, respectively; and ITS113.01-LS targets the MPER region. These SIV RhmAbs exhibit variable neutralizing capacity for SIVmac251, with ITS103.01-LS being the strongest neutralizer, followed by ITS102.01-LS and ITS113.01-LS (Figure S1D).20 The rationale to administer all four SIV RhmAbs was to exploit their different binding sites to achieve maximal targeting of SIV Env and to explore the possibility that both neutralizing and non-neutralizing antibody functions might influence viral rebound dynamics after ART interruption.21 One week after SIV RhmAb administration and at a corresponding time point for the six control RMs, daily ART was discontinued.

Delayed viral rebound following administration of SIV Env RhmAbs

Following analytical treatment interruption (ATI), plasma viral loads were monitored on days 7, 10, 14, 21, and 28 and then biweekly to capture the time to viral rebound (TTR). Rebound was defined as the first viral load >500 SIV RNA copies/mL plasma. All RMs in the control group experienced viral rebound in the first 2 weeks after ATI (Figure 1A). However, there was a significant delay in TTR in RMs treated with SIV Env RhmAbs (p = 0.0003, Figures 1A and 1B). The median TTR for the treatment group was 64 days compared to 7.5 days observed in the control group (p = 0.0007, Figure 1C). Notably, RhmAb-treated RMs displayed a wide range of rebound times, with three distinct subgroups. Early rebounders, RNf20 and RZc20, were viremic 21 days post-ATI, while RSe10 and RVf20 formed an intermediate group with viral rebound at 42 days post-ATI. The remaining four RMs rebounded much later, at 70 days post-ATI (Figures 1A and 1B). Area under the curve (AUC) analysis was performed on plasma viral loads from the day of ART interruption to 112 days post-ATI to gauge the cumulative viral burden experienced by RMs in each group. Again, there was a significant difference between the SIV RhmAb group and controls (median viral load AUC 350 log10 copies/mL in RhmAb-treated group versus 503 log10 copies/mL in controls, p = 0.0027, Figure 1D), indicating further modulation of viremia even after rebound occurred. However, post-ART rebound set points were similar between groups between days 84 and 112 (Figure 1E). One RM from the early rebound treatment group, RZc20, demonstrated impressive post-ART control, with viral load measured at 67 and 78 SIV RNA copies/mL at days 98 and 112, respectively (Figure 1A). This finding, along with the wide range in TTR, prompted us to investigate factors that contributed to the variations in post-ART viral dynamics within the treatment cohort.

SIV Env RhmAb concentrations and anti-drug antibodies in vivo

Serum concentrations of individual SIV Env RhmAbs were quantified by ELISA and are shown in Figure 2A. In the two early rebounders that became viremic at day 21 after ART interruption (RNf20 and RZc20), three of four RhmAbs, namely, ITS09.01-LS, ITS102.01-LS, and ITS113.01-LS, were last detected at day 28–35 after administration (day 21–28 of ATI). This trend of faster RhmAb clearance in the early rebounders was particularly pronounced for ITS113.01-LS; by contrast, 5 of the 6 other RMs still had measurable levels of ITS113.01-LS at day 77 after administration. Concentrations of ITS103.01-LS declined to undetectable levels in all RMs by day 42 after administration (day 35 of ATI). The mean half-life of ITS103.01-LS was 7 days, shorter than ITS09.01-LS (12 days), ITS102.01-LS (15 days), and ITS113.01-LS (10 days) (Figure 2B). These observed half-lives align with our prior work utilizing the same SIV Env RhmAbs in adult RMs.17

To investigate the differential longevity of SIV RhmAbs in circulation, we next quantified the formation of anti-drug antibodies (ADAs) against each RhmAb longitudinally for approximately 2 months. Figure 2C shows the ADA levels over time for each RhmAb. A distinct pattern again emerged, with the two early rebounders, RNf20 and RZc20, consistently exhibiting the highest ADA levels for 3 of the 4 RhmAbs (all but ITS09.01-LS). In animals that developed ADAs, an inverse relationship was observed between RhmAb concentration and ADA level, suggesting a direct impact of ADA formation on RhmAb persistence in the serum of treated RMs (Figure 2D).

SIV Env RhmAbs associated with a delay in time to rebound

To examine the relationships between RhmAbs and rebound dynamics, we next assessed potential correlations between days to rebound and the AUC of RhmAb concentration in serum from the first measurement to the day of rebound for each individual RM. This analysis demonstrated a very strong positive association for ITS102.01-LS (r = 0.9, p = 0.002) and weaker positive association with ITS113.01-LS (r = 0.6, p = 0.047) (Figure 2E), suggestive of a causal link between the concentrations of these RhmAbs and the delay in TTR in the treated RMs. No such association was identified for ITS103.01-LS, despite it being the most potent SIVmac251 neutralizer (Figure S1D). Additionally, there was no discernible relationship for the weakest neutralizing RhmAb, ITS09.01-LS. Similar analysis of TTR and ADAs revealed a strong negative association for both anti-ITS102.01-LS (r = −0.73, p = 0.025) and anti-ITS103.01-LS (r = −0.95, p = 0.0008) (Figure S2), implicating the CD4bs RhmAbs in determining viral rebound.

The development of possible resistance to the SIV RhmAbs and association with viral rebound were also examined. Mutations in Env associated with loss or gain of glycosylation sites are known to confer resistance to CD4bs antibodies.22 We, therefore, compared plasma virus sequences at one to two time points during viral rebound at four potential n-linked glycosylation sites (PNGS) that directly (positions 295, 371, and 479) or indirectly (414) affect CD4 binding. Compared to SIVmac251, we found evidence of a loss of PNGS at position 295 (NRT to SRT) in loop D in RVf20 at week 8 post-ATI and a gain of PNGS at site 414 (NTA to NTT) within the V4 loop in RNf20 (88% PNG by week 3 post-ATI and 100% by week 26) and in RFb20 (100% PNG by week 30) (Table S2). These mutations were associated with both loss and gain of glycans and appeared contemporaneously linked with viral rebound and becoming fixed in these three macaques. The remaining animals rebounded without evidence of any fixed mutation at the glycan sites of the CD4bs (Table S2) or of the V2 loop (not shown).

Broadening of antibody neutralization

We next sought to investigate the potential impact of passive bNAb administration on the autologous immune response. To determine whether the administered SIV Env RhmAbs influenced the de novo antibody responses against SIV, we measured neutralizing breadth at three time points: (1) pre-ART, (2) prior to RhmAb administration and ART interruption (pre-ATI), and (3) at peak viral rebound using IgG-purified plasma. Plasma was examined for neutralization against tier 1 (SIVsmE660.A8.CP3C), tier 2 (SIVmac251.30, the infecting strain), and tier 3 (SIVmac239.cs.23) viruses. All animals demonstrated high neutralizing titers against the easy-to-neutralize tier 1 virus at all time points and moderate neutralization against the tier 2 infecting strain at pre-ART and pre-ATI (Figure 3). High tier 1 neutralizing titers were maintained at peak viral rebound, and interestingly, five of eight RMs exhibited an increase in neutralization against the tier 2 infecting virus SIVmac251.30 (Figure 3). None of these five infants had detectable ITS103.01-LS in circulation at peak rebound, and only RVf20 had measurable ITS102.01-LS (13.5 μg/mL; Figure 2D). Interestingly, 7/8 RhmAb-treated infants (excluding RRh20) showed ITS113.01 levels above the IC50 for SIVmac251.30 (9.52 μg/mL)20 at peak rebound (Figure 2D), meaning that this RhmAb was not the driver of the observed differential neutralization in vitro and perhaps explaining the only weak association between ITS113.01-LS concentrations and TTR. Further, four of the five RMs with increased SIVmac251.30 neutralization also developed neutralizing activity against the tier 3 virus at peak viral rebound, albeit at low levels (Figure 3). By contrast, the infant RZc20 had 15.3 μg/mL ITS103.01-LS in serum at peak rebound (Figure 2D), and yet neutralization of SIVmac239.cs.23 was not observed in vitro despite the reported IC50 below 0.1 μg/mL19,20 (Figure 3), potentially due to high levels of ADAs. The control infants did not show evidence of increased neutralization breadth at peak rebound (Figure 3). These data suggest a broadening of neutralizing capacity in a subset of macaques through a vaccinal effect of RhmAb therapy, with virus neutralization in vivo and in vitro likely impacted by the presence of ADA.

Non-neutralizing antibody effector functions

Beyond neutralization, we hypothesized that key non-neutralizing effector functions of the used RhmAbs might influence viral rebound dynamics. We have previously characterized the Fc effector functions of these SIV RhmAbs in vitro.17 Here, purified IgG from plasma from the RhmAb-treated and control infants was analyzed using a systems serology approach. We assessed antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), antibody-dependent neutrophil phagocytosis (ADNP), and three antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) readouts (CD107α, MIP-1β, and IFNγ), comparing the pre-ATI (and pre-RhmAb for that group) time point to rebound peak. A similar pattern was observed for these antibody effector functions in the RhmAb group, with significant increases at rebound peak for ADCP and ADCC (Figure S3). Three infants in the control group also showed increases in ADCP and ADCC (Figures S3B and S3C). Interestingly, the only infants with positive ADNP scores at peak rebound in the RhmAb groups included three of the five macaques with detectable ITS09.01-LS, and we have previously showed that ITS09.01 had the highest ADNP capacity in vitro.17 The AUC of viral rebound was positively correlated with ADCP and ADNP levels at peak rebound in the RhmAb-treated group (Figure S4), disputing the hypothesis that these effector functions contribute to viral control. In fact, the infant with post-ART viral control (RZc20) consistently showed the lowest or second lowest Fc effector function (Figure S3). No correlations between antibody effector functions at either time point and TTR were found, suggesting that neutralization was the key factor responsible for delayed rebound in RhmAb-treated infants.

Enhancement of antiviral CD8+ T cell responses

We next considered whether administration of RhmAbs might be associated with enhancement of virus-specific T cell immune responses, as has been described previously for both SIV and HIV antibodies.17,23,24 This vaccinal effect is thought to result from virion:antibody immune complexes that are engulfed by antigen-presenting cells with subsequent antigen presentation to CD8+ T cells. As such, we measured SIV-specific T cell responses in both RhmAb-treated and control infants, comparing pre-ATI (and pre-RhmAb) to rebound peak, using an activation-induced marker (AIM) assay. At baseline prior to ATI, both groups exhibited similar low levels of SIV-specific CD8+ T cell activation (Figure 4A). However, at peak rebound, the RhmAb-treated group showed a significant increase in the frequency of activated SIV-specific CD8+ T cells compared to pre-ATI/pre-RhmAb (p = 0.0078; Figure 4A). A similar increase was not seen for control animals. Interestingly, we observed a negative correlation between SIV-specific CD8+ T cells at peak rebound and plasma viral load AUC during rebound in the RhmAb-treated group (r = −0.67, p = 0.0415; Figure 4B). Together, these data suggest that passive administration of virus-specific antibodies has a direct role in prolonging the time to viral rebound as well as the secondary benefits of (1) increasing potency and breadth of endogenous neutralizing antibodies and (2) stimulating antiviral CD8+ T cells that can contribute to viral control off ART.

Discussion

This investigation into the impact of passively administering SIV Env RhmAbs just prior to ART interruption in a (nonhuman primate) NHP model of pediatric HIV infection yielded several key findings. We confirmed that providing exogenous antibodies with neutralizing ability leads to delayed viral rebound after ART interruption, with a mean difference of 56.5 days. Quantifying the individual RhmAbs in serum and development of ADAs led to the conclusion that the CD4bs antibodies were the primary influencers of time to rebound, with a smaller contribution from the MPER-targeting RhmAb ITS113.01-LS.

The two infants who experienced the earliest rebound (RNf20 and RZc20) exhibited the most rapid decline in RhmAb serum concentrations. Specifically, in the case of RNf20, this rapid decline was observed for all four RhmAbs, while for RZc20, it was evident for three of four RhmAbs. This observation further underscores the association between RhmAbs and the timing of viral rebound. Interestingly, concentrations of the RhmAbs with both the strongest (ITS103.01-LS) and weakest (ITS09.01-LS) neutralizing ability against SIVmac251 in vitro were not directly correlated with TTR. One possible explanation is that development of high levels of ADAs precipitated the rapid decline in ITS103.01-LS levels, thereby limiting effectiveness, and, in fact, we noted that levels of ADAs to ITS103.01-LS was inversely correlated with TTR. In our prior work in adult macaques treated with the same RhmAb cocktail plus a latency reversal agent during ART, we also showed that ADAs to ITS103.01-LS were the most prominent.17 Repeated administration of the same RhmAb cocktail we used in adult RMs undergoing ATI did not result in significant ADAs, suggesting that prolonged exposure may diminish immunogenicity through the phenomenon of high-zone tolerance.25 In contrast, ITS09.01-LS, with comparable serum persistence to ITS102.01-LS and ITS113.01-LS and lower ADA development, still showed no association with TTR, pointing to neutralizing ability combined with limited ADA being the main drivers of delayed rebound. With this in mind, it is not surprising that the two early rebounders, RNf20 and RZc20, exhibited the highest levels of ADAs for all RhmAbs.

One benefit to therapy with Env-specific mAb therapy in HIV cure strategies is the potential for an additional immunogenic effect that aids in post-treatment virus control. Immune complexes composed of virus-specific mAbs and virions are theorized to elicit an augmented adaptive antiviral response through enhanced antigen uptake and presentation.14 This phenomenon could be manifested by an elevation in endogenous heterologous neutralizing antibodies,26 as observed in a subset of the RMs treated with RhmAbs in this study. Of the four RMs that had an increase in neutralization titers against the infecting strain (SIVmac251) and enhanced antibody breadth (with activity against the tier 3 SIVmac239cs.23), three were in the late rebound group (RRh20, RZj20, and RWj20) and the third was in the intermediate-rebound group (RVf20). This latter infant had detectable circulating ITS102.01-LS at peak rebound, potentially accounting for enhanced neutralization. Neither of the early rebounders (RNf20 and RZc20) developed an increase in neutralization capacity against tier 2 or tier 3 viruses. This result suggests a potential secondary benefit of mAb therapy beyond direct viral neutralization, offering promising implications for enhancing the host immune response against the virus, provided ADAs can be avoided.

The vaccinal effect can also manifest as enhanced virus-specific T cell responses, as described in both clinical trials of HIV bNAbs and in adult macaques treated with SIV RhmAbs or HIV bNAbs.17,23,24,27,28 In absence of ART, development of the cytolytic CD8+ T cell response is thought to contribute to post-peak decline of viremia,29,30 and elite controllers are characterized polyfunctional CD8+ T cells with high proliferative capacity and in vitro killing potential.31,32,33 Thus, enhancement of CD8+ T cell immune responses through passive infusion of bNAbs might be expected to contribute to viral control during ATI. Indeed, we found that the frequency of activated SIV-specific CD8+ T cells at peak viral rebound negatively correlated with rebound viremia in RhmAb-treated infants and that levels of these T cells were higher compared to pre-RhmAb treatment as well as to control infants. Future work to more deeply characterize CD8+ T cells in this and other similar studies, focusing on the characteristics associated with elite control of HIV, will be important as investigations to date of the vaccinal effect of bNAb immunotherapy have led to conflicting results.34

In summary, neutralizing mAbs specific to the HIV/SIV Env glycoprotein have emerged as a promising avenue to address the challenges associated with long-term ART. The findings from this study support and extend previous research, reinforcing the potential efficacy of mAbs as a safe approach in the pediatric setting. The observed delay in viral rebound after a single dose of SIV RhmAbs underscores their relevance in the pursuit of novel HIV cure strategies.

Limitations of the study

This research, while providing valuable insights into the potential benefits of Env-directed mAbs, has inherent limitations that should be considered. One notable constraint was the relatively small sample size, which may impact the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, it is crucial to acknowledge that while SIV serves as a highly relevant model for studying HIV, the two viruses are not identical. Variations in viral biology, immune responses, and disease progression between SIV and HIV could influence the applicability of study outcomes. The accumulation of viral diversity over 4 weeks of untreated SIVmac251 infection is likely much less than what is typically found in a child living with HIV.35 Moreover, while RMs represent a well-studied and validated model for HIV research, macaques may not fully replicate the complexities of human infection. Biological and immunological differences between humans and RMs underscore the need for prudence when translating findings from animal models to human therapeutic interventions.

Experimental model and study participant details

To investigate rebound dynamics following anti-SIV Env monoclonal antibodies, fourteen infant rhesus macaques (RMs, Macaca mulatta) were orally challenged with SIVmac251 to simulate postnatal infection via breastfeeding. The RMs were born at the Emory National Primate Research Center (ENPRC) and transferred to a nursery at two weeks of age, where they were housed in social pairs with full or protected contact for the study. We excluded RMs positive for the MHC alleles Mamu B∗08 and B∗17. At four weeks post infection, all fourteen infants were treated with a triple ART regimen for sixteen months. One week prior to ART interruption, eight RMs (five females, three males) were given a s.c. injection of four different SIV Env RhmAbs, and six RMs (two females, four males) remained on ART alone. Groups were balanced for peak viral loads and SIVgag copies/million CD4+ T cells from blood and lymph nodes at sixteen months of ART (Table S1). ART was then stopped for both the RhmAb treatment group and ART-only controls, and specimens continued to be collected for study until necropsy. RMs in the RhmAb treatment group had previously received the SMAC mimetic AZD5582 while on ART.36 This study was conducted in strict accordance with USDA regulations and the recommendations in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health and were approved by the Emory University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC PROTO201700571). Animal welfare was monitored daily. Appropriate procedures were performed to ensure that potential distress, pain, or discomfort was alleviated. The sedatives Ketamine (10 mg/kg) or Telazol (4 mg/kg) were used for blood draws and biopsies.

Method details

SIV infection of rhesus macaques

RMs were orally infected at 4 to 5 weeks of age with two consecutive doses of 105 TCID50 of SIVmac251. Four infants required multiple weekly 2-dose challenges prior to successful infection (range two to five challenges).

Antiretroviral therapy and RhmAb treatment

The RMs were treated with a triple ART regimen starting at four weeks post infection. The ART cocktail included two reverse transcriptase inhibitors: 5.1 mg/kg of Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF) and 40 mg/kg of Emtricitabine (FTC), plus 2.5 mg/kg of the integrase inhibitor Dolutegravir (DTG). ART was administered once daily at 1 mg/kg via the s.c. route. The Env-specific RhmAbs used in this study were isolated from SIV-infected rhesus macaques and expressed as full-length rhesus IgG1 modified to contain the LS-encoding mutation to maximize circulation half-life. ITS09.01-LS, ITS102.01-LS, ITS103.01-LS, and ITS113.01-LS target the vulnerable envelope sites V2, CD4 binding site (bs), CD4bs site proximal and MPER, respectively (Figure S1C). Eight RMs received 20 mg/kg of each RhmAb (80 mg/kg total) at one week prior to ATI via the s.c. route. The remaining six RMs did not receive any additional therapy prior to ART cessation at ATI.

Sample collection and processing

EDTA-anticoagulated blood samples were routinely collected for various analyses, including complete blood count, routine chemical analysis, and immunostaining. Within 1 h of phlebotomy, plasma was separated from the blood samples through centrifugation. Sera was isolated from plasma and stored separately at −80°C for future use. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were prepared using density gradient centrifugation. After washing, the PBMC suspensions were cryopreserved at −150°C until future use.

Plasma RNA quantification

RNA was extracted using QIAamp viral RNA columns (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, including an optional treatment with RNase-free DNase, and analyzed using one-step reverse transcriptase real-time PCR [ABI custom TaqMan Assays-by-Design] as previously described. A standard curve of known quantities of HIV gag RNA run in parallel was utilized for quantification. All samples were run and analyzed on an ABI 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems).

RhmAb pharmacokinetics and anti-drug antibody (ADA) assay

RhmAb concentration in the serum was measured by an Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) sandwich technique as previously described.17 This involved utilizing an anti-ITS monoclonal antibody with a mouse Fc region immobilized for capture and anti-rhesus-horseradish peroxidase for detection. Known concentrations of RhmAbs were used as a standard to calculate the concentration of individual RhmAbs in the sera. The lower limit of quantification was 3.0 ng/mL. ADA to each RhmAb was measured by a sandwich immunoassay using the fragment antigen-binding region (Fab) of each RhmAb immobilized for capture, with anti-Fc-rhesus-horseradish peroxidase used for detection. In this assay, standard concentrations of anti-ITS RhmAbs were found to be unreliable, so the optical density (OD) was used as a proxy for concentration.

Plasma neutralization assay

To evaluate for broadening of autologous neutralizing antibodies, IgG-purified plasma from both the pre-ATI and rebound peak timepoints was tested for neutralization against Tier 1 (SIVsmE660.A8.CP3C), Tier 2 (SIVmac251.30), and Tier 3 (SIVmac239.cs.23) viruses. IgG purification was done using the Protein A spin plate for IgG Screening per the manufacturer’s protocol (Thermo Scientific). Virus neutralization was measured using a standard TZM-bl reporter assay previously described.19 Briefly, serially diluted IgG purified plasma was incubated with SIV Env-pseudotyped virus for 30 min. Subsequently, TZM-bl target cells (10,000 cells/well) with 70 μg/mL DEAE-Dextran were added and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2. After 48 h, cells were lysed by addition of Steadylite Plus reagent (PerkinElmer) and shaking for 15 min at 600 RPM using an orbital plate shaker. Relative light units (RLU) was measured at 570 nm on a SpectraMax L luminometer (Molecular Devices). Each infection was performed in duplicate wells and each plate included a control column of cells only (no Ab or virus) and a control column of virus only (cells + virus without Ab) for background TZM-bl luciferase activity and maximal viral entry, respectively. Percent neutralization was determined by calculating the difference in average RLU between virus only wells (cells + virus column) and test wells (cells + Ab + virus), dividing this result by the average RLU of virus only wells (cell + virus column), and multiplying by 100. Negative results are reported as zero.

Antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) by monocytes

Human monocytes were extracted from leukopacks (StemCell). Leukopacks were obtained from anonymous donors. The human leukopacks were initially processed for red blood cell depletion with the EasySep RBC Depletion Reagent (StemCell). This was accomplished through the addition of EDTA to the leukopacks at a 1:1 ratio with EasySep Buffer (StemCell). Next, the leukopacks underwent a positive selection for CD14+ cells using the EasySep Human CD14 Selection Kit. The final CD14+ enriched PBMC population was quantified for concentration and viability using the Cell Countess 3 Automated Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher), and the total concentration of the cells was adjusted to 5E+6 cells/mL in R10 media which consist of (RPMI-1640 (Sigma Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma Aldrich), 5% penicillin/streptomycin (Corning, 50 μg/mL), 5% L-glutamine (Corning, 4 mM), 5% HEPES buffer (pH = 7.2) (Corning, 50 mM)).

Antigens were biotinylated using the EZ-link Sulfo-NHS-LC-LC-Biotin kit (Thermo Fisher) and filtered using Zeba Spin columns. The antigens were then coupled to fluorescent Yellow-Green neutravidin microspheres (Thermo Fisher). The bead-antigen conjugates were incubated with serum samples diluted at 1:50 in 1X PBS, pH = 7.4, for 2 h at 37°C to form immune complexes in a round-bottomed 96-well plate. The plates were then centrifuged and washed twice with 1X PBS to remove unbound complexes. The immune complexes were incubated with the PBMCs that were adjusted to 5E+5/well in 96-well round-bottom plates for 1.5 h at 37°C in 5% CO2. The monocytes were stained with CD14 PacBlue cocktail at 1:100 dilution and incubated for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. Next, the cells were washed with 1X PBS and fixed in the dark for 15 min at room temperature with 4% paraformaldehyde. After fixation, the cells were washed again and resuspended in 1X PBS.

Flow cytometry was performed using the iQue 3 (IntelliCyt). Monocytes were identified and gated through CD14+ staining. ADCP was quantified through Yellow-Green positivity (bead uptake), and population measurements were conducted using ForeCyt (v9.1). The antigen-specific PhagoScore was calculated as (% cells positive x Median Fluorescence Intensity of positive cells). Daily calibration of the iQue 3 was done with iQue Plus validation beads (Sartorius) diluted in 1X QSOL buffer (Sartorius).

Antibody-dependent neutrophil phagocytosis (ADNP)

For ADNP, antigens were coupled to fluorescence microspheres similar to ADCP. Neutrophils were enriched from human whole blood acid-citrate-dextrose (ACD) obtained from two anonymized donors (StemCell). Neutrophils (CD66+) were enriched using the StemCell Neutrophil Isolation Kit. First, the whole blood was transferred to a 50 mL conical, and then 50 μL/mL blood of RapidSpheres and 50 μL/mL blood of Isolation Cocktail were added and mixed with the whole blood, and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Neutrophil Isolation EasySep buffer was added to bring the total volume to 50 mL and mixed before being placed into the Easy50 magnet and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. At the end of the incubation, the enriched cell suspension was pipetted and collected in a new tube. The same volume of RapidSpheres from before was then added and mixed to repeat the process of incubation on the magnet, and the cell suspension was again collected in a new tube. The isolated cells were then resuspended at a concentration of 5E+5 cells/mL in R10 media.

Immune complexes were formed likewise to ADCP. Once the immune complex formation incubation completed, the plates were washed using PBS and centrifuged, and unbound supernatant was removed. Then, the isolated cells were added to the plates and incubated for 1 h at 37°C at 5% CO2. After incubation, the plates were again centrifuged and washed in the same manner using 1X PBS. Neutrophils were stained using anti-CD66 PacBlue diluted in 1X PBS at room temperature for 30 min. Cells were spun and supernatant was removed, and were washed again using 1X PBS. After the centrifugation and removal of supernatant, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. Cells were centrifuged and washed using 1X PBS. After centrifugation of removal of supernatant, cells were resuspended in ice-cold 1X PBS and analyzed using flow cytometry on the iQue 3.

Neutrophils were selected using the anti-CD66+ PacBlue. Phagocytic scores were calculated as above. All values reported are the mean of biological donor replicates. The use of human leukopacks and neutrophils to quantify antibody effector assays including ADCP, ADNKA, and ADNP responses in NHP samples has been previously reported and validated.37,38,39 All reagents and materials used are listed in the key resources table.

Antibody-dependent natural killer cell (NK) activation (ADNKA)

ADNKA was the approach used to study antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). To assess ADNKA capacity from the serum samples, ELISA plates were initially coated with 3 μg/mL of the target antigen and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Plates were washed with 1X PBS and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma Aldrich) diluted in 1X PBS for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, plates were washed with 1X PBS. Serum samples were diluted at 1:25 in 1X PBS and added to the ELISA plates to form immune complexes overnight at 4°C.

Human natural killer (NK) cells were isolated from fresh human peripheral blood leukopacks from anonymous donors using the EasySep Human NK Cell Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies). This immunomagnetic negative selection procedure labels non-NK cells with antibody complexes and magnetic particles, allowing their removal with EasySep magnets (StemCell Technologies). NK cell concentration and viability were determined using the Cell Countess 3 Automated Cell Counter. Cells were resuspended at 1.5E+6 cells/mL in R10 medium consisting of RPMI-1640 (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich), 5% penicillin–streptomycin (Corning; 50 μg/mL), 5% L-glutamine (Corning; 4 mM), and 5% HEPES buffer (Corning; 50 mM, pH 7.2). NK cells were activated overnight with 1 ng/mL interleukin-15 (IL-15; StemCell Technologies) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Following overnight immune complex formation, ELISA plates were washed with 1X PBS, and NK cells were added at a concentration of 5E+5 cells/mL in R10 medium supplemented with anti-CD107a–phycoerythrin (PE)–Cy5 (BD Biosciences), brefeldin A (5 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), and GolgiStop (BD Biosciences). Cells were incubated for 5 h at 37°C with 5% CO2.

After incubation, cells were washed with 1X PBS and stained for 20 min at room temperature with cell surface markers anti-CD3 Pacific Blue (BD Biosciences), anti-CD16 allophycocyanin (APC)-Cy5 (BD Biosciences), and anti-CD56 PE-Cy7 (BD Biosciences). Cells were then washed, fixed with Perm A (Life Technologies) for 15 min, washed again, and permeabilized with Perm B (Life Technologies) containing anti-MIP-1β PE and anti-IFN-γ FITC (BD Biosciences) for 20 min at room temperature. After a final wash with 1X PBS, cells were resuspended in 1X PBS for flow cytometric analysis.

Data, measured as fluorescence intensity, were acquired using an iQue 3 Cytometer (Intellicyt). NK cells were gated as CD56+/CD16+/CD3-, and activation was assessed as the percentage of cells expressing CD107a, IFN-γ, or MIP-1β. All assays were performed in duplicate using NK cells from at least two healthy donors (one male and one female).

SIV CD4bs potential N-linked glycosylation site (PNGS) sequencing

We utilized a simple PCR-based sequencing approach implemented on the MiSeq instrument to query the N-linked glycosylation sites within the CD4 binding site and the V2 loop of SIVmac251. PCR was performed using primers containing Illumina’s P5 and P7 adaptors and directly sequenced on MiSeq instrument. Viral RNA was extracted from plasma using QIAamp Viral RNA kit (Qiagen) and cDNA was generated using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (ThermoFisher) with a virus specific primer for the CD4bs (5′-TTCTGCCACCTCTGCACTCATGG-3′) and for the V2V3 loops (5′-CCTATCATTGATTGGTTGTGAGTG-3′). Two overlapping PCR amplicons were generated using High Fidelity Platinum Taq (ThermoFisher). For the CD4bs, primers DL.P7 (5′-TAGAACTTATATTTACTGGCATGG-3′) and V5.P5 (5′-GCCACCTCTGCACTCATGG-3′) were used. For the V2 loop, primers V2.V3.P7 (5′-CAGGATAATTGCACAGGCTTGG-3′) and V2.V3.P5 (5′-GCCACCTCTGCACTCATGG-3′) were used. PNGSs were extracted by hand and compared to the consensus SIVmac251 sequence.

Assessment of SIV-specific T cell responses and activation

SIV-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses were measured using a multiplexed activation-induced marker (AIM) assay adapted from Lemieux et al.40 Cryopreserved rhesus macaque PBMCs obtained before analytical treatment interruption (ATI) and at week 3 of ATI were thawed in a 37°C water bath and washed in prewarmed RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, penicillin/streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, and DNase (1:100 dilution of 2 mg/mL stock). Cells were then resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium (without DNase) and rested overnight at 37°C and 5% CO2 to reduce cytokine background and remove apoptotic cells damaged during the freeze-thaw process.41

The following morning, cells were counted and diluted to 0.5×106 cells per condition in complete RPMI 1640 medium containing DNase (1:100 dilution). PBMCs were stimulated for 20 h with overlapping peptide pools spanning the SIVmac239 Gag protein (BEI Resources, NIAID, NIH, HRP-12364) at a final concentration of 2.5 μg/mL per peptide, in the presence of co-stimulatory antibodies CD28 (clone CD28.2, BioLegend) and CD49d (clone 9F10, BioLegend) at 1 μg/mL each. DMSO-treated cells served as negative controls.

Following stimulation, cells were stained with LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue Dead Cell Stain (Invitrogen), followed by surface antibodies: CD20-PE-Cy5 (clone 2H7, BioLegend), CD16-PE-Cy5 (clone 3G8, BioLegend), CD14-PE-Cy5 (clone M5E2, BioLegend), CD3-APC-Cy7 (clone SP34-2, BD Biosciences), CD4-BV785 (clone OKT4, BioLegend), CD8α-BV711 (clone SK1, BioLegend), CD25-PE Vio 770 (clone REA570, Miltenyi Biotec), CD69-BV421 (clone FN50, BD Biosciences), and OX40-PE (clone L106, BD Biosciences). Cells were then permeabilized using Cytofix/Cytoperm (BD Biosciences) and stained for intracellular Ki67-R718 (clone B56, BD Biosciences). Samples were fixed with 1% paraformaldehyde and acquired on a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer.

SIV-specific T cells were identified using Boolean OR gating for cells co-expressing CD25+CD69+ OR OX40+CD25+ OR CD69+OX40+. This combination of activation markers was selected based on their complementary kinetics of optimal surface expression between 16 and 48 h post-stimulation,42 ensuring that each cell was counted only once. Net antigen-specific responses were calculated by subtracting the background values from those of unstimulated controls. Ki67 expression was additionally assessed within the total CD3+CD8+ T cell population as a marker of proliferative status, independent of antigen-specific gating. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.10).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Software (version 10). Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided p value of less than 0.05. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size, and investigators were not blinded to group allocations or when assessing outcomes. In Figure 1B, the survival curve comparing viral rebound (>500 copies/mL) in RhmAb treatment group to controls was constructed using a log rank test (Mantel-Cox test) and Gehan-Breslow Wilcoxon test to assign extra weight to early time points. In Figures 1C–1E, A Mann-Whitney test was used to compare the RhmAb treatment group to the controls. Area under the curve (AUC) for log10 viral load and SIV RhmAb concentrations was calculated using the trapezoid method to calculate total AUC for each animal that is not dependent on the baseline level using the approach described by Potteiger et al.43 Figures 2E, 3B, S2, and S4 analyses were performed using nonparametric Spearman rank correlations. Comparisons in Figures 3 and S3 were made using the nonparametric Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test.
