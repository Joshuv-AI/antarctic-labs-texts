Determination and prediction of standardized ileal amino acid digestibility of wheat for pullets during the brooding and growing phases
Authors: Shiqian Liu, Kaiyue Ren, Chong Chen, Tong Xing, Liang Zhao, Liren Ding, Feng Gao, Lin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWheat serves as a major alternative to corn in layer diets. However, the assessment and predictive models for standardized ileal digestibility of amino acids (SIDAA) from wheat in pullets have not yet been fully established, limiting precision formulation. This study aimed to evaluate the physical properties, conventional nutritional components, amino acid profiles and SIDAA in 10 wheat samples from different sources when fed to pullets during brooding (d 28-31) and growing (d 92-95) periods, and to develop SIDAA prediction equations base on physical properties, chemical components of wheats. A total of 594 and 396 Jingfen No.8 pullets, during brooding and growing periods respectively, were randomly assigned to one of 11 dietary groups. The diets included a nitrogen-free diet for measuring the basal endogenous losses of amino acids, and 10 test diets containing 92.53 % wheat as the sole source of amino acids. Moreover, all diets contained 0.5 % of titanium dioxide as indigestible indicator. The chemical components of the 10 wheat samples from different sources showed considerable variation. In particular, the coefficients of variation for ether extract (EE), crude fiber, neutral detergent fiber, calcium (Ca), and total phosphorus (TP) all exceeded 10 %. The SID values of the 15 analyzed amino acids differed significantly among 10 wheat samples (P < 0.05). During the brooding period, the SID of most amino acids were significantly correlated with gross energy (GE), crude ash, EE, Ca, methionine (Met), histidine (His), and tryptophane (Trp) (P < 0.05). During the growing period, the SID of most amino acids exhibited correlations with GE, crude ash, acid detergent fiber, TP, and essential amino acids, including Met, His and Trp (P < 0.05). Seventeen prediction equations were developed for each period: the serine equation showed the optimal model fit during brooding period (R² = 0.869), while four equations with R2 > 0.80 were identified for the growing period. In conclusion, this experiment demonstrates that the chemical properties of wheat can be used to establish accurate equations for predicting the SID of most amino acids in pullets, serving as a reliable tool for rapid and accurate evaluation of amino acid nutritional value of wheat in pullets during both the brooding and growing periods.Keywords: Amino acids, Wheat, Pullet, Standardized ileal digestibility, Prediction equations

Introduction

Feed accounts for 60-70 % of poultry production cost (Toghyani et al., 2015). With the continuous development and expansion of China's animal husbandry and feed industry, corn-soybean-meal–based formulations have long dominated feed production. In recent years, China's limited corn supply has prompted the poultry industry to seek viable alternative feed ingredients.

Wheat is one of the most widely used energy-protein ingredients in animal feed. Rich in a well-balanced array of nutrients, wheat delivers substantial quantities of protein and amino acids (AA), including leucine, arginine, glutamic acid and proline, making it an indispensable component in modern poultry diets. However, the standardized ileal digestibility (SID) of amino acids (SIDAA) in wheat is highly variable among cultivars and harvest years, primarily due to fluctuations in endogenous anti-nutritional factors and fiber content (Gilani et al., 2012). Given this variability, accurate evaluation and application of SID values of AA are particularly critical for formulating balanced diets for poultry. Moreover, the use of SID values enables an accurate formulation due to the additivity (Lee et al., 2020). Formulating diets based on SIDAA values allows for precise alignment of rations with animal AA requirements, thereby minimizing nitrogen excretion into the environment (Lu et al., 2020).

As nutrient supply in the brooding and growing phases critically shapes pullet development, immune function, and future laying performance, accurately assessing the SID of wheats from various sources is pivotal for optimizing the economic and environmental efficiency of egg production. The precise assessment of SID in feed ingredients for laying hens is of great importance for minimizing nutrient wastage in diet formulation (Oketch et al., 2023; Siegert et al., 2023). As the fundamental building blocks for protein synthesis, amino acids digestibility directly determines the efficacy of dietary protein. As a key energy ingredient in laying hen diets, it is critical for accurate quantification of amino acid digestibility of wheat to underpin precise, performance-driven feed formulation. However, while conventional apparent ileal digestibility of amino acids (AIDAA) fails to account for endogenous amino acid losses, the SIDAA corrects for these basal endogenous losses, thereby providing a more accurate estimate of the true amino acid availability in feed ingredients. Basal endogenous amino acids are unavoidable, diet-independent losses into the gut that arise from the physiological turnover of intestinal epithelial cells (Adedokun et al., 2015). A substantial body of literature has documented the measurement of ileal amino-acid flows in both broilers and laying hens using a variety of methodologies (Adedokun et al., 2007a, 2009). Consequently, ileal rather than fecal sampling is mandatory for reliable determination of standardized amino acid digestibility in wheat and other feed ingredients for pullets (Ravindran et al., 1999). This methodological shift from total-tract to ileal amino acid digestibility assessment is exemplified by Lemme et al. (2004). By accounting for basal endogenous amino acid losses, this approach enables a more accurate reflection of the true digestibility and absorption of amino acids. Nevertheless, systematic studies specifically targeting SIDAA of wheat for pullets during the rearing and growing phases remain scarce, and available data reveal substantial variability attributable to cultivar, harvest maturity, processing methods, and other factors.

Prediction equations for SIDAA based on the chemical properties of feed ingredients offer a practical alternative to determine SIDAA (Ebadi et al., 2011; Cowieson et al., 2019), overcoming the limitations of high cost and long duration associated with animal experimental assays, thereby providing a theoretical basis for dynamic diet adjustments. However, such models remain underdeveloped for predicting the amino acid digestibility of specific ingredients, like wheat, in laying hen pullets. Therefore, this study was conducted to determine the SIDAA of wheats for pullets during brooding and growing periods and develop prediction equations based on its physicochemical properties. The results aim to provide a scientific basis for precision feeding strategies and to enhance existing feed formulation databases.

Materials and methods

Wheat samples

In this study, wheat samples (coded W1 to W10) were collected from 10 provinces across China: Tianjin; Yangzhou (Jiangsu Province); Chengde (Hebei Province); Chengdu (Sichuan Province); Panjin (Liaoning Province); Zhumadian (Henan Province); Huainan (Anhui Province); Xianyang (Shaanxi Province); Wuhan (Hubei Province); and Nanjing (Jiangsu Province). Prior to chemical analyses, all wheat samples were ground and passed through a 40-mesh sieve and stored at 4 °C prior to chemical analysis.

Animals, experiment design and sample procedure

All animal procedures in this study were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (Approval Number: NJAU.No20241104222).

A total of 594 and 396 Jingfen No.8 pullets in the brooding (aged 28-31days) and growing (aged 92-95 days) phases, respectively, were used in this study. Their average body was 226.63 g at 28 days of age and 1104.90 g at 92 days of age, with both flocks exhibiting a uniformity exceeds 85 %. Pullets were randomly assigned to 11 dietary treatments in a completely randomized design, with 6 replicates per treatment. There were 9 birds per replicate during brooding period and 6 birds per replicate during the growing period. Treatment 1 was a nitrogen-free diet (NFD), while treatments 2 to 11 were the wheat-based experimental diets. Birds were housed in three-tiered stacked cages with ad libitum access to feed and water, and were fed three time daily (07:00, 11:00, and 18:00) under a 12-h light cycle. The ambient temperature was maintained at 33 °C with a relative humidity of 65 % to 70 % during the first week, and was then reduced by 3 °C each week until it reached 24 °C, with a relative humidity of 50 % to 60 %. During the non-experimental periods, all pullets had ad libitum access to a standard commercial diet and water. The experimental diets were provided for a continuous 72-h period from 28 to 31 days of age (brooding phase) and from 92 to 95 days of age (growing phase), respectively. At the end of each experimental period (days 31 and 95), all pullets were humanely euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation followed by carotid-artery exsanguination. The abdominal cavity was immediately opened, and the ileum was rapidly excised. After folding the ileum, the final 2-cm segment was discarded, and the remaining posterior segment was retained for digesta collection. Digesta from birds within the same replicate were gently flushed into an aluminum tray using a syringe filled with distilled water. All digesta samples were immediately stored at −20 °C, subsequently lyophilized, and ground for further analysis.

Sample preparation and analyses

The 10 wheat-based experimental diets were ground to pass a 2-mm screen and stored at 4 °C until subsequent analysis. The physical properties of wheat samples, including thousand-kernel weight (TKW), and thousand-kernel volume (TKV) were determined according to the methodologies described by Wang and Fu (2020). Conventional nutrient components of wheat samples, including crude protein (CP), ether extract (EE), crude fiber (CF), calcium (Ca), and total phosphorus (TP) were analyzed chemically in accordance with AOAC International (2000) methods: CP (990.03), Ash (920.39), CF (978.10), EE (942.05), Ca (968.08), and TP (985.35). Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) contents in wheats were determined using an Ankom220 Fiber Analyzer (Ankom Technology, Macedon, NY, USA) according to a modified version of the methods outlined by Van Soest et al. (1991) and Yun et al. (2023). Gross energy (GE) was determined using an oxygen-nitrogen calorimeter (TYHW-V, Hebei Tianyu Instrument and Meter Manufacturing Co., Ltd., China). Total starch (TS) and phytic acid (PA) contents in wheat samples were determined using commercial assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China; catalog nos. BC0705 for TS, and BC5840 for PA).

Sulfur-containing AA (Cys and Met) in wheat samples, wheat-based experimental diets and digesta samples were analyzed using a LA8080 automatic amino acid analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan). The samples were analyzed after filtration and volume fixing using the national standard Determination of Sulfur-containing Amino Acids in Feeds - Ion Exchange Chromatography (AOAC 994.12; AOAC, 1994). Trp determination was analyzed using an alkaline hydrolysis, and the content was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (U3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in accordance with the AOAC Official Method 982.30. The remaining fifteen AA were determined by conventional acid hydrolysis concentrations were measured with an automatic amino-acid analyzer (LA8080, Hitachi, Japan) following AOAC official methods (AOAC 994.12; AOAC, 1994). The quantitative determination of TiO2 in diets and digesta was performed according to the method by Liu et al. (2024).

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were initially organized using Microsoft Excel 2019 and subsequently analyzed by one-way ANOVA (SPSS Statistics 27.0) followed by Duncan's post hoc test. Through Pearson correlation and stepwise regression analysis, key interrelationships among the indicators were identified, and a corresponding SIDAA prediction model was developed. The results are expressed as the mean value among replicates of each treatment group. The value of P < 0.05 indicates a significant difference.

Results

Chemical compositions and AA contents of wheat samples

As shown in Table 1, the coefficients of variation (CV) of most conventional nutrients of wheats exceed 10 %, including EE, CF, NDF, Ca, and TP. On a DM basis, GE averaged 18.13 MJ/kg. CP, EE, crude ash, NDF, ADF, TP, Ca, and TS averaged 14.88 %, 2.48 %, 1.88 %, 20.06 %, 3.71 %, 0.55 %, 0.06 %, and 66.46 %, respectively. Bulk density, TKV, and TKW averaged 881.29 g/L, 58.29 L per 1000 grains, and 51.43 g per 1000 grains, respectively. The CV of conventional chemical compositions ranged from 0.89 % to 30.14 %, with DM exhibiting the lowest variability (CV = 0.89 %) and Ca the highest (CV = 30.14 %). The content of the anti-nutritional factor PA averaged 0.73 %, with a CV of 22.61 %. The CV of tryptophan (Trp), histidine (His), and tyrosine (Tyr) contents exceed 10 %. On a DM basis, essential amino acids (EAA) showed the following average contents: lysine (Lys) 0.42 %, methionine (Met) 0.25 %, threonine (Thr) 0.41 %, tryptophan (Trp) 0.10 %, arginine (Arg) 0.62 %, leucine (Leu) 0.89 %, phenylalanine (Phe) 0.62 %, histidine (His) 0.36 %, valine (Val) 0.60 %, and isoleucine (Ile) 0.48 %. The non-essential amino acids (NEAA) presented the following average contents: aspartic acid (Asp) 0.69 %, serine (Ser) 0.62 %, glutamic acid (Glu) 4.17 %, glycine (Gly) 0.57 %, alanine (Ala) 0.48 %, cysteine (Cys) 0.32 %, tyrosine (Tyr) 0.32 %, and proline (Pro) 1.46 %.

Discussion

In this study, the TKW, a key criterion for wheat grading, ranged from 766.9 to 947.4 g/L among the 10 samples, with a mean value of 881.3 g/L. The average TKW value observed in this study is consistent with the findings of Anderson and Bell (1983) and Jha et al. (2011) for five diverse cultivars. Among the conventional nutrients, the CP content ranged from 13.70 % to 16.15 %, exhibiting markedly greater variability (CV = 4.65%) than is typically reported. This variability corroborates the findings of Azhar et al. (2019), who demonstrated that wheat protein levels are highly dependent on cultivar and growing conditions. As indicated in Table 1, considerable nutrient variability exists among wheat samples of different origins, underscoring their representativeness. Furthermore, previous findings have established wheat cultivar as a critical determinant of digestibility variations, thereby highlighting the importance of considering region-specific data in cross-study comparisons (Bedford et al., 1998; Bedford and Schulze, 1998). The higher protein level in wheats may positively affect AA digestibility, which aligns with the findings of Amerah et al. (2007), who found a positive correlation between wheat protein content and AA availability in broilers. The wide range observed in NDF content of wheats (12.64 % to 29.20 %, CV = 24.69 %) aligns with the report by Choct (1999) who established fiber as a major factor affecting the efficiency of nutrient utilization from wheat-based diets. Both the contents of wheat Ca (CV = 30.14 %) and total phosphorus (CV = 14.86 %) exhibited high variability, which is consistent with the findings of Ravindran et al. (2005), who documented substantial mineral variability among feed ingredients. This variability is likely driven by cultivar genetics, soil levels, fertilization practices, harvest contamination, and analytical differences. Overall, the mean values of most routine proximate components aligned well with the reference ranges provided in the Chinese Feed Composition and Nutritional Value Tables (2024). It is noteworthy, however, that phytic acid contents in wheat samples ranged from 0.48% to 0.99 % and known for its antinutritional effects in poultry nutrition (Selle et al., 2009).

Variations in AA contents were observed across the 10 wheat samples with CV ranging from 12.27 % to 25.61 %. Among them, Lys content (0.36 to 0.45 %) demonstrated moderate variability (CV = 7.01 %), which is consistent with the finding reported by Yun et al. (2023). This is of particular significance, as Ali et al. (2023) have indicated that Lys is typically the first limiting amino acid in wheat-based poultry diets. The most significant variation was observed in wheat Trp levels (CV = 25.61 %), which is consistent with the variability in cereal Trp content noted by Adeola and Cowieson (2011). Although the contents of individual essential amino acids (EAA, such as Lys and Met) vary among different wheat samples, the total EAA proportion of wheats remains relatively stable, exhibiting only minor overall variability (CV = 5.18 %).

During the brooding and growing phases of pullets, endogenous amino acid losses at the terminal ileum exhibited significant developmental changes, which are consistent with previous findings in poultry nutrition research. Specifically, the ileal endogenous amino acid losses of pullets during the brooding phase were lower than those during the growing phase, which contrasts with the findings of Adedokun et al. (2007b). However, this pattern aligns with observations from Qiu et al. (2023), who reported that endogenous amino acid losses were higher in 35-week-old laying hens than in 21-day-old broilers, with corresponding differences in SID values between the two age groups. Collectively, these variations may be attributed to factors such as feed composition, animal breed, and age (Huang et al., 2006; Adedokun et al., 2009). Onyango et al. (2009) demonstrated that dietary phytic acid increases mucin secretion and endogenous amino acid losses from the chicken gut, with the magnitude of the loss depending on the specific form of phytate present. In line with this, other studies have indicated that antinutritional factors in poultry diets can reduce amino acid digestibility and elevate endogenous amino acid secretion (Angkanaporn et al., 1994). In the present study, however, ileal endogenous Met losses decreased from 264.74 to 211.59 mg/kg DMI between the brooding and growing phases of pullets, a trend that may be attributed to the enhanced metabolic efficiency of sulfur-containing amino acids in mature poultry (Lemme et al., 2004). This age-related decline aligns with the finding of Barua et al. (2021), who reported a reduction in endogenous Trp loss as birds aged. This phenomenon likely results from a relative decrease in mucin secretion and improved digestion and absorption of endogenous proteins with increasing age. Furthermore, the observed decrease in Met loss may also reflect its higher absorption rate in the gastrointestinal tract compared to other amino acids (Webb et al., 1990). Accurate determination of basal ileal endogenous amino acid losses is critical for implementing SID-based feed formulation (Adedokun et al., 2011; Adeola et al., 2016). In this study, most ileal endogenous amino acid losses increased with pullet age, which supports the previous findings that such losses are influenced by the age and physiological development of poultry (Adedokun et al., 2011, 2018).

Among the 10 wheats evaluated in this study, the SID of Thr was consistent with previous report by Fan et al. (1993) and Osho et al. (2019), with all values, except for Trp during the growing period, showing general congruence. The lower SID of Trp observed during the growing phase may be ascribed to its inherently low and highly variable concentration in wheats, which increased analytical and biological variability. Furthermore, the SID values obtained in this experiment were in substantial agreement with those reported by Zhao et al. (2019) for 10 wheat varieties. Consistent with their findings, Lys and Thr exhibited the lowest SID values among the essential amino acids. This observation can be explained by the fact that most portion of the Lys is sequestered within the cell-wall-bound aleurone and germ layers; when these tissues remain intact, Lys is physically shielded from proteolytic enzymes, resulting in reduced ileal recovery (Green et al., 1987). Similarly, the low SID of Thr is likely due to the continuous secretion and turnover of mucins—glycoproteins rich in Thr. A fraction of these mucins remains undigested at the distal ileum, leading to elevated Thr levels in ileal digesta stems from mucin accretion and consequently depressing the SID of Thr (Lien et al., 1997; Adedokun et al., 2018).

The low digestibility of certain amino acids may also be associated with antinutritional factors present in wheat. Phytic acid, a key antinutritional component in wheat, can chelate certain mineral elements that act as enzymatic constituents or activators, potentially impairing the activity of metal cofactors required by digestive enzymes (such as lipase, trypsin, and chymotrypsin) in animals. This interference may reduce enzymatic activity and, consequently, lower the digestibility of specific nutrients. Furthermore, dietary fiber and phytic acid can interact in ways that affect protein solubility, digestive enzyme activity, and amino acid digestibility in monogastric animals. NDF may physically encapsulate proteins, diminishing the efficiency of digestive enzymes, while phytic acid can form complexes with proteins and minerals, thereby hindering protein hydrolysis and amino acid absorption. As demonstrated by Pieper et al. (2015), dietary composition significantly influences pancreatic enzyme activity and intestinal protein metabolism, which may help explain the high variability in the digestibility of certain amino acids observed in the present study.

Accurate evaluation of amino acid digestibility in feedstuffs requires precise determination of digestible AA content and the development of predictive equations for the SID of amino acids. Such equations can significantly improve the formulation accuracy of premixes and enable more precise diet formulation. The primary advantage of using SID values lies on their capacity to correct for basal endogenous amino acid losses, thereby enabling the formulation of diets that accurately meet the metabolic requirements of pullets (Douglas and Parsons, 1999). Numerous recent studies have demonstrated that prediction equations for AID and SID of amino acids in broilers and swine diets can be reliably developed from the chemical attributes of feed ingredients (Feng et al., 2023; Kaewtapee and Mosenthin, 2024), thereby reinforcing the significant relationship between protein and amino acid digestibility (Sheikhhasan et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). Moreover, Cao et al. (2025) developed a prediction equation for SIDAA, identifying GE and standardized ileal digestible lysine (rLys) as the core predictive variables. Their research framework is directly applicable to wheat-layer systems. The efficiency of protein digestion governs the release and absorption of amino acids, and amino acid digestibility in turn reflects the extent of protein degradation and utilization within the digestive tract. Although the amino acid digestibility of wheat can be influenced by a host of factors, the present study identified CP, EE, NDF, ADF, and ash as key properties strongly correlated with the SID of most amino acids, suggesting they can serve as reliable predictors. Therefore, predictive equations based on these chemical constituents provide a powerful tool for accurately estimating the amino acid digestibility of wheat with diverse nutrient profiles, thereby enabling more precise nutritional formulation for poultry fed wheat-containing diets.

Conclusions

In conclusion, significant differences were observed in the chemical composition, amino acid content, and SID of most amino acids among wheat samples from different sources, suggesting the representativeness of the samples evaluated. Optimal prediction equations were developed for the brooding and growing phases, respectively. During the brooding phase, ash, ADF, and EE were identified as key predictors for the SID of Ser (R² = 0.89, P = 0.001). During the growing phase, TP, NDF, and ADF were the primary predictors for the SID of Tyr (R² = 0.931, P < 0.001). These findings demonstrate that conventional nutrition components and amino acid contents can serve as reliable predictors for estimating the SID of most amino acids in wheats. The developed prediction equations provide a practical tool for evaluating the amino acid digestibility of wheats in pullets, thereby supporting more precise diet formulation.
