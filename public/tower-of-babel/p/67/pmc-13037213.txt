Critical illness–related corticosteroid insufficiency after acute brain hemorrhage surgery: a prospective cohort study with a randomized trial of hydrocortisone
Authors: Moinay Kim, Hyunchul Jung, Seung Bin Kim, Hanwool Jeon, Yeongu Chung, Youngbo Shim, Sae Min Kwon, Jae Hyun Kim, Jaewoo Chung, Kyu-Sun Choi, Heui Seung Lee, Joonho Byun, Si Un Lee, Wonhyoung Park, Jung Cheol Park, Jae Sung Ahn, Seungjoo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCritical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI) is a potentially underrecognized complication in postoperative patients with acute brain hemorrhage. Its incidence, associated risk factors, and the therapeutic role of corticosteroids in this population remain unclear.MethodThis multicenter study combined a prospective observational cohort with a single-blinded randomized controlled trial. Adult patients aged 18–80 years who underwent neurological surgery for acute brain hemorrhage within 48 h of admission at tertiary care hospitals were eligible for inclusion. Acute brain hemorrhage was confirmed by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging and included both traumatic (e.g., epidural or subdural hematoma) and non-traumatic etiologies (e.g., aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage). Among 497 screened patients, 255 eligible postoperative patients underwent adrenal function testing using a high-dose corticotropin stimulation test (HDST) on postoperative day (POD) 2 or 3. Patients diagnosed with CIRCI (n = 64, 25.1%) were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive intravenous hydrocortisone or placebo. The primary outcome was neurological improvement at 30 days assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).ResultsCIRCI was diagnosed in 25.1% of patients. Independent predictors of CIRCI included traumatic subdural hematoma, epidural hematoma, mechanical ventilation, and fresh frozen plasma transfusion. While hydrocortisone did not significantly improve the primary outcome of 30-day neurological function, it significantly reduced mechanical ventilation duration (median [IQR], 5 [3–7] vs. 10 [6–16] days; p = 0.032) and ICU length of stay (5 [2–13] vs. 13 [3–18] days; p = 0.038). No increase in serious adverse events was observed.ConclusionCIRCI is common in postoperative patients with acute brain hemorrhage and is associated with specific clinical risk factors. Although targeted hydrocortisone therapy did not significantly improve functional outcomes at 30 days, it yielded significant improvements in short-term ICU parameters. These findings warrant further adequately powered studies to evaluate the role of corticosteroid therapy in patients with acute brain hemorrhage diagnosed with CIRCI.Clinical trial registrationThis study was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS), a primary registry of the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, under the identifier KCT0004425. Background

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is essential in regulating the body’s physiological response to acute illness [1]. Critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI) is characterized by an inadequate corticosteroid response to severe physiological stress, which may result from either a prolonged and excessive pro-inflammatory response or a diminished sensitivity of target tissues to glucocorticoids [2–4]. CIRCI is a life-threatening condition associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Its clinical manifestations, such as hypotension, fever, and tachycardia, are often nonspecific and may closely resemble common postoperative symptoms following major surgical procedures [5, 6]. The incidence of CIRCI varies considerably among patients with conditions such as sepsis, shock, and acute respiratory distress syndrome [3, 7, 8]. Notably, an increased prevalence of CIRCI has been associated with higher mortality rates [9]. Recent clinical trials indicate that CIRCI is present in 40–70% of trauma patients, with an associated mortality rate of approximately 30% despite therapeutic intervention [10].

The prevalence of CIRCI in neurosurgical patients remains inadequately defined. The occurrence of hypopituitarism has been reported to range from 30% to 70% of individuals undergoing rehabilitation following traumatic brain injury (TBI) [11]. Another study reported that 38% of aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) patients exhibited some degree of hypopituitarism three months after injury, with isolated hormonal deficits identified in 28% of cases [12]. Also, the patients undergoing surgical procedures are subjected to significant physiological stress, which may further predispose them to the development of CIRCI during the immediate postoperative period. The overlapping clinical presentation poses a substantial diagnostic challenge for clinicians, and delayed recognition and appropriate management may result in severe, potentially life-threatening complications. Studies have shown that the short-term administration of corticosteroids at physiological doses improves morbidity and reduces mortality in patients with CIRCI in patients with sepsis [13, 14].

Compared to non-surgical cases, patients with acute brain hemorrhage undergoing surgery are more susceptible to developing CIRCI for several reasons. First, patients requiring surgical intervention typically present with more extensive or life-threatening hemorrhage compared to those managed conservatively. The presence of larger hemorrhage volumes or mass effects may result in increased physiological stress, thereby predisposing these patients to adrenal dysfunction, including CIRCI. Second, the surgical procedure itself induces a substantial stress response, activating the HPA axis [15, 16]. This physiological response may lead to either excessive cortisol secretion or, conversely, corticosteroid insufficiency due to adrenal exhaustion or impaired regulatory mechanisms. Third, surgical patients are frequently subject to hemodynamic fluctuations, blood transfusions, and perioperative inflammatory responses (cytokine surges), all of which can adversely affect adrenal function [17, 18].

Given these considerations and the potential clinical implications, we designed a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to investigate the incidence of CIRCI in patients with acute brain hemorrhage following surgery. Additionally, the study aimed to identify factors associated with the development of CIRCI and to evaluate the potential clinical benefits of randomized steroid administration in patients diagnosed with CIRCI. Our hypothesis is that diagnosis and correction of CIRCI may indirectly influence neurological outcomes by reducing systemic instability and mitigating secondary brain injury, as inadequate cortisol responses during critical illness may exacerbate cerebral hypoperfusion, impair autoregulatory capacity, and increase vulnerability to secondary ischemic insults.

Methods

This multicenter, single-blinded, parallel-group, investigator-initiated study consisted of a prospective observational cohort and a RCT and was conducted across eight neuro–intensive care units (ICUs) between March 2020 and May 2024. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of all participating institutions. Written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment in all cases, either directly from the patient when decision-making capacity was preserved or from a legally authorized representative when patients were unable to provide consent due to impaired consciousness or neurological deficits. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable national regulations.

Study participants and inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients aged 18 to 80 years with a confirmed diagnosis of acute brain hemorrhage on computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance (MR) imaging who underwent surgical intervention at a tertiary-level hospital in Korea were included in this study. The etiologies of brain hemorrhage encompassed both traumatic causes—such as epidural hematoma (EDH), subdural hematoma (SDH), and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH)—and non-traumatic causes including aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) and etc. Eligible surgical procedures and other related interventions, provided that the surgery was performed within 48 h of hospital admission. Exclusion criteria included a documented medical history of critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI); current or prior use of corticosteroids for any underlying condition within the preceding six months; the presence of multiple trauma, defined as two or more concurrent traumatic injuries; prior exposure to etomidate; clinical or laboratory evidence of acute infection; a confirmed diagnosis of any malignancy; and a history of ICH within the past two years.

Allocation, concealment, randomization and masking

Eligible and consenting participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either steroid or matching placebo, using a block size of 4. Randomization was performed after confirmation of CIRCI based on the results of the high-dose corticotropin stimulation test (HDST) conducted on postoperative day (POD) 2 or 3. Adrenal testing was intentionally deferred beyond the immediate postoperative period to minimize confounding from acute surgical stress, anesthesia, and perioperative hemodynamic instability, which may cause transient hypercortisolemia and reduce diagnostic accuracy [7, 11]. The administration of steroid or placebo was initiated on POD 2 or 3 following the results of a HDST. Participants randomized to the steroid group received intravenous (IV) hydrocortisone (Cortisol™, Hanall BioPharma, Seoul, Republic of Korea) at a dose of 100 mg twice daily (total 200 mg/day). The control group received an equivalent volume of IV normal saline as placebo. The assigned treatment (steroid or placebo) was administered for 7 days. The placebo was indistinguishable in appearance from the active treatment. Final outcome assessment was conducted at 30 day by a blinded assessor who was unaware of group allocation.

Details of the intervention

Diagnosis of CIRCI - High Dose corticotropin Stimulation Test (HDST) protocol

The diagnosis of CIRCI was established in accordance with the Endocrine Society’s clinical practice guidelines on adrenal insufficiency (AI) [19]. These guidelines emphasize that the HDST using a high dose (250 µg) of cosyntropin is superior to other existing diagnostic methods. A HDST was performed on POD 2 or 3 for all patients who underwent surgery for acute brain hemorrhage. In cases where surgery was performed at night or during weekends, samples were collected on POD 2 or 3 and sent for analysis on the next available working day to ensure proper sample handling and accurate cortisol assessment. A peak cortisol level below 18 µg/dL (assay-dependent) at 30- or 60-minutes post-administration was considered indicative of CIRCI. Hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin < 2.5 g/dL) can artificially lower total cortisol levels, potentially leading to a false diagnosis of CIRCI despite normal adrenal function. Therefore, if the serum albumin level was below 2.5 g/dL prior to the HDST, albumin solution was administered, and the level was required to be corrected to above 2.5 g/dL before performing the test to ensure accurate cortisol measurement and proper adrenal function assessment. Upon confirmation of CIRCI, patients were randomized. The hydrocortisone dosing regimen for patients diagnosed with CIRCI was based on the most recent guidelines from the SCCM and the ESICM [4]. Patients assigned to the steroid group received IV hydrocortisone at a dose of 200 mg per day for the first three days, followed by a tapered regimen continued until day seven: 150 mg/day on day 4, 100 mg/day on day 5, 70 mg/day on day 6, and 30 mg/day on day 7, reaching a physiologic replacement dose prior to discontinuation, provided that clinical stability was maintained. We performed second HDST on POD 20 to assess adrenal function recovery in all patients. If CIRCI was still present, hydrocortisone was administered for an additional 7 days, then gradually tapered and discontinued.

Currently, no guideline provides specific recommendations regarding corticosteroid dosing for postoperative patients diagnosed with CIRCI. Therefore, the hydrocortisone regimen used in this study was adopted from prior randomized trials conducted in patients with multiple trauma, a population that shares comparable physiological stress and inflammatory characteristics with postoperative acute brain hemorrhage patients. In particular, the HYPOLYTE trial administered intravenous hydrocortisone at a dose of 200 mg/day for 3 days followed by tapering until day 7, demonstrating improved hemodynamic stability without an excess of serious adverse events [20]. This regimen was considered an appropriate physiologic stress-dose replacement strategy for postoperative CIRCI in the present study.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of the study was the 30-day neurological improvement (difference between admission and 30-day scores), assessed using the modified Rankin scale (mRS) [21], in patients diagnosed with CIRCI following steroid administration after surgery for acute brain hemorrhage. The secondary outcomes included the incidence of CIRCI in postoperative patients with acute brain hemorrhage and ICU parameters reflecting the potential effects of hydrocortisone in CIRCI diagnosed patients. These parameters included the duration of inotropic or vasopressor use, the need for blood transfusion, the duration of mechanical ventilation, laboratory findings and the length of stay in both the ICU and hospital. Also, adverse events potentially related to steroid were prospectively assessed from drug administration through day 28. Prespecified safety outcomes included infections (bacteremia, pneumonia, urinary tract infection, and sepsis), gastrointestinal bleeding or perforation, clinically significant hyperglycemia, delirium or neuropsychiatric symptoms, and other serious adverse events, which were adjudicated by the site investigators based on predefined clinical criteria.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis plan was finalized prior to database locking and data analysis to ensure methodological rigor and transparency. Baseline characteristics were summarized using mean (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, median (IQR) for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and number (%) for categorical variables. Because no randomized controlled trials to date have addressed CIRCI diagnosis or targeted corticosteroid therapy in postoperative acute brain hemorrhage, an a priori sample size calculation based on condition-specific functional outcomes was not feasible. Accordingly, effect size assumptions were derived from randomized and prospective studies conducted in comparable critically ill and neurotrauma populations [20, 22, 23]. Given the ordinal nature of the outcome and the limited sample size, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used instead of an ordinal logistic regression model. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. The absolute difference in outcome proportions between groups was calculated as (steroid − placebo) and reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To evaluate clinical factors associated with the development of CIRCI, univariate logistic regression analyses were first performed. Variables deemed clinically relevant a priori and/or demonstrating an association with CIRCI in univariate analyses were considered for inclusion in a multivariable logistic regression model. To reduce the risk of model overfitting given the limited number of CIRCI events, the number of covariates was deliberately restricted, and a backward elimination approach was applied to derive a parsimonious final model. Variables with a P value < 0.15 were retained in the final multivariable model. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs are reported. Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided P-value of < 0.05. We employed a modified ITT (mITT) population for the primary analysis, comprising all randomized patients who received at least one dose of the study medication. To enhance analytical clarity, the ITT population was primarily used, especially since only three patients did not receive any study intervention after randomization. The mITT analysis was subsequently conducted as a sensitivity analysis to confirm the robustness of findings. For the primary analysis, we conducted a complete case analysis within the ITT population, encompassing all patients with available mRS data at 30 days. Further details are provided in the statistical analysis plan.

Results

Prospective observational cohort: incidence and risk factors for CIRCI

Among the 497 patients screened, 255 were enrolled in the study. All patients underwent surgical intervention for acute brain hemorrhage. Based on the initial HDST results, CIRCI was diagnosed in 64 of 255 patients (25.1%), while the remaining 191 patients demonstrated normal adrenal function. Randomization was subsequently performed in the 64 patients diagnosed with CIRCI. Due to the failure of 3 patients to withdraw from the study after receiving the investigational drug, a total of 61 patients were included in the primary analysis (steroid group: 31 patients; placebo group: 30 patients) (Fig. 1). We first compared patients with and without CIRCI in the entire postoperative cohort to identify clinical factors associated with the development of CIRCI. The results demonstrated no statistically significant intergroup differences in demographic parameters, pre-existing comorbidities, underlying etiology of brain hemorrhage, surgical interventions, operative duration, or other key perioperative variables, suggesting that baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups (Table 1). A significant difference was observed in the etiology of brain hemorrhage between the two groups, with CIRCI being more frequently diagnosed in patients with traumatic brain hemorrhage. Specifically, CIRCI was more prevalent in cases of SDH (n = 85 [44.5%] vs. n = 37 [60.66%], p = 0.028) and EDH (n = 9 [4.71%] vs. n = 8 [13.11%], p = 0.036). Patients diagnosed with CIRCI were more likely to require mechanical ventilation (n = 47 [24.61%] vs. n = 25 [40.98%], p = 0.014) and intra- or postoperative norepinephrine support (n = 50 [26.1%] vs. n = 29 [47.5%], p = 0.049). Regarding intraoperative and POD 0–1 transfusion, fresh frozen plasma (FFP) administration was significantly higher in the CIRCI group (n = 28 [14.66%] vs. n = 19 [31.15%], p = 0.047). There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between patients who received corticosteroid therapy and those who did not among those diagnosed with CIRCI (Table 2).

Factors associated with the development of postoperative CIRCI

We conducted an analysis to evaluate the odds ratio (OR) for the development of postoperative CIRCI in patients with acute brain hemorrhage. The analysis identified several factors significantly associated with an increased risk of CIRCI. These included traumatic SDH (OR: 1.92, 95% CI: 1.17 to 3.46, p = 0.024), EDH (OR: 3.05, 95% CI: 1.24 to 8.30, p = 0.012), the requirement for mechanical ventilation (OR: 2.13, 95% CI: 1.16 to 3.91, p = 0.021), and postoperative FFP transfusion (OR: 1.90, 95% CI: 1.04 to 3.85, p = 0.048) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Randomized controlled trial: clinical impact of hydrocortisone in CIRCI

The primary outcome of this study was the 30-day mRS score, which was assessed to evaluate the long-term functional outcome of steroid therapy in patients diagnosed with CIRCI (Fig. 3). At 30 days after surgery, the median mRS score was 3 (IQR, 0–4) in the steroid group and 3 (IQR, 1–4) in the placebo group (p = 0.428). There were no significant differences in the overall distribution of mRS scores across ordinal categories between the two groups. Neurological improvement, assessed as the change in mRS from admission to 30 days, was 1 (IQR, 0–2) in the steroid group and 0.5 (IQR, 0–2) in the placebo group, with no statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.342). While no statistically significant difference in 30-day mRS scores was observed between the steroid and placebo groups, several key secondary ICU outcomes demonstrated meaningful differences (Table 4). Specifically, the duration of mechanical ventilation (days, median [IQR]) was significantly shorter in the steroid group compared to the control group (5 [3–7] vs. 10 [6–16]; 95% CI: 1.5–8.5, p = 0.032). Likewise, the ICU length of stay (days, median [IQR]) was significantly reduced in the steroid group (5 [2–13] vs. 13 [3–18]; 95% CI: -9.2 to -1.8, p = 0.038). Delayed extubation in some patients likely reflected residual impairment in consciousness, cautious extubation strategies in older patients with comorbidities, and inter-center variability in weaning practices. However, no significant differences were found in other clinical parameters, including postoperative vasopressor/inotrope duration, transfusion requirements, or total hospital length of stay. The second HDST was performed on POD 20 for all patients diagnosed with CIRCI. In the steroid administration group, a persistently reduced adrenal response indicative of CIRCI was observed in 9 out of 31 patients (29.03%). Similarly, the placebo group exhibited a comparable persistence of CIRCI in 11 out of 30 patients (36.67%), with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.525). Despite receiving optimal medical management, two patients in the placebo group succumbed to refractory intracranial hypertension. After adjustment for prior history of stroke, hydrocortisone administration remained significantly associated with shorter durations of mechanical ventilation and ICU stay, whereas no significant differences were observed in 30-day mRS scores or mRS change from admission to day 30 (Supplementary Table S1).

High dose corticotropin stimulation test (HDST) results

We analyzed the HDST dynamics and cortisol responsiveness in patients diagnosed with CIRCI, stratified by steroid administration status. The initial endocrinologic assessment, conducted on POD 2 or 3, revealed no statistically significant differences between the study and control groups baseline adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) levels or serum cortisol concentrations at time 0. Following randomization and one-week regimen of hydrocortisone administration, the subsequent endocrinologic evaluation demonstrated a statistically significant elevation in serum ACTH levels in the study group compared to the control group (25.36 ± 8.83 vs. 20.93 ± 9.92 pg/mL, p = 0.045). However, despite the observed divergence in ACTH secretion, serum cortisol concentrations at 0, 30, and 60 min post-steroid administration remained comparable between the two groups, demonstrating no statistically significant intergroup differences (Table 5).

Serious Adverse Events (SAE) associated with steroid administration

We investigated SAEs associated with hydrocortisone administration. Although infections were observed in both the hydrocortisone-treated and non-treated CIRCI patient groups, no statistically significant differences were identified between the groups in terms of the incidence of bacteremia, urinary tract infection, pneumonia, or sepsis (Supplementary Table S2).

Discussion

In this prospective, multicenter study of patients who underwent surgery for acute brain hemorrhage, 25.1% (64/255) were diagnosed with CIRCI. Among these patients, 61 were randomized in an embedded, single-blinded pilot trial to receive hydrocortisone or placebo. To the best of our knowledge, the incidence of postoperative adrenal insufficiency—classified as CIRCI in critically ill patients with acute brain hemorrhage requiring intensive care—has not been previously investigated. In this exploratory randomized analysis, corticosteroid therapy administered exclusively to patients diagnosed with CIRCI was associated with shorter durations of mechanical ventilation and ICU length of stay. However, no significant improvement in the primary functional outcome was observed, and these secondary findings should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and the pilot nature of the randomized trial, which was not powered to detect definitive clinical effects.

Our study demonstrated that postoperative patients with acute brain hemorrhage are more likely to be diagnosed with CIRCI when the underlying cause of hemorrhage is trauma (e.g., EDH and SDH), mechanical ventilation is required, inotropic agents are administered postoperatively, or FFP transfusion is performed perioperatively. While the association between acute brain hemorrhage and postoperative CIRCI has not been extensively studied, our findings align with previous research investigating CIRCI in critically ill patients. A RCT targeting patients with respiratory failure demonstrated that hydrocortisone administration in AI-diagnosed patients significantly improved ventilator weaning rates and reduced the duration of mechanical ventilation [24]. Furthermore, our results are supported by the HYPOLYTE (Hydrocortisone Polytraumatized) study, a RCT involving 150 patients with multiple traumas, which demonstrated that hydrocortisone administration in CIRCI-diagnosed patients significantly reduced the duration of mechanical ventilation and the need for vasoactive medications [20]. The authors emphasized the importance of stress-dose hydrocortisone administration for seven days in ICU patients diagnosed with CIRCI, particularly those with TBI.

One of the most pivotal studies investigating the role of corticosteroids in acute brain injury is the corticosteroid randomization after significant head injury (CRASH) trial [25]. This large-scale, RCT enrolled 10,008 adults with acute brain injury and randomly assigned them to receive either a 48-hour infusion of methylprednisolone (MP) or a placebo. The study concluded that patients in the steroid infusion group had a significantly higher risk of mortality within two weeks compared to the placebo group. Despite its significant contributions, the CRASH trial has certain limitations. First, corticosteroid therapy was administered without prior assessment of adrenal function, potentially leading to excessive steroid exposure in patients with normal adrenal function. Second, the choice and dosage of corticosteroid used in the study may not have been optimal. Given that hydrocortisone more closely resembles physiological cortisol and possesses greater mineralocorticoid activity than methylprednisolone [26]—an important factor in AI patients—we recommend hydrocortisone over MP for patients diagnosed with CIRCI. The findings from our study further support this recommendation. In patients diagnosed with CIRCI, the requirement for vasopressor was significantly higher compared to those without CIRCI. This is consistent with the known hemodynamic effects of hydrocortisone, which include increasing blood volume, enhancing vascular tone, improving endothelial responsiveness to vasopressors [27], and reducing time to shock reversal in critically ill patients [23, 28]. Additionally, the third National Acute Spinal Cord Injury Study (NASCIS III), a well-known large-scale RCT, demonstrated that high-dose MP was associated with serious adverse effects, including pneumonia and other infections [29]. The study recommended that MP be administered only within three hours of injury. Previous studies have consistently reported that high-dose glucocorticoid therapy is associated with an increased risk of infections across multiple organ systems, with severity often correlating with the administered dose [30, 31].

Based on the findings of our study, we propose the following recommendations. First, the incidence of postoperative CIRCI in patients with acute brain hemorrhage is not negligible, with a rate of 25.1%, particularly within the early postoperative period (POD 2–3). Patients with TBI, or those requiring mechanical ventilation, vasopressor support, or transfusions, appear to be appropriate candidates for HDST based on our findings. Second, while hydrocortisone administration was associated with shorter mechanical ventilation duration and ICU length of stay, and multivariable analyses were conducted to adjust for relevant confounders, these secondary outcomes were not adjusted for multiple comparisons and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. These findings highlight the potential role of hydrocortisone in optimizing ICU-related outcomes. Supporting this observation, a previous prospective study involving TBI patients reported an AI incidence of 53%, with a higher frequency of hemodynamic instability in those affected [32]. This carries important clinical implications, as current management strategies for acute brain injury heavily emphasize the prevention of secondary brain insults. In this context, physiologic-dose hydrocortisone may contribute to hemodynamic stabilization in patients diagnosed with CIRCI; however, any potential effects on secondary brain injury remain speculative and should be considered hypothesis-generating, given the exploratory nature of the randomized component. Furthermore, in our study, short-term hydrocortisone therapy was not associated with any SAEs, suggesting its safety in this patient population. Third, although baseline cortisol and ACTH levels were comparable between groups, patients who received hydrocortisone exhibited significantly higher ACTH levels on follow-up assessment. This suggests a possible reactivation of the HPA axis through negative feedback or central modulation. The absence of significant changes in stimulated cortisol levels may indicate that ACTH elevation precedes complete adrenal recovery, or that hydrocortisone exerts effects at the hypothalamic or pituitary level. These findings are consistent with the concept that CIRCI may represent a dynamic and potentially reversible dysfunction of the HPA axis, although this interpretation remains exploratory given the limited sample size. Therefore, early diagnosis and timely intervention may be essential in improving outcomes.

Limitation

We acknowledge that referral bias may exist as our study was exclusively conducted at large university-affiliated tertiary care institutions. The heterogeneity of the disease population, encompassing various etiologies of brain hemorrhage and the concurrent administration of multiple medications in the ICU environments limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader population. Additional neurological and sedation-related factors—including brainstem reflexes, sedative dosing, and intracranial hypertension—were not systematically collected across all centers and may represent residual confounders in the interpretation of 30-day functional outcomes. Backward elimination was used to derive a parsimonious multivariable model and to reduce the risk of overfitting, this approach does not fully eliminate the possibility of type I error, particularly in the context of multiple secondary outcome analyses. Although hypoalbuminemia was corrected prior to testing, the absence of free cortisol measurements, which are considered a more physiologically relevant biomarker [33], may limit the diagnostic accuracy of adrenal function assessment in this study. Additionally, although the mRS was the prespecified primary endpoint, we were unable to perform an ordinal logistic regression analysis due to the limited sample size of the randomized cohort and the lack of complete individual-level outcome data across all centers. As a result, the findings related to functional outcome should be interpreted with caution, acknowledging the inability to adjust for potential confounders. In addition, in patients with acute brain injury, the duration of mechanical ventilation is influenced not only by pulmonary function but also by neurological factors, including level of consciousness and airway protective reflexes. In the present study, the randomized CIRCI cohort predominantly consisted of patients with mild to moderate neurological impairment and this characteristic may partially explain the concordance of our ventilation-related findings with prior studies conducted in patients with primary respiratory failure. Although general principles of ventilator weaning in neurocritical care were shared across participating centers, extubation decisions require integration of multiple neurological and systemic considerations, which made implementation of a uniform extubation protocol across centers challenging. Despite its widespread use in diagnosing CIRCI in critically ill patients, the HDST may not identify certain forms of secondary AI, which are commonly seen in patients with TBI [34], particularly in cases of hypothalamic or pituitary dysfunction where the cortisol reserve is diminished but still yields a preserved response to ACTH. However, in this study, hydrocortisone therapy was restricted to patients with an inadequate cortisol response to HDST, in accordance with current SCCM/ESICM guidelines [4].

Conclusion

In this multicenter RCT, CIRCI was diagnosed in 25.1% of postoperative acute brain hemorrhage patients, particularly in those with trauma, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor use, or FFP transfusion. In an embedded exploratory randomized pilot trial, hydrocortisone therapy was associated with shorter durations of mechanical ventilation and ICU length of stay; however, the study was not powered to detect a definitive improvement in the primary outcome of 30-day mRS. Hydrocortisone was safe and may support early ICU recovery. Larger, adequately powered studies are needed to evaluate its effect on long-term functional outcomes.
