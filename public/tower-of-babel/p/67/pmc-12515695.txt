Sepsis‐Induced Anti‐Amphiphysin Antibody‐Associated Paraneoplastic Syndrome: A Case Report
Authors: Dexin Min, Meng Yuan, Chenda Feng, Wenjing Tang, Lichao Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSepsis is a severe systemic response to infection that can induce a variety of complications, including paraneoplastic syndrome (PNS). Anti‐amphiphysin antibody‐associated PNS is a rare autoimmune disorder associated with tumors. We review the case of a 27‐year‐old man who presented with a 3‐day history of fever, disturbance of consciousness, and oliguria. After 3 weeks of intensive care unit admissions, the muscle strength of his limbs gradually decreased to grade 0. The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) specimen revealed elevated protein, and electromyography showed peripheral nerve damage. The blood test for anti‐amphiphysin antibody titer resulted positive (1:30, RI < 1:30). Sepsis, diabetic ketoacidosis, and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) were followed by anti‐amphiphysin antibody‐associated PNS. He was treated with methylprednisolone and immunoglobulin. Rehabilitation therapy was initiated after discharge. One year of follow‐up indicated muscle strength at grade 4 with normal muscle tone, and chest CT did not reveal any pulmonary tumors. Anti‐amphiphysin antibodies are usually associated with neurologic diseases and are rarely related to sepsis. We aim to raise awareness of sepsis‐induced anti‐amphiphysin antibody‐associated paraneoplastic syndrome, which has a poor prognosis and a high case fatality rate. Early recognition and treatment are essential to improve the prognosis.Keywords: anti‐amphiphysin antibody, case report, paraneoplastic syndrome, rhabdomyolysis syndrome, sepsis

Introduction

Sepsis is a severe clinical syndrome caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, characterized by an imbalance between pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory mechanisms, and this immunological imbalance can lead to life‐threatening organ dysfunction [1, 2]. Sepsis causes a severe suppression of the immune system, with abnormal function of immune cells and a dysregulation of immune responses, leading to attacks on its own tissues and organs [3]. Paraneoplastic syndrome (PNS) is closely associated with the activation of the immune system by tumor‐associated antigens (TAAs) produced by tumor cells, leading to autoimmune reactions. These reactions can result in the development of neurological disorders [4, 5]. Anti‐amphiphysin antibodies are a type of autoantibodies associated with neurological disorders. Their target antigen, amphiphysin protein, is located on the vesicles of the presynaptic membrane of neurons and is involved in the exocytosis and reuptake of neurotransmitters. These antibodies have been closely associated with PNSs [6]. In this report, we present a case of PNS in a man that was proven at CSF analysis and electromyography examination, combined with positive results of screening for anti‐amphiphysin antibodies. He presented with clinical manifestations of anti‐amphiphysin antibody PNS on the basis of sepsis, with peripheral paralysis of extremities. This case not only supports the role of anti‐amphiphysin antibodies as a biomarker for paraneoplastic neurological diseases but also expands the understanding of the association between neurologic phenotypes and adverse events related to sepsis. Through this case, we aim to explore the potential pathogenic mechanisms, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic strategies of such syndromes, thereby providing a reference for future clinical practice and research.

Case Description

Case History

A 27‐year‐old man came to our attention in August 2023 because of a 3‐day history of fever, disturbance of consciousness, and oliguria. He had experienced a rapid weight loss of approximately 15 kg in the past half month, and there was no family history of neurological or autoimmune disorders.

Physical Examination and Investigations

A clinical examination revealed a moderate coma with acutely facial features. Pupils were 3 mm (RI 2‐5 mm) and the pupillary light reflex was delayed. A routine blood test showed high hematocrit (14.74 × 109/L, RI 3.5–9.5 × 109/L) and thrombocytopenia (14 × 109/L, RI 100–300 × 109/L). An arterial blood gas test revealed a decreased pH (7.22, RI 7.35–7.45). His blood sugar level (23.4 mmol/L, RI 3.9–6.1 mmol/L) and creatinine level (370.7 μmol/L, RI 53–106 μmol/L) were elevated, and uric ketone bodies resulted positive (+++). A lung CT scan revealed bilateral pneumonia and an abdominal CT showed fatty liver. The head CT showed no abnormalities. He was diagnosed as septic shock, MODS, diabetic ketoacidosis, bilateral pneumonia, and fatty liver.

Four days after admission, he regained consciousness. An arterial blood gas test revealed an elevated pH (7.53, RI 7.35–7.45). His blood sugar level (14 mmol/L, RI 3.9–6.1 mmol/L) and creatinine level (205.5 μmol/L, RI 53–106 μmol/L) were still elevated, but all of which had improved compared to before. His disturbance of consciousness was considered to be caused by diabetic ketoacidosis.

Two weeks after admission, investigations showed a myoglobin of 15,490.0 ng/mL, CKMB 6.95 ng/mL, creatine kinase (CK) 522 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 337 U/L.

Three weeks after admission, he exhibited progressive weakness in the limbs, reaching a strength level of 0. Deep tendon reflexes in the bilateral upper limbs were diminished, while those in the bilateral lower limbs were absent. No pathological reflexes were elicited, suggesting the possibility of peripheral nerve injury. The MRI of the head and cervical spine revealed no notable abnormalities. The electromyography (EMG) results indicated that the compound muscle action potential (CMAP) of the ulnar nerve was not elicited (Figure 1), while motor conduction of the median nerve was impaired, characterized by a normal distal latency, prolonged proximal latency, reduced amplitude, and slowed conduction velocity, suggesting possible demyelination or axonal injury. The latency of the APB was measured at 3.90 ms with an amplitude of 0.47 mV, and a velocity of 2.6 m/s, indicating that nerve conduction is normal in this segment; however, the low amplitude may suggest axonal injury or neurogenic reinnervation. The latency from the elbow to the APB was recorded at 8.08 ms, with an amplitude of 0.48 mV and a velocity of 2.8 m/s. The prolonged latency and reduced amplitude may indicate demyelination or axonal injury in the proximal nerve segment. The velocity from the elbow to the wrist was found to be 51.4 m/s, which falls within the normal range, suggesting that nerve conduction function in this segment is intact; however, the amplitude decreased to 2.1 mV, which may indicate damage to the nerve fibers (Figure 2, Table 1). Additionally, sensory nerve action potentials (SNAP) for both the ulnar and median nerves were not elicited, indicating severe impairment of sensory conduction (Figures 3 and 4, Table 2). These findings further corroborate the diagnosis of peripheral nerve injury. Treatments were initiated with intramuscular injections of mecobalamin and vitamin B1, along with neuromuscular electrical stimulation.

CSF analysis for leukocyte count (3 × 106/L, RI 0–8 × 106/L) and mNGS were both normal except for an elevation in protein (0.79 g/L, RI 0.15–0.45 g/L) and IgG (87.77 mg/L, RI 0–34.0 mg/L). Blood tests revealed elevated anti‐amphiphysin antibodies titer (1:30, RI < 1:30), an increase in CA‐724 level (28.38 U/mL, RI < 10.00 U/mL) and cytokeratin 19 fragments (7.29 ng/mL, RI < 5.00 ng/mL). For CSF analysis, we employed the most common and widely accepted cell‐based assay (CBA) method, using a Leica/DMI LED inverted fluorescence microscope manufactured in Germany. Antibody measurements were performed in duplicate and validated twice. The assay kit was developed in‐house by Jilin KingMed Medical Laboratory Co. Ltd. The detection of anti‐amphiphysin antibodies relies on introducing the amphiphysin antigen gene—the human amphiphysin protein gene—into mammalian cells (HEK‐293), driving high‐level expression of the antigen. Antibodies present in the patient's CSF (primary, anti‐amphiphysin antibodies) bind specifically to this antigen, and a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody directed against human IgG is then used to visualize the complex. PET‐CT revealed multiple small nodules in both lungs, some with slightly elevated metabolic activity. A diagnosis of anti‐amphiphysin antibody PNS was established.

Differential Diagnosis

Paraneoplastic syndrome usually presents subacutely, predominantly with sensory deficits; anti‐Hu/CRMP5 antibodies are often positive, a tumor can be found, and weight loss is common. Guillain–Barré syndrome manifests as acute, symmetric flaccid paralysis peaking within 2–4 weeks, albuminocytologic dissociation in CSF, and possible respiratory failure. Acute transverse myelitis evolves over hours to days with paraplegia, a sensory level, and sphincter dysfunction; spinal MRI shows long‐segment T2 hyperintensity and CSF pleocytosis.

Treatment and Interventions

An early treatment with fluid resuscitation, anti‐infection, and hemofiltration was started. Of note, he experienced oliguria lasting for 2 weeks, with an average daily urine output of 30 mL, characterized by dark brown urine. No chest pain was reported, and the diagnosis of rhabdomyolysis was established. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was administered. Following 10 days of treatment, both urine output and color normalized. Subsequently, electromyography showed peripheral nerve damage; treatments were initiated with intramuscular injections of mecobalamin and vitamin B1, along with neuromuscular electrical stimulation. Finally diagnosed as PNS, he was treated with methylprednisolone and immunoglobulin. Rehabilitation therapy was initiated after discharge.

Outcome

No significant improvement in limb muscle strength was observed. Rehabilitation therapy was initiated after discharge. One‐year follow‐up indicated muscle strength at grade 4 with normal muscle tone, and chest CT did not reveal any pulmonary tumors.

Discussion

Sepsis complicated with anti‐amphiphysin antibody‐associated PNS is rare. During sepsis, the immune system is severely suppressed, with abnormal immune cell function and immune regulation, which may lead to attacks on one's own tissues and organs [7]. This immune imbalance may result in the production of anti‐amphiphysin antibodies, leading to PNS. Sepsis is one of the common causes of rhabdomyolysis syndrome, and other factors such as hypotension, electrolyte abnormalities, hypophosphatemia, tissue hypoperfusion, hyperthermia, hypoxia, dehydration, and acidosis can also induce rhabdomyolysis in critical conditions [8]. Paraneoplastic syndrome (PNS) is a remote effect of cancer, which is related to immune mediation [4, 5]. Studies have shown that in patients with confirmed PNS, the primary tumors are most commonly lung cancer (especially small‐cell lung cancer), followed by breast cancer [9, 10]. Epidemiological data on PNS have been reported from Europe; for example, a population‐based study in northeastern Italy documented an incidence of approximately 1/100,000 person‐years and a prevalence of 4/100,000 [11]. In China, a retrospective cohort study of Han individuals in Shandong Province found an incidence of 1.9/100,000 person‐years; within this cohort, 40.7% of patients carried classic antibodies such as anti‐Hu or anti‐Yo, and 75% were ultimately found to have an underlying malignancy—most commonly small‐cell lung cancer [12]. The diagnosis of PNS is challenging and requires exclusion of direct involvement of the nervous system by cancer, such as brain metastases or carcinomatous meningitis, as well as indirect involvement due to coagulation disorders, treatment‐related neurotoxicity, and metabolic or infectious causes [13]. Amphiphysin is a 128‐kDa synaptic vesicle protein involved in the recycling of vesicle membranes from the axonal terminal plasma membrane after neurotransmitter exocytosis. Anti‐amphiphysin antibodies can recognize amphiphysin isoforms in brain and muscle tissues and are associated with various PNSs, including stiff‐person syndrome (SMS) and paraneoplastic necrotizing myopathy (PNM) [14]. The association of anti‐amphiphysin antibodies with paraneoplastic neurological diseases, especially in SMS related to breast cancer, has been widely recognized [15, 16]. The clinical features of PNM include rapidly progressive, symmetrical, and predominantly proximal muscle weakness, which is similar to the clinical manifestations of rhabdomyolysis syndrome in this case, suggesting that anti‐amphiphysin antibodies may be related to the occurrence of myopathy [17, 18].

Currently, the discovery of new phenotypes and antibodies has altered the diagnostic criteria for PNS, including the concepts of high‐risk phenotypes and intermediate‐risk phenotypes, as well as the categorization of high‐risk and intermediate‐risk antibodies [19, 20]. Among these, subacute sensory neuronopathy (SSN) is the most frequently observed neurological disorder associated with cancer. The most common specific antibody associated with SSN is Hu, followed by CV2/CRMP5 and amphiphysin antibodies [21, 22, 23]. The presence of anti‐amphiphysin antibodies suggests a potential PNS, necessitating further tumor screening [24]. We employed the most common and widely accepted method, the indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA). In our patient, the detection of anti‐amphiphysin antibodies is positive, which is consistent with the high‐risk nature in PNS patients as described in the literature [25]. The uniqueness of this case lies in the fact that the patient's anti‐amphiphysin antibody‐associated PNS and rhabdomyolysis syndrome are induced by sepsis, which is rare and associated with a poor prognosis. Although PNS was diagnosed through various examination methods, the presence of a tumor could not be directly confirmed through biopsy or other means. Currently, follow‐up tumor markers and lung CT scans have not revealed any tumors. Given that anti‐amphiphysin antibody‐associated PNSs are also related to breast cancer, and despite the patient being male with an extremely low probability of developing breast cancer, this possibility should still be monitored. The assessment of the patient's response to immunotherapy may be limited by individual differences and the heterogeneity of treatment, and further research is needed to determine the role of immunotherapy in such syndromes.

Conclusion

As mentioned above, this report emphasizes the rarity and severity of anti‐amphiphysin antibody‐associated PNS induced by sepsis. Through detailed case descriptions and literature reviews, we expand our understanding of the association between neurological phenotypes and adverse events related to sepsis, and provide valuable references for future clinical practice and research. Early identification and treatment are crucial for improving patient outcomes. This case increases awareness of such a rare syndrome and provides new directions for future research and treatment.
