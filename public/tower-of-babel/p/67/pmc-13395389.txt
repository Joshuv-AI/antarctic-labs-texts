Elevated Serum Nardilysin Is Inversely Associated with Cardiovascular Disease in Kidney Transplant Recipients
Authors: Anna Sączek, Krzysztof Batko, Małgorzata Banaszkiewicz, Jolanta Małyszko, Ewa Koc-Żórawska, Marcin Żórawski, Karolina Niezabitowska, Katarzyna Sobczyńska, Przemysław Miarka, Alina Bętkowska-Prokop, Katarzyna Krzanowska, Marcin Krzanowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney transplant recipients (KTRs) suffer from enhanced cardiovascular (CV) and metabolic burden due to interplay between pro-inflammatory and metabolic risk factors. Nardilysin (NRDc) and mitochondrial open reading frame of 12S rRNA-c (MOTS-c) are circulating peptides that represent candidate markers of cardiometabolic pathobiology, though their relevance in KTRs is unknown.MethodsWe measured serum NRDc and MOTS-c concentrations in an observational cohort of 150 patients (118 KTRs and aged-matched controls – 32 patients) using commercial ELISA kits. KTRs were followed for a median of 29.2 (25.3–31.9) months. Relationships with CV and metabolic parameters were assessed using Pearson’s correlation and penalized logistic regression. Renal allograft loss with all-cause mortality as competing event was examined using Fine-Gray regression.ResultsMedian serum NRDc (1.91 vs. 0.42 ng/mL, p < 0.001) and MOTS-c (178 vs. 142 ng/mL, p = 0.014) were significantly higher in KTRs, as compared with controls. NRDc showed a weak positive linear relationship with BMI (r = 0.21, p = 0.02). After adjusting for age and sex, higher standardized ln-NRDc was independently associated with lower odds of prevalent CV disease (OR 0.59, 95% CI: 0.34–0.97, p = 0.04). This CV disease association was stable after further adjustment for hsIL-6. We did not observe significant associations for MOTS-c and cardiometabolic features. In competing risk analysis, MOTS-c showed a consistent but nonsignificant trend toward lower risk of allograft loss (adj. sHR 0.50, 95% CI: 0.22–1.10, p = 0.08), while NRDc was not associated with allograft loss. Both serum NRDc and MOTS-c markers were not associated with all-cause mortality or follow-up eGFR decline.ConclusionsElevated circulating serum NRDc and MOTS-c levels in KTRs may reflect altered metabolic and inflammatory pathways. The inverse association between NRDc and prevalent CV disease warrants further investigation in longitudinal studies with incident CV events.

Introduction

Metabolic dysregulation and systemic inflammation are central processes that shape chronic kidney disease (CKD), while also negatively affecting patient outcomes after kidney transplantation (KTx). While successful KTx leads to clearance of uremic toxins and preserves glomerular filtration, patients who receive KTx (i.e., kidney transplant recipients [KTRs]) are a special patient population that requires maintenance immunosuppression and still suffers from a pro-inflammatory milieu, both of which contribute to lasting metabolic abnormalities and enhanced cardiovascular (CV) disorder development [1–5]. Pooled data from observational studies indicate metabolic syndrome (MetS) components are clinically relevant risk factors that negatively affect graft survival and enhance mortality due to CV disease in the post-KTx setting [6–9]. This is also supported by more recent evidence indicating a causal genetic link underlies MetS components and renal failure development pathways [10–12]. Taken together, the identification of circulating markers or noninvasive tools that closely parallel these pathobiological processes offers the promise for more individualized risk evaluation and stratification of KTRs to more intensive monitoring and/or therapeutic steps [13–17].

Mitochondrial open reading frame of 12S rRNA-c (MOTS-c) is a 16-amino-acid signaling peptide that is thought to regulate age- and fatty diet-dependent metabolic processes that shape insulin resistance and predispose to obesity [18]. Under metabolic stress via AMPK-dependent mechanisms, MOTS-c has been shown to translocate to the nucleus and interact with gene expression factors that shape adaptive cellular responses [19]. MOTS-c is reported as a potential protective molecule that moderates glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and lipid homeostasis, which can also be tied to reduced NF-kB activity, lower oxidative stress, and attenuated inflammatory activity [20]. Both experimental and clinical studies support the potential relevance of MOTS-c as a cytoprotective marker in kidney disorders. In patients with CKD, circulating MOTS-c concentrations were reduced in both skeletal muscle and serum [21]. In the setting of peritoneal dialysis, patients with high sarcopenia risk were characterized with lower MOTS-c levels [22]. Conversely, hemodialysis patients were shown to have higher serum MOTS-c concentrations, which were tied to higher rates of a composite of nonfatal CV events and mortality [23]. From a CV perspective, lower MOTS-c is tied to vascular endothelial injury [24, 25]. Changes in MOTS-c have also been observed in other disorders characterized by inflammation and oxidative stress, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [26], chronic fatigue syndrome [27], and type 2 diabetes [28].

N-arginine dibasic convertase (NRDC; NRDc) is an M16 family member peptidase that is involved in antigenic processing and epitope generation for cytotoxic T lymphocytes [29]. NRDC is present mainly in cytoplasm, and it is considered to be a moderator of inflammation (e.g., via TNF-α release) and altered cellular metabolism [30–33]. Recent data suggest regulation of insulin sensitivity by NRDC via interactions with transcription factors [33]. Higher serum NRDc levels have been tied to poor prognosis after myocardial infarction [34], while also reported as candidate marker for differentiation of transient ischemic attack (lower NRDc levels as compared with controls) in the emergency department scenario [35]. NRDc expression is also tied to worse outcomes in cancer populations [36, 37].

To our knowledge, serum MOTS-c and NRDc concentrations have not been previously evaluated in KTRs. In this study, we aimed to compare serum levels of both biomarkers between KTRs and healthy controls, assess their associations with cardiometabolic parameters, and explore their potential prognostic value for adverse outcomes including allograft loss, mortality, and graft function decline.

Methods

Patients

This was an observational cohort study enrolling 118 adult KTRs under care of the University Hospital in Kraków, Poland. Patients presenting for ambulatory care visits between September 1, 2016, and October 30, 2017, with at least 12 months of available post-KTx ambulatory monitoring history were considered eligible. We excluded patients in whom a history of active (upon presentation) or chronic infection (e.g., hepatitis, HIV), previous malignancy, post-parathyroidectomy status, or biopsy-proven acute rejection episode was recorded. Both paper and electronic medical records of KTRs were analyzed to extract information on clinical characteristics and patient status over follow-up. Additionally, both in-person and telephone contacts with the patient, family, and/or dialysis center were conducted to assess survivorship status.

Definitions

Clinical data of cardiometabolic relevance were extracted from medical records, including medication lists. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated based on the 2009 CKD-Epi equation [38]. Hypertension and dyslipidemia were identified based on documented diagnoses, physician assessments, or use of relevant pharmacotherapy. Due to statistical considerations [39], a composite for CV disease was defined as the presence of coronary artery disease (including myocardial infarction), venous thromboembolism, stroke, and heart failure. Anthropometric measurements (height, weight, BMI, waist-to-hip ratio), office-based mean arterial pressure, and smoking status were also recorded. Posttransplant diabetes mellitus was categorized in existing recommendations of the European Renal Association group [40], while MetS was defined according to modified ATP III criteria [41].

Biochemical Assessments

Blood samples were collected via peripheral venous puncture after an overnight fast. Samples were allowed to clot at room temperature, centrifuged under standard conditions to obtain serum, aliquoted, and stored at −70°C until analysis. Standard biochemical parameters were measured using three automatic analyzers at the University Hospital: Hitachi 917 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), Modular P (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), and Sysmex XE-2100 (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan).

We used commercially available ELISA kits to determine the serum concentrations of MOTS-c (CEX132Hu kit; Cloud-Clone Corp., Katy, Tx, USA) and NRDC (EH10653; FineTest, Wuhan, China). The MOTS-c assay had a detection range of 2.47–200 ng/mL and sensitivity of less than 0.99 ng/mL; the NRDc assay had a detection range of 0.313–20 ng/mL and sensitivity of 0.188 ng/mL. ELISA testing was performed in single batch by an experienced laboratory technician and according to the package manufacturer instructions. Intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were <10% and <12% for MOTS-c, and <8% and <10% for NRDc, respectively.

We also enrolled a convenience control group (n = 32; 16 [50%] females) of otherwise healthy volunteers with an age range of 29–74 years and mean age of 50 years. KTRs (n = 118; 39 [33%] females) showed comparable mean age of 50.0 years (standard deviation [SD] 13.7; p = 0.97), while the proportion of female patients was lower (p = 0.005).

Statistical Analysis

Analysis was performed using R 4.5.2 (R Core Team, 2026; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the use of publicly available packages. Continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentage (N, %). Cross-group comparison was performed using Kruskal-Wallis and Fisher’s exact tests following a robust approach. For comparison of serum marker concentrations between KTRs and controls, we utilized a heteroscedastic median-based one-way test from WRS2 due to interpretational considerations. NRDc, MOTS-c, and highly sensitive interleukin-6 (hsIL-6) analyses were performed after log-transformation due to right-skewed distributions. To improve interpretability, effect estimates were rescaled to report change per one SD of log-transformed serum marker concentrations. Moreover, due to low event rates, we used penalized Firth logistic regression as the primary analysis for clinical features – specifically, prevalence of CVD and renal function change (relative decline of ≥25% from baseline eGFR values). Poor prognosis was explored using competing risk models and Cox proportional hazards regression. Assumptions were tested via visual inspection and formalized tests of Schoenfeld residuals. Model discrimination performance was quantified using c-statistic and standard error (SE). We analyzed renal allograft loss with all-cause death as competing event using Fine-Gray approach to estimate the subdistribution hazard ratio (sHR). Additionally, nonparametric Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank tests were considered. In exploratory analyses, we classified patients into four clinical profiles based on median splits of serum marker concentrations (log-NRDc and log-hsIL-6), and overall survival rates were compared across groups. For sensitivity, we performed supplemental tertile-based analyses and assessed formal trend tests for all relevant endpoints. In general, multivariable models were limited to demographics (age and sex) due to the overarching importance of these biological factors and low event considerations. p values are based on two-sided tests, while p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive and Clinical Characteristics of KTRs

We enrolled 118 KTRs aged 21–75 years with ambulatory care follow-up of at least 12 months. Overall, the median (IQR) age of KTRs was 53 (41–60) years, with 79 patients (67%) being male. Median eGFR at the baseline visit was 56.9 mL/min/1.73 m2, and median BMI was 25.6 kg/m2. Prevalent hypertension was recorded in the majority of patients, while CVD was documented in 26 (22%), MetS in 35 (31%), posttransplant diabetes in 25 (21%). Median (IQR) follow-up was 29.2 (25.3–31.9) months during which 33 composite events occurred, including 21 all-cause deaths and 12 renal allograft losses. A decline in eGFR of at least 25% was observed in 22 of 104 patients (21%) with available sequential eGFR data from at least two ambulatory care visits; follow-up eGFR data were missing for 14 patients (12%). Comparison of NRDc and MOTS-c in KTx and Controls

Both serum markers were significantly elevated in KTRs compared with healthy controls (Fig. 1). The median NRDc concentration in the KTR cohort was 1.91 (IQR 1.20–2.81) ng/mL (p < 0.001; Fig. 1a), while the median MOTS-c was 178.40 (IQR 132.88–225.55) ng/mL (p = 0.014; Fig. 1b), based on a robust median-based one-way test.

Relationship of NRDc and MOTS-c with Prevalent CV Disease

For analysis, NRDc and MOTS-c concentrations were log-transformed and rescaled to enable interpretation of effects per 1-SD increase. In penalized logistic regression, higher log-NRDc was inversely associated with prevalent CVD in univariable analysis (odds ratio [OR] 0.62, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.38–0.96, p = 0.03). This inverse association persisted after adjustment for age and sex (OR 0.59, 95% CI: 0.34–0.97, p = 0.04; see model 2 – Table 2). In this model, both older age (OR 1.11 per year, 95% CI: 1.06–1.19, p < 0.001) and male sex (OR 3.27, 95% CI: 1.10–11.34, p = 0.03) were independently associated with the presence of CVD.

Relationship of NRDc and MOTS-c with All-Cause Mortality

Neither serum marker was associated with all-cause mortality. In age- and sex-adjusted Cox regression, the hazard ratios per 1-SD increase in log-biomarker were 1.16 (95% CI: 0.72–1.87, p = 0.53) for NRDc and 0.90 (95% CI: 0.56–1.45, p = 0.67) for MOTS-c. Concordance for the adjusted models was 0.728 (SE 0.062) for NRDc and 0.717 (SE 0.060) for MOTS-c, indicating that the moderate discriminative ability is likely attributable to demographic factors (i.e., due to univariable C-statistics of 0.519 and 0.569, respectively). Supplemental tertile-based analyses yielded concordant null findings for both NRDc (p for trend = 0.94) and MOTS-c (p for trend = 0.77).

Relationship of the NRDc/IL-6 Inflammatory Profile with Overall Survival

We explored whether combined serum NRDc and IL-6 concentrations could be used to classify patients into clinically meaningful subgroups defined by their respective median values, resulting in four groups of 27–32 patients each. In Kaplan-Meier analysis, we observed significant differences in overall survival across these groups (p = 0.019; see Fig. 3). Specifically, patients in the low NRDc/high IL-6 group were recorded the poorest survival rates, which is consistent with an NRDc-deficient and pro-inflammatory profile. However, in Cox regression adjusted for age and sex, the hazard ratio for this potential high-risk phenotype compared with all other patients was estimated at HR 1.73 (95% CI: 0.69–4.33, p = 0.24). This suggests that unadjusted survival differences may partly reflect the age and sex distribution across subgroups. In additional sensitivity analyses, we observed that hsIL-6 tertiles were characterized by a significant association with all-cause mortality in penalized logistic regression (OR 2.06, 95% CI: 1.14–3.96, p = 0.02), which remained consistent after age and sex adjustment (OR 2.04, 95% CI: 1.06–4.23, p = 0.03). In additional Cox regression analyses across hsIL-6 tertiles, there was consistent direction but formal tests did not show statistical significance (HR 1.65, p = 0.11).

Relationship of NRDc and MOTS-c with Renal Allograft Loss

We examined twelve cases of allograft loss during the follow-up period, with all-cause mortality treated as a competing risk event (see Table 3). Serum MOTS-c concentrations showed a consistent effect direction for predicting risk of graft loss across models. In univariable Fine-Gray regression models, the sHR per 1-SD increment in log-MOTS-c was estimated at 0.55 (95% CI: 0.25–1.19, p = 0.13), with consistent estimates after adjustment for age and sex (sHR 0.50, 95% CI: 0.22–1.10, p = 0.08). Across different exploratory models, we observed that the point estimate was relatively stable, for example, when adjusted for baseline eGFR (sHR 0.55, 95% CI: 0.27–1.10, p = 0.09; model C) and with addition of NRDc (sHR 0.48, 95% CI: 0.22–1.07, p = 0.07; model D). Conversely, NRDc was not associated with risk of graft loss (adj. sHR 0.76, 95% CI: 0.47–1.23, p = 0.26). In Kaplan-Meier analysis of death-censored graft survival, when comparing across MOTS-c tertiles, we did not observe a statistically significant relationship (log-rank test p = 0.55; Fig. 4), which falls in line with Gray’s test (p = 0.56). Moreover, neither serum NRDc (adj. OR 1.22, p = 0.45) nor MOTS-c (adj. OR 0.88, p = 0.60) concentrations were associated with an eGFR decline of ≥25% relative to baseline.

Bivariate Relationships between NRDc and MOTS-c with Clinical Characteristics

We observed a negative correlation between log-NRDc and serum phosphate (r = −0.32, p = 0.03; however, data available only for N = 47), while a positive linear relationship with BMI (r = 0.21, p = 0.02), fasting glucose (r = 0.18, p = 0.07) and waist-hip ratio (r = 0.16, p = 0.09) was recorded. NRDc was not significantly correlated with age, eGFR, or other clinical parameters. For serum MOTS-c concentrations, only a weak inverse association was observed with serum creatinine (r = −0.18, p = 0.05). NRDc, MOTS-c, and hsIL-6 were not significantly associated with one another (all p > 0.20).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the potential clinical relevance of serum NRDc and MOTS-c concentrations in KTRs. Our salient findings are as follows: (i) both serum NRDc and MOTS-c were elevated in KTRs as compared with healthy controls; (ii) NRDc showed weak positive correlations with BMI and fasting glucose, and was inversely associated with the presence of CV disease; (iii) MOTS-c showed no significant associations with cardiometabolic parameters; and (iv) neither candidate marker provided predictive value regarding allograft loss or decline in graft function in this cohort with a median follow-up of over 2 years.

The apparent enrichment of serum in NRDc post-KTx is novel. Since NRDc is a metalloprotease involved in antigen processing and inflammatory signaling, the observed increase could mirror a persistent pro-inflammatory milieu promoted by alloimmune activation, calcineurin inhibitor-related toxicity, and residual uremic toxins [1–3, 42]. Weak positive relationships between NRDc and metabolic parameters (i.e., BMI and fasting glucose) are consistent with the existing experimental data that point to the regulatory role of NRDc regarding insulin sensitivity and cellular metabolism [32, 33]. In turn, the inverse association between NRDc and CV disease is interesting, though it requires cautious interpretation. We recorded lower NRDc levels in patients with atherosclerotic disease, even after accounting for important traditional CV risk factors. This finding is controversial and counterintuitive with prior studies reporting that higher NRDc is tied to worse prognosis after MI [34]. However, other research groups have also shown that lower NRDc levels are observed in patients with transient ischemic attack, as compared with controls [35], suggesting differential interpretation depending on the context. We can speculate that NRDc may be used or downregulated in the disorders with severe vascular dysfunction, in a similar manner to complement consumption in, e.g., active lupus. Lower NRDc concentrations could also reflect a reduced “metabolic reserve” in KTRs with CV disease. Due to design, we are unable to study the directionality; therefore, future longitudinal studies with incident CV events in KTRs are of particular interest.

We also observed that serum MOTS-c was elevated in KTRs, as compared with controls, which contrasts with other groups reporting reduced MOTS-c levels in patients with CKD and sarcopenia risk on peritoneal dialysis [21, 22]. However, our findings fall in line with observations in hemodialysis subjects, in whom higher MOTS-c was associated with adverse CV outcomes [23]. Differential associations recorded across CKD populations could reflect differences due to treatment modality, the degree of kidney function, inflammation, or other, unknown factors [43]. MOTS-c is translocated into the nucleus following metabolic stress triggers [19], and as such, enhanced MOTS-c levels in KTRs could nonspecifically parallel a compensatory response [18, 20]. Based on earlier research, lower MOTS-c levels were linked to vascular endothelial injury in distinct populations [24, 25]. However, we did not observe any significant relationship between MOTS-c levels and any cardiometabolic parameters, which suggests its differential regulation and limited utility in KTR populations.

Pooled data from observation studies support the clinical relevance of MetS components in predicting graft survival and CV mortality in KTRs [6], which is supported by a genetic link [11]. The evaluation of circulating blood markers that are also mediators of pathobiological processes remains of high interest for the future goal of individualized risk evaluation in post-KTx management [44, 45]. Unfortunately, neither candidate metabolic marker could be characterized with prognostic value for allograft function decline, loss, or all-cause mortality. Due to moderate cohort size and low event rate [46], it is likely that statistical power is limited to effects of at least moderate strength, which may be further limited by a follow-up period of only 29 months on average, given the relatively stable nature of this KTR cohort. Most importantly, the cross-sectional, singular timepoint assessment of these serum markers, without accounting for longitudinal variation, may not adequately reflect the dynamic changes that precede events of clinical interest [47]. Baseline values in isolation are likely only reliable in determining a “resting/basal” state.

This study has several weaknesses that should be stated. Given the observational design, causal inference is limited and interpretation is mainly based on the array of background theoretical evidence [48, 49]. Due to non-interventional nature, we did not measure tissue expression of either NRDc nor MOTS-c, which would be particularly helpful for mechanistic interpretation, as would serial assessments of their concentrations. The selected ELISA assays have been validated for commercial use and have been used previously [26, 35], though they have not been extensively characterized in KTx populations and their potential for standardization and correspondence to the bioactive compounds of interest requires caution [50]. Confounding due to immunosuppressant or CV medications is another potential source of bias [51]. We are also unable to account for other clinically relevant confounders, such as immunological serology [15]. Lastly, this single-center study is based on a cohort of white Caucasian patients, which limits generalizability to more heterogeneous patient groups [52].
