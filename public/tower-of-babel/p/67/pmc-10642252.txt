Higher Odds of Adverse Cutaneous Reactions in Patients With Dermatomyositis Treated With Hydroxychloroquine Compared With Methotrexate
Authors: Astia Allenzara, Alison Hollis, Carolina Álvarez, Haejin Lovelace, Steve Maczuga, Matthew Helm, Nancy Olsen, Amanda Nelson, Galen Foulke
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveHydroxychloroquine (HCQ) use for the treatment of dermatomyositis (DM) has been associated with adverse cutaneous reactions. We applied a new user, active comparator, retrospective design to assess differences in adverse cutaneous reactions or hospitalizations between HCQ and methotrexate (MTX) use among patients with DM.MethodsWe used a national network of data from insurance registries (TriNetX), enrolling patients with two International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes for DM separated by 6 months or more who had a prescription for either (but not both) HCQ or MTX on or after DM diagnosis. Outcomes were adverse cutaneous reactions (ICD codes) or hospital admission (Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes) within 4 months from the prescription dispense date. Logistic regression was used to produce adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) comparing outcomes in the HCQ group (n = 1364) and the MTX group (n = 1400), adjusted for age at first DM diagnosis, year of birth, sex, and time from DM diagnosis to first prescription.ResultsOverall, we found no significant difference in odds of hospitalization in those taking HCQ (aOR 1.05; 95% CI: 0.79‐1.39) compared with those on MTX. Patients with DM on HCQ had 30% higher odds of adverse cutaneous reaction diagnosis compared with patients on MTX (aOR 1.30; 95% CI: 1.02‐1.59). Age at DM diagnosis was an effect modifier of this association, with higher odds of adverse cutaneous reaction among patients taking HCQ who were younger at diagnosis.ConclusionCompared with MTX use, HCQ use, especially in younger patients, may result in higher odds of adverse cutaneous reactions.

INTRODUCTION

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy involving the skin and muscle. Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) and other antimalarial drugs have been effectively used for patients with various rheumatic diseases, including DM, although HCQ use has been associated with a heterogenous range of adverse cutaneous reactions (1). Adverse dermatologic effects of HCQ were investigated in a systematic review in which most patients (72% of cases) had lupus erythematosus whereas only 5% of cases were patients with DM. This study found that the most common cutaneous reaction from HCQ was morbilliform drug eruptions (358 cases), followed by hyperpigmentation (116 cases), acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (27 cases), and Stevens‐Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis (26 cases). Most of these adverse cutaneous eruptions occurred within 4 weeks of HCQ initiation (2).

The reported prevalence of drug eruption in patients with DM on antimalarials varies in the literature from 5% to 31% (3, 4, 5, 6). One retrospective cohort series reported a 5% (2 of 44) frequency of adverse cutaneous drug reactions in patients with DM and 4% (4 of 136) in patients with cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE) (3). Another study found an adverse cutaneous reaction incidence for patients with DM treated with HCQ of 9.2% (11 of 120), which was significantly higher than that for patients with CLE treated with HCQ (0%, 0 of 150) even though patients with CLE had a higher prevalence of non‐HCQ drug allergies (100 drug allergies in patients with CLE vs. 60 drug allergies in patients with DM) (7). A retrospective case‐control study of patients with DM matched to patients with CLE by age, sex, and race found that patients with DM developed more cutaneous reactions (31% vs. 3%; P = 0.006) (4).

Therapeutic or adverse responses to HCQ may depend on patients’ immune profile and concomitant medication use. Patients with DM on HCQ who did not respond to therapy had significantly higher myeloid dendritic cell counts from skin biopsy samples compared with patients with DM who did respond to HCQ (8). Additionally there have been reports of increased disease activity (from rash) with HCQ initiation (9), along with difficulty in distinguishing between rash from a hypersensitivity‐type drug reaction and acute worsening of the skin disease in DM (5). Concomitant treatment may also impact the risk of an adverse cutaneous reaction because one study found patients with DM on immunosuppressive therapies were less likely to develop an adverse cutaneous reaction (7). At a single center, patients had similar rates of cutaneous eruptions across all antimalarial classes except for the combination of HCQ and quinacrine, which was significantly lower (hazard ratio 0.231, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.07‐0.82) (10). Furthermore, an adverse reaction was significantly associated with patients with DM having the myositis‐specific small ubiquitin‐like modifier 1 activating enzyme (SAE‐1/2) autoantibody and negatively associated with the presence of melanoma differentiation–associated gene 5 (MDA‐5) autoantibody (5). There is a signal of increased adverse cutaneous reactions in patients with DM on HCQ compared with patients with other diseases, such as CLE, which may be related to autoantibody status or increased disease activity not controlled by HCQ or represent an allergic cutaneous response.

How adverse cutaneous reactions compare in patients with DM treated with HCQ versus those treated with methotrexate (MTX) is unknown. The purpose of the current study was to determine the prevalence of hospitalizations and adverse cutaneous reactions in patients with DM treated with HCQ compared with those treated with MTX using a new user active comparator design in a large commercial database.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This is a retrospective analysis using the commercial insurance claims database TriNetX, a global health research network providing a deidentified data set of electronic medical records (diagnoses, procedures, medications, laboratory values, genomic information). The data are deidentified based on the standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) Privacy Rule. The process by which data sets are deidentified is attested to through a formal determination by a qualified expert as defined in Section §164.514(b) (1) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule. Patients from the US were included if they had a diagnosis of DM via International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) or International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) coding on two occasions at least 6 months apart, without any prior entries of DM 6 months prior to their first diagnosis, to ensure that the patient had DM and that it was not an erroneous first entry. Validation of ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 coding for DM has been described previously (11, 12). To capture a new user of either MTX or HCQ, patients with a record of MTX or HCQ that appeared before the DM code were excluded, as were patients who were on both MTX and HCQ. Medications were recorded when they were dispensed by the pharmacy. There was no information on the dosage of MTX or HCQ from the data set. Because of the high frequency of missing data for patient race and ethnicity, this was not reported. TriNetX covers most US regions, with approximately 20% of patients from the Northeast, Midwest, and West each and 40% from the South. Hospitalizations were defined by the presence of a hospitalization‐specific Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code (99231‐99233) that occurred within 4 months from the prescription dispense date. Adverse cutaneous reactions were defined by the presence of at least one relevant ICD code (Supplementary Table 1) that occurred in the first 4 months after the medication dispense date.

A new user, active comparator design was selected to minimize potential confounding in the comparison of HCQ use to MTX use in patients with DM (13). MTX was chosen as a comparator because it is a common treatment initiated at new diagnosis of DM. Logistic regression was used to produce adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% CIs for the outcomes comparing HCQ to MTX use. There were two primary outcomes: 1) adverse cutaneous reaction, defined by entry of a skin reaction ICD code (Supplementary Table 1), and 2) hospitalization, defined as entry of a specific CPT code. Both outcomes were assessed for the 4 months following dispense of MTX or HCQ. The length of 4 months was chosen to capture as much recent time surrounding the medication dispense date (ie, to include 90‐day fills as well as 30‐day fills). We assessed outcomes at the medication level; with each date the medication was dispensed in a new user, we assessed the subsequent 4 months from that date for the outcomes of adverse cutaneous reaction and hospitalization. For these models, correlation of multiple medications within patients was handled using generalized estimating equations with an autoregressive correlation structure in which correlation between outcomes is higher when close together but lower when further apart. We also assessed outcomes at the patient level; if at any time there was an outcome of either hospitalization or adverse cutaneous reaction, then the patient was found as having the outcome. The odds ratio was adjusted for age at first DM diagnosis, year of birth, sex, and time from DM diagnosis to the first prescription. Adjustment for these covariates was undertaken given that patients diagnosed at an older age or with a longer time between their first diagnosis and prescription may be more likely to have other comorbidities, be hospitalized, or have a harder‐to‐control disease. In addition to producing overall effects, interactions between medication use and other covariables were considered and included if significant at P < 0.10. Statistical analysis was performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

RESULTS

A total of 2764 patients with DM met inclusion criteria, of whom 1400 (51%) were on MTX and 1364 (49%) were on HCQ (Figure 1). Most patients (80%) were female and were diagnosed with DM in their 50s, although a range of ages were represented (Table 1). Of these individuals, 218 (8%) experienced a hospitalization outcome and 321 (12%) experienced an adverse cutaneous reaction outcome.

In both the patient‐ and medication‐level analysis, there was no evidence of a significant difference in hospitalization between those on HCQ and those on MTX. There was evidence (interaction P < 0.1) of an effect modification by sex, with higher odds of any hospitalization for women with DM on HCQ (vs. MTX) and lower odds of any hospitalization for men with DM on HCQ (vs. MTX), although neither effect was significantly different from the null (Table 2). Patients with DM on HCQ had 30% higher odds of having an adverse cutaneous reaction diagnosis compared with those on MTX. This effect was found to be significantly different by age at DM diagnosis, with higher odds of an adverse cutaneous reaction among HCQ users who were younger at DM diagnosis (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Using a new user, active comparator design in a large insurance claims database, we identified a frequency of 8% for hospitalization and 12% for adverse cutaneous reactions in patients with DM starting HCQ or MTX. Although not statistically significant, women with DM starting HCQ (vs. MTX) had more hospitalizations than men. Overall, patients with DM had 30% higher odds of adverse cutaneous reaction after starting HCQ compared with MTX, an effect that was amplified in younger patients.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use a new user, active comparator design to evaluate the frequency of hospitalizations and skin reactions in patients with DM on HCQ versus MTX. Previous studies have compared HCQ users with DM to HCQ users with other diseases, such as CLE (3, 4, 10). By comparing HCQ users to MTX users, we aim to increase the likelihood of similar patient characteristics as opposed to comparing HCQ users to non‐HCQ users or HCQ users with a different disease. Comparing adverse cutaneous reactions across diseases may bias results because DM and CLE are different diseases with different organ or systemic involvement.

An advantage of HCQ is its lack of immunosuppression and cytotoxicity; however, its utility may be limited by a lack of efficacy in treating DM. Although HCQ is used frequently for patients with amyopathic DM (76.5% [88 of 115 patients]), it may not be an effective monotherapy because adequate control was only achieved in 11.4% of cases and use may be associated with cutaneous hypersensitivity reaction, with 30.7% of patients developing such reactions (14). In another single‐center study, patients with DM with skin‐only manifestations treated with HCQ as a first‐line therapy had a response rate of 24.4% (10 of 41) (6). HCQ monotherapy may not be enough for disease control. When selecting therapy, it is important to counsel patients on this possible adverse reaction to HCQ, including cutaneous adverse events.

Prior work has highlighted the heterogeneity of DM through the difference in responses to HCQ. Patients with SAE‐1/2 antibody–positive DM had an odds ratio of 8.43 (95% CI: 1.98‐49.19), whereas the presence of anti‐MDA‐5 antibody was significantly negatively associated (odds ratio 0.06, 95% CI: 0.0004‐0.52) with patients with DM developing an adverse cutaneous reaction while on HCQ. Our study demonstrates that there may be increased odds of a cutaneous reaction in patients diagnosed at a younger age compared with patients diagnosed at an older age. Although care for patients with DM is shaped by the presence of myositis‐specific autoantibodies, DM disease heterogeneity may also extend to age at diagnosis because a younger age at DM diagnosis may predispose patients to an increased odds of adverse cutaneous reaction with HCQ treatment. It is possible that this association could be secondary to environmental exposures, with younger patients potentially having more sun exposure than older patients, or could reflect increased inflammatory activity. A previous study demonstrated that patients with DM who did not respond to HCQ therapy compared with those who did respond had a significantly higher amount of myeloid dendritic cells in biopsied skin (8).

Limitations of this study include the retrospective review of insurance claims. It is unknown if these medications are taken as prescribed; we can only be certain that patients purchased the prescribed medication. Notably for both groups, there was a significant delay from the first ICD code of DM to the dispense date of either MTX or HCQ. It is possible this could reflect the wait times to see a subspecialist or a trial of topical therapy first. Although the occurrence of hospitalization was known, the reason for hospitalization was not known. Furthermore, the skin reactions were submitted as ICD codes in accordance with previously validated methodology. We recognize the inherent limitation of being unable to personally confirm the diagnosis through chart review. It is possible that coding for adverse cutaneous reaction could represent a flare of DM skin disease (5, 9). Adverse cutaneous reactions are complex and are often a clinical diagnosis (15). It is possible the adverse cutaneous reaction could represent a hypersensitivity‐type drug reaction or increased disease activity potentially from the presence of increased myeloid dendritic cells found in HCQ nonresponders (8). If HCQ causes a flare of skin disease, this is an outcome of interest given a previous publication describing increased myeloid dendritic cells and production of interferon‐β in patients with DM who were nonresponders to HCQ (8). However, we assume such flares of skin disease not related to medication to be normally distributed in our large sample size and expect equal incidence across both comparator groups. Another limitation we considered is that patients with more severe disease may be prescribed MTX over HCQ, as HCQ has been recommended to try first for DM, followed potentially by MTX (6). As such, we anticipated a higher hospitalization rate with MTX owing to more severe disease. However, this difference from our expectations may represent an unexpectedly higher than anticipated hospitalization rate in patients taking HCQ. We did not assess for concomitant malignancy, so it is possible this could be a confounding variable causing increased hospitalization or cutaneous reactions from cancer or response to cancer therapy. An additional limitation is that although there is validation for the use of some ICD codes for drug eruptions (16, 17) to enhance confidence in diagnostic accuracy, there was no way to specifically determine that the rash was caused by the medication; rashes may thus reflect ongoing disease activity from DM in some cases (5, 9). Future directions could include further patient subtyping, such as paraneoplastic DM, and comparing patients on other medications, such as HCQ new users to azathioprine or mycophenolate new users.

In conclusion, we found 30% higher odds of adverse cutaneous reaction in patients with DM prescribed HCQ compared to MTX as a first‐line therapy. Age at diagnosis was an effect modifier, with higher odds of adverse cutaneous reaction in those taking HCQ who were diagnosed with DM at a younger age. This study, in conjunction with prior work (6) demonstrating low efficacy of HCQ monotherapy for the management of cutaneous DM, suggests that the HCQ risk–benefit profile may leave it poorly positioned as a first‐line therapy for DM.
