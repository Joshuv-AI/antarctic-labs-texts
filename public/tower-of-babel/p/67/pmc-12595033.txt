Design and evaluation of a novel pyridine-thiazole hybrid adsorbent as high-capacity adsorbent for methylene blue removal from water
Authors: Mohamed Khalifa, Ahmed Abdelkader, Sama Ammar, Omnia Khalifa, Wafaa Mohamed, Fatema Maklad, Rahma Ayman, Shaban Ali, Asmaa Reda, Eman Elsaka, Israa Shokr, Nihad Ghallab, Esraa Elsobhy, Shahd Elakraa, Yasmeen Elmasry, Reham Gaber, Donia Farag, Eslam Salama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the novel application of a pyridine-thiazole hybrid adsorbent, MPHT 4, for the efficient removal of methylene blue (MB) from contaminated water. The adsorbent was synthesized via a high-yield route and characterized using 1H/13C NMR, IR spectroscopy, HRESI-MS, elemental analysis, SEM, and BET surface area measurements. SEM revealed a homogeneous morphology with low porosity, while BET analysis indicated a surface area of 68.83 m2/g and a total pore volume of 0.113 cm³/g, classifying the material as a Type IV isotherm with micropores. The adsorption efficiency of MPHT 4 was systematically evaluated, achieving 86.7% removal of 25 ppm MB at pH 7 with a dosage of 1.0 g/L within 60 min. Kinetic studies confirmed a pseudo-second-order mechanism, while equilibrium data best fit the Freundlich isotherm, indicating multilayer adsorption on a heterogeneous surface. Thermodynamic analysis revealed a spontaneous and exothermic process, supported by a negative ΔG° (− 18.39 kJ/mol) and a positive ΔH° (29.92 kJ/mol). The adsorbent exhibited excellent stability and reusability over six cycles, facilitated by electrostatic interactions between MB and the negatively charged surface (pHPZC = 7.8). These findings highlight MPHT 4 as a promising, high-capacity adsorbent for MB removal, combining structural robustness, optimal porosity, and efficient regeneration for practical wastewater treatment applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19531-4.Subject terms: Environmental chemistry, Materials chemistry

Introduction

Global problems have arisen from the growing demand for and scarcity of clean water supplies brought on by population increase, industrialization, and protracted droughts1. One of the most fundamental humanitarian objectives is the provision of inexpensive, clean water, which is still a significant worldwide concern in the twenty-first century2. Wastewater treatment becomes a significant environmental and public health risk when contaminated water, including chemical and biological contaminants, is discharged into natural ecosystems. Therefore, the creation of effective materials that can remove or neutralize dangerous pollutants is essential to the field of environmental remediation3,4. One of the most difficult problems for the storage, purification, and reusing systems is the wastewater collected from many industries, including pharmaceuticals, textiles, dyes and metals5–8.

Pyridine is a common liquid waste product of a number of industrial processes, such as coking gasification and pharmaceutical manufacture. Pyridine’s toxicity to a number of bacterial species makes it persistent and dangerous in the environment since it prevents biodegradation processes. Furthermore, aquatic and soil ecology are influenced by water-contaminated dyes such as methylene blue, methyl orange, and methyl violet5–8. The treatment of wastewater contaminated with pyridine compounds9,10 and removal of cationic and anionic dyes11–15 has been extensively researched, with an emphasis on various removal strategies such as biodegradation11membrane-filtration12electrochemical processes like electrocoagulation13photocatalytic degradation or photodegradation14advanced oxidation processes15 and adsorption16–22. The method used is determined by criteria such as dye kind, concentration, and wastewater properties.

Heteroaromatic derivatives containing nitrogen like pyridine or containing nitrogen sulfur, such thiazole rings act as chelating agents in metal ligand chemistry, selectively removing metals from wastewater and reducing bacterial infection23–28. Heteroaromatic derivatives can form hydrogen bonds, π-π stacking, electrostatic, and other non-covalent bonding interactions. They also have a broad conjugation system and strong intramolecular electron transfer characteristics, allowing for chelation with metal ions29. As a result, the thiazole ring, with its distinct molecular structure and physicochemical features, has received significant interest in in various fields30–32. Additionally, Schiff bases, particularly those produced from thiosemicarbazide are appealing because of their numerous active sites and have attracted a lot of attention because of their ability to interact with different contaminants and create coordination bonds with metals33–39. In this study, we synthesized and characterized new pyridyl thiazolyl hydrazones for efficient removal of methylene blue from wastewater through adsorption mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Materials

6-Methylpyridine-2-carbaldehyde (98.0%) was obtained from Win–Win Chemical CO., Limited., China. Thiosemicarbazide (99%) and phenacyl bromide (98%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich company. All reagents and solvents were used without extra purification.

Characterization methods

Homogeneity of the product and follow-up of the reaction was checked by thin layer chromatography (TLC) on plates precoated with silica gel G (Merck; layer thickness 0.25 mm), used without pretreatment. The melting point was measured using the Stuart SMP10 (Stuart Scientific, Stone, UK). The NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker Avance 400 Nanobay (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA) instruments with signals from residual protons of DMSO-d6 solvent as the internal standard. The IR spectra was recorded on a Bruker Vector-22 spectrometer (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA). The HRESI experiment was performed with an Agilent 6550 iFunnel Q-TOF LC/MS (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), equipped with Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC. Elemental analysis was performed using a PerkinElmer PE 2400 CHNS/O analyzer. All reagents and solvents were purchased from either Acros (Geel, Belgium) or Sigma Aldrich (Burlington, VT, USA) and used without further purification. The morphological structure of the synthesized MPHT 4 was characterized via Scanning Electron Microscopes (SEM, JEOL JSM 6360LA, Japan) with gold coating. Moreover, the surface area and pore size distribution of the prepared MPHT 4 were determined via Belsorp-max automated apparatus (BEL Japan). All computational jobs were carried out using the Gaussian 09 W software package40. Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed to investigate the electronic ground-state properties, employing the B3LYP functional in conjunction with the 6-311G basis set.

To assess reusability, MPHT 4 was regenerated over seven cycles. In each cycle, 1.0 g of adsorbent was stirred in 100 mL of 0.01 M NaOH (350 rpm, 24 h), washed twice with deionized water and once with ethanol, and dried overnight at 70 °C for subsequent reuse.

Results and discussion

Synthesis of 2-(2-((6-methylpyridin-2-yl)methylene)hydrazineyl)-4-phenylthiazole (MPHT)

Novel hydrazone based on pyridyl thiazole rings MPHT 4 was synthesized via the one-pot reaction of pyridine carbaldehyde 1, thiosemicarbazide 2, and phenacyl bromide 3 in EtOH under reflux conditions (Fig. 1). Pyridyl thiazole derivative MPHT 4 was synthesized through condensation reactions of thiosemicarbazide 2 with pyridine carbaldehyde 1 and with phenacyl bromide 3 followed by formation of the thiazole ring that proceeds via intramolecular cyclization following nucleophilic attack by the thiosemicarbazide sulfur atom on the electrophilic carbon of phenacyl bromide. The advantages of this method are simple setup and straightforward product isolation, reproducibility, using cheap and ecofriendly solvent.

The structure of MPHT 4 was determined by IR, 1H NMR, 13C NMR, HRESI mass spectra and elemental analysis.

The integration of signals in MPHT’s 1H NMR spectrum corresponds to the anticipated number of protons. The 1H NMR spectrum of MPHT 4 displays 10 signals corresponding to 14 protons. Methyl group appeared at δ 2.69 ppm, nine aromatic protons of pyridyl and phenyl rings appeared in the expected region around 8.31–7.30 ppm and a singlet signal at 7.54 ppm confirms the thiazole ring closure. Azomethine proton HC = N appears at 8.22 ppm, usually downfield due to deshielding by the adjacent nitrogen atoms.

In addition, the N–H signal appeared as a broad singlet at 13.23 ppm (Fig. 2a,b). The N–H signal can be endo- or exocyclic. However, according to literature46the signal at 14.50–11.45 ppm is related to an exocyclic N-H. Consequently, the structure is confirmed by the chemical shift value (13.23 ppm), which is comparable to exocyclic thiosemicarbazones that have been previously described22,23.

Decontamination of MB dye using MPHT 4

The potential of MPHT 4 for removing methylene blue (MB) from wastewater was assessed via batch adsorption experiments. The original MB concentration versus different parameters plots for the MPHT 4 adsorption experiments was included in the supplementary data (Figures S1–S6).

Kinetics of MB adsorption onto MPHT 4

The adsorption dynamics of MB onto MPHT 4 were investigated over a 180-minute period at varying time intervals. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the initial adsorption phase was rapid, likely due to the high surface area and abundant functional groups on MPHT 447–49. Subsequently, the adsorption rate gradually decreased until equilibrium was attained. The high adsorption efficiency can be attributed to electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged functional groups of MPHT 4 (N-pyridine, N-NH hydrazone, and S-thiazole) and the positively charged MB molecules48,50,51. The system reached equilibrium within approximately 60 min, achieving a maximum MB removal efficiency of 86.7%. These findings confirm the effectiveness of MPHT 4 as an adsorbent for MB in wastewater treatment.

Effect of initial pH on the decontamination processes

The influence of initial pH on the MB adsorption process was investigated over a pH range of 1–11, as shown in Fig. 9a. The adsorption efficiency exhibited strong pH dependence, reaching a maximum removal of 86.71% at neutral pH (7) and decreasing to 58.21% under acidic conditions (pH 1)42. This pH-dependent behavior can be explained by the point of zero charge (pHPZC = 7.8, Fig. 9b) of the MPHT 4 adsorbent. The pHPZC is defined as the pH at which the surface of an adsorbent has a net zero electrical charge. At this pH, the number of positive surface charges equals the number of negative charges, resulting in a neutral surface43. At pH values above the pHPZC, the adsorbent surface becomes negatively charged, enhancing electrostatic attraction with the cationic MB molecules and resulting in higher adsorption capacity. Conversely, when the solution pH lower than the pHPZC, the adsorbent surface acquires a positive charge, leading to reduced MB adsorption due to electrostatic repulsion. The optimal adsorption at pH 7 suggests that near-neutral conditions provide the ideal balance between adsorbent surface charge and dye ionization state for maximum removal efficiency. These findings demonstrate that pH control is crucial for effective MB decontamination using MPHT 4, with neutral pH being the most favorable condition for wastewater treatment applications. The results are consistent with established adsorption mechanisms for ionic dyes on charged surfaces, where electrostatic interactions play a dominant role in the adsorption process45,53. To further validate the determination of the point of zero charge (pHPZC), we conducted zeta potential measurements, which revealed a surface potential of − 112 mV for MPHT 4. This strongly supports the presence of a negatively charged surface under the experimental conditions, confirming the electrostatic attraction between the adsorbent and the cationic MB dye41. These findings are consistent with the pHPZC results presented in Fig. 9b and reinforce the proposed adsorption mechanism.

Optimization of adsorbent dosage for MB removal

The influence of MPHT 4 dosage (0.25–8 g/L) on methylene blue removal was systematically investigated, revealing that increased adsorbent loading enhanced MB removal percentage while decreasing the adsorption capacity per unit mass (Fig. 10)54. This inverse relationship results from incomplete utilization of available adsorption sites at higher dosages, as evidenced by the optimal balance achieved at 1.0 g/L, which provided effective contaminant removal without excessive material consumption. The observed trends align with established adsorption mechanisms, where higher adsorbent quantities increase active site availability but may lead to site saturation effects, ultimately demonstrating the importance of dosage optimization for efficient wastewater treatment applications49,50.

Impact of initial methylene blue concentrations on the decontamination efficiency

Experiments examining MB adsorption at concentrations ranging from 5 to 200 mg/L revealed that capacity increased with higher initial concentrations (Fig. 11), consistent with literature reports55. The adsorption process displayed two distinct phases: an initial rapid uptake due to abundant surface sites, followed by slower removal as sites filled. This concentration-dependent behavior stems from the stronger mass transfer driving force created by larger concentration gradients. The MPHT 4 adsorbent demonstrated maximum efficiency at 100 ppm (72.1% removal), proving its robustness for MB remediation across different contamination levels50,51.

Reusability assessment

Eight consecutive adsorption-desorption cycles were performed to evaluate MPHT 4’s regeneration potential. As shown in Fig. 13, the material maintained satisfactory MB removal efficiency (> 60%) through five reuse cycles. The gradual decline in performance may stem from multiple factors affecting the adsorbent’s chemical stability and regeneration efficacy50,54. Potential mechanisms include: (1) partial blockage or modification of adsorption sites after repeated use, and (2) incomplete removal of MB molecules or degradation byproducts during NaOH treatment and washing, leading to reduced availability of active sites48,55.

Adsorption mechanism of MB by MPHT 4

Methylene blue (MB), a cationic thiazine dye, interact with MPHT4 through various non-covalent interactions, and these interactions lead to observed shifts in the infrared (IR) spectrum of 4. The IR spectra of MPHT4-MB (Fig. 14) showed characteristic bands at 3421 (N–H)hydrazine, 3174 (CH)azomethine,, 3099, 3068 (CH)aromatic, 2957(CH3), 1581 (C=N)azomethine, 1544 (C=N)pyridine ring, 1465, 1445, 1355 (C=C)aromatic, 1138 (N–N), 1005 (C=N)thiazole and 704 cm−1 (C–S)thiazole41.

(a) comparison of IR band positions of 2-(2-((6-methylpyridin-2-yl)methylene)-hydrazineyl)-4-phenylthiazole MPHT 4 and MPHT 4-MB and (b) Interactions between MPHT 4 and MPHT4-MB.

The largest shifts are observed for characteristic bands (CH)azomethine, (C = N)azomethine, (N–N), and aromatic fragment (C = N)thiazole (have ∆vmax = 149, 74, 39 and 47 cm−1, respectively), while (C–S)thiazole and (C = N)pyridine ring are less shifted after addition MB (have ∆vmax = 12 and 10 cm−1, respectively). Thus, it becomes obvious that the cationic MB is binding with MPHT 4 as shown in Fig. 14b, forming cation–π interaction and ion-dipole Forces42.

Conclusions

In this study, a novel pyridine-thiazole hybrid adsorbent, MPHT 4, was successfully synthesized and characterized for the efficient removal of MB from aqueous solutions. Structural analysis via SEM and BET revealed a homogeneous morphology with a surface area of 68.83 m2/g and micropore-dominated porosity (Type IV isotherm), facilitating effective dye adsorption. The adsorbent demonstrated exceptional performance, achieving 86.7% MB removal under optimized conditions (pH 7, 1.0 g/L dosage, 25 °C, 60 min), with kinetics best described by the pseudo-second-order model. Isotherm studies indicated that the Freundlich model provided the best fit, suggesting multilayer adsorption on a heterogeneous surface, while thermodynamic analysis confirmed the spontaneity (ΔG° = −18.39 kJ/mol) and exothermic nature (ΔH° = 29.92 kJ/mol) of the process. The adsorption mechanism was driven by electrostatic interactions, supported by the adsorbent’s negatively charged surface (pHPZC = 7.8) and confirmed through IR spectral shifts. Additionally, MPHT 4 exhibited remarkable reusability, maintaining > 60% efficiency over six regeneration cycles. These results underscore the material’s potential as a sustainable, high-capacity adsorbent for MB removal, combining structural integrity, optimal surface properties, and cost-effective regeneration for industrial wastewater treatment. Future work could explore scalability and performance in real wastewater matrices to further validate practical applicability.
