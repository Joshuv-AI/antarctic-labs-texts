Engineering and logistical concerns add practical limitations to stratospheric aerosol injection strategies
Authors: Miranda Hack, V Faye McNeill, Dan Steingart, Gernot Wagner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of reflective aerosols in the upper atmosphere (stratospheric aerosol injections, SAI) to limit incoming sunlight has been proposed as a potential means of countering anthropogenic climate change. Such a strategy ideates from observed cooling effects due to sulfate aerosol formation following volcanic eruptions. Solid mineral candidates have been proposed as a sulfate alternative, potentially lowering environmental risks like ozone depletion and absorption of radiation. The bulk of SAI modeling literature focuses on optimal deployment scenarios, in which practical constraints—microphysical, geopolitical, and economic—are not considered. Here, we explore several key micro- and macroscopic aspects of deployment that may directly increase risk, and the degree to which technical and governance approaches could be levied to offset it. We find that the risk and design space for SAI may be considerably constrained by factors like supply chains and governance. Logistical and technical considerations, most significantly difficulties in dispersing solid aerosols at scale in the desired size range, and the radiative properties of potentially formed aggregates, notably introduce uncertainties in the outcomes of solid-based SAI strategies more so than sulfate. We conclude that the design space for a “low-risk” SAI strategy, particularly with solid aerosol, may be more limited than current literature reflects.Keywords: Geoengineering, Governance, Supply chain, Injection technologiesSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Engineering

Introduction

Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI), as the most widely studied form of solar geoengineering, has found prominence as a “fast,” “cheap,” and “imperfect” potential climate change intervention1. With the ability to rapidly reduce global temperatures for costs on the order of $10 billion per year per C of cooling2, SAI presents an opportunity to temper key climate risks as a potential supplement to addressing the root cause by cutting greenhouse-gas (GHG) emissions. SAI is not without considerable risk: e.g. sulfate aerosol—the most commonly considered candidate material for SAI—can induce stratospheric warming due to absorption of upwelling infrared (IR) radiation and lead to ozone depletion via subsequent dynamical changes and surface reactions that convert stable halogen reservoir species to photolabile forms involved in catalytic ozone destruction3,4. Sulfate, largely proposed due to the natural analogue of volcanic eruptions, is generally formed in the stratosphere via the release of gaseous which oxidizes and condenses to form sulfate aerosol over the course of several weeks. This process, with some variations with injected mass flux, results in submicron aerosols that backscatter quite efficiently5. Alternative sulfate-based strategies have also been explored, though to a lesser extent. Direct injection of accumulation-mode might yield better size distributions, though the ability to control injection to generate aerosols in this size range remains subject to considerable engineering constraints and uncertainties6–8. COS, or carbonyl sulfide, a long lived gas that can contribute to stratospheric sulfate aerosol from surface injections, also potentially results in smaller, better-scattering particles; however, these have lower yields than and thus require larger injection rates9.

The use of mineral aerosols with more desirable optical and chemical properties to minimize these risks has been proposed. Candidates include calcium carbonate (), alpha alumina ( - ), rutile and anatase titania (), cubic zirconia (), and diamond10. However, the use of these minerals has primarily been modeled with the assumption that they could be dispersed on a grid box scale with a single, optimal sub-micron particle size11,12. Realistically, aerosolizing sub-micron minerals poses a variety of challenges. The ability to mass manufacture near-monodisperse nanomaterials in the desired size range is uncertain. Similarly, the ability to generate aerosols in a spherical, monomer form upon and after dispersal via breakup of agglomerates formed during storage, transport and injection is another major source of uncertainty.

On a broader scale, lack of coordinated SAI governance leads to further risks surrounding deployment strategies13,14. Location, timing, and magnitude of injections play critical roles in the size of risk and its consequences. Economic constraints stemming from inelastic supply chains for several of the proposed candidates may add further obstacles. We here explore several key micro- and macroscopic aspects of deployment strategies, exploring how these largely unexplored practical constraints may lead to increased risk, if left unaddressed, and under what circumstances technical and governance approaches could be leveraged to offset this.

Results

Deployment strategies and governance

Here, we explore potential environmental implications of varied SAI injection strategies and scenarios as found in current modeling literature. Strategies are grouped by latitude—the primary injection parameter controlling the latitudinal distribution of aerosol and resulting stratospheric aerosol optical density (sAOD), which dictates many climate impacts—and degree of governance, varying from “centralized” (international coordination) to “decentralized” (grassroots efforts by local, non-state actors)15. Stratosphere-based deployments are explored here (as they comprise the bulk of literature), though surface emissions of an alternative sulfur-containing material, such as COS, for the formation of stratospheric sulfate have been shown to potentially yield good control and uniformity over latitudinal sAODs9.

Table 1 summarizes the results of our findings. Centralized scenarios allow for the leveraging of several parameters: most notably, latitude of injection, but also altitude, longitude, season and magnitude. These parameters interplay with each other to change the risk space of an SAI strategy. Control over each of these parameters through a coordinated, governed approach may be able to leverage benefits obtained from tuning each of these parameters in coordination with each other. These benefits stem primarily from knowledge about transport in the stratosphere, particularly the Brewer-Dobson circulation (BDC), which follows a pattern of tropical upwelling, followed by poleward transport, and downwelling at mid- to high latitudes. The strength and directionality of this circulation at a specific injection location or time dictates the lifetime of the injected aerosol and the resulting spatial distribution, two key factors related to environmental impacts.

Altitude

Aerosols injected at higher altitudes have higher stratospheric lifetimes, with injections at altitudes greater than 20 km more likely to be advected by a deep branch of the BDC, slowing the time it takes for them to reach the tropopause16. This leads to larger burdens and stronger radiative forcing per injection rate17,18. In addition to this, high-altitude injections have been modeled to produce less stratospheric warming and smaller changes in stratospheric water vapor in comparison to low-altitude counterparts for the same degree of surface cooling19. This may be, in part, due to the concentration of longwave heating near the tropical cold point tropopause region for lower-altitude injections20.

However, lifetime-related benefits may fade as the magnitude of injection increases. Tilmes (2017) finds that the lifetime of high-altitude injections decreases at larger injection rates21. Kleinschmitt (2018) supports this, finding that the rate of change of burden achieved per unit injection decreases with injection height (for equatorial injections of 10 Tg S ), with an optimal range between 17 and 19 km22.

Altitude-related lifetime benefits may have trade-offs with regards to ozone chemistry and radiative properties. High-altitude injections in Tilmes (2018) resulted in a 18% decrease in March NH polar column ozone compared to an 8% decrease with low-altitude injections, largely in part due to changes in advection that, in the high-altitude case, increased mixing ratios of ozone-reactive tracers19. Altitude also impacts relative humidity conditions and the degree of hygroscopic growth, which could affect aqueous aerosol sizes, compositions and resulting optical properties23, though this is highly dependent on the size of aerosol injected24.

Longitude

Longitude of injection has been assumed to be somewhat arbitrary in most simulations2,25, with longitudinal transport evenly dispersing aerosols zonally. However, longitude may play a significant role in particle lifetime, particularly for lower-altitude injections26. Longitudinal variations in meridional circulations impacts transport of aerosols into and out of the tropical pipe, a stratospheric region near the equator isolated from mid-latitude isentropic mixing, enabling rapid vertical transport27. Injections at longitudes with equator-ward winds yield particles with a longer lifetime than those with poleward ones16. For high altitude injections, this difference is likely somewhat negligible, as aerosols would already be strongly held in the tropical pipe upon injection.

Season

Season of injection also influences lifetime. The BDC is stronger in the winter, so winter injections have longer residence times. Along with lifetime, injection timing can improve the uniformity of the aerosol burden, potentially limiting effects of uneven cooling. Laakso et al. (2017) shows that seasonally changing the injection area such that aerosols reach optimal scattering size in the season that their latitudes experience maximum solar intensity leads to a more spatially homogeneous forcing distribution compared to equatorial injections28. Springtime injections could lead to peak sAODs during the summer, matching maximum cooling capacity to peak sunlight18,29,30. This strategy limits the burden required to achieve such an AOD relative to annual injections, and has benefits for sea ice restoration, as well as reduced impacts on stratospheric dynamics and ozone30.

Magnitude

The magnitude of injection is significant in two ways. The magnitude of the injection strategy over its entire lifetime is generally proportional to environmental risk, while varying the magnitudes of instantaneous local fluxes—i.e. how pulsed or sustained injections are—generates different climate impacts depending on the aerosol candidate. The total magnitude of a cooling strategy plays a role in the radiative efficacy of the aerosols: as injections approach large magnitudes (> 20 Tg S ), their cooling efficiency levels off17. For sulfate aerosol, this is largely due to overgrowth due to coagulation, limiting the shortwave forcing efficacy of the aerosol. Larger aerosols also sediment faster, requiring higher injection rates to maintain a significant burden and increasing ozone-depletion related risks associated with aerosol surface area. Similar microphysics are also significant when considering whether injections should be pulsed (i.e. high local flux) or continuous (low local flux). The tendency of new condensation nuclei to coagulate with existing large particles leads to undesirably large particles under conditions of low local gas-to-particle flux, as shown by Heckendorn (2009), which modeled zonally averaged injections31. Alternatives to gaseous injection, such as direct injections of accumulation-mode might help overcome this type of microphysical constraint. This is explored by Franke (2021), who found that particle number size distributions (PNSDs) were shifted to larger radii for 25 Tg S injections versus 5 Tg S for more significantly than 32. Niemeier (2011) found that longitudinally dispersed injections led to overgrowth when compared to pulsed injections in a single grid box5. Pulsed injections may also lead to different climate responses: Duan (2019) found that pulsed injections generally led to more cooling over land a larger decrease in land runoff than sustained injections for the same total amount of global cooling33. Pulsed versus sustained injections for solids are less well-understood. Solids coagulate differently since nucleation and ultra-fine particles aren’t relevant; Vattioni (2024) and Weisenstein (2015) find that for continuous monomer injections over a large spread (S - N), coagulation increases with increases injection rate and decreasing monomer size, potentially favoring smaller local fluxes of monomers11,12.

The optimal deployment scenario likely requires high-altitude injections, or slightly lowered at longitudes with equatorward winds. These should be either pulsed, high-local flux injections (in the case of ) or continuous, low local-flux injections (in the case of solid aerosol) focused in the winter or spring months. Such a scenario yields lengthened residence times in the stratosphere, a good match-up between well-matched peak sAODs and incident sunlight and limited overgrowth due to nucleation on background aerosol () and coagulation (mineral candidates). Deployment at multiple mid-latitudes is optimal to limit the impacts associated with nonuniform sAODs, including shifting of the inter-tropical convergence zone (ITCZ), locking of the quasi-biennial oscillation (QBO) and resulting impacts on the hydrological cycle. Latitudinal and hemispheric uniformity is potentially the most crucial element of deployment strategy as it pertains to tropospheric climate impacts and feedbacks. Solid aerosol dispersal

Compiling existing literature on technology for the production of sub-micron solid aerosols gives some insight into technical challenges in developing a dispersal system for solid SAI candidates. Due to the solid aerosol candidates’ high density and small primary particle size, these are classified as Geldart Group C “hard to fluidize” materials35, meaning they resist flowing along with a gas as primary particles, instead forming large (several microns) agglomerates. Cohesive intermolecular forces tend to hold primary particles together, and as primary particle sizes decrease, these cohesive forces tend to decrease less significantly than opposing forces in a gas flow, resulting in agglomerates that resist breakup.

Table 3 summarizes relevant dispersal techniques for several candidates. These studies also demonstrate an additional challenge associated with dispersing sub-micron particles. All of these methods use low solid mass flows, in part due to the need to limit particle-particle interactions leading to agglomeration. This suggests a potential limitation on the ability to cost-effectively disperse large (> 5,000 kg/hour2) quantities in the desired PNSDs. Additionally, nearly all of these studies use multiple deagglomeration techniques. Several emphasize that impaction is the most effective form of agglomerate breakup, followed by particle acceleration (e.g. nozzles, Venturi vacuums). This motivates a dispersal scheme that involves a continuous particle impaction step, such as particle milling, followed by fluidization and nozzle spraying to encourage agglomerate breakup on dispersal.

Modeling coupled gas-particle flow in a nozzle may give an idea of breakup likelihood under various dispersal conditions. Weber number (a dimensionless number comparing drag exerted on an agglomerate to cohesive intermolecular forces acting to hold it together) is used to gauge the likelihood of an agglomerate breaking up, with We < 1 indicating that breakup is not possible in the system considered.

The peak at the center of the nozzle corresponds to the gas accelerating near the nozzle throat to reach the speed of sound condition. Due to the convex shape of nozzle, this is the region where the gas accelerates the fastest, inducing more significant drag on the agglomerate as it accelerates at a slower rate. Once the gas stagnates, the downslope of the peak represents the particle reducing its rate of acceleration until its velocity is once again very similar to the gas.

The conditions of the gas entering the nozzle significantly impact the likelihood of agglomerate breakup. Using the motion of the plane to direct fast-moving, low-pressure air through the nozzle (Fig. 1a, red) results in significantly reduced drag, largely due to a reduced speed of sound at low temperatures, limited room for acceleration at a large inlet velocity, and low gas density. High pressure, slower-moving gas is clearly necessary to impart sufficient drag on an agglomerate, indicating the need for some sort of heavy-duty (> 100-fold pressure increase) in-flight air compression system, or the on-board transport of a highly pressurized carrier gas, which may impact economic assessments of costs for injection, as well as potential safety concerns. Additionally, at higher solid mass fractions, Weber numbers near the throat are reduced, a result of the coupled nature of the solid and gas momentum equations, which limits the ability of gas-particle laden systems to reach Mach 1 at the nozzle throat49. If such a nozzle dispersal approach were adopted, this may reduce possible solid dispersal rates (as suggested by literature) additionally increasing injection costs by decreasing the total amount of aerosol able to be injected per flight. Estimates for cost of sulfur-based deployments stem nearly entirely from aircraft-related expenses, making such decreases in payload likely to significantly impact costs2. To achieve the roughly Weber 1 condition shown in Fig. 1b (e.g. with a compressed gas and a moderate solid flow rate) would require a gas to solid mass ratio of roughly 35:1, which would have significant implications for the solid payload per flight. An in-flight compression system might lower this estimate, but this would require further analysis and would have to be factored into the plane design itself.

Aggregate forcing

The potential formation of fractal aggregates in mineral candidates may have significant implications for the efficacy of radiative transfer in the SW. Though sub-micron monomers may scatter sunlight up to 3x as effectively as sulfate aerosol, aggregates, if formed, will likely be much less effective. Figure 2 shows shortwave top-of-atmosphere forcing for fractal aggregates as compared to monomers of near-optimal size for various solid aerosol candidates.

Clear-sky shortwave forcing efficiency of fractal aggregates of varying dimension and monomer number. Sulfate is shown with the optimal monomer’s scattering efficiency. Aggregates are comprised of optimally sized monomers for each candidate.

Generally, larger aggregates scatter less efficiently, as expected for increasing optical size parameters. Fractal dimension appears to play a role in aggregate scattering efficiency. For aggregates with fractal dimensions greater than 1.5 (i.e. less branched fractals), reductions in SW forcing efficiency are less severe. For fractals with = 1.1, aggregates quickly reach a near-0 forcing efficiency as they coagulate. These large aggregates would sediment quickly, requiring increased injection rates alongside larger burdens to achieve the same degree of shortwave forcing as optimal monomers. Both of these factors may increase associated risks, particularly of ozone depletion via introduction of aerosol surface area, but also of stratospheric heating, dependent on the longwave properties of such fractals.

In an injection scenario where aggregates are not broken up pre-dispersal or are formed rapidly in a concentrated diffusing plume, mineral candidates often lose their shortwave scattering advantages over sulfate. Aggregate formation, depending on size and dimension, shrinks the gap in risk between sulfate and mineral candidates, potentially making sulfate a less risky choice in comparison. Constraining our physical understanding of solid aerosol aggregation and dispersion is critical to fully assessing on which end of the risk spectrum “realistic” solid injection scenarios lie. In the absence of a more advanced understanding of stratospheric dispersion and coagulation dynamics, a solid injection strategy is suboptimal compared to sulfate purely on the basis of relatively high risk-risk magnitudes (e.g. significantly reduced shortwave fractal scattering efficiency and lifetimes) with poorly constrained risk likelihoods. In the case of perfect injection and dispersion (e.g. monomer dispersal), solids do have the capability to lower sulfate-associated risk. However, a less-optimal solid injection and dispersion strategy, in which aggregation occurs, extends the risk space significantly beyond the lower bound of most sulfate scenarios.

Discussion

Practical limitations of stratospheric aerosol injections, if not addressed, set considerable bounds on the realistic risk space for deployment and injection strategies. Control over the geographic distribution, size and settling trajectory of the aerosol is achieved largely through control over key deployment parameters. As such, the degree of governance will ultimately dictate the degree to which deployment parameters such as latitude, altitude, longitude, season and magnitude are optimized. As a result, governance and deployment coordination (or lack thereof) will dictate the evenness of resulting sAODs, lifetimes, and requisite burdens of the aerosol. All of these parameters are critical to costs and the magnitude of risks associated with injection.

Material choice is another key determinant in the climate impacts of an SAI strategy. Though solid aerosols have been proposed to reduce risks associated with sulfate, this analysis shows that design of such an SAI strategy may be limited by both economic and technical factors that are, to some degree, intertwined. Supply constraints may limit the practicality of several candidates, most notably diamond. Other currently supply-constrained but more demand-elastic materials (e.g., , ) could potentially respond favorably to increased demands. Abundance and relatively low cost of materials like calcite, alumina and sulfate favors their adoption over other candidates, though high-magnitude or prolonged SAI strategies may also run up against demand inelasticity.

Engineering concerns may add further constraints. Solid aerosols may be difficult to disperse en masse in optimal monomer form, leading to either increased costs associated with injection, or to the injection of aggregates with sub-optimal optical properties. Such properties are drastic enough, in the case of large aggregates with low fractal dimension, to significantly increase required burdens and lifetimes for solid aerosol candidates compared to sulfate. For the benefits of solids in comparison to sulfate to be realized necessitates monomer dispersal (though it should be noted that monomer dispersal does not eliminate additional risks of post-injection aggregation, which remain relatively unconstrained). Based on the analysis presented here, the requirements of monomer dispersal alone will significantly increase deployment costs, largely through decreases in aerosol payload due to the added mass of dispersion systems and/or carrier gases. The increase in costs – potentially up to an order of magnitude, based on the potential payload decreases estimated in the solid aerosol dispersal section – that might or should be accepted for a given decrease in risk remains a key uncertainty in any SAI strategies. We here show that logistic constraints favor sulfate on the basis of fewer uncertainties and a more well-defined risk space that is relatively invariant with price.

These practical limitations, if left unaddressed, push SAI scenarios further away from the idealized scenarios explored in the literature. A more complete understanding of “worst-case” tropospheric climate impacts through GCM model runs that simulate aggregate injection might better contextualize these results and allow for a more complete risk-risk picture. Critical here, as well, is a better understanding of how solid aerosol microphysics will lead to aggregation post-dispersal, which may further lower the upper bound on feasible solid injection rates, increasing costs. Quantifying the (relative) risk-cost trade-off of solid monomer dispersal – that is, the increase in cost for reduced payloads – will better inform the likelihood of the acceptance of increased costs in exchange for potentially lowered environmental risk. Moreover, the eventual risk of any SAI strategy ultimately will be bound by how it is governed and deployed. The development of technical and governance-based approaches to mitigate risks associated with deployment strategy, candidate selection, and aggregate injection is critical to the design or discussion of any realistic “low-risk” SAI strategy.

Methods

Deployment strategies and governance

Macroscopic aspects of injection strategy, including latitude, longitude, altitude, season and magnitude, affect stratospheric aerosol lifetimes and stratospheric aerosol optical density (sAOD) profiles. These profiles determine patterns of surface cooling and longwave heating, which dictate key climate impacts. Here, we explore existing SAI modeling literature to compile our current understanding of the impact of such factors on the risks and climate impacts of an SAI strategy.

Deployment strategies are grouped here by degree of global coordination. This metric would likely dictate the level of control over key parameters of injection strategy. Injection latitude is often regarded as the most significant parameter in dictating sAOD and resulting surface climate, so strategies that fall within these categories are grouped by the injection latitude they would likely entail.

First, a centralized scenario, in which injections are organized and coordinated by some sort of international, centralized governing body is considered. This scenario is possible only in the presence of synchronized global governance. Existing literature that models large-scale, global injection scenarios is grouped by latitude of injection, separated into tropical/equatorial3–6,17–19,21,22,24,] 26,30–33,50–163, polar21,24,28,55–57,] 128,134–137,147,150,152,] 164,165, and multi-latitude6,11,16,17,21,] 22,25,28–30,32,]61,] 86–89,104,107,] 109,113,119,120,122,127,] 128,135–137,145,] 147–150,152,157,163,165–222 strategies.

In the case of sporadic or absent governance, a decentralized scenario may arise, involving smaller actors (or groups of actors) who are only able to inject only in specific regions according to practical limitations (e.g. sporadic governance, financial, material). Such a scenario hasn’t been modeled explicitly in literature, but the most likely scenario results in a hemispherically asymmetric sAOD, with injections concentrated in the NH simply due to populations being concentrated there. Such asymmetric scenarios do exist in literature and are studied here (18,24,30,51,53,] 55,85,109,115,123,124,] 132–134,137,159,164,165,223).

Solid aerosol dispersal

Reviewing relevant literature for dispersing submicron solid aerosols demonstrates that this technology may pose a significant challenge. We illustrate challenges associated with agglomerate breakup using a two-phase fluid simulation for a choked convergent-barrel nozzle, one proposed means of solid dispersal. We model the acceleration of a gas such a nozzle, which imparts a drag force (drag coefficient modeled with Henderson’s semi-empirical model, which accounts for all regimes of Knudsen, Reynolds and Mach numbers encountered here) on a spherical agglomerate228.

Nozzle inlet geometry and length is constant across varied inlet gas conditions and solid mass flow rates, but throat geometry varies and is chosen by selecting the minimum throat area that satisfies the momentum balance throughout the entire nozzle. For higher solid mass flow rates, this results in larger throat areas, and flow profiles in which the Mach number of the gas approaches 1 at the end of the nozzle throat. In a single-phase system, gas velocity remains constant in the throat. In the throat of a two-phase system (unless the two phases are in thermal and mechanical equilibrium), momentum exchange between the two phases must take place. This means that the gas velocity in the throat is not constant but increasing. As a result, the choking condition is satisfied (i.e. the maximum gas velocity profile is reached) when the gas Mach number approaches 1 at the end of the throat. Under this condition, decreasing back pressure further is unable to induce an increase in gas velocity upstream of this Mach 1 point at the end of the throat, because these pressure waves cannot propagate faster than the speed of sound (the gas velocity at this end-of-throat point) to impact upstream flow patterns. It is this nozzle configuration used in drag and Weber number calculations.

The fluid is considered inviscid apart from interactions with the solid and forces aside from drag are disregarded. Motion in the radial direction is disregarded and the mass, momentum and energy balances of the solid and gas are coupled. The resulting mass matrix with variable coefficients is solved with MATLAB’s ode15s. Governing equations for this approach are are outlined in Kliegel (1963). A modified Weber number for a solid-gas system compares the magnitude of drag forces exerted on the agglomerate to cohesive intermolecular forces (in this case, van der Waals, with Hamaker’s constants estimated using Lifshitz theory)229,230.

Aggregate forcing

The impact of fractal aggregate formation on solid forcing efficiency has only been modeled as a result of coagulation during the lifetime of optimally dispersed monomers11. Given that dispersing optimally-sized solid monomers may be a challenge, here we simulate shortwave forcing efficiencies of fractal aggregates of each candidate, varying fractal dimension and number of optimally-sized monomers per aggregate. This is done using PythonicDISORT with a greenhouse gas atmosphere transparent in the shortwave (incident beam is AM0)231 and monodisperse aerosols constrained between the tropopause (17 km) and 22 km232. 16 streams are used, and weighted averaging is done over 9 zenith angles representative of a year-averaged latitude. Shortwave refractive index information is taken from Dykema (2016)10. Monomer optics are found with miepython and aggregate optics with an f90 translation of Botet’s mean field T-matrix method233–235, with structural prefactors determined based on the relation described in Gmachowski (2002)236.
