Genetic analyses of 104 phenotypes in 20,900 Chinese pregnant women reveal pregnancy-specific discoveries
Authors: Han Xiao, Linxuan Li, Meng Yang, Xinyi Zhang, Jieqiong Zhou, Jingyu Zeng, Yan Zhou, Xianmei Lan, Jiuying Liu, Ying Lin, Yuanyuan Zhong, Xiaoqian Zhang, Lin Wang, Zhongqiang Cao, Panhong Liu, Hong Mei, Mingzhi Cai, Xiaonan Cai, Ye Tao, Yunqing Zhu, Canqing Yu, Liqin Hu, Yu Wang, Yushan Huang, Fengxia Su, Ya Gao, Rui Zhou, Xun Xu, Huanming Yang, Jian Wang, Huanhuan Zhu, Aifen Zhou, Xin Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryMonitoring biochemical phenotypes during pregnancy is vital for maternal and fetal health, allowing early detection and management of pregnancy-related conditions to ensure safety for both. Here, we conducted a genetic analysis of 104 pregnancy phenotypes in 20,900 Chinese women. The genome-wide association study (GWAS) identified a total of 410 trait-locus associations, with 71.71% reported previously. Among the 116 novel hits for 45 phenotypes, 83 were successfully replicated. Among them, 31 were defined as potentially pregnancy-specific associations, including creatine and HELLPAR and neutrophils and ESR1, with subsequent analysis revealing enrichments in estrogen-related pathways and female reproductive tissues. The partitioning heritability underscored the significant roles of fetal blood, embryoid bodies, and female reproductive organs in pregnancy hematology and birth outcomes. Pathway analysis confirmed the intricate interplay of hormone and immune regulation, metabolism, and cell cycle during pregnancy. This study contributes to the understanding of genetic influences on pregnancy phenotypes and their implications for maternal health.Keywords: pregnancy phenotypes, genome-wide association study, partitioning heritability, pathway-based analysis, pregnancy-specific genetic associations

Introduction

Clinical laboratory measurements play a crucial role in helping detect and diagnose complex diseases.1 Unraveling the genetic architecture underlying these measurements is essential to help comprehend disease etiology. Over the years, genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have been conducted to investigate the genetic background of diverse phenotypes, including hematological,2,3 liver-related,4,5,6 kidney-related,7,8 metabolic,9,10 protein,1,11 and urine.12,13 Furthermore, the existence of biobanks and consortiums, such as the UK Biobank,14 BioBank Japan Project (BBJ),1 FinnGen biobank,15 and the DIAbetes Genetics Replication And Meta-analysis (DIAGRAM) consortium,16 has facilitated large-scale genetic analyses of clinical laboratory measurements.

Pregnancy is a distinctive phase in a woman’s life, and blood and urinary indicators play a vital role in evaluating both maternal and fetal health.17,18,19 Elevated maternal glucose levels signify an increased risk of gestational diabetes,20 while abnormal maternal hemoglobin levels can serve as a warning sign for preterm birth.21,22 However, there is a scarcity of studies delving into the genetic landscape of these laboratory features during pregnancy. In particular, traits observed in pregnant women, fetuses, and neonates have been understudied, especially within the Asian population.

We retrieved associations published to date from the GWAS Catalog23 and identified pregnancy-related traits using keywords such as “pregnancy,” “gestation,” “fetal,” “birth weight,” “miscarriage,” “stillbirth,” “preterm birth,” “placenta,” “preeclampsia,” and “prenatal.” While these keywords may not encompass all pregnancy-related traits, they should cover the majority of traits of interest. Our search revealed 56 diseases and traits in 36 studies (including 4 in purely Asian populations) and a total of 213 genome-wide significant associations. However, given the overall count of 25,225 traits and diseases, comprising 416,687 associations, it highlights a significant limitation in the representation of pregnancy-related traits.

Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) has grown in use recently, providing pregnant women with a sensitive and non-invasive screening option for detecting chromosomal disorders in fetuses.24 This advanced technology enables the identification of three major chromosomal disorders in the fetus, concurrently generating high-throughput genotype data.25 Previously, our team successfully demonstrated the viability of utilizing NIPT sequencing data for diverse genetic studies, including variant calling, population history, viral infection patterns, and GWASs.26 Moreover, we substantiated that the highly accurate imputation performance for low-sequencing-depth NIPT data ensures that GWAS analysis maintains robust statistical power in identifying genetic variants associated with traits.27

Here, we delved into the genetic background of 104 pregnancy phenotypes using genotype data from NIPT tests of 20,900 pregnant women. Our GWAS analysis uncovered 410 genome-wide significant associations for 66 distinct phenotypes. Notably, the majority (71.71%) of these trait-locus associations had been reported in either European or Asian populations. Beyond confirming known findings, we identified 116 novel hits for 45 phenotypes, with 83 successfully replicated in independent studies. Among them, we defined 31 potentially pregnancy-specific signals, including creatine and hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelet count (HELLP)-associated long non-coding RNA (HELLPAR) and neutrophils and ESR1. Subsequent analysis revealed significant enrichments in estrogen-related pathways and female reproductive tissues.

Partitioning heritability highlighted the essential roles of fetal blood, embryoid bodies, and female reproductive organs in pregnancy hematology and birth outcomes. Pathway analysis confirmed the complex interplay of hormone regulation, immune response, metabolism, and cell cycle processes during pregnancy. Finally, we developed an online PheWeb platform (https://db.cngb.org/MANE.PheWeb/) to share GWAS summary statistics. Our work provides a valuable theoretical foundation and serves as a reference for studying the genetic mechanisms underlying pregnancy-related phenotypes. Additionally, it contributes to a better understanding of the genetic basis of maternal disorders and postnatal outcomes.

Results

Imputation performance of NIPT sequence data

Initially, our study included 39,194 pregnant women who underwent NIPT testing. Then, samples with a sequencing depth less than 0.05× and mapping rates below 90% were excluded, resulting in 38,668 samples for analysis. Notably, starting in December 2018, the upgraded NIPT product led to an increase in sequencing depth. After removing 1,719 samples with unknown sampling dates, we analyzed 10,795 pregnant women with an average sequencing depth of 0.1049× before December 2018 and 26,154 participants with an average sequencing depth of 0.1644× thereafter, resulting in an overall average of 0.1525× (Figure S1A). The sequencing read length was consistent at 35 bp.

First, we assessed the performance of genome-wide imputation, which yielded a total of 8,134,302 SNPs (Figure S1B). Focusing on SNPs conforming to the p value for testing whether the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is true > 1e−6 and minor-allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05, the mean imputation accuracy (R) was 87.61%, and the coefficient of determination (R2) was 79.34% (Figure S1C) for the imputation of the 30 samples from the 1000 Genomes Project (STAR Methods). After imputing 14 samples with both NIPT sequence data and high-depth sequenced cell-free DNA data (45.39×), we identified 1,668,360 SNPs shared between the imputed and true datasets for comparison. Among these, 93.4% (1,557,992 SNPs) were common variants with an MAF greater than 0.05. The average imputation R2 was 84.95% (Figure S1D). We acknowledge that the high proportion of common variants is mainly due to the limited size (14 samples) of the high-depth sequence data, leading to the exclusion of low-frequency variants from the imputation accuracy calculation.

Additionally, we considered specific SNP frequencies for variants tested for maternal folate metabolism. Of 272 samples that underwent this genetic test, the average correlation R2 between imputed and true genotypes for three tested variants in genes MTHFR and MTRR was 0.528, with a maximum value of 0.809 (Figure S1E). These three specific SNPs cannot represent millions of genome-wide variants; however, this evaluation supplements assessments of the performance of genome-wide imputation. Additional details regarding the imputation performance of ultra-low-pass whole-genome sequencing data across various tools and reference panels are presented in a companion work (this issue of Cell Genetics).27 Following high-quality genotype imputation, the low-pass NIPT sequence data become suitable for genetics and genomics studies.

GWAS analysis of 104 pregnancy traits

We conducted GWAS analysis on 104 traits, each with a sample size exceeding 2,000 individuals. These traits spanned a diverse array of measurements, categorized into 11 distinct groups in pregnant women (Figure S2; Table 1): maternal (n = 5), postnatal (n = 6), electrolyte (n = 4), hematological (n = 24), hormone (n = 4), infection (n = 14), kidney-related (n = 4), liver-related (n = 8), metabolism (n = 6), protein (n = 5), and urinalysis (n = 24). The correlation heatmap displayed how phenotypes correlate pairwise (Figure S3). As anticipated, stronger correlations tend to occur within phenotype categories like hematological and urinary indicators, while correlations between categories are relatively weak.

Employing a genome-wide significance threshold of 5E−08, we identified 410 trait-locus associations across 66 traits (Tables 1 and S1). Among these, 294 loci (71.71%) have been reported previously in the GWAS catalog,23 while 116 were newly discovered. When considering the study-wide significance threshold of 4.81E−10 (calculated as 5E−08 divided by 104 for Bonferroni correction), we identified 275 trait-locus associations across 56 traits. Notably, 201 (73.09%) were known associations, and 74 were novel findings. The criteria for defining known or novel findings are outlined in the STAR Methods section.

We observed that several phenotypes were either unrecorded or lacked associations in the GWAS catalog, and our GWAS results effectively addressed these gaps. As an illustration, we provided GWAS summary results for platelet large cell ratio (P-LCR), representing the percentage of large platelets in the blood—a relatively new and underappreciated metric with limited existing studies. Studies have associated P-LCR with preeclampsia and eclampsia.28,29 Similarly, we explored the genetic background of prealbumin, also known as transthyretin, a hepatocyte-synthesized carrier protein binding to thyroxine and retinol-binding proteins. Low levels of prealbumin in pregnant women may result from an inadequate diet and malnutrition.30 Investigating the genetic underpinnings of these features and identifying associated variants are crucial for distinguishing high-risk pregnancies and constructing predictive models for related complications. Notable associations from our study include P-LCR with rs6688345 (p value = 1.70E−29), mapped on PEAR1, a type of platelet receptor, and prealbumin with rs3764479 (p value = 1.35E−16), located on TTR, which encodes the transthyretin protein—one of the three prealbumins.

The number of identified loci and calculated heritability for each phenotype are presented in Figure 1C, with detailed information available in Table 1. Notably, 33 phenotypes exhibited more than 5 significant loci, with 11 phenotypes surpassing 10 signals each. Platelet size deviation width, in particular, demonstrated the highest number of associations at 27. The heritability analysis revealed that 51 phenotypes exhibited a heritability of 10% or more, and among them, 36 displayed a standard error of less than 5%. Remarkably, within the subset of phenotypes with a standard error less than 5%, P-LCR boasted the highest heritability at 32.0%.

In summary, the hematology category emerged with the highest number of identified hits and heritability, closely followed by liver-related and kidney-related categories. These findings provide a comprehensive overview of the genetic architecture underlying various pregnancy-related phenotypes and their heritability patterns.

Pleiotropy analysis of associated genetic variants

Understanding pleiotropy, where a genetic locus influences multiple phenotypes simultaneously, enhances our understanding of shared genetic architecture across diverse phenotypes. To identify significant loci with pleiotropic effects, we evaluated the pleiotropy of 288 unique loci derived from the 410 trait-locus associations across 66 traits. Our analysis identified 99 pleiotropic loci associated with 49 phenotypes (Table S2). Notably, the most prevalent pleiotropy was observed at the PSMD3 locus (chromosome 17: 39,994,239–40,013,455), which influenced nine traits, all within the hematology category. The most significant association was between SNP rs3826331 and neutrophil counts (p value < 1E−300), followed by white blood cell counts and percentage of neutrophils. Other notable pleiotropic loci included HBS1L, associated with nine traits within the hematology category, such as mean corpuscular hemoglobin and mean corpuscular volume; ABO, linked to six traits across three categories; and RASA2, affecting four traits in three categories, such as percentage of lymphocytes and percentage of neutrophils. Our pleiotropy analysis revealed a shared genetic etiology between hematological and liver-related traits. Phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) Manhattan plots were employed to visually depict the pleiotropy of genetic variants (Figure 2).

Replication studies for novel associations

In total, we successfully replicated 83 of 116 (71.55%) genome-wide novel hits in independent replicates (78 by the Shenzhen study and 5 by the China Kadoorie Biobank cohort). Detailed information on the replication of trait-locus associations, specifying the study responsible for each replication, is presented in Table S1. Among the remaining 33 associations, 9 were not supported due to the absence of corresponding phenotypes in the replication studies, and 24 (20.69%) were not successfully replicated (Figure 3A).

For each phenotype, we delineated the number of trait-locus associations based on whether the traits were identified previously, novel and replicated, and novel but not replicated (Figure 3B). Some unreproduced associations hold promise for further investigation. Noteworthy examples included free triiodothyronine and rs657152 (p value = 2.69E−31), mapped to the ABO gene, as well as urine glucose and rs585961 (p value = 3.35E−19), mapped to the FBXL20 gene.

For replications from genetic correlation with the BBJ, our findings indicated that, among the 31 shared phenotypes, 26 exhibited genetic correlations exceeding 0.5, with the highest reaching 0.9768. Employing a significance threshold of 1.6E−03 (=0.05/31), 25 pairs demonstrated significant genetic correlations, underscoring the concordance between our results and those of the BBJ study (Figure 3C). Notably, the average Pearson’s correlation was 0.7549, reaching a maximum of 0.9573.

Potentially pregnancy-specific associations

Following our criteria for “potentially pregnancy-specific associations,” which have not been reported in previous GWASs of the general population but were replicated by the Shenzhen pregnant population, we identified 31 such associations linked to 26 unique genes (Figure 4A). The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (STAR Methods) revealed 13 significant pathways, with the top five being endocrine resistance (hsa01522), prostate cancer (hsa05215), genes targeted by microRNAs (miRNAs) in adipocytes (WP1992), estrogen signaling pathway (WP712), and miRNA regulation of prostate cancer signaling pathways (WP3981) (Figure 4B). Notably, these potentially pregnancy-specific associations underscored genes enriched in pathways related to endocrine functions and prostate cancer, suggesting a close relationship between pregnancy and prostate cancer, both of which are intricately linked to hormone regulation. Specifically, functional genes identified in the endocrine resistance pathway include ESR1, BCL2, and IGF1, while those in the prostate cancer pathway include BCL2, IGF1, and ERG. Furthermore, the protein-protein interaction analysis corroborated experimentally determined interactions between BCL2 and ESR1, implying a functional relationship between these two proteins.

For the gene expression analysis (STAR Methods), we observed that tissues related to pregnancy exhibited high average transcripts per million (TPM) values (Figure 4C). Among the top five tissues, three were related to pregnancy: cervix endocervix, uterus, and cervix ectocervix. Additionally, other pregnancy-related tissues, such as fallopian tube, breast mammary tissue, ovary, and vagina, also ranked highly. Within each tissue group, we computed the average of TPM values, with adipose, blood/immune, and pregnancy emerging as the top three groups. However, it is important to note that the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database contains data from non-pregnant donors. We anticipate that incorporating RNA sequencing data from pregnant women or placental samples will likely strengthen this observation.

For the pregnancy-related genes obtained from the GWAS catalog (STAR Methods), we conducted GSEA and gene expression analyses (Figure 5). The GSEA identified enrichment in 12 pathways, including factors involved in megakaryocyte development and platelet production (R-HSA-983231), blood coagulation (GO:0007596), the Rho cell motility signaling pathway, and estrogen-dependent gene expression (R-HSA-9018519), spanning categories such as cell growth, metabolism, and hormone regulation. This result confirms the complexity of pregnancy, with multiple pathways involved and interacting, as emphasized previously. Gene expression analysis revealed patterns similar to those observed with potentially pregnancy-specific genes. Both analyses indicated higher TPM values in the pregnancy and endocrine categories, while the digestive and central nervous system categories ranked lower. The top five tissues showing high expression were the testis, nerve tibial, ovary, cervix endocervix, and thyroid, with two belonging to the pregnancy category.

Among the potentially pregnancy-specific associations, we specifically highlighted two associations: those related to ESR1 and HELLPAR. The ESR1 (estrogen receptor 1) was identified to be associated with fasting glucose, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and various types of leukocytosis. The protein encoded by ESR1 regulates the transcription of numerous genes influencing gestation, metabolism, sexual development, growth, and other reproductive functions.31 Prior research has highlighted the critical role of estrogen receptor signaling in maintaining a successful pregnancy.32,33,34 Another association was observed between blood creatinine and SNP rs2946834, mapped on HELLPAR. HELLP syndrome is a severe complication of high blood pressure during pregnancy, and the correlation between creatinine levels and HELLP syndrome has gained increased attention in recent years.35,36,37 Our findings offer a genetic perspective to elucidate their relationship, warranting further exploration.

Pathway enrichment analysis

We observed that the most crucial associated pathways within the same category often share similarities (Figure S5; Table S3). For instance, pathways associated with counts and percentage of leukocytes involve immune regulation and signal transduction, like the Biocarta EGFR SMRTE pathway, where “EGFR” stands for “epidermal growth factor receptor” and “SMRTE” refers to “silencing mediator for retinoid and thyroid hormone receptors extended.” Glucose levels correlate with pathways primarily affecting pancreatic islet B cell development, such as Reactome regulation of β cell development (R-HSA-186712). Certain well-established functional pathways were also identified for specific phenotypes; for instance, the Reactome bile acid and bile salt metabolism pathway (R-HSA-194068) was notably linked to bile acids. Reactome hemostasis (R-HSA-109582) emerged as the primary pathway associated with platelet counts.

Pregnancy involves a complex interplay of biological pathways essential for maternal well-being and fetal development, including hormonal and immune regulation, cell proliferation and differentiation, metabolism, and more. Our pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the pivotal roles of these pathways throughout pregnancy (Figure S5; Table S3). For instance, pathways linked to birthweight encompass Reactome signaling pathways like NODAL (nodal growth differentiation factor) (R-HSA-1181150), associated with embryonic, stem cell, and placental development,38 and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) signaling pathway (hsa04912) for hormonal regulation. Pathways linked to twin pregnancy play critical roles in ensuring genomic integrity and cell cycle regulation during pregnancy. They monitor DNA damage, facilitate transitions between cell cycle phases (G1, S, G2, and M), and maintain cellular homeostasis, which is vital for proper embryonic and fetal development. Exemplar pathways include Reactome cell cycle checkpoints (R-HSA-69620) and Reactome M/G1 transition (R-HSA-68874).39

Partitioning heritability analysis

The heritability partition results not only predominantly aligned with established biological knowledge but also underscored the significance of cell types/tissues within the “pregnancy” group (Figures 6, S6, and S7; Table S4). For instance, in addition to the blood/immune tissue group, hematological phenotypes (e.g., eosinophils, percentage of eosinophils, percentage of lymphocytes, and percentage of neutrophils) exhibited strong associations with fetal blood and the cervix. Similarly, for specific phenotypes such as blood urea nitrogen and thyroid-stimulating hormone, in addition to their established relevant tissues like the urinary bladder and thyroid, pregnancy-related tissues, including the endometrium, uterus, ovary, and fetal blood, were prominently highlighted (Figure 6).

For pregnancy-related phenotypes, it was unsurprising that their relevant cell types/tissues were categorized within the pregnancy group (Figure 6; Table S4). Notably, twin pregnancy showed embryoid body cell type enrichment, while birth type exhibited fallopian tube cell type enrichment. Furthermore, birth weight and birth height displayed enrichments in ovary and embryonic stem cell types. Previous research has indicated a connection between birth weight and maternal ovary size.40,41 These findings underscore the importance of specific tissues and cell types in influencing pregnancy-related traits.

Post-GWAS analysis for phenotypes in the BBJ cohort

Beyond validating our discoveries via genetic correlation with the BBJ cohort, we also investigated the presence of pregnancy-related findings observed in our study within the BBJ cohort. Specifically, we conducted pathway-based and partitioning heritability analyses for shared phenotypes (Figure S8; Tables S5 and S6). The pathway-based analysis revealed consistent yet distinct functional pathways between our study and the BBJ cohort (Figure S8A; Table S5). For instance, eosinophil count showed identical top pathways in both cohorts, primarily in immune regulation groups such as the Biocarta NKT (natural killer T cell) pathway, Biocarta Th1/Th2 pathway, and KEGG cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (hsa04060). Differently, for creatinine level, a kidney function indicator, our study highlighted pathways related to prostate cancer (hsa05215) and aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption (hsa04960), while the BBJ cohort emphasized pathways in cancer (hsa05200) and endocytosis (hsa04144). Additionally, heritability partitioning revealed distinctions (Figure S8B; Table S6); for instance, blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels exhibited enrichment in pregnancy-related categories in our GWAS but not in the BBJ cohort. Our results showed that, there was a consistency in GWAS findings between our study and the BBJ cohort while also revealing pregnancy-related distinctions absent in the BBJ cohort.

Mendelian randomization analysis

The Mendelian randomization results in a heatmap of varying significance levels (Figure S9A). We identified four potential causal effects: EOS% (eosinophil percentage) with prostate cancer (beta = 0.19, p value = 3.00E−07), urine pH level with urolithiasis (beta = 0.80, p value = 3.25E−07), RBC (red blood cell) count with asthma (beta = 0.13, p value = 1.02E−05), and MCH (mean corpuscular hemoglobin) with Graves’ disease (beta = −0.13, p value = 1.98E−05) (Figure S9B; Table S7). The sensitivity analyses, which included tests for horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity, further enhanced the reliability and accuracy of our results, with p greater than 0.05 (Table S7). These findings aligned with phenotype-disease associations reported in clinical studies, such as the significantly lower levels of MCH observed in Graves’ disease patients42 and the increased risk of calcium stone formation associated with a urine pH above 6.0.43 The Mendelian randomization (MR) results provided valuable insights for guiding future mechanistic investigations.

We acknowledge that the causal relationship between EOS% and prostate cancer is somewhat unexpected, given that the exposure phenotype was only studied in pregnant women. In relation to EOS%, all GWAS signals were already known. Therefore, the observed causal relationship between EOS% and prostate cancer likely indicates that genetic variants associated with EOS% may influence prostate cancer by modulating EOS% levels in the general population. Additionally, prostate cancer, although predominantly affecting males, is fundamentally an endocrine disorder. Pregnancy, characterized by hormonal changes and endocrine regulation, may thus exhibit an ostensibly unrelated yet interconnected relationship with prostate cancer.

Discussion

Pregnancy constitutes a distinctive phase in a woman’s life that ideally is medically surveyed with regular examinations that may yield a substantial volume of clinical data. Despite this, genetic studies concentrating on pregnancy phenotypes, particularly in the Asian population, remain limited. With the widespread adoption of NIPT technology in recent years, the acquisition of high-throughput genotype data has become feasible. In this investigation, leveraging the genotype information from 20,900 pregnant women who underwent NIPT, we conducted a genetic analysis encompassing 104 pregnancy-related phenotypes. Impressively, we achieved successful replication of 71.71% of the trait-locus associations. Moreover, we uncovered 116 novel loci associated with 45 phenotypes, surpassing the genome-wide significance threshold. Notably, 83 of these loci were validated through independent replication studies. The pathway-based and partitioning heritability analyses not only affirmed existing biological knowledge but also unveiled several genetically pregnancy-specific insights. Our MR analysis identified four potential causal relationships between pregnancy phenotypes and complex diseases.

With the continuous expansion of NIPT sequencing data, we anticipate an increase in genetic investigations concentrating on maternal- and neonatal-related traits and diseases. The genome-wide association outcomes derived from these studies hold significant potential as a valuable reference, illuminating the landscape of maternal and child healthcare. To support and streamline such research endeavors, the systematic cataloging and summarization of these associations would be immensely advantageous. Numerous websites have been developed to serve as repositories for GWAS summary datasets, encompassing diverse phenotypes, and are readily accessible for researchers to explore and download. Notable platforms include the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/home), the MRC (Medical Research Council) Integrative Epidemiology Unit (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), the PheWeb archive (https://pheweb.sph.umich.edu/), and the BioBank Japan PheWeb (https://pheweb.jp/). In addition, we have introduced a user-friendly online website (https://db.cngb.org/MANE.PheWeb/) that offers a searchable, visualizable, and openly accessible database housing pregnancy-related SNP-trait associations. This resource is poised to play a pivotal role in unraveling the genetic underpinnings of maternal/postnatal-related phenotypes, offering valuable tools for scientists, clinicians, and users worldwide.

Limitations of the study

While our study serves as a crucial reference and data repository for delving into pregnancy phenotypes and complications, it is imperative to acknowledge that, although the genotype dataset encompassed nearly 40,000 samples, and pregnancy traits were sourced from over 30,000 women, the effective sample size for GWAS analysis, after reconciling genotype and phenotype datasets, was approximately 20,000. This effective sample size was further reduced by 20% when considering the genotype imputation accuracy R2. We recognize the need to amass larger sample sizes for more robust genetic investigations in the future. Furthermore, despite employing high-quality genotype imputation, our quality control process filtered only approximately 2 million SNPs for further analysis. This limitation primarily arises from the inherently low depth of NIPT sequencing, resulting in a multitude of undetected variants. This constraint may contribute to the observed reductions in heritability for maternal height (30.0%) and BMI (26.8%), which were 26.0% and 0.2% lower than results reported previously, respectively.44,45 It is important to note that the commercial nature of NIPT, driven by the necessity for high volume and low cost, makes increasing sequencing depth impractical. In response to this limitation, we opted for a solution that involves augmenting sample sizes.

Our GWAS analyses incorporated only maternal age and the first five principal components of genotype as covariates. Ideally, adjustments for additional variables, such as the time of NIPT sequencing to control for genotyping batch effects and gestational days for prenatal tests, should have been considered. However, both NIPT and prenatal tests may have been conducted multiple times. For NIPT sequencing data, all available fastq sequence files were aggregated, and for prenatal tests, the average was calculated for quantitative tests with multiple records. Consequently, there was no specific time point at which the tested value was measured. In an attempt to assess the impact of adjusting gestational days, we selected 93 phenotypes with multiple measurements, extracted one specific record with precise gestational age, and re-ran GWAS analyses by introducing gestational age as an additional covariate. The majority of phenotypes (79.57%) exhibited a correlation higher than 0.9 in both effect sizes (beta) and -log10(p value) before and after adjusting for gestational age (Figure S10).

The fourth limitation of our study is the two-sample MR analysis, which aimed to uncover potential causal effects of pregnancy phenotypes on diseases. However, it is important to note that the disease samples used in our analysis are from non-pregnant populations (the BBJ cohort), and the majority of our GWAS findings are known in the general population. This discrepancy suggests that the MR findings may not be pregnancy specific. Furthermore, while we defined potentially pregnancy-specific associations based on novel hits replicated by an external NIPT study, distinguishing between pregnancy-driven and non-pregnancy-driven GWAS signals remains challenging. As a result, we are unable to exclusively identify causal relationships applicable to pregnant populations. In the future, we intend to focus on potentially pregnancy-specific hits and conduct comprehensive investigations in both pregnant and non-pregnant populations. Additionally, we plan to explore their potential role as instrumental variables to uncover pregnancy-exclusive causal effects. Another limitation of MR is that, even if the horizontal pleiotropy test is not significant, the causal results may still be influenced by horizontal pleiotropy. This can occur due to several factors, including unmeasured or unknown pleiotropic pathways or complex pleiotropic structures.46

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants

The participants in this study were recruited from Wuhan Children’s Hospital, located in Wuhan city, China, during their prenatal tests conducted between 2017 and 2020. We obtained signed consent forms from each participant, and the study received approval from the Institutional Review Boards at Wuhan Children’s Hospital (2021R062) and BGI Research (BGI-IRB 21088). Additionally, it was approved by the Human Genetic Resources Administration of China ([2021] CJ2002). All individuals who chose to undergo non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) were included in the study, and their sequencing data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic information system. Notably, we employed NIPT sequencing data as the exclusive inclusion criterion to maximize the sample size for enhanced genotype imputation performance.

Genotype

Genotype data were generated during NIPT screening, typically starting from the 12th week of pregnancy. Specifically, 5mL of peripheral venous blood was collected and preserved at −80°C in EDTA-anticoagulated tubes. Plasma samples from pregnant women underwent genotyping through next-generation high-throughput sequencing, yielding ultra-low sequencing depth genotype data for each subject. In the initial years of NIPT implementation for chromosomal disorder screening, the sequencing depth ranged from 0.06X to 0.1X, and single-end reads were 35bp in length. In recent years, advancements in NIPT technology have provided higher sequencing coverage, allowing the sequencing depth to exceed 0.1X.

Phenotype

A wide spectrum of traits was systematically documented throughout the pregnancy and labor stages, encompassing maternal information, prenatal test measurements, and neonatal information. Maternal details, comprising age, height, weight, and blood pressure, were sourced from the clinical questionnaire administered before the NIPT screening. Neonatal information, including gestational week, delivery method, birth weight, and birth length, was recorded once, resulting in a singular entry for each variable. Prenatal tests encompassed a range of blood and urinary laboratory features, such as hematology tests, liver function, etc. These variables were measured at multiple intervals throughout the entire pregnancy period and were retrieved from the electronic information system in the hospital.

Method details

Genotyping and imputation

Initially, we excluded samples with a sequencing depth below 0.05X. Subsequently, for the retained samples, we employed FASTP to eliminate low-quality read regions and potential adaptor sequences from their fastq sequencing files.49 Following this, the reads from the samples were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using the BWA algorithm.50 Samples with a mapping rate lower than 90% were excluded from further analysis. Finally, we utilized the BaseVar algorithm for SNP calling51 and employed STITCH to impute any missing genotypes.52 It’s noteworthy that for each variant STITCH produced genotype dosage, a real number within the range of 0–2 instead of a genotype value (0, 1, 2). Two metrics were used to assess imputation accuracy: info-score and imputation R2. The info-score, a measure of imputation quality, is calculated as the ratio between the imputed and the most likely observed genotype information.53 Meanwhile, the R2 is determined as Pearson’s correlation between the imputed dosage and the true genotype. It’s essential to acknowledge that the effective sample size for GWAS is the product of the true sample size and R2.

We evaluated the imputation accuracy, R2, at both the genome-wide level and the specific SNP level. To assess the genome-wide R2, we obtained genotype files for 30 randomly selected individuals of Han Chinese descent from the 1000 Genomes Project (1KGP)54 and down-sampled their coverage to 0.1X. Simultaneously, we imputed the down-sampled data and the NIPT sequencing data using STITCH. The imputation R2 was then computed by comparing the after-imputed genotypes with the true 30X 1KGP genotypes. For each SNP, a single R2 value was calculated across samples, and within an allele frequency interval, the average R2 across all SNPs was determined. Additionally, recognizing that cfDNA fragments exhibit preferred genome coordinates, randomly down-sampling in-cell DNA might not precisely replicate the profiles of cfDNA. Consequently, we leveraged 14 samples with both authentic NIPT sequencing data and high-depth cfDNA data (serving as true genotypes) from a prior study.55 Combining these 14 NIPT samples with the original NIPT sample, we performed simultaneous STITCH imputation and then computed the R2 between the imputed and true genotypes of the 14 samples.

Furthermore, we evaluated the imputation performance at the SNP level using genetic variants associated with maternal folate metabolism, including three variants rs1801131, rs1801133, and rs1801394.56 The imputation R2 for each of these variants was then calculated by comparing the clinically reported results with the after-imputed genotype dosage. This assessment was conducted for 272 samples that underwent the genetic test for the three variants. We note that the certain SNP level imputation performance is merely a supplement to that of the genome-wide level.

Phenotype processing

We conducted a thorough data cleaning process to uphold the quality of phenotype data. Specifically, we replaced various symbols (e.g., hyphens, dots) with "NA," ensured consistent units for each variable, eliminated unit signals from values, and assigned values with mathematical symbols (e.g., >, <) to the theoretical upper or lower limits. Following data processing, all measurements were represented as either explicit numeric values or dichotomous statuses. In instances where quantitative variables were measured more than once, we calculated the average of all available records for each participant. For binary measurements, such as urine glucose, we considered the variable as positive once a positive result was observed. Comprehensive details regarding the characteristics of each trait were presented in Table 1.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Genome-wide association analysis

For each quantitative trait, we conducted a GWAS analysis employing a linear regression model, while a logistic model was fitted for binary traits using PLINK 2.0 with the command line option --glm.57 To ensure a standard normal distribution for quantitative phenotypes, we normalized them using the --pheno-quantile-normalize command. The imputed genotype, measured on a dosage scale ranging from 0 to 2, was utilized in the analysis for biallelic variants with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) p-value > 1e−6, and genotype missing rate <0.1. Covariates adjusted in the model included women’s age and the first five principal components (PCs). These covariates were scaled to have a mean of zero and a variance of one using the --covar-variance-standardize command. We established a genome-wide significance threshold of 5E−08 and a study-wide significance threshold of 4.81E−10 (=5E−8/104) through Bonferroni adjustment.

For a given locus, if all SNPs within it were situated less than 500 kb away from the reported trait-associated SNPs, and there was at least one SNP in LD (R2 > 0.1) with the reported SNP in the GWAS catalog23 as of September 2022, we categorized this locus as "known"; otherwise, it was considered "novel". We utilized a criterion of 0.1 to define known loci, referencing the Biobank Japan paper.1 The genomic inflation factors (λgc) were computed in R.58 Utilizing the GWAS summary statistics for each trait, we employed Linkage Disequilibrium score regression (LDSC)59 to estimate its heritability.

Pleiotropy analysis

We characterized pleiotropic effects for associated variants of different phenotypes based on two criteria: (i) they were in LD with an R2 value greater than or equal to 0.4, or (ii) they were physically closely related with a distance within 25 kb. The calculation of R2 between two variants was performed using LDlink60 and the East Asian (EAS) population in the 1000 Genomes Project on GRCh38.61

Replication study of novel GWAS associations

We conducted replication analyses using two distinct external datasets to validate our novel findings. The first dataset, referred to as Shenzhen study, comprised pregnant women who underwent NIPT and prenatal tests at Shenzhen Baoan Women’s and Children’s Hospital in Shenzhen, China. This cohort included 30,699 Chinese pregnant women aged 14–53 (average age: 29.6) recruited between 2017 and 2019.62 A total of 27 phenotypes with novel signals identified by our study were observed in the Shenzhen cohort (Figure S2). The second dataset was obtained from the China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB), the largest cohort comprising over 512,000 adults aged 30–79 (average age: 51, female: 49%, without pregnant individuals) recruited across China between 2004 and 2008.63 Seven phenotypes with novel signals identified in our study, but not replicated by the Shenzhen cohort, were observed in the CKB cohort (Figure S2). We defined reproducibility based on a threshold of 1E−4.

In addition to the independent replication studies, we conducted genetic correlation analyses for the 31 phenotypes shared between our study and the BBJ project (Figure S2). These phenotypes spanned seven categories, including protein, metabolism, hematological, among others. We acquired GWAS summary statistics from the website (http://jenger.riken.jp/result). The average age of participants in the BBJ GWAS study was 62.9 years, and the proportion of females was 45.5%. Alongside our own GWAS summary, we conducted genetic correlation analyses to assess the genetic similarity of the shared phenotypes.

Analysis on potentially pregnancy-specific loci

Here, we define a type of novel findings, which we name as “potentially pregnancy-specific associations”, if the phenotypes have been documented in GWAS catalog and the novel associations have been replicated by the independent Shenzhen NIPT study. In other words, these associations have not been reported in previous GWAS studies involving the general population and are potentially unique to the pregnant population. We combined the corresponding loci as a list of genes and performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis, and gene expression analysis.

The GSEA analysis was executed in GeneTrail (https://genetrail.bioinf.uni-sb.de/) by using over-representation analysis (ORA). The biological categories included GO (Gene Ontology),64 BIOCARTA,65 KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes),66 WikiPathway,67 and REACTOME.68 For PPI analysis, we used STRING to visualize the interaction network.69 The interactions encompassed various types, including known (e.g., experimentally determined), predicted (e.g., gene neighborhood), and others (e.g., text-mining). For gene expression analysis, we retrieved median gene-level TPM (transcripts per kilobase million) data from the GTEx database,70 extracted TPM values for the potentially pregnancy-specific genes, standardized TPM values per gene, and calculated the average across tissues.

In the Introduction section, we conducted a comprehensive search of keywords in the GWAS catalog, such as "pregnancy," "gestation," and "fetal," to assess the current research status of pregnancy-related traits. For the related diseases and traits, we compiled a list of genes to which the corresponding significant SNPs mapped. We defined these genes as "pregnancy-related genes" and conducted the GSEA and gene expression analyses, as detailed previously. To ensure the relevance of pregnancy and the mapped genes, we selected genes associated with pregnancy-related traits more than three times, resulting in a total of 89 genes (Table S8). We included this section to compare the analytic results of our potentially pregnancy-specific genes with those of the known pregnancy-related genes.

Pathway enrichment analysis

We utilized Pascal to compute gene and pathway scores based on summary data from GWAS.71 Specifically, Pascal employed the East Asian population as the reference population to calculate LD information. The gene sets for each pathway were defined using three external databases: BIOCARTA,65 KEGG,66 and REACTOME,68 comprising a total of 1,077 gene sets. For this experiment, a window size of 50kb was set, and the significance of the pathway was assessed using the empirical score. We applied a significance threshold of 1E−4.

Partitioning heritability analysis

We employed stratified LD score regression to estimate the polygenic contributions of functional categories to heritability in each trait.72 Utilizing 205 cell-type-specific annotations with gene expression data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project73 and Franke lab dataset,74,75 we categorized the 205 tissues and cell types into ten groups, including pregnancy, adipose, blood/immune, cardiovascular, central nervous system (CNS), digestive, endocrine, liver, musculoskeletal/connective, and others. The pregnancy group encompasses cell types such as A15.145.300.Fetal.Blood and A11.497.600.Oocytes, as well as tissues like the ovary and uterus. This classification, originally proposed by Finucane et al.,76 has been refined to include the pregnancy category. The multi-tissue microarray gene expression file, “Multi_tissue_gene_expr.EAS,” incorporates both the GTEx dataset and the Franke lab dataset with East Asian populations. The baseline model LD score was derived from the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 East Asian populations (1000G_Phase3_EAS).

Mendelian randomization analysis

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a method that utilizes measured variation in genetic variants (instrumental variables, IV) of known function to investigate the causal effect of a modifiable exposure (exposure variable) on disease (outcome variable) in observational studies. In this study, we employed pregnancy phenotypes as exposure variables, their corresponding significant SNPs as instrumental variables, and diseases in the BioBank Japan Project as outcome variables to conduct a two-sample MR analysis for identifying potential causal effects. Specifically, we utilized 51 pregnancy phenotypes with more than one genome-wide significant SNP in our study and obtained the summary statistics of 45 diseases from the BBJ project (http://jenger.riken.jp/result). The two-sample MR analysis was performed using the R function TwoSampleMR,77 with a significance level set at 2.18E−05 (=0.05/2295) based on the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method. To verify the robustness and validity of our causal results, we conducted additional tests for horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity. In particular, we employed the mr_pleiotropy_test and mr_heterogeneity functions to perform these assessments.

PheWeb construction

To offer a comprehensive online resource for searching, visualizing, and downloading GWAS summary statistics related to the 104 phenotypes, we crafted a PheWeb website named MANE (MAternal and NEonatal) PheWeb. This platform was developed following the PheWeb building pipeline available on the GitHub repository.78 Within the MANE PheWeb interface, users have the flexibility to search for specific traits, genetic variants, or genes. We furnished detailed summary statistics, along with a Manhattan plot and a QQ plot, for each analyzed trait.
