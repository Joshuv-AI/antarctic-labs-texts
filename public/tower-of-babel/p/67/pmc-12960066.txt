In vivo mapping of cortical amyloid beta‐myelin coupling in preclinical Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Mercedes Atienza, Jose L Cantero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAccumulating evidence suggests that amyloid‐β (Aβ) preferentially deposits in poorly myelinated cortical regions and may interfere with remyelination. We hypothesized that this vulnerability would manifest as altered Aβ–myelin coupling in preclinical Alzheimer's disease (AD).METHODSWe assessed spatial coupling between amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) and myelin content estimated from T1w/T2w ratio maps in 151 cognitively unimpaired older adults. Associations with preclinical AD, blood‐based biomarkers, resting‐state functional connectivity (rs‐FC), and subjective memory complaints (SMC) were examined.RESULTSCompared with controls, Aβ+ individuals exhibited increased positive and negative Aβ–myelin coupling in late‐ and early‐myelinating regions, respectively, likely reflecting compensatory remyelination versus progressive myelin loss, along with disrupted rs‐FC and greater SMC. Serum glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and plasma phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (pTau‐181) showed differential, region‐specific associations with Aβ–myelin coupling, with GFAP partially mediating tau‐related effects. Exploratory stratification suggested distinct biomarker profiles linked to the direction of coupling dominance, potentially reflecting different preclinical AD phases.DISCUSSIONAβ–myelin coupling may represent a sensitive, stage‐dependent marker of early AD–related cortical pathology.Keywords: amyloid beta, cortical myelin, blood biomarkers, preclinical Alzheimer's disease, subjective memory complaints

BACKGROUND

The early deposition of extracellular amyloid‐β (Aβ) plaques in the brain is a defining feature of Alzheimer's disease (AD) that is frequently detected in cognitively unimpaired older adults. 1 , 2 , 3 During the preclinical AD stage, Aβ deposition is largely confined to the neocortex, where it exhibits a diffuse spatial distribution. As AD progresses, Aβ pathology gradually extends to allocortical and subcortical regions, 4 , 5 thereby contributing to disease onset and progression through the amplification of neuroinflammatory responses 6 , 7 and the facilitation of tau propagation, ultimately leading to cortical neurodegeneration. 8 , 9

Pioneering studies have demonstrated that early Aβ plaques preferentially accumulate in cortical regions with low myelin density, 10 , 11 suggesting that reduced myelin in cortical gray matter (GM) may represent a feature of AD. 12 This hypothesis is supported by studies in animal models and humans. In APP/PS1 mice, a model of chronic Aβ plaque deposition, demyelination occurs in plaque cores within cortical GM, with the degree of myelin disruption correlating with plaque burden. 13 In 3xTg‐AD mice, a triple‐transgenic AD model, myelin abnormalities precede Aβ pathology. 14 These patterns are supported by human post mortem analyses across familial, sporadic, and preclinical AD cases, with plaque‐free neocortical regions showing neither significant demyelination nor oligodendrocyte loss, underscoring the regional specificity of myelin alterations in early AD. 15

Given the intrinsic capacity of the adult central nervous system (CNS) to repair myelin through remyelination, 16 local increases in cortical myelin associated with global Aβ burden has been interpreted as indicative of a regenerative process. 17 However, this endogenous repair mechanism becomes compromised as Aβ pathology accumulates due to impaired astrocyte‐microglia interactions 18 and reduced phagocytosis of myelin debris. 19 , 20 Additionally, Aβ plaques can directly alter remyelination, leading to increased oligodendrogenesis, thicker myelin, and shortened nodes of Ranvier, 21 , 22 with distinct functional consequences depending on the affected brain compartment. Remyelination of white matter (WM) lesions often results in near‐complete functional recovery, whereas remyelinated axons within cortical GM frequently lead to persistent disruptions. 23 These findings indicate that regional vulnerability of cortical circuits to early Aβ deposition may be shaped by local myelin deficits, with remyelination capacity modulating both plaque accumulation and functional impairment.

Building on evidence of myelin deficits in regions vulnerable to Aβ accumulation, 13 , 15 , 24 we investigated whether abnormalities in local Aβ–myelin relationships can be detected in vivo in the preclinical stage of AD. Cortical Aβ load was assessed using amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), while intracortical myelin content was estimated from the T1‐weighted/T2‐weigthed (T1w/T2w) signal ratio. We then quantified the local spatial coupling between these measures 25 to capture heterogeneous regional associations between Aβ burden and myelin content, without assuming a single temporal or causal sequence. We hypothesized that local Aβ–myelin coupling would be more pronounced in Aβ‐positive (Aβ+) individuals, operationally defining preclinical AD, 26 compared with Aβ‐negative (Aβ−) controls.

Beyond microstructural alterations, Aβ pathology and cortical myelin abnormalities may compromise functional network integrity before cognitive deficits become clinically evident. 17 , 27 , 28 We hypothesized that cortical regions exhibiting group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling also show local alterations in resting‐state functional connectivity (rs‐FC), linking microstructural pathology to network‐level functional consequences in preclinical AD.

Given the central role of astrocytes in myelin maintenance 18 and evidence of dysregulated astrocyte reactivity in early Aβ pathology, we hypothesized that higher serum levels of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a marker of astrocyte reactivity and a predictor of AD risk, 29 would be associated with stronger cortical Aβ–myelin coupling, reflecting alterations in the glial environment.

In addition to astrocytic dysfunction, other AD‐related pathological processes may compromise cortical myelin integrity, resulting in alterations to Aβ–myelin coupling. Indeed, pathological tau disrupts oligodendrocyte microtubule dynamics, impairing myelin maintenance and maturation. 30 , 31 Consequently, phosphorylated tau has been linked to oligodendrocyte and myelin dysfunction, 32 providing biological plausibility for tau‐modulating myelin integrity in the context of Aβ pathology. Plasma phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 (pTau‐181) correlates with both amyloid and tau PET, 33 , 34 , 35 and has been associated with imaging measures of myelin in asymptomatic adults, 36 offering a rationale to hypothesize that elevated plasma pTau‐181 levels modulate local Aβ–myelin interactions in preclinical AD.

Finally, recognizing that enhanced Aβ–myelin coupling may reflect an early AD–related brain environment, we investigated whether regional patterns of Aβ–myelin coupling were associated with self‐perceived cognitive deficits, a condition that approximately doubles the risk of dementia. 37 We hypothesized that greater Aβ–myelin coupling would be associated with greater subjective memory complaints in preclinical AD individuals.

METHODS

Participants

The sample consisted of 151 cognitively normal older adults. To rule out the presence of mild cognitive impairment or dementia, each participant underwent a complete neurological evaluation and a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment. Individuals with conditions such as stroke, epilepsy, head trauma, neurodevelopmental disorders, intellectual disability, alcohol abuse, hydrocephalus, or intracranial tumors, were excluded from participation. Inclusion criteria were: (i) normal performance on cognitive tests, based on age‐ and education‐adjusted reference values; (ii) score ≥ 26 in the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE); (iii) Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0, suggesting no evidence of dementia; (iv) autonomy in daily life; and (v) score ≤ 5 on the short form of the Geriatric Depression Scale, indicating no symptoms of depression. Self‐perceived cognitive deficits were assessed using the Memory Functioning Questionnaire (MFQ), 38 with higher scores indicating worse perceived memory functioning.

All participants provided their informed consent before taking part in the study. The protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee for Clinical Research of the Junta de Andalucía, following the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Blood biomarkers of AD

Blood samples were collected following an overnight fast, immediately processed, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Serum GFAP and plasma pTau‐181 were measured in duplicate using single‐molecule array (SIMOA) assays (Quanterix, MA, USA) on an SR‐X analyzer, according to the manufacturer's instructions. The average of the two measurements (pg/ml) was used for statistical analysis. To ensure measurement accuracy and consistency, samples exhibiting coefficients of variation greater than 20% were reanalyzed.

Magnetic resonance imaging acquisition and preprocessing

Brain images were acquired on a Philips Ingenia 3.0T CX magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner equipped with a 32‐channel receive‐only radio‐frequency (RF) head coil (Philips, Best, Netherlands) at the MRI facility of Pablo de Olavide University. The following MRI sequences were acquired in the same session: (i) 3D T1‐weighted (T1w) magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence in the sagittal plane with no space between adjacent slices: repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) = 2600 ms/4.7 ms, flip angle (FA) = 9°, acquisition matrix = 384 × 384, and an isotropic voxel size of 0.65 mm; ii) 3D T2w VISTA Turbo Spin Echo scan in the sagittal plane with no space between adjacent slices: TR/TE = 2500 ms/251 ms, FA = 90°, acquisition matrix = 384 × 384 mm, and an isotropic voxel size of 0.65 mm; and iii) T2w Fast Field Echo images using a blood‐oxygen‐level‐dependent (BOLD) sensitive single‐shot echo‐planar imaging (EPI) sequence in the axial plane, acquired in posterior to anterior phase‐encoding direction with 1 mm of gap between adjacent slices: TR/TE = 2000 ms/30 ms, FA = 80°, acquisition matrix = 80 × 80 mm, and an isotropic voxel size of 3 mm. Pulse and respiratory rates were simultaneously recorded using the scanner's built‐in pulse oximeter placed on the left‐hand index finger and a pneumatic respiratory belt strapped around the upper abdomen, respectively. We acquired 250 EPI scans preceded by four dummy volumes to allow magnetization to reach steady state. To ensure optimal B1 homogeneity, a B1 calibration scan was acquired immediately prior to the EPI acquisition. Before starting the EPI sequence, participants were instructed to remain still with their eyes closed, while avoiding falling asleep. MRI scans were visually examined after each acquisition; they were repeated if artifacts or excessive head movements were identified.

T1w scans were preprocessed using Freesurfer v6.0 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). The Freesurfer's pipeline included brain extraction, automated tissue segmentation, generation of WM and pial surfaces, topology correction, inflation, co‐registration, and projection of cortical surfaces to a sphere for the purpose of establishing a surface‐based coordinate system. 39 Pial surface misplacements and/or erroneous WM segmentation were manually corrected on a slice‐by‐slice basis by one experienced technician.

The resting state‐fMRI (rs‐fMRI) data were preprocessed using functions from AFNI (https://afni.nimh.nih.gov/afni), version AFNI_20.3.01. For each participant, high‐frequency spikes were eliminated (3dDespike), time‐locked cardiac and respiratory motion artifacts on brain BOLD signals were minimized using RETROICOR, 40 time differences in slice‐acquisition were corrected (3dTshift), EPI scans were aligned using the first volume as reference and rigid body motion correction (3dVolreg), and aligned EPI scans were co‐registered to their corresponding T1w volumes (align_epi_anat.py; cost function: lpc+ZZ). Volumes were excluded if more than 5% of voxels showed signal intensities deviating from the median absolute deviation of the time series (3dToutcount) or if head motion, quantified by the Euclidean norm (enorm), exceeded 0.3 mm. None of the participants showed more than 20% of artifactual volumes after applying censoring. Simultaneous regression was further applied to minimize the impact of nonneuronal fluctuations on the rs‐fMRI signal (3dTproject). Nuisance regressors included: (i) six head motion parameters (three translational and three rotational) along with their first‐order derivatives, (ii) time series of mean total WM/cerebrospinal fluid signal intensity, and (iii) cardiac and respiratory fluctuations plus their derivatives to mitigate effects of extracerebral physiological artifacts on brain BOLD signals.

Estimation of T1w/T2w ratio

T2w images were registered to T1w images using bbregister with trilinear interpolation and a boundary‐based cost function constrained to 6 degrees of freedom. 41 Individual T1w/T2w ratio volumes were sampled at 50% of the depth from the WM surface, yielding mid‐thickness surface maps of the T1w/T2w ratio. To minimize the contamination of cortical GM intensity by WM and cerebrospinal fluid intensities, the tissue fraction effect was corrected in individual T1w/T2w ratio maps using the region‐based voxel‐wise method derived from the geometric transfer matrix. 42 Finally, individual T1w/T2w ratio maps were projected onto the average cortical surface of the full sample, and smoothed using nonlinear spherical wavelet‐based denoising schemes. 43 All processing steps were visually checked for quality assurance.

Estimation of functional connectivity maps

Preprocessed rs‐fMRI scans were projected onto the fsaverage5 template. Seeds for rs‐FC analyses were obtained from cortical regions showing significant group differences in coupling between Aβ+ and Aβ− individuals, separately for negative and positive Aβ–myelin coupling. Surface‐based rs‐FC maps were generated for each seed across the entire cortex using the Fisher z‐transform of the corresponding Pearson correlation coefficients.

Amyloid PET acquisition and preprocessing

Participants received an intravenous injection of 300 MBq of 18F‐florbetaben (FBB; NeuraCeq®, Curium Pharma) 90 min before imaging on a Philips Gemini 16 PET/CT scanner (Philips, Best, The Netherlands). A dynamic 20‐min FBB‐PET scan was acquired, consisting of four 5‐min frames. Each frame was inspected for motion artifacts. As no significant head motion was detected, the four frames were averaged to create a single static FBB brain image for subsequent quantitative analysis. Amyloid PET images were corrected for radioactive decay, dead time, attenuation, and scatter before being reconstructed using an iterative algorithm with an isotropic voxel size of 2 mm.

Partial volume correction (PVC) of the FBB‐PET images was performed using the PetSurfer tool (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/fswiki/PetSurfer), employing a region‐based voxel‐wise method derived from the geometric transfer matrix. 42 Initial alignment of the cerebral FBB‐PET images to T1w scans was followed by the transformation of PVC‐corrected cortical FBB‐PET images into standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) values, using the mean PVC uptake in cerebellar GM as the reference region. The resulting cortical‐to‐cerebellum SUVR FBB‐PET images were then mapped onto individual cortical surfaces and smoothed using nonlinear spherical wavelet‐based denoising techniques. 43 To establish the global Aβ load in each participant, we computed an FBB‐PET composite across four large bilateral cortical regions. 44

Estimating local cortical Aβ–myelin coupling maps

Individual FBB‐PET and T1w/T2w ratio cortical maps were aligned to the fsaverage5 template using spherical registration. Cortical T1w/T2w ratio maps were primarily sampled at a cortical mid‐thickness depth, corresponding to 50% of the distance from the WM surface, which has been shown to provide a robust estimate of intracortical myelin while minimizing partial volume effects.

To estimate local cortical coupling between amyloid PET and T1w/T2w ratio maps in each subject, we applied a surface‐based, locally weighted regression approach. 25 This method has previously been used to characterize local cortical coupling between cerebral blood flow and the amplitude of low‐frequency fluctuations across development. 45

Briefly, local cortical coupling is a subject‐specific metric estimated on the cortical surface in template space. At each vertex, a regression slope is computed that reflects the local relationship between cortical Aβ burden and intracortical myelin content for each participant. As no temporal dimension is available for this regression, local variability is instead provided by neighboring vertices. For each vertex, a circular neighborhood was defined using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 15 mm, with greater weight assigned to vertices closer to the center and progressively less weight to more distant vertices. 25 In addition to the spatial bandwidth of the kernel, the regression framework included a parameter controlling the effective number of neighboring vertices contributing to the local fit, which was set to 10 vertices. This approach generated a single Aβ–myelin coupling map per subject, capturing the local spatial correspondence between the two imaging modalities and enabling subsequent group‐level statistical analyses.

To assess the robustness of local Aβ–myelin coupling estimates, we performed several complementary sensitivity analyses. First, in addition to the primary cortical mid‐thickness depth (50% of the distance from the WM surface), Aβ–myelin coupling maps were computed using T1w/T2w ratio maps sampled at more superficial (90%) and deeper (10%) cortical depths, in order to assess whether group differences between Aβ+ and Aβ− participants were consistent across cortical layers.

Second, sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the coupling estimates to key analytical parameters. Specifically, all analyses were repeated using a reduced surface‐based smoothing kernel of 10 mm. In parallel, the effective contribution of neighboring vertices in the locally weighted regression was reduced to 8 vertices. Together, these adjustments increased sensitivity to coupling within smaller cortical regions. 25

Estimation of local cortical coupling yielded distinct positive and negative Aβ–myelin coupling maps, reflecting different spatial relationships between Aβ burden and myelin content. Positive coupling maps highlighted cortical regions in which higher Aβ load was associated with greater myelin content, potentially indicative of local remyelination. In contrast, negative coupling maps identified areas where increased Aβ burden colocalized with reduced myelin content, consistent with demyelination or other deleterious interactions.

Statistical analysis

Sample stratification and characterization

Participants were stratified using two established FBB‐PET SUVR cutoffs for cerebral Aβ positivity: a conservative cutoff of 1.43 46 and a less conservative cutoff of 1.24. 47 Additionally, participants were stratified by plasma pTau‐181 status (pTau‐181+ vs. pTau‐181−) using a cutoff of 16.5 pg/ml. 34 While plasma‐based biomarker cutoffs are less standardized and may vary across assays and cohorts, this classification was considered useful for capturing biologically meaningful tau‐related differences within the study sample.

For the conservative FBB‐PET cutoff, 33 Aβ+ individuals (21.8%) were compared with 33 age‐, sex‐, and apolipoprotein E4 (APOE4) ‐matched Aβ− controls. The same 33 Aβ+ individuals were further compared with the full Aβ− group (n = 118) to examine potential effects of sample size. For the less conservative FBB‐PET cutoff, 40 Aβ+ individuals (26.5%) were compared with 40 age‐ and sex‐matched Aβ− controls. Plasma pTau‐181 subgroups were not matched due to an approximately equal distribution of pTau‐181+ (n = 75) and pTau‐181− (n = 76) participants.

Group differences in demographic measures, subjective cognitive scores, and blood‐based AD biomarkers were assessed using two‐sample t‐tests for continuous variables and χ 2 tests for categorical variables.

Cortical surface‐based analyses of Aβ–myelin coupling

Cortical surface‐based analyses were conducted using the SurfStat tool (https://www.math.mcgill.ca/keith/surfstat/). We first applied one‐sample‐t‐tests to positive and negative Aβ–myelin coupling maps separately to reduce spurious coupling between cortical regions. Multiple comparisons on cortical surface‐based analyses were controlled for by applying a hierarchical statistical model, 48 which first adjusted for family‐wise error rate within significant clusters over smoothed statistical maps using random field theory, followed by false discovery rate control within vertices of significant clusters on unsmoothed maps. Significant regions were identified using peak vertices on the Desikan‐Killiany brain atlas. 49

Vertex‐wise analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) were performed to assess group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling. Analyses included comparisons between Aβ+ and Aβ− participants using both FBB‐PET cutoffs, as well as pTau‐181+ vs. pTau‐181− participants. All analyses were adjusted for age, sex, APOE4 status, serum GFAP, and plasma pTau‐181. To assess robustness and ensure that non‐significant findings were not driven by correlations between blood‐based AD biomarkers and Aβ–myelin coupling, analyses were also performed using a reduced set of covariates (i.e., age, sex, and APOE4 status).

Aβ–myelin coupling analyses were all performed at a standard cortical mid‐thickness depth (50% of the distance from the WM surface). To investigate whether group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling varied as a function of cortical depth, we compared Aβ+ and Aβ− participants at 90% and 10% depth from the WM surface. These analyses were carried out using the primary Aβ–myelin coupling parameter configuration (i.e., FWHM = 15 mm; neighboring vertices = 10).

As a sensitivity check, group differences between Aβ+ and Aβ− participants were also evaluated using alternative configurations of the coupling parameters described in Section 2.7. These analyses were restricted to the primary cortical mid‐thickness depth.

For all significant predictors in these models, local standardized effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's f 2 and classified as small (S, 0.02 ≤ f 2 ≤ 0.15), moderate (M, 0.15 < f 2 ≤ 0.35), or large (L, f 2 > 0.35). To assess estimation precision, 95% bias‐corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap confidence intervals were also computed.

Regional analyses of myelin and amyloid maps

To further disentangle whether group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling between Aβ+ and Aβ− participants were primarily driven by local myelin alterations or by regional Aβ accumulation, we conducted vertex‐wise ANCOVAs on the T1w/T2w maps and on the FBB‐PET maps separately. These analyses were adjusted for the same full set of covariates (i.e., age, sex, APOE4 status, serum GFAP, and plasma pTau‐181) to control for potential confounding effects.

rs‐FC analyses

We further examined whether cortical regions showing group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling also exhibited altered rs‐FC patterns. For each region that exhibited significant group differences in the Aβ–myelin coupling analyses, the mean BOLD signal was used as seed to generate individual vertex‐wise rs‐FC maps. Group differences in these seed‐based connectivity maps were subsequently assessed using vertex‐wise ANCOVAs, both with and without serum GFAP and plasma pTau‐181 as covariates. Local standardized effect sizes were calculated as described above.

Blood‐based AD biomarkers analyses

Additional analyses were conducted to evaluate potential effects of serum GFAP and plasma pTau‐181 on local Aβ–myelin coupling, by including each marker separately as a covariate. Simple associations between each biomarker and Aβ–myelin coupling were first assessed. Then, in a subgroup of 66 participants (33 Aβ+ vs. 33 Aβ−), we tested the interaction between global Aβ load and pTau‐181 treated as a continuous variable. Finally, in the full sample (N = 151), we examined the interaction between categorized plasma pTau‐181 and continuous global Aβ load to assess whether associations with Aβ–myelin coupling depended on overall Aβ burden.

Associations with subjective cognitive measures

We first examined simple associations between subjective memory complaints, assessed with the MFQ, 38 and local Aβ–myelin coupling across the full sample (N = 151), adjusting for the same set of covariates. To evaluate whether these relationships varied with global Aβ load, interactions with categorized global Aβ status were analyzed in the corresponding subgroups, applying the conservative FBB‐PET cutoff.

Mediation analyses

When the inclusion of GFAP or pTau‐181 in the statistical model abolished a previously observed association between the variable of interest and Aβ–myelin coupling, we performed post hoc mediation analyses to assess whether the GFAP or pTau‐181 might act as a mediator.

For each analysis, a linear regression model was first run with the blood AD biomarker as the independent variable, the vertex‐wise Aβ–myelin coupling map (positive or negative) as the dependent variable, and the variable of interest, along with age, sex, and APOE4 status, included as covariates. Cortical regions showing significant associations between the blood AD biomarker and Aβ–myelin coupling in these models were first identified. For each significant region, the coupling value at the vertex with the maximum statistic was extracted and used as the outcome in mediation models, with the variable of interest as the treatment and the blood AD biomarker as the mediator. Single paths, indirect, direct, and total effects were estimated using bootstrap resampling with 5000 iterations, and homogeneity of residuals was assessed to ensure model assumptions were met. All mediation analyses were conducted in R (version 3.6.1) using the resampleMediation package.

Largely similar group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling were observed when the control group was expanded to include all Aβ− participants (33 Aβ+ vs. 118 Aβ−; Table S2 and Figure S1), when used a less conservative FBB‐PET cutoff of 1.24 (40 Aβ+ vs. 40 Aβ−; Table S3 and Figure S2), and when global cortical Aβ load was modeled as a continuous variable (Table S4 and Figure S3). Furthermore, comparable findings were obtained when the statistical model was unadjusted for GFAP and pTau‐181 (Table S5).

The observed group differences between Aβ+ and Aβ− participants remained consistent across different cortical depths (90%, 50% and 10% of the distance from the WM surface; Table S6 and Figure S4) and when alternative coupling parameters, specifically a reduced smoothing kernel of 10 mm and 8 neighboring vertices, were applied at cortical mid‐thickness depth (Table S7 and Figure S5), confirming the robustness of the primary findings.

Contribution of cortical myelin and Aβ burden to group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling

To disentangle the respective contributions of cortical myelin content and cortical FBB binding to group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling, we examined each measure separately. Significant group differences were observed for both (Table S8 and Figure S6). As expected, individuals with preclinical AD (Aβ+) showed reduced cortical myelin content and increased Aβ burden compared with Aβ− controls. Myelin deficits were locally confined to bilateral fronto‐temporo‐parietal cortices (Figure S6A,B), whereas Aβ burden was more widespread, encompassing most of the neocortex (Figure S6C,D). Regions of reduced myelin in the left supramarginal gyrus, medial orbitofrontal cortex, rostral middle frontal cortex, and right insular cortex overlapped with areas showing stronger Aβ–myelin coupling in preclinical AD. The frontal lobe exhibited the highest Aβ burden in both hemispheres, potentially enhancing Aβ–myelin coupling in frontal regions in preclinical AD.

In contrast to the widespread associations observed for GFAP, plasma pTau‐181 showed more spatially restricted relationships with Aβ–myelin coupling (Table 2; Figure 3D,E). When modeled as a continuous variable and adjusted for GFAP, pTau‐181 was associated with reduced positive Aβ–myelin coupling in the right middle temporal cortex (corrected p = 0.04).

Categorical analyses (pTau‐181+ vs. pTau‐181−) did not reveal significant group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling when serum GFAP was included as a covariate. However, when GFAP was removed from the model, pTau‐181+ individuals showed increased negative coupling in caudal middle frontal regions and increased positive coupling in medial orbitofrontal and rostral anterior cingulate cortices compared with pTau‐181− cases (Table S11; Figure S7).

Complementary analyses treating plasma pTau‐181 as a continuous variable without GFAP adjustment revealed comparable results, including negative coupling in caudal middle frontal and postcentral regions and positive coupling in middle temporal cortex (Table S12; Figure S8).

Given the sensitivity of these associations to GFAP adjustment, we next examined whether serum GFAP levels mediated the relationship between plasma pTau‐181 and Aβ–myelin coupling. Exploratory Aβ–myelin coupling‐based stratification analyses

Based on prior results that pTau‐181+ individuals exhibited stronger positive and negative coupling across distinct cortical areas (Figure S7) partially mediated by GFAP (Figure 4), and that plasma pTau‐181 levels were negatively associated with positive Aβ–myelin coupling in right middle temporal regions independently of GFAP (Figure S8), we conducted an exploratory, post hoc stratification analysis. The aim of this analysis was to examine whether AD–related biomarker profiles differed according to the dominance and spatial characteristics of Aβ–myelin coupling.

Participants (N = 151) were first stratified using mean global positive and negative Aβ–myelin coupling values across cortical regions showing significant group‐level coupling effects. Three subgroups were defined (n = 25 each): (i) positive‐dominant coupling (Pos‐DC): high positive coupling ≥75th percentile, non‐extreme negative coupling > 25th percentile; (ii) negative‐dominant coupling (Neg‐DC): low negative coupling ≤25th percentile, non‐extreme positive coupling < 75th percentile; and (iii) non‐dominant coupling (NDC), participants without extreme values in either coupling, with the lowest overall coupling magnitude selected among them.

Results showing differences in global Aβ load and blood‐based AD biomarkers across subgroups defined by this stratification are illustrated in Figure 5. A significant group effect was observed for global Aβ load after adjustment for age, sex, and APOE4 status (F 2,78 = 29.64, p < 0.0001), with higher Aβ load in the Neg‐DC group compared with both Pos‐DC and NDC (Figure 5A, upper panel). Serum GFAP also differed across groups (F 2,78 = 11.34, p < 0.0001), with higher levels in Neg‐DC relative to Pos‐DC and NDC (Figure 5A, lower panel). No significant group differences were observed for plasma pTau‐181 (F 2,78 = 1.94, p = 0.15) (Figure 5A, middle panel).

Relationship between local Aβ–myelin coupling and subjective cognitive status

We finally examined the relationship between subjective cognitive status and local Aβ–myelin coupling (Figure 6). More pronounced self‐reported memory complaints were associated with stronger positive Aβ–myelin coupling in the left pars triangularis (F 8,144 = 10.7, corrected p = 0.02, R2 = 0.07, flocal2 = 0.07, CI95%[0.01 0.18]; Figure 6A,B). To determine whether this association differed according to global Aβ status (using the FBB‐PET conservative cutoff of 1.43 for categorization), we tested global Aβ status × coupling interaction models. These analyses revealed a significant interaction effect (corrected p = 0.03), indicating that the relationship between subjective memory complaints and Aβ–myelin coupling varied as a function of global Aβ status. Specifically, greater subjective memory complaints in Aβ+ individuals were more strongly associated with increased negative coupling in left superior frontal regions (Figure 6C,D).

DISCUSSION

Understanding how Aβ pathology relates to cortical myelin remains a critical challenge in presymptomatic AD. In this study, we identified region‐specific alterations in Aβ–myelin coupling in individuals with preclinical AD compared with controls, suggesting that Aβ accumulation and cortical myelin changes co‐occur across distinct cortical territories prior to symptom onset. These effects were independent of APOE4 status, despite its well‐established role on both myelin regulation 50 , 51 and Aβ accumulation. 52 , 53 Notably, the findings were robust across FBB‐PET cutoffs, group definitions, coupling parameters, and cortical depths, and they were additionally associated with blood‐based AD biomarkers and SMC, highlighting their biological relevance in presymptomatic AD stages.

Importantly, while group differences in Aβ–myelin coupling were more pronounced in regions with higher regional Aβ burden than in regions showing myelin alterations, this does not imply that Aβ alone determines the observed coupling. Rather, the coupling metric provides insight into where Aβ preferentially deposits and how myelin may respond, for instance through compensatory remyelination or progressive loss. When considered alone, maps of Aβ or myelin differences offer limited information regarding disease staging in preclinical AD. Examining the spatial pattern of Aβ–myelin coupling, however, reveals additional, stage‐relevant information. Specifically, positive coupling is most prominent in late‐myelinating cortical regions, 54 which also show lower myelin content 55 and early Aβ deposition in longitudinal PET studies. 56 Conversely, negative coupling predominates in earlier‐myelinating regions associated with intermediate stages of Aβ accumulation. 56

The predominance of positive coupling may indicate preserved compensatory or remyelination‐related mechanisms in the context of regional Aβ vulnerability, whereas negative coupling likely reflects a stage in which elevated Aβ burden, together with associated astrocytic activation—as indicated by increased GFAP—hinders remyelination, ultimately leading to progressive myelin loss. Our exploratory stratification analyses, based on dominant positive versus negative Aβ–myelin coupling, support this interpretation. Dominant negative coupling was associated with higher global Aβ and GFAP levels, whereas dominant positive coupling corresponded to lower AD‐related biomarker levels, consistent with preserved myelin integrity or earlier‐stage myelin alterations. Notably, elevated plasma pTau‐181 was associated with a reduction in positive coupling restricted to the right middle temporal cortex, potentially reflecting early tau‐related compromise of remyelination mechanisms in this region.

It is important to note that, although myelinated axons are predominantly concentrated in WM, substantial myelin is also present in cortical GM, 57 where it shows regional variation, 58 predominantly unsheathes inhibitory neurons, 59 and is dynamically regulated by experience. 60 , 61 In both compartments, myelin is produced by oligodendrocytes derived from oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs). 62 Converging evidence indicates that Aβ pathology disrupts OPC differentiation and maturation, thereby promoting demyelination and limiting the brain's capacity for remyelination, 63 , 64 with degraded myelin basic protein co‐localizing with Aβ1–42 in cortical Aβ plaques. 65 Based on this evidence, we postulate that depending on the disease stage, individuals may exhibit either active remyelination, reflected by positive Aβ–myelin coupling in early phases, or progressive myelin loss linked to negative coupling in more advanced preclinical stages.

Evidence from experimental models and human studies suggests that remyelination is generally more efficient in cortical GM than in WM, 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 potentially due to intrinsic differences in oligodendrocyte lineage cells and their surrounding microenvironment. 70 Neuroimaging studies have similarly reported increased myelin–related signals in cortical regions that accumulate Aβ prior to clinical symptom onset, 17 , 71 which has been interpreted as reflecting abnormal or maladaptive remyelination responses. 21 , 22 Our findings extend this view by showing that cortical regions exhibiting positive Aβ–myelin coupling also present altered functional connectivity, suggesting that these remyelination‐related changes may not be benign.

More specifically, functional connectivity analyses revealed that cortical regions showing altered Aβ–myelin coupling, whether positive or negative, also exhibited disrupted functional connectivity, predominantly with ipsilateral frontal areas in the left hemisphere. These results suggest that early Aβ–myelin perturbations preferentially impact networks supporting language, executive control, and interoceptive integration in the dominant hemisphere. The observed left‐lateralized pattern is consistent with the HAROLD model, which posits early susceptibility of the dominant‐hemisphere followed by compensatory contralateral recruitment. 72 Overall, these findings indicate that microstructural alterations associated with Aβ burden and myelin integrity can propagate to system‐level functional disruptions in cognitively unimpaired older adults, particularly within lateralized frontal networks supporting higher‐order cognitive functions.

We further demonstrated that elevated serum GFAP levels are associated with both enhanced positive and negative Aβ–myelin coupling. These associations are independent of plasma pTau‐181 levels and are not affected by global Aβ status, suggesting that astrocytic activation occurs early and independently of both tau pathology and global Aβ status in preclinical AD. Mechanistically, this bidirectional pattern of Aβ–myelin coupling may reflect the dual role of astrocytes. On the one hand, activated astrocytes contribute to early axonal injury, as indicated by associations with axonal swelling and damage, which can enhance apparent myelin signals near Aβ plaques. 73 , 74 , 75 On the other, astrocytes support oligodendrocyte‐mediated remyelination 76 through trophic and structural mechanisms. 77 However, with increasing Aβ burden, a pro‐inflammatory environment develops that impairs OPC differentiation and remyelination efficiency, 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 potentially shifting the balance toward maladaptive coupling patterns.

Notably, plasma pTau‐181 appears to reflect tau‐related microstructural vulnerability in a spatially and context‐dependent manner, suggesting that tau pathology affects myelinated axons in a region‐ and cell‐type‐specific fashion. This is consistent with experimental evidence that pTau‐181 preferentially co‐localizes with myelinated axons of parvalbumin‐positive gamma‐aminobutyric acid‐ergic (GABAergic) interneurons exhibiting disrupted myelin in a mouse model of cerebral Aβ pathology, 83 supporting that pathological tau can directly compromise axonal integrity within vulnerable local cortical circuits.

Our findings indicate that tau‐related effects on Aβ–myelin coupling emerge prior to overt Aβ positivity, consistent with longitudinal evidence showing that plasma pTau‐181 elevations precede established Aβ biomarker thresholds. 84 , 85 Higher continuous pTau‐181 levels were associated with reduced positive coupling in the right middle temporal cortex, a region situated early in the classical progression of tau pathology. 1 Remarkably, this association was absent when plasma pTau‐181 was dichotomized, supporting the view that the effect likely reflects subtle, early tau‐related processes rather than the presence of established neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). The association persisted largely independent of GFAP, although mediation analyses indicated that astrocytic activity contributed partially to the observed effect. Dichotomized plasma pTau‐181 additionally revealed Aβ–myelin coupling differences in medial and lateral frontal regions, which were also partially mediated by GFAP. Together, these findings suggest that plasma pTau‐181 captures biologically meaningful tau‐related influences on Aβ–myelin interactions, modulated in part by astrocytic processes, without providing direct evidence for cortical NFT deposition at this early stage.

Building on the notion that Aβ–myelin coupling reflects distinct stages of preclinical AD, it is plausible that this microstructural measure may also relate to incipient cognitive deficits. Subjective cognitive complaints often precedes objective deficits and may capture subtle functional changes linked to underlying pathology. 86 Consistent with this idea, greater memory complaints were associated with increased positive Aβ–myelin coupling in the left pars triangularis, a region of the inferior frontal gyrus that plays a critical role in semantic processing and lexical selection, 87 , 88 which is known to be sensitive to the earliest AD‐related changes. 89 This positive association emerged independently of global Aβ burden, suggesting that subjective memory complaints at earlier stages may arise during a phase in which local microstructural responses remain active or reactive. In contrast, in Aβ+ participants, self‐reported memory complaints were more strongly linked to increased negative coupling in the left superior frontal cortex, pointing to a later phase in which compensatory mechanisms appear diminished and intrinsically vulnerable frontal regions exhibit myelin‐related disruption. These findings suggest that the biological substrate linking subjective cognitive symptoms to underlying pathology shifts across the preclinical AD continuum, and that both the direction and regional expression of Aβ–myelin coupling may help delineate stage‐sensitive patterns of early AD expression.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the modest sample size may limit the generalizability of the findings and underscores the need for replication in larger, independent cohorts. Second, the cross‐sectional design precludes inferences regarding temporal sequencing, causality, or prognostic relevance. Third, our analyses were restricted to plasma pTau‐181; therefore, it remains unclear whether comparable or stronger associations would be observed with other tau species or phosphorylation sites. Finally, because T1w/T2w‐derived myelin measures may be influenced by cortical iron content, 90 we cannot fully exclude a contribution of regional iron accumulation to the observed effects.

In summary, our study suggests that in preclinical AD, regional patterns of Aβ–myelin coupling may reflect two coexisting microstructural and pathologically distinct phenotypes. Positive coupling occurs in late‐myelinating, low‐myelinated regions that tend to accumulate Aβ early, reflecting areas where remyelination processes may still be active. In contrast, negative coupling predominates in regions accumulating Aβ at intermediate stages, signaling cortical territories where remyelination appears compromised and myelin loss progresses. Importantly, individuals with dominant positive coupling generally exhibit lower Aβ burden and GFAP levels than those with dominant negative coupling, suggesting that the former phenotype corresponds to an early stage of disease. While these observations are cross‐sectional, these findings allow us to hypothesize a temporal progression from positive to negative Aβ–myelin coupling within the preclinical phase. Associations of plasma pTau‐181 with both coupling types were partly linked to astrocytic activation, as indexed by GFAP, indicating that tau‐related processes may interact with local microstructural vulnerability and influence remyelination dynamics. Notably, these region‐specific microstructural alterations also manifest in functionally relevant cortical networks, connecting early changes in myelin and Aβ burden to subtle alterations in functional connectivity and self‐reported memory complaints. Together, these findings highlight Aβ–myelin coupling as a sensitive, integrative marker capable of capturing heterogeneity and putative staging within preclinical AD, providing a framework to detect early, region‐specific disruptions in myelin and associated pathology.
