Liquid-Metal-Filled Porous Elastomers for Highly Stretchable and High-Output Triboelectric Nanogenerators toward Wearable Electronics and Self-Powered Sensors
Authors: Siyu Du, Xiaohu Li, Jing Wang, Zhenke Chen, Yongri Liang, Xueai Li, Yingdan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Driven by the development of next-generation wearable and skin-attachable electronics, there is an escalating need for flexible self-powered systems, particularly for triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) that possess optimal dielectric properties and robust charge storage capabilities. However, it is challenging to achieve mechanical softness, high stretchability, and stable electrical outputs under continuous deformation due to the issue of structural failure under severe strains. Herein, we present a liquid-metal-filled porous elastomer (LMPE) architecture fabricated by foaming an Ecoflex 00-30 matrix using an aqueous suspension of LM droplets. In this structure, oxide-encapsulated LM microdroplets are strictly confined within independent pores of the matrix. This unique structural design enables the LMPE to simultaneously achieve skin-like mechanical compliance, stable dielectric properties, and superior triboelectric performance. The LMPE exhibits a low Young’s modulus of ∼70–125 kPa and an exceptional fracture strain of up to ∼650%, enabling robust operation under repetitive deformation. Consequently, the LMPE-TENG delivers an open-circuit voltage of ∼169 V, a short-circuit current of ∼9.5 μA, a transferred charge of ∼63 nC, and a peak power density of 42.63 W·m–3. Notably, it maintains >95% of its initial output after 4 h of cyclic operation and 3 months of storage. System-level demonstrations, including capacitor charging, driving LED arrays, material recognition, Morse-code transmission, and human-machine interactive control, highlight its multifunctionality. This work provides a scalable soft-matter platform integrating LM with porous elastomers for high-performance wearable TENGs, self-powered sensing, and intelligent human-machine interactions.

Introduction

The rapid proliferation of wearable electronics − has driven a growing demand for devices that are lightweight, highly flexible, and comfortable for continuous physiological monitoring and as human-machine interfaces. − However, a major bottleneck for these soft systems is the power supply. Traditional rigid batteries not only compromise the overall mechanical compliance of wearable devices but also require frequent charging or replacement. Therefore, developing self-powered sensing technologies that can operate sustainably without external power sources is highly desirable. − In the development of soft sensors, gel-based materials, such as hydrogels and ionogels, have been widely utilized owing to their excellent stretchability and skin conformability. − Nevertheless, their long-term reliability is severely compromised by intrinsic vulnerabilities, such as water evaporation in hydrogels and liquid leakage or phase separation in ionogels under prolonged stress. − To overcome these material constraints, triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) have emerged as a highly promising solution to drive the next generation of self-powered wearable sensing systems. , Operating on the fundamental coupling of contact electrification and electrostatic induction, TENGs function as active self-powered sensors by directly converting ubiquitous biomechanical movements into distinct electrical signals. Specifically, physical contact between two materials with distinct electron affinities induces surface charge transfer, while their subsequent separation drives an alternating electron flow through an external circuit. Because this electrical output is intrinsically responsive to the amplitude and frequency of applied mechanical stimuli, potential applications of TENGs have been extensively studied in diverse fields, including biomechanical energy harvesting, real-time healthcare monitoring, tactile perception, and intelligent human–machine interfaces. Despite tremendous progress in structural design and device engineering, the development of practical contact materials that simultaneously possess the characteristics of high triboelectric output, intrinsic mechanical softness, and long-term durability remains a critical challenge.

For the construction of high-performance flexible TENGs, elastomer-based materials are the preferred candidates. Due to the intrinsically low dielectric constants of pristine elastomers, the output performance of their corresponding TENGs is severely restricted. , Generally, dielectric engineering is a conventional approach to address these limitations and enhance triboelectric charge generation by introducing solid dielectric fillers. Yet, solid dielectric fillers in elastomers induce stress concentration, thereby compromising the intrinsic compliance of the elastomers. To overcome this trade-off, liquid metals (LMs), such as gallium-based alloys, have been increasingly investigated. , Combining room-temperature fluidity with metallic characteristics, LMs act as highly deformable fillers that dynamically accommodate applied strains without interfacial delamination. , Furthermore, incorporating porous architectures into LM-TENGs has been explored to lower the compressive modulus and enable omnidirectional signal detection. − For instance, a recent representative study utilized solid sugar particles as sacrificial templates to construct a stretchable, porous, LM-filled composite with an interconnected network. However, under repeated deformation, such interconnected structures are highly susceptible to LM leakage, because the lack of localized spatial confinement within the open-pore networks inevitably forces the liquid filler to exude during compression. In recent years, the paradigm shift toward recyclable, repairable, renewable, and resilient “4R” flexible electronics has become crucial for alleviating the global e-waste crisis. For the effective implementation of these sustainable design principles, innovative polymer matrices and structural configurations have been proposed. For example, conductive LM-glass polymer composites have been developed, utilizing dynamic covalent networks to achieve thermomechanical reprocessing and efficient chemical recycling of the LM filler. For environmentally friendly and transient applications, researchers have developed a smart LM-based polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)-fructose gel through a stress-assisted network mechanism, endowing stretchable electronic devices with good degradability and self-healing properties. Furthermore, structural manipulation techniques, such as the fabrication of porous elastomer films with controllable LM distributions, are being utilized to create highly customizable, stretchable, and recyclable patterned conductors while preventing short circuits under severe strain. While these pioneering chemical and structural engineering strategies have greatly improved the sustainability of flexible electronics, achieving the optimal balance between extremely high stretchability (e.g., >500%), excellent shock resistance, and high volumetric electrical output in a fully enclosed foamed matrix remains an ongoing challenge.

Herein, a liquid-metal-filled porous elastomer (LMPE) architecture is reported, in which oxide-encapsulated LM microdroplets are highly dispersed and strictly confined within independent pores volumetrically larger than the droplets themselves. This unique structural design enables the LMPE to simultaneously achieve skin-like mechanical compliance, stable dielectric properties, and superior triboelectric performance. As schematically illustrated in Figure , the as-prepared LMPE exhibits high sensitivity, superior flexibility, and an elastic modulus comparable to that of human skin. Unlike conventional interconnected porous networks prepared via complex solid-template methods, the LMPE platform in this work is achieved through an innovative multiphase emulsion-driven porogenesis strategy. Through the introduction of an aqueous colloidal suspension of LM into the elastomer matrix, followed by thermal curing, the dispersed water phase evaporates to leave behind an independent (closed-cell) porous architecture. Concurrently, the original LM droplets naturally remain securely confined within these newly formed pores. The inherent fluidity of the LM dynamically accommodates applied strains, while the fully independent closed-cell structure uniformly distributes applied stress and strictly confines the LM, successfully preventing liquid leakage and structural collapse. Specifically, the LMPE delivers a high open-circuit voltage (V OC ) of ∼169 V, a short-circuit current (I SC ) of ∼9.5 μA, and a power density of 42.63 W m–3. Importantly, even after 4 h of continuous cyclic use and 3 months of storage, the LMPE maintains its output performance. Empowered by these advantageous features, the remarkable multifunctionality of the LMPE across various practical scenarios is systematically demonstrated, rendering it highly suitable for self-powered force sensing, continuous human motion monitoring, signal transmission, and advanced human-machine interaction systems.

Experimental Section

Materials

Ecoflex 00-30 was purchased from Smooth-On Inc. (Pennsylvania, USA). The LM (75 wt % Ga and 25 wt % In, melting point = 16.5 °C) was obtained from Qiaotai Metal Technology Co., Ltd. (Dongguan, China). DI water was prepared in the laboratory.

Preparation of LMPE

Initially, Ecoflex 00-30 (Part A and Part B) was thoroughly mixed at a 1:1 mass ratio at room temperature. Subsequently, predetermined amounts of LM and DI water were directly introduced into the elastomer matrix. To optimize the porous architecture, DI water was added at mass fractions of 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8% relative to the Ecoflex 00-30. Meanwhile, to systematically investigate the effect of LM content, the mass ratios of Ecoflex 00-30 to LM were set to 5:0, 5:1, 5:1.5, 5:2, 5:2.5, and 5:3.

In a typical fabrication process, a green, surfactant-free, water-assisted physical foaming method was developed using Eutectic Gallium–Indium (EGaIn, 75 wt % Ga, 25 wt % In). The EGaIn was fragmented into microdroplets with a narrow statistical size distribution centered at ∼100 μm via high-shear mechanical mixing. To ensure optimal closed-cell formation, deionized (DI) water (6 wt %) was added to the Ecoflex 00-30 prepolymer and emulsified via high-speed mechanical stirring at 3000 rpm for 3 min. During the subsequent thermal curing at 120 °C, the localized water droplets underwent controlled physical vaporization to expand the cavities before cross-linking. During this thermal process, the dispersed water phase evaporated, leaving behind well-defined pores, while the LM microdroplets remained securely confined within these cavities, yielding the LMxPE samples (where x = 0, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3, representing the corresponding LM mass ratios).

Characterization

The surface morphology of the samples was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS Sigma 300). The mechanical properties of the LMPE composites were characterized using a universal testing machine (TFW-5S, Tuofeng). For dielectric characterization, each specimen was cut into circular discs with a diameter of 25 mm and a thickness of 1 mm to ensure conformal contact with the electrodes. Dielectric spectroscopy measurements were then conducted using an impedance analyzer (SI 1260, Solartron) under AC excitation conditions (1 V amplitude, frequency range: 0.1 to 106 Hz). The triboelectric performance of the samples was evaluated at room temperature by integrating a linear motor (BF01-37, LinMot) with an electrometer (Keithley 6517B). The linear motor was employed to simulate mechanical motion, while the electrometer was used to record the generated electrical signals. Unless otherwise specified, the electrical outputs of the LMPE-TENG were characterized using a linear motor under a fixed contact-separation frequency of 1 Hz and a separation distance of 5 cm. The dimensions of the LMPE sample were fixed at 4 cm × 4 cm × 1 cm (contact area of 16 cm2), with a commercial polyethylene terephthalate (PET) film serving as the counter-friction layer. A uniform impact force of 60 N was applied during all electrical measurements.

To ensure statistical reliability, all mechanical and electrical measurements were independently repeated with three distinct samples (n = 3). The data presented in the comparative plots represent the average values of these independent evaluations, demonstrating excellent reproducibility.

Results and Discussion

Structural Characterization of LMPE

The fabrication process of the LMPE is schematically outlined in Figure a. During the thermal curing process of the homogenized LM/DI water/Ecoflex 00-30 emulsion, the dispersed DI water droplets evaporate at elevated temperatures. This phase transition leaves behind numerous localized voids, thereby generating a well-defined porous network within the elastomer. The successful fabrication of the LMPE relies on the inherent interfacial thermodynamics of the multiphase system. The native oxide skin (Ga2O3) formed on the EGaIn microdroplets is inherently hydrophilic, establishing a robust hydrogen-bonding network with the surrounding DI water molecules. By significantly reducing the interfacial tension, this interaction effectively prevents the LM droplets from coalescing, thereby stabilizing the multiphase emulsion. Such an interfacial synergy ensures a uniform colloidal dispersion during the homogenization process, which is fundamental to the formation of the well-defined, independent porous architecture after the water phase evaporates. As the water evaporates and the pores form, the previously suspended LM colloidal droplets are naturally deposited and left behind, remaining securely anchored within these newly formed cavities, which effectively prevents LM leakage during macroscopic deformation.

(a) Schematic diagram showing the fabrication process of the porous LMPE. SEM images of LMPE at (b) low and (c and d) high magnification.

For the optimization of this porous architecture, the effect of DI water content was investigated. Optical microscopy images shown in Supporting Information (SI) Figure S1 reveal that Ecoflex 00-30 elastomers prepared with varying DI water mass ratios exhibit the formation of abundant pores, with an average pore diameter of approximately 250 μm. Specifically, the incorporation of 6 wt % DI water leads to an optimally distributed, independent (closed-cell) porous architecture. When the DI water ratio is further increased, adjacent pores begin to coalesce, forming structural defects. As confirmed by the FTIR spectra (SI Figure S2), the absence of characteristic −OH peaks at 3300 cm–1 and 1630 cm–1 indicates that residual water was completely removed after the curing process.

The internal microstructure of the LMPE was further examined using SEM (Figure b–d). The cross-sectional SEM images reveal a well-defined, closed-cell porous architecture, where individual LM droplets are strictly confined within discrete cavities with no visible interconnected LM channels. Even though the LMPE will experience cyclic compression in the following study, the optimized pore wall thickness effectively buffers the internal pressure exerted by the incompressible LM microdroplets under intense compression. Combined with the dynamic reconstruction and self-healing of the nanoscale native oxide layer (Ga2O3) on the LM surface under cyclic stress, this structural configuration prevents LM leakage or coalescence. Notably, the LM droplets exhibit a significantly brighter imaging contrast compared to the smooth Ecoflex 00-30 matrix due to the strong electron scattering of metals, confirming their physical confinement within the independent pores. Additionally, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was performed to characterize the elemental distribution (SI Figure S3). While the carbon (C) signal outlines the polymer framework, the gallium (Ga) signal inside the cavities appears relatively sparse. This is primarily attributed to the pronounced topographic shadowing effect of the deep porous architecture, which severely attenuates the characteristic X-rays emitted by the confined LM droplets. Nonetheless, when combined with the morphological evidence, these results conclusively confirm that LM predominantly accumulates within the pores, forming a stable and uniformly dispersed pore-confined LM phase throughout the elastomer matrix.

Mechanical Properties of LMPE

When DI water is introduced into the Ecoflex 00-30 matrix, a porous structure is generated after thermal treatment, significantly influences the mechanical behavior of the composite. To study the effects of the coexistence of LM fillers and the internal pores on the mechanical response of the material, the mechanical properties of LMPE samples were obtained to find out the composition ratio for achieving optimal mechanical performance. As shown in Figure , both the porous Ecoflex 00-30 and the LMPE samples exhibit remarkable mechanical robustness, stretchability, and flexibility. Specifically, the stress–strain curves as shown in Figure a intuitively reveal the impact of varying DI water contents. As the DI water ratio increases, the elevated porosity inherently leads to a gradual reduction in tensile strength. Concurrently, the elongation at break exhibits a partial recovery trend, with the optimal porous structure achieving a tensile strength of 397 kPa and a maximum strain of 509% at the breaking point. Furthermore, the elastic modulus of the porous Ecoflex 00-30 progressively decreases with the increasing amount of DI water, eventually stabilizing at approximately 80 kPa (Figure b). This modulus is highly comparable to, and even softer than, that of human skin (0.5–1 MPa), demonstrating the excellent mechanical compliance of the composite and confirming its tremendous suitability for wearable applications. Flexible sensors designed for integration with human joints must exhibit stable tensile performance, while TENG devices require reliable compressive resilience. The LMPE composite, composed of Ecoflex 00-30 and LM, utilizes the cross-linked polymer Ecoflex 00-30 as a flexible matrix due to its excellent stretchability. Owing to the near-zero Young’s modulus of Ga-based LMs (≈0 MPa), LMPE inherently possesses outstanding tensile and compressive deformability. This is attributed to the fluidic dynamics of the LM at room temperature, which allow efficient stress redistribution during deformation.

The fatigue resistance of flexible electronic materials affects the operational stability and lifetime of the corresponding devices. To evaluate this property, a cyclic tensile test was conducted on porous Ecoflex 00-30 at 200% strain for 10 loading–unloading cycles at a speed of 50 mm·min–1 (Figure c). A pronounced hysteresis was observed during the first cycle, mainly due to molecular chain disentanglement, weak interfacial interactions, and partial pore collapse, resulting in significant energy dissipation. In the subsequent cycles, the hysteresis loops decreased in area, and the peak stress reduced, indicating a transition toward a stable molecular orientation and defect distribution with lower energy loss. Based on its overall mechanical performance, the porous Ecoflex 00-30 containing 6 wt % DI water was selected for the fabrication of the LMPE. As shown in Figure d, the tensile strain of the LMPE increases with higher LM content, achieving a maximum elongation at break of ≈650% and a modulus of around 70 kPa (SI Figure S4). To isolate the mechanical roles of the porous structure and LM filler, control samples were evaluated (SI Figure S5a). The pure Ecoflex 00-30 foam exhibits a Young’s modulus of 75 kPa and 500% strain, while the solid composite shows 150 kPa and 700% strain. This demonstrates that the porous architecture determines the low modulus (70–125 kPa) by providing compressible volume, whereas the fluidic LM droplets deform in tandem with the matrix to preserve ultimate stretchability. Furthermore, cyclic tensile tests show a stable hysteresis loop (SI Figure S5b), attributed to the viscous deformation of LM cores and the dynamic microrupture/reoxidation of Ga2O3 shells. The compressive stress–strain curve (Figure e) reveals that LMPE exhibits low compressive stress under strains below 70%. This response is attributed to its high porosity (∼50%) and the fluidic nature of the LM fillers. During compression, the internal pores are initially compacted before the solid matrix bears the load. Due to its liquid state, the LM within the pores does not restrict this structural deformation and therefore has minimal impact on the overall compressive behavior. Consequently, LMPE achieves large strains under low stress, enabling the detection of low-intensity mechanical stimuli, a feature advantageous for TENG-based sensing applications. Furthermore, LMPE demonstrates excellent compressive elasticity and recovery. As illustrated in Figure f, even after 10 continuous compression cycles at a fixed strain of 50%, the compressive strength remains nearly unchanged. This durability arises from the gradual collapse and re-expansion of the internal pores, where elastic deformation dominates, allowing the material to recover its original shape upon unloading.

Working Principle and Electrical Performance of LMPE

To achieve optimal electrical output, the composition of the LMPEspecifically the effect of porosity and LM loading on electrical outputwas systematically investigated. Initially, the effect of porosity alone on output voltage was evaluated. As shown in SI Figure S6, the porous Ecoflex 00-30 exhibits markedly improved output voltage compared with the pristine Ecoflex 00-30, increasing from 37 to 71 V upon the introduction of DI water. When the DI water concentration exceeds 6 wt %, excessive pore coalescence occurs, which reduces the effective contact area, thereby resulting in the diminishment of electrical output. Therefore, Ecoflex 00-30 containing 6 wt % DI water was selected as the optimal matrix. Subsequently, the LM-to-Ecoflex 00-30 weight ratio was varied to further maximize performance (Figure b–d). As the ratio increased from 5:1 to 5:2.5, the open-circuit voltage (V OC ), short-circuit current (I SC ), and transferred charge (Q SC ) surged from 136 V, 3.65 μA, and 53.1 nC to maximum values of 170 V, 9.54 μA, and 63.3 nC, respectively. Nevertheless, when the LM content exceeded the optimal ratio (i.e., in LM3PE), these output parameters decreased to 104 V, 6.27 μA, and 43.7 nC. Under 70% compressive strain, the LM3PE sample exhibited macroscopic LM leakage (SI Figure S7). This leakage under compression likely leads to the deterioration of the porous network and subsequent internal charge neutralization, thereby reducing the effective surface charge density and overall triboelectric output.

(a) Schematic diagram of the charge transfer mechanism in LMPE-TENG and their porous structures variation under pressure; (b–d) VOC, ISC , and charge maps of LMPE-TENG at different LM contents; (e–g) VOC, ISC , and charge maps of LMPE-TENG at different frequencies.

These dynamic performance variations are fundamentally governed by the synergistic effects of the composite’s structural and dielectric properties. Unlike conventional solid conductive fillers (e.g., Ag nanowires, graphene, and MXene), the LM (EGaIn) used in this study exhibits both the fluidity of a liquid and the conductivity of a metal. Under external force, the fluidic LM redistributes dynamically within the elastomeric matrix, eliminating interfacial separation without compromising mechanical integrity. As schematically depicted for a single pore in Figure a, the porous architecture enables mechanical energy harvesting from multiple directions. Initially, no charge transfer occurs due to the lack of direct contact. Upon the application of an external force, the deforming Ecoflex 00-30 comes into contact with the confined LM. The difference in electronegativity drives electrons to transfer from the LM surface to the Ecoflex 00-30 matrix. Full contact within the pores establishes electrostatic equilibrium, while pressure release forces the pores to recover, driving electrons back to maintain equilibrium and generating an alternating current (AC). Macroscopically, the porous network enhances the dielectric response by allowing dynamic modulation of the internal air–polymer interfaces during compression. The reduced effective thickness under external stress increases the surface charge density and capacitance according to the following equations: 1 V=σx(t)ε 2 C=ε0εrSd where σ is the surface charge density, x(t) represents the distance between the two triboelectric layers, ε0 and ε r are the vacuum permittivity and the relative dielectric constant, d is the effective dielectric thickness, and S is the actual contact area. As the pore volume decreases, both S and the local charge density increase, leading to improved energy conversion efficiency. As the material is compressed, the pore volume decreases, leading to an increase in capacitance and consequently a higher output voltage. Therefore, the porous structure contributes to improved energy conversion efficiency by increasing both effective contact area and local charge density. − In addition to porosity, the amount of LM directly affects the composite’s polarization capability (Figure b–d). The remarkable enhancement in the triboelectric output of the LMPE-TENG can be attributed to the Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars (MWS) interfacial polarization. In this heterogeneous system, the numerous isolated conductive LM microdroplets, sequestered within the dielectric Ecoflex 00-30 matrix. Upon mechanical excitation, the high-contrast interfaces between the LM and the matrix promote intense charge accumulation and interfacial trapping. As LM content increases to the optimal level, this synergistic MWS effect significantly bolsters the effective dielectric permittivity and surface charge density of the LMPE, leading to a substantial rise in εr from 3.0 to 5.1 (SI Figure S8a), which is consistent with the capacitance growth from 4.5 pF to 6.9 pF (SI Figure S8c). Under compressive strain, the pores collapse and the distance between adjacent conductive LM droplets decreases sharply, leading to a dramatic increase in the effective dielectric constant (εeff) and structural capacitance (SI Figure S8d). This structural property enhances the charge accumulation capacity of the triboelectric layer, directly contributing to the outstanding electrical outputs. These macroscopic dielectric responses provide logical, indirect evidence to support the proposed MWS mechanisms.

To evaluate the structural integrity under operational stress, cross-sectional SEM characterization was performed on samples subjected to severe vertical compression. The micrograph conclusively demonstrates that, even under intense compression, the independent Ecoflex 00-30 pore walls remain fully intact without microcracking or collapse, definitively ruling out the formation of any conductive percolating networks or interconnected pathways during deformation.

Finally, the effect of mechanical compression frequency on the electrical output of the LMPE was investigated. As the operating frequency increased from 0.5 to 3 Hz (Figure e–g), both V OC and Q SC remained nearly constant at 120 V and 53.8 nC, while I SC rose from 4.98 μA to 25.98 μA. This behavior is explained by the shortened contact–separation time, which accelerates charge transfer. According to the equation: 3 ISC=ΔQΔt where ΔQ represents the transferred charge and Δt is the period of charge transfer. A smaller Δt naturally results in a higher instantaneous current, consistent with experimental observations.

Practical Applications of LMPE

Benefiting from the flexible Ecoflex 00-30 matrix and the intrinsic fluidity of LM, the fabricated LMPE exhibits significant potential as both an energy harvester and a self-powered sensor. To evaluate its practical energy-harvesting performance, the output characteristics under specific external loads were investigated using the sample LM2.5PE. As illustrated in Figure a, the output voltage gradually increases with load resistance, reaching a peak of 155 V at 100 MΩ, while the corresponding current exhibits an inverse trend. The output power (P) was calculated using the formula: 4 P=I2R where R represents the load resistance. A maximum power density of 42.63 W·m–3 was achieved at a load resistance of 100 MΩ, exceeding previously reported values for flexible TENGs. −

Owing to its excellent electrical output, the potential application of the LMPE for practical energy conversion and storage was further explored. The AC generated by the LMPE was rectified through a bridge circuit and stored in a capacitor (SI Figure S9). This stored energy could reliably power electronic devices such as LEDs and timers, as depicted by the equivalent circuit in Figure b. When charging capacitors of varying sizes, the charging rate inherently decreases with increasing capacitance. For instance, a 1 μF capacitor could be charged to 4.5 V within 100 s, whereas a 47 μF capacitor reached only 1 V in the same time frame. Using a 2.2 μF capacitor, 3.0 V was achieved in 100 s, and repeated charge–discharge cycles demonstrated excellent cycling stability (Figure c). Furthermore, at a working frequency of 1 Hz, the device could easily illuminate 60 commercial LEDs connected in series (Figure d).

Durability and stability are key performance indicators for practical applications. Continuous cycle testing confirmed the excellent long-term reliability of the LMPE. To evaluate the long-term operational stability, a continuous contact-separation cycling test was performed for 4 h (approximately 14,400 cycles) under a fixed vertical impact force of 50 N and an excitation frequency of 1 Hz (Figure f). The output voltage retained over 95% of its initial value, demonstrating excellent durability. To explore the ultimate failure mode when the stability eventually undergoes minor degradation, postcycling microstructural analysis was performed on samples after extended overfatigue operation. Cross-sectional SEM imaging (SI Figure S10) reveals that, even under intense compression, the independent Ecoflex 00-30 pore walls remain fully intact without microcracking or collapse, definitively ruling out the formation of any conductive percolating networks or interconnected pathways during deformation. Instead, the trace degradation is primarily governed by the continuous mechanical fatigue of the internal filler, where the gallium oxide (Ga2O3) shells undergo persistent microrupture and subsequent reoxidation. Under extended cyclic stress, this dynamic self-healing process gradually shifts toward a new equilibrium, resulting in a slightly thickened oxide layer. This thickened semiconducting shell increases the local screening effect and impedes the efficient charge induction at the interfaces, which perfectly accounts for the minor decline in the macroscopic triboelectric outputs.

The environmental robustness of the LMPE-TENG was evaluated under varying temperature and humidity conditions (SI Figure S11). As shown in Figure S11a, the output voltage remains highly stable in the temperature range of 0 to 50 °C, indicating the working stability of the device over a broad temperature range. In contrast, the output voltage predictably decreases as the humidity increases from 20% to 90% RH (SI Figure S11b) due to a microscopic water film accumulated on the surface that accelerates charge dissipation, while the hydrophobic matrix (contact angle >100°, SI Figure S12) prevents internal moisture absorption. To intuitively evaluate the comprehensive performance of the developed LMPE-TENG, a multidimensional radar chart was constructed by benchmarking it against recently reported state-of-the-art liquid-metal-based and porous-elastomer TENGs (Figure e). Key parameters evaluated include maximum tensile strength, long-term durability (cycle count), V OC , I SC , and power density. This systematic comparison firmly establishes the multidimensional superiority of the proposed LMPE-TENG for practical flexible power sources.

Finally, to evaluate its intrinsic self-powered force-sensing capability, the TENG devices were subjected to external pressures ranging from 10 to 60 N at a frequency of 1 Hz. As shown in Figure g, the V OC increases proportionally with the applied pressure. This highly sensitive response originates from two synergistic effects under compression: (i) the contact area between the fluidic LM and the Ecoflex 00-30 matrix expands, enhancing charge transfer; and (ii) the porous matrix is compressed, reducing the material’s thickness and increasing the effective relative permittivity, which in turn significantly elevates the surface charge density. A robust linear relationship exists between the V OC and the applied force (SI Figure S13), yielding a calculated sensitivity of 0.75 V/N, which surpasses values reported in prior studies. ,

Benefiting from its outstanding strain sensitivity, the LMPE can be employed as a self-powered sensor for encoding and decoding Morse code, enabling the precise conversion of mechanical deformation into electrical signals for information transmission. Morse code is a signaling system that represents letters, numbers, and punctuation marks using a specific sequence of “·” (dot) and “” (dash). , As illustrated in Figure a, rhythmic finger taps and presses can represent various combinations of dots and dashes to effectively transmit messages. For instance, the distress signal “SOS” can be successfully conveyed through specific tapping rhythms (Figure b). Due to its high strain sensitivity, the LMPE exhibits a fast response time of ∼171.10 ms and a low minimum actuation threshold of ∼0.2 N (SI Figure S14). In addition to strain sensing, the LMPE demonstrates excellent material recognition capabilities by generating distinct V OC when rubbing against different friction layers (Figure c). Specifically, the V OC values obtained from contact with PTFE, Cu, PI, and PET are 37, 55, 134, and 156 V, respectively. This ability to distinguish between various materials significantly reduces the possibility of accidental touches in practical applications. Building upon these sensing capabilities, the LMPE can serve as a core component for human-machine interaction (HMI). In a fundamental LMPE-based HMI system, the generated electrical signals act as analog inputs for MCUs to activate internal processing circuits and embedded programs. When a finger contacts the LMPE, the two act as a TENG pair, driving electrons to flow from the ground to the LMPE and generating an effective triggering current. These analog trigger signals are subsequently converted into digital signals by the MCU, which are then recognized by a corresponding computer program to execute predefined commands (Figure d). Therefore, the LMPE can be integrated into wearable devices for real-time computer program control. As a proof of concept, an LMPE system was attached to the thumb. By intentionally making contact with the ring finger, middle finger, index finger, and little finger, the system simulated the “up, down, left, and right” directional controls of a computer keyboard to successfully play the game “Snake” (Figure e; SI Video S1). Touching the corresponding fingers enabled rapid and accurate game control. By integrating this control system, the LM-based porous elastomer is demonstrated to be a novel, multifunctional wearable HMI. This technology holds immense potential for future IoT applications, including vehicle control, smart home automation (e.g., controlling light switches and air conditioner temperatures), and soft robotics. ,

Conclusion

In summary, a facile and highly effective method is employed to fabricate a LM-filled porous elastomer LMPE, in which DI water acted as a green porogen to securely embed LM droplets confined within independent pores dispersed in the Ecoflex 00-30 matrix. The prepared LMPE exhibits excellent tensile properties and skin-like flexibility, while maintaining structural integrity under prolonged deformation. Operating as a self-powered sensor, the LMPE delivers impressive electrical outputs under an external force of 50 N at 1 Hz, including a V OC of 169 V, an I SC of 9.5 μA, and a Q SC of 63 nC. Furthermore, a remarkable peak power density of 42.63 W·m–3 is achieved at a load resistance of 100 MΩ. The device maintains highly consistent output performance even after 4 h of continuous cyclic operation and 3 months of ambient storage, demonstrating exceptional durability and environmental stability. Capitalizing on these merits, the LMPE shows tremendous practical potential across diverse applications, encompassing self-powered force sensing, biomechanical energy harvesting, material recognition, and intelligent human–machine interactions. By bridging materials science, electronics, and computer engineering, this work provides a robust platform for high-performance wearable TENGs, bringing the practical deployment of intelligent self-powered sensing systems one step closer to reality.
