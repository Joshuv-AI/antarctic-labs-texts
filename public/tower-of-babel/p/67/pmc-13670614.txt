Exploratory development of prediction models for pharmacotherapy outcomes in trigeminal neuralgia: a combined analysis based on multi-source data
Authors: Hongan Yang, Baijintao Sun, Mingtao Li, Xuezhao Yang, Mingyan Li, Chuan Zhang, Hanfeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug-refractory trigeminal neuralgia (DRTN) represents a formidable challenge in clinical management, with approximately 30%–50% of patients eventually progressing to DRTN. Early identification of high-risk DRTN populations and prediction of the timing of pharmacotherapy failure are crucial for optimizing clinical treatment strategies.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 163 primary trigeminal neuralgia patients receiving carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine with comprehensive imaging. A partially-blinded approach was used for data extraction: 135 patients (December 2022–December 2024) formed the primary cohort and 28 patients (December 2021–December 2022) served as the temporally separated validation cohort. Data comprised clinical baseline characteristics, pain and emotional scale scores, and imaging parameters. A nested cross-validation framework (20 random seeds) was used for model development, with feature selection confined to the training cohort within each seed. Five machine learning models were compared via pooled ROC curves aggregated across seeds, and SHAP analysis interpreted the optimal model at the median-performing seed. Age-unadjusted and age-adjusted multivariate Cox regression models were constructed, with four parallel univariate screening thresholds evaluated in sensitivity analyses. Model performance was assessed by Kaplan–Meier analysis, time-dependent ROC curves, and the concordance index.ResultsThe support vector machine with radial basis function kernel (SVM-RBF) achieved the optimal performance, with average AUCs of 0.927 ± 0.041, 0.824 ± 0.049, and 0.806 ± 0.058 in the training, test, and validation cohort, respectively. In a sensitivity analysis using four univariate Cox screening thresholds, with age at first pain onset forced into all multivariate models, pain involved extent and medial temporal lobe atrophy (MTA) score were both identified as significant, independent candidate prognostic markers. Both Cox models showed moderate discriminative capacity for short-to-medium-term DRTN prognosis but attenuated long-term predictive efficacy.ConclusionThe SVM-RBF model, integrating multi-source heterogeneous data, demonstrated promising predictive performance for DRTN onset risk across the present cohort. The Cox models showed moderate discriminative capacity for short-to-medium-term prognosis, though long-term predictive accuracy was attenuated. These findings provide preliminary evidence for early risk stratification and individualized treatment of DRTN, while underscoring the need for improved survival prediction strategies to address the limited long-term performance of the current Cox models.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10194-026-02463-3.Keywords: Drug-refractory trigeminal neuralgia, Multi-source heterogeneous data, Machine learning, Cox proportional hazards regression model

Introduction

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN), a common chronic orofacial neuropathic pain condition, is characteristically manifested by recurrent brief episodes of lancinating, electric shock-like severe pain in the distribution territory of the trigeminal nerve [1–3]. This disorder severely impairs patients’ quality of life and is frequently accompanied by varying degrees of negative emotional states, sleep disturbances, and secondary activity limitations [4, 5].

Although evidence suggests that surgical interventions such as microvascular decompression (MVD) can achieve more durable and complete pain relief [6], in real-world clinical practice, the vast majority of newly diagnosed patients still prefer conservative pharmacotherapy as the initial approach, and multiple reviews and guidelines also recommend pharmacotherapy as the first-line treatment for TN [1, 2, 7]. Carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine are currently the first-line medications for controlling TN attacks, with an overall clinical efficacy rate of approximately 90% for paroxysmal pain [2]; however, long-term medication use is associated with diminishing efficacy and various adverse effects [8, 9], and the long-term pain control failure rate can reach 50% [7].

Currently, there is a lack of unified international criteria for defining drug-refractory trigeminal neuralgia (DRTN), yet the core consensus is highly consistent: when medications fail to adequately control pain or when patients cannot tolerate adverse drug reactions, a transition to surgical treatment should be recommended [1–3, 7, 10]. The European Academy of Neurology guideline on TN explicitly states that surgical treatment is not a necessary option for all patients, yet in clinical practice, many patients experience delayed referral for surgical intervention [7]. In fact, the clinical manifestations of TN exhibit considerable inter-individual variability, and the disease course is often characterized by irregular alternation between episodes and remissions. Consequently, the progression from effective pharmacotherapy to loss of drug efficacy or emergence of severe side effects exhibits substantial individual heterogeneity, and the transition at critical treatment junctures is often unpredictable, constituting a major challenge in clinical decision-making. Given the aforementioned disease progression characteristics, there is an objective need to establish a corresponding prediction system to identify high-risk DRTN populations at an early stage of treatment and to perform individualized prediction of the specific timing of DRTN onset, thereby providing a reference for optimizing intervention timing.

Existing studies have predominantly focused on postoperative prognostic evaluation in TN [11–15], and no study has yet investigated the risk factors associated with DRTN or established a drug resistance risk prediction system integrating multi-dimensional indicators. However, the construction of such a system faces certain challenges. The pathogenesis of DRTN is complex, and the relevant pathophysiological mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. Recent evidence suggests that TN disease progression involves not only peripheral neurovascular compression and nerve demyelination [1, 16, 17], but also potential associations with skull base bony anatomical morphology [18–21], pain clinical phenotypes and their accompanying negative emotions [22–25], and central nervous system structural and functional alterations [26–29].

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as an important technical approach in the field of clinical prediction, capable of mining complex associations among heterogeneous variables, and has been predominantly applied to predict surgical outcomes in TN [11, 30, 31]. Therefore, this study aimed to integrate multi-dimensional heterogeneous data to construct and validate machine learning models for predicting DRTN occurrence risk, and to further establish Cox regression models for prognostic prediction and risk stratification of DRTN onset timing. Through these two exploratory and complementary modeling strategies, we expect to provide preliminary evidence with certain reference value for informing future prospective research and large-sample multicenter studies.

Methods

All data analyzed in this study were derived from de-identified historical medical records routinely collected during standard clinical examinations and medication management. All patients were fully informed of all potential risks and all subsequent relevant clinical procedures before receiving examinations and medication guidance, and provided written informed consent as part of routine clinical practice; no research-specific consent was obtained, as this study was a retrospective review of existing records and involved no prospective intervention. The requirement for individual research informed consent was waived by the Institutional Ethics Review Committee of the hospital, which approved the study protocol, owing to the retrospective design and the use of fully de-identified data. All data analyses strictly adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Study population screening and study design

This cohort study was conducted at a large tertiary medical center. Between December 2021 and December 2024, 283 consecutive outpatients and inpatients diagnosed with trigeminal neuralgia and having complete relevant imaging examinations were enrolled, with the final follow-up date of June 2026.

Inclusion criteria for TN patients: (1) clinically diagnosed with TN meeting the diagnostic criteria of the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) [32]; (2) receiving carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine as first-line pharmacotherapy; (3) having undergone high-resolution computed tomography (CT) of the head and trigeminal nerve magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with clear, evaluable imaging data; (4) accessible and essentially complete clinical baseline data.

Exclusion criteria for TN patients: (1) secondary TN, including TN caused by multiple sclerosis, vascular malformations, and intracranial tumor lesions (e.g., meningioma, schwannoma); (2) loss to follow-up: inability to establish contact or complete follow-up assessment during the follow-up period; (3) poor imaging quality or incomplete data: MRI images with obvious motion artifacts, missing scan sequences, or insufficient resolution precluding imaging parameter measurement; (4) incomplete relevant clinical data: missing key variables in baseline clinical data precluding statistical analysis; (5) surgical intervention without prior pharmacotherapy: patients who underwent surgical treatment before an adequate trial of carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine, precluding assessment of pharmacotherapy outcomes; (6) non-use of carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine: patients who did not receive the aforementioned first-line medications and adopted other drugs as the initial treatment regimen; (7) subjective medication refusal or self-discontinuation: patients who failed to complete an adequate drug trial due to voluntary non-adherence, precluding determination of analgesic efficacy; (8) concurrent other treatments during effective pharmacotherapy: patients receiving acupuncture or physical therapy while first-line medications had already effectively controlled pain, potentially confounding the assessment of independent pharmacotherapy efficacy.

The primary cohort included 223 patients enrolled from December 2022 to December 2024. After sequential exclusion according to the above criteria—20 cases of secondary TN, 21 cases lost to follow-up, 35 cases with imaging and data quality issues, 5 cases undergoing surgery without prior pharmacotherapy, and 7 cases with medication-related issues—135 TN patients were ultimately included, with the detailed flow chart presented in Fig. 1. These subjects were randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 94) and a test cohort (n = 41) at an approximate ratio of 7:3; random allocation was performed using computer-generated random numbers to minimize selection bias.

Flow diagram of patients selection and cohort allocation

The temporally separated validation cohort comprised 60 patients enrolled from December 2021 to December 2022. After exclusion of 6 cases of secondary TN, 16 cases lost to follow-up, 9 cases with imaging and data quality issues, and 1 case not receiving carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine, 28 TN patients were ultimately included (46.7% of 60 eligible patients), with the detailed flow chart presented in Fig. 1. This temporal arrangement (earlier cohort as validation, later cohort as development) differs from the typical paradigm of validating on a temporally subsequent cohort. The rationale is that the earlier cohort (n = 60 after initial screening) was smaller and therefore served as a fixed validation set to avoid further reducing the development sample; additionally, patients in the earlier cohort had longer follow-up duration, enabling more complete outcome ascertainment.

DRTN grouping criteria and detail

Based on the core consensus regarding DRTN outlined in the Introduction, in clinical practice we assessed TN pharmacotherapy outcomes using the Barrow Neurological Institute (BNI) pain intensity scale, the most commonly used grading instrument in the trigeminal neuralgia field [33].

Patients graded as BNI IV–V or those who developed intolerable adverse reactions during follow-up (including but not limited to severe somnolence, dizziness, ataxia, and gastrointestinal reactions) were classified as having pharmacotherapy failure and assigned to the DRTN group. Among the 135 TN patients ultimately included in the primary cohort (section “Study population screening and study design”), 104 were classified into the DRTN group (74 with BNI grade IV–V as the primary outcome; 30 with intolerable adverse reactions as the primary outcome), and 31 into the non-DRTN group. It should be noted that in real-world clinical practice, these two categories are not mutually exclusive: a proportion of patients whose primary outcome was BNI IV–V also experienced concurrent adverse reactions that remained tolerable, while patients whose primary outcome was intolerable adverse reactions often exhibited some degree of diminished drug efficacy that did not reach BNI grade IV–V. These two conditions frequently coexist and are difficult to fully distinguish; DRTN patients differ only in the predominant emphasis placed on each of the two primary outcomes (all with a minimum follow-up duration exceeding 2 years and stable disease status at the last follow-up).

In the temporally separated validation cohort of 28 TN patients, 20 were classified into the DRTN group (13 with BNI grade IV–V as the primary outcome; 7 with intolerable adverse reactions as the primary outcome), and 8 into the non-DRTN group (all with a minimum follow-up duration exceeding 2 years; only 1 patient required a slight dose increase of first-line medication without reaching the maximum dosage during the follow-up period).

To further characterize the DRTN cohort and evaluate potential heterogeneity between the two primary outcome categories, we conducted an exploratory subgroup analysis comparing baseline clinical characteristics (Supplementary Table 1), pain- and emotion-related scale scores (Supplementary Table 2), and neuroimaging parameters (Supplementary Table 3) between patients with analgesic failure (BNI grade IV–V; n = 74 in the primary cohort, n = 13 in the validation cohort) and those with analgesic intolerance (intolerable adverse reactions; n = 30 in the primary cohort, n = 7 in the validation cohort).

Data collection

All data were extracted and organized by one investigator from electronic medical records and the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) imaging database, who also completed the collection of follow-up information for all enrolled patients. The study data encompassed three dimensions: clinical baseline characteristics, pain and emotional scale scores, and imaging measurement parameters. This investigator anonymized the data and masked outcome information and outcome-related data, then provided the MRI/CT data to two other investigators (interventional radiologists specializing in pain with over 5 years of experience) for independent partially-blinded measurement and assessment. In cases of data discrepancy, a final determination was made by a senior attending physician.

Clinical baseline characteristics: including sex, age at first pain onset, pain duration at follow-up endpoint, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), pain side, pain subtype (classical TN(CTN)/idiopathic TN(ITN)), presence of concomitant continuous pain, specific branches of the affected trigeminal nerve, pain involved extent, highest systolic blood pressure, hypertension risk stratification (no risk, low, moderate, high, very high), immune-related diseases, and routine hematological parameters, as detailed in Table 1. Pain involved extent was set as an ordinal categorical variable representing the number of affected trigeminal nerve branches (1, 2, or 3 branches), reflecting the spatial spread of pain across the trigeminal innervation territories. It should be emphasized that hypertension risk stratification (no risk, low, moderate, high, very high) was modeled as an ordinal variable with OR reported per one-level increase, for the following considerations: (1) these strata represent progressively increasing cardiovascular risk severity aligned with established clinical guidelines [34]; and (2) unlike isolated blood pressure values, this stratification integrates blood pressure levels, risk factors, organ damage, and comorbidities, providing a more comprehensive assessment of overall hypertensive and cardiovascular risk status.

All imaging assessments were independently performed by two specialized physicians in a partially-blinded manner. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to evaluate inter-rater agreement; for continuous data, ICC > 0.75 was required and the mean value was adopted to minimize measurement error; for categorical and ordinal data, complete agreement between assessors was required for each patient, and discrepancies were adjudicated by a senior attending physician.

Feature selection

The feature selection process was conducted in two parts (Figs. 2 and 4).

Part 1 (Fig. 2): Feature selection for machine learning models was conducted within a nested cross-validation framework using 20 independent random seeds (70%/30% training/test split per seed; Fig. 2). Within each seed, feature selection was strictly confined to the training set to preclude data leakage. The process proceeded as follows: initial screening was performed through inter-group difference analysis for each of the three data dimensions; variables with severe multicollinearity were excluded; the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control for multiplicity. Additionally, pain duration at the follow-up endpoint was excluded because it contains information on the duration of pharmacotherapy response, which is directly related to the study outcome, to prevent outcome data leakage. The remaining variables in each data domain were then subjected to univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses within each training set, retaining statistically significant variables as candidate predictive features. The selection frequency of each feature—defined as the number of seeds (out of 20) in which it was retained for modeling—was recorded to quantify selection stability (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Part 2 (Fig. 4): Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was first performed on all candidate variables derived from the three data sources (baseline clinical characteristics, scale scores, and imaging features). To examine the robustness of variable selection, four univariate screening thresholds were applied in parallel: P < 0.05 (primary analysis) and P < 0.10, P < 0.15, and P < 0.20 (sensitivity analyses). Given that the MTA score is inherently age-associated, age was forcibly introduced into every multivariate model to adjust for potential confounding, regardless of its univariate significance.

Machine learning model construction and evaluation

This study employed five machine learning algorithms for DRTN binary classification modeling: support vector machine with radial basis function kernel (SVM-RBF), support vector machine with linear kernel (SVM-Linear), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and logistic regression (LR). The selection of these algorithms covers multiple modeling frameworks: linear models (LR, SVM-Linear), kernel-based models (SVM-RBF), and ensemble tree-based models (RF, XGBoost), thereby enabling a comprehensive comparison of different modeling approaches for the DRTN prediction task. The pathogenesis of DRTN involves the interactive effects of multi-dimensional factors, and both linear associations and nonlinear effects may exist between predictor variables and the outcome. A single modeling framework is insufficient to capture such complex data structures; by covering linear, kernel function, and ensemble learning paradigms, the suitability of different algorithms for the DRTN prediction task can be systematically evaluated.

The procedure was as follows: (1) All models were implemented using Python 3.9.7 (https://www.python.org), based on the Scikit-learn and XGBoost libraries; (2) explicit regularization and model structural constraints were applied according to the inherent characteristics of each algorithm to suppress overfitting from the perspective of model complexity, thereby enhancing model generalizability and suitability for real-world clinical applications; (3) grid search combined with 10-fold cross-validation was employed for hyperparameter optimization on the training cohort, and model performance was sequentially evaluated on the test cohort and the validation cohort; (4) to ensure the stability, reliability, and reproducibility of model performance evaluation, this study adopted a multi-random-seed strategy, using computer-generated sets of 20 different random seeds to independently perform training–test cohort partitioning and model parameter initialization. For each random seed, the complete analytical pipeline, including dataset partitioning, feature selection within the training set, model training, and performance evaluation, was executed, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was subsequently calculated. The mean AUC across the 20 seeds (mean ± standard deviation) served as the core evaluation metric. Traditional machine learning studies typically use a single random seed for dataset partitioning; however, the random split of training and test cohorts inherently introduces randomness that may cause fluctuations in model evaluation metrics. Particularly for this study, with a relatively limited sample size, the quality of a single data partition may lead to inflated or deflated model performance estimates, thereby affecting the credibility of the conclusions to some extent. The multi-random-seed strategy substantially mitigates the interference of random partitioning on model evaluation by repeatedly assessing model performance across multiple independent data partitioning schemes, yielding more robust and reproducible generalization performance estimates; (5) additionally, the median-seed model (i.e., the model trained using the random seed yielding the median AUC) was selected for subsequent calibration curve analysis, decision curve analysis (DCA), and SHAP feature interpretation, avoiding result bias from selecting a model with overly favorable or unfavorable performance. The above study design aligns with current recommendations for improving the reproducibility of medical machine learning research [36], and complies with the latest TRIPOD + AI guidelines for transparent reporting of prediction model studies [37].

Model performance was comprehensively quantified from multiple dimensions using the following metrics: AUC comprehensively reflects the overall discriminative capacity of the model across different thresholds; calibration curves with Brier Score and Hosmer–Lemeshow (H-L) P value were plotted to assess the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes; DCA was used to calculate the clinical net benefit of the model across different threshold probability intervals, evaluating the practical value of the model from a clinical decision-making perspective. Details are presented in Fig. 2.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was employed to interpret the optimal machine learning model, elucidating the interpretable patterns of each feature’s contribution. The SHAP summary beeswarm plot visualized the direction and magnitude of each feature’s effect on model output. The feature importance bar chart based on SHAP values ranked features by mean absolute SHAP value, quantitatively characterizing the overall contribution of each feature to model prediction. Confusion matrices and predicted probability distribution histograms were plotted for both the test cohort and the temporally separated validation cohort, intuitively demonstrating the classification performance and prediction confidence distribution at the individual patient level. Details are presented in Fig. 3.

Cox regression model construction and evaluation

We defined time zero as the date when patients first received carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine therapy. This time point marks the commencement of standardized first-line medical treatment and serves as a well-defined, objective baseline for time-to-event analyses. The primary endpoint for Cox regression analysis was defined as the date of confirmed development of drug-resistant trigeminal neuralgia (DRTN), with diagnostic criteria specified in section “DRTN grouping criteria and detail”. For patients who maintained sustained therapeutic response and clinical stability on pharmacotherapy for more than two years without fulfilling the DRTN diagnostic thresholds, administrative censoring was applied at their last clinical follow-up. Additionally, subjects who remained free of DRTN onset by the study cutoff date were censored at their final follow-up time point.

Based on the four univariate Cox regression thresholds established in section “Feature selection”, Part 2, variables meeting each respective threshold were entered into a corresponding multivariate Cox regression model, with P < 0.05 as the multivariate inclusion criterion, yielding four parallel multivariate models (Fig. 4A and D). Given that the MTA score is inherently age-associated, age was forcibly introduced into every multivariate model to adjust for potential confounding, regardless of its univariate significance. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residual tests, with the non-proportionality P value used to confirm the assumption. Across all four screening thresholds, the MTA score remained a significant independent predictor after adjustment for age and all other retained variables (P < 0.05 in each model).

Using the primary-analysis model (univariate P < 0.05), the prognostic performance of an age-unadjusted and an age-adjusted multivariate Cox model was further compared. The discriminative capacity of the models was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) at 1, 3, and 5 years. The C-index provides an overall assessment of the model’s ability to distinguish between high-risk and low-risk individuals throughout the entire follow-up period, whereas time-dependent AUC characterizes the variation in discriminative capacity as a function of follow-up duration. Time-dependent AUC was calculated using the inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) method to mitigate estimation bias introduced by censored observations. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted for the high- and low-risk groups, and the log-rank test was used to assess the statistical significance of between-group survival differences, in both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 4E and J). Calibration of the Cox regression models was additionally assessed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of clinical data was performed using Python 3.9.7 (https://www.python.org) and R (version 4.1.2, https://www.r-project.org/). For inter-group comparisons, normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using independent-samples t-tests, non-normally distributed continuous variables using Mann–Whitney U tests, and categorical variables using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Binary logistic regression was employed for univariate and multivariate analyses of DRTN occurrence, with results presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs); Cox proportional hazards regression was used for time-to-event analysis, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. Time-dependent AUC was calculated using the IPCW method to handle censored data. All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 defined as statistically significant.

Results

Intergroup feature comparison and feature selection in the primary cohort

A total of 163 patients with primary trigeminal neuralgia were enrolled in this study, including 135 in the primary cohort (training cohort: n = 94; test cohort: n = 41) and 28 in the temporally separated validation cohort (Fig. 1). In the primary cohort, 104 patients were classified into the DRTN group and 31 into the non-DRTN group; in the temporally separated validation cohort, 20 patients were classified into the DRTN group and 8 into the non-DRTN group.

Tables 1, 2 and 3 are descriptive only, presenting the intergroup analyses and feature selection workflow for the primary cohort. All features adopted for modeling were derived within a nested cross-validation framework (20 random seeds). At each seed, the primary cohort underwent stratified splitting, after which feature selection was confined exclusively to the training cohort, followed by model evaluation on the withheld internal test cohort and the validation cohort.

Tables 1, 2 and 3 report the intergroup comparison results, including FDR-adjusted significance, univariate and multivariate logistic regression results, and cross-seed selection frequency for all candidate variables. Features selected through intergroup comparison are as follows:

(1) Clinical baseline characteristics (Table 1): pain involved extent (multivariate OR = 2.73, 95% CI: 1.20–6.20, P = 0.016, selection frequency: 11/20 seeds), concomitant continuous pain (multivariate OR = 9.40, 95% CI: 1.14–77.77, P = 0.038, selection frequency: 7/20 seeds), BMI (multivariate OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.01–1.39, P = 0.034, selection frequency: 4/20 seeds), age at first pain onset (multivariate OR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.12, P = 0.020, selection frequency: 3/20 seeds). The difference in hypertension risk stratification was statistically significant in univariate analysis (univariate OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.16–2.47, P = 0.006), whereas no statistical significance was observed in multivariate analysis (multivariate OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 0.83–1.91, P = 0.270); nevertheless, its selection frequency reached 9 out of 20 random seeds. Furthermore, sex, pain laterality, and immune-related diseases did not differ significantly between groups, and each of these variables was selected in only 1 of the 20 random seeds. Height, weight, affected trigeminal branches, and highest systolic blood pressure were excluded during initial screening due to multicollinearity; the remaining variables showed no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05).

(2) Pain and emotion-related scale scores (Table 2): MPQ (multivariate OR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.01–1.38, P = 0.036, selection frequency: 13/20 seeds). PHQ-9 score was statistically significant in univariate analysis (univariate OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.02–1.29, P = 0.043) but not in multivariate analysis (multivariate OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.97–1.24, P = 0.152); nevertheless, its selection frequency reached 4 out of 20 random seeds. VAS scores were excluded due to severe multicollinearity. The remaining scales (PFPR, PSQ, PASS, PCS, PHQ-2, GAD-7) showed no statistically significant inter-group differences (P > 0.05).

(3) Imaging characteristics (Table 3): NVC grade (multivariate OR = 3.454, 95% CI: 1.95–6.12, P < 0.001, selection frequency: 20/20 seeds) and foramen ovale area (per 0.01 cm²; multivariate OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.83–0.98, P = 0.010, selection frequency: 6/20 seeds). The remaining imaging indicators (NVC-to-brainstem distance segment, offending vessel type, brainstem compression, maximum diameter of the trigeminal porus, maximum area of Meckel’s cave, TPA, PRA, TNA, and MTA grade) showed no statistically significant intergroup differences (P > 0.05).

An exploratory subgroup analysis was performed to compare clinical characteristics, pain and emotion-related scale scores, and imaging characteristics between the analgesic-failure and analgesic-intolerance subgroups within the DRTN cohort. No statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed between subgroups in either the primary or validation cohort across these three domains (Supplementary Tables 1–3).

Calibration curves and decision curve analysis, generated based on median performance metrics across the 20 random seed replicates (Fig. 2), showed that the SVM-RBF model yielded non-significant H–L test P values in all three cohorts: training (Brier score = 0.073, H–L P = 0.250), test (Brier score = 0.087, H–L P = 0.550), and validation (Brier score = 0.124, H–L P = 0.354). SHAP analysis of the optimal model (SVM-RBF)

SHAP analysis was performed on the SVM-RBF model corresponding to the median-performing seed across the 20 random splits. In this seed, five features contributed to the model: NVC grade, MPQ, concomitant continuous pain, pain involved extent, and hypertension risk stratification; foramen ovale area, BMI, and age at first pain onset were not retained, consistent with their lower or variable selection frequencies across seeds (Tables 1, 2 and 3). The SHAP summary beeswarm plot (Fig. 3A) shows the impact and direction of each feature on model output, with features ranked from highest to lowest overall importance (top to bottom). SHAP analysis also revealed a nonlinear effect of NVC grade: when NVC grade reached 3, SHAP values increased substantially.

The feature importance ranking based on mean absolute SHAP values (Fig. 3B) was consistent with the beeswarm plot: NVC grade contributed the highest SHAP weight, followed by MPQ, concomitant continuous pain, pain involved extent, and hypertension risk stratification. Aggregated by data dimension, the imaging feature (NVC grade) contributed a mean SHAP value of 0.1691, the single scale score feature (MPQ) contributed 0.0796, and the three clinical features (pain involved extent, concomitant continuous pain, and hypertension risk stratification) collectively contributed a mean SHAP value of 0.2055.

The test cohort confusion matrix (Fig. 3C) showed: true negatives = 5, true positives = 30, false positives = 4, and false negatives = 2. The predicted probability distribution histogram (Fig. 3D) demonstrated that DRTN cases were predominantly distributed in the high-probability range (0.7–1.0). The temporally separated validation cohort confusion matrix (Fig. 3E) showed: true negatives = 3, true positives = 19, false positives = 5, and false negatives = 1. The predicted probability distribution histogram (Fig. 3F) revealed that DRTN cases were predominantly distributed in the high-probability range (0.8-1.0).

Cox model performance and time-dependent discrimination analysis

Based on the primary multivariate model (P < 0.05 univariate threshold), we compared the performance of age-unadjusted and age-adjusted Cox models in primary (n = 135) and validation (n = 28) cohorts (Table 6; Fig. 4E and J). In the primary cohort, the unadjusted and age-adjusted models yielded highly consistent C-indexes (0.644 vs. 0.648) and comparable 1-, 3-, and 5-year time-dependent AUCs, with marginal numerical differences observed between the two models (Table 6; Fig. 4F and I). Similarly, the validation cohort presented similar C-indexes (0.619 vs. 0.626) and temporal AUC profiles for unadjusted and age-adjusted models (Table 6; Fig. 3G and J). Overall, age adjustment produced negligible changes in model performance (maximal C-index difference = 0.007, maximal AUC difference = 0.034).

Furthermore, both models demonstrated moderate discriminative ability in the short to medium term (1 and 3 years), with reduced predictive performance in the long term (5 years). Kaplan–Meier and log-rank analyses confirmed significant DRTN-free survival differences between risk strata for both age-unadjusted (Fig. 4E) and age-adjusted (Fig. 4H) models, verifying the reliable risk-stratification capability of the established Cox models despite their moderate absolute discriminative power.

Discussion

This study systematically collected and integrated multi-source heterogeneous data encompassing clinical baseline characteristics, pain and emotional scale scores, and imaging measurement parameters. Leveraging machine learning algorithms and Cox regression models, we constructed and preliminarily validated an exploratory model system for predicting DRTN onset risk and onset timing, aiming to deepen the understanding of TN disease progression and provide preliminary reference for future prospective research and large-sample multicenter studies.

Multi-domain feature interpretation

The integration of heterogeneous data from multiple dimensions represents an important methodological strength of this study. In clinical practice, we frequently encounter the following dilemma: patients’ actual pain experience does not fully correspond to the severity of ipsilateral neurovascular compression (NVC), and moderate-to-severe NVC signs can also be observed in some healthy individuals. Furthermore, during regular follow-up, we observed that some patients with comparable clinical baseline profiles exhibited substantial heterogeneity in their pharmacotherapy and surgical outcomes. Although NVC is a key initiating factor in TN [1], we hypothesize that the specific disease progression and clinical manifestations are the result of multifactorial interactions. Therefore, we sought to utilize the most comprehensive multi-dimensional data available to dissect the heterogeneity of patients’ clinical symptoms and pharmacotherapy prognosis from the perspective of phenotypic characteristics.

Analysis of intergroup baseline clinical characteristics within the primary cohort identified pain involved extent, concomitant continuous pain, BMI, and age at first pain onset as independent predictors in multivariate logistic regression. Additionally, hypertension risk stratification, though not statistically significant in multivariate analysis of the primary cohort, was retained with high selection frequency (9/20 seeds) across the nested CV framework, reflecting its potential predictive effects in partial training subsets. Pain involved extent was the most stably selected clinical feature (11/20 seeds), suggesting that a broader spatial distribution of pain corresponds to more extensive nerve involvement and more severe pathological damage, with a correspondingly higher probability of pharmacotherapy failure. Concomitant continuous pain (7/20 seeds) was positively associated with DRTN risk in both univariate and multivariate analyses; however, given that only 1 of the 31 non-DRTN patients had concomitant continuous pain, the data structure approached near-complete separation, which yields unstable OR estimates with wide confidence intervals (multivariate OR = 9.40, 95% CI: 1.14–77.77). Nevertheless, the direction of this association remained consistent in SHAP analyses and retains a certain degree of reasonableness. Di Stefano et al. demonstrated through neuroimaging evidence that TN patients with concomitant continuous pain exhibit trigeminal nerve root atrophy, suggesting that continuous pain is not merely a superimposed clinical symptom but rather reflects more severe structural nerve damage and central sensitization processes [22]. Additionally, age at first pain onset (3/20 seeds) and BMI (4/20 seeds) were also independent predictive factors; we speculate that older age at onset may be associated with diminished repair capacity due to neurodegeneration [38], while elevated BMI may influence treatment response through systemic inflammatory states and alterations in pharmacokinetics [39, 40].

In the analysis of pain and emotion-related scale scores between groups within the primary cohort, MPQ score was the sole retained predictor (13/20 seeds). The MPQ quantifies pain experience across sensory, affective, and evaluative dimensions [41]; higher MPQ scores typically indicate more complex pain qualities and more pronounced central sensitization. Although the PHQ-9 (4/20 seeds) score was statistically significant in univariate analysis, it did not reach significance in multivariate analysis, suggesting that the predictive information contained in depressive symptoms may be substantially captured by the MPQ. This pattern aligns with evidence of a close interactive association between pain and depression in TN patients [24].

Analysis of imaging parameters revealed that NVC grade was the most influential predictor, being retained in all 20 seeds and ranked highest in SHAP feature importance. This finding is consistent with the well-established pathophysiological role of NVC in TN [1–3]. Maarbjerg et al. prospectively demonstrated that severe NVC causing nerve displacement or atrophy is strongly associated with the symptomatic side in classical TN [35]. The SHAP analysis in this study further revealed a nonlinear effect of NVC grade on model prediction: when NVC grade reached 3 (vascular compression causing nerve displacement/deformity/atrophy), SHAP values increased sharply, suggesting that severe compression may more readily facilitate the transition from drug responsiveness to drug resistance in TN patients, potentially reflecting that higher NVC grades often entail more severe structural nerve damage, thereby increasing the difficulty of achieving analgesia through sodium channel blocker-mediated modulation of neuronal excitability. Additionally, foramen ovale area was identified as a significant imaging predictor of DRTN. To improve clinical interpretability, the effect was rescaled to per 0.01 cm² increments. After multivariate adjustment, the OR was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.83–0.98, P = 0.010), indicating that each 0.01 cm² increase in foramen ovale area was associated with an approximately 10% reduction in the odds of DRTN. From an anatomical perspective, foramen ovale stenosis can lead to entrapment of the V3 branch during its transcranial course, resulting in chronic mechanical nerve stimulation and peripheral sensitization. Li et al. confirmed through morphometric analysis that the foramen ovale area in patients with primary trigeminal neuralgia is significantly smaller than in healthy controls, and that a narrowed foramen ovale may contribute to TN pathogenesis through persistent compression of V3 [20]. Subsequent research further identified a significant association between foramen ovale stenosis and concomitant continuous pain in TN [42], suggesting that bony entrapment and vascular compression may synergistically exacerbate nerve damage. Notably, although MVD can relieve vascular compression, it cannot alleviate bony entrapment caused by foramen ovale stenosis, which may partially explain the suboptimal postoperative outcomes in patients with concomitant foramen ovale stenosis.

Machine learning model performance analysis

Among the five candidate algorithms, RF achieved the numerically highest average AUCs across all three cohorts: 0.953 ± 0.022 (training), 0.866 ± 0.053 (test), and 0.808 ± 0.042 (validation) (Table 4). However, RF exhibited statistically significant miscalibration in both the test and validation cohorts (H-L P < 0.05), indicating systematic deviation between predicted probabilities and observed event rates. This reflects an inherent limitation of tree-based ensemble methods: the hard partitioning of decision trees drives ensemble-averaged probability estimates toward extremes, producing overconfident predictions that are poorly calibrated. As another tree-based ensemble algorithm, XGBoost also exhibited a similar but milder issue, indirectly suggesting that, given the current sample size, this limitation may be inherent to tree-based ensemble modeling.

Overall, SVM-RBF offered the most acceptable balance of discrimination and calibration among the evaluated algorithms under the present study conditions. Its validation AUC (0.806 ± 0.058) was essentially identical to that of RF (0.808 ± 0.042), and the H-L test did not reject calibration in any cohort (training P = 0.250, test P = 0.550, validation P = 0.354). Decision curve analysis indicated that SVM-RBF yielded favorable net clinical benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities in the validation cohort. This advantage stems from the capacity of the RBF kernel function to map input features into a high-dimensional Hilbert space, thereby constructing nonlinear decision boundaries in the feature space that capture complex nonlinear associations between predictor variables and the outcome. Furthermore, the discriminative advantage of the three nonlinear models (SVM-RBF, RF, XGBoost) over the two linear approaches, linear SVM (validation AUC = 0.778 ± 0.059) and logistic regression (validation AUC = 0.774 ± 0.046), indirectly corroborates that DRTN pathogenesis involves interactive effects across multi-dimensional factors.

In the validation cohort (n = 28), the SVM-RBF model at the median-performing seed correctly identified 19 of 20 DRTN cases (sensitivity = 95.0%, exact binomial 95% CI: 75.1%–99.9%). Although the model exhibited relatively high sensitivity in the present study, these performance estimates are subject to considerable uncertainty and should be interpreted as exploratory owing to the small validation sample size (n = 28) and wide confidence intervals. Moreover, 5 non-DRTN patients were misclassified as DRTN, indicating that model specificity still needs optimization and requires further evaluation in larger samples.

Cox model performance analysis

This study employed Cox regression modeling to analyze the temporal dynamics of DRTN onset, providing complementary prognostic information. Multivariate Cox regression, corroborated by a four-threshold sensitivity analysis, identified pain involved extent and MTA score as potentially independent prognostic markers of the timing of DRTN.

In the age-adjusted multivariate model, each additional affected trigeminal nerve branch was associated with a 61% increase in the hazard of DRTN onset (HR = 1.608, 95% CI: 1.109–2.333, P = 0.012). From a pathophysiological perspective, there is a plausible intrinsic association between broader pain involved and earlier DRTN onset: more extensive trigeminal nerve involvement indicates more severe nerve damage, which may accelerate the progression of TN.

The MTA score was independently associated with DRTN timing after forced adjustment for age (HR = 1.261, 95% CI: 1.048–1.518, P = 0.014), whereas age itself did not reach significance (HR = 1.020, 95% CI: 1.000–1.040, P = 0.056). In recent years, multiple TN-related neuroimaging studies have consistently demonstrated that the medial temporal lobe region—centered on the hippocampus and amygdala—is deeply involved in the affective-cognitive dimension of pain processing in TN and partially participates in the modulation of descending pain pathways [24, 26, 43–52].Medial temporal atrophy represents structural degeneration of the medial temporal brain tissue, and previous studies have confirmed its association with pain chronification, serving as a characteristic marker of adverse central neuroplastic changes [26, 53]. Therefore, we hypothesize that medial temporal lobe atrophy may compromise the central regulatory capacity for TN through multiple mechanisms, thereby accelerating the progression to DRTN.

Although the Cox models in this study demonstrated moderate discriminative capacity at the 1-year and 3-year follow-up time points, their discriminative efficacy declined when follow-up extended to 5 years. This finding is largely consistent with our expectations. The potential reasons for the time-dependent attenuation of model discriminative capacity include: (1) the multivariable Cox regression models incorporated merely 4 to 5 variables, a relatively small feature set that failed to fully capture the multi-dimensional pathogenic factors associated with long-term treatment outcomes; (2) as the follow-up period lengthens, various time-varying confounders that cannot be captured by baseline data accumulate progressively, gradually attenuating the predictive efficacy of baseline covariates. Medication adherence adjustments, dosage modifications, newly developed comorbidities, and pain phenotype evolution over the disease course were not incorporated into baseline data but may influence the trajectory of long-term pharmacotherapy response. The Cox models, relying solely on baseline indicators for modeling, cannot incorporate such dynamic changes; (3) the number of remaining subjects who developed DRTN during the later follow-up period progressively decreased, reducing the effective sample size for estimating time-dependent discriminative capacity and precluding precise determination of the model’s long-term prognostic value. Although the Cox models demonstrated limited long-term predictive capacity, the log-rank test indicated that they could preliminarily stratify patients by risk, suggesting potential utility in short-to-medium-term prognostic assessment that warrants further validation.

Limitation

This study has the following limitations that should be considered when interpreting the conclusions: (1) Prevalence enrichment and calibration concern: The DRTN prevalence in our modeling cohort was 77% (104/135), higher than the 30–50% refractoriness rate reported in real-world populations. This prevalence enrichment is primarily driven by poor follow-up adherence among patients with well-controlled pain, who were disproportionately excluded and thereby introduced bias in case distribution. Therefore, model predicted probabilities are calibrated only on this enriched high-prevalence cohort, with potential calibration drift observed upon extrapolation to routine clinical practice. Furthermore, the H-L test has limited statistical power in small sample sizes, compounding the aforementioned limitation; (2) The study design cannot completely eliminate selection bias and information bias. A total of 37 subjects across both cohorts were lost to follow-up, and the systematic exclusion of these cases may introduce attrition bias. Additionally, during follow-up, we observed that patients with satisfactory pain control and relatively stable disease were less motivated to attend follow-up visits and reluctant to undergo further examinations and medication guidance, whereas those with intractable pain were more inclined to continue seeking medical care, resulting in category imbalance in the follow-up population, which is the primary reason for the unbalanced sample sizes between groups in this study; (3) This was a single-center exploratory study with a limited sample size, and all cases were sourced from a single institution. As such, although the validation and modeling cohorts were separated by enrollment period, systematic bias between the two cannot be fully excluded. Consequently, while these findings carry preliminary reference value, they should be interpreted with caution. Future large-sample, multicenter cohort studies are warranted to more precisely evaluate model performance across different follow-up intervals and to validate the generalizability of these results; (4) The existing measurement precision and feature selection strategy have inherent limitations. Although the variables included in this study are relatively comprehensive in dimension, all selected features are conventional phenotypic indicators; particularly regarding imaging parameters, conventional imaging indicators cannot capture tissue microstructural and functional information compared with radiomics, deep learning, voxel-based morphometry (VBM), source-based morphometry (SBM), and functional MRI (fMRI) and other high-precision methods and techniques. Furthermore, this study adopted relatively stringent screening methods, which may have excluded variables with potential predictive value that did not individually reach statistical significance. In summary, we retrospectively reviewed and analyzed the data from cases that the model failed to correctly classify or predict, and identified some interesting associations. Therefore, in future studies, we will consider leveraging the aforementioned high-precision methods and techniques to address the limited and time-attenuating discriminative capacity of Cox regression models.

Conclusion

This study integrated multi-source heterogeneous data encompassing clinical baseline characteristics, pain and emotional scale scores, and imaging measurement parameters to construct and validate prediction models for DRTN onset risk and onset timing. The SVM-RBF model based on the radial basis function kernel demonstrated the best overall performance in DRTN risk prediction, and SHAP analysis revealed NVC grade as a key driving feature. Cox regression analysis identified pain involved extent and MTA score as significant, independent candidate prognostic markers for DRTN onset timing, though the time-dependent discriminative capacity of the models was limited. These findings provide preliminary evidence for early risk stratification and individualized treatment planning in TN patients receiving pharmacotherapy; however, given the limited long-term predictive capability of the Cox models and the single-center exploratory design, these results should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with large-sample, multi-center prospective cohorts are needed to further validate these conclusions and to explore superior survival time prediction modeling strategies.
