Effect of obesity on endovenous ablation outcomes in the Vascular Quality Initiative
Authors: Scott Levy, Patrick L Johnson, Christina M Fleischer, Joy Atwell, Gatlin Brissette, Kaycee Henderson, Nicholas H Osborne, Craig S Brown, Andrea T Obi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a known risk factor for chronic venous insufficiency in both incidence and disease severity. Limited previous work suggests that obesity is associated with worse outcomes after endovenous ablation. We analyzed patients who received truncal endovenous ablation with or without concomitant phlebectomy to better understand the relationship between obesity and postoperative outcomes.MethodsWe evaluated short- and long-term clinical and patient-reported outcomes among patients who underwent truncal endovenous ablation with and without phlebectomy in the Vascular Quality Initiative Varicose Vein Registry with body mass index (BMI) as the primary explanatory variable. Our primary outcome measures were improvement in revised Venous Clinical Severity Score (rVCSS) and total symptom score. Secondary outcomes included complications and recanalization of target vein. Pre- and postprocedural comparisons were performed using bivariate statistics, and multivariable logistic regression models were used to assess for confounding.ResultsFrom January 2015 to October 2025, we identified 10,494 adult patients (12,955 limbs) who underwent truncal endovenous ablation with either laser or radiofrequency ablation. Patients were 68.6% female, with a mean BMI of 29.9 kg/m2. A total of 51.2% of patients underwent concomitant phlebectomy. Median follow-up was 60 days (interquartile range, 16-133). Patients with higher BMI had higher preoperative rVCSS and total symptoms scores. The predicted probability of improvement in rVCSS and total symptom score was >92% for patients undergoing endovenous ablation with phlebectomy. BMI was not associated with increased rates of procedure-related complications or long-term follow-up complications [adjusted odds ratio (aOR), 1.12; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.89-1.41; P = .34; aOR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.92-1.08; P = .98, respectively for five-point change in BMI]. In patients receiving ablation alone, BMI was associated with increased risk of recanalization (aOR for five-point change in BMI, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.06-1.5; P = .009)ConclusionsDespite patients with higher BMI having worse preoperative disease, patients sustain very high rates of improvement across the BMI spectrum. Further, higher BMI was not associated with increased rates of complications. The addition of phlebectomy in patients with higher BMI may improve outcomes and reduce likelihood of recanalization. Given the difficulty of achieving meaningful weight loss, delaying venous procedures until weight loss is achieved may prevent patients from receiving indicated and effective treatment.Keywords: Obesity, Venous disease, Chronic venous insufficiency

Patients with obesity disproportionately suffer from and experience worse symptoms related to chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) leading to ambiguity surrounding treatment decisions in this population.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Endovenous intervention is often indicated for the treatment of CVI, and the outcomes have been shown to have high success rates with cost effective, durable results.6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 Recent data have shown that obesity may negatively affect outcomes after endovenous ablation, adding increased controversy to treatment decisions.14

Despite the significant work demonstrating high-quality outcomes after endovenous ablation, it is unclear whether patients with obesity experience worse postoperative outcomes after endovenous intervention. Several studies have evaluated different ablative techniques and how obesity effects recanalization rates, with results that vary by ablative technique.15,16 The few studies that have attempted to further evaluate clinical outcomes either included single-center analyses or used flawed methodological approaches, with varied results.14,17 The larger of these two studies did not account for the worse baseline disease severity in patients with obesity.14 These conclusions could result in discriminatory changes in practice pattern or insurance eligibility for obese patients.

In this context, we sought to further explain how body mass index (BMI) was related to both clinician- and patient-reported outcomes (PRO) after endovenous ablation. We used the Vascular Quality Initiative (VQI) Varicose Vein Registry (VVR), a national, multicenter registry to evaluate the short- and long-term outcomes of patients who received truncal endovenous ablation, with BMI as the primary explanatory variable to better understand the relationship between higher BMI and postoperative outcomes.

Methods

Data source and study population

The VQI VVR is maintained by the Society for Vascular Surgery and tracks data on superficial venous procedures. The data are prospectively collected and retrospectively reviewed for this study. The study included patients >18 years old with at least symptomatic Clinical, Etiologic, Anatomic, and Pathophysiologic (CEAP) clinical class C2 disease who underwent radiofrequency or laser ablation of lower-extremity truncal veins using between January 2015 and October 2025. Repeat ablation procedures on the same leg and staged ablation with phlebectomy were excluded. Given the known difference in outcomes between patients receiving ablation with and without phlebectomy, we analyzed two cohorts separately: patients undergoing ablation alone and those undergoing ablation with phlebectomy.18, 19, 20 Data collection procedures for the VVR have been described previously.21, 22, 23, 24

This study was reviewed by our university's institutional review board and waived the need for review and informed consent for this exempt analysis. Findings were reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.25

Study variables

For each case, initial reporting included patient demographics and clinical characteristics including history of previous varicose vein treatment, compliance with compression therapy, presence of deep reflux, history of any anticoagulation treatment, CEAP C classification, and revised Venous Clinical Severity Score (rVCSS) for both legs, as well as procedure-related data.26,27 BMI was calculated from height and weight recorded in the VQI database. For the unadjusted analysis, BMI was categorized into five-point increments from <20 to >50 kg/m2; however, BMI was used as a continuous variable for multivariable analysis. Follow-up includes an appointment within 3 and >3 months of the procedure. Data collected included the treated leg's CEAP C classification and rVCSS, along with clinical outcomes including complications and quality of life (QOL) survey responses. All analyses of postoperative outcomes used late follow-up data. For patients undergoing bilateral procedures, pre- and postoperative scores represent the average of the two treated legs' scores. Pre- and postoperative CEAP C class for bilateral procedures was taken as the maximum of the two legs. For patients undergoing bilateral-staged procedures, follow-up time was calculated as the average between the two procedures.

Patients also completed a QOL survey before the procedure and at follow-up. This assessment is a modification of the validated HASTI (Heaviness, Achiness, Swelling, Throbbing, Itching) utility and has been previously described.24,28 This survey includes seven parameters: heaviness, achiness, swelling, throbbing, itching, appearance, and work impact and asks the participant to grade each parameter on an ordinal scale. The complete PRO scale is included in Supplementary Table I (online only). The total possible scores range from 0 to 34. Total symptom score for patients undergoing bilateral procedures represents the average of the two legs.

Complications were collected at the time of the procedure and follow-up. The complete list of complications is listed in Supplementary Table II (online only).

Statistical analysis

We divided the analysis into two subgroups: patients receiving ablation alone and those undergoing ablation with phlebectomy. Demographics, procedural, and clinical characteristics are described using summary statistics. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance, presented as means with standard deviation (SD). Non-normally distributed continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test, presented as median with interquartile range. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test, presented as frequency counts with percentages. Between group comparisons of pre-, post-, and change in scores as well as complications were performed using Mann-Whitney U and Fisher exact test, respectively. Absolute change in rVCSS and total symptom scores were calculated as the preprocedural score minus the postprocedural score. “Improvement” was assigned a binary result, whether the postoperative score was less than the preoperative score.

We used logistic regression to estimate the association between BMI and (1) improvement in rVCSS, (2) improvement in total symptom score, and (3) rates of recanalization for the two cohorts. Covariates included were BMI, year, age, gender, race, previous varicose vein treatment, history of deep vein thrombosis (DVT), preoperative anticoagulant use, deep reflux presence, preoperative CEAP C class, rVCSS and total symptom score, preoperative compression use compliance and concomitant sclerotherapy. Predictive margins were calculated using model parameters at fixed BMI values to illustrate the change in probability of improvement across the BMI spectrum.29

Using the entire population, logistic regression was used to estimate the effect of BMI and phlebectomy on (1) procedure-related complications and 2) long-term follow-up (LTF) complications. Complications were analyzed at the patient level. For bilateral procedures, side-specific complication data were combined, with a complication considered present if it occurred on either limb. Given the low incidence of complications and large amount of missing data for LTF complications, fewer covariates were included in this analysis. Covariates included were BMI, concomitant phlebectomy, age, gender, previous varicose vein treatment, history of DVT, preoperative anticoagulant use, deep reflux presence, preoperative CEAP C classification, rVCSS and total symptom scores, preoperative compression compliance, and concomitant sclerotherapy. Predictive margins were calculated for the regressions that revealed an association with BMI and/or phlebectomy.

The alpha level was set at 5%. Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC) was used for all analyses.

Results

Study population

Of the 10,494 patients who met inclusion criteria for the study, 7195 patients (68.6%) were female, 2461 patients (23.5%) had bilateral procedures completed for a total of 12,955 limbs included for analysis. A total of 5369 patients underwent concomitant phlebectomy, making up just over half of the total population. The mean age was 56.2 years (13.8 SD), and the mean BMI was 29.9 kg/m2 (6.9 SD) with a median of 28.8. The mean preoperative rVCSS was 7.8 (3.2 SD), and the mean preoperative total symptom score was 14.0 (6.2 SD). The median follow-up was 60 days (interquartile range, 16-133). Baseline CEAP C classification distribution differed by BMI category, with higher BMI categories having a greater proportion of patients with CEAP C classification >3. Perioperative changes in clinical scores and rates of recanalization

Patients receiving ablation with phlebectomy had similar absolute change in rVCSS scores up to BMI < 50 kg/m2 (median five points), with BMI ≥ 50 kg/m2 showing a modest decline in absolute change (median of four; P < .0001, across BMI). Similarly, median absolute change in total symptom score was consistent up to BMI <50 kg/m2 (ranging from 11 for BMI <25 to 12 for BMI 45-50 kg/m2) with a decrease in the absolute change for BMI > 50 kg/m2 (median eight; P < .0001, across BMI). Among patients receiving ablation alone, absolute change in rVCSS and total symptom score was lower overall, but there were no significant differences across all BMI categories (P = .36 and .83, respectively). These findings are represented in Fig 1. There were significant differences between treatment groups across the BMI categories (Supplementary Fig, online only).

Overall recanalization rates were low, although a higher rate was seen in the ablation with phlebectomy group vs ablation alone (1.29% vs 1.17%; P < .001). On unadjusted analysis, there was no association between BMI and recanalization in the ablation with phlebectomy group (P = .115). However, recanalization rates in the ablation alone group varied across BMI categories, ranging from 1.03% to 1.9% at BMI < 25 to 40 kg/m2 and from 1.56% to 5.17% at BMI 40 to 50 kg/m2 (P = .025).

Incidence of postprocedural complications

Complications were rare (Supplementary Table II, online only). The overall rate of procedural complications was 0.3%. Both groups experienced low rates of procedure-related complications, and there was no difference in unadjusted rates (0.2% vs 0.4%; P = .07 for ablation with phlebectomy and ablation alone, respectively). Data on LTF complications were available for approximately 50% of the patients. The ablation with phlebectomy group had higher rates of complication at LTF (9.9% vs 7.7%; P = .007). Patients undergoing ablation with phlebectomy experienced higher rates of DVT (1.6% vs 0.8%; P = .01), hematoma (1.4% vs 0.1%; P < .0001) and pigmentation changes (1.3% vs 0.4%; P = .0004) at follow-up. Conversely, phlebitis was more common in the ablation alone group (2.3% vs 0.9%; P < .0001), as was proximal thrombus extension (2.3% vs 1.4%; P = .01).

Multivariable analysis

Improvement in rVCSS

BMI was not associated with reduced odds of improvement in rVCSS in the ablation with phlebectomy group [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) for five-point change in BMI, 0.95; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.85-1.05; P = .31]. BMI was associated with reduced odds of improvement in rVCSS in the ablation alone group (aOR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.86-0.96; P = .001). This corresponded to 1.3-percentage-point reduction in the probability of improvement per five-point increase in BMI, with an absolute difference of 7.9-percentage points across the observed BMI range (76.9% at BMI 20 vs 69.0% at BMI 50 kg/m2). The probabilities of improvement were >93% for all BMI categories in the ablation with phlebectomy group and ≥69% for all BMI categories in the ablation alone group. Improvement in total symptom score

The odds of symptom improvement after intervention were not associated with BMI in either subgroup (aOR for each five-point change in BMI, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.83-1.01; P = .07 and aOR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.90-1.02; P = .21 for ablation with phlebectomy and ablation alone, respectively). The probabilities for symptom improvement remained high across the full range of BMI categories for both subgroups (>92% and >83% for ablation with phlebectomy and ablation alone, respectively). These results are summarized in Fig 2.

Recanalization

BMI was not associated with recanalization in the ablation with phlebectomy group (aOR, 1.15; 95% CI, 0.96-1.39; P = .13 for five-point change in BMI). In contrast, BMI was associated with increased rates of recanalization in the ablation alone group (aOR for five-point change in BMI, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.06-1.5; P = .009), with probabilities rising from 0.89% at BMI 20 kg/m2 to 3.27% at BMI 50 kg/m2.

Complications

BMI was not associated with procedure-related or LTF complications (aOR, 1.12; 95% CI, 0.89-1.41; P = .34; aOR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.92-1.08; P = .98, respectively for five-point change in BMI). Phlebectomy was not associated with procedural complications (aOR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.31-1.28; P = .20) although phlebectomy was associated with LTF complications (aOR, 1.40; 95% CI, 1.12-1.75; P = .003), corresponding to an absolute increase of 2.63-percentage points in the predicted probability of LTF complication (average marginal effect, 2.63; 95% CI, 0.93-4.33).

Discussion

This analysis of VQI VVR data has three principal findings regarding the effects of BMI on outcomes after endovenous ablation for CVI. First, there was sustained probability of improvement of >92% in rVCSS and total symptom score across BMI in the ablation with phlebectomy group. Second, complication rates were low, and there was no association between BMI and rates of complications. Finally, BMI did not impact the rates of recanalization in the ablation with phlebectomy group; however, BMI was associated with higher rates of recanalization in the ablation alone group. These findings show that patients with obesity experience similar outcomes and have similar complication profiles, with a potential benefit from the addition of phlebectomy for patients with higher BMI.

Our work adds clarity to previous work evaluating the impact of BMI on outcomes after endovenous ablation. The landmark trials that resulted in the shift from surgical stripping to endovenous ablation as first-line therapy for CVI did not address how outcomes differ based on patient characteristics.9,11 As endovenous ablation has become the norm, many groups have sought to evaluate how patient factors affect outcomes. Previous work leveraged the Center for Vein Restoration electronic medical record system to report on the effect of obesity on outcomes after endovenous ablation.14 Deol et al14 reported on over 65,000 patients across BMI categories undergoing thermal ablation with or without phlebectomy and ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy. They found that increased BMI was associated with decreased improvement in postoperative rVCSS as well as QOL. Importantly, this group reported outcomes as relative improvement. We know from previous data that patients with higher BMI have worse baseline disease.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 Reporting outcomes as a relative improvement downplays the degree of improvement in the patients with worse baseline disease as the denominator (preoperative score) is higher. Obese patients may have the exact same, or even higher, improvement in their scores compared to nonobese patients but the relative improvement would be less. Bossart et al17 also investigated BMI and the effects on clinical outcomes after endovenous ablation. This group found no association between BMI and efficacy, measured by ultrasound evidence of the target vein occlusion.17 Although this work is needed, it is a small study that failed to adequately report the clinical outcomes recommended by current guidelines.30 In our study, despite BMI being associated with a decreased probability of improvement in rVCSS in the ablation alone group, the small (1.3%) absolute reduction observed per five-point increase in BMI is most likely clinically irrelevant. When faced with a patient with a higher BMI, providers can say with confidence that despite patients with lower BMI having a modestly higher probability of improvement, even patients at the highest BMI have high likelihood of success (>69% probability). Our data show that the probability of improvement in rVCSS was largely maintained across all BMI categories, most pronounced in the ablation with phlebectomy group (Fig 2). We found no association between BMI and probability of improvement in total symptom score across all BMI categories (Fig 2). Clinical guidelines recommend ablation therapy for those failing conservative management based on clinical trial data showing the modest improvement in rVCSS and QOL scores.31,32 Given these recommendations and our results, even patients at the highest BMI categories should be considered for endovenous ablation with or without phlebectomy for symptomatic CVI, although phlebectomy may provide additional benefit.

Previous work has investigated the effect of BMI on postoperative complications. Bossart et al17 reported no association between BMI and complications, though the sample size was small. This group evaluated major and minor bleeding, infections and thromboembolic events. Our work expands on this, including more patients for analysis and capturing both immediate procedure-related complications and LTF complications. We found that complications were, in general, rare after these procedures and that higher BMI did not increase the risk of experiencing any complication. We do report that concomitant phlebectomy was associated with increased rates of late complications, with complications occurring in 9.9% of patients with available data. This is counter to a recent meta-analysis by Aherne et al33 that reported that concomitant or staged phlebectomy was not associated with increased complications. This difference may be explained by the larger sample size in the meta-analysis. Our data may aid in decision making when faced with the decision on whether to perform ablation with phlebectomy in a patient with higher BMI, as we show there is no increased risk of complications for patients with higher BMI.

Regarding rates of recanalization, we show that BMI was not associated with higher rates of recanalization in the ablation with phlebectomy group. Recanalization has been shown to increase symptom recurrence and need for secondary intervention.34 Pisharody et al16 recently reported that BMI was associated with higher rates of recanalization after mechanochemical ablation for CVI. In a separate study by Ahmed et al,15 the authors reported that BMI had no effect on rates of recanalization after endothermal ablation. Our study, which only included patients undergoing thermal ablation, adds nuance to the findings of Ahmed et al15 as we conducted our analysis with a larger, multicenter cohort of patients. Our data also suggest a potential benefit of the addition of phlebectomy in patients with higher BMI, given the findings that, without phlebectomy, higher BMI incurred additional risk of recanalization. Given this, patients with higher BMI may benefit from the addition of phlebectomy to reduce the risk of recanalization.

Although we show that patients have high probability of improvement in rVCSS and total symptom score across the BMI spectrum, further studies are needed to define what the clinically meaningful difference is for treating CVI. Significant effort has resulted in validated clinical scoring systems and current guidelines stress appropriate follow-up and reporting of both anatomic and clinical outcomes but these guidelines fail to state what defines a successful surgery.26,27,30,32,35,36 Aurshina et al37 showed that recanalization is associated with symptom recurrence but, in this same study, 34% of patients with recanalized target veins did not have symptom recurrence, suggesting that anatomic outcomes are not the sole predictor of clinical outcomes. As venous disease is a disease that largely effects QOL, the argument could be made that patient QOL scores should be the primary outcome of interest. Future work should be focused on defining what the clinically meaningful difference is in all patients. Given the known association between BMI and larger vein diameter and the suggestion that larger veins require more energy for treatment, another area for future investigation is to determine whether patients with higher BMI require higher energy to achieve satisfactory outcomes.38,39

This study has limitations. First, recent data suggest that BMI is an imperfect measure and fails to accurately define obesity on the patient level.40 We acknowledge this, and our dataset does not include the anthropomorphic data needed to better define obesity as recommended; therefore, we elected to use BMI. We also acknowledge that we are determining our outcomes based on physician and PROs, which are accepted approaches but it is unclear how these translate to QOL improvements.24,27,28 Regarding long-term outcomes, median follow-up was 60 days, making our study limited to comment on long-term outcomes. As with all observational studies, there is a possibility of uncontrolled confounding. The VVR is made up purely of patients undergoing a procedure and, therefore does not contain information for patients who did not undergo treatment. This may introduce bias into our dataset by not including patients who were considered not to be surgical candidates. The VVR does not collect data on technical success which could introduce further bias into our measurement of outcomes. We have previously described this potential effect.24 We also had significant missing data at follow-up for complications. For example, approximately 50% of the patients did not have complication data at LTF (Supplementary Table II, online only). We cannot be sure whether these patients did not have complications or whether it is true missing data. The patients without data at LTF were not included in our analysis of the complications and this lowers the power of the statistical analysis of complications. Further, as with all complication data collection, patients with complications may be over-represented and those without may be under-represented, making the true assessment of complication rates to be difficult. Finally, the VVR represents a national sample of patients undergoing treatment of venous disease, and our results therefore represent a comprehensive assessment of outcomes in relation to BMI among patients undergoing truncal endovenous ablation. Despite the number of patients included in the VVR, this study includes only data on patients undergoing endovenous procedures at participating centers in the United States. There may be biases in the way that centers included in the VVR include patients across the BMI spectrum, but previous studies of patients included in the VVR have not suggested significantly different outcomes compared with patients outside the VVR.22,41,42 Despite these limitations, the question of the effect of BMI on outcomes after truncal endovenous ablation remains a clinically important question and VQI VVR database offers a real-world platform to effectively answer this question across a broad spectrum of practice patterns and patients.

Conclusions

Our nationally representative study has implications for the treatment of CVI in patients with obesity. Although previous data suggests that increasing BMI is associated with worse outcomes after endovenous ablation, our findings demonstrate sustained probability of improvement in both PRO and physician-reported outcomes across BMI, with no higher likelihood of complications. Given the overall higher likelihood of improvement and decreased probability of recanalization with the addition of phlebectomy, it may be warranted to consider phlebectomy in patients with higher BMI. In conclusion, that truncal endovenous ablation is a safe and effective procedure across all BMI categories. This suggests that patients should not be deferred or delayed surgery based on BMI alone.
