The Effect of the Coronal Alignment of Extension Stem on Component Displacement and Stress During Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Finite Element Study
Authors: Lingce Kong, Xingkai Wang, Huijun Kang, Zhenhui Huo, Ming Li, Fei Wang, Wei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe biomechanical interaction between coronal alignment and short extension stem use in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains insufficiently understood, particularly regarding the balance between tibial component stability and stress shielding. This study aims to investigate the effects of varying bone cut angles on the tibial side and the use of a short extension stem on tibial component displacement and stress distribution. Additionally, it will examine the stresses experienced by both cortical and cancellous bone during primary TKA, employing three‐dimensional finite element analysis and elucidating the biomechanical effects of combining kinematic alignment (KA) with a short extension stem in TKA.MethodsA healthy adult female volunteer was selected to create a tibial model of the left knee joint utilizing three‐dimensional reconstruction and finite element analysis software. Six finite element models were developed based on a standard tibial plateau prosthesis and a tibial prosthesis with a 30 mm short extension stem. These models were categorized according to mechanical alignment (MA), KA, and a 3° valgus alignment. The simulations were designed to replicate the loads and constraints experienced during weight‐bearing activities of the lower limb. Subsequently, we analyzed the displacement and stress distribution of the tibial prosthesis and examined the stress distribution within both the tibial cortical bone and cancellous bone.ResultIn the KA model, there was a notable increase in displacement, an elevation in mean cortical bone Von Mises stress, and a reduction in mean component Von Mises stress when compared to both the MA and the 3° valgus alignment model. The design of the short extension stem effectively decreased the displacement of the prosthesis but led to increased stress on the prosthesis itself. Additionally, this design led to a reduction in stress on the cortical bone, suggesting a more pronounced stress‐shielding effect.ConclusionThe application of KA in TKA will increase prosthetic displacement and reduce the stress‐shielding effect, while the application of short extension stem will reduce prosthetic displacement and increase the stress‐shielding effect. The combination of the KA and short extension stem may play a complementary role, but this needs further research to confirm.Keywords: finite element analysis, kinematic alignment, mechanical alignment, short extension stem, total knee arthroplasty

Introduction

Prosthesis loosening after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a serious postoperative complication, second only to periprosthetic infection as a cause of surgical failure [1, 2, 3, 4]. A substantial body of research indicates that loosening of the prosthesis frequently occurs on the tibial side, with coronal alignment playing a critical role in influencing the rate of loosening in this region [5, 6, 7].

Mechanical alignment (MA) has traditionally been regarded as the standard principle for TKA. This principle underscores the importance of performing osteotomies that are perpendicular to the force axes of the femur and tibia, leading to a neutrally aligned knee [8, 9]. The MA technique promotes an even distribution of pressure across both the medial and lateral compartments of the joint, thereby reducing the risk of wear and loosening of the prosthesis. Nevertheless, this technique has inherent limitations. It modifies the natural anatomy of the knee, which can disrupt the physiological balance of the ligaments and the kinematic characteristics of the joint. This alteration can change the tension of the soft tissues and disrupt the original kinematic balance, ultimately affecting the improvement of the patient's function and symptoms [10].

In recent years, kinematic alignment (KA) has been proposed as a method to restore the pre‐osteoarthritis anatomical morphology of the patient's knee by performing osteotomies parallel to the articular surfaces [11, 12]. However, the KA in TKA does not position the articular line of the prosthesis exactly perpendicular to the force axis, which can result in a slight varus or valgus alignment of the prosthesis. Although studies indicate that KA‐TKA does not lead to a higher rate of loosening of the tibial side of the prosthesis in the short term compared to MA‐TKA [13, 14], the biomechanical effects of the mechanical axis offset on the contact surface between the prosthesis and the bone remain unclear.

The design of the tibial prosthesis with a short extension stem has been shown to effectively distribute stress at the prosthesis‐bone interface, maintain prosthesis stability, and reduce the likelihood of prosthesis loosening [15]. However, the distal end of the extension stem may have some degree of displacement relative to the tibia, resulting in localized stress concentrations and end pain [16, 17]. The use of short‐extension stems in clinical practice has gradually increased in recent years, and the clinical efficacy is satisfactory. However, most clinical reports are about the MA technique, and there are few reports on whether KA‐TKA is advantageous in reducing stress on the prosthetic plateau and tibial when a short extension stem is used, and which alignment method is more beneficial when combined with an extension stem. Currently, there is no biomechanical study that systematically compares the combined effects of different alignments and short extension stems on the trade‐off between prosthesis stability and stress shielding. This gap hinders surgeons from selecting the optimal alignment and prosthesis combination for patients with varying alignments. Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to systematically evaluate the biomechanical effects of coronal alignment and short extension stem use in primary TKA. Specifically, this study aimed to: (i) compare tibial component displacement among MA, kinematic alignment, and 3° valgus alignment models; (ii) analyze the effects of a 30‐mm short extension stem on tibial component stability and stress distribution at the prosthesis–bone interface; and (iii) evaluate changes in cortical and cancellous bone stress under different alignment and prosthesis design conditions, thereby clarifying the biomechanical trade‐off between component stability and stress shielding. We hypothesized that KA more effectively transmits stress to the distal cortical bone, whereas short extension stems primarily reduce prosthesis displacement.

Materials and Methods

Modeling of the Tibia

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (Ke2024‐080‐1), and informed consent was obtained from the participant and her legal guardians. To produce a 3D finite element model, we imported the Computed tomography (CT) data of the left tibia of a healthy adult female volunteer into Mimics 21.0 (Materialize Company, Leuven, Belgium) software. CT was performed with a 64‐slice CT scanner (SOMATOM Sensation Cardiac 64, Siemens Medical Solutions, Germany). Sixty‐four overlapping 0.6 mm slices per rotation were acquired with the use of a focal spot periodically moving in the longitudinal direction (z‐flying focal spot, 0.6 mm collimation and 330 milliseconds gantry rotation time). In the global coordinate system, the long axis of the tibia was the line connecting the center of the ankle joint and the midpoint of the lateral and medial tibial condyles, which was defined as the z axis. The transverse plane (xy‐plane) was perpendicular to the long axis. To simplify the preparation of the models, the fibula was ignored. Threshold segmentation and 3D model calculation were used to build a 3D model of the tibia, and we exported the generated solid as an STL file. The file was imported into Geomagic 2021 (3D Systems Inc., North Carolina, USA) software, the mesh model was adjusted uniformly, and the optimized bone tissue model was obtained by smoothing and surfacing. Subsequently, the mesh model was imported into Solidworks 2021 (Dassault Systems SolidWorks Corp., Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) software to model the osteotomies at different angles. This study primarily focused on the biomechanics of the tibial side; therefore, the femoral component was not included in the model. This simplification is widely adopted in finite element studies investigating stress/displacement of the tibial prosthesis. During static standing, body weight is transmitted to the tibia through the tibial component [18]. The influence of the femoral component on tibial load distribution is mainly reflected in the mediolateral mechanical distribution, which has been incorporated into our loading configuration. The original tibia exhibited 3° of varus alignment. Bone cut was performed along its native joint line using the inverse KA approach. Correspondingly, a valgus bone cut of 3° was applied in the valgus model. The bone cut line of MA was perpendicular to the tibial mechanical axis; the bone cut line of valgus alignment was modeled by the 3° eversion rotations of the MA cut planes described above. KA bone cuts are represented by 3° varus alignment. According to previous literature, the bone cut line of KA was modeled by the 3° inward rotations of the MA cut planes [19]. In total, three alignments of tibial osteotomies were modeled (Figure 1). The three‐dimensional 10‐noded tetrahedral element was used for modeling. In order to ensure the convergence of displacement and stress, the mesh size of the stress region and contact region in the model was refined to the minimum of 1 mm, and the mesh independency was checked. The convergence tolerance of the contact analysis was set to 1%. Due to the different bone cut directions of each model and the differences in the use of prostheses, the number of mesh nodes in the full model ranged from 865,237 to 873,253, while the number of elements ranged from 586,441 to 590,870.

Modeling of Tibial Prostheses

Two different types of tibial prostheses were selected (Figure 2): (1) Legion (Smith & Nephew, America) with a 30‐mm short extension stem and (2) the Triathlon (Stryker, America) with a standard tibial plateau. Both prostheses were cobalt‐chromium alloy tibial components widely used in clinical practice, with similar articular surface geometries. This design eliminated potential confounding effects of inherent prosthetic design differences on stress/displacement outcomes, allowing for isolated investigation of the stem influence. The 3D geometry of the prostheses was acquired using reverse engineering with a laser scanner. An unused prosthesis (provided by the manufacturer) was scanned to avoid geometric errors caused by wear. The scanned point cloud was processed in Geomagic 2021 to generate an STL model, with a reconstruction accuracy of less than 0.05 mm, and subsequently assembled according to the manufacturer's guidelines. The tibial prosthesis was located at the geometric center of the tibia with a 3° posterior inclination. Since a motion model was not used, the profile above the spacer could be omitted and replaced with a platform. Based on two types of prostheses and the three bone cuts angles, the final model included the following six: MA‐without stem, MA‐short extension stem, KA‐without stem, KA‐short extension stem, 3° valgus‐without stem, and 3° valgus‐short extension stem.

Schematic diagram of the coronal position of the two tibial prostheses. (A) Legion (Smith &Nephew, America) with a 30‐mm short extension stem. (B) Triathlon (Stryker, America) with a standard tibial plateau.

Contacts, Material Properties, and Load Settings

The assembled model was imported into Ansys 2021R1 (SwansonAnalysis Systems Inc., Houston, Pennsylvania, USA) software for meshing and finite element simulation. Face‐to‐face contact was used between all components. A frictional contact with a coefficient of friction of 0.2 was applied between the prosthesis and bone. A bonded contact was used between the spacer and the prosthesis. The material properties of each component were shown in Table 1.

In the simulation, the distal tibia was constrained in all degrees of freedom. The maximum load used in the analysis was the maximum physiological load used in the previously described experiments [19, 20, 21]. The applied load in this study was set to three times the total body weight (70 kg × 3 = 2100 N), which corresponds to the peak vertical force during the gait cycle. MA, KA, and valgus alignment primarily affect the medial–lateral load distribution rather than the total vertical load; therefore, the same load setting was adopted. Based on the description of previous studies [22], the loads were set as follows: for the KA tibial bone cuts, the medial stress was 65% and the lateral stress was 35%; for the MA tibial bone cuts, the medial stress was 50% and the lateral stress was 50%; and for the external 3° tibial bone cuts, the medial stress was 35% and the lateral stress was 65%. Regarding the force site, in agreement with previously described experiments [20], it was the transverse diameter of the tibial prosthesis across the center of the medial‐lateral plateau, perpendicular to the upper surface of the prosthesis (Figure 3). The models were analyzed to compare prosthetic displacement, and the distribution and mean values of von Mises stress in the overall prosthesis as well as in the tibial cortical bone and tibial cancellous bone at the bone–prosthesis interface.

Prosthesis Displacement

Figure 4 shows the tibial prosthesis displacement for all models. The periprosthetic portion was displaced the most. The medial plateau edge showed subsidence due to medial loading. In contrast, the lateral plateau edge showed elevation. In particular, for the models of bone cuts from different angles, all MA osteotomies showed the least prosthesis displacement, while the KA osteotomies showed the most unstable maximum displacement. For different prosthesis types, models using prostheses with short extension stems exhibited less prosthesis displacement compared to conventional prostheses without stems. The prosthesis displacement of the KA‐short extension stem was reduced by 6.4% compared with the KA‐without stem model.

Prosthesis Stress Distribution

Figure 5 shows the von Mises stress distribution at the bone contact surface of the tibial prosthesis across all models. The stresses in the two models of the MA were more evenly distributed between the inner and outer sides; the stresses in the two models of the KA were mainly concentrated beneath the medial plateau of the prosthesis; and the stresses in the two models of the valgus were mainly concentrated below the lateral plateau of the prosthesis. Comparisons between bone cuts models with different angles showed that KA models exhibited relatively less prosthetic stress. However, this phenomenon was only more pronounced in the model with the short extension stem, and the difference was smaller in the conventional prosthesis without a stem. The prosthesis stress of the KA‐short extension stem was reduced by 6% compared to the MA‐short extension stem model.

Cortical and Cancellous Bone Stress Distribution

Figure 6 shows the von Mises stress distribution of the tibial cortical bone and cancellous bone across all models. The nephograms of the overall loading of the models show that the von Mises stress values were relatively high in the posterior tibial region, especially in the cortical bone at the edge of the contact point between the prosthesis and the tibia. The cancellous bone stress was also concentrated in the posterior region adjacent to the tibial cortex bone and the insertion part of the prosthesis, and with the change of the bone cuts angle of varus and valgus, the concentration of the cancellous bone stress was shifted to the medial and lateral sides as well. At the same cut angle, the short extension stem had less tibial cortical bone stress than without the stem. In the model with the same prosthesis, the varus bone cuts had greater cortical bone stress, whereas the difference between the MA bone cuts and the valgus bone cuts was small. For the distribution of stresses in cancellous bone, the use of a short extension stem resulted in a relative increase in mean cancellous bone stresses. The KA‐short extension stem had a 21% increase in cortical bone stress and a 5.4% decrease in cancellous bone stress compared to the MA‐short extension stem.

Discussion

Prosthetic loosening following TKA is primarily driven by abnormal stress alterations and micromotion at the bone–implant interface. Accordingly, optimization of coronal alignment and tibial component design is critical for improving long‐term outcomes. This study employed finite element analysis to systematically compare the biomechanical effects of three coronal alignments and two tibial component designs on prosthesis displacement and stress distribution. Our key finding—that the KA technique increases prosthetic displacement but reduces stress shielding, whereas the short extension stem reduces displacement but increases stress shielding—is consistent with the fundamental biomechanical principles of TKA and is further supported by recent clinical and biomechanical evidence, thereby providing a theoretical basis for personalized surgical planning.

Biomechanical Effects of Different Coronal Alignments

Precise prosthesis placement, proper lower extremity alignment and full grasp of surgical indications are crucial for reducing postoperative complications, extending prosthesis longevity, and improving long‐term TKA outcomes [24, 25, 26]. KA aims to restore the native joint line and anatomical structure in osteoarthritis, which in our model demonstrated distinct biomechanical characteristics. Compared to MA and 3° valgus alignment, KA significantly increased maximum prosthesis displacement, but reduced mean prosthesis stress and increased mean tibial cortical bone stress. These findings are consistent with recent studies highlighting the advantages of KA in stress optimization. Nakamura et al. [27] have investigated the biomechanical differences between KA‐TKA and MA‐TKA in patients with knee varus and found that tibial stresses were more concentrated under the medial prosthesis platform, and the differences were more pronounced with the increase in the degree of knee varus. A biomechanical study [28] demonstrated that a mild malalignment position induces less stress shielding than neutral alignment—a finding that directly supports our observation of reduced stress shielding in the KA model. Previous research has compared the impact of different platform‐designed prostheses on stress shielding after TKA [18], indicating that greater prosthetic stress is associated with a higher probability of bone loss [29]. Stress shielding, characterized by reduced cortical bone stress, is a key driver of long‐term tibial bone loss and subsequent prosthesis loosening. By increasing cortical bone stress, KA enhances physiological force transmission in the distal tibia, thereby reducing the risk of bone loss. A recent literature review [30] concluded that KA outperforms MA in improving knee range of motion, achieving more uniform stress distribution across the joint, and reducing wear—all of which may prolong the lifespan of the prosthesis. Our data further elucidate these clinical benefits: the risk of prosthetic material fatigue and wear is directly reduced, while the increased cortical stress promotes bone–prosthesis integration. However, Lozano et al. [31] showed that the results for prosthesis stresses, bone‐prosthesis contact stresses, and prosthesis displacement after KA‐TKA appear to be less satisfactory. Li et al. [17] also found that the tibial plateau stability was poorer in the KA compared to the MA and valgus model.

Biomechanical Role of the Short Extension Stem

Tibial prostheses with short extension stems are commonly used to compensate for instability associated with bone defects [23, 32]. Xie et al. [33] showed that the displacement at the bone‐prosthesis interface decreases as the length of the short extension stem increases. The presence of the short extension stem reduces the stress around the diaphysis, but greater stress is concentrated at the tip of the short extension stem. The short extension stem design in our model effectively mitigated instability induced by KA: across all alignments, the stem reduced maximum prosthetic displacement by 6.4%–7.9%. This enhancement in stability is supported by a recent meta‐analysis [34], which reported that short extension stems reduced the risk of prosthetic loosening of the tibial component compared to prostheses without stem, which was more pronounced in obese patients, and improved TKA survival by 1.04‐fold. The meta‐analysis attributed this benefit to the stem's ability to distribute loads to the tibial metaphysis and diaphysis, reduce localized stress concentration at the prosthesis–bone interface, and minimize micromotion—the primary contributor to prosthetic loosening. It is noteworthy that the impact of short stems on stress shielding requires patient‐specific consideration. The use of a short extension stem in primary or revision total knee arthroplasty (TKA) improves mechanical stability, but at the cost of stress shielding in the direction of the diaphysis [35, 36]. Our data indicate that the stem can reduce average cortical bone stress by 5.8%–6.9%, suggesting a mild stress‐shielding effect. In patients with diminished bone quality, short extension stems should be prioritized to enhance initial stability and prosthesis longevity, whereas prostheses without stems are preferred for patients with healthy bone to avoid unnecessary stress shielding and bone loss [37]. For patients with pre‐osteoarthritic varus alignment who benefit from KA for anatomical restoration and moderate bone loss, the combination of KA and a short extension stem achieves a balance: it preserves the KA‐related mitigation of stress shielding while utilizing stem‐derived stability to reduce prosthesis displacement.

Mechanistic Insights Into Aseptic Loosening and Clinical Implications

Aseptic loosening of TKA is a multifactorial process, with micromotion at the bone–implant interface serving as a central driver—triggered by factors such as constraint, coronal alignment, residual mechanical deformity, bone resection, and osteoporosis [38]. Our study focuses on two of these principal factors: alignment and prosthetic design. First, KA reduces prosthetic stress, thereby minimizing the risk of prosthetic fatigue and subsequent osteolysis induced by wear debris, a key pathway to loosening. This is particularly important given that elevated prosthetic stress is associated with increased bone loss [29]. Second, the reduction in displacement achieved with a short stem minimizes micromotion, thereby lowering the risk of aseptic loosening of the tibial component. Ultimately, decreasing prosthetic displacement and stress shielding is critical for reducing TKA revision rates. Our data indicate that the combination of KA and a short stem achieves this dual objective: it reduces displacement via the short extension stem and mitigates stress shielding through KA. For surgeons, this combination offers a biomechanically optimized option for patients with pre‐osteoarthritic varus alignment and mild to moderate bone loss—although long‐term prospective studies are required to validate its clinical survivorship.

Limitations

There are some limitations in this study: 1. The data model selected for this study was derived from a healthy adult female, which may limit its generalizability to patients with osteoarthritis or osteoporosis. Sclerotic bone in the medial tibia exhibits a higher elastic modulus, which may reduce stress shielding; conversely, osteoporotic bone may increase prosthetic micromotion due to its lower bone stiffness. Despite this limitation, the biomechanical trends observed in this study are consistent with the mechanical principles of TKA and may provide valuable insights for clinical decision‐making. Future studies should incorporate patient‐specific models with osteoarthritis or osteoporosis to validate our findings. 2. Our model did not include soft tissues such as ligaments and tendons, nor bone cement, which may underestimate the complexity of load transfer dynamics. The absence of ligaments could influence the medial and lateral compartment load distribution, while the omission of bone cement may elevate peak stresses in cortical bone. However, this study focused on the relative differences between alignment designs and stem usage rather than their absolute values. As bone cement is a rigid material, its influence on the force transmission process is relatively minor, which is also consistent with findings from multiple previous studies [19, 21, 39]. 3. This study only analyzed the Legion and Triathlon prostheses for comparison and could not be generalized to other prostheses. However, this study is still of great significance in terms of the trend of stress difference brought about by the use of short extension stems and the choice of coronal alignment for the selection of treatment modalities for different patients in the clinic.

Conclusion

The application of KA in TKA will increase prosthetic displacement and reduce the stress‐shielding effect, while the application of short extension stem will reduce prosthetic displacement and increase the stress‐shielding effect. The combination of KA and short extension stem may play a complementary role, but this needs further research to confirm.
