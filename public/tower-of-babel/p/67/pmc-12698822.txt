Mendelian analysis reveals novel pancreatic cancer-associated gut bacteria with tumor-suppressive effects
Authors: Yi Tong, Qian Li, Wei Zhang, Xingli Fan, Hong Tu, Yu Gan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe gut microbiota has been implicated in the development and progression of pancreatic cancer (PC), yet few specific bacterial species have been reported to exert functional effect on PC. This study aimed to identify novel PC-associated gut bacterial species with tumor-regulatory effects and explore the potential molecular mediators of their effect.MethodsA bidirectional two-sample mendelian randomization (MR) analysis was performed using summary statistics from the MiBioGen consortium (n = 18,340) for gut microbiota and from the GWAS Catalog (587 PC cases, 455,761 controls) to assess their causal relationship. A two-step MR analysis incorporating blood metabolite data (n = 8,299 from the GWAS Catalog) was conducted to investigate potential mediation via circulating metabolites. Functional validation of identified bacterial taxa was carried out in murine models of PC via oral gavage of representative species.ResultsSix bacterial taxa, including four genera, demonstrated reliable evidence of causal associations with PC. Eggerthella and Parasutterella were identified as protective for PC, whereas Sutterella and Coprococcus1 were associated with increased PC risk. Additionally, 26 blood metabolites showed causal associations with PC. After evaluating the causal relationships between PC-associated gut microbiota and blood metabolites, we found that the Eggerthella genus and Sutterella genus exert causal effect on PC by reducing the circulating levels of deoxycarnitine and hypotaurine, respectively. In two distinct orthotopic PC models, oral administration of Eggerthella lenta (E. lenta, the type species of the Eggerthella genus) or Parasutterella excrementihominis (P. excrementihominis, the type species of the Parasutterella genus) consistently exerted significant tumor-suppressive effects on the growth of pancreatic tumors. Moreover, E. lenta administration enhanced the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and NKT cells within orthotopic pancreatic tumors, suggesting a potential role of E. lenta in reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment to favor antitumor immunity.ConclusionsThis study identifies E. lenta and P. excrementihominis as novel PC-associated gut bacterial species with tumor-suppressive effects, offering potential candidates for probiotic-based interventions in PC.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13402-025-01098-x.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, Gut microbiota, Mendelian randomization, Blood metabolites

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the most aggressive and highly lethal malignancies, with a rising incidence worldwide [1]. Despite decades of research efforts, the five-year survival rate remains dismally low, and current therapeutic strategies have yielded only modest improvements in patient outcomes [2]. This limited progress is largely attributed to an incomplete understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving PC development and progression.

The human gut harbors trillions of microorganisms, forming one of the most complex biological ecosystems in the body [3]. Over the past few decades, a growing body of research has explored the intricate interplay between gut microbiota and a variety of diseases, including depression [4], Alzheimer’s disease [5], diabetes [6] and various cancers. Recently, increasing evidence implicated gut microbiota not only in intestinal malignancies [7], but also in extraintestinal cancers, including PC [8]. Notably, patients with PC exhibit reduced gut microbial diversity and a distinct microbial composition compared to healthy individuals [9]. Riquelme et al. conducted fecal microbiota transplantation from PC patients into mice and observed significantly reduced tumor growth in mice that received fecal microbiota from long-term survivors compared to those receiving microbiota from short-term survivors [10]. These findings suggest the potential role of gut microbiota in modulating PC progression.

Several studies have reported the potential relationship between specific bacterial taxa and PC. Enrichment of Streptococcus and Veillonella spp., along with a depletion of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, has been proposed as a microbial signature for identifying individual with PC [11]. Furthermore, enrichment of Bacillus clausii and Saccharopolyspora rectivirgula with the tumor microbiome, which is affected by gut microbiota, has been linked to long-term survival of PC patients. To date, only two bacterial species have been experimentally demonstrated to have a functional influence on PC: Porphyromonas gingivalis has been shown to promote the progression of PC in murine models, and Lactobacillus spp. to suppress pancreatic tumor growth and enhance the efficacy of gemcitabine through synergistic antitumor interaction [12, 13]. These findings suggest that probiotic complementation represents a novel therapeutic approach for PC and highlight the need to identify additional bacterial species with functional relevance to PC pathogenesis.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a genetic epidemiological approach that leverages genetic variations as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposures and outcomes [14]. The causal inference in MR relies on genetic variants that are strongly associated with the exposure of interest and are not related to any confounders of the exposure-outcome relationship, allowing researchers to analyze their impact on the outcome. Since genetic variants are randomly inherited, MR helps control confounding factors that often complicate observational studies, thus providing stronger evidence for causality. Several studies have applied this framework to explore the causal relationship between the gut microbiota and cancers [15, 16]. For example, Chen et al. identified six genera causally associated with non-small cell lung cancer using MR analysis [17]. In colorectal cancer (CRC), MR analysis has revealed a protective association with the Blautia genus [18], and subsequent experimental validation confirmed the tumor-suppressive effects of Blautia producta, a representative species within the genus, on CRC [19]. These examples support MR analysis as a reliable strategy for identifying gut microbiota with functional roles in tumorigenesis.

Emerging evidence suggests that circulating metabolites may serve as key mediators linking gut microbiota to extraintestinal cancers [20, 21]. Gut microbes influence a wide array of metabolic processes, including lipid [22], protein [23], bile acid [24], and carbohydrate metabolism [25]. Furthermore, they can regulate intestinal permeability by modulating intestinal epithelial integrity [22], thereby facilitating the translocation of microbial-derived or -modulated metabolites into systemic circulation [26], which can subsequently affect distant organs and tumor growth [27]. For instance, lithocholic acid and deoxycholic acid—both products of gut microbial metabolism—have been shown to inhibit breast and gastric cancers, respectively [28, 29]. Recent studies have begun to employ a two-step MR analysis [30], a validated approach to identify intermediate phenotypes that mediate the causal pathway between exposure and outcome, to explore these triadic relationships involving gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, and cancer risk, with evidence reported for esophageal cancer [31], thyroid cancer [32], and ovarian cancer [33].

In this study, we aimed to identify novel gut microbial taxa causally associated with PC using bidirectional two-sample MR analysis, and to functionally validate their effects in vivo using murine PC models. Furthermore, we investigated the potential mediating role of circulating metabolites through two-step MR analysis, thereby providing new insights into the mechanistic links between gut microbiota and PC progression.

Methods

Study design

Figure 1 explains the schematic diagram of the study design and displays that the causal interpretation of MR estimates relies on three assumptions [14]. The single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) used as IVs in this study meet the following conditions: (1) strongly related to the exposures; (2) only associate with the outcomes via the exposures; (3) not related to any confounder of the exposure-outcome association.

Design of the bidirectional and mediation MR analyses. Firstly, a two-sample bidirectional MR was performed to investigate the causal relationships between gut microbiota (exposure) and PC (outcome). Secondly, 741 blood metabolites (mediator) were selected for subsequent two-step MR

Data sources

The selected SNPs related to gut microbiota were from a GWAS dataset of the MiBioGen consortium (https://mibiogen.gcc.rug.nl), which was a large-scale GWAS that coordinated 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing profiles and genotyping data from 18,340 participants from 24 cohorts, of which 78% were Europeans. A total of 211 taxa (131 genera, 35 families, 20 orders, 16 classes, and 9 phyla) were included. Summary data of PC in the GWAS Catalog included 456,348 participants (587 European ancestry cases and 455,761 European ancestry controls). Summary data of blood metabolites in the GWAS Catalog included a sample size of up to 8,299 European participants. This GWAS database includes 982 plasma metabolites (excluding xenobiotics), of which 741 metabolites (classified into seven major pathways: lipids, amino acids, nucleotides, cofactors and vitamins, carbohydrates, peptides, and energy) have known characteristics and are included in our mediation MR analysis.

Data extraction

The genetic variants used as IVs of gut microbiota for MR have to be representative, therefore we selected SNPs at a more suggestive P-value of less than 1 × 10− 5, which is used in previous MR studies [34, 35]. Independent SNPs were then clumped to a linkage disequilibrium (LD) threshold of r2 < 0.001 at 10,000 Genomes reference panel [36]. LD refers to the tendency of genetic variants located close to each other in the genome to be inherited together, where alleles at two or more loci appear together on a single chromosome at a probability higher than expected by chance. In the meantime, we excluded SNPs whose F-statistic was < 10 to eliminate weak instrumental bias [37]. F-statistic is calculated as ; R2 is the proportion of variance in the exposure explained by the genetic instruments (the instrument strength); N is sample size; K is the number of instrumental variables (SNPs). ; EAF is effect allele frequency; β is effect size of SNPs on the exposure variable; SE is standard error. Finally, we used LDlink (https://ldlink.nih.gov/?tab=ldtrait) to remove SNPs related to confounders. For the final analysis, we retained only results involving at least three SNPs. To maintain a sufficient number of IVs, SNPs associated with PC and blood metabolites were also selected with P-values less than 1 × 10− 5.

Genetic analyses to elucidate causality

We first conducted a bidirectional MR analysis to explore the causal relationship between the gut microbiota and PC, and we used the common MR approach inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) method to determine effective estimates, the criterion of which is a P-value less than 0.05.

Mediation analyses link “gut microbiota–blood metabolites–PC”

We selected and utilized GWAS data for 741 blood metabolites from 8,299 European participants in the GWAS Catalog, applying a two-step MR [38] to identify potential mediators between the gut microbiota and PC. Based on the analysis of the causal relationship between gut microbiota and PC (β), we further assessed the causal effect of the mediators (741 blood metabolites) on PC (β2), as well as the causal effect of gut microbiota (6 significant taxa identified in the primary MR analysis) on the mediators (β1). The indirect effect, which refers to the causal effect of gut microbiota on PC via blood metabolites, can then be estimated as “direct effect A × direct effect B” (β1 × β2). Thus, the proportion mediated could be calculated as “indirect effect/total effect” ([β1 × β2]/β).

Sensitivity analyses

We utilized the MR-Egger method to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy. If MR-Egger intercept P-value < 0.05, it indicates the existence of horizontal pleiotropy. We also utilized Cochran’s Q test to evaluate heterogeneity. If P-value < 0.05, it indicates high heterogeneity and high potential of pleiotropy. Additionally, we performed MR-PRESSO analysis to detect and correct for horizontal pleiotropy by identifying outlier SNPs. The MR-PRESSO global test was used to evaluate overall pleiotropy, and pleiotropic outliers were removed iteratively until the global test result became nonsignificant (P > 0.05). The remaining SNPs were then used for re-estimating causal effects. In addition, exposures with fewer than three valid instrumental SNPs were not subjected to MR-PRESSO or MR-Egger analysis and were also excluded from interpretation.

All MR analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) using the “TwoSampleMR,” “MRPRESSO,” “tidyverse,” “ggplot2,” “MungeSumstats,” “data.table,” “ieugwasr,” “SNPlocs.Hsapiens.dbSNP144.GRCh38,” and “BSgenome.Hsapiens.NCBI.GRCh38” packages.

Cell culture

The murine PC cell line Panc02 was obtained from Frederick National Laboratory for Cancer Research. The luciferase-expressing murine PC cell line KPC1199 [39] was obtained from Dr. Jing Xue’s Laboratory of Renji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Both cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (BIOSUN, Cat# BS-0002-500) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (Gibco, Cat# 15070063). The human PC cell line Patu8988 was obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and was cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and used within 10 passages after thawing.

Bacteria strains and culture conditions

The bacteria Eggerthella lenta (E. lenta, DSM2243), Sutterella wadsworthensis (S. wadsworthensis, DSM14016) and Parasutterella excrementihominis (P. excrementihominis, DSM21040) were purchased from Leibniz Institute DSMZ-German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH. E. lenta was cultured in medium 339, S. wadsworthensis was cultured in medium 413 and P. excrementihominis was cultured in medium 413 supplemented with 5 % bovine serum. All bacteria were cultured anaerobically at 37 °C and were verified by PCR and agarose gel electrophoresis. Bacteria were passaged for no longer than 1 month after resuscitation and the first to ninth generation were used in the experiments.

Orthotopic murine models of PC

Male 3-week-old C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Sino-British SIPPR/BK Lab Animal Ltd (Shanghai, China) and randomly divided into phosphate buffered saline (PBS)-treated group and bacteria-treated groups. After one week of acclimatization, the mice were administered with PBS or indicated bacteria (E. lenta, S. wadsworthensis or P. excrementihominis 108-109 CFU/ 200 µL) by oral gavage once every three days until the end of experiment. Three weeks after oral gavage, the pancreases of mice were implanted with Panc02 (1 × 106 per mouse) or luciferase-expressing KPC1199 (2 × 105 per mouse) as described previously [40]. The mice implanted with Panc02 cells were sacrificed 21 days after tumor cell injection, and the tumor-containing pancreases were then excised and weighed. For the mice implanted with luciferase-expressing KPC1199 cells, in vivo imaging system (IVIS) (Berthold Technologies, Bad Wildbad, Germany) was used to monitor tumor development every week after tumor cell injection as described previously [41]. To quantify tumor burden in pancreas, the mice were sacrificed 14 days after tumor implantation. The tumor-containing pancreases were excised and immediately subjected to ex vivo bioluminescence detection using IVIS. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of SHRM (approval number: 20240926b) and were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Flow cytometry

Tumors were dissected into approximately 1 mm2 fragments and incubated in digestion buffer (0.05 mg/mL collagenase I, 0.05 mg/mL collagenase IV, 0.025 mg/mL hyaluronidase, 0.01 mg/mL DNase I in RPMI-1640 medium) for 20 min at 37 °C. An equal volume of digestion stop solution (4% FBS in RPMI-1640 medium) was added and the suspensions were then filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and washed with FBS-containing PBS. Then, the cells were stained with Viability-APC-Cy7 (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 13–0865), Viability-BV421 (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 13–0863), anti-mouse CD45-BV510 (BioLegend, Cat# 103137, clone 30-F11, RRID: AB_2561392), CD19-PE (BioLegend, Cat# 115507, clone 6D5, RRID: AB_313642), CD3-FITC (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 35− 0031, clone 145-2C11, RRID: AB_2621659), NK1.1-PE (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 50-5941, clone PK136, RRID: AB_2621804), CD4-PerCP-Cy5.5 (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 65 − 0042, clone RM4-5, RRID: AB_2621876), CD8-APC (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 20–0081, clone 53 − 6.7, RRID: AB_2621550), CD11b-APC-Cy7 (BD Biosciences, Cat# 557657, clone M1/70, RRID: AB_396772), Ly6G-PerCP-Cy5.5 (BD Biosciences, Cat# 560602, clone 1A8, RRID: AB_1727563), CD11c-FITC (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 35–0114, clone N418, RRID: AB_2621677), F4/80-PE (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 50-4801, clone BM8.1, RRID: AB_2621795), or the appropriate isotype controls after incubation with anti-CD16/CD32 antibodies (Tonbo Biosciences, Cat# 70–0161, clone 2.4G2, RRID: AB_2621487) to block nonspecific and Fc-mediated binding. The stained cells were detected using a FACSCelesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Dojindo, Cat# CK04) assay. Briefly, Panc02, KPC1199, and Patu8988 (2 × 103 cells/well) cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with DMSO or deoxycarnitine (AmBeed, Cat# A410057) at indicated concentrations. After 48 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration was evaluated using a scratch wound healing assay. Briefly, Panc02, KPC1199 and Patu8988 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well. When the cell density reached approximately 80%, a sterile 200 µL pipette tip was used to create a linear scratch. Detached cells were removed by PBS washing, and fresh serum-free medium containing DMSO or deoxycarnitine (20 µM, 100 µM) was added. Wound closure was monitored at 0 h and 20 h under an inverted microscope. The scratch width was measured by ImageJ and the migration rate was calculated as previously described [42].

Statistical analysis

The data for animal experiments were presented as means ± SD and were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software). The significance of the differences between groups was analyzed using the Student’s t test (for the normally distributed variables) and the criterion for significance was P < 0.05.

Results

Genetic variants for exposures

The number of SNPs used as IVs ranged from 3 to 19 for the 211 gut microbiota taxa in the MiBioGen consortium, 3 to 40 for the 741 blood metabolites and 22 for PC in the GWAS Catalog. As the F-statistic of IVs > 10 is considered effective for MR analyses, all F-statistic of IVs for gut microbiota (ranged from 14.6 to 35.4), PC (ranged from 19.6 to 26.4) and blood metabolites (ranged from 19.5 to 9430.7) are above 10.

Genetic causality and correlation between gut microbiota and PC

Figure 2a provided evidence of the causal effects of 211 taxa on the occurrence of PC. The IVW method revealed that 6 bacterial taxa were causally associated with PC (Fig. 2b). At the genus level, the Eggerthella genus (OR 0.652, 95% CI 0.442 to 0.960; P = 0.030) and the Parasutterella genus (OR 0.611, 95% CI 0.385 to 0.970; P = 0.037) were negatively associated with PC, while the Sutterella genus (OR 2.147, 95% CI 1.015 to 4.543; P = 0.046) and the Coprococcus1 genus (OR 2.096, 95% CI 1.089 to 4.034; P = 0.027) were positively associated with PC. At the family level, the Alcaligenaceae family (OR 0.453, 95% CI 0.230 to 0.891; P = 0.022) was negatively associated with PC. At the order level, the Bacillales order (OR 1.601, 95% CI 1.161 to 2.207; P = 0.004) was positively associated with PC. Outlier SNPs identified by MR-PRESSO analysis were excluded from interpretation and all results passed the heterogeneity test and the horizontal pleiotropy test.

When it comes to the causal effects of PC on gut microbiota, we found 5 bacterial taxa showed changes after PC (Fig. 2c). At the genus level, both the Tyzzerella3 genus (OR 1.047, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.094; P = 0.045) and the RuminococcaceaeUCG011 genus (OR 1.066, 95% CI 1.011 to 1.123; P = 0.018) showed an increase after PC. Two-step Mendelian randomization analyses of the potential mediating role of blood metabolites

Figure 3a provided evidence of the causal effects of 741 blood metabolites on the occurrence of PC. In the MR results, 26 blood metabolites were found causally associated with PC (Fig. 3b). As shown in the forest plot (Fig. 3c), 10 substances were classified within the amino acid metabolism pathway. Among them, cystine, hypotaurine, S-carboxyethylcysteine, N-succinyl-phenylalanine and 8-methoxykynurenate were negatively associated with PC, while valine, isovalerylglycine, vanillactate, dopamine 4-sulfate and pro-hydroxy-pro were positively associated with PC. Thirteen substances were classified within the lipid metabolism pathway. Among them, androstenediol disulfate, glycosyl sphingadienine, stearoyl-GPE, palmitoyl-GPG, myristoyl-palmitoyl-GPC, stearoyl-arachidonoyl-GPI, taurocholenate sulfate and taurodeoxycholic acid 3-sulfate were negatively related to PC, whereas deoxycarnitine, 4-methylhexanoylglutamine, picolinoylglycine, enyl-stearoyl-linoleoyl-GPE and 17alpha-hydroxypregnanolone glucuronide were positively related to PC. Additionally, biliverdin, which belongs to the hemoglobin and porphyrin metabolism pathway, is found to be negatively associated with PC. Meanwhile, two substances from the nucleotide metabolism pathway, 2’-O-methylcytidine and orotate, are positively associated with PC. Outlier SNPs identified by MR-PRESSO analysis were excluded from interpretation and all results passed the heterogeneity test and the horizontal pleiotropy test.

Verification of the causal effects of gut microbiota on PC

To validate the above MR analysis results, we further selected specific bacterial species from PC-associated bacterial taxa and observed their effects on PC in mice. The Bacillales order and the Alcaligenaceae family contain a large number of bacterial species, making it difficult to focus on a specific one. The species of the Coprococcus1 genus are unavailable for purchase. For the remaining three genera, we chose E. lenta, S. wadsworthensis, and P. excrementihominis for experimental validation. E. lenta, S. wadsworthensis, and P. excrementihominis are the type species of their respective genera (Eggerthella, Sutterella, and Parasutterella), serving as representative species first identified and named.

In the orthotopic PC models with murine Panc02 cells, oral gavage of E. lenta and P. excrementihominis were found to significantly inhibit the growth of orthotopic tumors (Fig. 6a-b). Mice orally gavaged with E. lenta had lower pancreas weight (0.250 ± 0.058 g versus 0.183 ± 0.018 g, P < 0.01) and pancreas-to-body weight ratio (0.011 ± 0.002 versus 0.008 ± 0.001, P < 0.01) at the time of sacrifice compared to the control group (Fig. 6a). Mice orally gavaged with P. excrementihominis also had lower pancreas weight (0.263 ± 0.057 g versus 0.177 ± 0.029 g, P < 0.01) and pancreas-to-body weight ratio (0.011 ± 0.002 versus 0.008 ± 0.001, P < 0.01) (Fig. 6b). Further examination of our results revealed that oral gavage of E. lenta led to a 26.86% decrease in pancreas weight and a 25.34% reduction in the pancreas-to-body weight ratio, whereas oral gavage of P. excrementihominis resulted in a 32.69% decrease in pancreas weight and a 32.89% reduction in the pancreas-to-body weight ratio, suggesting the PC-suppressive effect of these two bacterial species. For S. wadsworthensis that belong to the Sutterella genera positively associated with PC, oral gavage of this bacterium did not produce the anticipated promoting effects on Panc02 tumors (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Additionally, we observed that mice orally gavaged with S. wadsworthensis exhibited loose stools and significant body weight loss within one week (Extended Data Fig. 1b). In contrast, oral administration of E. lenta and P. excrementihominis did not result in any notable changes in body weight (Extended Data Fig. 1c-d).

To further validate the inhibitory effects of E. lenta and P. excrementihominis on PC, we used an orthotopic PC model established with the KPC1199 cell line expressing luciferase. In vivo imaging results showed that oral gavage of E. lenta of P. excrementihominis significantly inhibited the growth of KPC1199 tumors, as evidenced by the marked reduction in the fluorescence area in the pancreases (Fig. 6d and e). Specifically, the fluorescence area was reduced by 42.66% in the E. lenta treatment group and by 40.66% in the P. excrementihominis treatment group, supporting the inhibitory effects of these two bacteria on PC.

Experimental exploration of the anti-PC mechanism of E. lenta

Since our two-step MR analysis suggested that E. lenta exerted tumor-suppressive effects in PC by lowering circulating levels of deoxycarnitine, a metabolite genetically predicted as a potential PC risk factor, we then assessed the direct effect of deoxycarnitine on PC cells in vitro. The results showed that deoxycarnitine treatment significantly enhanced proliferation and migration of both murine and human PC cells, as evidenced by increased viability in CCK-8 assays (Fig. 7a) and accelerated migration in wound-healing assays (Fig. 7b). These findings indicate that deoxycarnitine acts as a tumor-promoting metabolite in PC. We also attempted to confirm the deoxycarnitine-regulating effect of E. lenta in vivo. However, we failed to detect plasma levels of deoxycarnitine in either E. lenta-treated or control mice (data not shown). Nevertheless, we observed a significant anti-PC phenotype in E. lenta-treated mice, suggesting the existence of deoxycarnitine-independent mechanisms.

(e) Statistical histograms showing percentage of B cells (CD19+), T cells (CD3+), CD4+ T cells (CD3+ CD4+), CD8+ T cells, NK cells (CD3− NK1.1+), NKT cells (CD3+ NK1.1+), granulocytes (CD11b+ Ly6G+), macrophages (CD11b+ Ly6G− CD11c− F4/80+), monocytes (CD11b+ Ly6G− CD11c− F4/80−) and dendritic cells (CD11b+ Ly6G− CD11c+).Data are presented as mean ± SD; *P < 0.05; ** P < 0.01 by unpaired Student’s t-test.

Accumulating evidence indicates that gut microbiota modulates tumor growth by remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment [43]. Therefore, we systematically analyzed various immune cells within the orthotopic pancreatic tumor microenvironment by flow cytometry (gating strategy: Fig. 7c). The results exhibited a significant increase in the proportion of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells following E. lenta treatment (P < 0.01, Fig. 7d-e). A modest, though statistically non-significant, elevation in NKT cell proportion was also observed (P = 0.08, Fig. 7e). Given the established anti-tumor functions of both CD8+ T cells and NKT cells [44, 45], these findings suggested a potential role of E. lenta in reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment to favor antitumor immunity.

Discussion

Emerging evidence has suggested a potential link between the gut microbiota and PC. However, despite the vast microbial diversity within the gut, only a limited number of bacterial species have been shown to exert functional effects on PC. In this study, we identified 6 gut microbiota taxa, including 4 genera (Eggerthella, Parasutterella, Sutterella, and Coprococcus1) that were causally associated with PC based on MR analysis. Notably, S. wadsworthensis, a representative species of the Sutterella genus, has previously been reported to be enriched in the feces of PC patients across multiple countries [11], consistent with our findings. The remaining 3 genera were identified for the first time as being associated with PC. Among them, E. lenta and P. excrementihominis, representative species of Eggerthella and Parasutterella genus respectively, were experimentally validated to inhibit tumor growth across distinct orthotopic PC models, highlighting their potential translational relevance. Given ongoing preclinical and clinical investigations into the use of probiotics for cancer treatment including PC [46–48], our findings support further explorations of E. lenta and P. excrementihominis as candidates for probiotic-based combination therapies. Moreover, other species within these genera may also possess tumor-suppressive properties and warrant further investigation.

To date, only one study has reported a role of E. lenta in cancer, showing that its increased abundance may mediate the cigarette smoke-induced promotion of CRC in mice [49]. By contrast, our findings demonstrate an anti-tumoral role of E. lenta in PC, suggesting the context-dependent nature of microbial functions across different tumor types. Our two-step MR analyses suggest that E. lenta may inhibit PC growth by reducing the circulating level of deoxycarnitine, which we identified as a PC risk factor. Elevated levels of deoxycarnitine have previously been observed in male patients with gastric cancer compared to healthy controls [50], suggesting its potential pro-tumorigenic role. Consistently, the direct tumor-promoting function of deoxycarnitine was experimentally validated in our in vitro assays. The negative association between deoxycarnitine and E. lenta raises the question of whether E. lenta can directly metabolize deoxycarnitine and thereby reduce its circulating level. Recent studies have identified the bacterial γ-butyrobetaine utilization (gbu) gene cluster as being responsible for converting deoxycarnitine into trimethylamine (TMA) [51]. However, we were unable to detect gbu genes in the genome of E. lenta (data not shown), suggesting E. lenta does not metabolize deoxycarnitine directly. Therefore, it is likely that E. lenta influences circulating deoxycarnitine levels indirectly—such as by modulating gbu-gene-containing bacteria within the gut microbiota. Notably, in experimental mice, the plasma levels of deoxycarnitine were undetectable regardless of E. lenta treatment. This may be due to limited substrate availability, as the standard chow for laboratory mice contain minimal L-carnitine—the main precursor for synthesis of deoxycarnitine [51].

Despite undetectable plasma levels of deoxycarnitine, E. lenta treatment still led to significant tumor suppression, indicating that additional deoxycarnitine-independent mechanisms may contribute to its anti-PC effects. Recent studies have shown that gut microbiota can influence the infiltration and activity of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, thereby promoting antitumor responses [43]. For example, Lactobacillus johnsonii enhances CD8⁺ T cell–mediated antitumor immunity and improves PD-1 blockade efficacy via the microbial metabolite indole-3-propionic acid across multiple cancer types including melanoma, breast cancer, and colorectal cancer [27]. In the context of PC, Lactobacillus reuteri has been shown to enhance NK cell–mediated antitumor immunity and suppress PC progression [52]. Notably, in our E. lenta-treated mice, immune profiling of pancreatic tumors revealed an increase in CD8+ T cell proportion, suggesting a potential role of E. lenta in reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment to favor anti-PC immunity. The precise underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

We also demonstrated P. excrementihominis as a novel PC-suppressive bacterium. However, no specific circulating metabolites were found to mediate its effect on PC. A recent multi-omics study demonstrated that morphine-induced expansion of P. excrementihominis was associated with alterations of bile acid levels [53]. Another study showed that P. excrementihominis abundance was associated with elevated levels of the bile acid metabolite ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) [54]. Bile acids have been implicated in PC pathogenesis; for instance, UDCA inhibits stemness and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in PC cells, while taurocholic acid has been shown to promote PC cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner [55]. Although our analysis did not detect any specific bile acid mediators—possibly due to the limited representation of bile acid species (32 out of ~ 70 known species) in the GWAS dataset—the hypothesis that P. excrementihominis may exert antitumor effects through modulation of bile acid metabolism remains plausible. Future investigations, such as targeted metabolomic profiling of bile acids, may help validate this hypothesis and shed light into the precise mechanisms underlying the tumor-suppressive effects of P. excrementihominis in PC.

The Sutterella genus was identified as a PC risk factor through our MR analysis. Consistently, S. wadsworthensis—a representative species of this genus—was previously found to be enriched in the feces of PC patients [11]. However, in the orthotopic PC xenograft murine model, we did not observe the tumor-promoting activity of S. wadsworthensis. While such models are usually used for evaluating tumor growth, they may not fully capture the contribution of gut microbiota to tumor initiation. In addition to model-related limitations, species-specific effects within the Sutterella genus may also account for this discrepancy. Further studies using spontaneous tumor models, such as the KPC mouse model, and various Sutterella species are needed to assess the impacts of specific Sutterella species on pancreatic carcinogenesis. Moreover, the absence of tumor-promoting effects by S. wadsworthensis might be due to its limited colonization in the host gut. Consistent with the previous study showing S. wadsworthensis exhibits mild pro-inflammatory properties [56], mice receiving oral gavage of this bacterium developed loose stools and significant weight loss within one week, indicating intestinal inflammation induced by S. wadsworthensis in our experiment. However, stable colonization requires further verification, such as measuring its abundance in fecal or intestinal samples. Given that chronic intestinal inflammation is a recognized risk factor for PC [57], S. wadsworthensis may promote pancreatic tumorigenesis via inflammation-mediated mechanisms.

Our study had several limitations. First, the microbiome profile in the MiBioGen Consortium was identified using 16S rRNA sequencing, which lacks the resolution of metagenomic sequencing and restricted our analyses to obtain more detailed insights into microbial species and their potential associations with PC. Second, our study primarily relied on GWAS summary data derived from individuals of European ancestry. While this design minimizes population stratification bias and enhances internal validity, it limits the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Future studies with larger and more ethnically diverse cohorts are needed to confirm whether these bacterial taxa and metabolites are relevant in non-European populations.

In conclusion, we identified two novel PC-associated bacterial species, E. lenta and P. excrementihominis, with tumor-suppressive effects. Mechanistically, E. lenta may exert its effect by reducing circulating deoxycarnitine levels. These findings provide new insights into the microbiota-PC axis and offer promising bacterial candidates for the development of microbiota-based combination therapies for PC.
