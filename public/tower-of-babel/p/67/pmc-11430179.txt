Aerodynamic Size-Dependent Collection and Inactivation of Virus-Laden Aerosol Particles in an Electrostatic Precipitator
Authors: Lan Wang, José Morán, Bernard A Olson, My Yang, Christopher J Hogan, Montserrat Torremorell
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrostatic precipitators (ESPs) may enable high particle collection efficiency with minimal pressure drop in HVAC systems. However, studies of pathogen collection and inactivation in ESPs at medium to higher flow rates are limited. Here, a single-stage, wire-plate ESP operated at flow rates of 51 and 85 m3 h–1 was used to study the removal of virus-laden aerosol particles for three different airborne viruses: (1) bovine coronavirus (BCoV), (2) influenza A virus (IAV), and (3) porcine reproductive and respiratory virus (PRRSV). Size-resolved measurements of collection efficiency were obtained using Andersen cascade impactors (ACI) sampling upstream and downstream of the ESP. All measurements were analyzed based on three distinctive but complementary methods: (1) fluorimetry to assess physical collection, (2) RT-qPCR to assess viral RNA concentrations and (3) virus titration to assess virus viability. In general, log reductions by virus titration were highest followed by those from RT-qPCR, and last fluorimetry, suggesting that a portion of virus may be potentially inactivated in flight in the ESP. An effective migration (deposition) velocity ranging from 3.10 to 10.05 cm s–1 was also determined using the spatially resolved measurements of virus collection on the ESP plates.Keywords: airborne viruses, electrostatic precipitator, collection, inactivation, particle size, wind tunnel

Introduction

The transmission of viruses through inhalation of virus-laden particles has been suggested as the main route of transmission for SARS-CoV-2,1 influenza,2,3 and several other pathogens. Airborne transmitted diseases are considered difficult to contain and control as airborne pathogens spread rapidly, especially in indoor environments. The cost of preventing and controlling airborne virus spread among humans (e.g., coronaviruses and influenza viruses) or animals (e.g., porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus, PRRSV) can be considerably high. For instance, the annual cost of PRRSV was estimated at 664 million USD in the USA.4 The development of control technologies preventing or reducing airborne virus transmission indoor or between agricultural settings may thus have relevant economic implications.5 Fibrous filters are currently standardized and adopted to remove potentially infectious particles from air in both livestock and human-indoor environments. However, the application of filtration has a non-negligible cost; filters need to be regularly replaced and lead to increased pressure drop in air handling systems, increasing energy consumption costs to maintain ventilation rates. There continues to be interest in developing alternative control technologies for airborne viruses, such as ultraviolet (UV) sources,6−9 nanotechnology-based systems,10,11 and nonthermal plasma-based systems.12 One of the most promising alternatives to filtration for airborne virus removal is electrostatic precipitation, as electrostatic precipitators (ESPs) are more established technologies than the aforementioned alternatives. ESPs are frequently used in efficient collection of particles in high flow rate systems, including in coal combustion systems and industrial settings, as well as in residential and commercial HVAC systems.13−15 ESPs, which may be single stage or two-stage, function by first passing particles through a region containing wires or needles with a high voltage applied. The high voltage facilitates corona discharge at the wire or needle electrode surface, generating ions, as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS).16,17 Because there is an electric field present, ions migrate into the bulk flow region of the ESPs, unipolarly charging particles through ion-particle collisions.18,19 Charged particles in turn, migrate electrophoretically toward grounded collection electrodes; which are either the same ground electrodes used in raising the wire or needle electrodes to high potential (single stage), or separate downstream electrodes (two-stage), where the field is generated between ground electrodes and another raised potential electrode (without corona discharge). By tuning flow residence time and applied potential, ESPs can be designed to be competitive with filters (both high MERV ratings and even HEPA filters) in terms of collection efficiency. Care also needs to be taken to control ROS densities; these must be sufficiently low as to not be a health hazard but may afford a means to inactivate pathogens in flight.20,21

Despite requiring application of electrostatic potentials, because of minimal pressure drop, ESPs can have considerably lower energy consumption than filtration and other technologies capable of virus inactivation.22 Additionally, while collection electrodes may require cleaning and maintenance, ESPs do not require annual or semiannual replacement, as is the case for fibrous filters, and is often the case for UV-sources. The efficacy of ESPs in collecting and inactivating viruses has been investigated previously, largely focusing on laboratory bench scale ESPs (commonly cylindrical-wire, single stage) with flow rates ranging from 8 to 85 L min–1 and bacteriophages.21,23,24 However, studies at higher flow rates are in need for actual application. In addition, it is important to work directly with viruses of relevance to airborne disease spread in humans and animals, or with more closely related surrogates than bacteriophages. For instance, arguably the viruses of greatest concern are influenza viruses and coronaviruses, important respiratory viruses detected in human exhaled breath,25,26 and PRRSV, which has driven swine producers to invest in expensive air filtration systems to prevent the virus introduction into herds.27,28 Both PRRSV and influenza viruses have been detected in filters used in swine barns.29

Motivated by the need for further investigation of ESP collection and inactivation performance, here we investigate aerodynamic size-resolved collection and inactivation of bovine coronavirus (BCoV, a betacoronavirus surrogate for SARS-CoV-2, 65–210 nm in diameter), influenza A virus (IAV, 80–120 nm in diameter) and PRRSV (50–74 nm in diameter) in a single stage wire-plate ESP. Importantly, the physical performance of the test ESP in terms of collection efficiency and size-resolved charging has been investigated in detail previously.30 In this study, measurements are carried out in either a well-characterized wind tunnel designed for sampling of virus-laden aerosols upstream and downstream of control technologies,31 or in isolation chambers under BSL-2 conditions in the case of IAV. Furthermore, by using cascade impactors for sampling viral aerosols and analyzing samples with reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), virus titration, and fluorimetry (with doped particles), we evaluate the ESP on its ability of physical and viral collection and virus inactivation by particle aerodynamic diameter, as nebulized viruses are incorporated into larger particles whose size distribution is determined by the produced droplet size distribution by the nebulizer, and the nonvolatile residue fraction in the nebulized suspension. In evaluating aerodynamic size resolved behavior of particles, we utilize data inversion routines to correct for the influences of impactor collection efficiency curves on results. While this is commonplace in evaluating aerosol mass collected by impactors,32−34 to our knowledge, impactor data inversion routines have not been applied previously when quantifying aerosol collection in terms of viral nucleic acid or viable viruses. We report the performance of the ESP for variable ESP voltages and air flow rates. We additionally report results of virus-laden particles deposited at different locations on the ESP collecting plates to infer deposition velocities. Finally, to investigate the effect of ROS and notably ozone generated by the ESP on viruses, we have evaluated virus inactivation by the exposure to different levels of ROS generated by the high voltage electrodes of the ESP. Overall, this study provides new information on the performance of an ESP in collecting and inactivating airborne viruses and reports new methods for evaluating air control technologies.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Setups

We used three different experimental setups (Figure 1). Both Setup-1 and Setup-2 were used for virus aerosolization and measurements of virus removal by the ESP. In both cases, aerosol samples were collected using Andersen cascade impactors, ACIs (Figure 1A) and particle size distributions were monitored using an optical particle counter (OPC; see Figure 1B).

Different experimental setups (1, 2 and 3) used for virus aerosolization and aerosol sampling. The inserts labeled as (A–E) showed details including the aerosol sampling instruments, the ESP, and the sampling foils and meshes installed in the ESP.

In Setup-1, the ESP (Figure 1C) was installed in the middle of a single-pass wind tunnel. This wind tunnel is made of 304-stainless steel and 50 cm diameter by 5 m length, utilized and described in detail in prior studies, including flow and particle concentration uniformity testing.9,30,31 It was operating at flow rates of 51 m3 h–1 (850 L min–1) and 85 m3 h–1 (1417 L min–1) during testing. To generate virus-laden aerosol, a virus suspension of 90 mL was injected using a syringe pump at a liquid flow rate of 3 mL min–1 into a custom-made nebulizer, following procedures described previously.9,30,31 Nebulization, with drying facilitated by the particle-free air flowing into the wind tunnel, lead to virus-laden aerosol particles spanning the 0.13–10 μm aerodynamic diameter range, with prior measurements showing that the size distribution agrees reasonably well with the particle size distribution from human respiratory emissions measured by Johnson et al.35 The particle size distribution depends largely on the nebulizer operating conditions and nonvolatile residue volume fraction in droplets, with minimal influence by the size of the viruses aerosolized.36 In Setup-2, a modular animal isolator system, designed to house animals under BSL-2 conditions, was used to provide required safety when working with IAV. The isolator system consisted of 3 self-contained airtight isolators built of stainless steel and plexiglass materials and connected in series. Isolators 1 and 2 were connected through a 43.18 cm long duct, and the ESP was installed in between isolators 2 and 3 using two specially designed metallic panels. Setup-2 emulated the working principle of the mentioned single-pass wind tunnel. HEPA filters were installed at the inlet (isolator 1) and outlet (isolator 3) to avoid contamination from or toward the system. A blower (Cyclonair Rotron Inc. CHE 3) was connected at the exit of isolator 3 to pull a flow of 51 m3 h–1. A VOAG voltage control unit was used to adjust the voltage of the blower to have the desired flow. The flow was monitored using a rotating vane anemometer (Pacer DA 400). Virus aerosol generation was carried out in a similar fashion to that in the wind tunnel, with the exception that 30 mL of virus suspension was used with the syringe pump operating at 1 mL min–1.

As shown in Figure 1C, the ESP consists of a total of 9 Tungsten wires of 40 cm length, 0.3 mm diameter, equally spaced along the ESP length of 122 cm and located at 6.7 cm from the collecting plates. These wires were connected to a high-voltage power supply unit (Matsusada model AU-30R1-LC) operated in positive polarity during particle collection tests. When exposed to high voltage, the ESP wires generated ions through a corona discharge, with an onset voltage ∼9.8 kV at relative humidities below 50%.30 Due to different experimental conditions, including different relative humidities encountered in different days (between 25 and 35%) when these experiments were carried out, the measured corona current was between 26–31 and 89–94 μA at the applied voltages of 12 and 14 kV, respectively. However, within each experiment (∼hours), the corona current was within ± 1 μA of the average value. Additional information related to the ambient conditions and the ESP corona current for all the experimental conditions are provided in Section S1 of the Supporting Information (SI).

The aerosolized particles were sampled from the upstream and downstream of the ESP using two custom probes of 30 mm inner diameter. The two probes were directly connected to two Mark II Andersen cascade impactors37 (ACIs) as shown in Figure 1A. Each of the impactors is connected to a vacuum pump (Copley LCP5 and Gast S55JXTJN8535) operated at 90 L min–1. We note that these impactors are more commonly operated at 28.3 L min–1. As in our previous works,9,30,31 we use 90 L min–1 to maximize the collection of viruses by the impactor stages, while minimizing operation time. The cut sizes (critical aerodynamic diameters) for each impactor stage at with a 90 L min–1 sample flow rate are reported by Roberts and Mitchell,38,39 as well as Yoshida et al.,38,39 and used here in data processing. The airflow was monitored using mass flow meters (TSI 4000 series) connected between the pumps and the ACIs. The ACIs were operated with a total of 8 stages (stages −2, −1, −0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5) together with an 81 mm diameter quartz fiber filter placed after the bottom stage. An optical particle counter (AeroTrak Hand-held Particle Counter Model 9306, TSI Inc., Shoreview, MN) was connected to the upstream of the ESP in the wind tunnel (Setup-1) using a custom probe of 10 mm inner diameter. A diluter was used to avoid coincidence errors in the OPC. The OPC was connected to the downstream of the ESP in the isolators (Setup-2). As shown in Figure 1D, the aerosol particles which deposited on the collecting plates of the ESP were sampled using square aluminum foils of 5 × 5 cm2 and 0.406 mm thickness. These foils were placed on an aluminum foil holder with the same thickness. These sampling foils were attached to the holder using aluminum tape placed from the bottom to avoid any interference to particle deposition (Figure 2). The foil holder (with the same length as the collecting plate and 2.5 cm width) was attached to the ESP grounded collecting plate using aluminum tape placed on both ends and, in the middle, to keep it in electrical contact. The square sampling foils were placed along the holder at 5 different locations, separated by a constant distance of 27.9 cm enumerated from 1 to 5 in the direction of the flow in the ESP.

Andersen cascade impactor plates sampled upstream and downstream the ESP (a); sampling foils and the holder removed from the ESP (b); Sampling foils installed into the ESP (c); Sampling foils placed into labeled Petri-dishes after sampling (d).

Setup-3 was used to investigate the effect of ROS produced by the high voltage electrodes on inactivating the collected viruses. The high voltage power supply unit was operated in either positive or negative polarity in these tests. To maximize the exposure of virus to ozone, we placed the high voltage electrodes at 2.5 cm from the collecting plate of the ESP, which resulted in the corona onset voltage decreasing to a value of ∼6.8 kV. The corona onset voltage was slightly larger in magnitude for positive polarity compared to negative polarity. An ozone analyzer (Thermo Scientific, Model 49C) was used for monitoring the volume fraction of ozone produced by the high voltage electrodes. As shown in Figure 1E, we developed custom sampling meshes (squared of 5 × 5 cm2; 1 mm opening size, 0.4318 mm wire diameter, McMaster-Carr 304 stainless steel mesh) which were inoculated with viruses. Subsequently, they were exposed to ozone generated by the high voltage electrodes, to quantify the virus inactivation by the ESP.

Test Procedure

Experiments were performed with the ESP operating under different conditions (3 different ESP voltages and 2 different air flows) in the wind tunnel (Setup-1) or the isolator chambers (Setup-2), as summarized in Table 1. Experiments with virus aerosolization of PRRSV and BCoV were conducted with Setup-1 while aerosolization using IAV was conducted with Setup-2. Experiments with the ESP operating at 0 voltage (or ESP off) were also performed (upstream-downstream correlation tests). Each of the conditions in Setup-1 was tested in triplicate, and each of those in Setup-2 was tested in duplicate. Each replicate consisted of continuous virus nebulization for 30 min (verified using a timer). Air samples were collected using two ACIs (Figure 1A) from the upstream and downstream of the ESP simultaneously in both Setup-1 and Setup-2, and an OPC (Figure 1B) upstream in Setup-1 and downstream in Setup-2. The sampling foils were placed inside the ESP (Figure 1C,D) during each test in the wind tunnel. When the virus nebulization was completed, the syringe pump and the vacuum pumps of both ACIs were turned off immediately. The flow in the tunnel was then shut down, and the ESP voltage was reduced to 0, when applicable. Figure 2a shows a picture of the impaction plates of both ACIs upstream and downstream after a test with the ESP operated at 14 kV. The ESP was disconnected from the wind tunnel 30 min after the nebulization was completed. The foil holder together with the sampling foils were removed from the ESP (Figure 2b), by removing the tape attaching the holder to the ESP (Figure 2c). Each of the sampling foils was placed into an independent Petri dish using tweezers (Figure 2d). New and clean sampling foils, holders and ACI plates were used for each replicate. Prior to the start of the virus aerosolization in each test, distilled water was nebulized through the pneumatic nebulizer for 15 min to avoid any clogging.

In assessing the effect of the high voltage electrodes on virus inactivation by generating ROS, we developed custom sampling meshes (Figure 1E). Each sampling mesh was submerged into a 30 mL virus suspension of either BCoV, IAV or PRRSV for 1 min, and then air-dry for 15 min in a biosafety cabinet. Then, these sampling meshes were exposed to ozone generated by the ESP high voltage electrodes for different incubation times including 5, 30, and 60 min.

Sample Processing and Testing

For virus aerosolization, the three viruses were prepared as described in Section S2 of the SI. Viruses were grown to titers of 107–107.5 Median Tissue Culture Infectious Dose (TCID50) mL–1 (50% tissue culture infectious dose) for BCoV and IAV, and 105–106 TCID50 mL–1 for PRRSV (MN-30100). Virus suspensions of BCoV and PRRSV used in Setup-1 were spiked with 0.3 g L–1 fluorescein dye, used as a physical tracer for particle penetration (leading to the color of the deposition plates in Figure 2). After each 30-min aerosolization test, each of the impaction plates of both ACI upstream and downstream were placed into an independent Petri dish. Each of the foil samples were removed from the holder and placed into an independent Petri dish. Viruses were eluted with 3 mL of appropriate virus growth media as collection liquid using a cell scraper. The filter on the final stage of the ACI was eluted with 15 mL of virus growth media. The pH of the collection liquid was adjusted to pH 9 using 1 M NaOH solution. After vortexing at 3000 rpm for 2 min, the filter fibers were separated by centrifugation at 3000g for 20 min. The pH of the 6 mL supernatant containing the eluted virus was readjusted to pH 7 with 5 M HCl solution. All samples were divided into 2 aliquots and stored at −80 °C after being tested. Virus titration, RT-qPCR, and fluorimetry were carried out for all the samples. Titration was also repeated in the remaining suspension from the nebulizer. In all instances we did not observe a decrease in virus titer from the orginal measurement, indicating that neither fluorescein doping nor storage in the nebulization system had an appreciable effect on virus viability over the course of experiments.

Viable viruses recovered from each stage of the ACIs were titrated in specific cells (vidS2 from SI) on the same day as collection, using the TCID50 method. Samples were 10-fold serially diluted from 10–1 to 10–7 in the appropriate virus cell growth media, then, 100 μL of each sample dilution were inoculated into four wells of specific cell monolayers in 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cytopathic effects were recorded at 5 days post inoculation for IAV, and 7 days post inoculation for BCoV and PRRSV. Virus titer results were calculated using the Kärber’s method40 and expressed as TCID50 mL–1. Viral RNA was extracted from 50 μL of samples using the MagMAX-96 Viral RNA Isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with a semiautomatic MagMAX Express-96 Deepwell Magnetic Particle Processor (Applied Biosystems by Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA). The RNA was eluted with 50 μL of elution buffer and quantified by quantitative RT-PCR using Ag Path-ID One Step RT-PCR Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). For PRRSV RT-qPCR, we used in-house developed PCR primers and probe targeting ORF-6 gene;29 for IAV RT-qPCR, primers and probe targeting the IAV matrix gene41 were used. The RT-qPCR reaction mixture (25 μL) components and the thermocycling conditions for PRRSV and IAV were described in ref (29) For BCoV RT-qPCR, primers and probe targeting the transmembrane gene42 were used. The BCoV RT-qPCR reaction mixture (25 μL) components and the thermocycling conditions were described in ref (9) The absolute viral genome copy numbers in each sample, expressed as RNA copies mL–1, were quantified using the cycle threshold (Ct) values and standard curves which were constructed using serial 10-fold dilutions of standard curve samples specific for each virus with known concentrations. In each run of RT-qPCR, no template control was used as negative control. Aliquots of 100 μL of each sample were placed in triplicate into 96-well black plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Roskilde, Denmark). The plates were sealed with a transparent film to prevent evaporation. Fluorescence intensity for each well was measured using a microplate reader (Model Synergy H1, BioTek, Winooski, VT), with excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 515 nm respectively, with a gain of 30. For virus suspensions used to inoculate sampling meshes in the virus inactivation test, BCoV and IAV were prepared the same as above (vidS2 from SI), and the titers were 107–107.5 TCID50 mL–1. PRRSV (L1C 1–4–4) was prepared as described in (vidS2 from SI), and the titer was 105 TCID50 mL–1. After incubation with ozone generated by ESP high voltage electrodes for a certain time, each of the sampling meshes was removed from the collecting plate of ESP and placed into a Petri dish. Similarly, the viruses were eluted with 3 mL of appropriate virus growth media using a cell scraper. Virus titration, RT-qPCR, and fluorimetry were carried out for all these samples with the same methods as described above.

Andersen Cascade Impactor Data Processing

After obtaining titration, RT-qPCR, and fluorescence signal for each impactor plate, we then corrected results for the impactor collection efficiency curves. We have found ACI collection is effective in preserving the infectivity of viruses43,44 and can be used in bioaerosol sampling.7 These instruments are also designed for size-resolved aerosol particles measurements. However, measurements need to be corrected by the imperfect collection efficiency of the impaction stages; this is particularly important for the stages devoted to collection of the largest particle sizes, where wall losses are more significant.45 However, correction is not simple, and requires a solution to an inversion problem which is commonly ill-conditioned.46 Different inversion approaches have been introduced for this purpose, including the maximum entropy method,33 Twomey-like algorithm,42 a fixed-point iteration algorithm,34 among others.32 Here, we carry-out data inversion as follows. Results and Discussion

Size-dependent Virus Collection

The nebulizer generated size distributions are shown in Figure S9 of the SI. Figure 3 shows the inverted size-dependent log reduction of the virus-laden aerosols measured by ACIs when the ESP was operating under different conditions. Figure 3a–c correspond to the PRRSV aerosol removal at 51 m3 h–1 and 14 kV, 85 m3 h–1 and 14 kV, 51 m3 h–1 and 12 kV, respectively; Figure 3d corresponds to BCoV aerosol removal at 51 m3 h–1 and 14 kV; and Figure 3e corresponds to IAV aerosol removal at 51 m3 h–1 and 14 kV. This log reduction is defined as log10(Cup/Cdown) where Cup refers to the measured concentrations upstream and Cdown refers to the measured concentrations downstream, based on the tests of fluorimetry, RT-qPCR and virus titration. No fluorescein was used in the tests with IAV in the isolator chambers. Fluorimetry results for all measurements in the wind tunnel yielded log reductions increasing with particle diameter, varying from 0.2 to 2.4 for particles in the 0.1 to 10 μm aerodynamic diameter range. RT-qPCR results yielded log reductions with a similar trend, and in exceeding 3.0 for IAV-laden particles >8 μm. Virus titration results yielded viable virus log reductions which were not monotonic with particle diameter but approached 4.0 for larger particles of BCoV and IAV. In Figure S7 of the SI, we summarize the log reductions based on the same three measurements for the correlation tests with PRRSV aerosol (at 51 and 85 m3 h–1), BCoV aerosol (at 51 m3 h–1) and IAV aerosol (51 m3 h–1). The log reductions for fluorescence and viral RNA were near 0 in general across the aerodynamic diameter range of 0.1 to 10 μm, although the RNA log reductions slightly increased for particles larger than 8 μm. The log reductions in Figure 3 were not corrected by the correlation tests given the low log reductions observed in correlation tests.

Log reduction in virus-laden particle concentrations based on fluorescence, viral RNA and viable viruses from upstream to downstream of the ESP as a function of particle diameter for porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV), bovine coronavirus (BCoV) and influenza A virus (IAV) at different flow rates and applied voltages. (a) PRRSV, 51 m3 h–1 and 14 kV; (b) PRRSV, 85 m3 h–1 and 14 kV; (c) PRRSV, 51 m3 h–1 and 12 kV; (d) BCoV, 51 m3 h–1 and 14 kV; (e) IAV, 51 m3 h–1 and 14 kV.

For all tested ESP operating conditions, we observed a higher log reduction for larger particles. This trend in collection efficiency has been reported in previous studies with noninfectious particles21,30,53,54 and is hence not surprising for the ESP used here, where larger particles are efficiently charged through field charging mechanisms and thus more effectively collected in the ESP. We also compare the removal efficiency of PRRSV aerosol at different flows for the three measurements in Figure 3a,b. When the flow rate increased, we observed a slightly lower collection efficiency at larger particle diameters, especially >2.6 μm, but similar collection efficiency (considering the error bars) at the smaller particle diameters.

The log reduction inferred from viral RNA concentrations was always higher than that of fluorescence, across the 1–10 μm aerodynamic diameter range, and the difference was larger for larger particles. This suggests some degree of RNA degradation/damage when viruses passed through the ESP. Due to the inherent variability in the TCID50 method for virus titration, the log reduction based on viable viruses shows more variation than other measurements. Even so, the log reduction of viable viruses usually exceeded those of viral RNA and fluorescence across multiple particle diameters, which provided additional evidence of virus inactivation. As the intense electric field at the ESP wire surface leads to production of ROS,23,55,56 there may be viruses inactivated via oxidation reactions for particles with trajectories bringing them close to the corona discharge wires, where ROS densities would be the highest.

In Figure 3a,c, the removal efficiency of PRRSV aerosol was higher overall when the voltage of the ESP increased from 12 to 14 kV, particularly for particles >1 μm. Figure 4 shows the log reduction of fluorescence collected from virus-laden aerosols and potassium chloride (KCl) particles measured previously at the same flow with the same ESP operating at the two voltages of 12 and 14 kV.30 For both KCl and PRRS virus-laden particles, we generally observed higher collection efficiency at higher voltages of the ESP, with a more pronounced effect for larger particles. Surprisingly, the particle removal for PRRSV aerosols at diameters <1 μm appears insensitive to the higher ESP voltage, even though the ESP collection efficiency of PRRSV reached minimum in this size range. It is also interesting to note that the collection efficiency for KCl was generally higher than PRRS virus-laden particles; this may be due the influence of the particle dielectric constant on particle field charging,57,58 as higher dielectric constant particles charge more efficiently in the supermicrometer size range. Changes in relative humidity, changing ion production rates, ion mobilities, and ion masses, may also have played a role in creating such differences.

Comparison of log-reduction of particles in an ESP between virus-laden particles and KCl particles.30 The KCl measurements were carried out using a differential mobility analyzer connected in line with a condensation particle counter (Dp < 0.25 μm) and an aerodynamic particle spectrometer (Dp > 0.3 μm).

At 12 kV and 51 m3 h–1, we observed a higher removal efficiency of BCoV compared to PRRSV-laden particles based on all the three measurements. As fluorescein was used as a nonbiological control in these tests, the removal efficiency of fluorescein by the ESP was supposed to be consistent in these tests. However, these tests with different viral aerosols were performed on different days in which the relative humidity in the ESP operating unit might have been different, leading to changes in ion production rates. In addition, the chemical compositions of the virus suspensions were different, particularly the protein content, e.g. there was 8% FBS in PRRSV growth media, and 4% BSA in BCoV growth media (vidS2 from the SI). The high protein content in the virus suspensions may help protect particles from overdrying and keep them existing in a semisolid state, which may play a role on the removal efficiency of virus-laden particles and virus inactivation by the ESP. Differences in extent of inactivation would be anticipated. PRRSV (order Nidovirales, family Arteriviridae) is an enveloped, positive-stranded RNA virus, and the PRRS virion is roughly spherical or oval-shaped particle 50–60 nm in diameter.59,60 BCoV (order Nidovirales, family Coronaviridae) is also an enveloped, positive-stranded RNA virus, but is pleomorphic, ranging from 65 to 210 nm in diameter.61,62 The different structures of these two viruses of PRRSV and BCoV may determine how the virions are associated with particles of different diameters, and influence the viability of these viruses under the conditions within the ESP.

Spatially Resolved Virus Collection

Figure 5 shows the relative particle concentration collected by the sampling foils as a function of the distance (x) from the ESP inlet. The ESP was operated at 51 m3 h–1 and 14 kV for BCoV (Figure 5a); at 51 m3 h–1 and 12 kV (Figure 5b), at 51 m3 h–1 and 14 kV (Figure 5d), and at 85 m3 h–1 and 14 kV (Figure 5c) for PRRSV. As we are interested in the relative extent of particle deposition on the collecting plates of the ESP, we normalized all concentrations by the concentration determined at the first sampling location, which was the closest to the device inlet. In the correlation tests (Figure S8 from the SI), the relative particle concentrations based on fluorescence and viral RNA were generally higher or close to 1, and there were slightly more particles deposited in the middle section of the collecting plate (x = 40 and 60 cm). Conversely, in all the tests with ESP on, we observed a clear exponential decrease in the deposition profiles along the ESP length. In tests where ESP operated with viral aerosols, the viral RNA and viable viruses showed a steeper decay as a function of the sampling distance, providing more evidence of virus inactivation and viral RNA degradation either in-flight, or on the ESP collecting plate. The differences between fluorescence and viral measurements were more apparent in PRRSV tests. Interestingly, the relative concentrations of the three measurements decayed more steeply for BCoV aerosols than PRRSV aerosols, and BCoV reached a lower level of relative collection at the exit of the ESP.

Virus Inactivation by ESP-generated ROS

To assess the effect of the high voltage electrodes on virus inactivation by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), we measured the viable virus concentration on the inoculated sampling meshes after incubating with ESP-generated ROS (CESP). We also measured control sample without ROS incubation (CControl). Figure 6 reports the log reduction, defined as the base 10 logarithm of the ratio of the concentration of control meshes (CControl) to the concentration of ESP-treated meshes (CESP). The high voltage electrodes of ESP were operated with positive polarity (Figure 6a) and negative polarity (Figure 6b). The log reductions of the three inoculated viruses, BCoV, IAV and PRRSV, were within ±0.2 and always close to 0 even through the incubation time increased. Therefore, we did not find significant virus inactivation by the ESP-generated ROS for viruses on collection electrodes. The measurements of ozone concentration are reported in S7 of the SI. The results suggest that any ROS facilitated inactivation in the ESP may be limited to primarily in-flight inactivation, specifically for particles with trajectories close to the ion-generating wire electrodes; there a sharp decrease in ozone concentration with distance from these electrodes.65
