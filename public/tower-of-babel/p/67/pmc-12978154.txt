A Rare and Delayed Presentation of Retinoblastoma in an 8-Year-Old Child: A Case Report
Authors: Ahmed Almaweri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRetinoblastoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in childhood and is typically diagnosed prior to the age of five. Late presentation beyond this age is uncommon and is often associated with delayed recognition, misdiagnosis, or restricted access to specialized eye centers.Case PresentationHere, we document a case of an 8-year-old male child who presented with leukocoria, exotropia, and progressive deterioration of vision in his left eye. Initially, he was misdiagnosed with congenital cataract at a rural primary healthcare center, which delayed proper referral and management. Subsequent clinical examination and imaging revealed a significant intraocular mass with calcification, highly indicative of advanced Group D intraocular retinoblastoma. A differential diagnosis was conducted to rule out conditions that may mimic retinoblastoma. The affected eye was enucleated, and histopathology confirmed the diagnosis, demonstrating Homer Wright rosettes without optic nerve or extraocular spread. Because no high-risk features were found, additional therapy was not necessary.ConclusionThis case report emphasizes the importance of maintaining awareness of retinoblastoma even in older children, as atypical or delayed presentations may occur. It also highlights the significance of timely recognition, successful referral, and comprehensive imaging with histopathological confirmation in guiding management and improving prognosis.

Introduction

Retinoblastoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in children, accounting for about 3% of childhood cancers. Primarily linked to RB1 tumor suppressor gene mutations, it occurs in approximately 1 in 15,000–18,000 live births, with 75% of cases being sporadic and unilateral.1–3 The most commonly documented initial signs of retinoblastoma are leukocoria and strabismus. Less frequently observed signs include proptosis, orbital mass, iris color changes, orbital cellulitis, red eye, and hyphema.4 Retinoblastoma typically manifests in early childhood, with the age of onset closely linked to the genetic factors involved. Bilateral hereditary retinoblastoma typically appears within the first year of life.5 In contrast, most unilateral cases are identified during the second year.6 Sporadic or delayed unilateral presentations may appear between two and five years of age.7 After the age of five, as shown in this case report, the occurrence of retinoblastoma is rare and typically indicative of a delayed diagnosis or an unusual clinical course.8 Although uncommon, late-onset retinoblastoma has been previously reported in the literature, particularly in sporadic unilateral cases. It may pose significant diagnostic challenges due to its atypical age of presentation. This age group often leads to a lower index of clinical suspicion, resulting in initial misdiagnosis.8 Retinoblastoma exhibits a survival rate of 95% when diagnosed early. Conversely, if not recognized promptly, it can lead to low vision, blindness, or even death.9 An important factor contributing to the late diagnosis and management of retinoblastoma is the long time taken by parents to recognize the condition and seek medical help.10 The late diagnosis and treatment of retinoblastoma play a significant role in mortality across numerous low- and middle-income countries. These delays are often caused by low public awareness, limited access to specialized ophthalmic care, and early misdiagnosis.11

Documenting cases of retinoblastoma in older children is crucial for raising awareness among parents and healthcare providers, promoting early diagnosis, and directing effective strategies to prevent misdiagnosis, treatment delays, and the associated risks of vision loss or life-threatening complications. In line with this, our study aims to report a rare case of retinoblastoma that was diagnosed at a later age than usual and to emphasize the critical importance of remaining attentive to the presence of retinoblastoma in children who are older than the typical age of onset, especially in areas where awareness and healthcare access are limited.

Case Presentation

An 8-year-old male child, who had been healthy before, was presented to our eye center with a painless and progressive decrease in vision in his left eye for approximately one year. His parents noticed a whitish reflex in the pupil, particularly visible in low-light conditions, but initially overlooked it as a normal reflex and did not seek medical care. They also observed an outward deviation in his left eye. The child did not have any past ocular trauma, surgery, or notable systemic disease. Additionally, there was no documented family history of retinoblastoma or other malignancies. This child was initially assessed at a rural primary healthcare center, where he was given a preliminary misdiagnosis of congenital cataract. He was then referred to the capital city, Sana’a, for a more comprehensive evaluation and appropriate treatment at a specialized eye care center. Upon examination, unaided visual acuity was found to be 20/20 for the right eye, whereas the left eye revealed a visual acuity of 20/80. The patient’s refraction was unremarkable. The external ocular and periorbital evaluation showed normal findings in both eyes, with no evidence of orbital involvement. However, an apparent white pupillary reflex (leukocoria) was observed in the left eye, which is a classic clinical sign demanding further intraocular investigation (Figure 1). Assessment of strabismus and ocular motility revealed full extraocular muscle movements in all directions with no restrictions, and the cover test demonstrated left eye exotropia in the primary gaze. During the slit-lamp assessment, the left eye demonstrated an unremarkable conjunctiva, cornea, and anterior chamber. The iris appeared normal, with intact pupillary reactions, and the lens was clear and structurally normal. There were no signs of anterior segment inflammation, including flare, cells, hypopyon, pseudohypopyon, or hyphema, and no neovascularization of the cornea or iris. In contrast, the right eye was entirely unremarkable, with a clear conjunctiva, cornea, and quiet anterior chamber, as well as a normal lens and iris appearance. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was within normal limits in both eyes. The fundoscopic evaluation of the left eye showed a significant creamy-white retinal mass occupying the posterior pole, with a predominantly peripheral growth pattern. Additionally, there was no evidence of retinal detachment or vitreous seeds. Dilated retinal vessels were observed to be directed toward the tumor. Despite the extensive intraocular involvement, the macula and optic nerve head appeared clinically intact at the time of examination, with no evidence of retinal hemorrhage or anterior segment involvement. The fundoscopic examination of the right eye revealed a completely normal fundus, with clear retinal structures and a healthy optic disc, reflecting a well-functioning contralateral eye. Ultrasonography (B-scan) of the left eye revealed a large, solid intraocular mass arising from the posterior segment. Morphologically, the lesion appeared dome-shaped with heterogeneous echogenicity, along with high internal reflectivity on the A-scan, which highly suggests the presence of calcification, an important and classic sign of retinoblastoma. In addition, posterior acoustic shadowing was noted behind the mass, further providing evidence of calcified elements within the tumor. The vitreous body was observed to be somewhat clear, with no signs of diffuse vitreous hemorrhage or opacities. These B-scan findings align with the clinical impression of retinoblastoma (Figure 2). Cranial and orbital computed tomography (CT) scans demonstrated the presence of a mass lesion in the left eye, arising from the retina, exhibiting an inward-growing pattern (endophytic) and showing areas of calcification. Importantly, there was no involvement of the optic nerve or extraocular structures. Regarding the right orbit, the CT scan was unremarkable. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and orbits was conducted to further evaluate the extent of the intraocular mass and rule out extraocular or intracranial involvement. The MRI showed a well-defined intraocular soft tissue mass that remained entirely within the globe. The lesion demonstrated a heterogeneous signal. Importantly, the scan excluded the presence of optic nerve thickening, and no perineural enhancement or intracranial extension was observed. Orbital fat, extraocular muscles, and brain structures were unremarkable. These results supported localized intraocular disease without high-risk imaging features.

We performed a differential diagnosis of retinoblastoma by evaluating some ocular conditions that may clinically mimic its manifestations, such as Coats disease, astrocytoma, ocular toxocariasis, vitreous hemorrhage, persistent fetal vasculature, familial exudative vitreoretinopathy, congenital cataract, and retinal detachment. The aim of this approach was to avoid misdiagnosis, prevent delays in treatment, and reduce the likelihood of unnecessary enucleation. Based on the clinical examination and multimodal imaging findings, the tumor demonstrated extensive intraocular involvement while remaining confined within the globe, with no evidence of optic nerve or extraocular extension. These features were consistent with advanced Group D intraocular retinoblastoma according to the International Classification of Retinoblastoma. Given the large tumor burden and poor visual potential, primary enucleation was indicated. A few weeks following the initial evaluation and CT imaging, the vision in the left eye worsened from 20/80 to 20/200, accompanied by a noticeable increase in leukocoria noted by both the parents and the clinical team.

After completing the required investigations and confirming the diagnosis, we proceeded with the enucleation of the involved eye, followed by the implantation of a silicone orbital sphere. The decision for enucleation was made to protect the patient’s life by preventing the substantial risk of extraocular invasion and metastatic spread. Following enucleation, the excised eye was sent to the histopathology laboratory to confirm the diagnosis of retinoblastoma and analyze high-risk indicators, including optic nerve, choroidal, and scleral infiltration. These results are critical for prognosis and for establishing the need for supplemental therapy. Histopathological examination of the enucleated left eye confirmed the diagnosis of retinoblastoma. The tumor measured 1.5 × 1.0 × 1.0 cm and exhibited a predominantly diffuse growth pattern composed of densely packed small round malignant cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, scant cytoplasm, and a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio. The tumor demonstrated marked hypercellularity with architectural disorganization. Homer Wright rosette formation was identified, characterized by tumor cells arranged in a circular pattern surrounding central eosinophilic neuropil-like material, consistent with neuronal differentiation. Areas of intratumoral necrosis and calcification were also observed. The tumor invaded the retina, subretinal space, and adjacent vitreous humor. There was no involvement of the optic nerve head, and the optic nerve resection margin was free of tumor. No extraocular soft tissue invasion was identified, and the choroid and sclera were free of tumor infiltration, thereby excluding high-risk histopathological features (Figure 3). Accordingly, the pathological staging was determined as pT1, with regional lymph nodes not assessed (pNx) and distant metastasis not assessed (pMx), resulting in an overall pathological stage of pT1, pNx, pMx. Based on these findings, the patient was considered to have a favorable prognosis, and no adjuvant therapy was required at this stage. Regular follow-up appointments were planned, including detailed ocular examinations and systemic monitoring, to allow for early detection of any recurrence or new lesions. In our case, the clinical timeline can be summarized as follows: the patient’s parents reported noticing ocular symptoms approximately one year prior to presentation. The initial assessment at a rural primary healthcare center resulted in a misdiagnosis of congenital cataract, and referral to a specialized eye center in Sana’a was delayed by approximately three months due to financial constraints. Following evaluation at our center, the diagnosis was established, and enucleation was performed four weeks later. Postoperatively, the patient was reviewed on the first postoperative day and subsequently at 3 and 6 months. At the time of writing this report, no evidence of recurrence or contralateral eye involvement has been detected, and annual follow-up has been scheduled. This case emphasizes the importance of timely surgical intervention and comprehensive histopathological assessment in guiding prognosis and subsequent management.

Discussion

This case report demonstrates a rare and atypical presentation of unilateral retinoblastoma, diagnosed in an 8-year-old child, which is considerably later than the usual age of onset. The diagnosis was definitively confirmed through a combination of clinical findings, including indirect ophthalmoscopy and imaging with B-scan, CT, and MRI, which visualized an intraocular mass containing calcifications consistent with retinoblastoma. Definitive confirmation was provided by histopathological evaluation, which exhibited characteristic Homer Wright rosettes. Importantly, adverse indicators such as optic nerve, choroidal, or scleral invasion were absent, conferring a promising prognosis and eliminating the need for supplemental therapy. Retinoblastoma is primarily diagnosed in early childhood, with over 95% of cases discovered before the age of five.5,7 Diagnosis at advanced ages, such as in our patient, is uncommon and usually reflects delayed detection, while an atypical biological behavior of the tumor may also play a role. Previous reports have documented retinoblastoma in older children and even teenagers up to 18 years of age, but these cases remain uncommon.8 Delayed diagnoses are often linked to misunderstandings of early signs, low public awareness, and restricted access to specialized ophthalmic services, particularly in low- and middle-income regions.11,12 Despite the fact that retinoblastoma is typically diagnosed prior to age 5, numerous studies confirm that cases of delayed presentation are found. In a cohort of 529 patients, 4.7% were diagnosed at age 5 or older, primarily with unilateral cases that were frequently misdiagnosed.13 A Turkish series reported that 6% of patients were aged 5 years or older, with common misdiagnoses and treated with enucleation, but excellent survival when the tumor was confined.14 An additional study of children older than 6 years revealed that approximately 30% were misdiagnosed, with 46% of cases manifesting at late stages.15 Contributing to this body of evidence, a retrospective study of 337 patients found that 5.3% were diagnosed after the age of 5, with 78% showing unilateral disease. Approximately half of these older patients (47%) exhibited atypical clinical characteristics, which resulted in a delayed diagnosis.16 Our case is consistent with these studies, demonstrating unilateral retinoblastoma with delayed detection, which was initially misdiagnosed as a congenital cataract. Although older age and atypical presentations are known to increase the likelihood of misdiagnosis, retinoblastoma remains generally recognizable through a simple fundus examination or B-scan ultrasonography. In our patient, the absence of essential diagnostic equipment, trained eye care personnel, and conflict-related limitations in the rural setting resulted in delayed referral and definitive diagnosis.

Since this case documents a rare late presentation of retinoblastoma at eight years of age, it is important to discuss why the tumor may grow or progress at varying rates among children. This variation is influenced by several factors. First, genetic differences play a major role: germline RB1 mutations often lead to early-onset, rapidly progressing, extraocular tumors, whereas sporadic unilateral tumors tend to grow more slowly and may remain intraocular even at older ages.17 In addition, Knudson’s two-hit hypothesis may partially explain the delayed presentation in this sporadic unilateral retinoblastoma. Unlike hereditary cases, both mutational events occur somatically within the same cell lineage, a stochastic process that often results in slower tumor initiation and later clinical onset.18 Second, in some cases, tumors may initiate postnatally, contributing to delayed presentation.19 Third, tumor differentiation and the degree of calcification further affect growth, with well-differentiated, highly calcified tumors generally progressing more slowly.20 Fourth, host immune factors, tumor microenvironment, and individual physiological factors can modulate tumor behavior.21,22 Finally, delays in diagnosis or limited access to specialized care can influence the stage at presentation.11,12 Together, these factors help explain why some children develop extraocular disease at a young age, whereas others—like our patient—present later with tumors confined to the globe. In accordance with these observations, the delayed diagnosis in our case was affected by various underlying factors. To begin with, low parental awareness, as the parents primarily interpreted leukocoria as normal, delayed medical consultation.10,23 Additionally, there was a preliminary misdiagnosis at a rural health center, where the condition was mistaken for a congenital cataract with strabismus secondary to the cataract, which further deferred referral to a specialized center.11 Moreover, the lack of access to specialized services required the patient to travel from a rural area to the capital, Sana’a, for proper assessment, highlighting healthcare challenges in Yemen, which is a low-income country.11,24,25

In this case, the initial preservation of 20/80 visual acuity—which later shifted to 20/200—despite a significant Group D tumor burden is attributed to the tumor’s predominantly peripheral growth pattern. Since the macula and optic nerve were initially spared from direct involvement, central vision remained relatively intact. This anatomical arrangement explains the apparent discrepancy between the advanced clinical stage and the residual visual function observed at presentation.

Interestingly, in our patient, tumor progression showed signs of rapid growth shortly after undergoing a CT scan. Clinically, this was evidenced by a drop in visual acuity from 20/80 to 20/200 and a significant increase in leukocoria, which was also clearly noticed by the parents. While diagnostic CT uses relatively low levels of radiation, experimental studies have indicated that even low-dose ionizing radiation alters tumor biological behavior through mechanisms such as enhanced angiogenesis and cellular invasion. We cannot prove causation from a single case; this temporal association serves as an interesting piece of data and underscores the importance of avoiding unnecessary CT scan exposure in retinoblastoma patients.26–29 Our case emphasizes the significance of meticulous differential diagnosis, as pseudoretinoblastoma can account for up to 50% of referrals for suspected retinoblastoma, with Coats disease acting as the most frequent simulator. This is especially relevant in delayed-presenting retinoblastoma, where the likelihood of pseudoretinoblastoma is comparatively higher, underscoring the necessity of a comprehensive evaluation to avoid misdiagnosis and needless interventions.30,31 Early detection of retinoblastoma is vital to ensure optimal prognoses. This case report highlights the importance of increasing awareness among parents and primary healthcare providers about key warning signs, such as leukocoria and strabismus, which can facilitate immediate medical consultation and referral.32 Even in older children, like our 8-year-old patient, extensive eye examinations are crucial, as delayed or atypical presentations can occur.15 Moreover, clear and efficient referral pathways from primary care to specialized clinics are essential to prevent delays caused by misdiagnosis or limited access to specialized centers.33,34 Implementing urgent referral protocols for suspected retinoblastoma can significantly improve early diagnosis and timely management, ultimately enhancing prognosis.35

In Yemen, facilities for retinoblastoma diagnosis and management are limited. At our center, assessment is based mainly on clinical examination, indirect ophthalmoscopy, and ocular ultrasonography, with MRI used selectively when available; CT is occasionally utilized in settings where MRI access is limited. Advanced globe-salvaging therapies are largely unavailable, making enucleation the most practical life-preserving option for advanced unilateral disease. National data on the burden of retinoblastoma in Yemen are poorly documented due to the lack of a centralized cancer registry, and delayed presentation with advanced-stage disease remains common in this low-income, conflict-affected setting.

Our decision to perform an enucleation in this case was based on multiple considerations. First, the delayed presentation in an 8-year-old child put the eye outside the usual therapeutic timeframe for globe-salvaging procedures.8 Second, the tumor was large and filled most of the vitreous cavity.7 Third, clinical and imaging results demonstrated that the tumor was limited to the globe without optic nerve or extraocular spread, confirming enucleation as a safe and definitive choice.5 Fourth, the restricted availability of advanced therapies in Yemen mandated prioritizing life preservation instead of ocular salvage.11,36 This decision is consistent with international guidelines recommending primary enucleation for advanced unilateral retinoblastoma when vision cannot be maintained and life preservation is the primary objective.37 Following enucleation, the patient was placed under rigorous and periodic follow-up, with ophthalmological and systemic evaluations. To date, we have confirmed the absence of any local recurrence or secondary lesions. The contralateral eye remains completely unremarkable, and the patient’s overall visual function and systemic health are well preserved. Ongoing monitoring is planned to ensure the timely identification of any late complications, consequently emphasizing the essential need for extended clinical observation even after successful enucleation for retinoblastoma.

This case has direct implications for clinical optometry practice, particularly in limited-resource settings, including conflict-affected regions such as Yemen. Optometrists are routinely the first eye care professionals to evaluate children with strabismus or leukocoria. Enhancing primary-level awareness and strengthening referral pathways can substantially reduce misdiagnosis, management delays, and the related risk of life-threatening adverse outcomes.

While this case offers valuable clinical observations, certain limitations must be considered when interpreting the results. This report presents a single patient, which limits generalizability. Due to the lack of advanced molecular diagnostic facilities in Yemen, genetic analysis of the RB1 gene was not performed, preventing correlation between the molecular background and clinical behavior and limiting our ability to assess hereditary risks and provide family counseling. Access to globe-salvaging therapies was restricted in our setting and influenced management decisions. The follow-up duration is relatively short, and longer surveillance is necessary to confirm sustained disease control. Finally, the observed rapid clinical deterioration after CT imaging represents a temporal association, and causality cannot be established.

Conclusion

This case report highlights a rare presentation of retinoblastoma diagnosed outside the typical age of manifestation. This case serves as a cautionary note that retinoblastoma, even though it is most commonly diagnosed in early childhood, can appear later and should remain part of the differential diagnosis in older children and adults with leukocoria and strabismus. The delayed diagnosis observed in this case is in line with previously reported cases documented in the literature, where late presentation—driven by a combination of biological and healthcare-related factors—is frequently associated with low parental awareness, initial misdiagnosis, and barriers to accessing specialized ophthalmic facilities. This case study underscores the critical importance of early recognition, rapid diagnosis, and the use of comprehensive imaging, along with histopathological confirmation, to ensure accurate management and optimize the patient’s prognosis.
