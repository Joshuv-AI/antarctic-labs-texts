Verbascoside Suppresses Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition and Mitochondrial Biogenesis by Targeting Anti-senescence Signaling in Castration-resistant Prostate Cancer
Authors: HSING-CHIA MAI, PEI-FANG HSIEH, CHUN-HSIEN WU, RICHARD C. WU, SIH-HAN CHEN, WEI-LUN HUANG, MU-CHIAO TUNG, YU-LIN YANG, VICTOR C. LIN, CHIANG-TING WANG
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimVerbascoside, a natural phenylethanoid glycoside, has demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC). This study aimed to elucidate the mechanistic pathways through which verbascoside exerts its antisenescence and anti-metastatic effects, focusing on epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), oxidative stress response, and mitochondrial biogenesis regulation in CRPC cells.Materials and MethodsCRPC cell models were treated under various concentrations of verbascoside. EMT markers, oxidative stress-related proteins, mitochondrial biogenesis regulators, and proinflammatory cytokines were assessed using Western blotting and ELISA. Cellular senescence and proliferation were evaluated through analysis of p38 MAPK activation and key cell cycle regulators (p16, p21, p27, and retinoblastoma protein (Rb).ResultsVerbascoside treatment inhibited EMT, reduced oxidative stress markers, and enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis, supporting cellular energy homeostasis. It also suppressed the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, and IL-1, associated with the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. Downstream signaling analysis revealed that verbascoside decreased p38 MAPK activation and down-regulated p16, p21, p27, and Rb, thereby attenuating prosenescence signaling and proliferation control.ConclusionVerbascoside attenuates CRPC progression by modulating EMT, alleviating oxidative damage, enhancing mitochondrial function, and inhibiting prosenescence signaling pathways. These findings highlight its therapeutic potential for targeting senescence-related mechanisms in aggressive prostate cancer and provide a basis for future CRPC management strategies.

Introduction

Verbascoside is a phenylethanoid glycoside derived from various medicinal plants and has demonstrated various pharmacological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer activities (1-8). Owing to these multifaceted biological effects, verbascoside has garnered significant interest for its oncological therapeutic potential, particularly concerning prostate cancer.

Prostate cancer is markedly characterized by its resistance to hormonal therapies and its propensity to undergo epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a critical process that allows for cancer cells to acquire enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities (1,2). EMT is driven by various factors, including oxidative stress and proinflammatory cytokine signaling, both of which are implicated in cancer metastasis (3,4). Recent evidence has suggested that verbascoside can inhibit EMT in cancer cells, potentially limiting metastatic progression (3,5).

Oxidative stress plays a crucial role in promoting EMT and modulating cellular aging processes through reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent pathways. Elevated ROS levels can induce cellular damage, contributing to tumorigenesis and cellular senescence (5-10).

Verbascoside has exhibited significant antioxidant properties, which may counteract oxidative damage and reduce intracellular ROS levels, thereby hampering EMT and delaying senescence (5,11-18). Furthermore, verbascoside up-regulates key components of mitochondrial biogenesis, including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) and nuclear respiratory factor 1 (NRF1), which are vital for sustaining mitochondrial health and cellular energy balance. Enhanced mitochondrial function may mitigate oxidative stress while fulfilling the energy demands of healthy nonsenescent cells.

Regarding cellular senescence, verbascoside has demonstrated potential in modulating the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), a pro-inflammatory state characterized by the secretion of cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, and IL-1 (19-22). These cytokines contribute to a tumor-promoting microenvironment, facilitating cancer cell survival and proliferation. By attenuating SASP factor secretion, verbascoside may decrease protumorigenic inflammation, thereby counteracting cancer cell persistence and growth-favoring processes.

Moreover, verbascoside has been implicated in the regulation of downstream signaling pathways involved in cellular senescence, particularly the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway, as well as key cell cycle regulators, including p16, p21, p27, and retinoblastoma protein (Rb) (9,23,24). The activation of these pathways typically reinforces cell cycle arrest and senescence, thereby limiting cell proliferation. A mechanism for reducing cellular senescence and restoring proliferative control, ultimately diminishing cancer cell viability, may occur following the modulation of these signaling molecules by verbascoside.

Therefore, verbascoside presents a promising multifaceted approach to cancer treatment by inhibiting EMT, reducing oxidative stress, enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis, and modulating senescence-associated cytokine secretion and signaling pathways. Its potential for targeting tumor progression and cellular aging mechanisms highlights its value as a novel therapeutic agent in prostate cancer treatment. To completely elucidate the mechanisms of verbascoside and explore its clinical efficacy in overcoming cancer resistance and metastasis, further research is needed.

Materials and Methods

Verbascoside preparation. Verbascoside was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). To prepare a stock solution, the compound was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), which was subsequently stored at 4˚C to maintain stability. Working concentrations were freshly prepared by diluting the stock solution in the culture media immediately before use, ensuring that the final DMSO concentration in the cell culture medium did not exceed 0.1% (v/v) to avoid any potential cytotoxic effects from the solvent.

Cell culture. LNCap cells were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (Rockville, MD, USA) and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37˚C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Enzalutamide-resistant LNCap cell lines (LNCap Enz-R) were generated through continuous exposure to enzalutamide 5 μM for >6 months. Dr. See-Tong Pang provided the enzalutamide-resistant LNCap cells used in this study.

3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. To evaluate cell proliferation, MTT assay was performed. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Following this initial incubation, the cells were treated with various verbascoside concentrations (0-10 μM) for another 24 h. Subsequently, 10 µl of the MTT solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 6 h to allow for formazan crystal formation. Subsequently, the crystals were dissolved using 100 µl of acidic isopropanol, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm, with 655 nm as the reference wavelength.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay. SOD activity, a critical indicator of cellular antioxidant defense against oxidative stress through superoxide anion (O2-) dismutation, was assessed using a colorimetric assay. The reaction involved nitro blue tetrazolium, which interacts with the superoxide anions to form a blue-colored chromogen. The absorbance of the chromogen was measured at 560 nm, with 650 nm used as the reference wavelength to correct for nonspecific absorbance. SOD activity was quantified using commercially available assay kits from BioVision (Milpitas, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell viability assay. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to grow until reaching 60%-75% confluence. Subsequently, the cells were treated with different NRG1 concentrations in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. After the treatment period, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline. Next, 10 μl of the CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37˚C for 2 h. Using a spectrophotometer (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), the absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Western blot analysis. Cell lysates were prepared using a lysis buffer containing 10-mM Tris, 1-mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM sodium orthovanadate (Na3VO4), 20 μg/ml aprotinin, 20 μg/ml leupeptin, 1 mM dithiothreitol, and 50 μg/ml phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. The lysates were resolved using 10% SDS-PAGE or gradient SDS-PAGE gels (7.5%, 10%, or 12.5%) as required for specific experiments. Proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes using an electroblasting apparatus. The membranes were blocked with 10% (w/v) nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline for 2 h at 37˚C. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight at 4˚C with the following primary antibodies: E-cadherin (ab53033, Abcam, Cambridge, UK; 1:2,000), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) (ab5694, Abcam; 1:2,000), AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) (ab32047, Abcam; 1:2,000), sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) (ab110304, Abcam; 1:2,000), PGC-1α (ab54481, Abcam; 1:2,000), NRF1 (ab34682, Abcam; 1:2,000), and β-actin (A5441, Sigma; 1:4,000). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies: goat antirabbit IgG-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) or donkey antigoat IgG-HRP (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, United States) at a 1:6,000 dilution. Immunoreactive bands were detected using a Western-Ready™ ECL Substrate Plus Kit (426316; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and visualized using the MultiGel-21® imaging system. To verify equal protein loading and transfer, β-actin acted as the loading control. All Western blot experiments were independently repeated three times using biological replicates, and representative results are shown. Densitometry analysis was performed using three biological replicates with technical duplicates for each condition.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. ELISA was used for quantifying the concentrations of secreted IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8 in the supernatant of cultured LNCap cells. The conditioned culture medium was collected from the treated cells and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min to remove any particulates. The clarified supernatant was collected, concentrated, and stored at -80˚C until analysis. To measure IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8 levels, a commercial sandwich ELISA kit (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) was used, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Using an ELISA reader, absorbance was recorded at 450 nm, and cytokine concentrations were calculated by comparing the absorbance values to a standard curve generated with known concentrations of the target proteins.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining. Cells were seeded in six-well tissue culture plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well to assess cellular senescence. Following a 2-day treatment with NRG1, senescence was evaluated using SA-β-Gal staining. Stained cells were visualized and quantified under a light microscope. For each treatment condition, six random fields were selected, and a minimum of 200 cells/field were counted. The experiment was repeated independently three times. The percentage of SA-β-Gal-positive cells was calculated and expressed as the fold induction relative to the control group.

Mitochondrial biogenesis assay. Mitochondrial biogenesis was assessed using the MitoBiogenesis™ In-Cell ELISA Kit (ab110217, Abcam) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde to preserve the cellular structure. Endogenous alkaline phosphatase (AP) activity was blocked by treating the cells with 0.5% acetic acid. The cells were subsequently permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100, followed by adding a 2X blocking solution to prevent nonspecific binding. The assay utilized primary antibodies targeting mitochondrially encoded cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COX-I) and nuclear DNA (nDNA)-encoded succinate dehydrogenase subunit A (SDH-A). Following primary antibody incubation, AP and HRP detection reagents were applied, and color development was initiated using a kinetic reaction. Absorbance was measured at 405 and 600 nm for SDH-A and COX-I, respectively, at 1-min intervals for 15 min. To quantify mitochondrial biogenesis, the ratio of COX-I to SDH-A was subsequently calculated.

Statistical analysis. All experiments were conducted at least three times independently. Data were presented as means±standard deviations in bar graphs. Comparisons among three or more groups were made using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using the PRISM software. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of verbascoside on cell proliferation and viability in hormone-sensitive and hormone-resistant prostate cancer cell models. This study utilized the following two prostate cancer cell models: the hormone-sensitive LNCap cell line and the hormone-resistant variant, LNCap-Enz, developed by treating LNCap cells with enzalutamide for >6 months to simulate a castration-resistant state. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of verbascoside on cell proliferation in both models. Cells were treated with different verbascoside concentrations (0.1, 1, and 10 µM), and the MTT assay was used for assessing cell proliferation.

As shown in Figure 1A, treatment with increasing verbascoside doses (0.1, 1, and 10 µM) dose-dependently decreases cell proliferation in the LNCap cell line. Similarly, in the hormone-resistant LNCap-Enz cells, verbascoside significantly inhibited cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 1B). Notably, the highest verbascoside dose (10 µM) led to an even greater reduction in proliferation in LNCap-Enz cells than that in the LNCap cells, suggesting that the hormone-resistant cell line was more sensitive to verbascoside.

(A) The hormone-sensitive LNCap cell line shows a dose-dependent reduction in cell proliferation upon treatment with verbascoside at concentrations of 0.1, 1, and 10 μM. (B) Similarly, the hormone-resistant LNCap-Enz cell line, treated with the same verbascoside doses (0.1, 1, and 10 μM), exhibits an even greater cell proliferation inhibition than LNCap, indicating increased sensitivity. (C) The viability of LNCap cells dose-dependently decreases following verbascoside treatment. (D) The LNCap-Enz cell line demonstrates a further reduction in viability across the same dose range of verbascoside, surpassing the effect observed in the LNCap cells. These results suggest that verbascoside has a stronger inhibitory effect on both cell proliferation and viability in hormone-resistant prostate cancer cells.

Furthermore, to assess cell viability, trypan blue exclusion assays were used. The results showed that, in the LNCap (Figure 1C) and LNCap-Enz cells (Figure 1D), increasing verbascoside concentrations (0.1, 1, and 10 µM) corresponded to a progressive reduction in cell viability, confirming a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect. These findings indicate that verbascoside effectively reduces prostate cancer cell proliferation and viability, particularly in the hormone-resistant LNCap-Enz cells, suggesting its potential role in modulating prostate cancer cell growth.

Verbascoside inhibited the EMT in hormone-sensitive and hormone-resistant prostate cancer cells. We analyzed the effects of verbascoside on the EMT in prostate cancer cells. EMT is a process commonly observed in cancer cells wherein epithelial markers are down-regulated and mesenchymal markers are up-regulated, promoting cell motility and invasiveness (25). In this study, to assess the impact on EMT-related protein expression, LNCap cells (representing hormone-sensitive prostate cancer) and LNCap-Enz cells (enzalutamide-treated LNCap cells for >6 months to simulate a hormone-resistant state) were treated with increasing verbascoside concentrations (0, 0.1, 1, and 10 µM). As shown in Figure 2A, the treatment of LNCap cells with verbascoside dose-dependently increases the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin, indicating enhanced cellular stability and adhesion. Concurrently, the expression of the mesenchymal marker SMA dose-dependently decreased, suggesting reduced cellular motility. Additionally, the expression of the EMT-promoting transcription factors, Snail and Slug, was progressively down-regulated with increasing verbascoside concentrations, indicating a shift toward a more epithelial-like phenotype with characteristics closer to those of non-transformed cells. Similarly, the analysis of the hormone-resistant LNCap-Enz cells (Figure 2B), revealed that verbascoside treatment led to an even greater induction of E-cadherin expression, stabilizing cell adhesion and inhibiting movement. Concurrently, a dose-dependent decrease in SMA expression was noted, further suggesting decreased cell migration. Furthermore, the EMT transcription factors, Snail and Slug, showed dose-dependent down-regulation with verbascoside treatment, reinforcing the observation that verbascoside promotes the reversion of these cells to an epithelial-like state.

These results suggest that verbascoside effectively inhibits EMT in hormone-sensitive and hormone-resistant prostate cancer cells, with a more pronounced effect noted in the hormone-resistant LNCap-Enz cells. This finding suggests that by promoting epithelial characteristics and inhibiting mesenchymal traits, verbascoside can contribute to reducing the metastatic potential of prostate cancer.

Verbascoside reduced SASP factors in hormone-sensitive and hormone-resistant prostate cancer cells. Cellular senescence acts as a natural barrier against tumorigenesis. Senescent cells exert both protumorigenic and antitumorigenic effects through cell-autonomous and paracrine mechanisms, with excessive secretion of factors termed the SASP. Depending on their environment, senescent cells can exert beneficial or harmful effects, and SASP factors serve as the primary mediators of senescent cell communication with adjacent cells, including stromal bystanders, immune cells, premalignant cells, and cancer cells. SASP is predominantly linked to chronic inflammatory signaling and includes pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8 (21,22,26-28). This study aimed to analyze the expression levels of these SASP factors (IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8) in two prostate cancer cell lines, LNCap and hormone-resistant LNCap-Enz, following treatment with increasing verbascoside concentrations (0.1, 1, and 10 µM).

Using ELISA, we measured the extracellular IL-6 levels following verbascoside treatment. In LNCap cells, IL-6 secretion dose-dependently decreased with increasing verbascoside concentrations (0.1, 1, and 10 µM) (Figure 3A). Similarly, in LNCap-Enz cells, IL-6 secretion dose-dependently decreased with verbascoside treatment (Figure 3D).

Moreover, we investigated the IL-1 expression in LNCap cells following verbascoside treatment. As illustrated in Figure 3B, IL-1 levels dose-dependently decrease with increasing verbascoside concentrations (0.1, 1, and 10 µM). In LNCap-Enz cells, a similar dose-dependent reduction in IL-1 secretion was observed following verbascoside treatment (Figure 3E).

Finally, we analyzed IL-8 secretion in LNCap cells following verbascoside treatment. Supernatants collected from treated cells showed a dose-dependent reduction in IL-8 levels with increasing verbascoside concentrations (Figure 3C). Similarly, in LNCap-Enz cells, IL-8 secretion dose-dependently decreased following verbascoside treatment (Figure 3F).

The results indicate that verbascoside effectively reduces the secretion of the SASP factors, including IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8, in both LNCap and LNCap-Enz cells. The observed dose-dependent suppression of these SASP markers suggests that verbascoside can attenuate senescence-associated proinflammatory signaling, thereby potentially modulating senescence markers in prostate cancer cells. Additionally, to analyze the cellular senescence status, we employed SA-β-Gal staining. As shown in Figure 3G, LNCap cells exhibit significant SA-β-Gal expression, which decreases with increasing verbascoside concentrations (0.1, 1, and 10 µM). A similar trend was observed in LNCap-Enz cells (Figure 3H), wherein high levels of SA-β-Gal staining were also noted. Treatment with increasing verbascoside doses effectively reduced the expression of this senescence marker in LNCap-Enz cells, reinforcing that verbascoside may play a crucial role in mitigating cellular senescence across different prostate cancer models.

Verbascoside enhanced antioxidant defense by increasing the SOD activity in hormone-sensitive and hormone-resistant prostate cancer cells. Oxidative reactions play a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and metabolic processes; however, the resulting free radicals can induce significant cellular damage over time. This damage may impair organ function, trigger inflammatory responses, promote apoptosis, and contribute to disease development and aging. Therefore, oxidative stress is closely related to aging, and antioxidant mechanisms exert a protective effect against aging-related cellular damage (5,9,29).

We here examined the effects of verbascoside on oxidative stress resistance in prostate cancer cell lines, specifically LNCap and LNCap-Enz. To assess the impact on antioxidant defense markers, particularly SOD activity, both cell lines were treated with increasing verbascoside concentrations (0.1, 1, and 10 µM). As shown in Figure 4, panels (A) and (B) illustrate the dose-dependent increase in SOD activation in LNCap and LNCap-Enz cells, respectively. Higher verbascoside concentrations significantly enhanced the SOD activity, indicating improved antioxidant capacity in both cell types.

Verbascoside stimulated mitochondrial biogenesis and enhanced mitochondrial function in prostate cancer cells. Recent studies on aging have emphasized mitochondrial biogenesis and functional decline as key mechanisms underlying cellular aging. Oxidative stress increases mitochondrial mass and activates mitochondrial biogenesis pathways, which counteract oxidative damage by slowing cell cycle progression and enhancing antioxidant signaling.

Herein, we analyzed mitochondrial biogenesis in response to verbascoside treatment in prostate cancer cells. To evaluate the expression ratio of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-encoded COX-I to nDNA-encoded SDH-A, mtDNA and nDNA were assessed using a commercially available kit. A higher ratio indicates increased mitochondrial biogenesis activity.

As shown in Figure 4B, the treatment of LNCap cells with the highest verbascoside dose (10 µM) causes a marked increase in mitochondrial biogenesis starting at 5-6 min post-treatment. Similarly, Figure 4D demonstrates that in another prostate cancer cell line, mitochondrial biogenesis is enhanced as early as 3 min post-treatment. These results indicate that verbascoside promotes mitochondrial biogenesis in prostate cancer cells, suggesting its potential for enhancing mitochondrial function and counteracting oxidative stress through mitochondrial pathway activation.

Verbascoside induced mitochondrial biogenesis via AMPK pathway activation in prostate cancer cells. We analyzed the expression of key mitochondrial biogenesis-related proteins, including AMPK, SIRT1, NRF1, PGC1, and mTOR, to further investigate the effects of verbascoside on mitochondrial biogenesis. As shown in Figure 5A, the treatment of LNCap cells with increasing verbascoside doses causes a dose-dependent activation of AMPK, SIRT1, NRF1, and PGC1, along with mTOR expression suppression. Similarly, in Figure 5C, the treatment of LNCap-Enz cells with the highest verbascoside dose (10 µM) demonstrates a dose-dependent activation of the mitochondrial signaling proteins, including AMPK, SIRT1, NRF1, and PGC1, along with mTOR expression inhibition. These findings support that verbascoside promotes mitochondrial biogenesis in prostate cancer cells by AMPK signaling pathway activation.

Verbascoside modulated oxidative stress-induced senescence pathways and enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis in prostate cancer cells. To evaluate the impact of verbascoside on the oxidative stress-induced expression of senescence-associated proteins, we treated LNCap cells with different verbascoside doses and employed the oxidative signaling inhibitor, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), to suppress oxidative stress signaling. We analyzed the expression of key senescence markers, including p38MAPK, P16, P27, P21, and Rb. As shown in Figure 6A, treatment with increasing verbascoside doses combined with NAC in LNCap cells reveal a significant modulation of oxidative stress and senescence protein pathways. Specifically, in NAC-treated cells exposed to the highest verbascoside dose (10 µM), we observed increased p38MAPK, P16, P27, and P21 expression, accompanied by decreased Rb expression, compared with cells without NAC treatment. Furthermore, a marked suppression of the senescence pathway proteins p38MAPK, P16, P27, and P21 as well as increased Rb protein levels were noted when cells were treated with NAC followed by verbascoside (10 µM) or verbascoside alone.

Similar patterns are observed in the LNCap-Enz cells, as illustrated in Figure 6C. Treatment with NAC alone increased the expression of the senescence pathway proteins p38MAPK, P16, P27, and P21 while reducing Rb levels. However, in cells treated with both NAC and verbascoside (10 µM) or with verbascoside alone, the p38MAPK, P16, P27, and P21 expression was suppressed, and Rb expression was increased. These findings suggest that verbascoside (10 µM) mitigates senescence signaling by modulating oxidative stress, thereby enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and reducing cellular senescence, potentially inhibiting the EMT in prostate cancer cells, as shown in Figure 7.

This study demonstrates that verbascoside, by inhibiting oxidative stress, activates SOD and mitochondrial biogenesis-related signaling proteins. Discussion

The findings of the study emphasize the complex effects of verbascoside on prostate cancer cells, particularly regarding hormone-sensitive (LNCap) and hormone-resistant (LNCap-Enz) models. Our results demonstrate that verbascoside significantly and dose-dependently inhibits cell proliferation and viability in both cell lines, with a markedly greater effect observed in the hormone-resistant LNCap-Enz cells. This finding suggests that verbascoside can offer therapeutic potential for managing advanced prostate cancer, particularly in cases wherein resistance to conventional therapies, including enzalutamide, develops.

Previous studies have reported that the development of resistance to androgen receptor-targeted therapies, including enzalutamide, is a significant challenge in the treatment of advanced prostate cancer (14,30-34). The enhanced sensitivity of LNCap-Enz cells to verbascoside observed in our study aligns with emerging evidence that resistant cancer phenotypes can be effectively targeted by natural compounds (16,35-37). The ability of verbascoside to inhibit proliferation in these resistant cells may be attributed to its influence on various signaling pathways involved in cell growth and survival, including EMT and SASP modulation (13,21,22,38,39).

Moreover, the observed dose-dependent effects suggest a potential for optimizing the verbascoside dosage in clinical settings, particularly for patients with CRPC. The ability of verbascoside to exert more pronounced effects on resistant cells could inhibit tumor growth while potentially overcoming therapeutic resistance. Therefore, further investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying verbascoside’s action could reveal additional therapeutic targets and enhance treatment strategies for advanced prostate cancer (23,24).

In conclusion, our findings substantiate the potential of verbascoside as a therapeutic agent for prostate cancer, particularly in hormone-resistant contexts. To explore its efficacy in vivo and its potential integration into existing therapeutic regimens, future studies are warranted.

EMT inhibition represents a critical mechanism by which verbascoside exerts its anticancer effects. EMT is characterized by increased cell motility and invasiveness, which are key contributors to cancer metastasis (3,13,25,30). Our results demonstrate that verbascoside treatment causes a significant increase in E-cadherin expression, along with decreased SMA and the EMT transcription factors, Snail and Slug. In the hormone-resistant LNCap-Enz cells, this shift toward a more epithelial phenotype is particularly pronounced, suggesting that verbascoside could play a substantial role in reducing the metastatic potential of prostate cancer (2,11,35,36). This aligns with recent findings by Won et al. (2025) (40), who demonstrated that natural compounds can effectively target EMT-associated signaling to suppress cancer progression. Such studies further support our observation that verbascoside reduces the metastatic potential of prostate cancer through EMT inhibition.

Moreover, this study elucidates the impact of verbascoside on the SASP. The observed reduction in proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1, IL-6, and IL-8, in both LNCap and LNCap-Enz cells indicates that verbascoside can mitigate cellular senescence while modulating the inflammatory microenvironment associated with tumor progression. This modulation is crucial because SASP factors can promote tumorigenesis through paracrine signaling, thereby contributing to a hostile tumor microenvironment (16,21,38,41-43). Verbascoside may help develop a less favorable environment for tumor growth and metastasis by addressing the SASP.

The implications of these findings transcend the immediate effects of verbascoside on cell behavior. By EMT inhibition and SASP modulation, verbascoside may enhance the efficacy of existing therapeutic strategies for prostate cancer, particularly in cases wherein resistance to conventional treatments is observed (34,44). To fully elucidate the potential of verbascoside as a therapeutic agent in prostate cancer management, further investigation into the molecular pathways involved in these processes is necessary. Notably, Bass (2019) (45) emphasized the significance of EMT and microenvironmental changes in prostate cancer progression and therapy resistance, which is consistent with our results showing that verbascoside suppresses SASP-associated cytokines and EMT drivers.

Oxidative stress is a well-established contributor to cellular aging and cancer progression, frequently leading to genomic instability and tumor development (46). Our findings demonstrate that by increasing SOD activity in both prostate cancer cell lines, antioxidant defenses are significantly enhanced by verbascoside. This antioxidant capacity enhancement may protect cells from oxidative damage, thereby contributing to cellular resilience and potentially slowing cancer progression (37,47,48).

Moreover, the ability of verbascoside to stimulate mitochondrial biogenesis represents a significant feature of its mechanism. The observed increase in markers of mitochondrial biogenesis following verbascoside treatment suggests that it promotes mitochondrial function, which is crucial for maintaining energy homeostasis and cellular health (48). The activation of key signaling pathways, particularly AMPK, SIRT1, and PGC-1α, reinforces the hypothesis that verbascoside enhances mitochondrial function, potentially counteracting the deleterious effects of oxidative stress and cellular senescence (27,49-54).

Finally, the modulation of oxidative stress-induced senescence pathways by verbascoside, as evidenced by changes in the expression of senescence markers, including p38 MAPK, p16, p21, and Rb, emphasizes its potential to influence cellular aging processes. By reducing the expression of prosenescence markers and enhancing Rb levels, verbascoside may effectively inhibit the senescence-associated decline in cellular function, which is frequently intensified in cancer (22,26,52,55,56). Our findings are also consistent with Saraji (2024) (57), who highlighted the role of natural compounds in modulating senescence and mitochondrial signaling in CRPC models (58-60). Together, these studies strengthen the rationale for verbascoside as a therapeutic candidate in advanced prostate cancer.

In conclusion, our study provides compelling evidence that verbascoside possesses significant anticancer properties by inhibiting cell proliferation, EMT, and SASP while enhancing antioxidant defenses and mitochondrial biogenesis. These findings suggest that verbascoside could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for CRPC management, warranting further investigation into its clinical applications and mechanisms of action.
