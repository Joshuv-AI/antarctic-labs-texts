Mitochondrial redox imbalance in triggering regulated cell death in kidney diseases
Authors: Wenping Zhu, Ran Zou, Ruotian Liu, Zhanjun Jia, Yue Zhang, Mengqiu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) are critical signaling mediators that maintain renal cell viability and physiological activity, participating in antimicrobial immunity, autophagy, and oxidative stress responses. However, imbalance between mtROS production and antioxidant defenses leads to mtROS accumulation, and the excessive accumulation of mtROS disrupts cellular homeostasis by inducing key signal molecules and oxidizing specific redox-sensitive cellular macromolecules (DNA, proteins, and lipids), causing oxidative stress. This stress can trigger diverse forms of regulated cell death (RCD) in the kidneys, including autophagy, apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis, cuproptosis, and PANoptosis, each with distinct genetic and biochemical features. Understanding oxidative cell death in the kidney informs the development of mtROS-targeted therapeutics for kidney disorders. This review provides a concise overview on the role of mitochondrial redox imbalance in initiating various types of RCD in kidney diseases, aiming to improve the understanding of cell death mechanisms and provide new insights for disease treatment.

Introduction

The kidney is considered as one of the body’s most energy-demanding organs, with substantial energy required to maintain various metabolic processes, plasma hemodynamics, and hydroelectrolyte homeostasis. The kidney primarily eliminates metabolic wastes through glomerular filtration. Subsequently, the glomerular filtrate—containing water, ions, and nutrients—is reabsorbed in the proximal tubules and the thick ascending limbs of the loop of Henle. Approximately 80% of the filtrate is reabsorbed by the proximal tubules, a process driven by the high density of Na+/K+-ATPase pumps on the basolateral membrane, which continuously consume ATP to sustain sodium transport (the primary energy consumer).1 Under normal physiological conditions, this energy demand is met mainly through mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation system (OXPHOS). In general, all cell types in the kidney require ATP to maintain cellular functions. However, different segments of the renal tubule exhibit distinct preferences for energy substrates, adapting to their specific functions and microenvironments. The proximal tubule has low glycolytic capacity but high energy demand. The oxidation of one molecule of palmitate yields 106 molecules of ATP, whereas glucose oxidation generates only 36 molecules of ATP.2 To maximize ATP production, the proximal tubule relies almost entirely on fatty acid β-oxidation for energy production. However, due to its high oxygen consumption, the proximal tubule is more susceptible to ischemic and hypoxic injury compared to cells in other segments.3 In contrast, the distal tubule and the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle exhibit high glycolytic activity,4 primarily utilizing energy derived from glucose breakdown to support reabsorption and secretion, generating approximately one-third of the ATP produced by the proximal tubule. Furthermore, in the collecting duct, where oxygen tension in the medulla is relatively low, ATP production depends mainly on anaerobic glycolysis. This process is characterized by the conversion of glucose to pyruvate and subsequently to lactate, yielding a net gain of two ATP molecules per glucose molecule.5,6 Additionally, ATP can be converted to GTP via the transphosphorylation action of nucleoside-diphosphate kinase, which transfers the high-energy phosphate group from ATP to GDP. GTP plays a critical role in maintaining tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle flux and mediating inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) fusion and cristae remodeling.7,8,9 Mitochondrial GTP is primarily confined to the matrix, and its concentration directly reflects the operational rate of the TCA cycle. By inhibiting glutamate dehydrogenase, GTP helps sustain TCA cycle metabolic flux homeostasis.7 GTP also provides energy for various mitochondria-dynamic-related GTPases, such as the dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) and the fusion proteins Mitofusin 1 and Mitofusin 2 and optic atrophy 1, thereby promoting mitochondrial fusion.8,9 Thus, the preservation of mitochondrial homeostasis and function is essential for maintaining optimal kidney performance.10

It is noteworthy that mitochondria also generate substantial amounts of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) during ETCs, particularly in response to external stimuli such as hypoxia, pharmacological agents, and energy depletion. MtROS, including superoxide anion (·O2−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), are primarily generated through enzymatic reactions within the electron transport chain (ETC) and during OXPHOS driven by the proton gradient. A moderate level of mtROS is essential for maintaining renal cell viability and function. However, due to its high oxidative metabolic activity, the kidney is particularly susceptible to mtROS-mediated injury. Stimulus-induced abnormal production of mitochondrial ·O2−/H2O2 causes oxidative damage of DNA, proteins, and lipids, thereby promoting the activation of multiple regulated cell death (RCD) pathways, ultimately contributing to the progression of renal disease.11

Our group has long been engaged in the research of mitochondrial dysfunction and kidney disease and was among the first in the international research community to propose that mitochondrial dysfunction constitutes an early event in the pathogenesis of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and that preserving mitochondrial function could serve as an effective early intervention target.12,13,14 Over the years, we have also proved that different forms of RCD driven by mitochondrial dysfunction serve as pivotal contributors to the pathogenesis of kidney diseases, with mtROS acting as a crucial factor in both the initiation and advancement.15,16,17,18,19,20 Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms that regulate mtROS production and activity is essential for designing innovative, targeted therapeutic strategies for kidney diseases. This review firstly elucidates the link between mitochondrial dysfunction and mtROS in renal pathologies. Then, the interactions between mtROS and various RCD pathways are examined. Finally, therapeutic strategies aimed at limiting mtROS production or enhancing its scavenging are summarized.

Mitochondrial injury and mitochondrial oxidative stress

As indispensable signaling molecules, mtROS participate in physiological processes such as metabolic adaptation, immune cell function orchestrating, and pathogen defense. In key nutrient-sensing tissues such as pancreatic β cells, the hypothalamus, and adipose tissue, finely balanced mtROS production is essential for the maintenance of normal metabolism.21 Furthermore, mtROS can act in concert with Ca2+ as integrated signals to coordinate cellular, tissue, and organismal responses to changes in nutrient supply and demand.22 Immune cells maintain optimal mtROS concentrations to sustain normal physiological functions.23 Basal levels of mtROS have been identified as positive regulators of Th17 cell differentiation and function, a process mediated by the increased formation of mtROS following T cell activation.24 mtROS generated at complex I following T cell receptor (TCR) stimulation drive T cell activation through the nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and active protein 1 (AP-1) signaling pathway.25 Mice administered with antioxidants exhibit impaired immunity following viral infection, indicating that mtROS are indispensable for T cell function.26 It is noteworthy that non-selective scavenging of mtROS disrupts these physiological functions. Clinically, broad-spectrum antioxidant strategies have demonstrated limited efficacy and, in some cases, have led to adverse outcomes. For instance, the antioxidant beta carotene was originally intended for the prevention of cardiovascular disease; however, its non-specific antioxidant effects increased the risk of lung cancer.27 Similarly, vitamin E supplementation has been associated with an increased risk of heart failure.28 Therefore, achieving localized mtROS modulation while preserving physiological systemic mtROS signaling is critical for antioxidant-based therapeutic strategies.

mtROS release results from the balance between ROS generation by the ETC and their subsequent elimination by mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes. Under normal OXPHOS conditions, only a small fraction of electrons leak and react with molecular oxygen at complexes I and III, forming limited amounts of ·O2−. Complex I transfers two electrons from nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) to ubiquinone (Q). Within its hydrophilic domain, flavin mononucleotide (FMN) accepts two electrons, forming FMNH2.29 Electrons subsequently pass through a series of iron–sulfur clusters, where Q is ultimately reduced to ubiquinol (QH2) at the Q-binding pocket. During this process, ·O2− is generated by complex I at two primary sites within the matrix: the IF (FMN site) and the IQ (Q-binding site).30 Complex III is recognized as the predominant site of superoxide in the ETC, facilitated by the Q cycle. Most ·O2− in complex III is produced via auto-oxidation of ubisemiquinone, where QH· acts as a key intermediate in electron leakage. In the Q cycle, QH· can directly donate electrons to O2, generating ·O2−. MtROS production is maintained in balance with intracellular scavenging under physiological conditions. However, under pathological conditions such as hypoxia, drug exposure, hyperlipidemia, and hyperglycemia, this equilibrium is readily disrupted, leading to mtROS accumulation. Dysregulated mtROS production is primarily attributed to ETC dysfunction and metabolic disturbances (Table 1). Hypoxic signaling is predominantly mediated by the mitochondrial ETC, with complex III serving as a critical site for ROS generation during hypoxia. Hypoxia prolongs the lifespan of the ubisemiquinone radical in complex III, facilitating electron transfer to oxygen and resulting in superoxide production.31 Additionally, hypoxia induces substantial succinate accumulation, and its enhanced oxidation upon reperfusion drives RET into complex I, triggering mtROS bursts.32,33 Certain clinical drugs, such as cisplatin and aminoglycoside antibiotics, can also induce excessive mtROS production by interfering with mitochondrial ETC function. Cisplatin can accumulate within mitochondria and directly inhibit the enzymatic activities of complexes I, II, III, and IV, increasing electron leakage and thereby inducing a mitochondrial-dependent ROS response.34 Notably, beyond impairing ETC function, cisplatin treatment leads to a significant reduction in the levels of the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-encoded protein MT-CO1, contributing to mtROS generation through mtDNA damage.46 Aminoglycosides, another class of commonly used clinical drugs, are considered to exert nephrotoxicity primarily through the inhibition of mitochondrial ETC activity and subsequent excessive ROS production.35 The hyperlipidemic environment drives lipid metabolic reprogramming in renal tubular epithelial cells (TECs), characterized by impaired fatty acid oxidation and lipid peroxidation, which in turn causes mitochondrial dysfunction and elevates mtROS production.36 Concurrently, hyperglycemia induces a metabolic shift in mitochondria from OXPHOS toward aerobic glycolysis. Causes of dysregulated mtROS production and impaired clearance mechanisms and the associated mechanisms

Additionally, dysregulation of protective mechanisms, such as NADPH depletion, impaired mitophagy, and compromised antioxidant systems, also serves as critical factors contributing to excessive mtROS production. The mitochondrial glutathione peroxidase (GPx) system and thioredoxin peroxidase (Prx) system rely on NADPH to scavenge H2O2 generated by the respiratory chain. As a cofactor for glutathione reductase (GR), NADPH provides electrons for GR to regenerate glutathione (GSH).38 When NADPH is depleted, GR activity declines, and GSH regeneration is hindered, resulting in the inability of the GPx system to clear H2O2, thereby leading to mtROS accumulation.38 Similarly, NADPH supports the regeneration of reduced thioredoxin; NADPH depletion impairs antioxidant system function, leading to increased mtROS and oxidative damage.39 Mitophagy, as a mitochondrial quality control mechanism, is primarily responsible for eliminating damaged mitochondria, preventing the accumulation of injured mitochondria and the elevation of ROS, thereby avoiding oxidative stress and cell death.40 It should be noted that although mitophagy initially functions as an adaptive and protective mechanism, it becomes dysfunctional under persistent oxidative stress. The excessive mtROS generated in turn further inhibits mitophagic function, amplifying oxidative stress.41 Furthermore, impairment of the mitochondrial antioxidant system also contributes to mtROS accumulation. Under stress conditions, the mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme systems—including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), Prx, and GPx—are activated to restore redox homeostasis. These four enzymes play essential roles in counteracting oxidative stress and scavenging mtROS. SOD, primarily represented by Cu/Zn-SOD in the intermembrane space and Mn-SOD in the matrix, catalyzes the dismutation of ·O2− to H2O2 within mitochondria. By scavenging ·O2− at its source, SOD serves as the starting point of the antioxidant cascade.42 The H2O2 generated then becomes the substrate for CAT, GPx, and Prx; without SOD, ·O2− cannot be converted into forms that can be processed by other enzymes. CAT, expressed in peroxisomes, is a major player in responding to acute, burst-like H2O2. It exhibits an extremely high reaction rate, rapidly decomposing H2O2 into O2 and H2O under conditions of elevated H2O2 concentration without consuming NADPH or GSH, thereby reducing intracellular H2O2 levels.43 GPx and thioredoxin peroxidase utilize reduced GSH and thioredoxin as substrates, respectively, to convert H2O2 into H2O within the mitochondrial matrix.44 GPx is also capable of scavenging lipid peroxides, which is crucial for protecting cell membrane integrity and preventing lipid peroxidation chain reactions.45 These four antioxidant enzymes collectively constitute a hierarchical and synergistic network that scavenges mtROS and assists the organism in resisting oxidative stress damage. When the antioxidant system is impaired or collapses, the body fails to effectively eliminate excess reactive oxygen species, leading to uncontrolled oxidative stress. This, in turn, triggers widespread oxidative damage to biological macromolecules (DNA, proteins, lipids), cellular dysfunction, and death, ultimately inducing various diseases.

Due to their substantial energy requirements, kidneys are particularly vulnerable to mitochondrial dysfunction. Under physiological conditions, mtROS production remains finely balanced with the antioxidant system. In the context of renal dysfunction, this balance is disrupted due to both increased mtROS generation and impaired antioxidant defense. Up to now, no study has quantified the relative contributions of these two factors to mtROS accumulation under pathological conditions; nevertheless, it is undeniable that both play important roles. The relative contribution of enhanced mtROS production versus impaired antioxidant scavenging is not fixed but rather depends on the pathological context, disease stage, and tissue type. In acute settings such as ischemia-reperfusion injury, succinate accumulates during ischemia and is rapidly oxidized by succinate dehydrogenase upon reperfusion, driving a burst of mtROS production that can overwhelm even intact antioxidant systems.47 Conversely, in chronic conditions such as diabetic kidney disease (DKD) or CKD, the core pathophysiological mechanism involves persistent low-grade ROS production, coupled with progressive NADPH depletion and downregulation of antioxidant enzymes, collectively contributing to sustained oxidative stress.48,49

The role of mtROS in kidney-disease-associated RCD

Autophagy

Autophagy, first proposed by Ashford and Porter in 1962, is a complex biological process that serves as a critical cellular homeostatic mechanism, essential for maintaining intracellular equilibrium.50,51 As the primary intracellular degradation system, macroautophagy (hereafter referred to as autophagy) delivers cytoplasmic components to lysosomes for breakdown through autophagosomes.51 Mitophagy is an autophagic process that mediates the specific recognition and degradation of damaged, dysfunctional, or superfluous mitochondria. In response to environmental and intracellular stress, autophagy can provide cells with new building blocks and energy to support repair and the maintenance of homeostasis. However, it can also contribute to cellular dysfunction and organ damage.

Autophagy is initiated through activation of the Unc-51-like kinase 1 (ULK1) complex, which consists of ULK1, autophagy-related protein 13 (ATG13), and AK-family-kinase-interacting protein of 200 kDa (FIP200). Under conditions of starvation or stress, autophagy is initiated by the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathways. The mTORC1 is dissociated from the ULK1 complex following AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of Rheb and Raptor, thereby relieving its inhibitory effect on ULK1.52 Concurrently, ULK1 is phosphorylated by AMPK at Serine 317 and Serine 777, which facilitates its interaction with ATG13 and FIP200 and promotes the formation of autophagic vesicles.53 Pro-autophagic signals are transduced by ULK1 through the phosphorylation of multiple substrate proteins, including Beclin-1 at Ser15, leading to activation of class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase complex I (PI3KC3-C1) complex. The PI3KC3 complex recruits the ATG5-ATG12-ATG16L conjugate and the ATG8/LC3 system via WD repeat domain, phosphoinositide interacting 2 (WIPI2), creating a positive feedback loop that promotes ubiquitination until membrane scission completes autophagosome formation.54,55 ATG4 processes LC3 to generate LC3-I, which is then activated by the E1-like enzyme ATG7. Following activation, LC3-I is subsequently transferred to conjugate with phosphatidylethanolamine to form LC3-II. LC3-II is then incorporated into the expanding phagophore membrane, facilitating the maturation and eventual formation of the autophagosome. The autophagosome subsequently fuses with lysosomes, during which ATG proteins are removed from its outer membrane, resulting in the formation of the mature autolysosome.

Mitophagy, the most well-characterized form of selective autophagy, is initiated when autophagosomes encapsulate damaged mitochondria. This process is primarily mediated by LC3-associated autophagy receptors operating through either ubiquitin (Ub)-dependent or Ub-independent pathways. Ub-dependent mitophagy is exemplified by the phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN)-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)-parkin pathway. Under physiological mitochondrial conditions, PINK1 is processed by presenilin-associated rhomboid-like protease (PARL), which facilitates its degradation via the Ub-proteasome system. This process maintains low levels of PINK1 and prevents aberrant activation of mitophagy. During mitochondrial dysfunction and associated depolarization (loss of MMP), the accumulation of mtROS inhibits the proteolytic activity of PARL. PINK1 translocation to the IMM is blocked, allowing it to evade PARL-mediated degradation. Consequently, PINK1 accumulates on the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) and undergoes autophosphorylation.56,57 OMM-associated PINK1 then recruits and phosphorylates Parkin, an E3 ubiquitin ligase, allowing Parkin to attach phospho-ubiquitin chains to multiple OMM proteins.58,59 These ubiquitinated mitochondria are recognized by autophagy adaptors (e.g., p62/SQSTM1) containing both Ub-binding domains and LC3-interacting regions. This recognition event bridges the ubiquitinated cargo to the LC3-decorated autophagosomal membrane, facilitating engulfment. The autophagosome fuses with lysosomes to form an autolysosome, thereby mediating the degradation of mitochondria. In the kidney, autophagy and mitophagy are essential for maintaining the structural integrity and physiological functions of resident cells, including podocytes, proximal TECs, and mesangial cells. Under pathological or stress conditions, mtROS function as a double-edged sword, with their concentration dictating opposing biological outcomes. At low concentrations, mtROS can induce protective autophagy that eliminates damaged organelles and maintains cellular homeostasis. In contrast, elevated mtROS levels not only cause direct damage to mitochondrial membranes and DNA but also promote pathological processes, including apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and fibrosis, via exacerbated oxidative stress.

Notably, mtROS are emerging as critical signaling molecules that finely regulate both autophagy and mitophagy. Specifically, mtROS can trigger mitophagy, leading to the selective encapsulation and degradation of damaged mitochondria, thereby preventing cellular injury.56,61,62 This creates a feedback loop: when damaged mitochondria produce excessive mtROS, mitophagy is rapidly activated to eliminate the dysfunctional organelles and mitigate further injury. However, Raimundo et al. demonstrated that excessive mtROS accumulation—resulting from mitochondrial dysfunction or oxidative stress—can lead to aberrant autophagy. In this scenario, autophagosomes fail to efficiently clear damaged mitochondria, which exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and creates a vicious cycle of escalating mtROS production and autophagic failure.63

The dysregulation of autophagy and mitophagy has been linked to the development of multiple kidney disorders. During the early stage of acute kidney injury (AKI), autophagy is activated in proximal tubular cells as an intrinsic protective response.64 Similarly, mitophagy has been demonstrated to exert protective effects in both AKI and DKD. For instance, mitophagy is activated in proximal tubules following ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), whereas deficiencies in PINK1 or Parkin exacerbate renal injury and mtROS production.65 However, renal biopsies from patients after an AKI episode reveal sustained elevation of autophagy compared to non-AKI controls. This persistent autophagic activity can induce a pro-fibrotic phenotypic transformation in tubular cells.66 Thus, the timely termination of autophagy during the recovery phase is critical to permit tubular cell proliferation and facilitate renal repair, given that sustained autophagy impairs the proliferative capacity of these cells.67 Therefore, maintaining optimal levels of autophagy and mitophagy is essential for kidney homeostasis. Consequently, the modulation of mtROS to fine-tune autophagic flux emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy for kidney diseases.68,69

Apoptosis

Apoptosis, the first recognized form of RCD, is characterized by nuclear changes, DNA fragmentation, chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, and the maturation of apoptotic bodies containing the remnants of dying cells.70 Apoptosis primarily occurs through two main pathways: the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway and the extrinsic (death receptor) pathway. The intrinsic pathway is initiated by cellular stress. Upon detection of these signals, BH3-only proteins act as pivotal initiators that directly activate Bax and Bak, pro-apoptotic members of the Bcl-2 family. This activation induces OMM permeabilization, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and cytochrome c (Cyt c) release.71 Cyt c subsequently forms the apoptosome complex with apoptotic protease-activating factor 1 (APAF1) and Caspase-9, which subsequently activates caspase-3, thereby initiating the execution phase of apoptosis.72 The extrinsic pathway is initiated through receptor-mediated signaling mechanisms. The interaction between ligands (e.g., tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and Fas ligand) and death receptors (e.g., tumor necrosis factor receptor [TNFR] and Fas receptor [FASR]) transmits signals intracellularly, recruiting adaptor proteins Fas-associated death domain protein (FADD) and TNFR-associated death domain protein (TRADD), as well as pro-caspase-8 and pro-caspase-10, thereby promoting the assembly of the death-inducing signaling complex, which subsequently activates caspase-8 or caspase-10 and triggers the execution phase of apoptosis.73

Accumulating evidence suggests that mtROS have a multifaceted role in regulating apoptosis, acting as both an inducer and a suppressor. Impairment of OXPHOS caused by mitochondrial dysfunction leads to excessive mtROS production, which influences the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). These triggers sustained activation of multiple channels including voltage-dependent anion channels (VDACs) on the OMM and K+-ATP channels on the IMM, leading to a collapse in MMP. Concurrently, mtROS promote the opening of Ca2+-dependent, non-selective pores on the IMM, thereby disrupting calcium homeostasis and increasing cytosolic Ca2+ levels.74 Calcium overload can activate various apoptosis-related enzymes, such as calpains and endonucleases, thereby promoting DNA degradation and apoptotic cell death.75 mtROS activate the JNK signaling pathway, inducing caspase-8/10-independent cleavage of Bid at multiple sites and generating truncated Bid (tBid), which subsequently triggers mPTP opening and perpetuates a vicious cycle of mitochondrial damage and apoptotic progression. Furthermore, mtROS can exacerbate kidney damage by stimulating pro-inflammatory signaling cascades, including activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and the NF-κB pathway. Renal cell apoptosis represents a central mechanism in the pathophysiology of kidney diseases. Under pathological conditions, apoptotic events may occur in multiple renal compartments, affecting various cell types, such as proximal and distal TECs, vascular endothelial cells, and glomerular cells.81 In IRI-induced AKI, tubular epithelial apoptosis serves as a defining pathological feature, with experimental ischemic AKI models exhibiting marked increases in the expression of Bax and Bak. Notably, both conditional knockout of Bax and global deletion of Bak confer significant protection against apoptosis in proximal tubular cells.82 Concomitantly, impaired renal perfusion induces vascular endothelial injury and promotes thrombotic microangiopathy, wherein severe apoptosis of peritubular capillary endothelial cells leads to microvascular rarefaction, thereby contributing to the transition from AKI to CKD.83 In DKD and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, podocyte apoptosis exacerbates significant glomerular injury and albuminuria, and this apoptosis-induced damage may be regulated by retinoic acid receptor responder protein 1.84 These findings collectively highlight the critical involvement of apoptosis in the pathogenesis and progression of various kidney disorders.

Pyroptosis

Pyroptosis is a Gasdermin-mediated RCD characterized by distinct inflammatory features.85 It was initially described by Cookson et al. in 2001 as a form of pro-inflammatory cell death in macrophages that depends on caspase-1 activity.86 In contrast to apoptosis, pyroptosis is distinguished by the deficiency of plasma membrane integrity. Pyroptosis may participate in kidney diseases through two pathways: the canonical pathway, which is driven by caspase-1, and the non-canonical pathway mediated by caspase-4, caspase-5, or caspase-11. The canonical pathway primarily involves the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, the formation of pore-forming proteins, and the release of interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18. Specifically, when Toll-like receptors (TLRs) detect pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs/DAMPs), the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome triggers the activation of caspase-1 via auto-cleavage. Activated caspase-1 then cleaves Gasdermin D (GSDMD), releasing the N-terminal fragment (N-GSDMD), which oligomerizes and integrates into the plasma membrane, forming pores that enhance membrane permeability.87,88 The activation of the inflammasome and cleavage of caspase-1 also promote the maturation and extracellular secretion of IL-1β and IL-18. Notably, the secretion of these cytokines through N-GSDMD-induced pores is a defining hallmark of pyroptotic cell death. In contrast, the non-canonical pathway does not require inflammasome formation for initiation. Instead, it is directly initiated by intracellular lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which activates caspase-4 and caspase-5 in humans or the mouse ortholog caspase-11.89,90 The activated caspase then cleaves GSDMD, producing the pore-forming N-GSDMD fragment that integrates into the membrane to mediate pyroptosis.87,91 mtROS plays a crucial role in regulating pyroptosis. During the initiation phase, mtROS accumulation can enhance the expression levels of NLRP3, caspase-1, and the precursor forms of IL-1β and IL-18 (pro-IL-1β/pro-IL-18).14 During the activation phase, mtROS promote the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome through the thioredoxin interacting protein (TXNIP) pathway.92 During the execution phase, mtROS can directly facilitate GSDMD cleavage and regulate its pore-forming activity93 (Figure 3).Figure 3The role of mtROS in pyroptosisPyroptosis includes the caspase-1-mediated classical pathway and the caspase-4/5/11-mediated non-classical pathway. An increasing body of evidence demonstrated that pyroptosis plays a significant role in the development of multiple kidney diseases.93,94,95,96,97,98 Single-cell RNA sequencing analyses of murine AKI models have shown that pyroptosis-positive TECs account for a large proportion of cell death in ischemic kidneys, suggesting that pyroptosis is a predominant form of TEC death in AKI.93 Accordingly, knockdown of GSDMD and caspase-11 has been shown to protect mice from cisplatin-AKI.96 Furthermore, the persistent upregulation of pyroptosis-related genes (Casp1, Casp4, Gsdmd, Il18, and Il1b) following AKI has been associated with the progression of renal inflammatory fibrosis.94 Beyond its critical role in TECs, pyroptosis has also been implicated in podocyte injury, contributing to the progression of renal pathology. Studies have detected significantly increased expression of caspase-11 and the presence of GSDMD-N fragments in podocytes from DKD patients, which is accompanied by reduced expression of podocyte-specific markers, foot process effacement, and fusion.95 Similarly, gut-derived trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) drives DKD progression by inducing pyroptosis via a mechanism involving the mtROS-NLRP3 axis.98 The NLRP3 inflammasome also promotes renal fibrogenesis by enhancing TGF-β/Smad signaling. Consistently, Nlrp3-deficient mice exhibit reduced superoxide and mtROS production and are protected against DKD and renal fibrosis.97 Collectively, these findings demonstrate that mtROS can induce pyroptosis through multiple mechanisms, thus playing a pivotal role in the onset and progression of kidney diseases.

Necroptosis

Necroptosis is a form of RCD mediated by the necrosome complex and is characterized by organelle swelling and rupture of the plasma membrane. It exhibits morphological similarities with both apoptosis and necrosis but is mechanistically and molecularly distinct from each. The initiation of necroptosis is typically triggered by insurmountable cellular stress, such as signaling through TNFRs. In the signaling pathway of TNF-α-induced necroptosis, TNF binds to TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1) to activate complex I, which consists of TRADD, receptor-interacting protein 1 (RIPK1), and TNFR-associated factor 2 (TRAF2). This process inhibits the activity of caspase-8, thereby relieving its suppressive effect on RIPK1 and RIPK3. As a core scaffold protein in necroptosis, FADD provides a structural basis for the reciprocal phosphorylation of RIPK1 and RIPK3 and supports necrosome assembly by recruiting inactivated caspase-8 and RIPK1.99 The necrosome is formed through the interaction between RIPK1 and RIPK3. This complex then recruits and phosphorylates mixed-lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL). Ultimately, phosphorylated MLKL oligomerizes and translocates to the plasma membrane, where it assembles disruptive pores, causing membrane rupture and the release of intracellular contents.100

mtROS, serves as a critical mediator of necroptosis, playing essential roles in both its initiation and execution. In TNF-α induced necroptosis, the generation of mtROS relies on RIPK3 and plays a crucial role cell death. Under various stress conditions, including oxidative stress, drug toxicity, or radiation, TNF-α signaling enhances mtROS accumulation in necroptotic cells, thereby promoting necrosome assembly. First, RIPK1 senses mtROS through oxidation of three critical cysteine residues (Cys257, Cys268, and Cys586), which induces its autophosphorylation at Ser161 and promotes the recruitment of RIPK3, which leads to the assembly of an active necrosome.15 Additionally, elevated mtROS can lead to increased p53 expression and its partial translocation to mitochondria. This results in the depletion of sulfiredoxin (SRX) and hyperoxidation of peroxiredoxin 3 (PRX3), ultimately triggering necroptosis.101 The elimination of mtROS using scavengers, such as butylated hydroxyanisole, has been shown to suppress TNF-induced necroptosis.102 Similarly, inhibiting the mitochondrial ETC with agents like amobarbital to reduce mtROS production also effectively blocks this cell death pathway103 (Figure 4).Figure 4The role of mtROS in necroptosisNecroptosis is a regulated cell death orchestrated by RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL. Necroptosis has been implicated in the pathophysiology of acute tubular necrosis, nephron loss, and maladaptive renal fibrosis.104 This form of RCD leads to the excretion of cellular contents and cytokines, thereby triggering robust inflammatory responses in adjacent tissues. The resulting necroinflammatory microenvironment can cause severe organ dysfunction and contribute to persistent renal tissue damage. The expression of critical necroptosis-associated genes, including RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL, is significantly upregulated during renal IRI.105,106 RIPK3-MLKL-dependent necroptosis has been demonstrated to initiate early renal injury following IRI.106 Furthermore, this necroptotic cell death triggers NLRP3 inflammasome activation, creating a feedforward loop that accelerates necroinflammation and ultimately drives the transition from AKI to CKD.107 In a model of sepsis-associated AKI, RIPK3 was shown to inhibit mitochondrial complexes I and III while upregulating NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4) expression, thereby enhancing mtROS production and exacerbating tubular injury.108 Genetic deficiency of either RIPK3 or MLKL conferred dual therapeutic benefits in septic mice, significantly improving survival while offering protection against multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, including AKI.109 Necroptosis is also considered as a critical pathomechanism in DKD. Ferroptosis

Since its initial description by Scott J. Dixon et al. in 2012, ferroptosis has been identified as a novel, iron-dependent modality of regulated cell death and has rapidly emerged as a major research focus in biology and medicine.113 This type of cell death has been associated with the onset of numerous human diseases, including a range of kidney disorders. The core pathological features of ferroptosis are characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation.

During renal injury, extracellular Fe3+ is driven by cells through transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) and subsequently reduced to Fe2+ by the iron reductase six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of prostate 3 (STEAP3). Meanwhile, ferritin can bind to nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) and be degraded to release the stored intracellular Fe2+. Excess intracellular Fe2+ acts as a pivotal catalyst for ferroptosis. Through the Fenton reaction, Fe2+ converts H2O2 into toxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH), thereby initiating a self-propagating chain reaction of lipid peroxidation. Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) within cellular membranes, such as arachidonic acid and linoleic acid, undergo peroxidation mediated by acyl CoA synthase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3), arachidonate lipoxygenases (ALOXs), and mtROS, forming lipid hydroperoxides (e.g., PLOOH/PUFA-OOH) that compromise membrane integrity.114,115 Beyond the core mechanisms of iron dysregulation and lipid peroxidation, several key antioxidant systems have been established as central regulators of ferroptosis: the system Xc−/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis, the ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1)/coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) pathway, and the dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH)/ubiquinol (CoQH2) system. System Xc, a cystine/glutamate antiporter formed by the solute carriers SLC7A11 and SLC3A2, primarily mediates the exchange of intracellular glutamate for extracellular cystine. Once transported into the cell, cystine is reduced to cysteine by the thioredoxin-related protein of 14 kDa (TRP14), thereby providing the rate-limiting substrate for GSH synthesis.116 GPX4 is a selenoprotein that employs GSH to convert harmful lipid hydroperoxides to non-toxic lipid alcohols, thereby safeguarding cellular membranes from peroxidative damage and maintaining membrane integrity.117 GSH availability is a critical determinant of GPX4 activity. Consequently, inhibition of GSH synthesis depletes this essential cofactor, diminishes GPX4 function, and thereby markedly sensitizes cells to ferroptosis. In the FSP1/CoQ10/NAD(P)H pathway, FSP1 protects cells from GPX4 inhibition-induced ferroptosis by reducing CoQ10 to its antioxidant form, CoQ10H2, independently of the intracellular expression levels of GSH, GPX4, and ACSL4.118 Similarly, the mitochondrial enzyme DHODH has been shown to protect against ferroptosis by reducing CoQ10 to CoQH2 in the IMM, thereby counteracting lipid peroxidation specifically within mitochondria, particularly when GPX4 function is compromised.119 However, it should be noted that Mishima et al. found the contribution of DHODH in ferroptosis to be minor and context-specific.120 Their findings indicate that the ability of DHODH inhibitors to sensitize cells to ferroptosis is primarily via inhibition of FSP1, rather than DHODH itself. The kidneys are highly metabolic organs responsible for iron filtration and reabsorption, playing a crucial role in systemic iron homeostasis. Substantial evidence has confirmed a close association between ferroptosis and various kidney diseases, with its functional relevance demonstrated in vivo in models of acute tubular necrosis and IRI. GPX4 is widely regarded as a central regulator and biomarker of ferroptosis.121 In AKI, renal GSH levels are depleted concurrently with elevated lipid peroxidation, accompanied by downregulation of GPX4 at both the protein and transcriptional levels. Consequently, modulation of GPX4 levels and activity has been shown to ameliorate the progression of kidney injury. Studies reveal that legumain deficiency alleviates ferroptosis and tubular damage in IRI or folic-acid-induced AKI models through elevating intracellular GPX4 levels.122 Similarly, C23, an antagonist of eCIRP, significantly alleviated the decrease in GPX4 expression in CKD induced by renal ischemia/reperfusion (RIR) and improved the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in RIR mice.123 In DKD, hyperglycemia initiates tubular iron overload and a concurrent depletion of antioxidant capacity. Iron overload significantly elevates intracellular ferrous iron (Fe2+) levels. Fe2+ converts hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) leaked from the ETC into highly toxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH) via the Fenton reaction. These oxygen radicals directly attack mitochondrial membranes, leading to the massive production and accumulation of lipid peroxides within mitochondria, which further impairs mitochondrial function. The resulting exacerbation of electron leakage subsequently amplifies mtROS generation. Concurrently, the depletion of antioxidants (GSH depletion) not only compromises the effective scavenging of baseline ROS produced by the respiratory chain but also diminishes GPX4 activity. This reduced GPX4 activity fails to reduce the accumulated lipid peroxides on mitochondrial membranes, causing sustained mitochondrial damage and further increasing mtROS production. Ferroptosis inhibitors can eliminate diabetes-induced lipid peroxidation and tubular cell death.124 Ferroptosis exacerbates kidney injury by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, which stimulates the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-18, further amplifying renal inflammation and contributing to the progression of fibrosis.125 Therefore, therapeutic strategies aimed at upregulating GPX4 to suppress lipid peroxidation, or upregulating SLC7A11/xCT to mitigate oxidative stress may effectively prevent ferroptosis-induced tubular epithelial cell damage and potentially halt or even reverse the progression of renal fibrosis.

Accumulating evidence indicates that mitochondria and their derived mtROS actively participate in the ferroptosis induction process. As a primary source of intracellular ROS, mtROS is considered a pivotal contributor to the initiation of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis.126,127 Under pathological conditions in the kidney, mitochondrial dysfunction leads to excessive mtROS generation, triggering the translocation of Bax to the OMM, where it rapidly forms dense punctate foci with Bak protein.128 Bax oligomerization at the mitochondria is markedly enhanced, contributing to MMP dissipation. and promotes the release of pro-ferroptotic factors (e.g., Cyt c),129 thereby promoting the execution of ferroptosis. Furthermore, mtROS can suppress GPX4 activity, impairing its ability to reduce lipid peroxides and thereby triggering ferroptosis.130 The accumulation of mtROS also directly exacerbates lipid peroxidation, leading to cellular membrane damage and contributing to kidney disease pathogenesis. In cisplatin induced AKI, mitochondrial dysfunction results in substantial mtROS retention. Scavenging mtROS with MnTBAP, a cell-permeable SOD mimetic, has been shown to restore mitochondrial homeostasis and inhibit ferroptosis following cisplatin challenge.13 Similarly, under hyperglycemic conditions, elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and mtROS contribute to podocyte ferroptosis and damage the glomerular filtration barrier, ultimately leading to proteinuria.131 Notably, mitochondrial protection conferred by the mtROS scavenger mitoquinone (MitoQ) has been demonstrated to effectively prevent GPx-dependent ferroptosis.131

Cuproptosis

Cuproptosis, a newly discovered form of cell death identified by Todd Golub’s team in 2022, is characterized by excessive copper ion accumulation, abnormal aggregation of lipoylated proteins, and loss of iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster proteins, culminating in cell death.132,133,134 Copper serves as an essential cofactor for numerous enzymes and plays a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis, with roles in mitochondrial OXPHOS, redox balance, and cell survival signaling. As a principal organ for copper excretion and homeostasis, the kidney is particularly vulnerable to copper-induced toxicity, which can manifest through the induction of regulated cell death, oxidative stress, and metabolic disturbances.

Excessive Cu2+ enters the cell primarily through the copper transporter 1 (CTR1) or solute carrier family 31 member 1 (SLC31A1)135 and binds to copper chaperones, including antioxidant 1 (ATOX1) and cytochrome c oxidase assembly homolog 17 (COX17) copper chaperone, which then deliver copper ions to mitochondria. Within mitochondria, these ions are reduced to the more toxic Cu+ by ferredoxin-1 (FDX1) and accumulate further. Intracellular copper overload directly interacts with lipoylated components of the TCA cycle in the mitochondrial matrix, leading to the aggregation of dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and depletion of Fe-S cluster proteins. This leads to mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction, proteotoxic stress, and ultimately triggers cell death.132,136 Cu+ specifically interacts with the lipoylation sites of DLAT and promotes its oligomerization, a process facilitated by the lipoic acid synthase (LIAS)-mediated protein lipoylation modification.134 The Cu+-lipoylated DLAT interaction induces abnormal DLAT oligomerization and the formation of insoluble protein aggregates, resulting in TCA cycle disruption and mitochondrial functional collapse. FDX1, as a core regulator of cuproptosis, plays a dual role in this process. On one hand, it directly interacts with LIAS to enhance its binding with glycine cleavage system protein H (GCSH), thereby promoting protein lipoylation. On the other hand, it provides electrons for Fe-S cluster biogenesis. Most Fe-S cluster proteins serve as coenzymes in ETC. Copper accumulation disrupts Fe-S cluster proteins, impairing ETC function and exacerbating oxidative stress via mtROS overproduction (Figure 6). Additionally, mitochondrial copper overload has been shown to selectively impair the activity of ETC complex IV, further compromising mitochondrial function and triggering mtROS bursts.137 Notably, cuproptosis shares some overlapping features with ferroptosis, such as lipid ROS accumulation. However, its defining characteristic is the strict dependence on FDX1-mediated lipoylated protein aggregation and Fe-S cluster disintegration. mtROS is a critical mediator of cuproptosis, primarily responsible for its cytotoxic effects. Cu+ catalyzes the production of mtROS via the Fenton reaction (Cu+ + H2O2 → Cu2+ +·OH + OH−), which induces severe oxidative stress, exacerbates lipid peroxidationin membranes, and results in damage to proteins and DNA.138 The massive generation of mtROS oxidizes key components of the Fe-S cluster synthesis pathway, particularly FDX1, thereby disrupting ETC function and accelerating cell death. Furthermore, mtROS depletes GSH pool, which inactivates GSH-dependent antioxidant enzymes and reduces the formation of Cu+-GSH complexes. This establishes a vicious cycle that leads to cytosolic Cu+ accumulation and drives cuproptosis progression.

In a cisplatin-induced AKI model, upregulation of SLC31A1 was shown to disrupt copper homeostasis, promote mtROS generation, induce cuproptosis, and exacerbate renal injury.139 Mishra et al. observed significantly higher plasma copper levels in children with acute renal failure compared to healthy controls, with non-survivors exhibiting elevated copper levels versus survivors. Notably, ceruloplasmin accurately predicted mortality in these patients.140 In DKD, excessive copper accumulation induces podocyte cuproptosis and disrupts the renal podocyte cytoskeleton.141 Additionally, a cross-sectional study found a significant positive correlation between serum copper concentrations and CKD prevalence.142 Furthermore, elevated serum copper was identified as an independent risk factor for CKD, being associated with lower estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and an accelerated decline in renal function.143

Gong et al. demonstrated that the selective Cu(II) chelator trientine ameliorates diabetic renal fibrosis.144 Overexpression of COX17 mitigates renal fibrosis by facilitating mitochondrial copper efflux, thereby preserving mitochondrial function and reducing mtROS levels.137 Furthermore, ultrasmall copper-based nanoparticles have shown therapeutic promise in ameliorating AKI by reducing mtROS and suppressing inflammatory responses, highlighting their potential for treating ROS-related diseases.145 Therefore, copper accumulation and the subsequent mtROS burst are critical drivers of renal dysfunction. Strategies to maintain copper homeostasis (including reducing Cu2+ accumulation and limiting cellular copper uptake), eliminate mtROS, and enhance antioxidant enzyme activity may mitigate renal damage from cuproptosis.

PANoptosis

PANoptosis, a concept first introduced by Malireddi et al. in 2019 refers to a novel form of RCD that is activated by specific stimuli and driven by a multifaceted protein complex termed the PANoptosome.146 This novel cell death modality simultaneously engages key mechanistic features of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, thereby providing a comprehensive host defense strategy against diverse stressors. While apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis have historically been viewed as distinct pathways, emerging evidence demonstrates their extensive crosstalk and convergence into the PANoptosome complex, which activates PANoptosis.147 PANoptosis, a distinct form of innate immune-mediated inflammatory cell death, has been linked to the development of several kidney diseases, thereby presenting novel potential therapeutic targets. Conversely, dysregulated PANoptosis can drive excessive inflammation and cause irreversible tissue damage, highlighting the critical need for its precise modulation.

Currently, five types of distinct PANoptosome complexes have been identified, including those nucleated by Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1), absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2), RIPK1, and leucine-rich repeat family pyrin domain-containing 12 (NLRP12).148,149,150,151 These complexes contain three functional modules: assembly domains, catalytic domains, and sensor domains. The interactions between death-fold domains (e.g., receptor-interacting protein kinase homotypic interaction motif [RHIM], pyrin domain [PYD], and death effector domain-containing protein 2 [DED2]) act as a crucial role in promoting the maturation of PANoptosome complexes upon recognizing stimuli.152,153 PANoptosome complexes can induce inflammatory cell death by activating caspases, gasdermins, and MLKL. ZBP1 is the first identified PANoptosis sensor.154 It directly binds to Z-type nucleic acids (Z-DNA and Z-RNA) generated by viruses or host cells, exposing its RHIM1 domain, which interacts with the RHIM domain of RIPK3, further recruiting RIPK1, phosphorylating MLKL, and initiating necroptosis.155 Simultaneously, RIPK1 recruits and activates caspase-8 via homotypic DED interactions. Activated caspase-8 then cleaves and stimulates the effector caspase-3/7, executing the apoptotic program. Furthermore, the RHIM2 domain of ZBP1 interacts with the caspase recruitment domain (CARD) of ASC. ASC then nucleates the NLRP3 inflammasome via PYD-PYD interactions, leading to caspase-1 activation.154 Additionally, ASC can directly promote caspase-8 activation through interactions involving its PYD and the DED of caspase-8. Consequently, caspase-1 and caspase-8 act synergistically to cleave GSDMD, leading to pyroptotic pore formation and inflammatory mediators release.156 The AIM2-PANoptosome is activated by cytosolic double-stranded DNA, which can be of pathogenic or host origin. AIM2 then nucleates a complex that can include pyrin and ZBP1, driving PANoptosis.157 DNA binding is mediated by the HIN-200 domain of AIM2, while its PYD domain interacts with the PYD domain of ASC to initiate downstream signaling.158 This ASC recruitment facilitates the assembly of the AIM2 inflammasome through CARD-CARD interactions with caspase-1. This process activates the apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis pathways through caspase-3/7, RIPK3/PIPK1, and caspase-8/GSDMD, respectively. Similar to ZBP1, RIPK1 can also serve as a core component of a PANoptosome. The RIPK1-PANoptosome has been extensively studied in the setting of Yersinia infection.159 During infection, RIPK1 nucleates a complex containing NLRP3 inflammasome components, caspase-3/8, and RIPK3. In contrast to ZBP1-driven PANoptosis, the Yersinia-induced process is dependent on RIPK1 but not ZBP1.160,161 Yersinia infection leads to TNFR1 activation, which in turn activates RIPK1. Activated RIPK1 then promotes PANoptosome assembly and induces PANoptosis by engaging RIPK3 via its RHIM domain and/or recruiting caspase-8 via homotypic DED interactions.159 NLRP12 functions as an intracellular innate immune sensor that assembles a PANoptosome complex with ASC, caspase-8, RIPK3, FADD, and RIPK1 in response to heme, PAMPs during hemolytic conditions, or TNF stimulation. This assembly drives inflammatory cell death through PANoptosis.150 Signaling through TLR2/4 and the adaptor protein MyD88 upregulates Nlrp12 expression. Concurrently, these pathways activate NF-κB and ERK, leading to increased IRF1 expression and ROS generation. The synergistic action of these signals facilitates PANoptosome assembly and promotes PANoptosis.150,161 Although the assembly and triggering factors for PANoptosomes differ, they integrate and execute multiple regulated cell death pathways through coordinated activation of various signaling cascades at downstream levels.

PANoptosis is a novel inflammatory form of cell death that integrates characteristics of pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, in which mtROS serves as a pivotal upstream regulator. GSDMD pore formation can subsequently exacerbate mitochondrial damage, resulting in further mtROS release and creating a feedforward loop that amplifies the pyroptotic arm of PANoptosis. The role of PANoptosis in kidney diseases has attracted significant attention from researchers. Studies have shown that PANoptosis is markedly activated in various renal pathological processes: in the cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model of AKI, increased AIM2 expression has been shown to initiate PANoptosis167; cisplatin accumulation in renal TECs similarly induces PANoptosis, while sustained inflammation and fibrosis promote the progression from AKI to CKD.168 In addition, PANoptosis is activated in podocytes of DKD mice, potentially mediated through the TRAIL/DR5 pathway.168 The level of PANoptosis activation positively correlates with the severity of kidney injury, and targeted inhibition of the PANoptosome can significantly alleviate renal damage. In a hemolysis model, NLRP12 was shown to drive AKI by activating the inflammasome and PANoptosome. Conversely, Nlrp12−/− mice were significantly protected, exhibiting reduced PANoptosis and increased resistance to AKI and associated lethality.150 PANoptosis is also implicated in aristolochic acid nephropathy (AAN), as evidenced by the significant upregulation of its molecular markers. The histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor FK-228 was found to alleviate AAN-related renal fibrosis by upregulating proline-serine-threonine-phosphatase-interacting protein 2 (PSTPIP2), which in turn suppresses PANoptosis in TECs.169 Studies have shown that a range of naturally bioactive compounds, including lycopene and 3,4-methylenedioxy-β-nitrostyrene), as well as targeted drugs (such as HDAC inhibitors and PSTPIP2 activators),169,170,171,172 exert significant renoprotective effects by regulating the PANoptosis pathway. Although several modulators of PANoptosis have been identified, offering new therapeutic strategies for kidney diseases, current research remains largely confined to cell and animal studies. Further investigation is needed to clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms involved and to advance clinical translation.

Candidate anti-mtROS therapeutics and their basic mechanisms of action

Direct scavenging of mtROS

Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants, including MitoQ, Skulachev quinone (SkQ), and elamipretide (SS-31), have demonstrated efficacy in reducing mtROS levels, protecting mitochondrial integrity, and ameliorating kidney injury. MitoQ functions as a potent mtROS scavenger. Upon accumulation in the mitochondrial matrix, the compound is converted by the electron transport chain into its active QH2 state, which can directly quench reactive species and thereby limit oxidative injury.175 In cisplatin-induced AKI, MitoQ has been proven to prevent ferroptosis in renal TECs by attenuating lipid peroxidation.190 It also reverses defective mitophagy and ameliorates renal tubular injury in DKD.191 Furthermore, in DKD, MitoQ mitigates oxidative damage in renal tubules by limiting pyroptotic signaling, partly through decreased expression of IL-1β and caspase-1.192 The SkQ family molecules (e.g., SkQ1 and SkQR1) comprise two key structural components: a quinone-based antioxidant that directly neutralizes radicals, and a lipophilic, positively charged moiety that drives their potential-dependent accumulation across the IMM, leading to their sequestration within the mitochondrial matrix.193 This targeted localization allows SkQ to scavenge mtROS at its primary site of generation (the IMM), preventing its diffusion and subsequent damage to other cellular components. In models of pre-existing renal impairment, combined treatment with SkQ1 and nicotinamide riboside reduces mtROS-mediated damage to the renal cortex (including endothelial, glomerular, tubular, and tubulointerstitial cells) and decreases fibrosis, providing long-term protection against AKI-to-CKD progression.194 Notably, SkQ1 alleviates renal tubular cell apoptosis in mice with IRI by reducing mtROS and reversing the downregulation of antioxidant genes. However, its therapeutic efficacy and safety profile appear inferior to those of MitoQ.194 Furthermore, studies have revealed that SkQR1 treatment restores the expression of BCL-2 in pyelonephritis, thereby mitigating renal cell death and reducing animal mortality.195 SS-31 is the most extensively characterized mitochondrial-targeted peptide in both preclinical and clinical research. It exerts minimal effects on healthy mitochondria but selectively stabilizes damaged mitochondria by preventing lipid peroxidation, structural damage, and electron leakage, demonstrating efficacy across various kidney disease models. SS-31 can ameliorate DKD by maintaining physiological superoxide levels and suppressing the expression of BAX and caspase-3 in renal TECs.177 Furthermore, Hao et al. found that SS-31 also exerts renoprotective effects by inhibiting angiotensin-II-induced podocyte apoptosis and cardiolipin peroxidation via the Sirt6-PLD6 axis.178 Additionally, SS-31 has been shown to preserve mitochondrial function via the JAK2/STAT3 pathway and attenuate necroptosis in podocytes.196 However, it should be noted that while short-term administration of MitoQ is safe in healthy individuals and does not induce nephrotoxicity,197 long-term data in patients with kidney disease are lacking, and its therapeutic efficacy in kidney diseases remains to be established. In contrast, current evidence supporting the therapeutic effects of SkQ and SS-31 is limited to preclinical animal models, and their efficacy and safety in humans require further validation through clinical trials.

Inhibition of mtROS generation

CoQ10 exerts its effects by improving the efficiency of electron transfer in the ETC, reducing electron leakage-induced mtROS production, and stabilizing MMP.179 The renal protective effects of CoQ10 have been demonstrated in type 2 diabetes,198 where it potentially protects diabetic renal tissues by maintaining mitochondrial function and attenuating mitochondrial H2O2 production. A trial examining the safety, tolerability, and impact of CoQ10 administration on oxidative stress in patients found that most subjects tolerated CoQ10 well, and supplementation with CoQ10 reduced oxidative stress levels in the subjects.199 However, the study included a relatively small patient cohort and had a short duration; therefore, the long-term safety of CoQ10 administration should be interpreted with caution. The mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 has been demonstrated to suppress excessive mitochondrial fission by inhibiting the GTPase activity of Drp1, thereby maintaining mitochondrial network integrity and reducing mtROS production.180 In murine models of renal IRI and cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity, Mdivi-1 pretreatment attenuated renal tissue mtROS generation and reduced caspase-3 activation and apoptosis.200 The therapeutic effects of Mdivi-1 have only been demonstrated in “in vitro” studies; therefore, its efficacy and safety profile remain largely unknown. Clinical application in humans is still a distant prospect. Interestingly, metformin has also been demonstrated to reduce mtROS production by inhibiting ETC complex I and showed potential in attenuating the progression of DKD.181 In a randomized controlled trial, metformin demonstrated a favorable safety profile in patients with CKD and was associated with a potential reduction in the risk of mortality and cardiovascular events in individuals with stage III CKD.201 Furthermore, metformin therapy has been shown to prevent kidney injury and mitigate renal function deterioration in patients with bladder cancer undergoing cisplatin-based chemotherapy, suggesting its potential utility in attenuating cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity.202

Protecting mitochondria from oxidative damage

Urolithin A, an intestinal metabolite of the dietary tannin ellagic acid, exhibits potent antioxidant activity and promotes the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria through mitophagy, thereby reducing mtROS production. Preclinical studies have shown that oral administration of nanoparticle-encapsulated Urolithin A significantly improves survival in mice with cisplatin-induced AKI by reducing renal oxidative stress and apoptosis.182 Although Urolithin A has demonstrated beneficial effects, additional clinical investigations are warranted to elucidate its therapeutic potential in patients with kidney disease. Notoginsenoside Fc, a novel saponin isolated from Panax notoginseng—a traditional Chinese herb widely used in China—has recently been identified as a promising therapeutic candidate for DKD. Fc exerts significant renoprotective effects in DKD by downregulating 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2) expression, thereby suppressing pyroptosis-related genes (including TXNIP, cleaved caspase-1, and GSDMD-NT), alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and regulating mtROS production.183 Additionally, Achyranthes aspera is a widely recognized medicinal plant traditionally used to treat a variety of disorders, notably renal dysfunction. It has been demonstrated not only to inhibit the PANoptosis pathway but also to protect mitochondrial integrity and attenuate cisplatin-induced nephrotoxicity by regulating mtROS levels through upregulation of the cGMP/PKG, cAMP, AMPK, and mTOR pathways that mediate autophagy and mitophagy.170 Additionally, our previous study has shown that 84-B10, a small-molecule Lonp1 activator, showed potential to decrease mtROS production in renal TECs in aristolochic acid and cisplatin-induced AKI models.17,184 It is worth noting, however, that Urolithin A, Fc, Achyranthes aspera, and 84-B10 are currently only at the preclinical stage of investigation, and their therapeutic efficacy in patients with kidney disease has yet to be validated.

Novel nanomedicines for precise regulation of mtROS and kidney injury

Conventional antioxidant therapeutics fail to achieve the desired therapeutic outcomes due to poor pharmacokinetics and short renal retention time.203,204,205 Moreover, these antioxidant agents inevitably induce systemic scavenge of ROS or stimulation of anti-ROS systems, which may lead to impaired antimicrobial ability of phagocytes and impaired antivirus ability of the Th17 cells.206 In cancer therapy, multiple treatment modalities, such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy, depend on ROS generation to induce cancer cell death. Inadvertent reduction of ROS by antioxidant interventions could therefore attenuate the efficacy of these therapies or even promote tumor progression.207,208 Consequently, recent therapeutic strategies for kidney diseases have increasingly focused on the development of targeted delivery of therapeutics to injured kidney.209,210 By restricting antioxidant delivery to the diseased kidney, it is theoretically possible to achieve localized ROS modulation, alleviate renal cell injury induced by oxidative stress, and regulate the inflammatory renal microenvironment, while simultaneously preserving systemic ROS homeostasis. This approach aims to “tune” rather than “abolish” ROS, thereby safeguarding the essential physiological functions of other organs and the immune system.211 Furthermore, it is important to note that caution should be taken to imply anti-ROS strategies during infection and anti-tumor therapy.

Recent advancements in nanotechnology have facilitated the design of novel nanomedicines for targeted therapeutic modulation, offering a promising approach for treating kidney diseases. An ultrasmall platinum single-atom enzyme (Pt/SAE) with multiple antioxidant enzyme activities was developed by Ye’s group. This nanozyme mimics SOD and catalase, catalyzing the conversion of superoxide anions into water and oxygen while exhibiting remarkable mtROS-scavenging capacity, thereby reducing pro-inflammatory macrophage infiltration and preventing renal IRI.185 Another nanoparticle, a black phosphorus/cerium catalytic tunable nanozyme (BP@CeO2-PEG), was found to enhance ATP regeneration by accelerating its catalytic “redox cycle” through an increased Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio, effectively eliminating cisplatin-induced mtROS. Furthermore, BP@CeO2-PEG nanozymes were shown to suppress oxidative-stress-induced apoptosis in renal TECs by modulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.186

To achieve kidney-specific targeting, a highly mitochondria- and kidney-targeted antioxidant nanodrug was developed by Liu’s group. This nanodrug accumulates extensively within renal tubules and preferentially localizes to the damaged tubular mitochondria in DKD.187 The aminoacetic acid group of selenocysteine in this formulation displays a strong binding affinity toward OMM proteins, including translocase of the outer membrane (TOM) 20, TOM34, TOM40, TOM70, VDAC1, and VDAC2.187 Furthermore, it can be highly enriched within the mitochondria of renal tubular cells, demonstrating superior mitochondrial targeting efficiency in the kidney. It not only eliminates mtROS but also releases soluble selenium that converts into bioactive GPX4, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis, protecting mitochondria, and restoring renal function.187

Various bioactive molecules derived from traditional medicines possess considerable potential for mtROS scavenging. However, their multi-target nature, poor water solubility, and extensive first-pass metabolism often result in off-target effects and insufficient bioavailability. Novel nanomedicine delivery systems incorporating traditional Chinese medicinal compounds have been developed for precise kidney targeting A curcumin–copper complex nanoparticle (Cur-Cu@PEG) was constructed by Huang et al. Cur-Cu@PEG primarily comprises Cur-Cu nanoparticles stabilized with polyethylene glycol (PEG). Owing to their ultrasmall diameter (approximately 5.8 nm), Cur–Cu@PEG exhibited kidney-specific accumulation at all examined time points in mice with AKI. This nanoparticle stably targeted the kidneys, attenuating ROS accumulation and suppressing caspase-3-dependent apoptosis and pyroptosis in renal TECs during AKI.188 Additionally, several natural phenolic compounds have been extensively explored in antioxidant nanozyme development owing to their potent ROS-scavenging activity and favorable biocompatibility. A team led by Yuan developed a metallophenolic nanozyme that preferentially accumulates in the kidneys and exhibits low toxicity toward other organs. This nanozyme exhibited catalase-like activity, catalyzing the decomposition of H2O2 into oxygen. Furthermore, it promoted the conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II and enhanced p62 degradation, thereby regulating renal mitophagy, restoring mitochondrial homeostasis, and ameliorating AKI.189 It also should be noted that the nanoparticles designed for targeted kidney therapy have demonstrated extremely low toxicity, favorable biocompatibility, and a robust safety profile in animal models. Although they have not yet been translated into clinical applications, these findings establish a solid foundation for their potential future use in clinical practice. More recently, a novel mechanism of inter-organellar mtROS transfer was elucidated by Peter K. Kim’s group. They discovered for the first time that acyl-coA binding domain-containing protein 5 (ACBD5) and protein tyrosine phosphatase-interacting protein-51 (PTPIP51) form physical contact between peroxisomes and mitochondria to directly transfer mtROS.212 Notably, during mtROS bursts, the efficiency of this transfer increased nearly 3-fold, establishing a stress-responsive buffering mechanism that preserves mitochondrial function.212 This groundbreaking discovery proposes a new paradigm for kidney disease treatment—inter-organellar antioxidant therapy.

Conclusion and perspectives

In this review, we have summarized the pivotal role of mtROS in triggering various RCD pathways in kidney diseases and have highlighted the significance of regulating mtROS in renal pathology. Targeting mtROS through antioxidant interventions and mitochondrial functional enhancement may offer novel therapeutic avenues for kidney disease management. mtROS act as a double-edged sword, as their concentration determines distinct physiological or pathological outcomes. A crucial challenge lies in the efficient and sensitive detection of mtROS levels and in determining the critical threshold at which therapeutic intervention becomes necessary. Furthermore, emerging nanomedical technologies have recently demonstrated promising therapeutic potential in kidney diseases by enabling precise renal targeting and clearance of mtROS. However, their clinical translation remains hindered by multiple challenges, including safety concerns, large-scale synthesis, and pharmacokinetic stability. Future research should aim to strengthen the translational bridge between basic research and clinical application, thereby providing a robust scientific basis for developing highly effective and low-toxicity mtROS-targeted therapeutics. In conclusion, mtROS-targeted interventions offer a promising approach for alleviating renal injury in preclinical models. Nevertheless, further investigations are required to fully elucidate their translational potential and long-term clinical efficacy.
