Effect of a microencapsulated blend of organic acids and bioactive compounds on the quality and visual appraisal of broiler meat
Authors: Caio Tellini, Laura Adriane de Moraes Pinto, Felipe de Souza Evangelista, Isadora Pegoraro Pallaoro, Beatriz Tiemi Onishi, Bianca Roldan Lara, James Barbosa da Silva, Jovanir Inês Müller Fernandes
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
For 2160 broilers, were raised from 1 to 42 d of age, was evaluated the consequences of microencapsulated blend of organic acids and bioactive compounds on dietary supplementation in broilers on meat quality and consumer acceptance during 200 d. Broilers were randomly distributed in a completely randomized design with 6 treatments: Negative control (NC): basal diet; Positive control (PC): NC+ Zinc bacitracin 15%; B150: NC+150 g/t of the microencapsulated blend of organic acids and bioactive compounds; B300: NC+300 g/t of the microencapsulated blend of organic acids and bioactive compounds; B450:NC+450 g/t of the microencapsulated blend of organic acids and bioactive compounds; and B600:NC+600 g/t of the microencapsulated blend of organic acids and bioactive compounds. The poultry meat characteristics (thawing loss, cooking loss, shear force, color and microstructure of the meat), oxidative stability (lipid oxidation, antioxidant activity DPPH and ABTS) and consumer acceptance (visual appraisal and willingness to buy) were evaluated. Color parameters (L and b), thawing losses and shear force were not significantly different among the treatments (NC, PC, B150, B300, B450 and B600; P > 0.05). The highest level of a was in the PC. The cooking losses were the greatest in B600. No treatment showed changes in muscle fibers. The antioxidant activity for DPPH was higher for B600. For ABTS, B150 and B300 presented the least lipid oxidation. When evaluating consumers' visual preference, B300 had the greatest in consumer preference and B150 and B300 had the greatest purchase intention on the first day of storage. After 6 d, B300 continued to be the most preferred and B150 and B450 began to show the greatest purchase intention. The B300 treatment showed a protective effect on lipid stability and consumer preference. These results highlight the importance of using a precise additive dosage during animal production to guarantee the meat's quality and satisfy consumers’ demands.

INTRODUCTION

The ongoing pursuit of human well-being significantly influences dietary nutrition choices. Animal protein, known for its rich supply of vitamins, minerals, amino acids, and fats, is essential for achieving high standards of diet quality. Consequently, enhancing animal production becomes imperative (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022a; Abdel-Wareth; Kehraus; Südekum, 2019; Wan et al., 2017). This requires a holistic approach encompassing genetics, diet, sanitary conditions, and animal welfare.

The essential oils are extracted from a variety of vegetal materials, such as flowers, flower buds, seeds, leaves, branches, peels, wood, fruits, and roots (Atarés; Chiralt, 2016; Laranjo et al., 2019). They contain a variety of aromatic compounds, including terpenoids, alkaloids, lectins, polypeptides, polyphenols, quinones, flavonoids, and tannins (Souza et al., 2019; da Silva et al., 2021). The main compounds found in essential oils are well-documented for their beneficial biological functions (da Silva et al., 2021; Santana de Oliveira et al., 2023).

They can interact with tissues such as muscle and adipose cells, acting as antioxidants, boosting immunity, and enhancing hepatocyte responses to oxidative stress (Chen et al., 2023). As a result, some of these compounds are classified as bioactive. These benefits promote animal homeostasis, thereby improving overall animal performance, health, and meat shelf life.

The organic acid is defined as a substance that contains one or more carboxyl in its molecule (Adil et al., 2010). The bactericidal activity is attributed to the combined effects of concentration, pKa and chelation capacity, which are responsible for the breakdown of amino acid metabolism, DNA synthesis and energetic metabolism of microorganisms (Peh et al., 2020).

Among the various strategies to boost animal production, the use of essential oils in combination with organic acids have been one of the most studied and effective subjects (Liu et al., 2020; Obeidat et al., 2022). These compounds can work synergistically to offer both sanitary and metabolic benefits to the animal. Additionally, the positive effects on the intestinal microbiota are well-documented, particularly when these compounds are administered in a microencapsulated form (Abd El-Hack et al., 2022b; Adil et al., 2010; Stefanello et al., 2020).

The literature presents a gap in studies that investigate the effects of these compounds on the structural aspects of meat, which is essential to identify possible changes that may impact its sensory and functional properties. Some studies indicate that essential oils and organic acids can influence meat quality by improving muscle oxidative stability, reducing losses during thawing, and preservation color during storage (Abdel-Wareth; Kehraus; Südekum, 2019; Ali, 2016; Dauksiene et al., 2021; Ibrahim et al., 2021; Laghouati; Arbouche; Arbouche, 2020; Pham et al., 2022). However, research on the effects of supplementing these compounds in broiler chickens on meat quality and consumer acceptability is still limited.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to evaluate the effect of using a microencapsulated blend of organic acids and bioactive compounds on poultry meat quality. This study highlights the blend's impact on oxidative patterns, the visual appraisal and willingness to buy poultry breast in commercial conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Local, Animals and Diets

This experiment was approved by the Committee of Ethical Use on Experimentation Animals (Protocol n° 16/2021) and carried at the Poultry Experimentation Laboratory, Federal University of Paraná – Sector Palotina, Palotina, Paraná, Brazil). Two thousand and sixteen Cobb® 500 originating from matrices from the same batch at 40 wk of age were raised from 1 to 42 d of age. Broilers were randomly distributed in a completely randomized design with 6 treatments and 8 replications, with 42 birds per experimental unit. Poultry Meat Sample Preparation

Animal slaughter followed the norms of slaughter from the state inspection service of Brazilian laws. After the animals were slaughtered, the Pectoralis major (PM) was manually removed from each carcass with an aluminum knife and packed at a cool temperature (4°C ± 1°C). After that, the chicken breasts (6 treatments, 8 repetitions and 5 broilers per experimental unit) were packed on plastic and carried to the Poultry Experimentation Laboratory, Federal University of Paraná – Sector Palotina. One portion was kept refrigerated (4°C ± 1, 24 h) for 1-d evaluation of meat characteristics and oxidative stability, while another was frozen (−18°C ± 1) for the same evaluations to be repeated after 60 d of refrigerated storage. Evaluations of meat oxidative stability continued to be performed subsequently at 90 and 150 d of freezing (−18°C ± 1).

To evaluate the consumer acceptance, the breast (Pectoralis major) without skin, bones and fat was removed from the carcass manually and packed individually on plastic trays covered with retractile film (120 μm, permeability to O2:1 cm3/m2/24 h at 5°C and relative humidity of 75%) and wrapped in a way that simulated the condition in Brazilian market conditions (illuminated display at 4°C, 12 h/d) during 12 d of storage.

Poultry Meat Characteristics

Color

The instrumental color was evaluated with the CIELab system, using a chromameter (HunterLab Co., VA) with a sight angle of ten degrees. Six measurements at random points were recorded per sample to obtain data on the lightness (L*), redness (a*) and yellowness (b*).

Thawing and Cooking Losses

To evaluate losses due to thawing, initially, the breast samples were weighed, packaged in closed polyethylene plastic bags without the presence of air and frozen (–18°C ± 1°C for 24 h). After freezing, the samples were removed and thawed under refrigeration (4°C ± for 24 h) (Oliveira et al., 2015).

The cooking losses were measured according to the approach used by (Boiago et al., 2014; Dalle Zotte et al., 2017). The breasts were previously weighed, individually packed in polypropylene bags, and cooked in a bain-marie (80°C). The breast temperature was monitored by a thermocouple (Incoterm, 145 mm, Incoterm LTDA, Brazil) inserted in the geometric centre of the meat. After reaching an internal temperature of 72°C, the breasts were removed from the bags, dried with a paper towel, cooled to 25°C and weighed again. Losses (%) were calculated each day as a percentage in relation to the initial weight.

After 60 d, the meats were previously thawed under refrigeration (4°C, approximately 24 h) as described by Zhuang and Savage (2013) and followed the same procedure described above.

Shear force

The shear force was determined using a TA-HD texturometer (Stable Micro System, Surrey, England) equipped with a Warner–Bratzler blade. The equipment was configured with a 10 kg load cell and a cross velocity of 1 mm/s. Rectangular cooked breast pieces, 1 mm/s in section (6 pieces), were cut perpendicularly (Honikel, 1998).

Microstructure

Poultry breast microstructure analysis was conducted after a day of storage with a scanning electron microscope at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV. Samples with a day of storage were fixed in glutaraldehyde (20% V: V), dehydrated by an alcohol series (70–99% ethyl alcohol V: V) and dried on a critic point drying chamber (8 cycles) for the sample's microstructure preservation. They were assembled on aluminum stubs and covered with gold to observe the meat microstructure. The micrometric scale was projected at the same optical conditions (de Moraes Pinto et al., 2023).

Poultry Meat Oxidative Activity

The evaluations of meat oxidative stability were carried out on d 1, 60, 90 and 150.

Lipid Oxidation

Lipid oxidation was measured using a TBARS assay (Oliveira et al., 2021). Five grams of poultry breast meat was homogenized with TCA solution (7.5% Trichloroacetic acid, 0.1% Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid and 0.1% Gallic acid, 1:2 m/V) in Ultra Turrax (Tecnal Turratec TE-102, Piracicaba, SP, Brazil, 7000 rpm, 1 min) and centrifuged (4000 rpm, 4°C, 15 min). The supernatant was filtered and mixed (1:1 V/V) with the TBARS reagent (1% Thiobarbituric acid, 562.5 mM HCl, 15% Trichloroacetic acid, 1:1 m/V). Then, the mixture was boiled (100°C, 15 min), cooled (25°C), and the absorbance was measured (532 nm). Results are expressed as mg of MDA/kg of poultry meat according to the standard curve of malondialdehyde (1,3,3-tetramethoxypropane).

Antioxidant Activity (Radical Scavenging ABTS and DPPH)

Breast antioxidant activity was measured with 2,2-difenil-1-picrilhidrazil (DPPH) and 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid); ABTS) radical scavenging according to (Li et al., 2009) and (Re et al., 1999) respectively. Analysis was carried out on the 1st and 60th day of storage. The poultry extracts were obtained by extraction (1:2 m/V of methanol) in an Ultra-Turrax homogenizer (Tecnal Turratec TE-102, Piracicaba, SP, Brazil), followed by centrifugation (4,000 rpm, 25°C, 15 min) and filtration in quantitative paper. Radical reduction (ABTS+) was obtained through the interaction of 7 mM ABTS solution (5 mL) with 88 mL of potassium persulfate (140 mM). The mixture was incubated in the dark (25°C, 16 h) and diluted with ethanol until an absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.2 nm. An extract aliquot (30 µL) was mixed with the ABTS+ solution (3000 µL and the absorbance was measured (734 nm) after 6 min (Re et al., 1999). The DPPH radical scavenging activity was measured by adding methanolic DPPH solution (60 μM, 1:9 V/V) to the poultry extract and incubated for 30 min in the dark and measured in a spectrophotometer at 515 nm. The ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging activity (%) was calculated by Equation:ABTSandDPPHradicalscavengingactivity(%)=(1−(Asamplet=0Asamplet))*100which: Asamplet=0 is the absorbance of the sample at zero time and Asamplet is the absorbance of the sample.

Consumer Acceptance

Meat Visual Appraisal and Willingness to Buy

For visual appraisal, the breasts were photographed in standardized conditions as described by (Passetti et al., 2017) with some changes. Breasts were unpacked, and photographs were taken on the first to the twelfth day of storage with a Nikon Coolpix B500 (manual mode shutter velocity 1/20; opening size F5.3, ISO 1600; focal distance 40 mm) digital camera. The digital camera was assembled on a photograph handler fixed perpendicularly at 45 cm, with 2 fluorescent tube lamps D65 as standard illuminant to distribute light uniformly through the sample and avoid external light interference. A mini-color-checker GretagMacbeth (Colour-Confidence, Birmingham, Great Britain), with 24 colored patches, were photographed with each poultry breast to verify colour reproduction capacity.

Consumer preference analysis was conducted at the Poultry Experimentation Laboratory, Federal University of Paraná – Sector Palotina, Palotina, Paraná, Brazil, in a room prepared for the exams. Thirty consumers were arbitrarily selected based on the Brazilian population outline. Examinations were developed in an equipped room with a media gadget (Epson Powerlite S27 2700 – lamps UHE 200 watts). Pictures (a total of 432: 6 treatments × 6 repetitions × 12 d) were randomly shown one by one. Casual delineation was used to avoid ordination effects. Before the beginning of the visual analysis, consumers received an initial training, to evaluate, in 5 s, each photograph with an interval of 10 s, to avoid fatigue. Consumers were instructed to assess the acceptability of the poultry color (ranging from 1 to 9), disregarding other aspects such as size, myopathies, bruises, exudate, and subcutaneous fat. Purchase intention was also assessed (whether they were willing to buy or not). Although 30 consumers took part in the visual exam, 7 had missing data and were disregarded from the socioeconomic outline and consuming habits analysis (Chan et al., 2013; Prado et al., 2015; Possamai et al., 2018) .

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were developed using SPSS (23.0 version; IBM SPSS Statistics, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, EUA) for Windows OS. Treatment and storage time were determined as fixed coefficients in factorial planning, and treatment was the only fixed effect evaluated. Consumers were taken as random effects and the blocking effect section. Differences were significant when P < 0.05 and the Tukey test was performed.

For visual appraisal analysis, the participants’ answers (visual acceptance and willingness to buy) for each meat sample were used. After that, visual characteristics were evaluated, and the treatment (diet) and storage period were considered fixed effects. For the consumer acceptance examination, diet and storage were considered fixed effects, and the consumers were taken as random effects. Differences were examined with Tukey's test (P < 0.05). Answers related to habits and buying preferences were analyzed based on the answer frequency. To analyze the score development and its standard deviations at the storage period, a simple regression for the effect after each day was performed (Passetti et al., 2017; de Moraes Pinto et al., 2023).

The water retention capacity of broiler chicken meat is influenced by changes in muscle structural characteristics (Petracci et al., 2015).

Antibiotic growth promoters (AGP) are known to promote more effective muscle development (Fernández Miyakawa et al., 2024). Well-developed muscles with intact cell membranes result in better water retention capacity in the meat, contributing to a juicier texture and better quality (Liu et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021).

Recent studies report that bioactive compounds can act as antioxidants, protecting muscle cells and proteins from oxidative damage, preserving the structure of the meat and consequently contributing to the maintenance of water retention capacity (Mohamed and Mansour, 2012; Ali, 2016; Raeisi et al., 2016; Obeidat et al., 2022). However, in this study, it was observed that cooking loss was higher when the chicken diet was supplemented with a blend of organic acids and microencapsulated bioactive compounds (B600) at a dose of 600 g/T. This increase in cooking loss may be related to the specific effect of the dose used. Essential oils can become toxic to cells and cell membranes due to their pro-oxidant effects and deterioration of muscle structures, especially proteins, impacting the water retention capacity of muscle structures (Avila-Ramos et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2018).

Recent studies on antioxidants highlight the importance of several factors in their performance, such as the type of free radicals formed, where and how these radicals are generated, and the ideal doses to ensure protection (Halliwell et al., 1995; Bianchi and Antunes, 1999; Young and Lowe, 2001). Thus, a bioactive compound may function well as a protector in 1 system but may fail or even have a negative effect in another system (Luna et al., 2010; Soler-Cantero et al., 2012).

In meat, lipid oxidation develops and rapidly grows as the number of free radicals and hydroperoxides builds because of its own pro-oxidant actions (Narciso-Gaytán et al., 2010; Xiong et al., 2020).

Compounds with built-in antioxidant activity in the diet can be transferred to the muscle to prevent or reduce oxidation of metabolic processes and enhance the meat quality (Falowo; Fayemi; Muchenje, 2014; Obeidat; Nusairat; Obeidat, 2022). Normally, these compounds are added to the animals’ diets at moderate levels because the addition of these compounds at higher levels can lead to undesirable effects, like pro-oxidative activity (de Oliveira Monteschio et al., 2017).

Previous studies about diet supplementation or antioxidant addition in poultry diet have highlighted the role of antioxidants in the preservation of oxidative stability and minimization of meat deterioration throughout storage and processing. (Ali, 2016; García et al., 2007; Luna et al., 2010).

The supplementary questionnaire distributed to consumers provided information on consumption habits and preferences (Supplementary Table 1 and 2). A gradual decline in visual appraisal was expected due to oxidative processes that caused meat deterioration. Bioactive compounds are known for their meat protection capacity, which contributes to sensorial properties maintenance, makes the meat more attractive to consumers at the purchasing moment, and increases the meat's shelf life (de Sousa Ribeiro et al., 2022; Luna et al., 2010). However, in high quantities, these compounds can have pro-oxidative effects (Luna et al., 2010; Soler-Cantero et al., 2012), as shown in B450 and B600 treatments. Garcia et al. (2017) reported that with the addition of lemongrass essential oil to the diet of broilers resulted in a brighter color (Cázares-Gallegos et al., 2019) evaluated the supplementation of oregano essential oil in the diet of broilers and observed a positive effect on meat quality. de Moraes Pinto et al. (2023) state that color preservation makes the meat more attractive and accepted by consumers at the purchasing moment.

CONCLUSIONS

The B300 treatment demonstrated a protection effect on lipid stability and consumer acceptance on poultry meat. Although bioactive compounds contributed to maintaining the quality and sensory properties of the meat, the B450 and B600 treatments were inclined to the pro-oxidative behavior. Results highlighted the key importance of precise dosages and strict control of additives used in animal productions to ensure quality and acceptability of the meat by demanding consumers.
