History of Presentation

A 31-year-old man presented with acute-onset dyspnea and palpitations on the fourth postoperative day following elective cardiac surgery. The symptoms developed suddenly at rest and were persistent, prompting urgent clinical evaluation. The patient described an uncomfortable sensation of rapid heartbeats associated with mild shortness of breath but denied chest pain, syncope, presyncope, dizziness, or focal neurological symptoms. On physical examination, the patient was alert and oriented. He appeared mildly distressed due to palpitations but was not in overt respiratory failure. Heart rate was elevated and regular, and blood pressure was preserved, with no evidence of hypotension or shock. Oxygen saturation was within normal limits on room air. Cardiac auscultation revealed tachycardia with regular rhythm and normal prosthetic valve sounds, without new murmurs. Lung examination demonstrated mild bibasilar crackles without overt pulmonary edema. Peripheral perfusion was preserved, and there were no signs of volume overload or peripheral edema.

Overall, the clinical presentation was characterized by symptomatic postoperative tachycardia in a hemodynamically stable patient.

Past Medical History

The patient had undergone uncomplicated surgical aortic valve replacement with concomitant replacement of the ascending aorta for aortic valve disease and ascending aortic pathology. The perioperative and immediate postoperative course was uneventful. He had no prior history of arrhythmias, syncope, or known conduction system disease. There was no history of ischemic heart disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, or congenital heart disease. The patient was not known to have hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or other chronic systemic illnesses.

He was not receiving long-term antiarrhythmic therapy before surgery. There was no relevant family history of inherited arrhythmia syndromes or sudden cardiac death.

Differential Diagnosis

In the early postoperative setting following cardiac surgery, sustained narrow-complex tachycardia with atrioventricular dissociation requires careful evaluation. The differential diagnosis includes junctional ectopic tachycardia (JET), atypical atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia with upper common pathway block, orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia (AVRT) using concealed accessory pathways, atrial tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, and fascicular ventricular tachycardia.

Given the patient’s symptoms and recent cardiac surgery, a systematic diagnostic evaluation was undertaken. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography excluded pulmonary embolism and other acute pulmonary pathology. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved biventricular systolic function, a normally functioning prosthetic aortic valve and ascending aortic graft, and no evidence of pericardial effusion or acute heart failure.

Continuous telemetry monitoring revealed sustained narrow-complex tachycardia with atrioventricular dissociation and ventricular predominance (V > A). Intermittent sinus capture beats were observed during tachycardia (Figure 1). A 12-lead electrocardiogram obtained during tachycardia confirmed narrow QRS tachycardia with QRS morphology identical to sinus rhythm. Narrow QRS morphology excluded ventricular tachycardia, and the absence of right bundle branch block morphology argued against fascicular ventricular tachycardia. Atrioventricular dissociation excluded AVRT, and ventricular predominance (V > A) made atrial tachycardia unlikely.

Taken together, the postoperative setting, atrioventricular dissociation, ventricular predominance, identical QRS morphology to sinus rhythm, and intermittent sinus capture beats strongly supported an automatic junctional mechanism most consistent with JET.

Given the patient’s preserved hemodynamic status and absence of ischemia or heart failure, an initial conservative management strategy was adopted. The patient was placed under continuous cardiac monitoring, and reversible postoperative factors known to precipitate tachyarrhythmias were systematically addressed.

Electrolyte levels were reviewed and corrected as needed, with particular attention to potassium and magnesium levels. Adequate pain control was ensured, and the patient was assessed for potential contributing factors, including anemia, hypoxia, infection, or volume imbalance; none were identified as significant contributors.

Given the electrocardiogram (ECG) features consistent with an automatic rather than reentrant tachyarrhythmia, electrical cardioversion was not pursued. Similarly, empiric administration of antiarrhythmic drugs was avoided, as inappropriate therapy may exacerbate junctional automaticity or lead to unnecessary adverse effects.

No invasive electrophysiological study was performed, as the diagnosis was established based on noninvasive ECG and telemetry findings, and the clinical course was stable.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Over the subsequent days, the tachycardia gradually decreased in frequency and ultimately resolved spontaneously. The patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout hospitalization, with no episodes of hypotension, syncope, or heart failure. No recurrence of tachycardia was observed on continuous telemetry before discharge.

The patient was discharged in good clinical condition with outpatient cardiology follow-up. At follow-up, he remained asymptomatic and in stable sinus rhythm, with no recurrence of palpitations or dyspnea. No long-term antiarrhythmic therapy was required.

Discussion

JET is an automatic tachyarrhythmia arising from the atrioventricular junction and is most commonly encountered following pediatric congenital heart surgery. In contrast, postoperative JET is rarely described in adults and may therefore be underrecognized in clinical practice.1,2

The proposed mechanisms of postoperative JET include surgical trauma to the atrioventricular junction, local inflammation, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and postoperative autonomic imbalance.3, 4, 5 These mechanisms are particularly relevant during the early postoperative period following cardiac surgery.

Recognition of JET relies primarily on careful ECG interpretation. In the present case, telemetry and 12-lead ECG recordings demonstrated narrow QRS tachycardia with atrioventricular dissociation, ventricular predominance (V > A), identical QRS morphology to sinus rhythm, and intermittent sinus capture beats, supporting an automatic junctional mechanism.

The principal differential diagnoses included atypical atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia with upper common pathway block and orthodromic reciprocating tachycardia using concealed nodofascicular or nodoventricular pathways. However, these arrhythmias are reentrant mechanisms that would typically demonstrate reproducible initiation patterns or perturbation of the tachycardia circuit. In contrast, the tachycardia observed in this patient demonstrated stable automatic behavior with spontaneous resolution during supportive management.6, 7, 8, 9, 10

Although invasive electrophysiological confirmation was not performed, the overall ECG findings strongly favored postoperative JET as the most likely diagnosis.

This case highlights the importance of recognizing postoperative JET in adults, as accurate ECG-based diagnosis may prevent unnecessary cardioversion, invasive procedures, or inappropriate antiarrhythmic therapy.

Study limitations

First, the diagnosis of JET rests entirely on noninvasive ECG and telemetry findings; no invasive electrophysiological study was performed to confirm the mechanism. Second, although intermittent sinus beats were observed during tachycardia, unequivocal capture beats with a measurable return cycle were not demonstrated, which would have provided stronger evidence for atrioventricular dissociation with ventricular predominance. Third, the possibility of atypical atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia with upper common pathway block or nodofascicular/nodoventricular reciprocating tachycardia cannot be definitively excluded without electrophysiological study. Accordingly, the diagnosis should be regarded as a clinical presumption based on the overall electrocardiographic and clinical picture rather than a confirmed electrophysiological diagnosis.

Conclusions

JET should be considered in adult patients presenting with narrow-complex tachycardia and atrioventricular dissociation in the early postoperative period following cardiac surgery. Careful ECG interpretation may help differentiate automatic from reentrant tachycardias and avoid unnecessary interventions. As detailed in the study limitations, unequivocal capture beats were not documented and invasive electrophysiological confirmation was not pursued; the diagnosis is therefore best characterized as a clinical presumption, with the overall noninvasive findings most consistent with postoperative JET.
