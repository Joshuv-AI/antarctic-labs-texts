Functionalized bacterial cellulose membranes with controlled biodegradation and β-estradiol delivery properties for potential intrauterine adhesion prevention
Authors: Lu Cheng, Chunyan Zhong, Yuguang Zhong, Jun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntrauterine adhesions pose a significant challenge to female fertility, with current treatments showing limited efficacy and safety concerns, including poor biocompatibility and secondary trauma risks. In this work, we present a novel biodegradable bacterial nanocellulose (BC)-based physical barrier that integrates controlled drug release capabilities for preventing intrauterine adhesions. Through TEMPO-mediated oxidation and N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting, we developed BC membranes exhibiting superior biocompatibility (cell death rate <5%) and selective fibroblast inhibition. The system achieves controlled biodegradation over 14 days while maintaining mechanical integrity (tensile strength >2.0 MPa) and providing sustained β-estradiol release. This approach represents a significant advancement in intrauterine adhesion prevention strategies.

Introduction

The global Intrauterine adhesions (IUA), also known as Asherman’s syndrome, is a gynecological condition characterized by the formation of fibrous adhesions between uterine walls due to endometrial trauma, resulting in partial or complete obliteration of the uterine cavity. Epidemiological studies indicate that while the incidence of IUA in women of reproductive age is approximately 0.3–2%, this rate significantly increases to 25–30% among high-risk populations, particularly in women who have undergone curettage procedures or those with recurrent miscarriages or infertility [1–5].

IUA significantly impacts female reproductive health. Clinical data demonstrate that patients with IUA history achieve pregnancy rates of only 42.8–66.1% [6]. It has been reported that approximately 31% of IUA patients experience reduced menstruation, while 37% develop amenorrhea [7]. A retrospective cohort study involving 1564 patients revealed that, compared to the control group without IUA, IUA patients who underwent adhesiolysis surgery exhibited significantly higher frequency of pregnancy complications, including placenta accreta (17.6% vs 1.4%), placenta increta (5.4% vs 0.7%), placenta previa (8.1% vs 2.0%), and postpartum hemorrhage (21.6% vs 3.4%) [8].

The current universal treatment for IUA comprises hysteroscopic adhesiolysis combined with estrogen therapy to promote endometrial regeneration [4]. However, the wound healing process following surgery frequently leads to adhesion recurrence. To mitigate this risk, post-operative placement of physical barriers such as intrauterine devices (IUDs), balloons, or anti-adhesion biological scaffolds is commonly employed. A prospective randomized control trial has shown that placing an intrauterine balloon for 28 days following hysteroscopic adhesiolysis was found to effectively reduce IUA recurrence rates to 22.2%, improve menstrual patterns in 82.2% of cases, and increases conception rate to 60.6% [9]. However, such long-period balloon treatment carries high risk of infection and recurrence of adhesions, and brings great inconvenience to patients’ daily lives.

Nevertheless, current physical barrier approaches for preventing IUA still exhibit several limitations [10, 11]. First of all, some anti-adhesion materials exhibit biocompatibility issues, e.g., metallic components in copper-containing IUD may potentially elicit inflammatory responses or foreign body reactions. Secondly, synthetic polymeric materials, such as polylactic acid, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), polypropylene oxide, and hydrophilic polyethylene oxide, present challenges related to either insufficient biodegradability or the release of potentially toxic degradation fragments, necessitating subsequent removal procedures that risk secondary trauma [12, 13]. Conversely, natural materials, such as collagen, chitosan, and hyaluronic acid, often exhibit unpredictable degradation rates that may be either too rapid or too slow, potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy [14, 15]. Thirdly, most currently available physical barrier materials serve solely as physical separators, lacking biological activity to facilitate endometrial repair [10, 11]. Therefore, the development of advanced anti-adhesion materials with optimal biocompatibility, controlled biodegradability, and active endometrial repair promotion capabilities is of importance for effectively reducing post-operative adhesions and associated pregnancy-related complications. Moreover, recently developed innovative technologies, such as imaging-guided in vivo sound printing [16], injectable conductive hydrogel network construction [17], and microneedle infiltration and release systems, show promising potential to provide advanced technical support for the design and fabrication of functional anti-adhesion materials.

Nanocellulose, particularly bacterial cellulose or biocellulose (BC), has emerged as a promising biomaterial in diverse biomedical applications owing to its distinctive physicochemical characteristics and superior biocompatibility [18]. BC exhibits high purity, crystallinity, mechanical strength, and biological compatibility, making it an optimal biomaterial candidate [19]. Extensive research has demonstrated the successful implementation of BC-based materials in various biomedical applications, including wound dressings, artificial skin substitutes, drug delivery systems, and tissue engineering scaffolds, with several products achieving commercial success [20]. BC biofilms possess exceptional water retention capacity and tissue-like flexibility, enabling adaptation to diverse anatomical configurations within the uterine cavity. Additionally, the biological inert nature of BC minimizes potential inflammatory responses and foreign body reactions during transplantation or direct tissue contact. Lastly, the structural and compositional modulation of BC enables precise control over scaffold degradation kinetics, yielding glucose as the primary degradation product, which can be readily metabolized through normal physiological pathways.

Although BC-based anti-adhesion materials for IUA prevention or treatment have not achieved clinical implementation, similar materials utilizing oxidized regenerated cellulose (Interceed®) have demonstrated clinical efficacy. Clinical investigations have demonstrate that combined application of Interceed® with IUDs significantly reduces IUA recurrence rates and improves pregnancy outcomes compared to IUD treatment alone [2]. However, long-term assessments regarding biodegradation kinetics and cellular/tissue compatibility remain to be elucidated.

Although BC has exhibited remarkable potential in preventing and treating IUA, several technical challenges necessitate further technological advancement and optimization. First of all, the inherent hydrophilicity of unmodified BC nanofibers may compromise adequate adhesion to the uterine wall. Secondly, native BC membranes exhibit limited biological functionality in terms of promoting endometrial repair and actively facilitating tissue regeneration. Additionally, third, the absence of endogenous cellulase systems in human body fluids precludes autonomous BC degradation, necessitating subsequent surgical removal with associated risks of secondary trauma.

To address these challenges, this study proposes a multifaceted strategy for modification and functionalization of BC biofilm to develop advanced functional membranes for potential IUA prevention. The strategy encompasses of three aspects with interconnected objectives. The primary objective focuses on surface functionalization through free radical-mediated N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting to modulate the membrane’s hydrophobic properties, thereby achieving optimal anti-adhesion characteristics through minimized cellular attachment. This comprehensive approach aims to provide innovative solutions for both the prevention and treatment of intrauterine adhesions. Secondly, biodegradation kinetics of the membranes are supposed to be precisely regulated through a dual approach combining oxidative pretreatment and in situ cellulase incorporation. Thirdly, positive therapeutic efficacy of the membranes would be achieved via the integration of estrogen loading and controlled release systems. We hope that this integrated strategy would contribute to the development of bioactive materials for IUA management, potentially addressing the prevention and therapeutic aspects of intrauterine adhesions.

Materials and methods

Materials

The 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine nitrogen (TEMPO, 98%), N-Vinyl pyrrolidone (NVP, 99%, stabilized with NaOH), ascorbic acid (99.7%), H2O2 (30%), NaBr (99%), NaClO (10–15% active chlorine), cellulase, β-estradiol (98%) and other chemicals were received from Sinopharm Co., Ltd. and used without additional purification. Biological reagents and materials, including fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), NIH 3T3 fibroblast cells, Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), Hoechst 33342, and Alexa Fluor 488, were all purchased from Beyotime Biotech (Shanghai, China).

BC production and purification

The BC was biosynthesized via static cultivation of the A. xylinum strain 1.1812 in a culture medium comprising glucose (20 g/L), yeast powder (5 g/L), peptone (5 g/L), Na2HPO4 (2.7 g/L), MgSO4 (0.3 g/L), citric acid (1.15 g/L), and ethanol (1.5%v/v). The cultivation was maintained at 30 °C for a period of 7–10 days, following previously established protocols [21]. The resultant BC membrane was purified by cooking in 0.1 M NaOH supplemented with 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate for 30 min, followed by extensive washing with distilled water until neutral pH was achieved. The purification process was repeated for at least three times to ensure the residual bacterial fragments were removed and the endotoxin level was below the threshold specified by Chinese pharmacopoeia requirement (0.5 EU/mL). The purified BC membrane with 5–8 mm in thickness was stored at 4 °C for further modification.

BC membrane surface modification

The purified BC membrane was first oxidized using the TEMPO/NaBr/NaClO system following our previous report [21]. In brief, the BC membranes were first punched into small specimens (5 × 5 mm), then dispersed in solution containing TEMPO (0.1 mmol/g)/NaBr (1.0 mmol/g). The oxidation process was initiated through the dropwise addition of NaClO (10 mmol/g), followed by dropwise addition of NaClO (10 mmol/g), while maintaining pH at 10.5 through periodic addition of 0.5 M NaOH. The reaction proceeded at room temperature for either 6 or 24 h, yielding TOBC6 and TOBC24, respectively. At the end of the reaction, 0.5 M HCl was added to neutralize the reaction mixture, followed by sufficient distilled water washing.

The oxidized BC membranes (TOBC6 and TOBC24) were subsequently subjected to free radical-mediated modification. The BC membranes with different oxidation times (TOBC6 and TOBC24) were dispersed in 50 mM ascorbic acid solution at a concentration of 0.5%, followed by adding 50 mM 30% H2O2 to initiate the free radical generation at room temperature for 30 min. The grafting of NVP onto TOBC was started by adding the NVP (0.1 M/g) into the BC suspension and raising the temperature to 60 °C under the N2 environment. The reaction was maintained for 12 h for TOBC6 and 24 h for TOBC24, yielding TOBC6_PN12 and TOBC24_PN24, respectively. The modified BC membranes were filtrated and washed with distilled water until the pH and conductivity of the filtrate close to that of distill water and then stored at 4 °C for further functionalization.

Functionalization of BC membrane using microneedle infiltration

The modified BC membranes were functionalized via in situ incorporation of cellulase and the β-estradiol using microneedle-assisted infiltration (A20, R. LIN, China). The optimal concentrations of cellulase and β-estradiol were predetermined through preliminary experiments and clinical dosage recommendations to achieve controlled membrane degradation within a therapeutic period of 1–2 weeks while maintaining appropriate drug release kinetics [22–25]. The cellulase complex (20,000 U/g cellulose) and the beta-estradiol (100 mg/g cellulose) were dissolved in PBS at 4 °C and then loaded in the syringe. The mixture was incorporated into the membranes using the automated infiltration apparatus equipped with a 36-needle inoculator array (needle dimensions: 2.5 mm length, 125 μm diameter). The infiltration process was conducted on a temperature-controlled glass plate maintained at 4 °C, with solution delivery parameters set at 0.1 mL/min flow rate and 5-s pulse vibration intervals. The functionalized BC membranes were subjected to freeze-dried at −50 °C for 3 days to yield the functionalized BC aerogels ready for use.

Surface chemistry and physical properties characterization

The physicochemical characterization of BC and its derivatives was conducted using multiple analytical techniques. The FTIR spectra of the BC and its derivatives were recorded using an ATR-FTIR spectrometer (Alpha-II, Bruker, Germany) in the range of 4000–550 cm−1 with 32 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted using a K-Alpha spectrometer (USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al X-ray source (E = 1486.6 eV). The C-C bond peak at 284.8 eV was employed as the reference for binding energy calibration.

Surface wettability was evaluated via contact angle measurements using a Dataphysics OCA 20 goniometer (Germany). Measurements were performed using 5 μL water droplets, with readings taken 10 s post-deposition. Three independent measurements were conducted at different locations for each sample to ensure reproducibility.

Thermal stability analysis was performed using a TGA 4000 thermogravimetric analyzer (Perkin Elmer, USA). Samples were heated from 25 °C to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min under nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate: 20 mL/min).

Mechanical properties were assessed using an E43 universal testing machine (MTS, USA) equipped with a 500 N load cell. Specimens (5 mm in width) were tested at a speed of 5 mm/min. Sample dimensions were precisely measured using a micrometer for stress calculations. Five replicates were tested for each sample under controlled conditions (50% relative humidity, 25 °C).

Morphological characterization and surface elemental analysis (C, N, O) were performed using scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (Quanta FEG 250, FEI, USA) operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV.

In vitro biodegradation of the membranes and the release of the β-estradiol

The biodegradation kinetics of BC and modified membranes, along with β-estradiol release profiles, were evaluated using gravimetric analysis and UV spectrophotometry, respectively, following established protocols [25, 26]. In brief, the membrane specimens (5 × 5 mm) loaded with cellulase and β-estradiol were immersed in PBS and incubated in 12-well plate at 37 °C. The plates were maintained on an orbital platform shaker at 20 rpm throughout the experiment. Morphological changes of the membranes were documented photographically at 12-h intervals. At predetermined time points, the membrane suspensions were centrifuged (5000 rpm, 5 min) to separate the supernatant from the solid residue for subsequent analyses. The β-estradiol concentration in the supernatant was determined spectrophotometrically at 280 nm using a predetermined standard calibration curve. The drug release rate was calculated as a function of the initial β-estradiol loading and sample mass. The membrane degradation rate was quantified by determining the residual solid mass after drying at 105 °C for 8 h, expressed as a percentage of the initial sample mass. Control experiments were conducted using unmodified BC membranes and BC membranes loaded with cellulase only, subjected to identical experimental conditions.

Cytocompatibility and hemocompatibility evaluation

The cytocompatibility of the materials was assessed using both indirect and direct contact methods with NIH 3T3 fibroblasts and RAW 264.7 macrophages. For indirect evaluation, membrane specimens (5 × 5 mm) were extracted in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) at 37 °C for 72 h, following ISO 10993-12:2021 guidelines. The extracts and negative control (pristine DMEM) were used to culture cells at a seeding density of 8 × 103 cells/well in 12-well plates. Culture conditions were maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 95% relative humidity, with daily medium renewal.

For direct contact studies, membrane specimens were sterilized under combined UV and ozone exposure for 30 min prior to cell seeding. Cells were seeded directly onto the membrane surface at 8 × 103 cells/well under identical culture conditions, with daily medium replacement.

The Calcein/PI Live/Dead assay kit (C2015L, Beyotime, China) was utilized to stain the indirect cultured cells following the manual. Fluorescence images of the cells were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (XD-202, Jiangnan, China). Relative cell death ratios were quantified using ImageJ software analysis of the fluorescence images [27, 28].

The Actin-Tracker Green 488 (C1345L, Beyotime, China) and Hoechst 33342 kits (C1027, Beyotime, China) were utilized to stain the direct cultured cells following the manual. Confocal images of the cells on the membranes were acquired using a confocal microscopy (TCS SP5, Leica).

Hemolysis studies were conducted according to ASTM F756-13 standards, following our previously established protocol [29]. Fresh human whole blood was collected in EDTA-anticoagulated tubes from healthy volunteers. Erythrocytes were isolated by centrifugation (3000 rpm, 5 min) and resuspended in PBS. Membrane specimens were incubated with 1 mL erythrocyte suspension at 37 °C for 4 h. Following centrifugation (3000 rpm, 5 min), the absorbance of supernatant was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (Multiskan MK3, Thermo Fisher, USA). PBS and deionized water were used as negative and positive control for hemolysis activity calculation.

Statistical analysis

All biological experiments were performed in triplicate. For cell viability assays, each condition was tested in six independent experiments conducted on different days. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software. Results are presented as means ± standard deviation. Statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and t-test, with significance defined as ***p < 0.001.

Results

Surface chemistry analysis

Following static fermentation for 7–10 days, A. xylinum strain biosynthesized a distinctive light-yellow pellicle at the gas-liquid interface. Subsequently, the pellicle was subjected to purification, surface chemical modifications, and functionalization, with the objective of developing an innovative intrauterine adhesion barrier material exhibiting controlled biodegradability, anti-adhesion properties, and sustained drug release characteristics for potential application in intrauterine adhesions management.

The physicochemical properties of the BC, its TEMPO-oxidized derivatives, and subsequent radical-initiated grafting products were systematically characterized through multiple analytical techniques. The comprehensive characterization included Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy for structural analysis, X-ray diffraction (XRD) for crystallinity assessment, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) for surface chemical composition analysis, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) for thermal stability evaluation, and surface water contact angle measurements for wettability determination, as illustrated in Fig. 1A–D.

Physicochemical characterization of pristine BC and its derivatives: A, B TEM analysis with insert picture of the purified BC, C FTIR spectroscopic analysis, D XRD diffraction patterns, E thermogravimetric profiles, and F mechanical properties assessed via stress-strain measurements

FTIR spectroscopic analysis of BC revealed multiple cellulose characteristic absorption bands (Fig. 1A). The prominent band at 3350 cm−1 was attributed to the hydroxyl (OH) stretching vibrations within the cellulose molecular architecture. Two distinct peaks observed at 2977 cm−1 and 2897 cm−1 were characteristic of CH stretching. The absorption band at 1643 cm−1 was assigned to either the COO’ stretching of the cellulose terminal hemiacetal moiety or the presence of absorbed H2O molecules. Additional characteristic bands were identified as follows: CH2 bending at 1423 cm−1, CH bending at 1366 cm−1, C-O ring stretching or C-OH bending at 1167 cm−1, antisymmetric in-phase ring stretching at 1107 cm−1, C-O stretching at 1058 cm−1, antisymmetric out-of-phase stretching at 897 cm−1, and OH out-of-plane bending at 663 cm−1.

Upon TEMPO-mediated oxidation (coded as TOBC6 and TOBC24), a distinctive absorption band emerged at 1605 cm−1, corresponding to COO’ stretching vibrations, indicating successful oxidation of primary hydroxyl groups to carboxyl functionalities. Subsequent radical-initiated N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting resulted in the appearance of absorption bands in the modified products (coded as TOBC6_PN12 and TOBC24_PN24) at 1473 cm−1 and 1285 cm−1, which are characteristic peaks of C-N stretching vibrations. Furthermore, characteristic absorption bands at 1731–1736 cm−1 were assigned to C=O stretching of carboxyl groups, while N-H vibrations associated with polyvinylpyrrolidone were observed at 1650–1654 cm−1.

XRD analysis (Fig. 1B) of BC exhibited well-defined diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 14.713°, 16.882°, and 22.845°, which demonstrated excellent correlation with the standard diffraction pattern of natural cellulose (PDF#50-2241, 2θ = 14.988°, 16.487°, 22.781°), confirming the identity of the fermentation product. The observed diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 14.713°, 16.882°, 22.845°, and 34.456° were indexed to the (1-10), (110), (200), and (0047) crystallographic planes of cellulose I, respectively. Quantitative analysis using the Segal method revealed a crystallinity index of 87.4% for pristine BC. Sequential TEMPO-mediated oxidation for 6 and 24 h resulted in an initial increase in crystallinity index to 90.6% for the TOBC6, followed by a slight decrease to 88.5% for the TOBC24. Notably, the subsequent radical-initiated N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting process did not induce significant alterations in the crystallinity index.

Thermal stability characteristics of BC and its derivatives were systematically evaluated using TGA, as illustrated in Fig. 1C. Pristine BC exhibited a maximum decomposition rate at 310.1 °C, accompanied by a significant mass reduction of 54.7% and a residual inorganic content of 10.1% at 780 °C. In contrast, all modified derivatives demonstrated distinctive three stages thermal degradation profiles, characterized by two discrete decomposition events occurring at approximately 230–250 °C and 340 °C. Specifically, TOBC6 exhibited sequential mass losses of 16.4% and 56.6% at 247.3 °C and 342.5 °C, respectively, yielding a final residual content of 6.1% at 780 °C. TOBC24 demonstrated comparable thermal behavior with mass reductions of 15.0% and 54.9% at 239.6 °C and 343.2 °C, resulting in 6.4% residual content at 780 °C. Similarly, TOBC6_PN12 displayed mass losses of 15.5% and 54.9% at 241.4 °C and 340.8 °C (final residue: 7.7%), while TOBC24_PN24 showed mass reductions of 16.2% and 55.1% at 236.5 °C and 341.4 °C (final residue: 7.6%).

Mechanical characterization revealed that pristine BC membrane possessed a wet tensile strength of 2.66 MPa with an associated strain of 32.7% (Fig. 1D). Following TEMPO-mediated oxidation, TOBC6 and TOBC24 exhibited tensile strengths and corresponding strains of 2.61 MPa/30.1% and 2.17 MPa/33.4%, respectively. Prolonged oxidation duration resulted in reduction in tensile strength while maintaining similar strain values. Subsequent radical-initiated grafting further decreased the mechanical properties, with TOBC6_PN12 and TOBC24_PN24 demonstrating reduced tensile strengths and strains of 2.26 MPa/27.5% and 2.16 MPa/33.0%, respectively.

XPS analysis of pristine BC and its derivatives yielded comprehensive surface chemistry data, as presented in Fig. 2. Survey scans revealed characteristic photoelectron peaks at binding energies of approximately 284.8 eV, 398.3 eV, and 531.2 eV, corresponding to C1s, N1s, and O1s (Fig. 2A), respectively. The deconvoluted high-resolution C1s spectra revealed four distinct chemical environments: C-C (283.2 eV), C-O (284.8 eV), O-C-O (286.2 eV), and O-C=O (287.5 eV) bonds, with C-C signals potentially attributable to adventitious carbon or surface contamination (Fig. 2B). O1s high-resolution spectra exhibited two characteristic peaks at 529.3 eV and 531.2 eV, assigned to C=O and C-O-H bonds (Fig. 2C), respectively. N1s high-resolution spectra revealed peaks at 398.3 eV and 400.1 eV, corresponding to grafted C-N bonds and -NH2 groups in the residual bacterial protein in BC (Fig. 2D), respectively.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopic analysis of pristine BC and its derivatives: A XPS survey spectra, and high-resolution deconvoluted spectra of B C1s, C O1s, and D N1s

Surface wettability analysis, as depicted in Fig. 3, demonstrated that pristine BC membrane exhibited a water contact angle of 28.1°. TEMPO-mediated oxidation for 6 h and 24 h enhanced the hydrophilicity, resulting in decreased contact angles of 24.1° and 21.2°, respectively. Conversely, subsequent radical-initiated N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting for 12 h and 24 h led to increased hydrophobicity, demonstrated by elevated contact angles of 39.6° and 47.3°, respectively.

Surface wettability assessment through water contact angle measurements of various membranes, determined at 10 s following the deposition of 5 μL water droplets

Morphology analysis

Morphological characterization via SEM (Fig. 4) revealed that freeze-dried BC maintained its intricate nanofibrous network structure, with surface elemental analysis indicating 2.93% nitrogen content. TEMPO-mediated oxidation followed by freeze-drying resulted in the formation of numerous thin-walled porous architectures while partially preserving loose nanofibrous structures. Extended TEMPO oxidation (24 h) was associated with a reduction in surface nitrogen content to 1.26%. Following radical-initiated N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting, the membrane surface underwent significant morphological transformation, exhibiting a dense, corrugated structure, and absence of the original porous and nanofibrous features. Surface elemental analysis from the energy dispersive spectrometer confirmed successful grafting of the N-vinyl pyrrolidone onto the surface, evidenced by increased nitrogen content of 9.73% and 10.95% after 12 h (TOBC6_PN12) and 24 h (TOBC24_PN24) reaction duration, respectively.

In vitro biodegradation and β-estradiol release

The in vitro biodegradation profiles of BC and its derivatives were systematically investigated, as illustrated in Fig. 5A. Pristine BC membrane exhibited neglectable degradation, with approximately 1% mass loss observed during a 14-day dynamic incubation period. Following the in situ incorporation of cellulase via microneedle-assisted infiltration, BC-e membrane demonstrated significantly enhanced degradation kinetics, characterized by three distinct phases: an initial lag phase (days 1–4), followed by accelerated degradation (post day 5), reaching a plateau phase after day 9, ultimately achieving 71% cumulative degradation by day 14. TEMPO-mediated oxidized derivatives (TOBC6 and TOBC24) exhibited similar degradation profiles but with earlier shifted transition points and elevated terminal degradation rates, correlating positively with oxidation duration. For instance, TOBC24 membrane demonstrated accelerated degradation initiation from day 4, achieved steady-state conditions post day 7, and attained 83% cumulative degradation by day 14. Subsequent radical-initiated N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting reaction resulted in preserved degradation patterns and delayed transition points, and decreased terminal degradation rates. For instance, TOBC6_PN12 membrane exhibited a 3-day delay in degradation transition (8 days vs. 5 days for BC-e), ultimately achieving 60% degradation.

Functional characterization of membrane properties: A In vitro biodegradation profiles, B β-estradiol release profiles, C cell death ratio assessment of NIH 3T3 fibroblasts and Raw 264.7 macrophages after 1- and 3-day exposure to membrane extracts (*** p < 0.001), and D hemocompatibility analysis through hemolysis ratio evaluation

The controlled release characteristics of β-estradiol from BC and its functionalized derivatives were evaluated under in vitro conditions accompanying the membranes degradation, as demonstrated in Fig. 5B. The β-estradiol was co-encapsulated with cellulase through microneedle-mediated incorporation into the BC nanofibrous architecture. The release kinetics were modulated by cellulase-mediated degradation of the nanofibrous network. Pristine BC membrane, lacking cellulase incorporation, demonstrated minimal degradation-mediated release, with only surface-associated β-estradiol being released under dynamic incubation conditions, resulting in 8.5% cumulative release. In contrast, cellulase-incorporated BC-e membrane exhibited a distinctive release profile characterized by rapid β-estradiol release between days 3 and 7, followed by a sustained plateau phase and achieving 91.8% cumulative release by day 14. TEMPO-mediated oxidized membranes demonstrated release profiles that correlated with their respective degradation kinetics as shown in Fig. 5A, with extended oxidation duration resulting in enhanced release efficiency, exemplified by TOBC24 achieving 98.8% cumulative release by day 14. Consistent with degradation patterns, radical-initiated grafting of N-vinyl pyrrolidone induced step-down release kinetics characterized by decreased release rates and lowered terminal release efficiency, with TOBC-6_PN12 exhibiting sustained release beyond day 10, ultimately achieving 83.5% cumulative release by day 14. Mathematical modeling of β-estradiol release profiles revealed best fit to the Korsmeyer-Peppas model (R² = 0.90–0.98). The release exponents fall below 0.5, indicating the Fickian diffusion-controlled release. This mechanism suggests that the β-estradiol release primarily occurs through diffusion from the hydrated BC matrices, enabling sustained therapeutic delivery over the observed 14-day period.

In vitro cytocompatibility and hemocompatibility

The in vitro cytocompatibility of BC and its derivatives was evaluated through indirect contact assessment using membrane extracts, as demonstrated by fluorescence microscopy images of NIH 3T3 fibroblasts and Raw 264.7 macrophages in Fig. 6. Following one day of co-culture with cells, all membrane extracts supported favorable cell growth with minimal observation of red fluorescence-labeled dead cells. Extended culture to day 3 revealed significant proliferation of green fluorescence-labeled viable cells, accompanied by a concurrent increase in cell mortality. Quantitative analysis of cell death rates during 1–3 days of co-culture with various membrane extracts is presented in Fig. 5C. Pristine BC and moderately modified samples (TOBC6 and TOBC6_PN12) exhibited low cell death rates ranging from 0.5–2.5% for both cell types, showing no statistically significant difference from the control (***p < 0.001). In contrast, TOBC24 and TOBC24_PN24 membrane extracts induced significantly elevated cell death rates, reaching a maximum of 5.0% (***p < 0.001).

Direct contact cytocompatibility assessment of BC and its derivatives was visualized through immunofluorescence confocal laser scanning microscopy of NIH 3T3 fibroblasts and Raw 264.7 macrophages, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Compared to the control, NIH 3T3 fibroblasts demonstrated comparable growth and proliferation on pristine BC and moderately modified membranes (TOBC6 and TOBC6_PN12), maintaining normal cellular morphology with dense cytoskeleton and pseudopod. However, TOBC24 and TOBC24_PN24 surfaces exhibited restricted cell growth and proliferation, characterized by limited cell population expansion and absence of healthy cellular pseudopodial structures characteristic of proper adhesion. Immunofluorescence confocal microscopy analysis of Raw 264.7 macrophages revealed normal growth and proliferation across all membrane surfaces, with slightly reduced cell populations on TOBC24 and TOBC24_PN24 samples, though these differences were not significant.

F-actin cytoskeleton (green, Actin-Tracker Green 488) and cell nuclei (blue, Hoechst 33342). Scale bar: 20 μm

The hemocompatibility of BC and its derivatives was assessed through hemolysis rate evaluation, with results presented in Fig. 5D. BC and its TEMPO-mediated oxidized membranes demonstrated relative hemolysis rates below 0.2%. Subsequent radical-initiated N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting reaction resulted in moderately increased hemolysis rates, with samples modified through 12 h (TOBC6_PN12) and 24 h (TOBC24_PN24) grafting reactions exhibiting hemolysis rates of 0.33% and 0.57%, respectively.

Discussion

Clinical investigations have demonstrated that intrauterine adhesions represent a primary cause of female infertility [1, 30]. While hysteroscopic adhesiolysis combined with physical barriers and hormone therapy has been widely adopted in clinical practice, conventional intrauterine devices composed of balloon materials or synthetic polymers present significant limitations, including poor biocompatibility, biological inertness, and the necessity for removal procedures [31]. These limitations consequently introduce risks of secondary trauma and infection, resulting in persistently high post-operative adhesion recurrence rates. To address these challenges, this study proposes a novel active physical barrier therapeutic approach through the integration of bacterial cellulose biosynthesis with surface chemical modification and functional enhancement strategies. This proposed approach prioritizes three critical aspects: primarily ensuring biological safety, subsequently addressing the controlled degradability of the physical barrier material, and ultimately facilitating active participation in endometrial regeneration with drug release while maintaining barrier function and adhesion prevention. The following discussion systematically examines these three fundamental aspects.

The FTIR spectral analysis of BC exhibited characteristic absorption peaks of natural cellulose I (Fig. 1A), while X-ray diffraction patterns demonstrated crystalline diffraction peaks consistent with standard cellulose PDF cards (Fig. 1B), further confirming its cellulosic properties [32]. These findings align with our previous investigations and other reports utilizing A. xylinum strains for fermentation [21]. FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 1A) and XPS (Fig. 2) analysis [33–37] of BC and its derivatives confirmed successful introduction of carboxyl groups and N-vinyl pyrrolidone molecules onto the BC molecular structure [38–40]. While these modifications resulted in modest reduction of thermal stability, the onset degradation temperatures remained above 200 °C, maintaining compatibility with extreme sterilization processing conditions (e.g., autoclave at 121 °C) [19, 41].

Surface chemical modification of BC membranes resulted in slight reduction of tensile strength; however, minimum tensile strength and strain values were maintained above 2.0 MPa and 25%, respectively. These mechanical properties of the BC membranes align well with clinical requirements for intrauterine applications (e.g., wound healing materials typically require tensile strengths of 1–32 MPa), demonstrating sufficient flexibility for insertion through a standard cervical canal while providing adequate rigidity for proper positioning [42–44].

The biocompatibility of BC has been previously reported in our earlier studies [19, 21], and the current investigation further validates these findings. Both BC membranes and their functionally modified derivatives demonstrated excellent cellular compatibility in both indirect contact assessments using membrane extracts and direct contact co-culture experiments. Notably, even TOBC24_PN24 membrane extracts, which exhibited the highest cell death rate, only reached approximately 5%, substantially below the 20% threshold for cytotoxicity determination according to the ISO 10993-5 (i.e., cell viability should not fall below 80%) [45].

Notably, NIH 3T3 fibroblasts exhibited certain growth inhibition in TOBC24 and TOBC24_PN24 membrane extracts, showing the highest cell death rates (4.6% and 4.8%). Meanwhile, in the direct contact co-culture experiments, these materials also demonstrated significantly reduced cell proliferation compared to control culture plates, indicating their capacity to prevent fibroblast adhesion and growth. However, both indirect and direct contact experiments revealed no significant inhibitory effects on macrophage growth across all membrane types. Given that tissue adhesion in intrauterine adhesions is primarily influenced by fibroblast proliferation and adhesive growth [46], the material’s inhibitory effect on fibroblast growth effectively prevents wound surface fibroblast adhesion and proliferation while maintaining non-toxicity to immune cells, thus demonstrating excellent cell compatibility [47].

The modified BC membranes’ dual functionality of inhibiting fibroblast adhesion while maintaining acceptable immune cell compatibility stems from selective surface chemical modification while preserving BC’s natural cellulose molecular and crystalline structure. This was achieved through TEMPO-mediated oxidation to introduce carboxyl and partial aldehyde groups, followed by free radical-initiated N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting. Previous research has established that residual aldehydes (typically 0.05–0.20 mmol/g) from TEMPO-catalyzed cellulose oxidation exhibit moderate cytotoxicity without causing excessive cell death, providing an ideal scaffold platform for anti-adhesion material development [48–51]. Additionally, surface-grafted N-vinyl pyrrolidone molecules significantly enhanced material hydrophobicity (Fig. 3), creating a barrier to fibroblast adhesion [52, 53]. The grafting modification also partially sealed the membrane’s porous structure (Fig. 4), impeding cellular migration and infiltrative growth, thereby enhancing anti-adhesion efficacy. Furthermore, hemolysis evaluation revealed relative hemolysis rates below 5% for all materials, meeting the threshold requirements of medical device hemocompatibility assessment standards ISO 10993-4 or ASTM F756-17, thus confirming excellent hemocompatibility [54]. It is worth to note that although our initial biocompatibility studies demonstrated favorable short-term safety profiles, long-term tissue responses require careful consideration. Chronic implantation may trigger sustained inflammatory responses or fibrous encapsulation, potentially affecting therapeutic efficacy. Future studies will evaluate extended implantation periods, focusing on local tissue remodeling, inflammatory mediator profiles, and potential immune sensitization.

To achieve controlled biodegradation of the physical barrier material within the uterine cavity, given the absence of cellulase in the human body, this study employed two strategies: introducing carboxyl groups through TEMPO-mediated oxidation to weaken inter-chain hydrogen bonding, and in situ incorporation of cellulase within the cellulose fiber network. As demonstrated in Fig. 5A, TEMPO-mediated oxidation combined with embedded cellulase effectively degraded BC membranes in a 14-day period, with degradation rates controllable through adjustment of TEMPO oxidation duration and subsequent N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting. BC and its functionally modified membranes typically maintained intact three-dimensional structure for 7 days, providing effective physical barrier function, followed by gradual membrane disintegration and degradation. The degradation products, including glucose and partially undegraded cellulose fibrils, can be absorbed in the body or eliminated through bodily fluids [55, 56]. This degradation cycle aligns with clinical intrauterine adhesion treatment periods and allows for material selection based on individual patient requirements for varying degradation rates.

In the treatment of intrauterine adhesions, estrogen therapy is essential alongside physical barrier separation of uterine cavity wounds to promote rapid endometrial tissue regeneration [11]. Conventional clinical approaches typically involve oral administration or transdermal application of β-estradiol preparations. However, oral administration presents several challenges: pharmacokinetic limitations affect drug efficacy, with low doses failing to achieve therapeutic effects at target sites, while high doses risk toxicity to metabolic organs such as the liver [57]. Patients frequently report adverse reactions, including abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, headaches, and breast tenderness, along with potential risks of endometrial cancer development [58–61]. Transdermal applications, while sharing similar risks with oral administration, face additional challenges of absorption and metabolism difficulties, coupled with potential skin irritation responses [62].

Recent years have witnessed the emergence of sustained-release local drug delivery as a novel therapeutic strategy for intrauterine adhesions [63–65]. For instance, Feng et al. [66] developed and clinically evaluated an intrauterine stent capable of sustained estrogen release within the uterine cavity. Their findings demonstrated significantly improved reduction rates in adhesion recurrence compared to conventional post-operative balloon therapy combined with oral estrogen administration (93.33% vs. 58.5%, p < 0.001). However, the silicone rubber-based delivery system remains non-biodegradable in vivo, necessitating removal post-treatment and thus presenting risks of secondary trauma. Furthermore, while this delivery system enables precise estrogen dosing (0.21 mg/day), its complex structure, incorporating stents, cables, and strings, may cause patient discomfort and inconvenience, while also presenting potential infection risks.

In our current study, the β-estradiol is in situ encapsulated within modified BC membranes, stuck within BC’s ultrafine nanofiber network structure. As the BC membrane undergoes degradation, both free and encapsulated estrogen are gradually released, allowing direct absorption by endometrial tissue. It is important to note that the β-estradiol loading dose employed in this study (100 mg/g cellulose) does not represent actual clinical dosage requirements. While it may exceed typical clinical doses (2.0 mg, q.d.) [67], it represents the maximum loading capacity of our delivery system. For clinical translation, we envision significantly lower drug loadings based on established therapeutic windows. Our system allows precise dose adjustment through modification of both drug loading and cellulase carrier concentration, enabling customization to match standard clinical dosing requirements while maintaining delivery efficiency. Moreover, β-estradiol release kinetics are directly influenced by BC membrane degradation profiles. Therefore, future research and application investigations will need to optimize both cellulase and β-estradiol dosages within BC membranes based on actual clinical dosage requirements and treatment durations, ultimately achieving precise and personalized therapeutic outcomes. Compared to current anti-adhesion barriers, our BC-based system offers several advantages. Unlike hyaluronic acid gels, which typically dissolve within 24 h, BC maintains structural integrity for extended periods. While synthetic polymers can trigger inflammatory responses, BC demonstrates superior biocompatibility. Though intrauterine stents provide mechanical support, our system uniquely combines barrier function with drug delivery at potentially lower cost.

While our in vitro results demonstrate promising therapeutic potential, several key considerations must be addressed before clinical translation. From a regulatory perspective, our technology would likely be classified as a combination product, necessitating comprehensive evaluation under both drug and medical device frameworks. Manufacturing scale-up presents significant challenges in maintaining batch consistency and product stability. Safety considerations must address long-term biocompatibility, degradation product toxicity, and immunogenicity. Clinical implementation will require standardized protocols, healthcare provider training, and cost-effectiveness evaluation. Future studies should focus on addressing these challenges through comprehensive preclinical safety studies and optimization of manufacturing processes, ultimately facilitating the successful clinical transition.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the development of an innovative BC-based physical barrier system for potential intrauterine adhesion prevention. The modified membranes exhibit excellent biocompatibility, appropriate mechanical properties, and controlled biodegradation profiles through our dual-modification strategy of TEMPO-mediated oxidation and N-vinyl pyrrolidone grafting. The controlled biodegradation profile, achieved through structural modification and in situ cellulase incorporation, aligns well with clinical treatment requirements. Furthermore, the integration of β-estradiol within the BC nanofiber network enables sustained local drug delivery, potentially overcoming limitations associated with conventional systemic hormone administration. While our results show promise for clinical application, several limitations warrant attention. The optimal drug loading concentration requires further investigation, and long-term in vivo safety studies are needed. Future work should focus on clinical translation, including dose optimization, evaluation of chronic tissue responses, and comparison with existing treatments in larger animal models. Nevertheless, this integrated approach combining physical barrier function with controlled drug delivery represents a significant advance in intrauterine adhesion treatment.
