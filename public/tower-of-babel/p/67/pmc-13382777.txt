Limitations in Blood Pressure and Frailty Categorization
Authors: Ava Suko, Sarvani Chalamalasetty, Aayush Visaria
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

It is with great pleasure that we read the article by Song et al. entitled “The Effect Modification of Systolic Blood Pressure on the Association of Frailty and Cardiovascular Disease in Community‐Dwelling Adults.” [1] We would like to provide additional clinical context, hypothesize potential explanations for findings, and suggest further sensitivity analyses to improve future studies.

Studies have shown that SBP can be significantly altered by variables such as diet [2], stress [3], activity levels [4], comorbidities [5], and medication use [6]. Furthermore, SBP itself can fluctuate quite markedly day‐to‐day and even minute‐to‐minute due to changes in environment or measurement technique. Consequently, as only baseline SBP was included, participants may have shifted into different BP categories throughout the duration of the study, which may introduce exposure misclassification. Baseline SBP would further misclassify true BP status due to potential changes in antihypertensive medications.

Similar limitations exist for frailty calculation and classification. Since the authors utilized a deficit accumulation approach at baseline for frailty determination, and factors such as cognitive ability [7], depression levels [8], and motor functioning [9] are subject to change with age, disease progression, and lifestyle changes, it cannot be justifiably assumed that the defining deficits remain constant throughout the study duration.

A key finding was that frailty was most highly associated with CVD risk amongst the middle SBP (120‐129 mmHg) group. Given that the percentage of participants undergoing anti‐diabetic treatment at baseline in the 120–129 mmHg group exceeded those in the ≥130 mmHg group, this increase in frailty‐CVD associations in the 120–129 mmHg group may partially be attributed to complications with relevant comorbidities. We speculate that, to an extent, the frailty index may be serving as a partial indicator of diabetic disease progression rather than a true measure of frailty. Analyzing the percentages of participants with self‐reported physician‐diagnosed diabetes across each SBP category at baseline in future studies would serve to further strengthen or negate this claim.

Confounding factors such as physical exertion, procedural anxiety, and autonomic dysfunction, which are common amongst patients with diabetes, may impact initial SBP readings. Frail individuals exhibit greater physiologic variability, orthostatic changes, and varying hydration statuses, which may result in greater SBP misclassification at baseline. Namely, these variables may result in a potential undue overabundance of participants in the 120–129 mmHg and ≥130 mmHg categories when utilizing the average of three consecutive BP readings. To reduce this effect, future studies may employ sensitivity analyses that take the average of the final two BP readings [10, 11], rather than all three.

This study suggests that overall physiologic vulnerability may also contain prognostic value for CVD risk beyond established factors such as hypertension, diabetes, and cholesterol levels. However, experimental repeat studies should be conducted that monitor continuous frailty and SBP measures beyond baseline before this study can be applicable for long‐term, clinical implementation.
