Pre‐Treatment Resilience as a Predictor of Veterans' Post‐Traumatic Stress Disorder Symptom Outcomes Immediately and Up To 12‐Months Following Intensive Cognitive Processing Therapy
Authors: Michelle K. Ptak, Dale Smith, Sarah Pridgen, Philip Held
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveResilience is associated with lower post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) severity and may play a beneficial role in PTSD treatment. The present study explored if pre‐treatment resilience and changes in resilience during intensive treatment for PTSD were associated with PTSD and depression symptom change among veterans during and up to a year after treatment.MethodsA total of 727 veterans (age: M = 45.1 years, SD = 10.3) participated in a 2‐week Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)‐based Intensive PTSD Treatment Program (ITP). Resilience was assessed at the start and end of the program, while PTSD and depression were assessed every other day of the program and 3‐, 6‐, and 12‐months post‐treatment.ResultsVeterans with higher pre‐treatment resilience tended to have lower PTSD (b = −0.24, p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.04) and depression severity (b = −0.10, p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.07) throughout the ITP. Pre‐treatment resilience was not associated with differing treatment trajectories for PTSD (b = −0.01, p = 0.492) or depression (b = −0.01, p = 0.184). Improvements in resilience from the beginning to end of treatment were associated with improvements in PTSD (b = −0.24, p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.02) and depression symptoms (b = ‐0.10, p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.01). Pre‐treatment resilience was associated with PTSD severity 3‐ and 12‐months following treatment, and with depression severity 3‐ and 6‐months post‐treatment. Changes in resilience during treatment were associated with PTSD severity up to 1 year post‐treatment, and up to 3 months for depression severity.ConclusionThe findings highlight that patients may experience similarly beneficial treatment trajectories regardless of their pre‐treatment resilience. This may offer hope to patients with lower resilience that they can still achieve meaningful PTSD and depression symptom reductions.

Introduction

Veterans, a population often exposed to multiple traumatic events as part of their military training and service, are at a high risk for post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), with an estimated 9.4% being affected by this debilitating disorder in their lifetimes (Wisco et al. 2022). Veterans with PTSD are also at a heightened risk for comorbid disorders such as Major Depressive Disorder, which affects approximately 47.4% (Nichter et al. 2020). Thus, it is crucial to consider the presence of depression symptoms in addition to PTSD, as their co‐occurrence is associated with worse treatment outcomes compared to PTSD alone (Tural et al. 2012).

Resilience is defined as the ability to successfully adapt to adversity and stress (Horn and Feder 2018). However, definitions vary in the literature (Leys et al. 2020); given the complexity of this construct, it is generally accepted that resilience may have multiple meanings or presentations (Southwick et al. 2014). Some view resilience as a trait due to its associations with the Big Five personality traits (Burtaverde et al. 2021; Oshio et al. 2018), stability over time (van der Meulen et al. 2019), and potential associated genetic factors (Maul et al. 2020). Others suggest that resilience is a dynamic process or skill due to an observed degradation of resilience as trauma load increases (Fossion et al. 2013), impediments in it being associated with a dysfunctional family unit (Fossion et al. 2015), and improvements with certain therapies (Helmreich, Kunzler, et al. 2017; Kent et al. 2011; Loprinzi et al. 2011).

Resilience is associated with overall well‐being (Bag et al. 2022; Rink et al. 2022) and has been demonstrated to be a protective factor against the development of PTSD symptoms after trauma exposure (Lee et al. 2014). Importantly, an individual who may be considered “resilient” can still be diagnosed with PTSD and require treatment for it. Associations between resilience and PTSD symptomology have been widely studied among veterans. For example, Green et al. (2010) found that resilience moderated the relationship between combat exposure and PTSD symptoms, such that higher levels of resilience were protective even after experiencing a higher number of combat‐related traumatic events. Blackburn and Owens (2016) replicated findings that veterans with higher levels of resilience tended to have lower levels of PTSD symptom severity when accounting for combat exposure. Among those with a PTSD diagnosis, greater resilience was negatively associated with alcohol misuse, suicidality, depression severity, and health concerns. Overall, most trauma‐exposed US veterans (67.7%) are considered psychologically resilient, based on low self‐report PTSD and depression severity (Isaacs et al. 2017).

Some research has investigated the interactions between resilience and PTSD symptoms during treatment. For example, McGuire et al. (2018) found that veterans who received Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT) for 6 weeks experienced large increases in resilience, and that greater increases in resilience during treatment were associated with fewer PTSD symptoms by the end of treatment. Another study has shown that higher resilience at the start of treatment was associated with a greater likelihood of experiencing PTSD symptom reduction at the end of treatment (Davidson et al. 2012). More study is needed to expand upon and replicate what we know about the associations between resilience and treatment response in trauma populations. In particular, the potential impacts of resilience on treatment outcomes in shorter intensive interventions has not yet been investigated. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, no study has explored the long‐term impact of resilience on PTSD symptoms after treatment completion.

CPT is an evidence‐based treatment for PTSD focused on identifying and altering negative trauma‐related beliefs (Resick et al. 2024). CPT is commonly delivered in a once‐weekly format but can also be delivered in an intensive format, in which individuals receive at least three sessions per week (Held and Wachen 2023), Sciarrino et al. 2020). It may be used either as a standalone intervention or with adjunctive services in intensive PTSD treatment programs (ITPs; Ragsdale et al. 2020). The intensive delivery of CPT has been shown to be associated with large reductions in both PTSD and depression severity (e.g., Galovski et al. 2022; Held, Smith, et al. 2022; Held, Kovacevic, et al. 2022), and lower drop‐out rates compared to traditional weekly CPT (88.9% vs. 44.4%; Weinstein et al. 2023). Due to their brevity, intensive treatments may have the potential to quickly promote resilience in patients; to our knowledge, no studies have yet investigated whether 2‐week intensive treatment can improve resilience more than traditional once‐weekly therapy.

The present study aimed to determine whether pre‐treatment resilience and changes in resilience during a CPT‐based ITP were associated with changes in veterans’ PTSD and depression symptom severity during the program and up to a year following program completion. Understanding the role of resilience in the context of intensive treatments may help elucidate the complex role of resilience in treatment response and whether it acts more as a stable trait or learned skill. It could also answer if resilience, whether it was already present or learned during treatment, continues to influence symptom severity long‐term, and may potentially suggest it as a target for intervention. Based on prior research (Davidson et al. 2012; McGuire et al. 2018), we hypothesized that higher pre‐treatment resilience and improvements in resilience during treatment would predict greater reductions in PTSD during and following the program beyond what may be explained by improvements in depression severity. Similarly, we hypothesized that higher pre‐treatment resilience and changes in resilience would predict greater improvement in depression severity during and after the program beyond what may be explained by PTSD improvements.

Methods

Participants

A total of 727 veterans and service members (moving forward, “veterans” will also refer to service members) participated in a 2‐week‐long CPT‐based ITP at the Rush University Medical Center's Road Home Program: Center for Veterans and Their Families between July 2020 and May 2024. They were 45.1 years old on average (SD = 10.3), 52.8% identified as male, and 63.3% white (see Table 1). All study procedures have been approved by the Rush University Medical Center Institutional Review Board.

Program Description

Veterans were placed into either a combat trauma cohort or a military sexual trauma (MST) cohort based on the index trauma they decided to work on during treatment. To determine eligibility for the program, they completed clinician‐administered diagnostic interviews and self‐report measures. They were accepted if they met for a PTSD diagnosis on the Clinician‐Administered PTSD Scale for DSM‐5 (CAPS‐5; Weathers et al. 2018), but excluded if they were actively suicidal or homicidal, had high current substance dependency in which withdrawal could be dangerous, unmanaged mania or psychosis, or a serious medical issue.

The ITP took place over 2 weeks, for 10 days (see Held, Smith, et al. 2022; Held, Kovacevic, et al. 2022 for more information about the program). A total of 16 50‐min‐long CPT sessions took place. Each CPT session was followed by 60‐min to complete CPT homework assignments. In addition to CPT, veterans also received adjunctive services such as art therapy, acupuncture, and skills groups (i.e., mindfulness, sleep hygiene, cognitive health and flexibility, and emotion and communication skills). Self‐report measures were administered every other day during the program and again 3, 6, and 12 months after the program ended.

Measures

Demographics

During the intake process, veterans self‐reported the following demographic characteristics: sex, age, race, ethnicity, marital status, and education level.

Connor‐Davidson Resilience Scale‐10 (CDRISC‐10)

The CDRISC‐10 is a 10‐item self‐report measure of psychological resilience (Connor and Davidson 2003). Items are rated on a scale from 0 (Not true at all) to 4 (True nearly all the time). Total scores range from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. The CDRISC‐10 was administered at intake and Day 10. In the present sample, Cronbach's alphas for pre‐ and post‐treatment were 0.89 and 0.90, respectively.

PTSD Checklist for DSM‐5 (PCL‐5)

The PCL‐5 is a 20‐item self‐report measure of PTSD symptom severity based on DSM‐5 criteria (Weathers et al. 2013). Each item's severity was rated on a scale from 0 (Not at all) to 4 (Extremely). Total scores can range from 0 to 80, with higher scores indicating more severe PTSD symptomology. The PCL‐5 was administered at intake, Days 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, and 10 of the ITP, and at all follow‐up points. In the present sample, Cronbach's alphas ranged from 0.92 to 0.96 across timepoints.

Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9)

The PHQ‐9 is a 9‐item self‐report measure of depression symptoms based on DSM‐5 criteria (Kroenke et al. 2001). Items are rated on a scale from 0 (Not at all) to 3 (Nearly every day). Total scores can range from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe depression. The PHQ‐9 was administered at intake, Days 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, and 10 of the ITP, and at all follow‐up points. In the present sample, Cronbach's alpha ranged from 0.84 to 0.88 across timepoints.

Statistical Analysis

Gibbons' variant of Cohen's d for repeated measures was utilized to examine amount of change in resilience, depression, and PTSD severity during the program (Gibbons et al. 1993). Linear mixed effects models (LMMs) were used to examine the association between changes in resilience and PTSD and depression severity during and following the program. LMMs are particularly appropriate for longitudinal analyses due to their ability to accommodate individual differences in baseline values and change, as well as flexibility in variances and covariances over time. Models explored the main effects of baseline resilience values as well as their interactions with time to assess whether overall differences in PTSD or depression severity during and after the program existed based on pre‐treatment resilience or whether change in outcomes over time differed based on resilience, respectively. These models included quadradic and cubic time to model expected non‐linear time trends and used all program outcome timepoints. Amount of change in resilience during the program, utilized as a difference score between pre‐ and post‐program measurements, was also included to explore its association with post and follow‐up outcome scores. Models adjusted for age, sex, cohort type, race, ethnicity, marital status, and education level. Additionally, models predicting PTSD severity adjusted for depression severity, and models predicting depression severity adjusted for PTSD severity, as time‐varying covariates. Further, the strength of association between resilience and PTSD and depression outcomes was obtained using Edwards et al. (2008) variant of R 2 for fixed effects in LMMs. This partial R 2 metric examines the strength of association between predictor and multivariate repeated outcomes when adjusting for the potential contribution of other model predictors. Random intercepts, but not slopes, were included based on likelihood ratio and information criteria comparisons supporting only the former.

Finally, pre‐treatment resilience as well as amount of change in resilience during the program were examined as predictors of PTSD and depression severity at post‐program, 3‐, 6‐, and 12‐month follow‐up by including their interactions with time and examining their marginal effects at each specific timepoint using estimated simple slopes derived from the time x resilience interaction. Interactions with time were introduced in separate models due to corresponding changes in inter‐pretation of main effects. Missingness to follow‐up timepoints was also examined to assess the viability of the missing at random (MAR) assumption of this statistical modeling approach, which indicated that missingness was associated only with sex, with females being more likely to complete follow‐up surveys. As missingness at any timepoint was not associated with resilience or any clinical severity measures, or changes in them, the plausibility of MAR appears justifiable. Analyses and figures were completed using R version 4.3.2.

Results

Overall, veterans experienced a modest increase in resilience during the program (M = 2.81, d = 0.40 [95% CI: 0.33, 0.46]), as well as large improvements in both depression (M = 5.21, d = 0.92 [95% CI: 0.83, 1.00]) and PTSD severity (M = 20.72, d = 1.29 [95% CI: 1.19, 1.39]). Pre‐treatment resilience was a significant predictor of overall PTSD severity during the treatment program timepoints (p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.01), but not its interaction with time (p = 0.492), indicating that participants with higher resilience had generally lower PTSD severity scores, but that trends did not differ over time based on resilience. Improvements in resilience were also associated with lower PTSD severity (p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.02). Aside from significant linear, quadratic and cubic time trends (p's < 0.001), only age (p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.08), race (p = 0.038, R 2 = 0.01) and depression (p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.39) were significant. Being older and non‐White were associated with generally higher PTSD severity during the program, and improvements in depression predicted improvements in PTSD severity (see Table 2). Higher pre‐treatment resilience was also associated with lower PTSD severity at the completion of the program as well as at 3‐month and 12‐month follow‐up timepoints, but not at 6‐month follow‐up (see Table 3). Similarly, greater amounts of resilience improvement during the program were associated with lower PTSD severity scores at post and all follow‐up timepoints.

Discussion

Results suggest that patients with greater resilience or greater improvements in resilience during treatment generally experienced lower PTSD and depression severity during and after treatment, though the magnitudes of this effect appear to be fairly small that is, although higher pre‐treatment resilience was associated with lower PTSD and depression severity at the start and end of treatment, symptom severities reduced by similar amounts regardless of resilience. This could be attributed to patients likely obtaining resilience‐enhancing skills during their time in the ITP via CPT and adjunctive services, so whether they enter treatment with higher resilience was not associated with their symptom trajectories. These findings are encouraging for patients and clinicians. Since resilience is often seen as a strong protective factor against the development of PTSD, some may feel discouraged at what they perceive as a lack of resilience within themselves. However, our results suggest that resilience may only weakly predict one's improvement in treatment and that patients with lower resilience could still experience significant PTSD symptom reductions. Thus, neither clinicians nor patients should view low resilience as a barrier to treatment success.

The results also demonstrate that improvements in resilience were seen by the end of CPT and were associated with lower PTSD and depression symptom severity, although the effects of this are also small. This finding is consistent with what McGuire et al. (2018) found. Similarly, we believe that these findings may be attributable to the skills patients obtain during CPT and other cognitive therapies, such as challenging maladaptive beliefs, identifying their emotions when such beliefs arise, and generalizing these skills to other scenarios (Adler et al. 2015; Resick et al. 2024). As part of the ITP, veterans also participated in various adjunctive services, such as groups focusing on mindfulness, cognitive flexibility, and communication, that could have further promoted resilience (O'Connor et al. 2023). Numerous skills developed both during CPT and adjunctive services are commonly thought of as being a part of the resilience construct, such as self‐efficacy, healthy coping skills (particularly leaning on social support from other veterans and mindfulness practice), and adapting to change (Connor and Davidson 2003). It is possible that improvements in resilience may derive from an increase in cognitive flexibility as patients are taught to evaluate evidence for and against trauma‐related beliefs and consider alternative thoughts. This process may promote a more adaptive way of viewing one's trauma, which may correspond with reductions in PTSD and depression severity (McGuire et al. 2018). More research is needed to replicate this finding and clarify the mechanisms by which resilience increases during treatment. Our findings appear to support the view of resilience as a dynamic and learned set of skills, as opposed to a relatively innate trait, due to these observed improvements possibly stemming from skills taught in CPT. This is consistent with prior work, which has also demonstrated improvements in resilience following therapy (Helmreich, Kunzler, et al. 2017; Kent et al. 2011; Loprinzi et al. 2011). However, further research is needed to better elucidate whether resilience acts as more of a stable trait or a learned set of skills during PTSD treatment.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of resilience on long‐term symptom outcomes after PTSD treatment. The findings partially supported our hypothesis; we found that pre‐treatment resilience predicted PTSD and depression severity at some timepoints following treatment completion, though again with small effects. Additionally, we found that changes in resilience during the ITP were associated with PTSD severity up to a year after treatment, as well as depression severity up to 3 months after treatment. Since greater resilience is associated with lower PTSD and depression severity during treatment and is generally a protective factor against more severe symptomology (Blackburn and Owens 2016; Green et al. 2010), it would be expected that this association would continue post‐treatment. However, given the small effect sizes, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Administration of the CDRISC‐10 at the follow‐up timepoints may have been better suited for predicting other follow‐up outcomes. Additionally, other factors not accounted for by self‐report measures, such as continuation of therapy after the ITP and likelihood of continuing to exercise CPT skills, may be more robust predictors of symptom severity and may explain why significance was not found at all timepoints. These factors may be indirectly influenced by resilience, as it could be hypothesized that those with greater resilience may feel more motivated to practice therapeutic skills or pursue mental healthcare, and that improvements in resilience may correlate to a greater likelihood of engaging in these practices. Since resilience could be accounted for by this variety of factors, and the findings have small effects, this may also explain why significance was not reached at all follow‐up timepoints. Given that this study is the first to examine the impact of resilience on long‐term treatment outcomes, future research should replicate and expand on these findings.

Several limitations are worth noting. First, all measures were self‐reported; although these measures are clinically valid and relevant (Blevins et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2022; Yan et al. 2023), patients may have been influenced by external factors, such as survey burden or social‐desirability bias, that could potentially have had a minor impact on results. As mentioned earlier, the administration of the CDRISC‐10 carries with it specific limitations, as it was only measured at the start and end of treatment. For one, veterans participating in the ITP spent nearly all day for 2 weeks in intensive therapy and were housed near the treatment center. Although CPT may be emotionally taxing for some individuals, it could be argued that since veterans were away from potential stressors at home and working on personal symptom improvement, they had little to no opportunity to exercise some of the skills the CDRISC‐10 assesses (i.e., seeing the humorous side of problems and bouncing back after hardship). Moreover, the CDRISC‐10 was not measured at any of the follow‐up timepoints, so it is unable to be determined how resilient veterans were months after treatment and if changes in resilience during treatment persisted long‐term. We also could not account for the potential influence of follow‐up resilience, more specific indicators of resilience (i.e., gratitude, help‐seeking, social support), or systemic barriers (i.e., financial restraints or limited access to mental healthcare) on other follow‐up measures. Future research should explore alternative ways of measuring resilience and whether measuring specific traits would adequately assess resilience, as well as if gains in resilience during treatment were sustained long‐term. Another limitation is that it could be inferred that because all study participants were in a treatment program for PTSD, there may inherently be a higher level of pre‐treatment resilience among this sample compared to veterans with PTSD who are not seeking treatment. Further, difference scores are an imperfect metric for denoting change in CD‐Risc. Additionally, other clinical variables, such as prior suicide attempt, prior hospitalization, and medication history, were not accounted for in the analysis, potentially limiting generalizability for those with more severe clinical presentations. Generalizability may also be limited for other trauma types besides combat trauma and MST. Finally, not all participants completed outcome measures at follow‐up timepoints, and although missingness in these follow‐up measures was not associated with severity at end of program, the potential for missingness not being at random cannot be dismissed.

This study expanded our understanding of the associations between resilience and treatment for PTSD and provided novel evidence of the possible impact of resilience on long‐term treatment outcomes. Overall, these results are encouraging for both patients seeking PTSD treatment and clinicians providing it. Not only did patients experience significant PTSD symptom improvement regardless of baseline resilience, they also appeared to experience gains in resilience. This finding provides hope that CPT may foster beneficial resilience‐enhancing skills, even if it is not explicitly designed to do so; however, more research is needed to ascertain this. In addition to replication, future research should focus on improving resilience‐enhancing programs targeted at service members (McInerney et al. 2022) so that they can better maintain their well‐being after trauma exposure, determining what specific aspects of CPT seem to promote resilience, and if gains in resilience during treatment were maintained long‐term.
