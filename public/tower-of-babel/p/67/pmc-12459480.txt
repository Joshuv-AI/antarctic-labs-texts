Efficacy of low-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation in the treatment of sleep disorders in the early stage of Parkinson disease: A single-center double-blind controlled study
Authors: Zhaohai Feng, Meiying Li, Yingwei Zheng, Wenjing Ping, Zeng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of low-frequency repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) for improving sleep disturbances in patients with early-stage Parkinson disease (PD). Eighty patients with PD (Hoehn-Yahr grades 1–3) with sleep disorders were randomized into a treatment group (n = 40) that received 1 Hz rTMS over the right DLPFC (90% motor threshold, 20 minutes/day for 10 days) and a control group (n = 40) that received sham stimulation. Outcomes including Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, polysomnography parameters, Hamilton Depression, and Hamilton Anxiety scores were assessed at baseline and 1 month after treatment. Compared to baseline, the treatment group exhibited significant improvements in the Hamilton Depression, Hamilton Anxiety, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale scores (all P < .001). Polysomnography data revealed prolonged total sleep time (TST, P < .001), reduced sleep latency (SL) (P < .001), fewer nighttime awakenings (P < .001), enhanced sleep efficiency (P < .001), and shortened rapid eye movement SL. In contrast, the control group showed no significant changes, except for a worsened TST (P < .001). Between-group comparisons demonstrated superior outcomes in the treatment group for all sleep and mood metrics (P < .001). All participants completed the study, and no severe adverse effects were observed. Low-frequency rTMS stimulation of the unilateral DLPFC region can improve sleep disorders in early PD patients in the short term, and the improvement of sleep quality may be manifested in the extension of TST, improvement of sleep efficiency, shortening of SL, and reduction of the number of nighttime awakenings. The improvement of sleep disorders may be related to the improvement of anxiety and depression symptoms in patients, which persist for at least 1 month, supporting rTMS as a safe adjunct therapy for PD-related sleep disorders.

1. Introduction

Parkinson disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease worldwide.[1] The clinical symptoms primarily include motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, postural abnormalities, and gait disorders, as well as non-motor symptoms such as depression, cognitive impairment, and sleep disorders.[2] Emerging research highlights a significant interplay between circadian rhythm disturbances, sleep cycle disruptions, and neurodegenerative disorders. The complex bidirectional relationship between these factors manifests across multiple physiological levels, with mounting evidence suggesting that circadian dysregulation and sleep abnormalities not only accompany but may also contribute to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative conditions. Notably, chronic disruptions in circadian timing systems and persistent sleep disorders have been identified as potential risk factors for the development and progression of various neurodegenerative diseases.[3] Sleep disorders are common in patients with PD and have a serious negative impact on quality of life.[4] The main treatment for sleep disorders in PD is adjustment of anti-PD drugs, followed by the application of sleep aid drugs, such as benzodiazepines.[5,6] While pharmacological interventions (e.g., benzodiazepines) offer transient relief, their long-term use is limited by tolerance and dependency.[7] Therefore, sleep disorders in patients need other novel, non-pharmacological treatments. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), a noninvasive neuromodulatory technique, is a novel noninvasive brain stimulation therapy. rTMS can change the sleep structure of healthy subjects and has a good therapeutic effect on primary sleep disorders, demonstrating superior efficacy in enhancing stage III sleep and rapid eye movement sleep cycles relative to the control groups.[8] Based on pharmacological therapy, combined rTMS therapy improves not only motor symptoms in patients with PD but also non-motor symptoms such as sleep disorders.[9] While clinical implementation requires further high-level evidence verification, accumulating research has identified multiple cortical targets associated with symptom amelioration.[10,11] The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is a key region in the prefrontal cortex, involved in executive functions, working memory, and decision-making.[12] In PD, degeneration of dopaminergic pathways affects the DLPFC, leading to cognitive and mood symptoms.[13] The right DLPFC has been strategically targeted using low-frequency rTMS protocols to address PD-related depression based on the neurophysiological rationale of correcting hemispheric imbalance characterized by right DLPFC hyperactivation and left DLPFC hypoactivation.[14] This lateralized approach capitalizes on the DLPFC’s established role in emotional regulation and mood disorder pathophysiology. Beyond affective symptoms, emerging evidence implicates the right DLPFC in mediating executive functioning deficits commonly observed in PD populations. These cognitive impairments frequently manifest as deteriorations in working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control domains, which may indirectly influence motor performance through impaired movement planning and execution mechanisms.[15] Furthermore, preliminary investigations suggest that low-frequency DLPFC stimulation could ameliorate sleep architecture disturbances, potentially through the modulation of cortico-thalamic circuits involved in sleep–wake regulation.[8] It has shown promise for modulating sleep architecture.[16] Previous studies have suggested that low-frequency rTMS (≤1 Hz) over the DLPFC enhances GABA activity and normalizes sleep–wake cycles.[17] However, the evidence for its efficacy in PD-associated sleep disorders remains unclear. A clinical study on the specific efficacy of rTMS at the parietal lobe in the treatment of PD patients with sleep disorders.[18] Although the therapeutic efficacy of DLPFC-targeted rTMS for core motor symptoms remains controversial, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have reported heterogeneous outcomes across bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor subdomains.[19] This variability may stem from methodological differences in stimulation parameters, treatment duration, and outcome measurement selection. Current research limitations underscore the need for standardized protocols and multimodal assessment frameworks to elucidate DLPFC stimulation’s comprehensive therapeutic profile in this neurologically heterogeneous population. Moreover, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the intervention’s capacity to address concurrent non-motor manifestations, particularly the complex interplay between psychological disorders and sleep dysfunction in PD progression. Few RCTs have investigated the therapeutic potential of unilateral DLPFC-targeted rTMS for improving sleep quality and psychological symptoms in patients with PD. Therefore, the aim of this study aimed to explore the therapeutic effect and safety of low-frequency rTMS applied to DLPFC in the treatment of sleep disorders in PD.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and participants

A single-center double-blind (both participants and outcome assessors) controlled study was conducted in Maanshan People’s Hospital. For neurostimulation interventions like rTMS, a standardized effect size of 0.6 to 0.8 on sleep scales (e.g., Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale [PDSS]) is typical. With α = 0.05 and power = 80%, 40 participants per group suffice.[20] Hence, 80 PD patients with sleep disorders (mean age: 68.6 ± 8.1 years, including 44 males and 36 females) were enrolled from the neurology Department of Maanshan People’s Hospital from March 2023 to April 2024. Participants were randomized 1:1 to the active or sham rTMS groups using a computer-generated sequence. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the study intervention. Participants were met the following inclusion criteria: all patients with primary PD met the 2015 movement disorder society diagnostic criteria for clinically established PD[21]; all patients can cooperate with the completion of sleep related assessment scale score, all patients can complete polysomnography (PSG) monitoring and can cooperate with the interviewer; all patients meet the diagnostic criteria for sleep disorders; family members or themselves agree to participate in the study, and sign the study informed consent. The exclusion criteria included individuals with PD who have dementia that preclude valid scale assessment or psychiatric disorders requiring pharmacological intervention, contraindications for transcranial magnetic stimulation including intracranial metallic implants or magnetic-sensitive medical devices, poorly controlled hypertension (SBP > 180 mm Hg or DBP > 100 mm Hg) despite antihypertensive therapy, severe dysfunction in major organ systems (cardiac, hepatic, renal, or pulmonary), history of seizure disorders or epileptic episodes; Advanced disease progression classified as stages 4 to 5 on the Hoehn and Yahr scale, and other forms of Parkinsonism, including multisystem atrophy, supranuclear palsy, and encephalitic Parkinsonism. Treatment was maintained throughout the trial.The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Maanshan People’s Hospital (Medical Ethics Review of Maanshan People’s Hospital [2023] No. 03-02).

All participating patients were asked not to use hypnotic drugs or receive other treatments related to insomnia 2 weeks before starting the sessions. All repetitive rTMS sessions were performed during the daytime while the participants were awake. Each patient received dopaminergic and anticholinergic drugs, including carbidopa/levodopa (Sinemet) 250/25, half a tablet 3 or 4 times daily, amantadine hydrochloride (an N-methyl-D-aspartic acid receptor inhibitor) twice daily, and an anticholinergic (Cogentin) twice daily.

2.2. Methods

2.2.1. Intervention protocol

rTMS was administered using a therapeutic device, Magneuro100 (Nanjing Weisi Medical Technology Co., Ltd, China). The right DLPFC (corresponding to the F4 region per the international 10–20 electroencephalography system), was targeted. The parameters included a frequency of 1 Hz, stimulation intensity at 90% of the resting motor threshold, and daily 20-minute sessions for 10 consecutive days.[22] The interventions were conducted in a dedicated sound-attenuated treatment room to minimize confounding factors. The control group received sham stimulation (daily 20-minute sessions for 10 consecutive days). All participants maintained standardized anti-Parkinsonian pharmacotherapy (individually optimized regimens) and abstained from psychoactive medications (e.g., hypnotics, antipsychotics) throughout the study and 30-day follow-up period.

2.2.2. Outcome assessments

Sleep-related metrics were evaluated using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), PDSS, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD), and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA). Assessments were performed at baseline (24 hours pre-intervention) and 30 days posttreatment. The thresholds for psychological symptom classification were as follows: HAMD score ≤ 8 (no depression) and HAMA score ≤ 7 (no anxiety), with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity.

2.2.3. Secondary measures

Motor and functional outcomes were assessed using the unified Parkinson’s disease rating scale sections II/III (UPDRS-II/III) and Hoehn and Yahr staging at baseline.

2.2.4. Polysomnography

Nocturnal PSG (Compumedics Grael Series, Australia) was performed pre-intervention and 30 days posttreatment, with ≥6 hours of continuous recording. The parameters included electroencephalography, electrooculography, electromyography, respiratory effort, pulse oximetry, limb movement, and synchronized video monitoring. Sleep architecture analysis adhered to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine Manual[23] and the Chinese expert consensus guidelines.[24] Exclusion criteria included inadequate recording quality or a sleep duration of <4 hours.

2.2.5. Sleep disorder diagnosis

PD-related sleep dysfunction was defined by: PSQI-adjusted score (excluding hypnotic use) > 7; PDSS total score ≤ 90; objective PSG abnormalities: sleep latency (SL) > 30 min, apnea-hypopnea index > 5 events/hours (obstructive sleep apnea), or Periodic Limb Movement Index ≥ 5 events/hours.[25]

2.2.6. Safety monitoring

Contraindications and informed consent were obtained before enrollment. Adverse events (e.g., headache, dizziness, and seizures) were systematically documented. Treatment discontinuation occurred due to intolerable side effects.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk). Normally distributed continuous variables (Shapiro–Wilk test, P > .05) are expressed as mean ± SD and compared using independent/paired t tests for between-and within-group analyses. Non-normal data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney/Wilcoxon test. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies (%) and assessed using the χ² or Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

3.3. Posttreatment psychological and sleep assessments

As presented in Table 2,at the 1-month follow-up, the treatment group demonstrated superior outcomes compared to the controls, with significantly lower scores in HAMD, HAMA, and PSQI (P < .001), and higher PDSS scores (P < .001). Significant improvements in sleep architecture were observed in the treatment group, including increased TST and SE, along with reduced NA, SL, and rapid eye movement latency (all P < .001).

3.4. Longitudinal analysis of PSG parameters

Within-group comparisons in Table 2 revealed significant improvements in the treatment group, with increased TST and SE, and decreased SL and NA compared to baseline (all P < .001). The rapid eye movement latency and sleep stage proportions remained unchanged (all P > .05). Conversely, the control group exhibited a significant TST reduction (P < .001), while other PSG parameters remained stable (all P > .05).

3.5. Comparative treatment outcomes

As presented in Table 3, before treatment, no significant differences were observed between groups in the HAMD, PSQI, and PDSS assessments (all P > .05). However, despite similar scores on these assessments, the treatment group demonstrated significantly higher HAMA scores at baseline than the control group (P < .001). Posttreatment, the treatment group showed significant improvement across multiple parameters at the 1-month follow-up, including reduced HAMD, HAMA, and PSQI scores and increased PDSS scores (all P < .001). The control group exhibited worsened HAMD scores (P < .001), whereas other parameters showed no significant changes (all P > .05).

4. Discussion

The symptoms of PD include a spectrum of motor and non-motor manifestations, such as depression, sleep disorders, and constipation, especially with a high incidence of sleep disorders, with sleep–wake dysregulation emerging as a particularly debilitating comorbidity affecting 60% to 98% of patients.[4] While cardinal motor symptoms such as resting tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity, and postural instability are routinely assessed using the UPDRS Parts II-III, non-motor sequelae, including depression, sleep fragmentation, and autonomic dysfunction, frequently impose greater quality-of-life impairments.[26] Current therapeutic algorithms prioritize dopaminergic medication optimization as first-line management for PD-associated sleep disorders, although residual symptoms often persist despite pharmacotherapy. Many patients with sleep disorders in PD require the adjunctive use of sleep-inducing drugs such as benzodiazepines, but the therapeutic effect remains unsatisfactory.[4] This therapeutic gap has prompted the exploration of neuromodulatory strategies, particularly rTMS, given its noninvasive nature, favorable safety profile, and capacity to induce neuroplasticity through cortical excitability modulation.[27]

Emerging evidence suggests that rTMS exerts chronobiotic effects by targeting key nodes in sleep regulatory networks in primary sleep disorders.[28] In healthy populations, low-frequency (≤1 Hz) bilateral stimulation of the DLPFC enhances slow-wave sleep architecture via GABAergic potentiation and BDNF-mediated synaptic remodeling.[7] Translational studies in PD cohorts demonstrated divergent outcomes, with early trials failing to demonstrate significant sleep improvements,[14] while more recent investigations reported clinically meaningful benefits. A randomized clinical trial demonstrated that 1-Hz repetitive rTMS targeting the right DLPFC augmented the therapeutic effects of Tai Chi Chuan in elderly patients with sleep disorders and mild cognitive impairment, showing significant improvements in sleep quality and cognitive performance.[29] These benefits potentially correlate with neuroplasticity modifications, suggesting that nonpharmacological approaches may offer dual therapeutic advantages for sleep rehabilitation and mild cognitive impairment progression delay, in PD research, low-frequency rTMS targeting the right DLPFC significantly reduced Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) scores at 10-day and 1-month post-interval compared to sham stimulation (P < .05). Therapeutic responders exhibited longer disease durations and lower baseline levodopa equivalent doses than nonresponders.[30] A randomized double-blind controlled trial revealed sustained clinical improvements and sleep architecture modulation (particularly rapid eye movement sleep enhancement) persisting 3 months post-rTMS intervention in PD patients.[31] Mechanistically, these findings collectively suggest that DLPFC-targeted rTMS may normalize cortical excitability patterns and restore sleep-wake regulatory networks, with therapeutic efficacy potentially influenced by disease chronicity and dopaminergic medication status.[32] This neuromodulatory approach shows promise for the management of excessive daytime sleepiness and sleep-related comorbidities in neurodegenerative disorders.

Our findings align with this trajectory, demonstrating statistically significant improvements in both subjective (PSQI, Parkinson’s Disease Sleep Scale) and objective (PSG) sleep metrics following right-sided DLPFC stimulation at 1 Hz. Specifically, treatment responders exhibited reduced SL (P < .01), decreased nocturnal awakenings (P < .05), and enhanced SE (P < .001), Consistent ting with the mentioned research findings, it is demonstrated that unilateral low-frequency rTMS stimulation of the DLPFC can ameliorate sleep disturbances in PD.

The neurophysiological substrates underlying these improvements are likely to involve multisystem modulation. DLPFC stimulation appears to normalize thalamocortical dysrhythmias, which are characteristic of PD-related sleep fragmentation.[33] Some scholars hold that rTMS treatment for sleep disorders in patients with PD may be achieved by stimulating or inhibiting neural activities in different brain regions to alter cortical excitability, thereby improving subjective sleep quality and insomnia symptoms,[34] while concurrently addressing comorbid affective disturbances. Previous studies have shown that high-frequency rTMS over the DLPFC has an ameliorative effect on both depression and depression comorbid with PD.[22,35,36] A meta-analysis demonstrated that rTMS significantly improves sleep quality in patients with depression, including those with mild to severe depression. Furthermore, the analysis revealed that groups utilizing a stimulation frequency of 1 Hz, those undergoing treatment for more than 4 weeks, and those receiving rTMS exclusively exhibited superior efficacy in enhancing sleep quality among patients with major depressive disorder when the rTMS stimulation target was the DLPFC, whether on the left or right side.[37] Our cohort demonstrated correlated reductions in HAMA and HAMD scores, supporting the hypothesis that rTMS exerts indirect sleep benefits via mood stabilization, which may restore fronto-striatal connectivity through long-term depression-like plasticity, thereby attenuating excessive glutamatergic tone in cortico-basal ganglia circuits.[38] Furthermore, animal models have suggested that rTMS-induced upregulation of ventrolateral preoptic nucleus activity can stabilize sleep-wake transitions disrupted by progressive dopaminergic degeneration.[39]

Notwithstanding these promising results, the methodological constraints warrant cautious interpretation. Our single-center trial employed a modest sample size, which is potentially underpowered to detect subtle neurophysiological effects. In previous studies, the sleep conditions of patients were mainly determined by a scale. Such self-reported results may have increased the risk of recall bias. In this study, PSG monitoring was performed to verify the objectivity and effectiveness of treatment. It is worth noting that the follow-up period of this study was relatively short (30 days), and we were unable to assess the benefits that lasted for >3 months as observed in previous studies on the treatment of refractory insomnia.[40] Critical questions persist regarding optimal stimulation parameters, such as current protocols that exhibit marked heterogeneity in frequency, laterality (unilateral vs bilateral), and treatment duration across trials.

Future investigations should prioritize multicenter RCTs incorporating multimodal biomarkers (quantitative EEG and serum BDNF levels) to elucidate dose-response relationships and neuroplastic adaptation timelines. Standardization of stimulation coordinates using neuro-navigation systems would enhance spatial precision, while extended follow-up periods (≥ 6 months) could clarify the treatment durability. Exploratory analyses stratifying patients by PD subtype (tremor-dominant vs postural instability/gait difficulty) may identify phenotype-specific responsiveness, given the emerging evidence of differential sleep architecture alterations across motor subgroups.

In conclusion, our findings corroborate the growing evidence that low-frequency rTMS targeting the right DLPFC constitutes a safe and efficacious adjunct therapy for PD-associated sleep dysregulation. The observed polysomnographic improvements, particularly in sleep continuity metrics, complement subjective sleep quality enhancements, suggesting a clinically meaningful restoration of circadian integrity. While neurobiological mechanisms require further elucidation, the dual benefits in the affective and sleep domains position rTMS as a promising multimodal intervention in PD management. Addressing the current limitations through rigorous translational research will be essential to optimize therapeutic protocols and establish evidence-based guidelines for neuromodulation in neurodegenerative sleep medicine.
