Strengths and weaknesses in techniques employed to measure the effects of dietary modulation on intestinal microbiota composition and function
Authors: Juha Apajalahti, Teemu Rinttilä
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this paper, intestinal microbial habitats in monogastric animals are discussed, with special reference to the intestinal segments of poultry. Physicochemical conditions in different segments vary and determine the selection of microbes that have nutritional and thermodynamic possibilities to thrive in the habitat. Understanding basic microbial ecology is a prerequisite for the knowledge-based development of microbiota modulating feed additives and ingredients. A prevalent research approach involves analysing intestinal parameters and calculating correlations between analytical data, animal health, and performance. However, this method cannot establish causal relationships or capture the dynamics of microbial metabolite production and their uptake by the intestinal brush border. An alternative or complementary approach is to use an ex vivo method, where the fresh, fully functional, authentic microbial community of the target intestinal segment is studied in the laboratory. For this approach to produce unbiased data, extremely careful handling of the oxygen-sensitive bacteria and precise replication of the physicochemical conditions of the target intestinal environment are critical. With such an ex vivo model, the effect of feed additives on the rate of metabolite production by the native microbiota can be accurately evaluated. All research approaches have their pitfalls, the details of which are discussed. Small methodological details can have a major impact on the reliability of the results. The most reliable approaches are unfortunately labour-intensive, and a successful outcome requires the use of multiple approaches in parallel. Although no research approach is perfect, combining multiple methods allows a comprehensive understanding to emerge gradually.

Introduction

The gastrointestinal tract is a highly complex and dynamic environment. It is like a multi-compartment fed-batch fermenter, where complex substrate (feed) is being fed at varying intervals and the final outputs are animal biomass, microbial biomass and feed material that cannot be further broken down and utilised in the gastrointestinal tract of a monogastric animal. The high complexity comes not only from the microbial community with numerous microbe-to-microbe interactions, but also from the highly complex functions of the intestinal tissue. One can envision growing bacteria in a fermentation vessel that is itself metabolically active and continuously removes selected chemical compounds from and secretes others to the fermentation medium. The host animal side provides the microbiota with a habitat of constant temperature and continuous substrate flow, and the microbial part provides the host with nutritious and energy-rich metabolites, helps in the digestion of resistant feed components, and protects from harmful microbial invaders. Intestinal microbiota and the host animal live in a mutualistic relationship and the entity is collectively referred to as a superorganism (Dethlefsen et al., 2007; Lederberg, 2000).

Many applications such as human and animal medicine and nutrition would greatly benefit from being able to understand and control intestinal microbiota and its interactions with the host animal. However, despite the sophisticated analytical tools and models developed over the decades, scientific understanding is still limited. Increasing our knowledge would benefit from the ability to measure metabolites, bacteria and environmental conditions at real time directly from different segments of the gastrointestinal tract, which for obvious reasons is difficult without the use of invasive methods. Consequently, current knowledge regarding the complexity of the intestinal tract and its microbiota in different intestinal segments is more advanced in animals than in humans. However, observations with animal subjects can and are being used to predict the analogous phenomena in human subjects.

In this paper, poultry is used as an example of a monogastric animal. However, the same principles can be applied to other species of monogastric animals. This paper will discuss the basic knowledge of the intestinal microbiota and the habitats that they live in. Furthermore, it focuses on the methods used for studying the effects of feed additives and comparison of research approaches such as correlation studies and the ex vivo laboratory models. With ex vivo tools it is possible to reduce the number of animal trials needed and gain more knowledge, and in some cases information that would not be possible to measure in vivo. Each approach has its pitfalls and technical details need to be considered critically when it is used in scientific experiments.

Characteristic features of different intestinal habitats

Physicochemical conditions

Diversity in the microbial kingdom (Monera) is vast. It has been speculated that for all materials produced by nature there is a microbe or microbial community that can degrade them. Depending on the chemical nature of a compound its microbial degradation may require specific physicochemical conditions. Some compounds can be degraded only through oxidative mechanisms and in the presence of molecular oxygen while some require initial reduction steps to be metabolised. Therefore, some microbes have adapted to living in aerobic habitats while some require absolute anaerobiosis. Many highly specialised microbes cannot live and maintain vital functions if the conditions are not suitable (Gottschalk, 1986).

All living organisms need energy for their anabolism (building complex molecules) and for maintaining vital functions. Production of energy is called catabolism, and its main principle is that an electron from a degradable compound (substrate) is transferred to another compound referred to as an electron acceptor. The energy released in this reaction is commonly bound to ATP and used in many cell functions that require energy. All aerobic bacteria and higher animals use molecular oxygen (O2) as an ultimate electron acceptor and the final product of O2 reduction is H2O. When O2 is not present in a habitat, the obligately aerobic bacteria are replaced by bacteria that are capable of utilising electron acceptors other than O2 (Gottschalk, 1986). The further one moves in the gastrointestinal tract, the narrower the spectrum of potential electron acceptors gets and the more specialised the bacteria must be in their ATP production. Compounds are increasingly reduced (packed with electrons) and less electron acceptors are available. Indeed, in an anaerobic habitat it is both the lack of organic substrates and the lack of electron acceptors that limit bacterial growth. The distal parts of the intestinal tract (caecum and colon) are dominated by specialised strictly anaerobic bacteria that do not tolerate O2. An example of an ultimate electron acceptor is CO2 that can be reduced to methane by methanogenic archaea. The potential of the residual compounds to donate or accept electrons is characterised by their oxidation-reduction potential. In the presence of O2, redox potential is always high. In the intestinal lumen there is little free O2 and therefore there are no actively growing bacteria that would strictly depend on its presence.

A clear drop in redox potential can be measured when moving from the upper to lower intestine. Thermodynamics may seem as a remote topic when discussing microbiota, but it is important to understand that bacteria can produce energy and grow only when the redox potential is at an acceptable range for that specific bacterium. To understand the wide range of redox potentials in different habitats, it is worth mentioning that in aerobic environments outside the gastrointestinal tract, e.g. surface soil, redox potential can be hundreds of millivolts above zero. In contrast, redox potential in the caecum or colon is lower than −300 mV. In the distal small intestine redox potential ranges from −150 to −200 mV (Apajalahti et al. unpublished). Interestingly, redox potential is not commonly measured in intestinal microbiota studies, although redox electrodes are simple and easy to use. The importance of redox potential in survival and composition of bacteria in different intestinal segments is critical. Many bacteria that grow in the caecum or colon could not grow in small intestine due to its too high redox potential. If the intention is to grow or run experiments with specific intestinal bacteria or bacterial communities under laboratory conditions this must be considered, and physicochemical conditions must be adjusted accordingly. Even when failing to adjust the redox potential appropriately there will still be growth from a cultivated caecal digesta inoculum but the spectrum of microbes growing will differ substantially from that present in the authentic habitat, removing the value of any results obtained.

Segment-specific bacteria and metabolism

In poultry, the crop is the first intestinal segment with high fermentation intensity. A mixture of feed and water entering the crop provides bacteria with a highly nutritious environment. Only a fraction of feed enters the crop, the exact proportion being dependent on the age, feeding phases (intermittent or ad libitum), light cycles etc (Classen et al., 2016). However, the part entering the crop is subjected to highly intense bacterial fermentation. The crop is dominated by lactic acid producing bacteria such as Lactobacillus crispatus, Limosilactobacillus reuteri (previously Lactobacillus reuteri), Ligilactobacillus salivarius (previously Lactobacillus salivarius) and other species earlier assigned to the genera Lactobacillus, Enterococcus, and Streptococcus (Abbas Hilmi et al., 2007). A common feature of all the mentioned lactic acid bacteria is that their growth is possible only in a habitat that can provide all the required vitamins, amino acids, and simple carbohydrates (Morishita et al., 1981).

Feed and digesta from the crop are then exposed to digestive fluids of the host with the pH first dropping to 1 to 2 in the proventriculus, then raising back to 6 to 7 in the duodenum. The low pH together with antibacterial digestive fluids such as refluxed bile acids damage bacteria severely and active bacterial growth is restored only towards the distal small intestine (ileum). For the above-mentioned reasons the density of active bacteria is low in the proximal small intestine (jejunum) where the high concentration of amino acids and carbohydrates in the feed digest would otherwise provide an ideal growth medium for lactic acid bacteria (Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016). Without the factors inhibiting bacterial growth in this segment the host brush border would undoubtedly capture a much smaller proportion of dietary nutrients. The more proximally the nutrients are taken up from the small intestine by the host the less will be consumed by the active bacteria competing for the same substrates lower down. The small intestine is dominated by the same lactic acid bacteria that dominate the crop (Rinttilä and Apajalahti, 2013). Consequently, lactic acid is the main metabolic product, followed by small amount of acetic acid, the amount of which depends on the proportion of heterofermentative species among the lactic acid bacteria. Lactic acid is produced in two stereoisomer forms, L- and D-lactic acid, depending on the specificity of the lactate dehydrogenase enzyme the bacteria carry. Some lactic acid bacteria produce only one of the isomers while some produce a racemic mixture of the two (Hammes and Vogel, 1995). The reason why this may be important is the fact that L-lactic acid is effectively absorbed from the intestine of broiler chickens but D-lactic acid only poorly. This has been shown by feeding a bolus with either pure L- or D-lactate to 5-week-old birds and measuring lactate from blood during the next 5 h after lactic acid introduction (Apajalahti et al., unpublished). Lactic acid that is absorbed has a high energy value for the host, only slightly lower than the value of glucose, thus production of D-lactate represents a “lost opportunity” for the host. The effect of the stereoisomers produced, the presence and activity of racemase enzymes that can shift the stereoisomers to each other, and the effect on fermentation in the distal intestine have been scarcely studied. It is noteworthy that the longer volatile fatty acids (ie greater than 2 carbons in length, e.g. propionate, butyrate and valerate) are not produced by bacteria in the crop and small intestine of healthy animals (Apajalahti and Rinttilä, 2019; González-Ortiz et al., 2019).

When moving from the small intestine to the caecum or colon, the change of habitat is drastic. The profile of bacterial species, the metabolites produced, and the physicochemical conditions change dramatically, and the change is not gradual but abrupt (Table 1). The habitat in caecum is highly reduced, providing good conditions for the growth of strictly anaerobic bacteria. The community is typically highly complex with many uncharacterised genera/species; e.g. of the families Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae. From an animal productivity point of view, the most significant change is that the role of microbes is no longer energy/nutrient consuming but instead energy producing. In the upper intestine (crop and small intestine) lactic acid bacteria are competing with the host animal for simple carbohydrates, amino acids, and vitamins. In the lower intestine (caecum and colon) only the bacteria (or community) that utilise complex carbohydrates and can synthesise the required amino acids and vitamins themselves can survive in the niche. In the lower intestine the role of a community is pronounced, and the functions of the individual species are less important. Mutualistic relationships, complex feeding webs and dependence on each other prevail. These interdependencies explain why it is very difficult to grow these bacteria as single species in the laboratory. As a community, no single species needs to be able to maintain all functions vital for the growth; individual bacteria can specialise on certain functions and with them support the entire community. Mechanisms by which microbiota modulators can affect animal production

Products for the control of harmful microbes

When discussing the intestinal microbiota and its role in animal performance, the simplest and best understood effect is that of a single intestinal pathogen. Indeed, professionals with any scientific background understand that pathogens such as enteropathogenic Escherichia coli or Clostridium perfringens are harmful and can cause anything from mild to lethal disorders in the host's gastrointestinal tract. The truth is somewhat more complicated since E. coli is part of the commensal microbiota in the intestine of the monogastric animals and C. perfringens can be present even at high numbers without causing necrotic enteritis or even mild symptoms. In addition to these pathogens causing diseases in the host animal itself, there is also concern about some foodborne pathogens that may be harmless for the host but pathogenic for humans when entering through the food chain. Pathogenic serovars of Salmonella enterica and Campylobacter jejuni are examples of pathogens that poultry can carry without any noticeable symptoms but can cause serious disease outbreaks for consumers. It is worth noting that all the well-known pathogens are easy to grow as pure cultures in the routine laboratory, and that may be one reason why they are so well known. It raises the question of whether there are hidden pathogens that are unknown due to their unculturability under laboratory conditions.

For the products of feed additive companies, the known pathogens are obvious and easy targets. Since non-therapeutic antibiotics cannot be routinely used in an increasing number of countries, companies are trying to develop alternative means to reduce the numbers of known pathogens. The simplest testing procedures are in vitro studies where the pathogen of interest is grown in the laboratory in the presence of an increasing concentration of a potential product. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC value) obtained is then used as a measure of product effectiveness against the tested pathogen. Naturally, a low MIC value would be good, but equally relevant is the MIC value for the beneficial or commensal microbes. Intestinal disorders can be caused if commensal bacteria are inhibited more effectively than the target pathogen. For example, many common antibiotics such as bacitracin methylene disalicylate (BMD) and penicillin are more effective against the Gram-positive commensal bacteria (e.g. most lactic acid bacteria) than the Gram-negative pathogens (e.g. E. coli and C. jejuni) (Apajalahti and Kettunen, 2006; Proctor and Phillips, 2019). In the intestinal microbial community, there is a constant competition between the bacterial species fighting for a specific niche. Eventually, the least fit member of a community (lowest MIC value) will lose the competition and becomes washed out.

An intestinal bacterium growing freely in the intestinal lumen must have a growth rate that exceeds the rate of digesta passage. Even a small negative effect of a feed additive on the growth rate may be crucial, leading to a reduction in the growth rate to a level below that required to compensate for digesta passage rate and thus there is ultimate washout of a bacterium. This applies especially to the linear part of intestine. In appendices such as caeca the situation is somewhat more complex because the peristaltic emptying movement never empties the lobe completely and bacterial growth rate is not constant but varies depending on the phase of the semi-continuous fed-batch cycle.

Slowly growing bacteria can resist washout from the intestinal habitat by adhering to the intestinal epithelium. Gram-negative enteric pathogens such as specific strains of S. enterica and E. coli are efficient in adhering to the intestinal epithelium. The initial adherence is on the mucus coating, followed by penetration into epithelial cells. For the adherence, the bacterial surface has adhesins that can specifically bind to receptors in the host mucin or the surface of epithelial cells (Torres et al., 2005). If this interaction can be disturbed, it may be possible to inhibit the colonisation of a pathogen. A product that masked either the adhesin on the surface of a bacterium or the receptor on the surface of the host cell, would inhibit the binding and in the best-case lead to washout of a pathogen. Feed additives tested for the purpose include yeast hydrolysates, oligosaccharides and live-fed bacteria (probiotics).

The efficiency of potential products can be tested by using a simple mucus adherence model where the wells of microtiter plates are coated with intestinal mucus recovered from the target intestinal segment of a target animal (Apajalahti et al., 2019). This is one of the possible mechanisms of the competitive exclusion principle where the introduced bacterium competes with a pathogen for binding sites on the intestinal epithelium. When the bifidobacteria in this example were introduced to the mucus-coated wells before the pathogen, the adherence was numerically inhibited more effectively suggesting that the bifidobacteria had more time to mask the receptors on the mucus layer. Microbiota modulators that improve feed conversion efficiency

For decades antimicrobial compounds were routinely used in the diets of production animals. The rationale for using nontherapeutic antibiotics (antimicrobial growth promoters [AGP]) was the improved animal health and performance. Although the use of therapeutic antibiotics common in human medicine was avoided in animal feeds, authorities became increasingly concerned about the spread of antibiotic resistance genes from the intestinal microbiota to the environment and eventually to human consumers. Bacteria were found to carry cassettes of resistance genes and the development of resistance to one concomitantly brought resistance to multiple antibiotics (McMillan et al., 2019). In many but not all countries, nontherapeutic antibiotics have now been banned. However, in some countries the ban led to an increase in the use of prescribed antibiotics.

The mechanisms of action of AGP have been speculated upon for a long time. In farms with compromised management and hygiene it is possible that AGP are a means to control potential pathogens and prevent outbreaks leading to impaired feed conversion ratio (FCR) and animal health. However, AGP also appeared to improve performance in well-managed production units. The objective was to understand the mechanisms, and therefore a feeding trial was conducted with broiler chickens and commonly used antimicrobials, such as the coccidiostat narasin, the AGP BMD, and the therapeutic antibiotic penicillin. Significant shifts in ileal microbiota composition were observed during the 35-d trial. Narasin reduced the numbers of dominant bacteria (mainly lactobacilli) by 25% and increased the body weight of birds by more than 20% (Table 3). When BMD was added together with narasin, the density of the total ileal bacteria was reduced by 75% and the body weight was increased by more than 40%. When narasin was combined with penicillin the effects were equal to those with BMD. Interesting changes occurred also in microbiota composition. Normally, lactic acid bacteria dominate in the small intestine of the broiler chickens, comprising over 90% of all bacteria present. When BMD or penicillin was introduced the population of lactobacilli collapsed and E. coli gained space. The above-mentioned antibiotics are known to have a much greater inhibitory effect on the Gram-positive bacteria such as lactic acid bacteria but less of an effect on the Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli. As mentioned in the previous section, lactic acid bacteria compete with the host animal for the simple carbohydrates, amino acids, vitamins etc., since these bacteria need to capture all these from their environment. Therefore, it is likely that the reduction in the numbers of lactobacilli means that there is less competition for nutrients and more nutrients are available for the host. This can be expected to have a beneficial effect on the body weight gain and the feed conversion efficiency (unfortunately not measured in this mechanistic trial). Unlike lactobacilli, E. coli can synthesise all amino acids and vitamins when it is provided with only one of the many sugars it can utilise and ammonium nitrogen.

Following the ban of antibiotics, there has been a boom in the development of other bacterial inhibitors to replace AGP. Often these new product candidates are referred to as alternatives to antibiotics although the effects on intestinal microbiota are completely different. For example, a probiotic from the genus Lactobacillus is being referred to as an alternative to an antibiotic that is specifically demolishing the intestinal lactobacilli population. It is obvious that such products that are designed to increase lactobacilli numbers may compete out specific pathogens but they do not positively affect competition for nutrients in the small intestine or improve FCR by the same mechanism as the antibiotic they are supposed to replace. Other products, however, that claim to be bacterial inhibitors (plant-based phenolics, resin acids etc.) may function through the same mechanisms as AGP and improve FCR.

Products with a positive effect on epithelial health

In addition to controlling harmful microbes and improving nutrient competition, microbiota modulators can also benefit the host by stimulating microbial metabolites that act directly on the intestinal epithelium. Among these metabolites, short-chain fatty acids (SCFA) are the most prominent, with butyrate being particularly interesting for its multifunctional effects on host physiology, including reinforcement of barrier integrity, modulation of immune responses, and support of nutrient absorption. Although produced in the broiler caecum, butyrate is rapidly absorbed and functions systemically as both an energy substrate and a signalling molecule. These systemic effects mean that, in addition to the caecal epithelium, butyrate produced in the caecum also influences more distant tissues such as the small intestine, where it improves tight-junction integrity, promotes enterocyte differentiation, stimulates mucin and host defence peptide secretion, and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines (Guilloteau et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2018).

Butyrate arises mainly from the fermentation of non-digestible carbohydrates by anaerobic bacteria in the caecum. Common producers of butyrate are Gram-positive bacteria, all belonging to the bacterial class Clostridia. The most common bacterial genera are Anaerobutyricum and Anaerostipes of the family Lachnospiraceae, Faecalibacterium of the family Ruminococcaceae and Butyricicoccus of the family Oscillospiraceae (Eeckhaut et al., 2011). These microbes synthesise butyrate primarily through the energy-efficient butyryl-CoA: acetate CoA-transferase (BUT) pathway and, to a lesser extent, the ATP-dependent butyrate kinase (BUK) pathway (Louis et al., 2004). Because the BUT pathway requires acetate as a co-substrate, the availability of acetate in the caecum can limit overall butyrate yield (Koh et al., 2016).

Microbial cross-feeding plays an important role in maintaining a balanced fermentation, a characteristic of which is that metabolic intermediates do not accumulate. Lactate produced by lactic acid bacteria becomes utilised by specialised butyrate producers such as Anaerobutyricum spp. and Anaerostipes spp. Lactate utilisers work in synchrony with lactate producers thus preventing fermentation disorders such as lactate accumulation that would eventually lead to caecal acidosis (Apajalahti and Rinttilä, 2019; Shetty et al., 2020).

Dietary strategies that stimulate broiler caecal butyrate production include providing substrates that support the growth of butyrate-producing bacteria. Prebiotics such as resistant starch, inulin, and xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS) increase the fermentable carbohydrate supply. Inulin supplementation has been shown to increase caecal butyrate concentrations in broilers (Rehman et al., 2008). Xylo-oligosaccharide supplementation has been shown to enhance butyrate production and improve gut health (Singh et al., 2021) and has also been reported to enrich butyrate-producing bacteria and the butyryl-CoA: acetate CoA-transferase genes in the caecum (De Maesschalck et al., 2015). In addition to direct prebiotic supplementation, exogenous enzymes such as xylanases and endo-glucanases can release fermentable oligosaccharides in situ from cereal arabinoxylans. Studies have shown that such enzymatic degradation enhances substrate availability for butyrate producers and significantly increases caecal butyrate concentrations (Bedford and Apajalahti, 2018; Kouzounis et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023).

To confirm that feeding poultry a xylanase produced substrate in situ for butyrate producers, a mechanistic broiler feeding trial using a wheat-soy-based diet with or without a commercial xylanase was conducted. As presented in Table 4, birds receiving the xylanase showed significantly higher caecal butyrate concentrations compared to the control diet, both on d 15 and 28. Total SCFA concentration was also elevated at both time points. The results suggest that enzymatic degradation of arabinoxylans leads to enrichment of AXOS utilising butyrate producers in the caecum and stimulates overall SCFA production (Bedford and Apajalahti, 2018). Analysis of microbiota composition-zoom in approach

One practical concept to address the challenges in the analysis of highly complex microbial communities is the progressive zoom-in strategy, which provides a structured framework for linking microbial taxa and functional pathways to broiler health and performance. According to this strategy, analysis begins with broad, unbiased community-level profiling of the microbiota to detect general shifts, then it progresses to exploratory sequencing to reveal specific taxa and functions, and culminates in targeted rapid assays that provide quantitative confirmation of specific taxa or functional genes in intestinal samples (Fig. 1). With the use of multiple analytical methods together, this approach makes associations more reliable and helps in generating meaningful biological hypotheses. The following sections describe the commonly employed methods at each stage of this workflow.Fig. 1Schematic presentation of the zoom-in approach for the microbial community analysis and identification of indicator organisms. When the microbial analysis is based on DNA, it is highly important that DNA isolation and purification are quantitative and represent the microbial community in the original digesta sample accurately. One should not take it for granted that commercial DNA isolation kits are reliable in this respect-studies have shown that yields, reproducibility, and representation of bacterial diversity can vary depending on the kit used (Wegl et al., 2021). Therefore, lysis efficacy should be monitored (e.g., via microscopy; Apajalahti et al., 1998) and expected DNA yield can be estimated from the known microbial densities in the sample. If the isolated DNA preparation does not represent the target microbial community, the outcome from any subsequent DNA analysis method will be flawed.

Profiling of total microbial communities

Percent guanine plus cytosine (%G + C) profiling of chromosomal DNA was originally developed for soil microbiota analysis in the 1990s (Holben and Harris, 1995). Afterwards, the method was modified to be used in the profiling of intestinal microbiota (Apajalahti et al., 1998). Although this method is not widely adopted in current microbiota studies due to its technical complexity, it is routinely applied in the zoom-in workflow as an unbiased, PCR-independent approach for the initial community-level profiling. This method relies on the principle that each bacterial species has a characteristic genomic GC content, which determines the buoyant density of its DNA in caesium chloride density gradient. Fractionation of the total community DNA separates chromosomes from each other based on their GC content, producing reproducible profiles of the total bacterial community.

The primary advantage of the %G + C profiling is that it does not require prior sequence information to be used for primer or probe design, thereby avoiding biases inherent in PCR-based approaches. While this method does not identify taxa specifically, it can reveal whether communities differ between cohorts, such as birds on different diets or healthy versus diseased (McCracken et al,). Since the GC content of all described bacterial species is known, a shift in a certain place in the %G + C profile provides the first indication of bacterial taxa that have been affected.

Chromosomal DNA can be prepared for further analysis by dividing it into, for example, 5% G + C fractions, each representing a defined GC content range. Further analysis of these fractions with a narrow GC content range by a PCR-based method is advantageous because in mixed PCR reactions where the GC content of the target microbes range from 20% to 80%, low GC templates tend to denature more efficiently and dominate amplification, whereas high GC templates are often underrepresented. Thus, PCR with DNA template with a narrow GC content range provides much more reliable quantification data. Furthermore, the DNA polymerase enzyme used in PCR reactions functions with varying efficiency when the DNA templates have different GC contents. In conclusion, by dividing the community DNA into narrow %G + C fractions before sequencing, the described biases are reduced, increasing the likelihood of detecting also low-abundance organisms or those with extreme GC content (Holben et al., 2004; Krogius-Kurikka et al., 2009).

Exploratory sequencing

Once higher-level differences in community structure are revealed by %G + C profiling, sequencing-based methods can resolve which microbial taxa are the cause of the observed changes. High-throughput sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene is the most widely used approach in poultry microbiome studies (Oakley et al., 2014). Microbial DNA is PCR-amplified using broad-range primers targeting conserved regions flanking the hypervariable segments of the 16S rRNA gene. The resulting amplicons are sequenced in parallel, generating millions of reads that are clustered into operational taxonomic units or amplicon sequence variants and compared with reference databases for taxonomic assignment. This method is cost-efficient and scalable, generating large datasets on poultry intestinal microbiota. When applied consistently and reproducibly, 16S rRNA gene sequencing is an effective technique for comparative analyses aimed at detecting treatment-associated shifts in microbiota composition. However, it suffers from well-recognized limitations. No primer target site is 100% conserved; unpredicted primer mismatches mean that no amplification scheme captures all bacterial taxa at equal efficiency, leading to potential under- or overrepresentation of specific groups. Resolution is often limited to genus or family level, which complicates functional interpretation, since even closely related taxa may differ physiologically (Pollock et al., 2018). Consequently, while 16S rRNA gene sequencing provides valuable exploratory information, it is insufficient on its own to establish either accurate relative abundance or functional links between the microbiota and host physiology.

To overcome these limitations, shotgun metagenomic sequencing offers a more comprehensive alternative. Total microbial DNA is sequenced without PCR amplification, avoiding primer bias and enabling higher taxonomic resolution, often down to species or strain level. Importantly, shotgun metagenomics also provides functional information, as genes encoding metabolic pathways, resistance determinants, or virulence factors can be annotated. This enables linking taxonomic shifts to changes in functional potential, which is essential for understanding host–microbiota interactions (Glendinning et al., 2020; Peña et al., 2025). Although shotgun sequencing is more expensive, computationally demanding, and analytically complex than amplicon sequencing, it is increasingly adopted as sequencing costs decline, and bioinformatics tools improve. Although this approach may eventually be the way to proceed, currently it is not always practical when up to hundreds of samples comprising several timepoints, intestinal segments of interest, treatments and multiple replicates are involved in the study. The produced sequence data would be massive, and its analysis today would be a challenge.

Together, 16S rRNA gene sequencing and shotgun metagenomics form the exploratory middle tier of the zoom-in strategy. The former allows cost-efficient surveys of bacterial diversity across large cohorts, whereas the latter provides deeper, functionally informative datasets that guide targeted quantitative assays.

Because sequencing-based approaches primarily provide relative abundance data, complementary quantitative methods are often required to determine absolute bacterial numbers or changes in total bacterial load that may be relevant for biological interpretation (Vandeputte et al., 2017).

Quantification by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Exploratory sequencing generates valuable information about which taxa or functional groups are associated with different performance outcomes or intestinal health status. However, qPCR remains the gold-standard technique for confirming and quantifying these associations across large numbers of intestinal samples (Rinttilä et al., 2004; Smith and Osborn, 2009). Well-designed assays target selectively the bacterial taxa or functional gene of interest, and amplification is measured in real time via fluorescent dyes. By comparing cycle threshold values against known standards, absolute quantities can be determined across several orders of magnitude.

Unfortunately, there are many published papers reporting qPCR primers that do not, based on today's knowledge, quantify the taxa they are claimed to, but have either too strict or too broad specificity. This can be partly due to the lack of relevant sequence information at the time the primers were designed. Before using any primers, one should therefore always recheck the specificity against current sequence databases.

Well-designed and correctly targeted qPCR is largely free from the biases seen in broad-range PCR during amplicon sequencing, providing true quantitative data. This makes it particularly valuable for verifying sequencing results in the original bacterial community DNA, assessing the biological relevance of observed differences, and once the assays are designed based on sequencing results, providing a cost-effective method for routine analysis of broiler intestinal samples. The main limitation of qPCR is that it requires prior knowledge of the targets, making it sub-optimal as a stand-alone tool in exploratory studies, but it represents the quantitative endpoint of the zoom-in workflow.

Benefits of the zoom-in framework

Applied sequentially, the methods described above provide a layered view of the poultry intestinal microbiota. Broad community-level %G + C profiling offers an initial, unbiased indication of whether microbial communities differ between experimental groups. Sequencing approaches then identify the taxa and functional pathways underlying these differences, generating information about which organisms and genes may contribute to observed performance outcomes. Finally, targeted qPCR allows precise measurement of specific microbes or functional genes in the original samples, providing reliable and reproducible data that help clarify potential cause-and-effect relationships (Fig. 1).

By progressively narrowing the focus, the zoom-in strategy reduces methodological biases and enables a structured path from broad community patterns to quantitative evidence of differences in microbiota composition. The zoom-in approach is a research tool that reveals which DNA biomarkers are relevant for the high throughput analysis by means such as qPCR. While the described DNA-based methods can reveal the potential functional capacity of the microbial community, they only indicate that target DNA is present at a certain abundance. They do not, however, provide information regarding the induction or expression of enzymes, enzyme function or production of metabolites.

Analysis of microbial metabolites

While microbiota analysis reveals which bacteria and their functional potential are present in different gut compartments, it does not necessarily indicate which metabolic processes are active. The presence of a bacterial species at high abundance suggests that it is actively growing and has found an ecological niche in the gut. However, the most definitive insight into microbial activity requires assessment of the production of metabolites such as volatile fatty acids and lactic acid (collectively referred to as SCFA) or protein fermentation end-products (Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016; Rinttilä and Apajalahti, 2013). Indeed, in most cases it is not the presence of a bacterial cell itself that is important, but rather the metabolic products, toxins, and other compounds produced by the bacteria, which mediate effects on host health and performance. This makes metabolite profiling an essential complement to microbiota analysis for linking microbial composition to animal performance and intestinal health.

Analysis of microbial enzyme activities

In addition to metabolite profiling, direct measurement of microbial enzyme activities provides important insight into the functional potential of intestinal communities. While the abundance of a functional gene or metabolite can suggest microbial activity, enzyme assays confirm that the genes encoding for the catalytic proteins are actually expressed and active in the digesta. Microbial enzymes such as xylanases, β-glucosidases and proteases, reflect the ability of the microbial community to degrade specific dietary components, such as non-starch polysaccharides and proteins, linking microbial composition to nutrient utilisation and feed efficiency. Unlike metagenomic or meta transcriptomic analyses, which reveal the genetic potential or transcriptional activity of microbes, enzyme assays provide direct evidence of catalytic function in the gut environment. Together, these measurements offer a comprehensive perspective on microbial functionality, providing data that can help interpret how intestinal communities contribute to nutrient breakdown and overall intestinal microbiota activity.

Potential problems in the correlation approaches

When product effects are detected in an animal trial with clear effects on health and performance, it is important to understand what the biological mechanisms are. If the effects are expected to be mediated by activities linked to intestinal functions, it is common that digesta and tissue samples are collected for various analyses to increase understanding on the mode-of-action. Among the most common analyses, samples are quantified for various microbes, microbial metabolites, potential substrates for microbes (sugars and amino acids), immune markers and inflammation markers. If the studies are successful, one can show a link between some of the markers, inclusion of a specific product and possibly animal health. Such results would suggest that the product tested is affecting specific intestinal functions, which in turn would lead to improved animal health and performance. This is intriguing, but a correlation between the parameters of interest is not a valid proof of a direct link between them or even causality. A test product can concomitantly affect several independent parameters which may have nothing to do with each other. However, clear correlations may well lead to a hypothesis and guide the research in a direction that could eventually reveal the product mode-of-action.

Analysis of microbiota composition is straightforward, because microbes in the intestine of the monogastric animals are relatively stable, not digested and under normal circumstances not transported across epithelia. Also, the microbial density in the crop, ileum and caecum of poultry is so high that the analysis results are in most cases little affected by ingested microbes. While microbiota analysis identifies which organisms are present and potentially enriched, it does not indicate whether these microbes are functionally active or driving the observed effects on host performance (Kers et al., 2018). Many bacterial species have multiple choices in their anabolism and catabolism. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing can detect the presence of functional genes encoding for specific enzymes but does not show whether they are expressed. That would require transcriptomics which involves reverse transcription and other steps that are subject to biases. Therefore, transcriptomics is not quantitative and, by no means, a practical routine tool for the hundreds of samples required if the research were to be properly replicated and valid.

Intermediates and end products of bacterial metabolisms are the final indication of the activity of a metabolic route. Their analysis in digesta samples provides a snapshot in the habitat with a complex network of reactions. Interpretation of the metabolite findings in in vivo settings can be challenging because the residual concentration of a compound in the lumen reflects a dynamic balance of multiple processes. The residual concentration of a metabolite in luminal samples reflects the net balance of its production by microbes, transformation by other microbes, and absorption by the host epithelium (Louis and Flint, 2017). This makes the direct assessment of microbial activities challenging. Misinterpretations are common but can be avoided if the limitations of the approach are acknowledged.

For example, a product may significantly increase the rate of butyrate production by a bacterium, but it remains undetected in the analysis of the acid from the digesta because of the high rate of uptake or utilisation of butyrate by the intestinal epithelium. A well-developed and healthy epithelium may consume more butyrate as an energy source than a damaged epithelium. Consequently, one would detect reduced residual concentration of butyrate, even in the case where bacteria produce it at an elevated rate.

Furthermore, the ratios between different SCFA detected in the digesta may not reflect the rate of their production because 1) one may be used at a higher rate by the epithelial cells (butyrate), 2) the rate of passive transport through the epithelium is affected by specific pKa values of the acids, 3) some acids are transported by active transporters while some are not, and 4) some metabolites are not end products but intermediates in the microbial community etc. Similarly, elevated lactate concentrations may indicate either overproduction by lactic acid bacteria, reduced activity of lactate-utilising bacteria, or reduced transport across the intestinal epithelium. Levels of protein fermentation end-products, such as branched-chain fatty acids (BCFA) or phenolic metabolites of aromatic amino acids, are influenced both by microbial activity and by the amount of bypass protein reaching the lower intestine (Apajalahti and Vienola, 2016; Neis et al., 2015).

The experimental arrangements described above do not reveal the causal direction in the observed associations. For instance, does the poor feed conversion efficiency lead to increased lactic acid fermentation in the small intestine, or does the intense lactic acid fermentation contribute to the poor feed conversion efficiency? Is a measured activity a cause or a consequence of the parameter it is correlated with?

Laboratory fermentation models described in more detail in the following sections of this paper provide a controlled solution to these interpretive challenges. Interpretative challenges are eased because absorption by the host epithelium does not occur.

Ex vivo fermentation studies

What does ex vivo in intestinal microbiota research mean?

The Latin term ex vivo means “out of the living”. This term has originally been used for studies conducted with plant or animal tissues excised from the living organism, to be studied in a laboratory environment for mechanisms etc. It is a characteristic of ex vivo studies that tissue samples are kept alive only for a limited period and preferably in a condition that keeps all possible functions unchanged, as close to authentic as possible. A difficulty in studying mechanisms in vivo is that there are multiple concomitant interacting activities. Therefore, the kinetics of reactions is very difficult to measure. It is typical in a well-functioning biological system that no intermediate metabolites accumulate, but the reactions in a metabolic pathway are tightly linked; a metabolite produced is further converted to other products at the rate corresponding to its rate of production. That makes it difficult to study the kinetics of individual partial reactions. Indeed, one of the major benefits of an ex vivo approach is that a specific tissue can be isolated from other tissues and this way its functions and kinetics studied uncoupled from interacting reactions.

In the Alimetrics laboratory (Espoo, Finland), the term ex vivo is also used to describe studies conducted with intact intestinal microbial communities rather than single bacterial cultures or defined mixtures. Animal species- and gut compartment-specific ex vivo fermentation approaches based on authentic intestinal inocula have been applied in broilers and pigs to study fermentation responses to dietary interventions under controlled conditions (Apajalahti et al., 2009; Davies et al., 2024; Kettunen et al., 2015). These microbial communities are often highly complex, comprising mixtures of specialised bacteria whose individual functions are not fully understood. This is especially true when dealing with anaerobic microbial communities in habitats such as the caecum, colon or rumen. Such bacterial communities have been referred to as tissues carried by the host but still being technically outside the host animal. They play an equally important role in the host wellbeing as many other tissues of an animal. Indeed, the metabolic activity of an intestinal microbial community can be equal to or even greater than the activity of other tissues of the animal body.

Unlike other tissues of an animal host, the intestinal microbial community is largely independent of molecular oxygen and in the lower intestine oxygen is toxic. This is because strictly anaerobic bacteria do not have the machinery to quench oxygen radicals, which aerobic organisms possess. Such characteristics of anaerobic microbial communities bring special requirements for their handling. While ex vivo studies with other animal tissues require highly active oxygen gas (e.g. carbogen) to keep tissues alive, studies with anaerobic microbial communities require total anaerobiosis and often protective anoxic gas flow. That is especially the case with caecal, colon and rumen bacteria, while bacteria in the small intestine, which are almost solely lactic acid bacteria, are oxygen tolerant.

The term in vitro means “in the glass or tube”. In many cases in vitro experiments are used to answer simple practical questions and often they have not been designed to accurately mimic the original habitat. An example is monitoring the effects of potential microbial inhibitors or stimulators on the growth of specific harmful or beneficial bacteria. Such in vitro studies are easy to replicate, but the physicochemical conditions and the growth media are far from those in the authentic habitat. Both in vitro and ex vivo approaches have their roles in basic research and R&D projects aimed at new product development. Often in vitro, ex vivo and in vivo studies are used consecutively for example when the objective is to screen for effective products from many product candidates and doses. Simple in vitro experiments can easily handle even hundreds of product treatments while it is less practical to use ex vivo and in particular in vivo approaches for so many treatments. For most research groups the handling of numerous treatments ex vivo and in vivo is not possible and therefore preselection is needed when moving from in vitro to ex vivo and further from ex vivo to in vivo.

Importance of maintaining authenticity in ex vivo research

Microbial communities can be highly complex, and they truly function as a community, not as a collective of independent microbes. In the community microbes are dependent on each other in many ways. Individual members help in adjusting the physicochemical conditions and providing the required substrates and cofactors. The community as a whole evolves towards the maximum efficiency and the individual microbial species are interdependent supporters of the community. Through intestinal tissues the host is secreting compounds to intestinal lumen, and some may well be crucial for the growth of specific microbes and the function of the entire microbial community.

It is not realistic to assume that all physicochemical and nutritional conditions prevailing in the intestine could be replicated exactly in the laboratory. However, in a sophisticated ex vivo setting it is possible to mimic the authentic conditions relatively well. There are some very important key considerations.1)Growth medium. The exact nutrient and cofactor requirements of all bacteria in the community are not known. When the growth medium is prepared from the intestinal digesta recovered from the intestinal segment of interest, all the required nutrients, those produced by other members of the community and those secreted from intestinal tissue, are captured.2)Inoculum. Inoculum must be from the intestinal segment of interest. It could be from the crop, ileum, caecum or colon depending on the intestinal segment mimicked. It is very important that the digesta inoculum is fresh and kept under conditions required by the bacteria present. This means that digesta inoculum from the caecum, colon and rumen must be kept anoxic. Freezing or exposing the inoculum to air changes its composition substantially and produces significantly different fermentation results (see the examples in the next paragraph).3)Physicochemical conditions. Intestinal microbes in the upper intestine (crop and small intestine) are not strict about the presence or absence of molecular oxygen. However, many anaerobic microbes of the caecum, colon and rumen require complete absence of oxygen and, furthermore, low redox potential to be able to gain energy in their catabolism. Microbes in the gastrointestinal tract produce organic acids, which tend to lower the pH under ex vivo conditions where acids are not removed. Therefore, it is important to monitor pH and ensure that the concentration of acids does not exceed levels prevailing in the intestinal segment mimicked. A healthy pH in the ileum, caecum, colon and rumen is close to neutrality while in the crop it is lower (pH 4 to 5).

Examples of potential pitfalls in ex vivo approaches

As mentioned above the work with the strictly anaerobic microbes present in fresh digesta material may feel tedious and challenging. Also, it is an absolute requirement to have a laboratory equipped for work with anaerobes; e.g. an anaerobic glove box and means to scavenge traces of O2 from the protective gases used. It is also important that there is an animal facility nearby so that fresh material is available to be used as inocula and media for the studies. Due to the obvious complexity of the arrangements, it is tempting to run ex vivo studies by taking short cuts. If fresh inocula are not used or anaerobicity is not maintained, the problems arising are not necessarily detected. This is because strong microbial growth and metabolism will develop, but the gained growth is not by microbes that represent the original microbial community.

However, the activities and composition of the original microbial community of the donor animals would, most likely, have changed. If an artificial growth medium would be used, the change in microbial community would be even more pronounced.

Considering host physiology when studying product effects ex vivo

When testing the effects of any dietary additives/ingredients on the gastrointestinal ecosystem by using ex vivo or in vitro laboratory models it is important to understand what the fate of an additive is in the digestive tract. The use of a laboratory model is relevant only when the products/molecules studied are known to reach the intestinal segment mimicked. Also, the product should be in the form identical to that introduced in the experimental vessel.

Examples of dietary components that provide little or no relevant information when studied in ex vivo/in vitro models.1)Complete feed. In broiler chickens, only crop bacteria are exposed to complete feed. For ileal and caecal bacteria complete feed is a foreign substrate. Starch and other easily digestible components would cause highly intense bacterial fermentation in all segments of the intestine. This does not occur in live animals because in in vivo only the most resistant feed components reach the distal intestine.2)Products that are absorbed. The bulk of organic acids, most sugars, amino acids etc. are absorbed from upper intestine. It is not relevant to study their effect on ileal, caecal or colon microbiota because they never reach those sites at any effective concentration.3)Products that are digested. Polysaccharides, lipids and proteins are in most cases hydrolysed by digestive enzymes and do not serve as substrates for ileal, caecal or colon microbiota. In cases where the products of hydrolysis are not absorbed in the upper intestine, they should be introduced to ex vivo model after hydrolytic pretreatment.4)Products that are conjugated. In some cases, products are absorbed and enter the enterohepatic circulation, become conjugated in the liver and re-enter the upper intestine in conjugated form. Conjugated forms of molecules are often less inhibitory (toxic) to organisms than the free form.

There are no general rules for which products should or should not be studied in laboratory models, but it is important to consider the abovementioned aspects when running such studies.

In addition to the form of tested products, it is important to understand the other limitations of the ex vivo models. The animal interface is missing and, therefore, all the indirect effects resulting from the modulation of the immune system and other host functions cannot be studied in the ex vivo models described above. When the ex vivo studies use media prepared from the authentic digesta, all the undefined components secreted into the lumen by the host are also included. If the medium would be constructed from the defined components it is likely that it would not contain all substrates and cofactors the intestinal microbial community is exposed to in its authentic habitat.

Ex vivo methods for studying microbiota adaptation to diet additives

Microbiota variation between individual broiler chickens

With the ex vivo intestinal fermentation models it is possible to study also how microbiota in a specific intestinal segment adapts to different diets. For example, if an exotic carbohydrate is introduced to the diet, it is to be expected that caecal bacteria capable of utilising that product will enrich after prolonged feeding. Alternatively, if an antimicrobial compound is introduced, it is likely that microbes resisting that compound will eventually enrich.

Adaptation of a microbial community to various diets/additives can be studied by inoculating an ex vivo fermentation study with inocula from differently fed animals. To get reliable results it is vital that 1) fresh inocula are used, 2) inocula are handled with caution, and 3) the fermentation time is relatively short. If the fermentation will be continued for more than 8 to 12 h, the results no longer reflect the characteristics of the enriched microbial community in the donor animal, but those evolving in the fermentation vessel. To be able to show that diets led to enrichment of different microbes or physiological functions, the magnitude of the diet effect must clearly exceed the animal-to-animal variation. That can only be demonstrated if inocula are taken from individual animals, meaning that true biological replicates are used.

Adaptation of intestinal microbiota to feed additives

A study was conducted to determine whether the ex vivo approach was applicable to showing differences in microbiota adaptation when different diets were used. Furthermore, authentic substrates were prepared from the intestinal contents of the differently fed birds. With the approach used it is possible to show whether the additives affected 1) the caecal microbiota composition and their induced activities and 2) the characteristics of the substrate that fed the caecal microbiota when different additives were used.

In the study, broiler chickens were fed three different diets. A basic wheat-soya-based diet for broiler chickens served as the control diet, against which the other diets were compared.. The second diet was amended with the coccidiostat narasin, which was expected to have some inhibitory effect on small-intestinal microbiota, but the effect on caecal microbiota was unknown. The third diet contained a xylanase enzyme. There are previous publications suggesting that xylanase inclusion stimulates caecal fermentation (Choct et al., 1999; Singh et al., 2021). Birds on the three different diets were used as sources of ileal and caecal digesta which were used to prepare three different fermentation media for the ex vivo study. In addition, 10 individual birds from each diet were sources of the corresponding three caecal inocula. In the full factorial study all three media were fed to all three inocula types, giving altogether 9 different experimental groups.

The results revealed that both the substrates and inocula from differently fed birds had a significant effect on fermentation. However, statistical analysis showed that there were no significant inoculum × substrate interactions. Table 9, Table 10 summarise some of the main effects of inocula and substrates from differently fed birds. Although the focus of this paper is on methodology, not in speculation about individual results, some of the findings were unexpected. The inocula from both narasin- and xylanase-fed birds appeared to be slightly less active than the inocula from the control birds. This suggests that the caecal bacterial density is lower, or that the bacterial metabolism is somehow suppressed. These inocula produced significantly more BCFA, fermentation products of branched-chain amino acids (Table 9). With the substrate prepared from the xylanase-fed birds this was most likely due to a higher concentration of soluble arabinoxylan produced by the enzyme in situ. Narasin is known to suppress fermentation in the small intestine. This most likely spares substrates for caecal bacteria and could be detected as increased caecal fermentation with the substrate prepared from the narasin-fed birds. The example study shows that the ex vivo approach described can be used to evaluate, not only microbiota adaptation but also the effect of additives on the composition of substrates available for bacteria in various intestinal segments. The presented cases mainly used caecal ex vivo fermentation as an example; studies with this intestinal segment are the most challenging due to the oxygen sensitive nature of the caecal bacteria. However, the approach works equally well for crop and ileal fermentation studies. The substrates and inocula must then be recovered from the digesta at or proximal to the intestinal segment modelled.

Conclusions

During the last three decades the techniques to analyse the composition of complex microbial communities by culture-independent methods have greatly advanced. There are means to detect and enumerate practically every microbe inhabiting the gastrointestinal tract. However, the lack of profound understanding of quantitative bacterial lysis, DNA isolation, primer and probe design, and bioinformatics may lead to major biases, flawed conclusions, and publications that confuse rather than build up the common knowledge base.

Parallel to the revolution in the DNA-based microbial analysis, the knowledge of microbial physiology and ecology of anaerobic bacterial communities appears to be in the skillset of relatively few research groups working in the field of the gastrointestinal health. The detailed information on DNA sequences (operational taxonomic units and metagenome-assembled genomes) continues to accumulate, but the functions and physiology of the carrying microbes are in many cases missing. This suggests that improving our understanding would require a return to the basics of microbial physiology and metabolism.

Development of ex vivo laboratory models is an example of going back to basic physiology. In the intestinal microbial community, also the uncharacterised microbes that scientists have failed to isolate and grow as pure cultures, are actively growing in synchrony with the better characterised members of the community. It would be invaluable to develop further and increase the use of such techniques in intestinal microbiota research to gain more knowledge.

Analysis of intestinal samples from the animal trials is the most common research approach when the objective is to try and understand more about the intestinal health. Unfortunately, the complexity of the dynamic microbe-to-microbe and host-to-microbe interactions makes the matrix very complicated. It is highly important to understand the limitations of the approach and avoid over- and misinterpretation of the data produced.
