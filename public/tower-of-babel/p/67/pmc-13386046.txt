How equity is operationalised in learning health systems: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Erica Y Lau, Justin J Koh, Chenoa Cassidy-Matthews, Krisztina Vasarhelyi, Corinne M Hohl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLearning health systems (LHS) aim to improve care through repeated cycles that link data, learning and action. These systems have a goal to uphold equity through purposefully including patients and other stakeholders in co-design and using real-world data to identify and respond to inequities. However, evidence shows that LHS do not automatically produce equitable outcomes, and a gap persists between stated equity goals and the practical mechanisms used to build equity into system design, measurement and decision-making. This scoping review examines how equity and related concepts, including antiracism and Indigenous self-determination, are operationalised within LHS.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a scoping review using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and report the findings in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. To capture equity-oriented learning beyond formally labelled systems, we define LHS by function rather than name, focusing on systems that demonstrate linkages between data use, learning and action. We will search PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and PsycINFO, supplemented by hand searches of relevant journals for peer-reviewed articles published from 2015 to July 2026. Two reviewers will independently screen titles, abstracts and full texts. Using a structured extraction form, we will chart how equity is defined and operationalised within LHS, including governance and decision-making processes, data and measurement practices, learning activities and contextual factors that shape equity-oriented learning. We will synthesise findings descriptively and thematically to map where in the LHS cycle equity is operationalised, what equity dimensions are addressed and where reporting gaps remain. Findings will inform researchers, health system leaders and communities on how equity can be built into routine LHS processes.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required as this review synthesises published literature. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations.

Introduction

Learning health systems (LHS) are intended to help health systems improve by learning from routine care. In simple terms, they use data generated during everyday service delivery to identify problems, test changes and adjust practice over time. The National Academy of Medicine (formerly the Institute of Medicine) defines LHS as systems in which science, data, incentives and culture work together so that new knowledge is generated during care delivery and quickly used to improve practice.1 This learning process can be understood through a three-phase cycle: performance to data, data to knowledge and knowledge to performance.2 In this cycle, care experiences and outcomes are converted into data, data are interpreted and translated into knowledge, and knowledge is used to inform practice, policy or system change. This approach relies on regular collection and review of data, deliberate decision-making and clear responsibility for acting on what is learnt.3

LHS are often described as well suited to advancing equity because they can combine real-world data with patient and stakeholder engagement, bringing lived experience and community priorities into problem definition, learning, and action.46 When equity is intentionally built into system design, these features can help systems identify differences in care across populations and respond to unmet needs. However, these features do not ensure equitable outcomes on their own. Decisions about what data are collected and from whom, who is included in interpreting findings and how actions are selected can introduce bias. As a result, some populations may remain under-represented, and important inequities may go undetected or be reinforced through the learning process.79

In this review, we define equity as fairness in how health systems allocate resources, benefits and influence over decisions, while recognising that social and structural conditions shape access to care, health outcomes and participation in system learning.4 10 We understand equity as operating through four interconnected dimensions. Distributional equity concerns the fairness of access, quality, outcomes and resource allocation across population groups.6 10 11 Procedural equity concerns who participates and holds standing in priority-setting, representation and decision-making.12 Recognitional equity concerns whose identities, knowledge, histories and definitions of wellness are recognised as legitimate.6 13 Structural and relational equity concerns the institutional practices, power relations, racism, colonialism, Indigenous self-determination and data sovereignty that shape system learning.4 14

In LHS, this commitment to equity needs to be translated into system learning and action. Equity shapes what problems are prioritised, which population groups are represented in data, what indicators are used to assess differences in care or outcomes, who interprets findings and how learning becomes policy, service delivery or practice change. How that translation happens depends on whose perspectives shape the learning process.

Over the past decade, researchers and practitioners have called for more explicit ways to integrate equity into LHS, often linking equity to antiracism and Indigenous self-determination and positioning it as a core principle of LHS. Frameworks and commentaries describe approaches like co-production and cultural safety as ways to support equity-oriented learning.46 1517 Yet progress has been uneven. Many LHS describe aspirations to equity or participation, but far fewer describe how these commitments operate in everyday system processes.

Recent reviews show that practical system-level mechanisms for advancing equity are often missing or inadequately specified. Few studies describe who is responsible for equity-related decisions, how data are reviewed to identify inequities or what actions follow when differences across groups are observed.45 1820 Measures that assess equity—particularly those examining variation by ethnicity, social position or structural conditions—are frequently absent or underdeveloped.45 1820 Earlier reviews have identified additional gaps: weak integration of social and structural determinants of health into routine data use,2123 limited attention to power-sharing and accountability in governance2426 and unclear processes for interpreting equity-related findings and translating them into action.15 27 Most prior reviews have focused on systems that explicitly identify as LHS, describing their organisational structures, implementation conditions or evaluation maturity more generally.2832 They have paid less attention to how equity is operationalised within LHS or within LHS-like systems that do not use the LHS label. These gaps leave health systems endorsing equity as a value without clearly specifying the mechanisms through which it is measured, governed, acted on or sustained.

To address these gaps, this scoping review aims to examine how equity is operationalised in the real-world application of LHS approaches. We will map where in the LHS cycle equity is addressed, including performance to data, data to knowledge and knowledge to performance, and how equity is embedded into governance, participation, decision-making, implementation and accountability. We take a functional approach rather than limiting analysis to systems that explicitly label themselves as LHS. This approach matters because equity-oriented learning often occurs in community-led, Indigenous-governed or antiracist initiatives that apply continuous learning principles without adopting the LHS label.33 By examining how equity-related governance, data use, decision-making authority and measurement practices operate in real-world settings, this review aims to help researchers, health system leaders and communities understand how LHS can be designed to promote equity, inclusion and responsiveness to those most affected by health inequities.

We conducted a preliminary search of MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and the Joanna Briggs Institute’s (JBI’s) Database of Systematic Reviews and Implementation Reports and confirmed that this review does not duplicate existing evidence.

Methods and analysis

Methodological frameworks

We will conduct a scoping review to understand how health systems put equity into practice as part of their routine learning and improvement work. A scoping review is appropriate for this aim because it allows mapping how equity is defined, applied and documented across diverse settings, rather than evaluating the effectiveness of specific interventions. We will follow guidance from the JBI methodology for scoping reviews.34 35 We will report the review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).36

Search strategy

We will use a three-step search strategy in line with JBI guidance.34 35 First, we conducted an initial search in MEDLINE to identify relevant articles and examine the language used in titles, abstracts and indexing terms. Second, we developed a comprehensive search strategy that combined: (1) terms related to LHS and learning-oriented improvement models and (2) terms related to equity and inequity. We intentionally included learning-oriented approaches that function similarly to LHS—such as quality improvement collaboratives and practice-based research networks—even when sources do not use the term “learning health system”. We refined the search strategy in consultation with a health sciences librarian. Third, we will adapt the search strategy for additional databases, including CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science and PsycINFO.

Because equity-oriented LHS are not always described using standardised terminology, database searches alone may not identify all relevant sources. We will therefore supplement database searches with citation tracking and reference list review of key conceptual and foundational papers. To reduce concept dilution, we will combine LHS-adjacent terms with concepts indicating system-level learning, including learning, feedback, data, evidence, measurement, implementation, adaptation, sustainment, systemwide activity, networks and collaboration. Where each database supports proximity operators, we will use them to link LHS-adjacent terms with these system-level learning concepts. We provide final search strategies in online supplemental materials.

We will also conduct a targeted grey literature search to identify relevant system-level work not indexed in bibliographic databases. Sources will include ProQuest Dissertations and Theses, selected organisational websites and Indigenous-specific resources identified through X̱wi7x̱wa Library. We will focus on health authorities, research networks and policy organisations across jurisdictions, with priority given to Canadian health systems and relevant international organisations where feasible. We will document the search process in a search log.

Study selection

We will collate all identified citations in EndNote and remove duplicates. Screening will occur in three stages using the eligibility criteria (table 1). Reviewers will use a structured screening form to record decisions at each stage (figure 1). In stage 1, two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts for publication year, language, relevance to a health system context and equity relevance. Sources that meet or may meet these criteria will proceed to full-text screening. At title/abstract screening, sources will be retained when eligibility is uncertain, particularly when learning or equity relevance may be clarified only at full-text review.

Structured screening form used across the three screening stages. Top: title and abstract screening. Middle: functional learning health system (LHS) assessment during full-text screening. Bottom: assessment of equity operationalisation within LHS or LHS-like systems during full-text screening for inclusion in the final review.

In stage 2, two reviewers will independently assess full-text sources using the structured screening form to determine whether each source describes a functional LHS. A source will be classified as a functional LHS or LHS-like system if it demonstrates both system-level scope and a data–learning–action linkage. Sources do not need to describe all phases of the LHS cycle in equal detail, and the cycle may be distributed across different teams or structures. However, the source must provide enough information to show that data or evidence are connected to learning, interpretation, decision-making, action or system improvement over time. Sources that describe only data collection, measurement, dashboards, registries or reporting without a link to learning or action will be excluded. We will apply these criteria during screening so that broader models, such as quality improvement collaboratives, practice-based research networks, community-governed data systems and cross-sector data initiatives, are included only when they describe system-level learning consistent with a functional LHS.

In stage 3, reviewers will assess whether sources that meet the functional LHS criteria describe how equity is operationalised within one or more phases of the LHS cycle, including mechanisms such as governance, participation, priority-setting, measurement, implementation, accountability, resource allocation, and action. Sources that mention equity without describing how it is operationalised within system-level learning, decision-making or action will be excluded.

Before full-text screening, reviewers will pilot the eligibility criteria on a sample of sources, discuss discrepancies and refine the decision rules as needed. Inter-rater agreement will be assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Reviewers will record reasons for exclusion and resolve disagreements through discussion or, when needed, consultation with a third reviewer. The selection process will be documented using a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram.

Data extraction

Two reviewers will independently extract data using a standardised extraction form, which we will refine as needed (table 2). In addition to bibliographic and contextual information, we will extract data aligned with the review questions. This includes how equity is defined or conceptualised; where equity is addressed within the LHS cycle, including performance-to-data, data-to-knowledge and knowledge-to-performance processes; which equity dimensions are reflected (distributional, procedural, recognitional and structural and relational); and how equity is embedded into LHS processes, such as governance, participation, priority-setting, decision-making, implementation, accountability and action. We will also extract whose perspectives are described as shaping problem definition, data collection, interpretation, decision-making and action; which equity-related indicators, data sources and measurement approaches are reported; and what challenges, tensions or constraints are described in operationalising equity.

Data synthesis

We will synthesise extracted data using descriptive and thematic approaches. The synthesis will focus on four areas: (1) where equity is operationalised within the LHS cycle: performance to data, data to knowledge and knowledge to performance. Sources may address equity in one or more phases and do not need to report all phases in equal detail. (2) Which equity dimensions appear across sources and how these dimensions relate to specific LHS processes. For example, we will examine whether procedural equity is more commonly described in governance and participation processes, while distributional equity is more commonly reflected in measurement, stratification or resource allocation. (3) Whose perspectives are reflected at each stage of the LHS cycle—from defining problems to acting on findings. We will pay particular attention to the role of equity-deserving communities, patients, Indigenous partners, clinicians, health system leaders and other stakeholders. (4) Where reporting gaps remain, by identifying where extraction domains are fully reported, partially reported, not reported or unclear. This will help surface where the literature provides concrete operational detail and where it falls short in describing how equity is put into practice within LHS. We will present findings in tables and narrative summaries to provide insights into patterns, variation and gaps across system types and contexts. Where possible, we will explore differences between systems that explicitly identify as LHS and those that meet functional criteria without using the label.

Patient and public involvement

We did not involve patients or the public in the design of this scoping review protocol.

Ethics and dissemination

This review uses published and publicly available literature and does not require ethical approval. For institutional research ethics or research integrity inquiries, the relevant contact is the University of British Columbia Office of Research Ethics: RSIL@ors.ubc.ca. We will disseminate findings through peer-reviewed publications and present them at conferences and workshops.
