Pathophysiology, prevention and treatment of postoperative peritoneal adhesions: a WSES narrative

However, adhesions are not easily diagnosed with imaging, and often only become apparent when they cause intestinal obstruction symptoms. The pathophysiology of adhesion formation is complex, thus despite advances in surgical techniques and postoperative care, adhesions remain a persistent problem in clinical practice. Despite evidence for efficacy of some strategies in reducing adhesion formation question remain regarding the indications and impact on clinically relevant outcomes. The objective was to synthesize current evidence on adhesion pathophysiology and translate it into a clinically applicable "Narrative". Expert Panel Selection and Composition: The expert panel was composed of international specialists in general, trauma, and emergency surgery. Experts were selected based on their clinical leadership and academic contributions to the fields of peritoneal surgery and postoperative complication management. The panel included senior representatives from major surgical departments in Singapore (Sengkang and Singapore General Hospital), The Netherlands (Radboud University), and Italy (University of Bologna and Bufalini Hospital). No language restrictions were applied to the search strategy. Studies were screened based on their ability to address three specific pillars: surgical approach (MIS vs. Open), technical manoeuvres (haemostasis and tissue handling), and the use of mechanical or chemical adjuncts. Introduction

Adhesions are a common complication following surgery, characterised by the abnormal formation of fibrous bands between tissues and organs [1]. These adhesions can cause significant morbidity, such as pain, intestinal obstruction and increased risk of iatrogenic injuries and difficulties during subsequent surgeries. However, adhesions are not easily diagnosed with imaging, and often only become apparent when they cause intestinal obstruction symptoms. The pathophysiology of adhesion formation is complex and involves a cascade of inflammatory and repair processes that can disrupt normal tissue healing and lead to the formation of abnormal fibrous connections.

Despite advances in surgical techniques and postoperative care, adhesions remain a persistent problem in clinical practice. Various preventive strategies have been proposed and studied, including the use of barrier agents and modifications in surgical techniques [2].Despite evidence for efficacy of some strategies in reducing adhesion formation question remain regarding the indications and impact on clinically relevant outcomes. Therefore, we aim to evaluate the evidence in a comprehensive review to guide optimal preventive strategies. This systematic review aims to provide a thorough examination of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying adhesion formation and assess the efficacy of existing preventive strategies to guide future research and clinical practice in the management of adhesions.

Pathophysiology of adhesions

Traditionally, Adhesion formation involves three main pathways: (1) inhibition of the fibrinolytic and extracellular matrix degradation systems, (2) induction of inflammatory response triggering production of cytokines and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and (3) induction of tissue hypoxia, causing increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [3]. Recent research has also shown that pro-inflammatory activation of macrophages, impaired recruitment and activation of peritoneal fibroblasts, mitigated epithelial-mesenchymal-transition (EMT), enhanced fibrinolysis and impaired angiogenesis also contribute to peritoneal adhesion formation[4, 5].

Adhesions are thought to be caused by imbalance in the combination of wound healing, hypoxia, coagulation, inflammation, and fibrin degradation [6, 7]. When tissues are traumatised, there will be a cascade of fibrin exudate, cytokine production, cell migration, vascular oedema, and the suppression of fibrinolytic activity [8]. There is also increasing interest in the role of macrophages in adhesion formation. Early studies emphasized the pro-inflammatory actions of classically activated (M1-like) macrophages—thought to promote peritoneal inflammation through the release of proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species [9]. Emerging evidence suggests that this binary M1/M2 classification, however, oversimplifies the diverse and dynamic phenotypes that macrophages exhibit in the peritoneal environment. Nevertheless, macrophages do have an important role. Newer studies by Zindel et al. in 2021 also proposes that contamination of gut microbes increases post-surgical adhesion formation. Adhesion myofibroblasts arise from the mesothelium, and this transformation is driven by epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signalling [10]. Under ischemic conditions, there is reduced fibrinolytic activity and fibrin cannot be resorbed, which causes fibroblast infiltration and scar tissue formation [7]. The fibrous tissue shrinks and gains strength as it matures, progressing to mature fibrous tissue in about six weeks [11].

Internal scar tissue contains macrophages, eosinophils, red blood cells, tissue debris, mast cells, and fibroblasts. Some authors reported that postsurgical adhesions also include nerve fibres and endothelial cells, which causes pain in patients [12]. New studies also suggest that early events of adhesion formation relied on activation of mesothelial cells rather than fibroblasts depositing matrix [13, 14]. Evidence points to mesothelial fusion as the initiating event, with fibroblasts and immune cells acting as subsequent modulators that perpetuate adhesion formation [13, 14]. Another study in 2020 further supports this, and postulates that adhesions are caused by membrane protrusions and fusions between mesothelial surfaces, both of which rely on calcium signalling. These are early events which are thought to precede the currently known stages of mesothelial proliferation, thickening, and scar formation [14].

Much remains unknown regarding formation of adhesions. Some researchers hypothesize. that although immune cells and inflammatory mediators play a significant role, it is highly likely that there is cross talk between each of them. Even among the same cell type, their role in surgical adhesion formation may differ (e.g. pericardial macrophages may act slightly differently from peritoneal macrophages). Surgery likely causes a disruption of the environment that keeps pro-adhesion formation markers in a homeostatic state, thus causing pathologic formation. However, it is not established whether wound healing and post-surgical adhesion formation have the same cellular and molecular origins [15]. Thus, studies need to be made to evaluate how cells and mediators stay in a homeostatic state that effectively balances their pro- and anti-inflammatory properties under physiologic conditions.

Although the mechanism of adhesion formation is not completely understood, this has not hindered continuous research to come up with ways to prevent adhesions.

Prevention of adhesions

There are many ways that adhesion formation can be reduced. We propose the bundle below to reduce post operative adhesions. The logic behind surgical technique is that all adhesion formation is a response to peritoneal injury. Therefore, minimizing injury by meticulous surgical technique alone will not completely prevent adhesion formation. For complete prevention of adhesion formation, there is always some form of adjuvant will be needed.

Below is a suggested bundle of techniques aimed at minimizing post-operative adhesions.

Bundle 1: attempt MIS/ laparoscopic surgeries whenever possible

Laparoscopic techniques in general have a lower rate of postoperative adhesion formation [16]. Based on an observational study done by Stommel et al. in 2018, adhesions to the incision were present in 78.9% after open and 37.7% after laparoscopic resection. Incidence of abdominal wall adhesions and of any adhesion was significantly higher after open resection [17]. Based on a cohort study published in Lancet, 1.7% of patients who underwent laparoscopic surgery were readmitted for disorders directly related to adhesions, compared with 4·3% of patients in the open surgery cohort over the study span of 2 yrs (June 2009–June 2011). In multivariate analyses, laparoscopy was also shown to reduce the risk of adhesions directly related readmissions by 32%, and of adhesion possibly related readmissions by 11% compared with open surgery. Laparotomies also cause dehydration and increased risk of adhesions from the usage of light, heat, and abrasion from use of dry abdominal drapes [18].

Evidence suggests that the incidence of adhesions at the site of closure after laparotomy is approximately 22% with peritoneal closure and 16% without peritoneal closure [20]. In women with ovarian cancer, closure of pelvic and periaortic peritoneum appears to result in greater adhesion formation than is observed when the dissected areas are left open [21]. However, parietal peritoneal closure at primary caesarean delivery has been observed to yield significantly fewer dense and filmy adhesions [22]

Bundle 3: usage of barriers (mechanical and chemical barriers)

While mechanical barriers for preventing intra-abdominal adhesions are widely utilized with demonstrated efficacy, the evidence base for chemical barriers remains limited, largely due to a scarcity of human trials. Although their current role in clinical practice is minor compared to physical barriers, this section will briefly review the academic literature surrounding chemical adhesion prevention.

Mechanical barriers

Of all mechanical barriers, SEPRAFILM©, and Adept© may arguably be the most used out of all especially in bowel surgery. The concept of mechanical barriers is that they form a biodegradable spacer, with no active ingredients, just keeping the wound surfaces apart during critical phases in peritoneal healing thereby preventing the formation of attachments.

SEPRAFILM© acts as a bioresorbable, hydrophilic adhesion barrier, made of two anionic polysaccharides: modified sodium hyaluronate (HA) and carboxymethylcellulose (CMC)1. It is intended to reduce the incidence, extent, and severity of postoperative adhesions between abdominal wall, omentum and other intra-abdominal organs [23]. In a randomized, prospective, double-blinded, multicenter clinical study involving 183 patients undergoing 2-stage intestinal resection, more than half of the patients treated with SEPRAFILM© were adhesion-free 8–12 weeks following the initial operation compared to 6% of untreated patients [24]. The use of hyaluronate carboxymethylcellulose has also been discussed in a systematic review and meta-analysis to safely reduce clinically relevant consequences of adhesions [2]. However, care must be taken to avoid wrapping SEPRAFILM© around anastomoses as it may increase anastomotic leak and fistula rates [25]. There is evidence suggesting that hyaluronate carboxymethylcellulose reduces the incidence of reoperations for adhesive small bowel obstruction (RR 0·49, 95% CI 0·28–0·88) [26].

Adept© [27] (4% Icodextrin solution) is also a commonly used agent in laparoscopic surgery that has shown safety and efficacy in reduction of adhesion formation. This is further supported by the P.O.P.A trial [28] done by Catena et al. which concludes that usage of Adept© is safe and effective in adhesion reduction. For icodextrin, reoperation for adhesive small bowel obstruction did not differ significantly [25].

Gynecare Interceed © is an alternative made of oxidised regenerated cellulose, aiming to preclude formation of de novo and recurrent adhesions. It has been reported to reduce the incidence and extent of adhesions compared to good surgical technique alone. However, it becomes ineffective in the presence of blood [29]. In one of the recent systematic reviews, it was suggested that oxidised regenerated cellulose reduced the incidence of adhesions (relative risk [RR] 0·51, 95% CI 0·31–0·86) [25].

Liquid barriers such as polyethylene glycol (Spraygel©) have been proven to reduce adhesion formation [30]. Chaturvedi et al. reported that an ultrapure alginate-based antiadhesive barrier gel reduced postoperative adhesion formation in a rat model [31].

Chemical agents

Other than physical barriers, chemical agents actively interfere with peritoneal healing. Medications that are immunosuppressive, such as corticosteroids, or antagonize the fibrin system such as reteplase, have been tested in humas, however there is risk for wound healing problems and bleeding. With increasing knowledge of pathophysiology, newer agents are being developed that are immunomodulating instead of suppressive.

Some research also explored use of corticosteroids in the prevention of postoperative adhesions [32], which so far have proven itself by reducing adhesions in rat models but have not proven itself in human usage thus far. Moreover, corticosteroids should be used cautiously as it could interfere with normal wound healing.

Reduction of intestinal bacterial burden, with either medical means (antibiotics) or mechanical means (bowel preparation), may reduce postoperative adhesions [33]. Intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics also helps to decrease the bacterial burden and are hypothesized to reduce the risk and severity of adhesions [34]. However, there is not strong evidence.

In microsurgeries performed by gynaecologists, frequent intraoperative irrigation was done with heparinized fluid to prevent tissue desiccation and blood clots. This is postulated to contribute to reducing adhesions rate [35].

Pioglitazone, a PPAR-γ agonist, helped reduce postoperative adhesion formation without compromising anastomotic healing in a mouse model. But similarly, its effects for usage in humans still needs to be studied [36].

Statins have shown anti-inflammatory and pro-fibrinolytic properties, and have anti-adhesive properties, but more studies are needed to further assess [37–39]. Use of simvastatin in a rat model also reportedly reduced incidence and severity of peritoneal and small bowel adhesions [40]. Lovastatin and atorvastatin also shows similar benefits, as they appear to increase tPA mRNA levels in peritoneal tissue and tPA activity, leading to increased fibrinolysis [37–39].

Since hypoxia is postulated to contribute to adhesion formation, research have dived into determining whether inhibiting hypoxia-driven pathways can mitigate post-surgical adhesion. Use of HIF-inhibitor in mice showed that disruption of the HIF1-alpha pathway could potentially curtail adhesion formation in a mouse model of peritoneal adhesions [5, 41, 42].

In rat models, selective immunosuppression (eg with anti-IL-1 neutralizing antibodies), appeared to have a significant impact on rates of postoperative peritoneal adhesion formation [43]. Anti-IL-6 receptor monoclonal antibody used in a mouse model also showed reduction in neutrophil recruitment and adhesion formation [44].

EW-7197, a transforming growth factor-β type I receptor kinase inhibitor, was also studied in recent years, and is proven to have therapeutic properties against postsurgical adhesion band formation in mice model [45, 46].

Studies on animal models also showed that intraperitoneal administration of a neutralising antiserum to vascular permeability factor/vascular endothelial growth factor limits postoperative adhesion formation [47]. Studies on humans showed that bevacizumab administration could effectively reduce cardiac adhesion formation after first sternotomy [48].

Studies also show potential benefits of using N-acetylcysteine (NAC) since it’s an antioxidant bearing sulfhydryl groups with excellent free radical scavenging. The generation of harmful ROS is key to postoperative adhesion formation following hypoxic conditions. N-acetylcysteine has been found to be effective in preventing post-operative pericardial adhesions in a rabbit model when applied with a sponge [49].

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase are scavengers of ROS and have been reported to significantly reduced adhesion formation in a rat model [50].

Treatment of adhesions

Resolving adhesion intestinal obstructions may involve either conservative and/or surgical approach, depending on the severity and underlying causes.

Conservative management with bowel decompression using a nasogastric tube can be considered at the start. This allows for the bowel to rest and decompress while providing supportive intravenous fluids to correct electrolyte imbalances and waiting for the obstruction to resolve by itself. Trial of water-soluble contrast can also be considered for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Meta-analyses have shown that WSCS is a safe and effective treatment, which aids in significantly reducing the need for surgery and in the length of hospital stay [51].

Emergency surgery may be necessary to directly visualise extent and possible complications of the obstruction and to allow for adhesiolysis, possibly bowel resection of non-viable segments, to be performed.

Laparoscopic surgery is preferred over laparotomies whenever possible as it is safer, less extensive adhesion (re)formation, earlier return of bowel movements, reduced postoperative pain, and shorter length of stay [52–54]. However, care must be taken in selecting candidates for trial of laparoscopy as laparoscopic operations performed in setting of very distended bowel loops and multiple complex adhesions could increase the risk of enterotomies and result in delayed diagnosis of perforations[55, 56].

Although surgeries performed for intestinal obstructions are done in an emergency setting, some have attempted to find out the role of elective surgeries to be performed in those with adhesions that have not progressed to intestinal obstructions yet. A 2023 study by Van Den Beukel et al. [57]was done to evaluate if elective adhesiolysis in patients suffering from chronic abdominal pain reduces the long-term risk of developing adhesive small bowel obstruction. It concluded that more than one in four patients with chronic adhesion-related pain develop episodes of adhesion intestinal obstruction when treated non-operatively. Elective adhesiolysis reduces the incidence of intestinal obstruction in patients with chronic adhesion-related symptoms.
