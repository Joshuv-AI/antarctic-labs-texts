Fatal granulomatous amoebic encephalitis due to Acanthamoeba sp. with bacterial co-infection in an immunocompetent critically ill patient: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Naila Boukoub, Hassan Amjoud, Salsabil Fahde, Mina ELKhayari, Awatif ELHakkouni, Abdelhamid Hachimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFree-living amoebae are common, ubiquitous environmental saprophytic protozoa that can cause granulomatous encephalitis and other serious neurological infections. Despite being uncommon, these infections primarily affect immunocompromised patients and have a poor prognosis if treatment and diagnosis are delayed.Case presentationWe describe the case of a 48-year-old woman who was admitted to the hospital due to convulsions linked to a persistent fever, right-sided motor deficit, and consciousness issues. She had no prior medical history. An MRI of the brain showed nodular granulomatous lesions. Microorganisms and cystic formations suggestive of an Acanthamoeba infection were found in the cerebrospinal fluid upon parasitological examination. Twenty-four days after beginning sterol-inhibiting derivative treatment, the patient died.ConclusionCentral nervous system infections caused by free-living amoebae are rare and serious; preventive measures can reduce the incidence of these infections.

Introduction

Free-living amoebae (FLA) infections are uncommon and were first described in 1965. Among the many families of free-living amoebae, only a few genera contain species that are pathogenic to humans, including Acanthamoeba spp., Naegleria fowleri, Balamuthia mandrillaris, Sappinia pedata, and Vermamoeba vermiformis [1], [2]. Acanthamoeba and Naegleria are referred to as amphizoic amoebae. The pathogenicity of Leptomyxidae amoebae was also identified in 1990 [1]. Potentially pathogenic free-living amoebae can invade different human tissues and can present as one of the following predominant clinical forms [2], [3], [4], [5]:

• Central nervous system infections (CNS), such as amoebic granulomatous encephalitis (AGE) due to Acanthamoeba spp., Balamuthia mandrillaris, and Sappinia pedata, and primary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM) due to Naegleria fowleri.

• Eye infections, the vast majority of which are caused by Acanthamoeba spp. and amoebic keratitis (AK), although co-infections with Vermamoeba vermiformis have also been reported.

• Disseminated infection, especially systemic Acanthamoeba infection, which may involve multiple organs.

In this study, we present a clinical case of free amoeba infection in a 48-year-old woman and review the literature.

Case presentation

A 48-year-old woman from a rural area with no significant medical history was admitted to the neurology department for neurological disorders. Eight days prior, she experienced neurological deterioration characterized by right hemiparesis and focal motor seizures that were secondarily generalized six days later. These neurological symptoms match with fever and systemic decline. Brain MRI revealed supra- and infratentorial nodular granulomatous lesions with contrast enhancement and meningeal involvement (Fig. 1). The lumbar puncture showed clear cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with elevated protein levels (0.53 g/L), low glucose levels (0.61 g/L; CSF/serum ratio 0.3), and 90 white blood cells/μL (70% lymphocytes). However, India ink staining, culture, and PCR results were negative. Treatment was initiated with fluconazole (400 mg/day) and antiepileptic drugs. Her neurological condition subsequently deteriorated, leading to transfer to the medical intensive care unit (ICU). Upon arrival, the patient was comatose with right hemiparesis and recurrent generalized tonic-clonic seizures. On examination, she was tachypneic (40 breaths /min) with hypoxia (10-liter O2, 90%) and stable hemodynamically, requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation. The thoracoabdominal CT showed bilateral alveolus-inflammatory lung disease with micronodules plus infectious lymphadenopathy. A distal lung sample grew carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, while blood cultures identified methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus. Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis (17,000/µL), lymphopenia (700/µL), and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (295 mg/L). Serologies (HBV, HCV, HIV, toxoplasmosis) and immunology studies (antinuclear, anti-DNA, and anti-neutrophil cytoplasm antibodies, immunoglobulins, complements, and lymphocyte subsets) were within normal limits (Table 1). Referent infectious disease recommended broadening antimicrobial therapy to include fluconazole, metronidazole, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, imipenem, and corticosteroids. Despite this, the patient's condition failed to improve. Subsequent parasitological CSF examination then identified amoeboid organisms with acanthopodia and cysts, confirming Acanthamoeba infection. Discussion

Central nervous system amoebic infections

Ubiquitous in the environment, free-living amoebae find their natural habitat in fresh water, damp soil, and air conditioning systems [6]. Regarded as the most virulent agent, Naegleria fowleri causes primary amoebic meningoencephalitis (PAM) [6]. The capacity of this amoeba to quickly penetrate the central nervous system, mostly through the nose along the olfactory nerve, is what sets it apart. With a higher risk during events including warm, stagnant water [7], young, healthy people are the most at-risk group. Acanthamoeba spp. and B. mandrillaris create more subtle granulomatous encephalitis. They are responsible for some of the most challenging central nervous system (CNS) infections in infectious disease pathology [8]. With their uncommonness, fast progression, and catastrophic fatality exceeding 95% for free-living amoebae CNS infections [9].

These fatal neurological infections are primarily caused by a few free-living amoebae species such as Naegleria fowleri, Acanthamoeba spp., Balamuthia mandrillaris, and Sappinia pedata [2], [4], [10]. Although less common, Vermamoeba vermiformis has also been implicated as a potential pathogen, particularly in coinfections affecting the central nervous system [11]. These species distinguish themselves by their ability to infect immunocompromised patients and cause slower-progressing infections, which further complicates diagnosis [12].

In the present case, the diagnosis of Acanthamoeba infection was confirmed by direct parasitological examination of cerebrospinal fluid, which revealed amoeboid organisms with acanthopodia and cysts. This finding is consistent with the subacute clinical presentation of granulomatous amoebic encephalitis (GAE) observed in our patient, who presented with progressive neurological decline over several weeks. Despite the absence of overt immunosuppression as documented by normal immunological parameters.

Invasion mechanisms

These amoebas employ complex molecular systems for neurologic intrusion. The rapid progression of Naegleria fowleri as well as its use of cytotoxic proteases to quickly alter brain tissue characterizes it [10]. Traversing along the olfactory nerve, it arrives in the central nervous system in only a few days [13]. Acanthamoeba spp. use sophisticated techniques like growing resistant cysts and surviving inside immune cells. This flexibility lets it survive in the body and bypass immune defense mechanisms [3]. In our patient, the presence of Acanthamoeba cysts in the cerebrospinal fluid likely reflects the capacity of this amoeba to form resistant cystic forms, enabling it to evade host immune defenses and persist within the central nervous system. Although cell-to-cell contact seems to be required for this process and the organism may have an affinity for binding to the extracellular matrix [8], the pathogenesis of Balamuthia mandrillaris is not yet well understood. In people, nevertheless, the inhalation or inoculation of B. mandrillaris cysts or trophozoites across the skin seems to start an infection. These creatures then seek organs, mostly the brain and skin, where the disease advances by direct tissue migration or circulation. One hypothesis for a potential infection pathway from a mouse model of Balamuthia encephalitis is that the pathogen adheres to the nasal epithelium after inhalation before moving along the olfactory nerve to reach the central nervous system [14].

Clinical features and diagnosis

The first symptoms of amoebic meningoencephalitis often mimic bacterial or viral meningitis, including high fever, intense headaches, meningeal stiffness, and quick neurological impairment. Naegleria fowleri causes a fulminant Primary Amoebic Meningoencephalitis (PAM), leading to coma and death within a few days. In contrast, Acanthamoeba and Balamuthia mandrillaris cause a more insidious Granulomatous Amoebic Encephalitis (GAE), with progressive headaches, neurological deficits, and focal signs. Acanthamoeba can also cause keratitis in contact lens wearers and disseminated infections [1], [8], [9].

Amoebic diseases are mostly identified by microscopical analysis, culture, molecular biology, serology, and analysis of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Microscopic identification of motile trophozoites in fresh CSF is crucial for Primary Amoebic Meningoencephalitis (PAM), supplemented by a flagellation test for N. fowleri [13]. For Granulomatous Amoebic Encephalitis (GAE), brain or skin biopsy is essential; histopathological stains such as May–Grünwald–Giemsa (MGG) and hematoxylin–eosin–saffron (HES) reveal trophozoites and cysts. Acanthamoeba cysts are double-walled and star-shaped, while Balamuthia cysts are larger and spherical, requiring immunolabeling for definitive differentiation [8], [14]. PCR targeting 18S rDNA or 16S mitochondrial genes is the most sensitive method for species-specific detection in CSF or tissue samples [15]. CSF analysis typically shows neutrophilic pleocytosis and low glucose in PAM and lymphocytic predominance in Acanthamoeba meningitis. Serology is less useful for PAM but can aid in diagnosing GAE [14]. In our patient, CSF analysis revealed lymphocytic predominance (70% lymphocytes) with elevated protein levels and low glucose, which is consistent with the typical profile described for Acanthamoeba meningitis. Regarding diagnostic confirmation, direct microscopic examination of CSF allowed identification of Acanthamoeba trophozoites and cysts. PCR was negative, possibly due to low parasite load or PCR inhibitors. Culture on non-nutrient agar was attempted but unsuccessful, likely because anti-amoebic therapy had already been initiated. Brain biopsy was not performed due to the patient's unstable condition, and post-mortem examination was not authorized by the family. Nevertheless, the presence of cysts in CSF is considered highly suggestive of Acanthamoeba infection in the appropriate clinical context.

Treatment and perspectives

Because of the quick progression of the illness and the intrinsic resistance of free pathogenic amoebae to traditional therapies, treatment of amoebic meningoencephalitis presents a major clinical challenge. Early administration of a combination therapy comprising amphotericin B, rifampicin, azithromycin, fluconazole, miltefosine, and dexamethasone in the case of Naegleria fowleri has rare cases of survival if therapy is rapidly started after the appearance of symptoms [16]. Still, mortality rates above 95% mostly result from late diagnosis and a lack of awareness. For Acanthamoeba and Balamuthia mandrillaris encephalitis, the approach also relies on a combination of antibiotics (pentamidine, sulfadiazine, fluconazole, and miltefosine), sometimes given empirically with unusual and unpredictable therapeutic success [8].

Researchers are looking for fresh molecular targets and repurposing current medications. Though its effectiveness varies depending on the species involved, miltefosine, an anti-leishmanial medicine, is a rather good example. It works by causing apoptosis in trophozoites and breaking up cell membranes. Some Acanthamoeba and Balamuthia strains, nevertheless, display partial natural resistance to this molecule, underscoring the need for more targeted pharmaceutical research [3]. Additionally, a top priority is the creation of treatments aimed at cysts, especially resistant forms, which sometimes evade most traditional therapies and help to explain the chronicity and reoccurrence of infections [8]. Genomic and transcriptomic techniques have produced notable advancements. Several proteins critical for survival of the Balamuthia mandrillaris have been discovered by analysis of its transcriptome, including enzymes engaged in energy metabolism, transport proteins, and heat stress proteins. New drug targets might come from these proteins [17]. The structure-guided approach to medicine design paves the way for precision medicine in the treatment of these neglected diseases. The absence of consistent animal models and the paucity of clinical cases present significant hurdles to the exacting assessment of novel substances, nevertheless. An international joint project is required for the centralization of cases, standardization of procedures, and quick validation of possible therapies. In our patient, despite the early initiation of combination therapy including fluconazole, metronidazole, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, and corticosteroids, the clinical course was rapidly fatal, highlighting the limited efficacy of current treatment options for Acanthamoeba encephalitis.

Polymicrobial infections involving free-living amoebae and bacterial pathogens have been previously documented. A recent case series by Shobhana et al. reported two cases of Acanthamoeba meningoencephalitis, one of which was associated with Escherichia-coli co-infection [18]. Notably, Acanthamoeba is known to act as a co-host for several bacteria, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and endosymbiosis between amoebae and bacteria may increase their virulence [18]. In our patient, the coexistence of Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus likely contributed to the rapid progression to refractory septic shock.

Conclusion

Central nervous system infections caused by free-living amoebae are rare and serious. The diagnosis of Acanthamoeba encephalitis can be challenging, particularly when PCR results are negative. Here, direct microscopic examination of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was crucial for identifying trophozoites and cysts, enabling a rapid diagnosis despite the absence of positive PCR. However, diagnostic confirmation was limited by the unavailability of brain biopsy and post-mortem examination. This may be because they are often misdiagnosed or not diagnosed at all, which highlights the importance of choosing the right samples and diagnostic methods. The fatal outcome was likely exacerbated by polymicrobial infection, underscoring the potential impact of bacterial co-infection on prognosis in these already severe infections. Nevertheless, raising public awareness and implementing preventive measures can reduce the incidence of these infections.
