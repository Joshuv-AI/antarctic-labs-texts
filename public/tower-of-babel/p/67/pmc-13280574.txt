Early-life rotavirus infection susceptibility and later gastrointestinal cancer protection: Reverse antagonistic pleiotropy and potential vaccine benefits
Authors: Lúa Castelo-Martínez, Jacobo Pardo-Seco, Alberto Gómez-Carballa, Sara Pischedda, Xabier Bello, Alba Camino-Mera, Federico Martinón-Torres, Antonio Salas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Whole-exome sequencing in severe rotavirus cases identified seven candidate SNPs, several linked to gastrointestinal cancer pathways.•Transcriptomic and epigenomic data supported functional involvement of top candidate genes and suggested roles in cancer progression.•Polygenic risk score analyses indicated reduced gastrointestinal cancer risk among individuals with severe early-life RV infection.•Evidence of recent positive selection in key SNPs supports a model of reverse antagonistic pleiotropy, suggesting early RV susceptibility may confer later-life cancer protection.Keywords: Rotavirus, Exomes, Sequencing, Susceptibility, Gastric cancer, Antagonistic pleiotropy, Vaccines

Introduction

Rotavirus (RV) is a leading cause of severe gastroenteritis in infants and young children worldwide (Dennehy, 2012), causing severe acute diarrhea in children under five years of age and, accounting for approximately 128,500 deaths annually (Troeger et al., 2018), which represents about 5 % of deaths in this age group (Dennehy, 2012; Dennehy, 2015; Tate et al., 2012). Over 85 % of these fatalities occur in countries across Africa and Asia (Haider et al., 2019). Clinical signs range from brief watery diarrhea to severe diarrhea with vomiting and fever, potentially leading to dehydration, shock, electrolyte imbalance, and, in extreme cases, death. Less common signs include malaise and, rarely, neurologic complications such as convulsions, encephalitis, or encephalopathy (Esona and Gautam, 2015; LeClair and McConnell, 2024).

Two orally administered vaccines, Rotarix and RotaTeq, have shown efficacy rates of 72–100 % in high- and middle-income countries of America, Asia, and Europe, but only 49–72 % in low-income countries of Africa and Asia, highlighting the ongoing disparities in access (Dennehy, 2008; Glass et al., 2006). Global vaccine coverage remains around 59 %, with 131 countries introducing the vaccine into their routine infant immunization by the end of 2024 (Abou-Nader et al., 2018; Clark et al., 2023; Tate et al., 2010; World Health Organization, 2025).

Despite the severity of RV infection, little is known about how the host genome influences susceptibility. Most studies have examined transcriptomic responses to infection and vaccination. Salas et al. (Salas et al., 2016) reported roles for genes involved in cell cycle, chromatin condensation, and immune response; while Gómez-Carballa et al. (Gomez-Carballa et al., 2020) found vaccine-induced overexpression of genes associated with gastrointestinal disease, inflammation, and immune activation. However, no study has comprehensively assessed host genomic susceptibility to RV infection.

Certain pathogens exhibit oncolytic or immunomodulatory properties that suppress tumor growth. For example, oncolytic viruses such as reovirus (including RV), adenovirus, and herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) selectively replicate in and lyse cancer cells, while simultaneously stimulating anti-tumor immune responses (Lichty et al., 2014; Russell et al., 2012). Thus, certain infections can induce lasting alterations in host physiology, modulating immune surveillance, cellular signaling, and the tissue microenvironment in ways that influence tumor development (Hoption Cann et al., 2006; Javid et al., 2023; Newman and Zloza, 2017; Zhu et al., 2017). These effects may be dualistic, either promoting tumorigenesis or enhancing the detection and elimination of transformed cells. For instance, oncogenic pathogens such as Helicobacter pylori, human papillomavirus (HPV), hepatitis B and C viruses (HBV, HCV), and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) are directly linked to gastric, cervical, liver, and nasopharyngeal cancers, respectively. They contribute through chronic inflammation, insertional mutagenesis, inhibition of tumor suppressor pathways, and genomic instability (El-Serag, 2012; Young and Rickinson, 2004; zur Hausen, 2002), Supplementary Text.

In this study, we investigate the genetic susceptibility of children to RV infection using Whole-Exome Sequencing (WES). Because our top candidate Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) and genes are also associated with gastrointestinal cancers, particularly gastric cancer, we explored whether early-life RV infection may influence cancer risk in adulthood. To complement WES, we adopted a multi-omics approach to these variants correlate with gene expression and DNA methylation in RV-infected samples. Analysis of gastric cancer datasets enables us to assess epigenetic and transcriptional changes contributing to cancer susceptibility. This integrative strategy highlights genomic factors associated with RV infection and their potential implications for cancer prevention and therapy.

Materials & methods

Sampling

A cohort of RV-infected children admitted to the hospital due to moderate or severe symptoms was prospectively collected between 2013 and 2014 at the Hospital Clínico Universitario of Santiago de Compostela (Galicia, Western Spain).

Written informed consent was obtained from all Galician donors before the research. The rights of participants were safeguarded during the research, and their identities were protected. The study complies with all applicable Spanish regulations, including the Biomedical Research Act (14/2007-3 of July), the Autonomy of the Patient Act (41/2002), Decree SAS/3470/2009 for observational studies, and the Data Protection Act (15/1999). The experimental protocol was approved by the Galician Ethics Committee (registration code: 2012/301).

Sequencing

We followed the sequencing procedure described previously (Camino-Mera et al., 2024). DNA was extracted from blood samples using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega). Sample concentrations were determined using fluorometric quantification via the Qubit system, while purity was evaluated through spectrophotometry using NanoDrop. DNA integrity was assessed with TapeStation. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 100 bp) of the SureSelectXT libraries, previously enriched, indexed, and multiplexed, was conducted on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc.). The quality of the FastQ files was analyzed using FastQC v0.11.9 (Brown et al., 2017), and alignment was performed with BWA 0.7.17-r1188 (Li, 2013) employing the BWA-MEM algorithm against the human reference genome 38 (HG38). Mapping quality was assessed using Mosdepth 0.3.2 (Pedersen and Quinlan, 2018), Bamtools 2.5.2 (Barnett et al., 2011) and Samtools 1.15 (Li et al., 2009) with MultiQC 1.12 collector (Ewels et al., 2016) for summary metrics. Bam files were processed following Gatk 4.2.5.0 best practices (McKenna et al., 2010) and annotated with the CADD database GRCh38-v1.6.

Statistical analysis

We removed indels, duplications, monomorphic and triallelic variants, and set a genotyping rate threshold below 95 %.

To explore potential familial relationships and genome ancestry of RV donors, we used population reference datasets from The 1000 Genome Project (http://www.1000genomes.org; hereafter 1000G), selecting those representing four major continental regions: a) African: Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria (YRI), Esan in Nigeria (ESN), and Mende in Sierra Leone (MSL), b) European: Iberian population in Spain (IBS), British in England and Scotland (GBR), Utah residents with Northern and Western European ancestry (CEU), and Tuscany in Italy (TSI); c) East Asian: Southern Han Chinese (CHS), Chinese Dai in Xishuangbanna from China (CDX); and Japanese in Tokyo (JPT); and mixed American: Peruvians from Lima (PEL), Colombians from Medellin (CLM), and individuals of Mexican ancestry from Los Angeles USA (MXL); Fig. 1. To assess potential familial relationships within the RV cohort, we generated a pairwise kinship matrix based on genotype data and visualized it as a kinship plot in R (http://www.r-project.org), following established methods (Gómez-Carballa et al., 2013). We used ADMIXTURE (Alexander et al., 2009) to perform a maximum likelihood estimation of individual ancestries. We computed Identity-By-State (IBS) values to quantify genotype similarity at each locus and constructed a Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) in R (Mardia et al., 1979) to detect outliers and facilitated visualization of the ancestral background.

For the association analysis, we used a cohort of healthy controls (HC) recruited in Galicia (the GALOMICs dataset; n=98) matched with cases by geographic origin and ancestry, which basically overlaps the description in Pardo-Seco et al. for this database (Pardo-Seco et al., 2025a; Pardo-Seco et al., 2025b). Additionally, a validation association test was conducted using control population datasets of European ancestry from the 1000G. Specifically, we used data from IBS, GBR, TSI, and CEU.

Three different association analyses were performed: i) a single-point Fisher’s exact test for allelic association, ii) genotype-based association analysis, and iii) gene-based association analysis. Allele and genotypic statistical association tests were conducted only for variants with Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) >0.05 in HC and Hardy-Weinberg P-value >0.001 for both case and HC cohorts (Fig. 2). The genotypic test was performed in PLINK using the –model flag, which applies a 3 × 2 chi-squared to assesses whether genotype distributions (e.g., AA, AB, BB) at a given SNP differ significantly between cases and HC, without assuming a specific inheritance model. The genomic inflation factor (lambda λ) was calculated as the ratio of the empirical median of the observed chi-squared distribution to the expected median under the null hypothesis (Devlin and Roeder, 1999). For the genotype-based association, we used the CADD-weighted Sequence Kernel Association Test (SKAT) (Wu et al., 2011) to evaluate the cumulative effect of multiple variants within a gene on a phenotype. Only genes containing at least two variants were considered in this analysis (Table 1).

PLINK v1.9 (Purcell et al., 2007) was used to perform single-point analyses. For the graphic representations and most computations, R statistical software was used. The SKAT R package was used to compute the SKAT gene-based association test.

We employed DisGeNET (Piñero et al., 2017), MalaCards (Rappaport et al., 2017), and PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) to gather information regarding the best SNP and gene candidates and connections with the phenotypes of interest.

Given the association analysis results linking RV susceptibility variation to gastric and other gastrointestinal cancers, we computed the Polygenic Risk Score (PRS) for various cancers in the RV-infected patients and HC. We queried the PRS Catalog (https://www.pgscatalog.org/) using the term ‘Digestive System Carcinoma,’ which broadly encompasses all gastrointestinal cancers. Most PRS-associated variants are located in non-coding regions, leading to a reduced overlap with the variants identified in WES studies. To mitigate this limitation and ensure sufficient SNPs were represented in the RV exomes, we selected PRS models comprising large variant sets (>100K), increasing the likelihood of capturing relevant variants within our exome data. Additionally, we restricted our selection to PRS derived from populations of European ancestry to match the ancestry of our RV cases. Based on this criteria, we selected six PRS related to gastrointestinal cancers: a) Pancreatic cancer (6,351,686 variants; 80,904 retained in RV exomes (Hu et al., 2024)), b) Colon cancer (1,119,238 variants; 39,396 retained (Thomas et al., 2020)), c) Oral cavity cancer (1,119,238 variants, 37,981 retained (Fritsche et al., 2020)), d) Esophageal cancer (356,743 variants, 37,158 retained (Fritsche et al., 2020)), e) D12 Benign neoplasm of the colon, rectum, anus and anal canal (BNCRA; 1,059,939 variants, 37,732 retained (Jung et al., 2024)), and f) Malignant neoplasm of rectum, rectosigmoid junction, and anus (MNRRA; 1,104,018 variants; 37,065 retained (Fritsche et al., 2020)). The PRS catalog does not currently include a suitable PRS for gastric cancer. To assess differences in PRS distributions between cases and HC, we conducted a Wilcoxon test. PRS calculations were performed using PLINK.

We investigated signals of recent positive selection associated with the three most significant SNPs by examining extended haplotype patterns across the chromosomes where these variants are located. First, we used Beagle v.5.5, to phase the haplotypes (Browning et al., 2021) and impute sporadic missing genotypes (Browning et al., 2018), and then, we used the rehh package (Gautier et al., 2017) to analyze the extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) define as the probability that two areas determined by the ancestral and derived alleles at a focal SNP are identical by descent over a given distance surrounding it (Sabeti et al., 2002). Therefore, EHH evaluates how far a stretch of DNA remains unchanged (without recombination) around a selected allele; a high EHH value extending over a long distance suggests that the allele has rapidly increased in frequency due to positive selection, not allowing enough time for recombination to break down the haplotype. To measure this probability, we used two complementary statistics with the corresponding -Log10 P-values: i) the ratio between populations (RSB) based on the comparison of the EHH patterns of the same allele between two populations (Sabeti et al., 2007), and ii) the cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH) that evaluated the Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) decay across the region and EHH values (Sabeti et al., 2007) comparing RV and HC samples, to detect different selection pressures between them.

RNAseq data from RV-infected patients

We used previously published RNA-seq data (Jackson et al., 2023) to investigate the expression of candidate genes identified in our genomic study. The dataset includes transcriptomic profiles from febrile children with viral and bacterial infections, as well as HC. We filtered the dataset to retain only transcriptomes from RV-infected patients (n=5) and European HC (n=45). After removing lowly expressed genes, raw RNA-seq data were normalized using both the DESeq2 (Love et al., 2014) and RUVSeq (Risso et al., 2014). Reference genes for the RUVg function were selected by identifying genes with stable expression (adjusted P-value>0.1) in a naïve differential expression analysis comparing RV-infected patients with HC.

To increase the sample size of the RV group, we integrated the RV and European HC samples from Jackson et al. (Jackson et al., 2023) with previously published RNA-seq data Gómez-Carballa et al. (Gomez-Carballa et al., 2020), which also included RV-infected patients (n=6) and HC (n=13). Lowly expressed genes were filtered out separately in each dataset, and only commonly retained genes were used for the data integration. Batch effects between datasets were corrected using the COMBAT-Seq R package (Zhang et al., 2020), with HC samples serving as a reference. The merged dataset was then processed following the same normalization and correction procedures applied to the Jackson et al. (Jackson et al., 2023) dataset.

To correct for potential confounding effects due to age and sex differences between comparison groups, we normalized data using the RemoveBatcheffect function from the limma R package (Ritchie et al., 2015).

To assess statistical significance between severity groups, we computed a Wilcoxon test. A detailed summary of the samples included in the analysis is provided in Table S1.

Gene expression data from gastric cancer

Microarray gene expression data from gastric cancer samples were downloaded from the public repository Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) public repository under accession numbers: GSE26899 (Illumina HumanHT-12 V3.0; n=12 Normal tissue [NT] and n=96 gastric cancer tissue [GT] from different locations), GSE79973 (Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0; n=10 NT and n=10 GT) and GSE208099 (Agilent SurePrint G3 Human GE v3; n=16 NT and n=16 GT).

Raw datasets were pre-processed and normalized separately using the limma R package (Ritchie et al., 2015) for Illumina® and Agilent® microarray data and the Oligo R package (Carvalho and Irizarry, 2010) for the Affymetrix® dataset. When applicable, batch effects and sex-related variability were corrected using the RemoveBatcheffect function from the limma package. Statistical significance between severity groups was computed using a Wilcoxon test. All graphics were created in R (www.r-project.org) using the ggplot2 R package (Wickham, 2016). The 7-gene signature score was calculated as standardized z-scores using the zscore method implemented in the GSVA R package (Hänzelmann et al., 2013).

DNA methylation data from gastric cancer

DNA methylation data for gastric cancer were obtained from the epigenomic study by Huang et al. (Huang et al., 2018) and downloaded from GEO (accession number: GSE103186). All samples were hybridized to the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450K BeadChip. The dataset includes 108 samples collected from patient biopsies, encompassing both metaplastic and normal gastric tissue, and sampled from three distinct gastric regions: antrum (n=76), cardia (n=23), and body (n=9). To provide reference profiles, normal gastric samples were also analyzed from these regions, with 39 from the antrum, 11 from the cardia, and 11 from the body. Additionally, 22 cases of mild intestinal metaplasia in the antrum were included. The total number of samples in this dataset is 191. Raw data were downloaded, filtered, and normalized using the minfi R package (Aryee et al., 2014).

Pathways analysis

An over-representation analysis (ORA) was carried out with the seven candidate genes using the ClusterProfiler R package (Xu et al., 2024) and the Biological Processes terms from GO (Gene Ontology) as the reference database. Gsva method included in the Gene Set Variation Analysis or GSVA R package (Hänzelmann et al., 2013) was employed to calculate the pathway activity of the significant biological processes (involving >1 candidate gene; n=12) derived from the ORA in the RV merged dataset and in the gastric cancer datasets (from both gene expression and DNA methylation data).

Predictive signatures

To evaluate the predictive power of the significant SNPs and associated genes, two PRS were calculated to assess differential genetic risk among individuals with RV and HC. The first PRS was derived from a comprehensive set of 92,096 SNPs identified in the exomes of both RV and HC. The second PRS was restricted to a refined subset comprising only the significant SNPs associated with the RV phenotype, specifically, 18 SNPs in total: 6 within BCAT1, 7 within A4GNT, and 5 additional SNPs mapping to other significant loci. To ensure unbiased estimation, a Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) approach was employed. For each genome, the PRS was computed by training the model on all other individuals in the dataset, iteratively repeating this process until every subject’s PRS had been estimated. This procedure was applied independently to both the RV and HC groups.

The predictive potential of the candidate genes retained following data processing was assessed using gene expression data (from RV and gastric cancer datasets) and DNA methylation data (from the gastric cancer dataset). Predictive models were constructed using a generalized linear model (GLM). Model performance was evaluated by calculating the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (CI) with the pROC package (Robin et al., 2011).

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

A total of 23 patients under 4 years of age (ranging from 1.3 to 40.6 months) underwent exome sequencing in this study. Among these, 57 % were female and 43 % were male. Two patients had a history of prematurity, and none had received the RV vaccine. The primary reason for hospital admission was gastroenteritis. The condition was classified as severe in 30 % of cases, moderate in 48 %, and mild in 22 %, with an average hospital stay of 5.5 days (ranging from 1 to 14 days). This distribution mirrors the clinical burden of RV infections, with nearly one-third of the cases presenting with severe manifestations. No significant adverse outcomes or mortality were reported at discharge (Table 1).

Description of genome variation

A total of 107,074 biallelic SNPs were identified after filtering out indels, no-biallelic positions, monomorphic SNPs, and variants with genotyping rate below 95 %. For population stratification analysis, the RV dataset was merged with the HC population datasets, resulting in 106,820 coincident variants. After filtering and merging, 93,690 variants were selected for downstream analysis.

A family relationship analysis revealed two samples consisting of a sibling relationship; consequently, the sample with the highest missing rate was removed (Fig. 1A). Additionally, MDS and a genome-ancestry analysis were conducted to visualize the ancestral background of the samples (Fig. 1B, 1C). The average European ancestry component was 88.9 %, except in two samples that exhibited non-European ancestry backgrounds. These two samples showed low European component, 7 % and 70 %, and significant American ancestry components of 63 % and 17 %, respectively (Fig. 1C). To ensure genetic ancestral homogeneity in both the case and HC groups, these two samples were excluded from the final analysis.

We conducted a genotypic test only on the best SNP candidates in the allele association test. All of them surpassed the Bonferroni threshold: rs2046524 (P-value=8 × 10-14, gene=BCAT1); rs4304220 (P-value=9 × 10-13, gene=CLDN4); rs2724692 (P-value=7 × 10-12, gene=A4GNT); rs9379 (P-value=3 × 10-11, gene=CKAP2); rs9395327 (P-value=3 × 10-9, gene=PTCHD4); rs10929938 (P-value=7 × 10-10, gene=TANC1); and rs4902046 (P-value=2 × 10-6, gene=PRKCH); Table 2.

Gene-level analysis conducted using SKAT initially identified 26,535 genes. After applying a filter requiring each gene to contain at least two variants, the list was refined to 16,830 genes. Two genes passed the Bonferroni correction threshold, namely, BCAT1 (P-value=9 × 10-13) and A4GNT (P-value=3 × 10-9); Table 2.

To further investigate disease associations of the candidate SNPs and genes, the MalaCards human disease database (Rappaport et al., 2017) was used to examine links between the top candidate genes (two of which [BCAT1 and A4GNT] ranked among the top three in both allelic and gene-based association analyses, and one [CLDN4] identified through allelic analysis alone) and known human conditions. A striking pattern emerged: gastrointestinal system cancers repeatedly surfaced as top-ranked diseases across all three genes, suggesting a compelling oncogenic connection. For BCAT1, gastric cancer ranked 15th out of 178 associated diseases, while CLDN4 showed even stronger associations, with gastric cancer ranked 2nd out of 192, and pancreatic cancer 7th. The most interesting observation was observed for A4GNT, with pancreatic cancer ranking 1st among associated diseases. Other notable associations for A4GNT included gastric adenocarcinoma (3rd), gastric cancer (6th), pancreatic intraductal papillary-colloid carcinoma (7th), and even pancreatic and cerebellar agenesis (9th). Colorectal cancer also appeared prominently in the data, further reinforcing the potential role of these genes in gastrointestinal oncogenesis.

Upon querying the DisGeNET database, and in agreement with MalaCards findings, we confirm that all the top candidate SNPs and genes have previously been associated with cancer, particularly with gastric cancer. This connection opens a new line of investigation, prompting us to further explore the potential link between RV infection and cancer susceptibility.

To deepen this investigation, we computed PRSs for various gastrointestinal cancers in our cohort of RV-infected patients and HC (Fig. 2C). All the SNPs identified as significant in the allelic test were included in all PRSs, except those in the TANC1 gene, which was not represented in the PRS models for pancreatic, esophageal, rectum or colon cancer, indicating a potential role of these variants in modulating the risk of different gastrointestinal cancers. Our dataset consisted of a total of 113,797 coding variants (including biallelic SNPs and indels), with a genotypic rate of 0.99. The PRS for pancreatic cancer was significantly lower in RV-infected cases than in HC (P-value=0.01). Other PRSs also showed lower values in cases than in HC, though statistical significance was marginal for esophageal cancer (P-value=0.07), colon cancer (P-value=0.10), and MNRRA (P-value=0.10). In contrast, PRS was not statistically significant for oral cavity cancer (P-value=0.62) or BNCRA (P-value=0.93). Collectively, the consistently lower PRS values observed in RV-infected individuals suggest that their genetic background may confer a protective effect against specific gastrointestinal cancers, relative to those observed in the general population (HC).

Natural selection signature on candidate SNPs

Focusing on the top three candidate SNPs, rs2046524, rs2724692, and rs4304220, we assessed selection pressure to detect positive selection sweeps by analyzing differences in EHH around the SNPs of interest between RV cases and HC (Fig. 3). A one-sided analysis was conducted (as currently done in the literature, e.g. (Ren et al., 2025; Si et al., 2024)), focusing on the direction of allele frequency changes and identifying whether the SNPs of interest ranked within the top 5 % of the EHH distribution across the chromosome.

For chromosome 12 (6,291 SNPs), rs2046524 ranked within the top 5 % in both RSB and XP-EHH statistics, with negative values indicating a signal of recent positive selection in the RV cohort (RSB=–1.73, -Log10 P-value=1.38; XP-EHH=–3.67, -Log10 P-value=3.92); Fig. 3A. Other SNPs within the same gene, BCAT1, also showed significant values, suggesting a selective sweep potentially acting across the entire gene. This observation is consistent with previous findings suggesting that susceptibility to RV may be influenced not just by this SNP, but by BCAT1 more broadly. A similar pattern was observed for rs2724692 on chromosome 3 (5,777 SNPs), which also ranked in the top 5 %, but in the opposite direction, with positive values suggesting a recent selective event in the HC population (RSB=6.65, -Log10 P-value=10.84; XP-EHH=3.28, -Log10 P-value=3.29); Fig. 3B. In contrast, rs4304220 on chromosome 7 (6,243 SNPs) showed borderline significance: it was significant for RSB (RSB=3.55, -Log10 P-value=3.71) and near-significant for XP-EHH (XP-EHH=1.33, -Log10 P-value=1.04), also with positive values pointing to recent positive selection in the HC group (Fig. 3B).

Gene expression in acute rotavirus infection and gastric cancer

Gene expression profiles from children admitted to the hospital with confirmed RV infection were investigated, focusing on the expression of candidate genes (A4GNT, BCAT1, CKAP2, CLDN4, PTCHD4, PRKCH, TANC1). Principal component analysis (PCA) of whole blood transcriptome data clearly showed that RV infection induces distinct transcriptomic changes that differentiate RV-infected children from HC (Fig. 4A). This pattern was consistently observed in both the dataset from (Jackson et al., 2023) and the merged cohort (Gomez-Carballa et al., 2020; Jackson et al., 2023). Three candidate genes (A4GNT, CLDN4, and PTCHD4) did not pass the low-expression filtering threshold in either dataset and were therefore excluded from further analysis. Among the remaining genes, PRKCH and TANC1 did not significantly differ in expression between RV-infected patients and HC. However, a significant up-regulation associated with RV infection was observed for genes BCAT1 (P-value=0.0038) and CKAP2 (P-value=0.018) in the Jackson et al. (Jackson et al., 2023) dataset. In the merged cohort, these expression changes remained significant for BCAT1 (P-value=0.0025), confirming its up-regulation in RV-infected samples.

To further investigate the potential link between these candidate genes and cancer, we studied their expression in gastric adenocarcinoma tissue samples using data from three independent microarray gene expression datasets downloaded from GEO (GSE26899, GSE79973, and GSE208099); Fig. 4B and Fig. S1. The GSE26899 dataset included samples from different gastric tumor regions. A global comparison of the seven-candidate gene expression between gastric tumor and normal tissue samples across the three datasets was conducted using a z-score signature calculation. The results indicated a general trend of overexpression in gastric tumor samples compared to normal tissue samples, which reached statistical significance in two out of three datasets (GSE26899: P-value=0.0051, GSE79973: P-value=0.0021); Fig. S1.

Examining individual gene expression patterns, CLDN4 exhibited a consistent significant up-regulation in gastric tumors with respect to normal tissue samples in GSE26899 (P-value=0.004) and GSE208099 (P-value=0.043). Although the P-value of CLDN4 in GSE79973 did not reach the threshold for statistical significance (P-value=0.052), it was borderline (Fig. 4B). Similarly, CKAP2 was significantly upregulated in gastric tumor samples in two out of three datasets (GSE26899: P-value=0.0024; GSE208099: P-value=9.1 × 10-7), whereas BCAT1 was significantly overexpressed only in GSE79973 (P-value=7.3 × 10-4). In contrast to other candidate genes, PRKCH exhibited a reverse expression pattern, showing lower expression in gastric tumor samples compared to normal tissue samples, but the difference was statistically significant only in the GSE208099 dataset. While the P-value for PRKCH in this dataset did not reach statistical significance, it was close to the threshold (P-value=0.055); Fig. 4B.

Over-representation analysis of the seven candidate genes identified 209 pathways containing at least one of these genes (Table S2). Interestingly, top pathways (adjusted P-value < 0.05) were all related to cell junctions: ‘bicellular tight junction assembly’, ‘tight junction assembly’, ‘apical junction assembly’, and ‘tight junction organization’; all of them involving CLDN4 and PRKCH genes (Table S2). Considering only biological routes with more than one of the candidate genes implicated (n=12; Fig. 4C), and after recalculating the adjusted P-value, we found that the most significant pathway involving the highest number of candidate genes was ‘cell junction organization’ (CLDN4, PRKCH, and TANC1 genes). Two other significant pathways related to the mitotic cell cycle arise from the ORA analysis and are associated with BCAT1 and CKAP2 genes.

Functional activation profiles of these pathways in blood samples from RV acute infection and in gastric cancer tissues were explored through GSVA. This analysis showed statistical differences in pathway activity between RV patients and HC for all pathways (Fig. 4D). RV infection leads to significant under-regulation of pathways related to cell junctions, skin development, and epidermis development, and, conversely, an upregulation of mitotic cell cycle-related pathways. Interestingly, the activation patterns observed in tumoral and normal tissue from the gastric adenocarcinoma datasets GSE79973 and GSE208099 precisely mirror those seen in RV and HC, suggesting common underlying mechanisms between the two conditions despite involving different tissues. However, no statistically significant differences between tumoral and normal tissue were observed for the GSE26899 dataset (Fig. 4D). This lack of statistical significance is most likely due to the different tumor types and locations/samples considered in this dataset. When analyzing only samples from gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST), the same activation patterns emerged (Fig. 4D). GISTs are the most common mesenchymal tumors of the gastrointestinal tract.

DNA methylation of candidate genes in gastric cancer samples

The DNA methylation analysis of gastric cancer samples focused on CpG sites associated with the five candidate genes (A4GNT, BCAT1, CLDN4, PRKCH, and TANC1), identified from the genomic association analysis. A total of 56 CpG sites exhibited significant methylation differences between metaplastic and normal tissues across the three gastric regions (antrum, body, and cardia; Table 3; Table S3). In the antrum, several CpGs displayed significant methylation changes between metaplasia and normal tissue. Notably, a CpG site associated with A4GNT showed a distinct methylation pattern in metaplastic tissue compared to both mild metaplasia and normal tissue. BCAT1 showed the most extensive differential methylation, with 25 CpGs significant, 17 of which also distinguished metaplasia from mild metaplasia. Similarly, five CpGs annotated to CLDN4 demonstrated significant differences between metaplasia and normal tissue. For PRKCH, nine CpGs were differentially methylated, including one that also distinguished metaplasia from mild metaplasia. TANC1 exhibited significant methylation differences in 11 CpG sites, with three also differentiating mild metaplasia and metaplasia. Only two CpG sites within BCAT1 and one within TANC1 were significantly methylated between mild metaplasia and normal antrum tissue.

Predictive value of the best signature

The candidate SNPs and genes collectively exhibit strong predictive capacity for distinguishing RV patients from HC. The mean PRS in RV samples was 2.09 when calculated using all available SNPs, and 1.79 when restricted to the significant SNPs within the candidate genes. In contrast, HC displayed markedly lower PRS values: –0.42 using all SNPs and –0.36 using the prioritized SNPs and genes (Fig. 6A). This consistent difference across models highlights the discriminatory potential of the identified variants.

In parallel with genomic findings, the expression profiles of the seven candidate genes also demonstrated strong predictive power. In the context of RV infection, a subset of these genes (TANC1, BCAT1, PRKCH, and CKAP2) effectively distinguished infected samples from HC, yielding an AUC of 0.80 (95 % confidence interval [CI]: 0.63–0.98). Similarly, across multiple gastric cancer datasets, the full set of candidate genes (TANC1, BCAT1, PRKCH, CKAP2, CLDN4, A4GNT, and PTCHD4) consistently distinguished tumor tissue from adjacent normal tissue with high accuracy. Specifically, in dataset GSE26899, the model achieved an AUC of 0.91 (95 % CI: 0.85–0.97); in GSE208099, the AUC reached 1.00 (95 % CI: 1.00–1.00); and in GSE79973, the AUC was 0.91 (95 % CI: [1.00–1.00]; Fig. 6B. Additionally, DNA methylation patterns across these same genes showed excellent predictive performance in classifying gastric tumor vs. normal tissue. In dataset GSE103186, six of the candidate genes (BCAT1, CLDN4, A4GNT, CKAP2, TANC1, and PRKCH) yielded a combined AUC of 0.97 (95 % CI: 0.95–0.99), further supporting the diagnostic relevance of this gene panel (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the host genomic predisposition to RV infection using a WES approach. A single-point allelic and genotypic association analysis identified seven candidate variants mapped to seven distinct genes (A4GNT, BCAT1, CKAP2, CLDN4, PTCHD4, PRKCH, and TANC1). Concurrently, a gene-based association test, identified two genes (BCAT1 and A4GNT), both also detected in the single-variant analysis. Notably, all candidate SNPs and genes associated with RV infection predisposition have a functional connection to oncogenic pathways, particularly those involved in gastrointestinal malignancies, suggesting a potential shared genetic architecture between early-life viral susceptibility to RV and long-term cancer risk.

The most statistically significant SNP, rs2046524, is in the BCAT1 gene. BCAT1 (branched chain amino acid transaminase 1) plays a regulatory role in macrophage function (Papathanassiu et al., 2017). It is associated with hypervalinemia and hyperleucine-isoleucinemia (Wang et al., 2015), and acts as a prognostic marker in gastric cancer (Xu et al., 2018), activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and contributing to angiogenesis (Shu et al., 2021). The second most significant SNP, rs4304220, maps to the gene CLDN4, a member of the claudin family of integral membrane proteins. CLDN4 has been implicated in ovarian (Rangel et al., 2003), breast (Murakami-Nishimagi et al., 2023), and gastric cancer (Hashimoto and Oshima, 2022), serving as a prognostic marker (Ohtani et al., 2009). The third most significant SNP, rs2724692, is in A4GNT. A4GNT is primarily expressed in the stomach and pancreas (Nakayama et al., 1999), and is responsible for O-glycan biosynthesis, including the production of αGlcNAc, a molecule that inhibits Helicobacter pylori growth (Zheng et al., 2009) and acts as a tumor suppressor in gastric cancer (Karasawa et al., 2012). While some studies have suggested a link between the A4GNT and gastric cancer, especially in the context of H. pylori infection, the evidence remains inconclusive (Shimizu et al., 2003; Zheng et al., 2009). Among the other SNPs, rs9379 in CKAP2, encoding a cytoskeleton-associated protein, showed strong association. CKAP2 has been implicated in various cancers, including cervical (Guo et al., 2023), lung (Zhang et al., 2022), and gastric cancer (Bae et al., 2003; Kim et al., 2015). The rs9395327 variant in PTCHD4 is of particular interest, as PTCHD4 is a p53 target gene. Overexpression of its antisense long non-coding RNA has been shown to inhibit gastric cancer cell proliferation (Wang et al., 2023). The variant rs10929938 is in TANC1, a gene with an ankyrin repeat domain involved in tumorigenesis (Yang et al., 2019). Finally, rs4902046 in the PRKCH gene, a member of the protein kinase C (PKC) family, has been associated with multiple cancers, including ovarian and breast cancer, as well as gastric atrophy (Goto et al., 2010).

An integrative analysis using the MalaCards and DisGeNET databases also pointed to a link between the candidate genes and gastrointestinal cancers. PRS analysis revealed that RV-infected patients exhibited significantly lower genetic risk for several gastrointestinal malignancies (Supplementary Text). These findings suggest a potential modulatory role of host-pathogen interactions during childhood, possibly through long-term immune or epigenetic reprogramming.

To better understand the potential link between RV infection in children and gastric or gastrointestinal cancers, we explored evolutionary signatures of positive selection acting on the three top candidate SNPs. Signals of differential selection pressure were detected for the three most relevant SNPs, with evidence of positive selection affecting BCAT1 and A4GNT. For BCAT1, the selection signatures suggested that the process primarily influenced RV cases, while for A4GNT and CLDN4, the values pointed to a more recent positive selection event in HC. These contrasting patterns may help explain the observed differences in susceptibility to RV infection, potentially shaped by distinct selective sweeps acting on these genes.

We further investigated gene expression patterns of the best candidate genes identified in the WES study in RV-infected children. We observed altered gene expression in BCAT1 and CKAP2. Given that the strongest susceptibility genes for RV infection have also been linked to gastrointestinal cancers, we extended our analysis to investigate whether these genes exhibit consistent patterns of transcriptional regulation and DNA methylation. At the transcriptomic level, we observed significant alterations in the expression of BCAT1, CLDN4, CKAP2, and PRKCH in gastric cancer. Additionally, analysis of methylation changes across different gastric cancer tissues revealed extensive differential methylation in A4GNT, BCAT1, CLDN4, PRKCH, and TANC1. This multi-omic approach provides compelling evidence that genes associated with RV infection in children also exhibit altered gene expression and epigenetic modifications in gastric cancer samples.

We subsequently explored biological pathways associated with the top seven candidate genes to elucidate potential mechanisms linking RV infection and cancer development. We identified several significantly enriched pathways, primarily related to tight junctions and the cell cycle, both of which show alterations in RV-infected children and adults with gastric cancer. Tight junctions are essential for maintaining epithelial integrity and have been implicated in RV infection as well as in cancer. RV disrupts tight junction protein distribution, compromising barrier function (Soliman et al., 2018; Torres-Flores et al., 2015), while in cancer, particularly in highly metastatic cells, tight junction expression is often reduced or lost (Salvador et al., 2016). These shared pathways may represent a key mechanistic link, warranting further investigation. Moreover, RV infection and gastrointestinal cancers share common molecular and immunological mechanisms (Supplementary Text). Emerging evidence (Shekarian et al., 2019) suggests that RV may act as an oncolytic virus, with studies demonstrating that existing RV vaccines possess both immunostimulatory and antitumor properties, particularly relevant for gastrointestinal cancers (Supplementary Text).

We propose a mechanistic model that integrates all pieces of evidence (Fig. 7). First, susceptibility variants increase the risk of developing severe RV infection (except TANC1, which appears protective), pointing to seven candidate genes. Some of these genes exhibit altered expression in RV-infected patients. Second, multiple lines of evidence link these genes to gastrointestinal cancers, particularly gastric and pancreatic cancers. Third, our analysis of PRSs suggests that RV-infected patients have a lower genetic predisposition to gastrointestinal cancers, indicating a potential inverse relationship between RV susceptibility and protection against gastrointestinal cancers. Fourth, shared molecular mechanisms, particularly tight junction regulation, may underlie both conditions. Disruption of tight junctions is implicated in RV infection and cancer progression, and our findings suggest directionally consistent pathway alterations. Finally, growing interest in RV’s oncolytic potential and the therapeutic applications of RV vaccines further highlights the importance of unraveling this complex relationship.

Conceptual model of antagonistic pleiotropy linking RV susceptibility and gastrointestinal cancer protection. Our model proposes an example of antagonistic pleiotropy, in which genetic factors that predispose infants to more severe RV infection simultaneously provide long-term protection against gastrointestinal cancers. This dual effect illustrates how evolutionary pressures may sustain certain genetic variants in the population, despite their early-life disadvantages. Our findings indicate that susceptibility to RV infection in our cohort is influenced by specific genetic factors, with risk associated with genes such as BCAT1, A4GNT, CLDN4, CKAP2, PTCHD4, and PRKCH, and a protective effect linked to TANC1. Interestingly, these patients also exhibit a genetic profile, as reflected in PRS, that suggests protection against various gastrointestinal cancers, particularly gastric cancer [point 1]. Based on these observations, we propose that RV infection may induce epigenetic modifications, such as changes in DNA methylation, which could alter gene expression profiles in infected children [point 2]. These molecular changes may persist throughout childhood development and into adulthood, potentially establishing biological conditions that influence susceptibility to gastrointestinal system cancers [point 3]. RV infection and potentially exposure to live attenuated vaccines likely target tight junction proteins and other biological pathways. This interaction may disrupt epithelial barriers, offering a mechanistic explanation for the paradoxical effects: facilitating viral entry during infection while conferring long-term protection against gastrointestinal cancers [point 4]. Furthermore, the systemic immune response to RV, particularly through CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ, may play a dual role in both viral control and the mechanisms underlying cancer prevention [point 5].

Detection of positive selection signatures supports reverse antagonistic pleiotropy, whereby variants increase early-life infection risk but reduce adult disease susceptibility (e.g., cancer or autoimmunity). Several immune-related genes exhibit such patterns (Supplementary Text).

Long-term consequences of early-life RV infection remain incompletely understood. RV can induce persistent gut mucosa alteration, including epithelial disruption, inflammation, and altered mucin production (Ramig, 2004). While detrimental early, such changes may confer protection, consistent with reverse antagonistic pleiotropy (Fig. 7). Sustained epithelial remodeling and immune activation may reduce susceptibility to intestinal metaplasia or dysplasia. Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation changes observed here, may mediate long-term regulation of proliferation, apoptosis, and inflammation (Supplementary Text).

In the context of RV vaccines, an important question is whether vaccination could induce lasting immunological and mucosal changes without tissue damage from natural infection. Vaccination might preserve beneficial long-term epigenetic and immune imprinting while eliminating early morbidity, effectively decoupling the trade-off. This parallels the concept of trained immunity and heterologous (non-specific) vaccine effects, whereby certain live-attenuated vaccines (e.g., measles or BCG) induce long-term functional reprogramming of innate immune cells and have been associated with reduced all-cause mortality beyond their target pathogens (Higgins et al., 2016; Netea et al., 2020; Netea et al., 2016), potentially including protection against inflammation-driven diseases such as cancer.

The main limitation of this study is its statistical power, primarily due to the sample size. This constraint increases the risk of false positives arising from random genetic variation, potentially leading to spurious associations. Although further investigation in larger cohorts is required, the top candidate variants/genes exhibit consistent directional trends, supporting a potential link to gastric cancer. Furthermore, multiple lines of evidence, including signatures of positive selection, as well as transcriptomic and epigenomic data from both RV and gastric cancer samples, support a meaningful genetic, physiological, and evolutionary connection between these two conditions. In addition, available gene expression and epigenomic datasets remain limited, highlighting the need for validating in larger, independent cohorts, particularly given potential implications for prognosis and RV vaccination.

The infection-cancer interplay depends on pathogen type, infection duration, host genetics, immunity, and tissue specificity. Our results suggest a potential link between RV infection and pathways involved in cancer cell proliferation, as the candidate genes may affect both susceptibility to severe RV infection and RV-mediated long-term cancer risk. While gastric cancer develops in older adults (Isobe et al., 2013), severe RV infections primarily affect infants and young children, who often experience acute symptoms before acquiring immunity through natural infection or vaccination. We hypothesize that severe or prolonged RV infection in early life could reduce susceptibility to gastric or gastrointestinal cancers through persistent immune programming, epithelial remodeling, and epigenetic reconfiguration. RV vaccination might prevent early morbidity but also preserve these potential long-term protective effects, paralleling heterologous benefits observed with other vaccines. The prospect that early-life exposure, whether through infection or vaccination, may influence cancer risk later in life is compelling and highlights the need for comprehensive, longitudinal studies to elucidate these relationships.
