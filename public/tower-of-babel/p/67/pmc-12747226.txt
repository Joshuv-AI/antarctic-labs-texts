Bladder paraganglioma presenting with atypical abdominal pain: A diagnostic challenge
Authors: Yuchun Luo, Juncheng Huang, Hongwei Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 70-year-old man presented with a 10-day history of intermittent abdominal pain. Initial evaluation revealed a bladder nodule on imaging, but cystoscopy showed no tumor. Subsequent investigations including elevated urinary normetanephrine levels and MRI identified a hypervascular bladder mass supplied by the right internal iliac artery. Laparoscopic partial cystectomy confirmed a paraganglioma with positive immunohistochemistry (synaptophysin+, chromogranin A+). Postoperatively, symptoms resolved, and antihypertensive therapy was discontinued. This case highlights the importance of considering catecholamine-secreting tumors in patients with unexplained abdominal pain and hypertension, even without classic symptoms.

Introduction

Bladder paragangliomas are rare neuroendocrine tumors arising from chromaffin cells of the sympathetic nervous system, accounting for <0.06 % of all bladder neoplasms and <1 % of all pheochromocytomas/paragangliomas.1 These tumors are typically functional, secreting catecholamines such as norepinephrine, which classically manifest as episodic hypertension, palpitations, headaches, or syncope triggered by bladder distension or micturition.2 However, approximately 10∼20 % of cases present atypically without adrenergic symptoms, posing diagnostic challenges.3

The mean age at diagnosis is 40∼50 years, with no clear gender predilection.4 Imaging features include hypervascularity on contrast-enhanced CT/MRI and diffusion restriction on DWI, often mimicking urothelial carcinoma.5 Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathology (characteristic “Zellballen” architecture) and immunohistochemical positivity for neuroendocrine markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin A).6 Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment, with laparoscopic approaches increasingly favored for reduced morbidity.7

This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of bladder paragangliomas presenting with isolated abdominal pain, emphasizing the need for heightened clinical suspicion in patients with unexplained symptoms and incidental bladder lesions.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old man with a history of inguinal hernia repair presented to the gastroenterology department with a 10-day history of intermittent, non-radiating abdominal pain. He denied classic adrenergic symptoms (e.g., palpitations, headaches, sweating) or urinary complaints. Initial vital signs were stable (BP: 100/79 mmHg; HR: 87 bpm). Physical examination revealed mild tenderness in the left lower quadrant without rebound or guarding.

Laboratory Findings: ①Urinalysis: Microscopic hematuria (RBC: 161.63/μL; normal: <5/μL). ②Biochemistry: Hyperuricemia (UA: 629 μmol/L; normal: 208–428 μmol/L) and hypertriglyceridemia (TG: 3.58 mmol/L; normal: <1.7 mmol/L). ③Tumor Markers: Normal PSA (0.307 ng/mL) and CEA (2.06 ng/mL).

Imaging: ①Abdominal Ultrasound: A 21 × 11 mm hypoechoic nodule on the right posterior bladder wall (Fig. 1). ②Pelvic MRI: A 17 × 16 × 9 mm hypervascular mass at the right anteroinferior bladder wall, supplied by the right internal iliac artery, with diffusion restriction on DWI (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Endoscopy: Colonoscopy: A 0.5 cm sigmoid colon tubular adenoma was resected, confirmed as benign on histology.

Surgical Intervention: Laparoscopic partial cystectomy revealed a well-circumscribed, encapsulated tumor (1.7 × 1.6 × 0.9 cm) arising from the left anterior bladder wall. Intraoperative findings showed no mucosal invasion.

Histopathology: ①Gross: Gray-tan tumor with solid cut surfaces. ②Microscopy: Nests of polygonal cells with granular cytoplasm ("Zellballen” pattern) (Fig. 4). ③Immunohistochemistry: Synaptophysin (+), chromogranin A (+), S100 (+), and Ki-67 (1 %+) (Fig. 5).

Postoperative Course: Antihypertensive agents were discontinued postoperatively. The patient resumed normal voiding after catheter removal on postoperative day 6, with complete resolution of abdominal pain. Surveillance MRI at 6 months showed no recurrence.

Differential diagnosis

The patient's presentation of abdominal pain and incidental bladder nodule prompted consideration of the following diagnoses:

(1)Bladder Urothelial Carcinoma. Supporting Features: Bladder nodules on imaging, microscopic hematuria. Contradictions: Cystoscopy showed no mucosal lesions, and histopathology confirmed neuroendocrine origin (synaptophysin+, chromogranin A+).

Final Diagnosis: Bladder paraganglioma, confirmed via histopathology and immunohistochemistry.

Key Analysis: Bladder paraganglioma was prioritized due to hypervascularity on MRI and neuroendocrine marker positivity, despite atypical symptomology. Urothelial carcinoma was excluded early due to normal cystoscopy and lack of mucosal involvement. The absence of classic catecholamine excess symptoms delayed biochemical testing, highlighting the diagnostic challenge in non-functional variants.

Treatment

Preoperative management included alpha-blockers (prazosin) and beta-blockers (metoprolol) to mitigate catecholamine release risks, intravenous hydration to prevent postoperative hypotension, and prophylactic cefatriaxone. Under general anesthesia, a laparoscopic partial cystectomy was performed: a 1.7 × 1.6 × 0.9 cm encapsulated tumor near the left anterior bladder neck was completely resected via an infraumbilical port, preserving the mucosal layer with minimal intraoperative blood loss (10 mL). The bladder defect was closed in layers using absorbable sutures. Postoperatively, multimodal analgesia and continued antibiotics were administered. The pelvic drain was removed on postoperative day 2, and the urinary catheter was withdrawn on day 6, after confirming normal voiding function. Pathology confirmed a bladder paraganglioma (synaptophysin [Syn+], chromogranin A [CgA+], Ki-67 1 %). Antihypertensive agents were discontinued, with blood pressure stabilizing at 120–130/70–80 mmHg. Follow-up included pelvic MRI at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively and annual 24-h urinary normetanephrine testing, with no recurrence observed at 6 months.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient resumed normal voiding immediately after urinary catheter removal on postoperative day 6, with complete resolution of abdominal pain and no postoperative complications. Histopathological confirmation of bladder paraganglioma (synaptophysin+, chromogranin A+, Ki-67 1 %) supported curative resection. Antihypertensive therapy was discontinued, and blood pressure remained stable within 120–130/70–80 mmHg. A structured surveillance protocol included pelvic MRI and 24-h urinary normetanephrine testing at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, with no evidence of recurrence at the 6-month follow-up. The patient reported restored quality of life, emphasizing pain-free daily activities and normal urinary function. Long-term monitoring will continue annually to detect potential late recurrences, aligning with guidelines for neuroendocrine tumors.

Discussion

Bladder paragangliomas are notorious for their heterogeneous clinical manifestations. While classic symptoms such as hypertensive crises triggered by micturition are well-documented8, this case highlights the diagnostic dilemma posed by non-adrenergic presentations. The patient's isolated abdominal pain, absence of hypertension, and lack of palpitations or sweating deviated from the “classic triad,” aligning with reports that up to 20 % of paragangliomas present atypically.9 This underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for neuroendocrine tumors in patients with unexplained abdominal symptoms, even in the absence of catecholamine excess signs. Biochemical screening (e.g., 24-h urinary normetanephrine) should be prioritized in such scenarios, as subtle elevations may precede overt symptoms.10

Contrast-enhanced MRI emerged as the cornerstone for preoperative evaluation in this case. The hypervascularity and diffusion restriction observed on DWI (Fig. 2, Fig. 3) are hallmarks of paragangliomas, distinguishing them from urothelial carcinomas or inflammatory lesions.5 The tumor's arterial supply from the right internal iliac artery further supported its neuroendocrine origin, a feature critical for surgical planning. Current guidelines recommend multiparametric MRI as the first-line imaging modality for suspected bladder paragangliomas due to its superior soft-tissue resolution and ability to delineate vascular anatomy.11

Laparoscopic partial cystectomy was chosen over transurethral resection (TURBT) or open surgery to balance oncological efficacy and morbidity reduction. TURBT was avoided due to risks of intraoperative catecholamine release and incomplete resection in extramural tumors.12 The minimally invasive approach allowed precise tumor excision with clear margins while preserving bladder function, consistent with studies demonstrating shorter hospital stays and lower complication rates compared to open techniques.13 Preoperative α/β-blockade, though controversial in normotensive patients, was empirically administered to mitigate potential intraoperative hemodynamic instability—a strategy supported by recent consensus statements.14

Histopathology confirmed the tumor's benign behavior, evidenced by the characteristic “Zellballen” architecture, low Ki-67 index (1 %), and absence of necrosis. Immunohistochemical positivity for synaptophysin and chromogranin A remains the gold standard for diagnosis.15 While most bladder paragangliomas exhibit indolent behavior, malignant potential correlates with SDHB mutations or Ki-67 > 3 %.16 Genetic testing, though not performed here, is increasingly advocated to stratify recurrence risk, particularly in younger patients.17

Postoperative surveillance protocols must address the lifelong recurrence risk (10–20 %).18 This patient's follow-up regimen—annual MRI and urinary normetanephrine testing—aligns with NCCN guidelines for pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma.19 Patient education on symptom recognition (e.g., recurrent pain, hypertension) is equally critical, as late recurrences may manifest decades after initial resection.20
