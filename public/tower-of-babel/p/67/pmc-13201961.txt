Aplastic or twig-like middle cerebral artery (Ap/T-MCA) is a rare cerebrovascular anomaly characterized by unilateral stenosis or hypoplasia of the proximal middle cerebral artery (MCA) and the presence of a plexiform network of abnormal vessels in the horizontal (M1) segment. The condition was first described by Cekirge et al. in 2005 as an “unfused MCA,” and, in the same year, Liu et al. introduced the term “twig-like MCA.”1,2 Because its angiographic appearance mimics that of moyamoya disease, accurate differentiation between these two entities is crucial. In fact, Ap/T-MCA has been historically misdiagnosed as moyamoya disease, emphasizing the diagnostic challenges involved.3

In East Asian populations, the RNF213 (ring finger protein 213) p.R4810K variant has been identified as the major genetic susceptibility factor for moyamoya disease.4 In recent years, although only 2 cases have been reported, Ap/T-MCA cases carrying this RNF213 variant have been described, suggesting that Ap/T-MCA may share a genetic background with moyamoya disease.5,6 Here we report the cases of 3 patients with Ap/T-MCA, all of whom had the RNF213 p.R4810K variant. These include 1 previously reported case presenting with hemorrhage and 2 newly identified siblings.7

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 67-year-old man with hypertension and a family history of moyamoya disease presented with a sudden, severe headache. Neurological examination was normal. Noncontrast CT revealed a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) in the left sylvian fissure. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) demonstrated a plexiform network of fine vessels replacing the horizontal segment of the left MCA, with preservation of the distal MCA branches. Lenticulostriate arteries originated directly from this network. No aneurysm or other vascular malformation was identified, and the other intracranial arteries appeared normal. No transdural collateral supply from the external carotid artery was present, whereas leptomeningeal anastomoses from the anterior cerebral artery (ACA) were visible. Contrast-enhanced CT confirmed the presence of an abnormal vascular network within the subarachnoid space (Fig. 1). Based on these findings, a diagnosis of Ap/T-MCA was made.

D and E:Digital subtraction angiograms showing a reticular vascular network in the left M1 segment (arrowheads), giving rise to normal M2 branches and lenticulostriate arteries. The patient was managed conservatively, and follow-up DSA on day 5 showed no aneurysm. His headache improved gradually, and he was discharged. This case was previously reported in 2024.7

Case 2

A 24-year-old woman presented with transient involuntary movements of the left upper limb. MRI revealed stenosis of the right proximal M1 segment and a plexiform vascular network, without evidence of infarction or hemorrhage. DSA demonstrated stenosis of the M1 segment of the right MCA and the horizontal (A1) segment of the right ACA, and a plexiform network replacing the M1 portion. The distal insular (M2) segment of the MCA was preserved. Lenticulostriate arteries arose from this network. No transdural collateral supply from the external carotid artery was detected. Conversely, leptomeningeal anastomoses from the ACA were identified. Contrast-enhanced CT confirmed the abnormal network in the subarachnoid space (Fig. 2).

Case 2. A and B: Contrast-enhanced CT scans confirming the abnormal network in the subarachnoid space (arrows). C and D: Digital subtraction angiograms demonstrating stenosis of the right M1 and A1 segments with preserved M2 branches (arrowheads). Lenticulostriate arteries originate from the network, and ACA leptomeningeal anastomoses are noted.

Ap/T-MCA was diagnosed, and the patient was treated conservatively. Her symptoms gradually improved, and she remained neurologically stable during follow-up.

Case 3

A 43-year-old woman, the older sister of case 2, presented to a local clinic with numbness and weakness affecting the left face and limbs. MRI revealed poor visualization of the right MCA; however, no infarction or hemorrhage was detected. She was referred to our hospital for further evaluation. Contrast-enhanced CT showed a duplicated right MCA with a small-caliber distal internal carotid artery (ICA) and severe stenosis of the right M1 and A1 segments. Although no discrete vascular network was apparent on CT, DSA confirmed proximal stenosis of the duplicated MCA, accompanied by a plexiform vascular network in the horizontal portion. Lenticulostriate arteries originated from this network (Fig. 3). Based on these findings, Ap/T-MCA was diagnosed. Because the patient experienced transient ischemic attack (TIA) symptoms, aspirin therapy was initiated. She remained free from stroke during follow-up.

Discussion

Observations

Reports of Ap/T-MCA remain limited, and its pathogenesis, embryological origin, and clinical characteristics have not been fully elucidated. Although no established diagnostic criteria currently exist, previous studies have described Ap/T-MCA as unilateral stenosis or occlusion of the MCA with a plexiform vascular network replacing the proximal trunk. This vascular network gives rise to lenticulostriate arteries, while the distal cortical branches maintain normal antegrade flow. The absence of a transdural collateral supply from the external carotid artery and the frequent presence of leptomeningeal anastomoses from the anterior or posterior cerebral artery are additional features that help to distinguish Ap/T-MCA from other cerebrovascular disorders.8,9 All 3 of our patients demonstrated these characteristic findings.

Embryology of the MCA

During normal cerebral arterial development, the primitive ICA divides into cranial and caudal branches. The cranial branch gives rise to the anterior choroidal artery, while a primitive plexiform arterial network forms distal to this point. Through a process of selective fusion and regression, this network consolidates into a single MCA trunk. Incomplete fusion or persistence of portions of the primitive plexus may lead to developmental variants such as duplication, fenestration, or the twig-like configuration seen in Ap/T-MCA. The frequent coexistence of congenital anomalies involving the ACA or MCA, as well as the abnormal origin of lenticulostriate arteries from the reticular network, supports the hypothesis that Ap/T-MCA represents a congenital vascular anomaly arising from aberrant embryological development.10–12

Conversely, Ota and Komiyama pointed out that Ap/T-MCA may represent a secondary change following stenosis of the proximal MCA.9 Although Ap/T-MCA is often discovered in association with cerebrovascular events, most cases occur in adults, and reports of pediatric or adolescent onset are exceedingly rare. Because primitive arterial walls are structurally fragile against hemodynamic stress, aneurysms related to abnormal flow would be expected to develop within the residual arterial network and present at a younger age. Furthermore, it is unlikely that a primitive plexiform arterial network would persist into late adulthood. Normal development of the cerebral hemispheres during the fetal period requires appropriate formation of the MCA; however, patients with this condition exhibit no developmental abnormalities of the distal MCA or cerebral hemispheres. In addition, recent reports have described de novo T-MCA cases, suggesting the possibility of acquired or progressive onset.13 Whether T-MCA represents a congenital anomaly, an acquired lesion, or both, remains undetermined at present. Given the rarity of the condition and the requirement for long-term follow-up, accumulation of additional reports of acquired T-MCA may be difficult.

Differentiation From Moyamoya Disease

The most critical differential diagnosis for Ap/T-MCA is moyamoya disease. Moyamoya disease is characterized by progressive stenosis or occlusion of the terminal ICA and the development of basal ganglia collateral vessels, which appear as the classic “moyamoya vessels” on angiography. Although moyamoya disease typically presents bilaterally, in recent years an increasing number of unilateral cases have been reported.14 In the 2021 revision of the diagnostic criteria, unilateral cases were formally included.15 It is only natural that awareness of unilateral moyamoya-like vasculopathies, including Ap/T-MCA, has progressively increased. A study by Goto et al. compared radiological and clinical characteristics of Ap/T-MCA and moyamoya disease, identifying key differences: Ap/T-MCA demonstrates an abnormal vascular network in the subarachnoid space, while moyamoya disease is characterized by basal ganglia collateral vessels and progressive terminal ICA stenosis.3 In the same study, 20 of 29 patients (69%) with Ap/T-MCA were initially diagnosed with moyamoya disease. Differentiation from moyamoya disease is often difficult, and Ap/T-MCA cases may be hidden among moyamoya disease cases. Clinically, Ap/T-MCA tends to exhibit a lower overall incidence of ischemic stroke but a relatively higher incidence of hemorrhagic stroke compared with moyamoya disease.3

Association of RNF213 Variants With Ap/T-MCA

Recent genetic studies have identified RNF213 as the most significant susceptibility gene for moyamoya disease. In 2010, the RNF213 p.R4859K variant was first reported in Japanese patients with moyamoya disease, and, soon after, RNF213 p.R4810K was recognized as a major East Asian–specific risk allele.4,16,17 The RNF213 p.R4810K variant is found in approximately 90% of Japanese moyamoya disease patients but is absent in Caucasian populations. The association between RNF213 p.R4810K and moyamoya disease is extremely strong, with an odds ratio of approximately 340 for disease occurrence among carriers. However, because 1%–2% of healthy East Asians also carry this variant, it is unlikely that RNF213 p.R4810K alone is sufficient to cause disease. Additional genetic, environmental, or hemodynamic factors are thought to influence disease expression.

In Japanese populations, RNF213 p.R4810K is the only known genetic susceptibility factor for intracranial arterial stenosis, including atherosclerotic thrombotic stroke. Moreover, this variant has been associated with pulmonary hypertension, coronary artery disease, and systemic hypertension, leading to the broader concept of “RNF213-related vasculopathy.” This spectrum encompasses both congenital and acquired vascular abnormalities, unified by shared molecular and pathophysiological mechanisms involving RNF213 dysfunction.18–21

The first case of Ap/T-MCA carrying the RNF213 p.R4810K variant was reported in 2020.5 Only one other case was reported in 2022, and the number of documented cases before the present report had been limited to two.6 With our series included, 5 cases have now been reported.5–7 Our 3 cases, particularly the 2 siblings, provide further evidence supporting a genetic contribution to Ap/T-MCA and raise the possibility of familial occurrence (Table 1). Considering that RNF213 p.R4810K is implicated in both moyamoya disease and other intracranial stenotic disorders, Ap/T-MCA may represent one phenotype within the spectrum of RNF213-related vasculopathy.

In contrast, the RNF213 p.R4859K variant, although previously reported in moyamoya disease, has not been associated with Ap/T-MCA. Among the reported cases, none has carried RNF213 p.R4859K, suggesting that the pathogenic role of this variant may differ across cerebrovascular phenotypes.22 In moyamoya disease, homozygous RNF213 p.R4859K carriers develop symptoms earlier and have more severe clinical courses than heterozygous or wildtype individuals, implying a dose-dependent genetic effect.23 However, no data currently support a correlation between homozygosity for RNF213 p.R4810K and the clinical phenotype of Ap/T-MCA. Further studies involving larger genetic cohorts and functional analyses are needed to clarify this potential relationship.

Lessons

We present 3 cases of Ap/T-MCA associated with the RNF213 p.R4810K variant. This report is the first to suggest a possible familial occurrence for the disease. These findings suggest that Ap/T-MCA may not be a sporadic developmental variant, but rather a genetically mediated vascular phenotype that shares a pathogenic mechanism with moyamoya disease and other RNF213-associated arteriopathies. Accumulation of additional genetically characterized cases will be essential to establish their diagnostic criteria, define their natural history, and guide future management strategies.
