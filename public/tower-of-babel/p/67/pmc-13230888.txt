Elevated trimethylamine levels characterize impaired muscle mass response to leucine-enriched protein supplementation in older adults at risk of sarcopenia
Authors: Hsiang-Yu Tang, Chi-Jen Lo, Hung-Yao Ho, Cheng-Hung Yang, Chen-Yu Chang, Yu-Hsuan Ho, Ji-Tseng Fang, Chih-Ming Lin, Mei-Ling Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Leucine-enriched supplementation is a primary intervention for sarcopenia, yet individual responses vary. We integrated 1H-NMR metabolomics with clinical assessments in 47 older adults at high sarcopenia risk to identify metabotypes associated with improvements in muscle mass and strength. Following a 12-week intervention, distinct metabolic trajectories emerged between responders and non-responders. Notably, urinary levels of the gut-derived metabolite trimethylamine (TMA) and phenylpyruvic acid exhibited divergent trends across outcome-defined groups. Elevated TMA was associated with a blunted muscle mass response to leucine supplementation and with impaired myogenic differentiation and compromised myotube integrity in vitro, supporting a potential role in limiting myogenic capacity. These findings highlight the gut-muscle axis as a key modulator of heterogeneous responses to nutritional intervention and provide a metabolic framework for stratifying individuals in sarcopenia prevention strategies.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is an age-related skeletal muscle disorder characterized by progressive loss of muscle mass and muscle function, commonly occurring in older adults1. With no pharmacological agents currently approved to treat sarcopenia, resistance exercise is recommended, and nutritional supplementation is often used as an adjunct2,3. Nutrition interventions are widely adopted among older adults, given the essential role of nutrients in muscle maintenance4. However, translation of experimental and clinical findings into dietary recommendations is limited by inconsistent results, lack of appropriate indicators and mechanistic validation, and individual differences in genetics and lifestyle factors5,6. Establishing tailored nutritional interventions for sarcopenia remains challenging, in part due to substantial inter-individual metabolic variability.

Leucine-enriched amino acid or protein supplements have been extensively studied as a nutritional strategy to counteract sarcopenia. Previous studies have demonstrated that leucine supplementation can stimulate muscle protein synthesis in older adults7,8, and may improve clinical indicators of sarcopenia in randomized clinical trials9,10. However, several studies have reported that prolonged leucine supplementation may not affect muscle mass and strength in older adults11–14. The inconsistent findings may be due to individual variations in dietary responses, highlighting the challenge of predicting who will benefit from supplementation.

Metabotyping uses metabolic parameters to classify individuals with similar metabolic profiles and offers a tool to identify those who may respond favorably to nutritional interventions15–17. It provides a framework to identify individuals who may benefit from nutrition advice and to capture individual metabolic responses to dietary exposure, which is essential for understanding the role of nutrients such as leucine in preventing sarcopenia.

Metabolomics provides in-depth metabolic phenotyping and contributes to the development of precision nutrition18,19. Through simultaneous assessment of exogenous diet-derived metabolites and endogenous metabolic pathways, metabolomics allows for evaluation of nutrient intake, metabolic adaptations, and biological responses to interventions17,20. Metabolic profiling not only reflects nutrient intake but also captures individual differences in muscle anabolic capacity and gut microbial metabolism, offering insights into the heterogeneity of response to leucine-enriched protein supplementation in older adults at risk of sarcopenia. Despite the extensive study of leucine-enriched protein supplementation, current research often overlooks inter-individual metabolic heterogeneity and gut microbiota–derived metabolites, limiting the ability to predict responders and non-responders in clinical settings. Thus, elucidating the associations between outcomes of leucine-enriched protein supplementation and individual metabotypes may represent a foundation for developing personalized nutritional strategies for sarcopenia patients.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate heterogeneity in anabolic responses to leucine-enriched protein supplementation in older adults at high risk for sarcopenia, with a focus on individual metabotypes and gut microbiota-associated metabolites.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants and the outcomes of nutritional supplementation

Among the 47 participants at high risk for sarcopenia who completed the 12-week nutritional intervention (mean age 71.51 ± 5.30 years; 33 females, 14 males), 68% of participants had improved hand grip strength (HGS) and 49% had improved appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI). Based on individual responses to the intervention, participants were stratified into four response groups: those with improved HGS only (HI, n = 16), those with improved ASMI only (AI, n = 7), those with improvements in both metrics (AH, n = 16), and those with no improvements (NN, n = 8) (Fig. 1 & Table S1). Changes in fat mass distribution between the trunk and limbs varied across outcome groups

Trunk fat mass significantly increased in both the HI and NN groups, which were characterized by a decline or lack of improvement in ASMI. The increases in fat mass positively correlated with the rise in BMI but negatively correlated with changes in ASMI. Although total fat mass changed significantly following supplementation, this change was primarily driven by alterations in trunk fat mass rather than limb fat mass. Changes in trunk fat mass, but not limb fat mass, were significantly and inversely correlated with changes in ASMI (r = −0.388, p = 0.007) (Table 3). Specific metabolite profiles across the four distinct outcome groups

To obtain more accurate insights, 1H-NMR spectroscopy was applied to analyze plasma and urine metabolites following the 12-week supplementation. The multivariate analysis with PLS-DA demonstrated that supplementation could alter the overall metabolite levels across all 47 participants, regardless of outcome classification (Fig. S1A). Levels of formic acid, 3-hydroxybutyric acid, and triglyceride in low-density lipoproteins subfraction 4 (TG-LDL4) significantly decreased in plasma. The plasma levels of glutamic acid, alanine, leucine, methionine, free cholesterol in LDL2 (FC-LDL2), and the urinary levels of proline betaine, trimethylamine (TMA), alanine, 1-methylguanidine, leucine, and pyruvic acid significantly increased (Fig. S1B).

Within-group analysis using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests revealed distinct metabolite changes for each outcome group. Thirteen, 15, 11, and 2 metabolites significantly changed in the AH, HI, AI, and NN groups after the 12-week supplementation. In the AH group (improvement in both HGS and ASMI), plasma LDL subfraction 6 (LDL6)-containing TG (TG-LDL6), phospholipids (Pho-LDL6), apolipoprotein-B (ApoB-LDL6), particle number of LDL6 (PN-LDL6), tyrosine, alanine, and glutamic acid were significantly increased, while plasma formic acid, 3-hydroxybutyric acid, and TG in LDL subfraction 4 (TG-LDL4) were significantly reduced. Urinary levels of propionic acid, leucine, and alanine also showed marked increases (Fig. 2A). In the HI group (improved HGS only), plasma levels of LDL subfraction 2 (LDL2)-containing cholesterol (Cho-LDL2), Apo-B (ApoB-LDL2), free cholesterol (FC-LDL2), and particle number of LDL2 (PN-LDL2), methionine, glutamic acid and leucine were significantly increased, while plasma formic acid, acetic acid, and TG in LDL subfraction 5 (TG-LDL5) were significantly decreased. Urinary levels of pyruvic acid, syringic acid, TMA and 3-hydroxyisovaleric acid showed significant increases, while urinary phenylpyruvic acid was markedly reduced following supplementation (Fig. 2B). In the AI group (improved ASMI only), plasma levels of phospholipid in very low-density lipoprotein subfraction 5 (Pho-VLDL5), apolipoprotein-A1 in high-density lipoprotein subfraction 3 (ApoA1-HDL3), phospholipid in HDL3 (Pho-HDL3), and free cholesterol in HDL (FC-HDL) exhibited significant increases, along with urinary guanidinoacetic acid, caffeine, proline betaine, theobromine, and uracil. Plasma formic acid and urinary creatine levels were decreased considerably following supplementation (Fig. 2C). Only two metabolites, plasma pyruvic acid and urinary hippuric acid, were significantly reduced in the NN group (no improvement in either HGS or ASMI) (Fig. 2D). A limited number of altered metabolites were consistently observed across various groups, highlighting distinct and outcome-specific metabolic signatures associated with different responses to supplementation. Correlation and regression analyses of ASMI and HGS changes

To further assess the robustness of the grouping-based findings, correlation and multiple regression analyses were performed, treating changes in ASMI and HGS as continuous variables across all participants, with adjustment for age and sex. Several metabolites that showed significant pre–post changes in the group-based analyses demonstrated consistent associations with functional outcomes. For ASMI, urinary alanine and phenylpyruvic acid exhibited positive trends, whereas urinary TMA and plasma 3-hydroxybutyric acid showed inverse associations. Plasma acetic acid demonstrated a modest positive association with ASMI change. For HGS, urinary guanidinoacetic acid and TMA were among the metabolites showing associations with strength change, while other amino acid–related metabolites displayed weaker or non-significant relationships. Detailed correlation coefficients and regression statistics are provided in Table S3. Given the limited sample size and multiple-testing burden, these analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Gut metabolites alterations associated with divergent muscle mass outcomes

Both the AH and HI groups showed improvements in handgrip strength; however, only the AH group exhibited a concomitant increase in ASMI. To explore the metabolic mechanisms underlying divergent muscle mass responses, pathway-level analyses were performed following the 12-week nutritional supplementation. The AH and HI groups shared similar alterations in glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, as well as alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism.

Discussion

Previous studies have addressed the use of protein supplements for preventing sarcopenia, but the benefits of such regimens remain controversial2,14. We focused on older adults identified as being at high risk for sarcopenia (i.e., those with low muscle mass or strength), aiming to implement early nutritional strategies to delay or prevent the onset of clinically defined sarcopenia. Participants exhibited heterogeneous responses to 12 weeks of leucine-enriched protein supplementation, with improvements observed in muscle strength, muscle mass, or both. These divergent outcomes were accompanied by distinct plasma and urinary metabolite profiles, suggesting that individual metabolic states may drive variability in both supplementation response and muscle outcomes.

Although supplementation caused similar changes in most metabolites, gut microbiota–associated metabolites such as TMA and phenylpyruvic acid exhibited different trends between the AH and HI groups during muscle mass progression. Despite no significant differences in baseline sex, age, metabolites, or biochemical parameters between the two groups, these divergent responses to the same nutritional supplementation suggest that gut microbiota may play a critical role in modulating individual outcomes.

The abundance of TMA-related bacterial genera—defined as those concurrently harboring both cntA/B and cutC/D gene clusters21—was higher in participants without ASMI improvement than in those with ASMI gain after 12 weeks of supplementation. This finding suggests a potential involvement of TMA-producing microbiota in host metabolic pathways that may influence muscle health22.

TMA is produced by gut microbiota primarily through two enzymatic pathways: the choline TMA-lyase complex (CutC/D)23, and the carnitine monooxygenase/ reductase system (CntA/B)24. Despite growing evidence linking TMA-producing bacteria to adverse health outcomes, their functional contribution and inter-individual variability remain incompletely understood. Given the substantial heterogeneity of the gut microbiome25, differences in microbial metabolic capacity and microbe-derived metabolites such as TMA may represent key determinants of individual responses to leucine supplementation.

Beyond its microbial origin, TMA and its hepatic oxidation product, TMAO, have been implicated in multiple pathophysiological processes. Elevated circulating TMAO levels have been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes26,27, and gut dysbiosis has been shown to contribute to increased systemic TMAO exposure. Aging-related alterations in gut barrier integrity have been reported to increase intestinal permeability, potentially facilitating gut-to-blood translocation of microbial metabolites. In line with this concept, older rats exhibit higher circulating TMA levels than younger counterparts, a phenomenon attributed to increased intestinal permeability28. Similarly, the elevated urinary TMA detected in these older adults in the present study may reflect, at least in part, altered gut microbial metabolism and/or enhanced gut-derived TMA translocation. As TMAO has been proposed to be involved in muscle wasting29 through a putative gut-TMAO-muscle axis, excessive TMA production following supplementation may contribute to attenuated anabolic responses. Notably, participants in the HI group (improved HGS only) presented higher urinary TMA and plasma TMAO levels after supplementation, whereas those in the AH group (improved HGS and ASMI) exhibited opposite trends. In vitro studies provide mechanistic support for this association between elevated TMA and impaired muscle mass gains in humans, demonstrating that both TMA and TMAO impair myotube formation and stability. Consistent with these morphological alterations, we further observed a dose-dependent suppression of key myogenic regulatory factors following TMA exposure, including MyoD and Myogenin (MyoG), which play central roles in myogenic differentiation and myotube formation. Downregulation of these proteins is consistent with the reduced fusion index and myotube diameter observed in our C2C12 model, supporting impaired myogenic differentiation. Although our in vitro data provide mechanistic support for the association between elevated TMA and impaired muscle mass gain, the concentrations used should be interpreted with caution. Circulating TMAO levels in humans are typically in the low micromolar range30, whereas physiological TMA concentrations, particularly at the tissue level, remain poorly defined. In this study, TMA and TMAO were applied at 0.1–1 mM to probe potential direct cellular effects rather than to recapitulate circulating levels, and thus were used to assess cellular susceptibility under acute exposure conditions. Similar concentration ranges have been widely used in prior in vitro studies, and TMA has been shown to exert stronger direct cellular effects than TMAO in smooth muscle cells28. Importantly, TMA exerted inhibitory effects on myogenic differentiation at lower concentrations than did TMAO, suggesting greater potency. Together, these findings support a working model in which excessive gut-derived TMA production may contribute to attenuated muscle anabolic responsiveness in susceptible individuals.

Alteration of phenylalanine metabolism in the AH and HI groups is noteworthy. The subjects of the HI group showed a decline in urine phenylpyruvic acid but higher plasma levels of phenylalanine (Fig. S3). Alternatively, a portion of phenylalanine may undergo transamination by certain gut anaerobes, resulting in the formation of phenylpyruvic acid. In addition, colonic bacteria transform the dietary phenylalanine into phenylpyruvic acid through deamination of phenylalanine, which is converted to phenylacetic acid31. Phenylacetic acid conjugates with glutamine in the liver and kidney to predominantly form phenylacetylglutamine (PAGln) in humans, which is associated with cardiovascular diseases31–33. Whether the observed reduction in urinary phenylpyruvic acid reflects altered microbial phenylalanine metabolism or diversion toward PAGln biosynthesis warrants further investigation.

The small sample sizes of the NN (n = 8) and AI (n = 7) groups may have limited the statistical power and attenuated the apparent efficacy of the intervention. As shown in Table S4, most metabolites in the AI and NN groups exhibited low statistical power (< 0.5), with only a small number of metabolites reaching moderate-to-high power, indicating a high risk of Type II error. Therefore, the absence of significant changes should not be interpreted as evidence of no metabolic effect. It is noteworthy that the NN group had an MNA score of 23.14, which falls within the range indicative of a risk of malnutrition (17–23.5), despite no significant differences were observed in the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) scores among the four groups. This suboptimal nutritional status may have attenuated the efficacy of the intervention in this group. Additionally, the subjects of the AI group had baseline HGS values that markedly exceeded the established normative range, which may limit improvement in HGS following nutritional supplementation. Moreover, the smaller number of participants also limits the statistical analysis.

The body composition analysis revealed changes in the amount and body distribution of muscle and fat in different outcome groups after a 12-week supplementation. Concurrent presence of excess fat and low muscle mass or function, known as sarcopenic obesity, is increasingly recognized for its clinical and functional implications, which may affect the outcome of intervention. Accumulating evidence suggests that high body fat increases the risk for sarcopenia34–36. However, a recent study has indicated that increases in triglycerides offer protection against sarcopenia37, adding complexity to this issue. Our findings indicate that an increase in fat mass of the legs, but not that of the trunk, in AH subjects may have physiological implications. Lipids in skeletal muscle may accumulate as intermuscular adipose tissue (IMAT) or intramyocellular lipids (IMCL), which have distinct metabolic implications38. IMAT is generally associated with insulin resistance, muscle strength loss, and mobility impairment39. Whereas, higher IMCL content in older adults is concomitant with enhanced oxidative activity in their skeletal muscle40, and with improved insulin sensitivity41. It is possible that the AH subjects preferentially accumulated IMCL rather than IMAT; however, this distinction cannot be resolved by bioimpedance analysis and warrants further investigation. In the HI and NN groups, there were increases in trunk fat mass but not in leg. It has been reported that trunk fat and total body fat are inversely correlated with the mitochondrial capacity of skeletal muscles42. Additionally, visceral fat accumulation is predictive of the decline in skeletal muscle mass43. It is likely that an increase in trunk fat mass in the HI and NN groups may impede the muscle mass gain after supplementation.

Following supplementation, AH subjects showed a shift toward smaller, denser particles (LDL6). Increased small dense LDL (sdLDL) formation is associated with an increase in hepatic lipase (HL) activity44. Transcription of the HL-encoding gene LIPC is activated by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) and hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α)45. Previous studies have shown that expression of PGC-1α is induced by β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate, a catabolite of leucine46. Leucine supplementation may therefore influence sdLDL formation through this pathway, although direct causal links remain to be established. Although sdLDLs (such as LDL6) are generally considered more atherogenic47, their production may also reflect adaptive remodeling in response to altered lipid availability48. In agreement with this, nutritional intervention that diminished carbohydrate intake and increased n3-polyunsaturated fatty acid consumption led to a reduction in sdLDL49. However, the actual levels of LDL6 in AH individuals are not higher than those of other groups. Further studies will be required to clarify the physiological relevance of this lipid remodeling and its potential relationship to muscle mass and strength adaptations.

The role of nutrition in preventing and treating sarcopenia has received ample attention. The groups of subjects classified according to their metabolite profiles exhibit differential responses to diets and interventions19,50,51. A metabolomic approach that assesses an individual’s response18,52 and identifies their metabolic profiles (metabotypes) can be applied to formulate appropriate personalized nutrition plans18,53. It is conceivable that the metabolite profiles of biofluids such as plasma or urine provide the information needed for this purpose. The application of personalized nutrition concepts to dietary intervention is particularly appealing, especially for older adults at different ages and with various diseases54,55.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small number of participants in each post-hoc-defined response subgroup may limit statistical power, thereby increasing the risk of both false-negative and false-positive findings. Accordingly, the subgroup analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Future studies with larger sample sizes and independent cohorts will be required to validate these observations and to establish their generalizability. Second, physical activity was assessed qualitatively rather than quantitatively, and changes in exercise volume during follow-up were not objectively measured. Therefore, residual confounding by unmeasured variation in physical activity, particularly resistance exercise, cannot be completely excluded. Third, we did not analyze the human genome in this study, although the genetic differences affect individuals’ responses to nutritional supplementation56. Our ongoing study will combine genomic, gut microbiomic, and metabolomic data in our analysis, and the resulting data metrics can be used to adjust the dietary intake of individuals.

Collectively, this study demonstrates that older adults at high risk for sarcopenia exhibit heterogeneous responses to leucine supplementation that are closely linked to distinct metabolic profiles. Beyond improvements in muscle strength and mass, our findings highlight gut microbiota–derived metabolites as potential modulators of anabolic responsiveness in aging muscle. In particular, elevated urinary TMA levels and increased abundance of TMA-producing bacterial genera were associated with attenuated appendicular muscle mass gain following supplementation. Mechanistically, our in vitro data provide biological plausibility for this association, showing that TMA—and to a lesser extent TMAO—can impair myogenic differentiation and compromise myotube integrity at concentrations relevant to microbial metabolism. These observations suggest that excess gut-derived TMA may contribute to an anabolic-resistant phenotype in older adults, potentially limiting the efficacy of otherwise beneficial nutritional interventions. Taken together, these results support a model in which individual metabotypes, shaped in part by gut microbial activity, influence the musculoskeletal response to dietary supplementation during aging. This integrative view underscores the need to move beyond one-size-fits-all nutritional strategies and toward personalized interventions informed by metabolic and microbiome profiles. Future studies incorporating multi-omics approaches and larger cohorts will be essential to further validate these mechanisms and to refine precision nutrition strategies for the prevention and management of sarcopenia.

Methods

Study population

Older adults aged 60 years and above were recruited from a community-based health screening center in northern Taiwan during routine annual geriatric evaluations conducted between April 2021 and April 2022. Participants voluntarily sought preventive health assessments. Eligibility screening was performed by trained clinicians using standardized medical history reviews, physical examinations, and structured questionnaires. Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), with scores ≥26 indicating normal cognition, and independent ambulation was confirmed by clinical assessment. Physical activity was assessed at baseline using a structured questionnaire, in which participants reported whether they met the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation of at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity physical activity. Participants were instructed to maintain their habitual physical activity throughout the intervention period. Individuals with severe cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, or malignant diseases were excluded. A total of 468 individuals were initially enrolled, of whom 19 were excluded due to incomplete clinical data. The remaining 449 participants underwent assessments of muscle mass and muscle strength according to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 criteria57. Individuals with either low muscle mass or low muscle strength were categorized as having high risk for sarcopenia and were invited to participate in the intervention (Fig. 1). Those meeting the full criteria for sarcopenia were not included in the study. Among the 140 individuals identified as having risk for sarcopenia, 56 provided written informed consent to participate in a 12-week nutritional supplementation program. During the intervention period, 9 participants withdrew due to personal or health-related reasons. Consequently, 47 participants completed the intervention and were included in the final analysis. Blood and urine samples were collected under overnight fasting conditions and analyzed at the Chang Gung Memorial Hospital Clinical Biochemistry Laboratory. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (IRB No. 202100551A3) on June 11, 2021, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT07309120).

Assessment of body composition, muscle strength, and physical performance

Body composition, such as muscle mass and fat mass, was measured using bioimpedance analysis using the Tanita MC-780MA (Tanita Corporation, Japan). The appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) was calculated by dividing the ASM (kg) by the square of height (m2). Low muscle mass was defined as ASMI < 7.0 kg/m2 for males or <5.7 kg/m2 for females57. Muscle strength was assessed by hand grip strength (HGS) using a hand dynamometer (Jamar Plus + Digital Hand Dynamometer; Sammons Preston, Bolingbrook, IL, USA). Low muscle strength was defined as grip strength < 28 kg for males or < 18 kg for females57. Reduced physical performance was defined as gait speed (GS) less than 1.0 m/s during a standardized 4-meter walk test.

Nutritional assessment, supplementation, and outcome definition

Following baseline evaluation, participants classified as being at high risk for sarcopenia— defined as presenting with either low muscle mass or low muscle strength— received individualized nutritional counseling from a registered dietitian. Nutritional status was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment Short-Form (MNA-SF), a validated screening tool for older adults.

Participants were provided with supplement-containing sachets and instructed to consume two servings per day for 12 weeks. Each serving (8 g) contained 4.1 g protein (including 0.98 g leucine, 0.58 g glutamate, 0.36 g aspartate, 0.39 g lysine, and 1.79 g other amino acids), 1 g fat, and 2.4 g carbohydrate, providing an additional 35 kcal per serving. Regular follow-up assessments were conducted throughout the 12-week intervention period. Participants were asked to return both used and unused supplement sachets to assess compliance. Compliance was defined as consumption of more than 80% of the prescribed sachets.

To evaluate inter-individual variability in response to the nutritional intervention, a post-hoc exploratory analysis was conducted in which participants were stratified according to observed changes in HGS and ASMI after the 12-week intervention, relative to baseline. Four response groups were defined: participants with improvements in both HGS and ASMI (AH); improvement in HGS alone (HI); improvement in ASMI alone (AI); and no improvement in either parameter (NN) after 12 weeks (Fig. 1).

NMR spectroscopy analysis

The plasma sample (100 μL) was mixed with an equal volume of plasma buffer (75 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.4), and the urine sample (180 μL) was mixed with 20 μL urine buffer (1.5 M sodium phosphate, pH 7.4) for analysis. NMR analysis was conducted using 600 MHz spectrometers with a TXI probe and the SampleJet robot cooling system set to 6 °C (Bruker Biospin GmbH, Rheinstetten, Germany).

The NMR analysis of plasma specimens was performed as described previously58. The 38 plasma metabolites were automatically identified, quantified, uploaded, and analyzed by plasma IVDr in plasma/serum B.I.Quant-PS platform. Parameters of lipoprotein subfractions were generated using the specialized Bruker IVDr Lipoprotein Subclass Analysis (B.I.-LISA) method. This unique approach involves mathematical analysis to quantify the −CH2 (δ = 1.25 ppm) and −CH3 (δ = 0.8 ppm) peaks of the 1D spectrum. The data were normalized to the Bruker QuantRef sample within Topspin using a PLS-2 regression model.

Two experiments were conducted in automation for urine sample: a 1D 1H experiment with solvent presaturation (64 scans, 65,536 data points, spectral width of 12,019.23 Hz) was followed by a 2D J-resolved experiment (2 scans, 8192 data points, spectral width of 10,026.74 Hz) at 300 K. All data were processed in automation using Bruker Topspin 3.6.2 and ICON-NMR. Bruker IVDr automatically performed the identification and quantification of metabolites in urine with the help of B.I.Quant-UR eTM.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Stool samples were collected in sterile containers and stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted using the iCatcher Stool DNA Kit (CatchGene Co., Ltd., Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The full-length bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using barcoded primers and the KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Roche). Amplicon libraries were prepared and sequenced on a PacBio Sequel IIe platform using circular consensus sequencing (CCS) mode to generate high-fidelity (HiFi) reads. CCS reads with a minimum predicted accuracy of Q30 were retained for downstream analyses. Sequence data were processed using QIIME2 (version 2022.11). Exact amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred using the DADA2 denoising pipeline, which resolves sequences at single-nucleotide resolution. Representative ASV sequences were taxonomically assigned using the NCBI 16S ribosomal RNA reference database. Feature tables were generated at both genus and species levels for subsequent analyses. ASV abundances were normalized within each sample by dividing individual ASV counts by the total ASV count per sample and scaling to 10,000 to account for differences in sequencing depth. Normalized ASV values were subsequently aggregated at the genus level for downstream statistical analyses and visualization.

Cell culture and treatments

Murine myoblast C2C12 cells (CRL-1772, ATCC, Manassas, VA) were cultured in a Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 4 mM glutamine, 4.5 g/L of glucose with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific (TFS), Waltham, MA, USA). Cells were incubated at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. For the investigation of the effect of trimethylamine (TMA) and trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), C2C12 cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS until 80% confluence, then the medium was replaced with a differentiation medium (DMEM with 2% horse serum (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA)) containing different concentrations of TMA or TMAO. During the differentiation stage, the medium was changed every day, and the formation of myotubes and their morphology were examined. Myotubes began to form after 3-4 days, and multinucleated muscle fiber cultures were studied after 7-10 days.

Myotube and nuclear staining

C2C12 cells at the mid-differentiation stage were treated with 0.1 or 1 mM TMA or TMAO, 5 days after induction of myotube formation. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. Following fixation, cells were rinsed 3 times with PBS, and non-specific sites were blocked with PBS containing 2.5% IgG-free BSA for 1 hour at room temperature. Cells were incubated with Myosin Heavy Chain 4 (MyHC4) monoclonal antibody (clone MF20, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank [DSHB], Cat# MF20) at 1:1000 in PBS/2.5% BSA overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor™ 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Cat# A11032) at 1:1000 in PBS/2.5% BSA for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Molecular Probes, Cat# H1399) for 1 hour at room temperature. The images of nucleus (Hoechst 33342) and myotubes (MF20) were analyzed using the “Myotube Analyzer” software, with conversion to pixel units for quantification. Cell count was defined as the number of nuclei in a defined area. The fusion index was calculated as the ratio of nuclei within the MF20-positive region to the total number of nuclei. Myotube diameter was measured from the midline to the edge of the MF20-stained region, with >100 measurements per image used to generate a distribution of myotube diameters.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation. For metabolite data, zero values were imputed as 50% of the minimum non-zero value. Non-parametric tests, including the Kruskal-Wallis H and Mann-Whitney U tests, were applied when the group sample size was <20. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to determine differences between categorical variables. An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusting for age and sex, was performed to compare between-group differences. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for within-group comparisons between baseline and 12-week measurements. Metabolites showing significant pre-post changes were summarized as fold changes (12-week/baseline). As an exploratory step to facilitate biological interpretation, metabolites were further prioritized based on fold-change magnitude (> 1.5 or < 0.66), and those exhibiting opposite directional changes between the AH and HI groups were highlighted for downstream interpretation. To account for multiple testing in metabolomics analyses, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was additionally performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Changes in ASMI (ΔASMI) and handgrip strength (ΔHGS) were calculated as the difference between post‑intervention and baseline values (12‑week – baseline). These continuous variables were used in correlation and multiple regression analyses adjusted for age and sex to evaluate associations with metabolite changes. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1) and SPSS (IBM, New York, NY, USA), with a two-sided p value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) was employed for multivariate analyses, including partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), pathway analysis, and correlation analysis. Post-hoc power analyses were conducted based on paired pre–post comparisons of metabolites within the AI and NN groups. Effect sizes were calculated from observed mean differences and standard deviations, and statistical power was estimated accordingly.
