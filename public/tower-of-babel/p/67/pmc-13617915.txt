RankVar: machine learning-based variant ranking and reinterpretation for rare genetic diseases
Authors: Yuan Zhang, Mian Umair Ahsan, Peng Wang, Xiaoqi Lin, Ian M. Campbell, Cong Liu, Wendy K. Chung, Chunhua Weng, Kai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrior biological knowledge and phenotype information can help identify disease genes from whole genome/exome sequencing studies, but how best to incorporate external knowledge with variant data remains challenging. We developed a machine learning algorithm called RankVar to prioritize causative variants for rare diseases, based on clinical notes and genome/exome sequencing profiles.MethodsRankVar uses a random forest classifier trained on ~ 1 million variants from the 1000 Genomes Project with spiked-in pathogenic variants. For testing, we compiled sequencing data and phenotype information from several independent datasets: 260 subjects from the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) with positive genetic diagnosis of various Mendelian diseases, 135 subjects from Birth Defects Biorepository (BDB), as well as 356 and 97 subjects with candidate causal variants for autism spectrum disorders from the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC) and the Simons Foundation Powering Autism Research for Knowledge (SPARK), respectively.ResultsRankVar achieves a top 10 variant accuracy of 90.0%, 81.5%, 46.1%, and 76.3% for CHOP, BDB, SSC, and SPARK, respectively, with improved performance over existing approaches. Notably, RankVar successfully identified X-linked and Y-linked disease-causal variants, such as KDM6A (p.N915Kfs5*) and SRY (p.W98X), as the top candidate variants. Moreover, we evaluated RankVar for genomic reinterpretation of 130 unsolved CHOP cases with hearing loss and successfully identified 61 candidate causal variants after manual review.ConclusionsIn summary, RankVar performed favorably relative to existing methods in our evaluation, accommodated different genetic models and X/Y chromosome variants, and may provide a useful framework for prioritizing variants in monogenic or oligogenic diseases. We anticipate that RankVar may aid in primary genetic diagnosis, genome reinterpretation of previously unsolved cases, and the discovery of novel disease genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13073-026-01701-2.

Background

Every year, approximately 7 million children worldwide are born with serious genetic diseases, such as Mendelian disorders caused by chromosome abnormalities or genetic variants in a single gene [1, 2]. Despite the remarkable advances in rare disease genetics, current technology still fails to identify the underlying causal variant in a significant fraction of affected individuals. It is estimated that the diagnostic rate using exome or genome sequencing is generally between 20% and 50% depending on the indication for testing [3, 4]. Diagnostic yields also varied for disorders likely to have a monogenic cause than for disorders likely to have a complex cause [3]. Newly discovered disease genes and gene-phenotype relationships are reported constantly, aiding in the diagnosis of previously unsolved cases [5, 6]. Bioinformatics methods to identify likely diagnostic variants also update frequently, which may re-rank variants so that true causal variants rank higher from the list of candidates. Therefore, periodic reanalysis of the undiagnosed cases could result in new molecular diagnoses over time [7, 8]. However, the implementation of routine reanalysis remains limited in clinical laboratories due to systemic barriers such as reimbursement [9].

Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) have been quite successful in the identification of novel disease-associated genes and have been used for clinical diagnostics [10–12]. An individual exome typically harbors over 30,000 variants compared with the genomic reference sequence, and roughly 10,000 of them are predicted to lead to nonsynonymous amino acid substitutions, alterations of conserved splice site residues, or small insertions or deletions [13]. Even after applying sophisticated bioinformatic tools to remove variants with high population allele frequency and low sequencing quality, hundreds of variants can remain [14]. Identifying the causative variants from this list is therefore time-consuming and requires domain knowledge [14, 15]. Several computational tools, including ANNOVAR [16], snpEff [17], VEP [18], Jannovar [18] and VAT [19], address this problem mainly by employing a number of predefined variant filtering steps, such as keeping nonsynonymous and splice variants, variants with high conservation scores, and those with low alternative allele frequencies. However, each genome is thought to harbor ∼100 loss of function (LOF) variants with ~ 20 genes completely inactivated [20]. This implies that merely ranking candidate genes on the basis of the rarity and predicted pathogenicity of variants found in them without the clinical context will not result in accurate identifications of disease-associated genes from WES/WGS data.

Prior biological knowledge and phenotype information may also help pinpoint genes that contribute to disease [21–24]. Several phenotype-aware prioritization tools can incorporate phenotype data, such as Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms, in identifying causal genes from next-generation sequencing (NGS) data, including AI-MARRVEL [25], Exomiser [13], Xrare [26], PhenIX [27], LIRICAL [28], Genomiser [29], Phenoxome [30], VARPP [31], Amelie [32], Moon [33], GEM [34], Emedgene [35], DeepPVP [36], and others. To ensure relevance and practicality, in our study, we identified actively maintained software programs and further narrowed down the selection to include locally executable software with high performance based on recent benchmarking studies. Three methods were ultimately selected for our benchmarking: Exomiser, PhenIX, and LIRICAL. Exomiser [13] is particularly effective for diagnosing Mendelian disorders by integrating phenotype similarity scores, population allele frequencies, and functional variant predictions. However, Exomiser may struggle with complex cases involving polygenic contributions or ambiguous phenotype descriptions. PhenIX [27], part of the phenotype-driven variant interpretation suite, emphasizes phenotype matching and gene prioritization by comparing patient phenotype with known gene-phenotype associations. Its reliance on curated knowledge bases may, however, limit its applicability for novel or extremely rare phenotypes. LIRICAL [28] employs a probabilistic framework to evaluate phenotype and genotype data simultaneously and excels at ranking causative genes even in ambiguous scenarios. However, its performance may depend on the completeness of phenotype data and accurate ontology mapping.

Even though these phenotype-driven approaches have gained wide applications in clinical diagnosis, the diagnostic yield in real clinical settings remains limited and falls short of the performance shown in simulation studies [37]. The potential causes for the discrepancy might be the incompleteness, heterogeneity, imprecision, and noise in disease phenotype descriptions, or challenges in addressing complex genetic mechanisms [24, 38]. Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) has garnered substantial interest for improving the interpretation of clinical genome sequencing. To address the limitations of existing methods, we developed RankVar, a method that integrates machine learning algorithm with phenotype data and sequencing profiles to prioritize disease-causing genes and variants. This approach combines the interpretive power of machine learning with the precision of phenotype-driven analyses, offering a promising strategy for advancing the diagnosis of Mendelian disorders and other rare diseases. More importantly, when combined with text-processing algorithms such as Doc2HPO or PhenoGPT [39–41], the method can directly use clinical notes to identify candidate disease variants from WGS/WES data. Thus, RankVar has the unique advantage that it may be directly integrated into clinical or research workflows, enabling variant ranking/interpretation from sequencing data without the intermediate process of phenotype characterization. We trained our model on 2504 samples from the 1000 Genomes Project (1KGP) with spiked-in pathogenic/likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants, and tested its performance on seven independent datasets (Table 1): 260 subjects from the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) with positive genetic diagnosis of various Mendelian diseases [42], 356 from the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC) [43], 97 from the Simons Foundation Powering Autism Research for Knowledge (SPARK) [44], one subject reported by Kleyner et al [45], and 135 subjects from the Birth Defects Biorepository (BDB). We also evaluated on 5912 synthetic subjects derived from Phenopacket Store [46], and 130 unsolved CHOP cases with hearing loss as a phenotype [42].

Methods

Training and validation datasets

The 1000 Genomes Project (1KGP), an international collaboration, has sequenced the whole genome of approximately 3,000 individuals from different worldwide populations [47]. We used whole genome sequencing Variant Call Format (VCF) files from 2504 unrelated samples representing 26 populations within 1KGP as the training data. We first added one known pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant (“spike-in variant”) from the ClinVar database [48] (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar, updated to 2024–06–11 on hg38 coordinate) to each individual, designating it as the causal variants. For each causal variant, we mapped the corresponding gene to HPO terms using the HPO database [49] (https://hpo.jax.org/data/annotations). We then randomly selected between 1 and 40 HPO terms to simulate each individual’s clinical phenotype. To more closely model real-world variability, we introduced additional “noise” by randomly adding additional HPO terms (“noise terms”) from the entire set of available HPO annotations, with fractions ranging from 0.2 to 0.5. We repeated the spike-in process 100 times, each time creating a new training dataset (each consisting of 2504 samples) by assigning 2504 different causal variants, gene–phenotype mapping, and simulated HPO terms to 2504 genomes. Altogether, 250,400 simulated samples were generated for training. The detail criteria for selecting spike-in ClinVar variants used in training are provided in the Supplementary Methods, and the full list of variants is available in Additional file 1: Table S1.

As a validation dataset, we used 698 related samples from 1KGP. The overall process was similar to that of the training set. However, unlike in training data, these pathogenic variants were exclusively chosen from ClinVar updates between 2024‑06‑11 and 2024‑09‑17 on hg38 coordinate. This design allowed us to evaluate our model’s ability to detect newly reported pathogenic variants in the updated ClinVar database.

Testing datasets

We used multiple independent testing datasets to evaluate RankVar, including 260 subjects from the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) with positive genetic diagnosis of various Mendelian diseases [42], 356 from the Simons Simplex Collection (SSC) [43], 97 from the Simons Foundation Powering Autism Research for Knowledge (SPARK) cohort [44], and 135 subjects from the Birth Defects Biorepository (BDB) [50]. We also evaluated RankVar using 130 unsolved CHOP cases with hearing loss as a phenotype [42] and 5912 synthetic subjects derived from Phenopacket Store [46]. The CHOP positive genetic diagnosis and BDB cohorts were used to evaluate RankVar on clinically documented causal variants/genes, the SSC and SPARK cohorts were used to evaluate candidate causal de novo coding variants in autism spectrum disorder, the CHOP hearing loss cohort was used to assess the utility of RankVar for genomic reinterpretation, and the Phenopacket Store derived dataset was used to evaluate generalizability in synthetic phenotype-driven diagnostic scenarios.

CHOP positive genetic diagnosis cohort: We utilized data from 260 subjects at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) as a testing dataset [42]. They were affected with various Mendelian diseases, with a manually documented positive genetic diagnosis from the clinical laboratories. Each case includes VCF files of exome sequencing data, phenotype information, and a diagnostic variant identified through molecular methods or curated by clinical experts. To ensure patient privacy and to safeguard genome information, all analyses were conducted in an environment called Arcus within CHOP. We utilized HPO terms that are extracted from clinical notes either manually or automatically using Phenominal [51] by clinicians. Only positive phenotypic features relevant to the presenting clinical notes were recorded, while excluded terms (e.g., “no seizures”) were not captured, with an average of 10 HPO terms per individual.

SSC and SPARK autism cohorts: We further used the Simons Foundation Powering Autism Research for Knowledge (SPARK) [52] and Simons Simplex Collection (SSC) [43] cohorts as testing datasets. The whole genome sequencing data of SPARK and SSC were made available via Simons Foundation Autism Research Initiative (SFARI) and can be requested through SFARI Base (https://www.sfari.org/resource/sfari-base). All participants were recruited under a centralized institutional review board (IRB) protocol (Western IRB Protocol no. 20151664). De novo coding variants identified in SSC and SPARK cohorts, as reported by the original studies in their supplementary materials, were mapped to our SSC and SPARK identifiers [53]. We identified 1004 probands from the SSC cohort and 213 probands from SPARK cohorts, all diagnosed with autism spectrum disorders, carrying de novo coding variants. We recognize that there may be false positive (non-diagnostic) variants in this list, and therefore performed analyses on genes within the SFARI gene list, which includes genes implicated in autism susceptibility (https://gene.sfari.org/). Among genes with de novo mutations, 24.9% in SSC and 35.1% in SPARK were classified as SFARI categories 1–3/S (high-confidence autism risk genes). Finally, 356 probands from the SSC cohort and 97 probands from SPARK cohorts who carry candidate causal variants were included in the testing datasets (Additional file 1: Table S2-S3). Note that our ranking analyses were performed on all variants in probands without knowing the de novo status. Additionally, we also tested a subset of 18 SPARK samples with confirmed disease causal genes to assess RankVar’s performance. Wright et al. [54] previously identified 1,903 returnable likely pathogenic or pathogenic variants in 1861 SPARK participants through microarray genotyping and exome sequencing. Since a fraction of them are also subject to WGS, we were able to match 18 of these participants to our SPARK WGS dataset and used the list of returnable variants to evaluate RankVar’s performance.

Birth Defects Biorepository cohort: The Birth Defects Biorepository (BDB) is an IRB-approved protocol established by the Center for Fetal Diagnosis and Treatment and the Department of Surgery at the CHOP [50]. Its primary purpose is to provide a mechanism to store and permit access to biological specimens and longitudinal participant clinical and research data to support and enhance research on children with birth defects. Patients diagnosed with a birth defect, mothers carrying a fetus with a prenatally diagnosed birth defect followed at CHOP, and their biological family members are eligible to participate in the BDB. Whole-genome sequencing of all samples was performed on Illumina NovaSeq 6000, utilizing sequencing-by-synthesis kits to produce 151 bp paired-end reads. Output from Illumina software was processed by the Picard data-processing pipeline to yield CRAM or BAM files containing demultiplexed, aggregated aligned reads. GATK HaplotypeCaller is used to call SNVs and indels aligned to hg38. Manta and cnvnator are used to call copy number variants (CNVs) and structural variants (SVs) [55, 56]. A subset of these samples is also subject to clinical exome or clinical microarray with documented genetic testing results, providing us with clinically validated disease causal genes. In total, we used data from 135 subjects (including 16 with causal gene disrupted by SVs/CNVs) with WGS, phenotypes, and causal genes for evaluation.

CHOP hearing loss reinterpretation cohort: We utilized whole exome sequencing data from 130 subjects affected with hearing loss, manually curated by clinical experts at CHOP but without known causal genes or variants, to conduct additional test. Some of these subjects may have syndromic hearing loss. Using ClinGen (https://search.clinicalgenome.org), we identified 176 candidate genes associated with hearing loss (Additional file 1: Table S4). We compared this candidate gene list with the top predicted genes for each person to assess the overlap and for additional manual examination.

Phenopacket Store derived synthetic testing dataset: To further assess RankVar’s generalizability, we evaluated its performance using a synthetic dataset derived from the Phenopacket Store, which contains over 8,000 published cases with detailed phenotype, disease, and genetic information. This dataset enables a more comprehensive evaluation of the model’s performance in realistic diagnostic scenarios. Because Phenopacket Store does not provide matched sequencing data (e.g., VCFs), we are instead using 698 WGS validation data from 1KGP to simulate the testing datasets. A total of 5,912 coding/splicing variants with associated HPO terms from the Phenopacket Store were included in the analysis (Additional file 1: Table S5). For each simulated dataset, we randomly assigned one Phenopacket variant (“spike-in variant”) and associated Phenopacket phenotypes to each individual and labeled it as the causal variant. We repeated the spike-in process 10 times, generating 10 simulated testing datasets, each consisting of 698 samples with a unique set of Phenopacket annotated causal variants and the corresponding HPO terms.

Sample preprocessing and annotation

For all datasets mentioned above, we first detected variants (GT = ’alt’) from individual sequencing data as VCF files using Bcftools (version: 1.17) and annotated the variants based on ANNOVAR [16] (human genome hg38). First, variants annotated as missense and loss-of-function variants, including frameshift deletion, frameshift insertion, startloss, stopgain, stoploss and canonical splicing (< = 2 bp from exon/intron boundary), were retained. We then filtered the calls to keep variants with genotype quality (GQ) ≥ 25, Allelic depth (AD) ≥ 15, and an allele frequency (gnomAD) < 0.01%. In addition, we also retained, “whitelisted” variants with a gnomAD > 0.01% that are annotated as known pathogenic variants in clinical databases (this list is available as part of the RankVar software). Many of these “whitelisted” variants could be causal variants for recessive diseases.

Selection of features in machine learning

RankVar uses a machine learning algorithm to prioritize causative genes/variants for rare disease based on a rich set of phenotypic and genetic features. A detailed overview of feature processing in our predictive model is summarized in Additional file 2: Supplementary Methods and Additional file 1: Table S6. Briefly, we selected 72 raw features spanning gene constraint, disease database, evolutionary conservation, inheritance patterns, variant impact, phenotype-based gene prioritization, dbNSFP function predictions, splicing prediction, and gene expression regulation. Additionally, we have implemented an option that allows users to input a list of genes that can be disrupted by CNVs/SVs. These genes are treated as LOF sensitive, with RankVar assigning LOF-related features to them and incorporating them into the gene prioritization process. We observed that many of the 72 features were highly correlated, so we performed hierarchical clustering to select the optimal subset. Dendrograms were generated to visualize the hierarchical relationships among features.

To evaluate whether model performance was driven by ClinVar derived gene-level information, we performed a sensitivity analysis by retraining and evaluating RankVar after removing the clinvarNumP/LP feature. We compared the performance of the model without clinvarNumP/LP with the original RankVar model across the synthetic validation dataset and the independent CHOP, BDB, SSC, and SPARK testing datasets.

Additional evaluation of phenotype-matched and phenotype-unmatched P/LP variants

To evaluate whether RankVar can distinguish between multiple P/LP variants present in the same individual, we performed an additional analysis in the testing dataset. We identified individuals carrying two different P/LP variants, including one variant in a gene implicated in the individual’s disease and another variant in a gene not clearly related to the presenting phenotype. RankVar was then used to rank all candidate genes/variants for each individual using the same feature set and ranking procedure as in the main analysis. We compared the rankings of the disease-relevant P/LP variants with those of phenotype-unmatched P/LP variants to assess whether RankVar preferentially prioritizes variants relevant to an individual’s phenotype, rather than uniformly prioritizing all P/LP variants.

Model training

We used 2504 samples from 1KGP with spiked-in pathogenic variants from ClinVar to train RankVar. Three machine learning algorithms—XGBoost, random forest, and a neural network—were evaluated. To determine the optimal parameters, 698 validation dataset was used for tuning hyper-parameters. The parameters that yielded the highest performance in ranking causal variants with the top 1, top 5, and top 10 were selected. To avoid overfitting due to a ~ 250:1 negative-to-diagnostic variant ratio in the training sample, we weighted the minority class more heavily when calculating impurity scores during tree construction. We also assessed feature importance using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [57], which calculates the contributions of each feature to the model’s prediction. Finally, we are benchmarking RankVar with Exomiser, PhenIX, and LIRICAL (Additional file 2: Supplementary Methods).

Case study

Kleyner et al. [45] reported a case study identifying a de novo mutation in the ANKRD11, which led to the recognition of KBG syndrome in the sequenced proband. In this family, the parents and siblings were healthy, while the proband exhibited symptoms including epilepsy, severe developmental delay, distinct facial features, and hand anomalies, without an identified genetic diagnosis. We utilized the clinical notes and genetic data from this case to validate RankVar for prioritizing the causal gene. Since the VCF files for the family are available as supplementary materials of the original publication, we also used this case as an example in the RankVar software package to illustrate its use.

Subjects in the CHOP dataset receive molecular diagnoses through clinical laboratories with curation from clinical experts. For case studies, each selected case underwent detailed review, including analyzing the prioritization rankings generated by the model and comparing them to established benchmarking methods. We also performed feature importance analysis to understand why the method works for each particular case.

Approximately 50% of the diagnostic variants in the training, validation, CHOP, and BDB testing datasets are classified as missense, whereas in the SSC and SPARK testing sets, over 70% of de novo variants are missense (Additional file 2: Fig. S2A). After filtering for SFARI genes, 52% and 65% of variants are missense in SPARK and SSC, respectively. In the BDB dataset, which included CNVs/SVs, 21% of the variants are startloss and 9% are stopgain. Additionally, about 92% of subjects from CHOP testing dataset have one diagnostic variant (Additional file 2: Fig. S2B), each having an average of 10 HPO terms (Additional file 2: Fig. S2C). Meanwhile, 27.0% and 12.2% of affected individuals have 2 or more de novo coding variants for SSC and SPARK datasets, respectively (Additional file 2: Fig. S2D and S2E). For BDB dataset, 31.9% of individuals have 2 or more diagnostic variants (Additional file 2: Fig. S2F).

Model development using synthetic training and validation datasets

For the training and validation datasets, we randomly selected between 1–10, 1–20, 1–30, and 1–40 HPO terms per individual to simulate clinical phenotype. To simulate realistic scenarios, we introduced “noise terms” at fractions ranging from 0.2 to 0.5. The results indicated that the number of randomly selected HPO terms and the fraction of noise affected the model's performance slightly (Additional file 2: Fig. S3). Balancing both accuracy and computational efficiency, we finalized a configuration of 1 to 20 HPO terms per individual with a noise fraction of 0.2.

Across the validation dataset, we compared the performance of three machine learning algorithms, including random forest, XGBoost, and a neural network for ranking causal variants. The random forest algorithm outperformed both XGBoost and neural network in ranking causal variants (Fig. 2B), achieving a precision-recall area under the curve of 0.98, compared to 0.96 for XGBoost and 0.89 for the neural network (Fig. 2C). This highlights the model’s effectiveness in detecting newly reported pathogenic variants from the updated ClinVar database. In the original random forest model, the top ranked features included the Phen2Gene score, the gene-level ClinVar P/LP burden feature (clinvarNumP/LP), and the MetaRNN score (Fig. 2D). Importantly, clinvarNumP/LP represents a gene-level prior summarizing previously reported P/LP variants in a gene and does not encode the ClinVar clinical significance of the candidate variant itself. Based on its superior validation performance, we selected the random forest algorithm from scikit-learn as the backbone machine-learning algorithm for our study.

To assess whether this ClinVar derived gene-level feature (clinvarNumP/LP) drove model performance, we retrained and evaluated RankVar after removing clinvarNumP/LP. The model without clinvarNumP/LP showed largely similar performance with the original model. In the validation dataset, the random forest model continued to outperform XGBoost and the neural network, achieving a PR-AUC of 0.98, compared with 0.92 for XGBoost and 0.85 for the neural network (Additional file 2: Fig. S4). After removing clinvarNumP/LP, the top three features were the Phen2Gene score, MetaRNN score, and OMIM gene score. In independent testing datasets, RankVar without clinvarNumP/LP achieved top 10 variant accuracies of 90.0%, 82.2%, 48.3%, and 75.3% for CHOP, BDB, SSC, and SPARK, respectively, which were comparable to the original model results of 90.0%, 81.5%, 46.1%, and 76.3%. These results suggest that RankVar’s performance was not primarily driven by the ClinVar derived gene-level burden feature.

RankVar outperforms established methods on three independent datasets

To benchmark RankVar with other widely used tools, we evaluated three locally executable, freely available methods, including LIRICAL, Exomiser, and PhenIX, using four independent datasets (CHOP, BDB, SSC, and SPARK). Our model outperformed all three benchmark methods in ranking the known diagnostic variants in CHOP and BDB dataset, or candidate causal variants in SSC and SPARK dataset (Fig. 2E; Additional file 2: Fig. S5). RankVar achieves top 10 variant accuracy of 90.0%, 81.5%, 46.1%, and 76.3% for CHOP, BDB, SSC, and SPARK, respectively. Additionally, across the four independent datasets, RankVar achieves over 9% higher top 1 accuracy and over 10% higher top 10 accuracy than benchmark methods.

For SSC and SPARK cohorts, we further evaluated RankVar on genes within the SFARI gene list (categories 1–3/S, representing high-confidence autism risk genes). To more accurately reflect model performance, we conducted a secondary evaluation restricted de novo coding variants in SFARI gene category 1/S, which represent the highest-confidence autism risk genes. After filtering variants by SFARI categories 1/S, our model’s performance improved substantially (Fig. 2F). In the SSC dataset, top 1, top 5, and top 10 accuracies increased from 23.3%, 37.6%, and 46.1% to 46.0%, 63.3%, and 68.3%, respectively. Similarly, in the SPARK dataset, top 1, top 5, and top 10 accuracies rose from 52.6%, 74.2%, and 76.3% to 65.7%, 88.6%, and 88.6%, respectively. We also tested a subset of 18 SPARK samples with clinically confirmed disease causal genes to further assess RankVar’s performance. RankVar continued to outperform all three benchmark methods, with substantial improvements in accuracy, achieving top 1, top 5, and top 10 accuracies of 77.8%, 88.9%, and 94.4%, respectively (Additional file 2: Fig. S6). However, we acknowledge that there can be high correlation between SFARI gene and phenotype (autism)-based gene scores. Therefore, it is not surprising that filtering by SFARI gene category may enrich phenotype-relevant genes, leading to higher Phen2Gene scores and making cases more likely to be predicted as positive during our test. On the other hand, this can help us prioritize a set of genes that are more likely to be diagnostic in real case studies.

Accuracy of RankVar on different variant types and inheritance patterns

Given that over 70% of the variants were classified as missense, we evaluated the model’s accuracy separately for each variant type. Our model performed better on loss-of-function variants than on missense variants across the independent testing datasets (Additional file 2: Fig. S7), likely due to the stronger weights on functional impact of loss-of-function variants. Additionally, the RankVar model demonstrated higher accuracy for candidate genes with dominant inheritance mode (as defined in OMIM database) compared with recessive ones across CHOP, BDB, SSC, and SPARK, likely reflecting the differing model weights underlying these inheritance modes. Among the 260 CHOP subjects with known diagnostic genes, 56 have a causal gene with a recessive mode of inheritance, while others have the diagnostic genes annotated as dominant/mixed in OMIM. Of the 56 subjects, 24 carry compound heterozygous variants, 29 carry homozygous variants, but the other 3 carry only one variant in the diagnostic gene. For SSC/SPARK, as autism spectrum disorders likely have oligogenic architecture, the traditional dichotomization of dominant/recessive genes may be less relevant here; we are using de novo variants as candidate causal variants in the SSC/SPARK datasets, which may partially explain the higher performance on genes with dominant mode of inheritance. Nevertheless, if there is a clear expectation that a recessive inheritance pattern for a disease is observed in a family, it is possible to use this information to adjust RankVar to use this evidence.

Evaluation on phenotype-matched and phenotype-unmatched P/LP variants

Because individuals may carry P/LP variants that are unrelated to the presenting disease, we further evaluated whether RankVar could distinguish disease relevant P/LP variants from phenotype unmatched P/LP variants within the same individual. In the testing dataset, we identified individuals carrying two different P/LP variants, one in a gene implicated in the given disease and another in an apparently irrelevant gene. RankVar successfully prioritized the disease relevant P/LP variants over phenotype unmatched P/LP variants. P/LP variants in disease relevant genes had a mean gene rank of 1.18 (SD = 1.63), whereas P/LP variants in apparently irrelevant genes had a substantially lower priority, with a mean gene rank of 15.63 (SD = 11.83; Additional file 2: Fig. S8). Therefore, RankVar preferentially prioritizes variants relevant to an individual’s phenotype, rather than uniformly prioritizing all P/LP variants.

Examination of selected cases

To further test the efficacy of our model, we utilized phenotype and genotype data from one proband, who had a de novo mutation in ANKRD11 and was diagnosed with KBG syndrome previously. We evaluated RankVar on the exome data on the proband, but without using exome sequencing data from parents, that is, we did not consider de novo status of genetic variants. When integrated into our model, ANKRD11 was ranked as the top candidate gene by RankVar, demonstrating the model's ability to nominate candidate genes accurately. We used this case as an example in the RankVar software repository to help users understand the procedure to use the software for real data analysis.

In addition, we also examined several subjects at CHOP who received positive genetic diagnoses from the clinical laboratory previously. We examined a subset of these individuals with known diagnoses to understand how RankVar works differently than other methods (Fig. 3). The CHD7 gene is the primary genetic cause of CHARGE syndrome. For one subject, our model placed the CHD7 variant (p.Y835Sfs*14) at the top rank, outperforming other benchmarking tools that all ranked it outside the top 10. Examination of feature plot shows that phenotype information did not contribute to the good performance of this case, but OMIM_score and the frameshift status of the variant are the main contributors. Another case involved an individual carrying recessive disease-causing variants in the SBDS gene (p.K62X and c.258 + 2 T > C), where our model ranked these variants within the top 1 and top 5, respectively, again surpassing the performance of benchmarking algorithms. Examination of the feature plot shows that phenotype information plays a significant role in the correct identification of causal variants in this case. Kabuki syndrome is often caused by KDM6A, following an X-linked dominant inheritance pattern. For an individual diagnosed with Kabuki syndrome, our method ranked the variant (KDM6A: NC_000023.11:g.45079189del; p.N915Kfs*5) as the top 1, outperforming the benchmarking methods. Again, we found that frameshift status of the causal variant is a major contributor to the correct prediction, in addition to the OMIM score. Finally, a case involving a Y-linked disease-causing variant (SRY: p.W98X) was also successfully prioritized by our model, which ranked it as the top 1. Although this variant is classified as VUS in ClinVar, the phenotype information results in very high score in the feature plot for RankVar, thus ranking this variant at the top. Predicting candidate causal gene for subjects affected with hearing loss at CHOP

We applied our model to predict candidate causal genes for 130 subjects affected with hearing loss but without a clinically documented causal gene in the clinical notes. All notes were manually reviewed with a short clinical annotation (a few words) of phenotype. We acknowledge that it is possible that a recent genetic diagnosis was made by clinical laboratories but was not reflected in clinical notes. We obtained VCF files from clinical exome sequencing and performed variant reinterpretation by RankVar. Among this group of individuals, the RankVar model identified 24 variants—within the hearing loss gene curation expert panel—as the top-ranked candidates, including GJB2, MYH9, MYH14, MYO7A, STRC, TBC1D24, LOXHD1, COL9A2, ACTG1, and BTD. Evaluation of RankVar on variants and phenotypes from phenopacket store

Of the 5,912 variants with associated HPO terms from the Phenopacket Store dataset included in our analysis, 61.7% were classified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic, 31.5% as uncertain significance, 6.7% as have conflicting class of pathogenicity, and 0.07% as benign/likely benign, based on the ClinVar annotations. To assess RankVar’s applicability to real-world phenotypes, we evaluated its performance using a synthetic dataset derived from Phenopacket Store (See Methods). RankVar consistently demonstrated strong performance across all simulated samples, ranking approximately 88% of causal variants in the top 1, 98% within the top 5, and 100% within the top 10 (Fig. 4C; Additional file 2: Fig. S9), supporting its effectiveness in realistic diagnostic scenarios.

Discussion

In this manuscript, we describe RankVar, a machine learning-based variant ranking and reinterpretation method for whole genome/exome sequencing data. The RankVar method utilizes a random forest model trained with around 1 million variants from thousands of 1KGP samples to prioritize causative genes/variants for rare disease based on the clinical notes and sequencing profiles of affected subjects. Our method effectively handled different genetic models and X/Y chromosome variants and can be used for more complex polygenic diseases such as autism disorders, in addition to single-gene rare diseases. Moreover, the model improves the rate of accurate genetic diagnosis by identifying candidate causal genes in unsolved cases, outperforming benchmarked methods and proving its reliability in real-world clinical applications.

Our pipeline outperformed existing benchmark tools such as Exomiser, PhenIX, and LIRICAL. The superior accuracy of our method in prioritizing causative variants is evident from the accurate top-10 variants prioritized achieved across the CHOP, SSC, BDB, and SPARK datasets. Exomiser, PhenIX, and LIRICAL are widely cited and well-established tools in the research community, frequently used as benchmarks. However, their use in the clinical setting is not yet universal or standardized. In this study, we used these tools as benchmark comparators to enable a rigorous and representative evaluation of our method against current phenotype-aware prioritization approaches. Unlike Exomiser and PhenIX, which rely heavily on pre-curated gene-phenotype associations, our machine learning model dynamically learns patterns from large-scale variant data and phenotype features, allowing it to generalize better to new data. This adaptability is particularly crucial in cases involving loss-of-function variants, where our pipeline outperformed benchmark tools by effectively leveraging feature importance metrics derived from TreeExplainer. Additionally, the pipeline showed strong performance with dominant inheritance patterns, likely due to its ability to prioritize variants with higher predicted pathogenicity in a robust manner. In contrast, tools like LIRICAL, while effective in evaluating both phenotype and genotype data simultaneously, depend heavily on the completeness and precision of phenotype descriptions. Our method addresses these limitations by integrating both structured phenotype data and clinical notes, allowing it to perform well even in scenarios where phenotype information is incomplete or ambiguous. AI-MARRVEL [25], a Knowledge-Driven AI System for diagnosing Mendelian disorders, has achieved superior accuracy compared to existing methods (e.g., Exomiser, LIRICAL, and PhenIX) for genetic diagnosis. However, due to licensing restrictions, the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) related database is not included in the public download, thus, direct assessment of AI-MARRVEL was not feasible.

We initially applied XGBoost, random forest, and neural network algorithms as machine learning models. Since our selected feature set is relatively small (20 features), we found that the neural network did not outperform traditional approaches. Based on the validation performance, we selected the random forest algorithm from scikit-learn as the backbone machine-learning algorithm for our study. A key advantage of our approach is its scalability and flexibility in handling diverse genetic models and complex phenotypes. A random forest algorithm can accommodate a wide range of input data types and is not constrained by curated knowledge bases, as is the case with many benchmark tools. This flexibility enables our pipeline to adapt to various genetic architectures, including polygenic contributions and sex chromosome variants. Furthermore, the interpretability provided by TreeExplainer allows our model to dynamically adjust to new data and prioritize features most relevant to the specific case, making it better suited for diseases with heterogeneous phenotypic presentations, such as autism spectrum disorders. Autism spectrum disorder encompasses a broad spectrum of clinical manifestations, genetic contributions, and comorbid conditions [58–60], making traditional gene-disease association methods less effective in capturing its full complexity. By integrating phenotype and genotype information in a dynamic, data-driven manner, our pipeline enhances variant prioritization for autism and other complex disorders where genetic and clinical variability pose significant challenges.

To better reflect real-world diagnostic conditions, we re-evaluated RankVar without excluding all ClinVar-reported pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants from the background genomes. Our initial strategy aimed to minimize confounding during training by ensuring that only intentionally “spiked-in” variants represented disease-causing events. However, in clinical practice, individuals often carry pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants unrelated to their diagnosed condition. Excluding all such variants can result in an overly idealized evaluation. In the repeat analysis, we retained background ClinVar pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants and found that RankVar’s performance remained robust and consistent with prior results. Additionally, we used 1KGP genomes for spiked-in training to simulate the candidate variant environment of clinical sequencing analysis. The 1KGP background variants provided realistic competing candidates and negative examples, allowing RankVar to learn to prioritize phenotype matched causal variants within each individual genome rather than classify isolated variants. Thus, the 1KGP background was used to define the ranking context, not as a direct genome wide predictor of the pathogenicity of the spiked-in variant.

Our approach also has several limitations. The current model relies on HPO annotations to map clinical phenotypes to genes. The HPO team is continuing the development of HPO and integration of other model organism phenotypes, which will keep improving the diagnostic rate of HPO based methods. Moreover, our model is primarily suited for prioritizing coding and splicing variants, which limits its capacity to prioritize noncoding variants. Noncoding regions, including regulatory elements such as enhancers, promoters, and untranslated regions (UTRs), are increasingly recognized for their role in disease. Since the vast majority of ClinVar pathogenic variants are in coding regions, our model would be less powerful for noncoding regulatory variants, even though phenotype score and a few regulatory function prediction features like CADD were included. The development of more robust noncoding variant prioritization tools and the integration of datasets like ENCODE or GTEx to provide tissue-specific regulatory context could enhance the pipeline’s capability in this area. This expansion would be especially important for diseases driven by regulatory variants or epigenetic mechanisms. Another challenge is the differing proportions of missense variants across datasets. In the CHOP testing dataset, approximately 50% of variants are classified as missense, whereas in the SPARK and SSC datasets, over 70% of variants fall into this category. This likely reflects the fact that CHOP cases are enriched for confirmed pathogenic variants (particularly LOF), while the autism cohorts include individuals with autism without intellectual disability for whom de novo LOF coding variants are unlikely to be major contributors. The SPARK and SSC datasets include de novo coding variants, many of which are missense and not necessarily pathogenic. Moreover, our model's performance on CHOP surpassed that on the SPARK and SSC datasets. The classifying coding de novo variants as pathogenic in the SPARK and SSC datasets likely includes a high proportion of false positives, which may contribute to the lower performance of RankVar compared to the CHOP dataset, where the causal variants were carefully curated by clinical experts. To address this, we restricted our analysis to variants in SFARI-listed genes. However, such evaluation focused on genes within the SFARI list does not fully reflect the complexity of real-world clinical diagnostics. Furthermore, many SFARI genes are associated with syndromic forms of autism disorders that include additional clinical features (e.g., seizures, intellectual disability), and if a proband does not show the full syndrome, the variant may not actually be causative in that case. Due to the lack of detailed phenotype-genotype curation in the SSC and SPARK datasets, we were not able to systematically verify whether proband phenotypes matched the known syndromic associated with the implicated genes. Additionally, the RankVar performs better for cases with causal variants being likely LOF (nonsense and frameshift) variants compared to missense variants. In practice, missense variants are more challenging to assess and thus it is more important to improve prioritization scores for this variants class. Further development can rebalance the fraction of different types of variants in training data and can utilize more sophisticated deep learning models to improve the precision of missense variants interpretation. A further limitation is the absence of inheritance information, such as de novo status. This is primarily because the model was trained by spiking in pathogenic variants from ClinVar into unrelated samples from the 1KGP, which lack family trio data. In clinical genetics, de novo status is an important feature, particularly in severe early-onset Mendelian disorders, and its absence may limit accuracy in trio-based diagnostic settings. To address this, future versions of RankVar could incorporate inheritance information in two ways. First, users could supply de novo status derived from family trio sequencing using established tools (e.g., GATK or DeNovoGear), allowing RankVar to upweight or re-rank variants marked as confirmed de novo. Second, inheritance status could be included as a model feature during training, by retraining RankVar on datasets that contain trio or pedigree information. This would allow the model to learn the contribution of inheritance status to pathogenicity prediction. Integrating inheritance features is a key direction for future work and will further enhance the clinical utility of RankVar in real-world diagnostic pipelines. Additionally, in RankVar, variants are scored individually, and compound heterozygous events are not explicitly modeled as variant pairs. In cases where a gene contains two heterozygous variants that together form a compound heterozygous configuration, RankVar currently scores each variant separately without inferring their joint effect. This represents a limitation, particularly in autosomal recessive disorders where compound heterozygosity is a common disease mechanism. We plan to further extend RankVar to include compound heterozygous-aware logic by identifying potential compound heterozygous pairs based on phasing and parental genotype, and by implementing gene-level scoring for variant combinations. Finally, because the 1KGP dataset has been widely used and integrated into many downstream pipelines, tools and filtering strategies may be inadvertently optimized for this dataset, potentially making variant filtering and prioritization artificially easier. To better assess generalizability and simulate real clinical scenarios, it would be more realistic to train and evaluate the model using more modern datasets such as TOPMed. We plan to access such datasets in the future work to further enhance the model’s generalizability.

Because RankVar was trained on ClinVar P/LP spike-in variants, ClinVar derived features warrant careful scrutiny. The clinvarNumP/LP feature was designed to capture a gene-level prior reflecting the burden of previously reported P/LP variants within a gene, rather than the clinical significance of the candidate variant itself, the latter of which would constitute direct label leakage. Even so, gene-level ClinVar derived features may introduce a degree of circularity when training labels are also drawn from ClinVar. To assess this, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which clinvarNumP/LP was excluded during model training, and performance remained largely unchanged across both the validation and independent testing datasets. The synthetic validation PR-AUC should therefore be interpreted with caution, and the independent testing cohorts (CHOP, BDB, SSC, and SPARK) provide a more clinically relevant assessment of variant prioritization performance. We also evaluated a clinically relevant scenario in which an individual carries more than one P/LP variant, including one variant in a phenotype-matched disease gene and another in an apparently phenotype unmatched gene. RankVar generally prioritized the phenotype-matched P/LP variant over the phenotype-unmatched P/LP variant, supporting RankVar does not rank P/LP variants solely according to pathogenicity annotation, but instead integrates phenotype-gene relevance to prioritize variants in an individual specific context.

The clinical applications of our pipeline extend beyond rare disease diagnosis. Its ability to continuously integrate new genomic and phenotypic data makes it a promising tool for periodic reanalysis of unsolved cases, a crucial unmet need as novel disease genes are discovered. Moreover, by reducing the manual burden of variant interpretation, the pipeline could lower costs for large-scale clinical genomic laboratories, facilitating broader adoption of precision medicine. Moving forward, enhancing its scalability, incorporating additional variant types, and refining noncoding variant interpretation will further strengthen its utility in clinical genomics and complex disease research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our pipeline represents a significant advancement in integrating phenotype and genotype information for variant prioritization. By combining a machine learning algorithm with phenotype data and sequencing profiles, our approach improved the rate of accurate genetic diagnosis and has the potential to assist in primary diagnosis, reanalysis of unsolved cases, and discovery of novel disease genes. Future work will focus on improving its generalizability, incorporating additional data types (e.g., gene–gene interaction), leveraging transformer-based models, and expanding its applications to noncoding variants, further enhancing its utility in clinical genomics.
