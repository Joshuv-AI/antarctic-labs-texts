Pediatric Digital Artery Pseudoaneurysm From Repetitive Compression While Gripping Toys
Authors: Kayoko Uno, Shankar Rajeswaran, Erin Kelley, Kento Takaya, Kazuo Kishi, Akira Yamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Pseudoaneurysm of the finger is a rare condition, commonly caused by penetrating injury. However, repetitive blunt trauma can also lead to vascular injury, particularly in individuals exposed to chronic minor compression, such as industrial workers and athletes. Accurate diagnosis and appropriate surgical planning are essential, as pseudoaneurysms may cause distal ischemia or digital nerve dysfunction if left untreated. We report a rare case of a 4-year-old boy with a pulsatile mass on his right middle finger, without any history of overt trauma. He had a habit of holding hard toys between his fingers and sometimes slept while gripping them, suggesting repetitive minor compression as the likely cause. Ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm of the digital artery. Intraoperative angiography confirmed the pseudoaneurysm and robust collateral circulation, allowing safe excision without the need for vascular reconstruction or vein grafting. Postoperative perfusion remained intact, and at 5 months postoperatively, there was no recurrence of pseudoaneurysm. This case highlights that chronic, low-grade compression can cause digital pseudoaneurysm, even in children, and emphasizes the importance of including this entity in the differential diagnosis of pediatric finger masses, as well as the value of intraoperative angiography in planning minimally invasive, circulation-preserving treatment.

Pseudoaneurysm of the finger is rare and is most often caused by penetrating trauma.1–3 Although ultrasonography is useful for diagnosis, preoperative planning often requires more detailed vascular imaging such as magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), computed tomography angiography (CTA), or conventional angiography.1,4–6 We report a rare case of a child without any history of overt trauma who developed a pseudoaneurysm of the digital artery, likely due to repetitive minor blunt injury from hard toys, which was successfully treated with angiography-guided, minimally invasive surgical excision.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 4-year-old boy with no significant medical history presented to the orthopedic clinic with a 3-month history of a pulsatile mass on the ulnar side of his right middle finger (Fig. 1). He occasionally complained of morning pain but had no persistent discomfort, bruising, or skin injury. The mass had gradually increased in size. At this point, the differential diagnosis included congenital aneurysm, pseudoaneurysm, vascular malformation, and tumor. According to his parents, although there was a possibility that he experienced trauma without their awareness, he frequently held multiple toys between his fingers and sometimes slept while gripping them, which may have caused repeated compression or pinching of the fingers. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays how the patient held multiple toys between his fingers, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E905.)

Preoperative photograph showing a pulsatile mass on the ulnar side of the right middle finger, with preserved sensation and full range of motion.

Ultrasonography revealed a pseudoaneurysm of the ulnar digital artery, and the patient was referred to the plastic surgery department via the pediatric surgery clinic. On examination, there was pulsatile swelling on the right middle finger at the level of the middle phalanx, with preserved sensation and full range of motion.

Ultrasound demonstrated a hypoechoic soft tissue lesion measuring 1.6 × 0.7 × 1.1 cm with turbulent flow, consistent with a posttraumatic pseudoaneurysm. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a partially thrombosed pseudoaneurysm of the ulnar proper palmar digital artery at the level of the middle phalanx. As the lesion continued to enlarge, angiography-guided open excision was planned.

Surgical Procedure

Under general anesthesia, intraoperative angiography was performed by placing a 22G angiocath in the ulnar artery at the wrist in a retrograde fashion. Angiography demonstrated a pseudoaneurysm with an identifiable entry point and distal runoff vessels (Fig. 2). Through a zigzag incision and after tourniquet application, the lesion was carefully dissected under the microscope. The proximal and distal ends of the involved artery and accompanying vein were identified, consistent with the angiographic findings. The displaced digital nerve, shifted toward the radial side, was meticulously preserved (Fig. 3). The ulnar digital artery and its distal branch arising from the pseudoaneurysm were ligated, and the pseudoaneurysm was completely excised. After tourniquet release, repeat angiography confirmed adequate perfusion of the fingertip, and the wound was closed with sutures. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which reveals preserved runoff to the distal digit via the paired digital artery, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E906.) (See Video [online], which shows the pre- and postoperative angiographic findings.) The postoperative course was uneventful, with normal color and capillary refill. The patient was discharged the same day. He was advised to avoid compressing toys between his fingers, and he now carries his toys in a bag with straps.

DISCUSSION

Pseudoaneurysm of the finger is rare and often difficult to diagnose.2 Accurate diagnosis is essential, as the lesion may progressively enlarge or cause distal clot embolization, which may result in ischemia, gangrene, or digital nerve dysfunction, and may even lead to bone erosion or joint degeneration.6 When a pseudoaneurysm is suspected, MRA or angiography is generally recommended, and ultrasonography and CTA can also be helpful.1,4–6 MRA is known as a minimally invasive tool but requires prolonged immobility and patient cooperation, which can be challenging in children. It also offers relatively limited spatial resolution for small digital vessels. Ultrasonography is an alternative imaging modality, which is quick and noninvasive; however, it has limitations in assessing the entire vascular anatomy and adequately assessing smaller collateral vessels. CTA can provide broader anatomical visualization but yields static images. Although angiography is invasive and carries risks related to arterial puncture and contrast use, it allows evaluation of in-and-out flow and collateral circulation.7

Penetrating trauma is the most common cause of pseudoaneurysm,1–3 but chronic blunt trauma has also been implicated, particularly in individuals exposed to repetitive hand stress, such as industrial workers and athletes, including handball players or baseball catchers.3,6 In our case, there was no external injury or bruising, but repetitive compression from holding hard, angular toys likely led to pseudoaneurysm formation.

Surgical excision is generally recommended for treatment, with vascular reconstruction such as anastomosis or venous grafting performed when necessary.1,4,6 Ultrasound-guided thrombin injection has also been reported as a minimally invasive alternative.8,9 In our case, because the lesion was located in the finger and the patient was very young, preservation of distal perfusion was critical, and there was concern for ischemia secondary to embolus with a solely endovascular approach. Intraoperative angiography was critical in delineating the lesion and ensuring that there was robust distal flow from the paired digital artery before resection of the pseudoaneurysm.

Although digital perfusion can be assessed clinically, digital ischemia has been reported to occur several days after surgery.10 The use of intraoperative angiography allowed us to confirm adequate distal perfusion and collateral flow objectively.

The limitation of this case report is the short follow-up period. However, this report highlights the importance of collaboration between various departments, which enabled an elegant and minimally invasive approach to managing a challenging clinical entity.

CONCLUSIONS

We report a rare case of pseudoaneurysm of the middle finger in a child without any history of overt trauma. Pre- and postoperative angiographic evaluation under general anesthesia confirmed both the pseudoaneurysm and collateral circulation, enabling safe and minimally invasive surgical resection. This case underscores the importance of recognizing that even seemingly harmless objects such as toys can cause pseudoaneurysm through chronic compression and highlights the role of angiography in optimizing treatment planning and preventing ischemic complications.
