A bilayer hydrogel with a lower layer ROS-responsive Au-CeO2 antioxidant release and an upper layer sustained release bFGF for promoting repair in diabetic wound therapy
Authors: Haobing Li, Wenzhang Jin, Tingfeng Xiao, Shunfu Wang, Qi Tang, Kaixin Zhu, Ziyin Xie, Xiang Su, Wa Zhang, Yue Si, Mengxuan Zhou, Jingang Han, Jingyong Huang, Yihui Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic wound healing remains a formidable clinical challenge, hindered by multifactorial obstacles including chronic inflammation, persistent oxidative stress, impaired angiogenesis and dysfunctional extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling. Current therapeutic modalities often fail to address these multiple pathological links simultaneously, resulting in limited efficacy. To address this challenge, this study constructs a bilayer hydrogel (Au-CeO₂-bFGF): the lower layer is formed by cross-linking poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), tetrakis(4-sulfophenyl)boric acid (TSPBA) and oxidized dextran (PTOD) to create a reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsive network loaded with Au-CeO2 (PTOD-Au-CeO₂) to dynamically scavenge excessive ROS and alleviate oxidative stress and inflammation; the upper layer, composed of methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) and sodium alginate (HASA), is loaded with basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (HASA-bFGF) for sustained release to promote ECM remodeling and angiogenesis. PTOD-Au-CeO₂ degrades in response to ROS, releasing antioxidant Au-CeO₂ to exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, and HASA-bFGF slowly releases bFGF to facilitate cell migration and tube formation in vitro. In a diabetic mouse wound model, the Au-CeO₂-bFGF responds to the high ROS concentration at the wound site, degrading the lower layer network to release Au-CeO₂, thereby scavenging local ROS and resolving inflammation. Following the amelioration of the inflammatory microenvironment, the upper layer continuously releases bFGF, significantly enhancing collagen deposition, ECM remodeling and neovascularization, ultimately achieving wound closure. In summary, the Au-CeO₂-bFGF achieves integrated intervention in the multiple pathological stages of diabetic wounds through a synergistic, spatiotemporal strategy of "anti-inflammation and antioxidant first, pro-repair later" orchestrated by its bilayer structure, providing a novel and intelligent therapeutic strategy with significant potential for clinical translation in the management of complex wounds.

Introduction

Diabetic wounds represent a paradigm of clinically recalcitrant wounds, where impaired healing primarily stems from a persistent oxidative stress microenvironment, unresolved chronic inflammation, and compromised neovascularization (Chang and Nguyen, 2021; Manisha et al., 2025). In normal wound healing, the endogenous antioxidant system effectively scavenges excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby promptly terminating the inflammatory response and facilitating the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative phase. In contrast, diabetic wounds are characterized by the sustained overproduction of ROS and chronic inflammation (Zhang et al., 2021), resulting in the prolonged retention of M1 macrophage polarization (Mao et al., 2024), inhibition of fibroblast function and impaired endothelial cell migration (Zhang et al., 2020). These pathological changes ultimately obstruct granulation tissue formation and re-epithelialization. Current clinical management of diabetic wounds relies primarily on traditional approaches, including surgical debridement, skin grafting, and infection control (Dwivedi et al., 2024; Fisher et al., 2010). However, the efficacy of these conventional methods is often limited, particularly when wounds are accompanied by neovascular dysfunction and persistent inflammatory responses. On the other hand, emerging therapies such as platelet-rich plasma (PRP), hyperbaric oxygen therapy, stem cell therapy and exosome-based interventions have demonstrated promising potential in promoting diabetic wound healing (Golchin et al., 2022; Monami et al., 2025). Nevertheless, these emerging therapies face significant bottlenecks, including the propensity of active ingredients to undergo rapid inactivation, suboptimal delivery efficiency, and concerns regarding long-term safety (Kolimi et al., 2022). Consequently, these novel methods have not yet been widely adopted in clinical practice. Therefore, the development of innovative and efficacy-based strategies for diabetic wound healing represents an urgent clinical unmet need.

Persistently elevated levels of ROS are a hallmark of impaired healing in diabetic wounds and are closely correlated with clinical manifestations such as wound chronicity, increased infection risk, and delayed tissue repair (Deng et al., 2021). The chronic hyperglycemic environment triggers sustained local inflammatory responses within the wound, leading to the aberrant activation of a large number of infiltrating immune cells and the subsequent release of excessive ROS, which disrupts redox homeostasis (Jain et al., 2025). Excessive ROS not only directly attack proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids—thereby compromising cellular function—but also damage vascular endothelium, inhibit neovascularization, and degrade critical components of the extracellular matrix (ECM) (Kunkemoeller and Kyriakides, 2017). These effects severely impede re-epithelialization, vascularization, and tissue remodeling. Furthermore, these alterations exacerbate the accumulation of inflammatory cells and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Landen et al., 2016), establishing a vicious cycle of "ROS–inflammation–impaired repair" that drives the wound toward a chronic, non-healing state. Therefore, modulating redox homeostasis, promoting neovascularization and repairing the ECM have emerged as key strategies for intervening in diabetic wounds and breaking this vicious cycle.

Although natural antioxidant enzymes possess the capacity to scavenge ROS and suppress inflammation, their clinical translation is severely constrained by their short half-life, poor stability, and high costs (Al-Madhagi and Masoud, 2024; Forman and Zhang, 2021). In recent years, biomimetic nanozymes have emerged as ideal alternatives to overcome these clinical bottlenecks due to their structural tunability, high catalytic activity, robust stability and scalability for large-scale production (Jin et al., 2024b; Tang et al., 2021). Particularly in the diabetic wound microenvironment, nanozymes can dynamically scavenge excessive ROS by mimicking the multi-enzymatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), thereby alleviating oxidative stress (Li et al., 2024). Cerium dioxide nanozymes have been widely applied in the treatment of ROS-related diseases due to their unique SOD- and CAT-mimicking activities (Zou et al., 2024), conferred by the reversible valence switching between Ce3+ and Ce4+. Generally, a higher proportion of surface Ce3+ correlates with enhanced SOD activity, whereas Ce4+ enrichment favors CAT-like catalysis (Yang et al., 2024b). However, the catalytic activity of cerium dioxide nanozymes is constricted by multiple factors, including surface oxygen vacancy concentration, exposed crystal facets, and size effects (Cai et al., 2025). Consequently, precisely regulating surface valence distribution and active site exposure has become the core challenge in enhancing their catalytic efficiency within the complex diabetic wound microenvironment. To address this, a core-shell Au-CeO2 structure can be constructed by encapsulating cerium dioxide around gold nanoparticles (Bhagat et al., 2018). By leveraging the interfacial synergy and strong metal-support interaction (SMSI) between gold and the ceria carrier, the redox cycling of Ce3+/Ce4+ in the shell layer is promoted, and oxygen vacancy density is increased, thereby significantly enhancing the synergistic efficiency of dual SOD and CAT activities (Othman et al., 2024).

Basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) acts as a potent pro-angiogenic factor that specifically binds to receptors on the surface of endothelial cells, activating intracellular signaling pathways to significantly accelerate endothelial proliferation and migration, thereby promoting neovascularization (Sun et al., 2024). Furthermore, bFGF stimulates fibroblast activity and accelerates the synthesis and deposition of the ECM (Yang et al., 2011). This drives the growth of granulation tissue and the process of re-epithelialization, effectively compensating for the limitations of antioxidant-only approaches in terms of structural tissue reconstruction and providing the essential biological scaffold and nutritional support required for wound repair (Koria, 2012).

Hydrogels have emerged as ideal wound dressing carriers due to their high water content, excellent biocompatibility and controllable degradability (Liang et al., 2021). However, traditional single layer hydrogels often fail to meet the dynamic and multi-stage therapeutic demands of diabetic wound healing. During the initial phase, characterized by acute inflammation and oxidative stress, an ideal dressing requires rapid responsiveness and efficient ROS scavenging (Niu et al., 2025). Conversely, the subsequent proliferative phase necessitates the sustained release of pro-angiogenic factors to support neovascularization and tissue remodeling. Although single-layer hydrogels can co-load antioxidant nanozymes and bFGF, their "burst release" kinetics fail to achieve the necessary spatiotemporal regulation (Yi et al., 2025). This limitation often leads to the premature depletion of antioxidant components and the degradation or wastage of growth factors during the peak inflammatory period, significantly compromising therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, we have developed a layered bilayer hydrogel system featuring "staged" release capabilities to break the vicious cycle of "ROS–inflammation–healing impairment".

Addressing the persistent ROS stimulation in the early stages of diabetic wounds, the lower layer was constructed using tetrakis(4-sulfophenyl)boric acid (TSPBA), poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and oxidized dextran via reversible dynamic covalent crosslinking between phenylboronic acid and cis-diols, loaded with Au-CeO2 nanozymes. TSPBA possesses unique ROS-responsive properties: in high-concentration H2O2 environments, the phenylboronic acid moieties are oxidized to phenylboronates, causing the rapid dissociation of the crosslinked structure (Yu et al., 2022). This triggers the immediate release of Au-CeO2 nanozymes to scavenge ROS and inhibit inflammatory progression. In contrast, the upper layer was fabricated via photo-crosslinking of methacrylated hyaluronic acid (HAMA) and sodium alginate, loaded with bFGF. This layer exhibits high water content and a slower degradation rate, which preserves bFGF activity. It slowly releases bFGF as inflammation subsides, precisely matching the proliferative and remodeling phases of neovascularization and matrix reconstruction. The synergistic interplay between the physical barrier of the bilayer structure and the response mechanisms enables a "sequential clearance and reconstruction" intervention strategy: the bottom layer responds to the ROS burst to instantly release Au-CeO2 for inflammation control, while the top layer relies on slow degradation to continuously supply factors for repair. This precise spatiotemporal coupling is designed to ultimately enhance the efficiency and quality of diabetic wound healing.

In summary, this study designs a novel bilayer hydrogel comprising a ROS-responsive lower layer for Au-CeO2 release and a upper layer for sustained bFGF delivery (Au-CeO2-bFGF). This design aims to achieve stage-specific temporal therapy aligned with the natural cycle of wound healing, thereby facilitating the recovery of diabetic wounds. Specifically, the lower layer was constructed via the dynamic crosslinking of TSPBA, PVA and oxidized dextran to encapsulate Au-CeO2 (PTOD-Au-CeO2), while the upper layer was formed via photo-crosslinking of HAMA and sodium alginate to encapsulate bFGF (HASA-bFGF) (Fig. 1). Together, these components constitute a bilayer hydrogel system with distinct release characteristics. We first evaluated the morphological characterization and antioxidant activity of Au-CeO2, followed by an investigation of the microstructure, physicochemical properties, and ROS-responsive degradation behavior of the bilayer hydrogel. In vitro experiments were conducted to assess the hydrogel's antioxidant performance, anti-inflammatory capabilities, cytocompatibility and pro-angiogenic potential. Furthermore, using a full-thickness diabetic wound model, we investigated the effects of the bilayer hydrogel on inflammation resolution, angiogenesis and healing quality. Our results demonstrate the successful construction of a novel bilayer hydrogel dressing that integrates the dynamic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory functions of Au-CeO2 with the sustained pro-repair activity of bFGF. By precisely matching the distinct stages of diabetic wound healing, this system shows immense potential for the clinical treatment of chronic, refractory wounds.Fig. 1Schematic diagram of the preparation of Au-CeO2-bFGF and its application in treating diabetic wounds in mice.Fig. 1

Materials and methods

Rosup, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), Cy5, FITC, citrate-phosphate buffer and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). DAPI, calcein-AM and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO) antibody (A22900), anti-CD31 antibody (A19014), anti-α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) antibody (A7248) were purchased from ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Mouse inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and Arginase-1 (Arg1) enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, anti-Collagen I antibody (ab138492), anti-Collagen III antibody (ab184993) and all secondary antibodies were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). ELISA kits for detecting mouse tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-12 (IL-12), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) were purchased from Lianke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Mouse Fibronectin ELISA Kit was purchased from Beijing Biolab Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mouse Laminin, MMP2 and MMP9 ELISA Kits were obtained from Beijing Huamei Biotech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Total Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) Assay Kit with WST-8 was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The detection kits for hydroxyl radical (•OH), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and RAW 264.7 cells were obtained from the Wuhan Procell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All C57BL/6 mice were ordered from the Zhejiang Weitong Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.

Synthesis of Au-CeO2

Under ice-bath conditions, 600 μL of 0.024 M HAuCl4 and 1.4 mL of 0.1 M Ce(NO3)3 were added to 50 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 64 μL of ammonia solution (25–28%) dissolved in 3 mL of deionized water was rapidly added, followed by continuous stirring for 25 s. Immediately thereafter, 3 mL of 0.0467 M Ce(NO3)3 solution was added to the system, and stirring was continued for an additional 15 min at room temperature. After the reaction, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 ×g and 4 °C for 60 min to collect the product. The obtained precipitate was washed three times with deionized water, repeating the aforementioned centrifugation step after each wash to separate and purify the product. Finally, the sample was dried at room temperature to obtain Au-CeO2 (Li et al., 2018).

Characterization of Au-CeO2

The morphology of Au-CeO2 was characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi H-7000FA, Japan). The particle size and Zeta potential were measured using a particle size analyzer (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS 90, UK). Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher, USA) was performed to obtain the infrared spectrum. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 Advance, Germany). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis of Au-CeO2 was conducted using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo ESCALAB 250XI, USA). Nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherms were obtained using a surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2460, USA), and the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method was employed to evaluate the surface area and pore size. H2 temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) profiles were recorded using a chemical adsorption instrument (Micromeritics AutoChem II 2920, USA).

Evaluation of enzyme-like activity

The superoxide anion scavenging ability of Au-CeO₂ was determined using a Total SOD Assay Kit (WST-8 method, Beyotime, China). The H2O2 scavenging capacity was measured using a Catalase Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). The comprehensive antioxidant performance was evaluated using a Total Antioxidant Capacity Assay Kit (ABTS method, Beyotime, China). The scavenging rate of DPPH free radicals was assessed using a DPPH Free Radical Scavenging Assay Kit (Solarbio, China). All experimental procedures were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, and all experiments were conducted in triplicate (Liu et al., 2020).

Synthesis of Au-CeO₂-bFGF

First, 200 μL of 5% TSPBA, 50 μL of 5% oxidized dextran and 0.2 mg of Au-CeO₂ were thoroughly mixed. Subsequently, 150 μL of 8% PVA was added and stirred rapidly to obtain a homogeneous mixture. The mixture was allowed to stand to form the lower layer hydrogel (PTOD-Au-CeO₂) (Chen et al., 2024). Next, 100 μL of 4% HAMA, 100 μL of 3% sodium alginate, 20 μL of bFGF (100 μg/mL) and 1 mg of LAP were added onto the surface of the lower layer hydrogel. These components were mixed thoroughly and irradiated with UV light for 45 s to form the upper layer hydrogel (HASA-bFGF). Consequently, the Au-CeO₂-bFGF bilayer hydrogel was constituted by the combination of the lower and upper layers.

Characterization of Au-CeO₂-bFGF bilayer hydrogel

The structural characteristics of the freeze-dried Au-CeO₂-bFGF were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; ZEISS GeminiSEM360, Germany). Chemical characterization was performed using a FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher, USA). The thermal stability of the hydrogel was determined using a thermogravimetric analyzer (Rigaku, Japan).

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of the hydrogel were measured using a rheometer (Discovery HR 30, USA) equipped with a 25 mm diameter parallel plate. To determine the stiffness of the hydrogel, the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were measured at a constant strain of 1% and room temperature. The frequency sweep range was set from 0.1 to 100 rad/s.

Degradation experiment

The hydrogels were immersed in PBS and PBS containing 1 mM H2O2 (pre-warmed to 37 °C), respectively. At predetermined time points, the hydrogels were retrieved, freeze-dried and weighed.

The degradation rate was calculated using the following formula:

Degradation rate (%) = [(Initial mass of hydrogel − Mass of hydrogel at a given time point) / Initial mass of hydrogel] × 100%.

Release experiment

To track the release profile, Au-CeO₂-bFGF were prepared using FITC-labeled bFGF (bFGF-FITC, custom-synthesized by GenScript). The fluorescently labeled hydrogels were immersed in PBS and PBS containing 1 mM H2O2 (pre-warmed to 37 °C), respectively. At predetermined time points, the supernatant was collected. The fluorescence intensity of the supernatant was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, Varioskan LUX, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm. The concentration of bFGF-FITC in the supernatant was calculated using a standard curve correlating concentration with fluorescence intensity. The concentration of Au in the supernatant was determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The release rates of Au-CeO₂ and bFGF were calculated according to the following formulas:

Au-CeO₂ release rate (%) = (Au concentration at a specific time point / Initial Au concentration) × 100%.

bFGF release rate (%) = (Fluorescence intensity at a specific time point / Initial fluorescence intensity) × 100%.

Antioxidant performance assay

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. Serum-free DMEM containing 50 μM DCFH-DA was added, and the cells were incubated in the dark for 30 min, followed by washing three times with PBS. After treatment with 500 μL of 50 μg/mL Rosup (a ROS inducer), the cells in the experimental group were further treated with 200 μL of the corresponding hydrogel (formed from 200 μL of hydrogel precursor solution; subsequent steps followed this principle), while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. After incubating in the dark for 3 h, the cells were washed three times with PBS, stained with DAPI for nuclei and finally observed using a fluorescence microscope to assess the fluorescence intensity of each group.

TUNEL staining

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. After induction with DMEM containing 50 μM Rosup for 1 h, the experimental group was treated with 200 μL of the corresponding hydrogel, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. The culture continued for 24 h. The medium was then discarded, and the cells were rinsed three times with PBS. Staining was performed according to the instructions of a commercial TUNEL assay kit (Beyotime, China). The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the fluorescence intensity of each group was observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Anti-inflammatory properties

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and treated with 1 μg/mL LPS for 24 h to simulate an inflammatory response. For the experimental groups, 200 μL of the corresponding hydrogel was added to each well, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. After incubating for 12 h, the cell culture supernatants were collected. The concentrations of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-12, were measured using corresponding commercial ELISA kits.

Following supernatant collection, the adherent cells were lysed in RIPA buffer, and total protein was extracted via centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C). After normalizing protein concentrations by the BCA method, the intracellular levels of iNOS and Arg1 were quantified using ELISA kits.

Cell migration assay

A scratch wound healing assay was employed to evaluate the effect of the Au-CeO₂-bFGF on the migration of HUVECs. HUVECs were cultured in 6-well plates until approximately 100% confluent, and the cell monolayer was scratched using a 200 μL pipette tip. The experimental groups were treated with 200 μL of the corresponding hydrogel per well, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. Subsequently, the cells were cultured in serum-free DMEM for 24 h. Cell migration was visualized using Calcein fluorescence staining. Images of cell migration were captured under a microscope, and the migration rate was calculated using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

Matrigel (ABW Bio, China) was uniformly spread onto 24-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min to solidify. HUVECs were seeded onto the Matrigel at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. The experimental groups were treated with 200 μL of the corresponding hydrogel per well, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. After incubation for 12 h, HUVECs were stained with Calcein-AM, and tube formation was observed under a fluorescence microscope. Simultaneously, the cell culture supernatants were collected, and the concentration of VEGF was detected using a commercial VEGF ELISA kit.

Evaluation of cytocompatibility

The cytotoxicity of the Au-CeO₂-bFGF against HUVECs was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Beyotime, China). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 3.0 × 103 cells per well. The experimental group received 10 μL of hydrogel per well, while the control group received an equal volume of PBS. After 24 h of treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific Varioskan LUX, USA) to assess cell viability.

Animal experiments

Thirty-six healthy male C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were selected. All animal experiments were conducted in a blinded manner. The researchers remained blinded to the group allocations throughout the experiments and the subsequent analysis of results. First, the mice were fed a high-fat diet (60% of calories from fat) for 8 weeks. Subsequently, STZ was dissolved in 1 mL of 0.1 M citrate-phosphate buffer (pH 4.5) to a final concentration of 1%. Based on body weight, the mice were intraperitoneally injected with STZ at a dose of 25 mg/kg for 4 consecutive days. The mice were fasted for 12 h before the first injection and fed again 2 h post-injection. Mice were provided with 5% glucose drinking water 4 h after injection, which was switched to normal water 10 h later. For subsequent STZ injections, fasting was not required, while the other steps remained consistent. One week after the injections, fasting blood glucose levels were measured. Mice with a fasting blood glucose level exceeding 16.7 mmol/L were considered successfully modeled and used for subsequent experiments (Weng et al., 2025). In the subsequent animal experiments, destructive sampling was performed on days 7 and 14, with the number of independent biological replicates set to n = 3 for each time point.

The successfully modeled mice were numbered and randomly divided into 6 groups using a random number table: (1) Control group, (2) PTOD group (treated with PTOD hydrogel), (3) PTOD-Au-CeO2 group (treated with PTOD-Au-CeO2 hydrogel), (4) HASA group (treated with HASA hydrogel), (5) HASA-bFGF group (treated with HASA-bFGF hydrogel) and (6) Au-CeO2-bFGF group (treated with Au-CeO2-bFGF bilayer hydrogel).

After anesthesia with 3% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), all mice were shaved and the skin was disinfected with povidone‑iodine. A full-thickness circular skin wound with a diameter of 10 mm was created on the dorsum of each mouse using a 10 mm skin biopsy punch. A circular rubber ring was fixed around the wound using medical adhesive to prevent skin contraction in mice. The day of wound creation was recorded as day 1. Corresponding circular hydrogels (10 mm diameter) were applied to the dorsal wounds of each mouse. Wound images were captured on days 1, 3, 7, 10 and 14, and the wound area was measured using ImageJ software.

In vivo degradation assessment of Au-CeO2-bFGF

The PTOD-Au-CeO2 lower layer hydrogel was labeled with Cy5, and the HASA-bFGF upper layer hydrogel was labeled with FITC. Briefly, Cy5 (10 μM) was mixed into the PVA hydrogel precursor solution to prepare the Cy5-labeled PTOD-Au-CeO2 lower layer hydrogel. Similarly, FITC (50 μg/mL) was incorporated into the HASA hydrogel precursor solution to prepare the FITC-labeled HASA-bFGF upper layer hydrogel. After the fluorescently labeled bilayer hydrogels were applied to the dorsal wounds of the mice, the Cy5 and FITC fluorescence signals at the wound sites were collected on days 1, 3 and 7 using an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system, and the fluorescence intensities were quantitatively analyzed.

In vivo release study of Au-CeO2-bFGF

Au-CeO2-bFGF bilayer hydrogels containing fluorescently labeled bFGF-FITC were applied to the dorsal wounds of mice. On days 1, 3 and 7, the undegraded residual hydrogels were collected from the wounds. The collected hydrogels were immersed in PBS solution (pH 7.4) and incubated in the dark at 37 °C until completely degraded. The degradation solution was collected, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm to quantitatively analyze the release of bFGF-FITC. The release of Au-CeO2 was evaluated by determining the Au and Ce content in the degradation solution using ICP-MS.

Histological staining analysis

Mice were euthanized on days 7 and 14, and dorsal wound tissue samples were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After routine histological processing, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned to a thickness of 4 μm. Subsequently, histological examination was performed via Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining. Collagen deposition in the wound was evaluated using Masson's trichrome staining, and the distribution and morphology of elastic fibers in the tissue samples were assessed using Van Gieson (VG) staining.

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), paraffin sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval and blocking of non-specific sites. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies (dilution ratio 1:100) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies at 25 °C for 1 h and finally observed under an optical microscope.

For immunofluorescence staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by blocking of non-specific sites. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies (1:200) at 4 °C overnight, washed with PBS, and incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (1:500) at room temperature for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the sections were mounted for observation and analysis under a fluorescence microscope. All stained sections were evaluated by blinded investigators.

DHE staining

Wound tissues collected on days 7 and 14 were embedded in OCT compound, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and sectioned into 8 μm thick frozen sections. The sections were stained with 10 μM DHE in the dark for 30 min, and then counterstained with DAPI for 3 min. Finally, the samples were observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Detection of cytokines in wound tissue

Wound tissues were collected on days 7 and 14, immersed in liquid nitrogen, ground on ice, and subjected to ultrasonication. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Using corresponding assay kits, the levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, iNOS, Arg1, VEGF, PDGF, TGF-β1, α-SMA, MMP2, MMP9, fibronectin, and laminin in the supernatants were detected. All operations were performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Detection of antioxidant indicators in wound tissue

Wound tissues were collected, washed with ice-cold PBS, and homogenized using a high-throughput tissue grinder. The homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatants were collected. The activities of •OH and SOD, as well as the levels of MDA and GSH, were measured using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons not involving time, one-way ANOVA was used. For time-dependent variables, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was performed. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, followed by Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Significance levels are marked as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; ‘ns’ indicates no significant difference. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times using biological replicates.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Au-CeO2

Au-CeO2 was synthesized via a self-redox reaction between HAuCl4 and Ce(NO3)3 in an ammonia solution. The dried Au-CeO2 appeared as a black-purple powder (Fig. 2A), and its aqueous dispersion exhibited a purple color (Fig. 2B). TEM images revealed that Au-CeO2 possessed a core-shell nanostructure, with the Au core encapsulated within a CeO2 shell (Fig. 2C). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) elemental mapping confirmed that Au was primarily concentrated in the core of the nanoparticles, while Ce and O were uniformly distributed (Fig. 2D), indicating the successful coverage of the Au core by the CeO2 shell. Particle size analysis showed that the average diameter of Au-CeO2 was approximately 19.82 ± 6.31 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.12, suggesting good monodispersity (Fig. 2E). The zeta potential of Au-CeO2 was measured to be 34.6 ± 1.03 mV (Fig. 2F). The FTIR spectrum of Au-CeO2 displayed characteristic Ce—O absorption peaks in the range of 400–600 cm−1 (Fig. S1A), confirming the presence of interfacial interactions between Au and CeO2 (Stadnichenko et al., 2021). The crystallographic characteristics of Au-CeO2 were characterized by XRD, which revealed characteristic peaks corresponding to Au (JCPDS No. 04-0784) and CeO2 (JCPDS No. 34-0394) (Fig. 2G) (Khan et al., 2014). Furthermore, XPS was employed to investigate the composition and valence states of Au-CeO2. The results showed Au 4f peaks located at 83.9 eV (Au0) and 87.6 eV (Au+) (Fig. 2H), while the Ce 3d spectrum exhibited typical coexisting peaks of Ce3+ and Ce4+ (Fig. 2I) (Lei et al., 2015). This confirmed the presence of strong electronic interactions between Au and CeO2, which facilitates the formation of catalytic active sites. To further verify the antioxidant performance of Au-CeO2, we compared it with commercial CeO2 by investigating its CAT-like and superoxide SOD-like activities, assessed by the scavenging capacity for H2O2 and superoxide anion (O2•−), respectively. The experimental results indicated that the scavenging rate of Au-CeO2 for H2O2 exceeded 50% at a concentration of 100 μg/mL, whereas commercial CeO2 failed to reach 50% even at 200 μg/mL (Fig. 2J). Similarly, the scavenging rate of Au-CeO2 against O2•− was significantly superior to that of CeO2, demonstrating that Au-CeO2 exhibits enhanced SOD-like activity (Fig. 2K). These findings suggest that Au-CeO2 possesses multi-enzymatic activity capable of removing both O2•− and H2O2, with performance significantly superior to that of CeO2. Additionally, ABTS, a common reagent for evaluating radical scavenging ability, was used to further assess the antioxidant capacity by measuring the clearance of ABTS+• (Jin et al., 2024a). The results showed that the ABTS+• scavenging rate of Au-CeO2 was significantly higher than that of CeO2, and the disparity between the two widened with increasing dosage (Fig. 2L). The DPPH radical scavenging assay further confirmed that the ability of Au-CeO2 to scavenge DPPH• far exceeded that of CeO2 (Fig. S1B). Subsequently, kinetic analysis was performed to calculate the Michaelis-Menten constant (Km). The Km value of Au-CeO2 for H2O2 was 3.524 mM, with a CAT-like activity of 6.021 U/mg. These results indicate that Au-CeO2 possesses excellent and stable antioxidant properties.

Since the catalytic activity of nanoparticles depends on the active sites exposed by the nanozyme, the surface-to-volume ratio of the particles was further investigated via nitrogen adsorption isotherm analysis. The BET surface area of Au-CeO2 was approximately 10 times that of CeO2 (Fig. S1C). Additionally, mesopores ranging from 5 to 20 nm were detected in Au-CeO2 (Fig. S1D). The core-shell structure of Au-CeO2, characterized by a high surface-to-volume ratio and large voids, facilitates the full exposure of catalytic sites and promotes the interaction between ROS and the nanozyme. Besides, the catalytic competence of ceria-based nanoparticles is governed by the proportion of Ce(III) to Ce(IV) and the nature of surface oxygen species. XPS analysis revealed that the Ce(III)/Ce(IV) ratio in Au-CeO2 (26.5/73.5) was approximately twice that in CeO2 (13.6/86.4) (Fig. S1E). Simultaneously, surface oxygen from defect sites (Oβ) is reported to play a pivotal role in most catalytic processes. Quantitative analysis indicated that the increase in Ce(III) promoted the proportion of Oβ in Au-CeO2 (47.1%) compared to CeO2 (28.3%) (Fig. S1F). The conversion rate from Ce(IV) to Ce(III) represents a critical bottleneck limiting the regeneration of the nanozyme activity of ceria nanoparticles. Consequently, temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) analysis was conducted. The H2-TPR profile exhibited a distinct reduction peak at 200 °C for Au-CeO2, whereas no significant peak was observed for pure CeO2 (Fig. S1G). This phenomenon is likely attributed to the fact that Au nanocores within Au-CeO2 significantly facilitate electron migration, coupled with the excellent reducing capability of surface Au—H species, thereby effectively accelerating the activation of reactants. These results indicate that Au-CeO2 not only possesses a higher Ce(III) content and oxygen vacancies, but also accelerates the reduction of Ce(IV) to Ce(III), thereby enhancing the catalytic activity of Au-CeO2.

Inspired by the superior antioxidant capacity, we utilized RAW 264.7 cells to evaluate the intracellular ROS scavenging ability of Au-CeO2. Cells were subjected to ROS stimulation to simulate oxidative stress, followed by treatment with DCFH-DA (Lai et al., 2025). The results showed that intracellular ROS levels in the control group were significantly elevated, presenting distinct green fluorescence, whereas cells treated with Au-CeO2 exhibited almost no observable green fluorescence (Fig. S2A). Further quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity indicated that while CeO2 also significantly reduced green fluorescence, its effect was inferior to that of Au-CeO2 (Fig. S2B). In conclusion, these results demonstrate that Au-CeO2, with its superior enzyme-mimicking activity, can effectively scavenge ROS and protect cells from oxidative stress.

Synthesis and characterization of Au-CeO₂-bFGF

To address inflammation triggered by the persistent generation of ROS in diabetic wounds, we developed a bilayer hydrogel consisting of a lower layer for ROS-responsive self-regulating Au-CeO2 antioxidant release and an upper layer for sustained bFGF release to promote repair. The lower layer hydrogel was constructed by cross-linking PVA, oxidized dextran and TSPBA with Au-CeO2 encapsulation to form an ROS-responsive network. The upper layer was formed by photo-cross-linking HAMA and sodium alginate loaded with bFGF. Together, these layers constituted the Au-CeO₂-bFGF bilayer hydrogel. The microstructure of the Au-CeO₂-bFGF was further characterized using SEM (Fig. 3A–B). The results indicated that the lower layer hydrogel possessed a relatively uniform pore size and a dense structure, whereas the upper layer exhibited a looser, highly porous three-dimensional network. Notably, the SEM images revealed a distinct interface boundary between the two hydrogel layers without any obvious gaps (Fig. S3A). This indicates the formation of a robust and stable interface between the layers, ensuring the dressing functions as a single, cohesive unit while maintaining its overall structural integrity. Quantitative analysis of the pore differences between the two layers revealed that the lower layer had an average pore size of 2.68 ± 1.17 μm and a porosity of 26.29 ± 4.23%. In contrast, the upper layer displayed an average pore size of 53.33 ± 4.62 μm and a porosity of 56.21 ± 4.56% (Fig. 3C and Fig. S3B). This structural variance endows the bilayer hydrogel with distinct functional properties: the dense network of the lower layer effectively encapsulates Au-CeO2 and enables ROS-responsive release, while the loose, porous 3D network of the upper layer provides a higher water content that not only helps maintain the bioactivity of bFGF but also creates a favorable physical environment for its sustained, slow release. FTIR analysis showed no significant differences in spectral peaks between the lower layer hydrogel with and without Au-CeO2 loading. Similarly, for the upper layer hydrogel, the primary peaks remained largely unchanged regardless of bFGF loading (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that the incorporation of Au-CeO2 and bFGF did not alter the chemical structure of the hydrogels themselves, suggesting both are physically encapsulated within the hydrogel matrix. Additionally, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the Au-CeO2-bFGF demonstrated that the PTOD-Au-CeO2 exhibited superior thermal stability at high temperatures, whereas the HASA-bFGF underwent significant decomposition at lower temperatures, likely due to its higher organic content (Figs. Rheological testing further elucidated the viscoelastic properties of the Au-CeO2-bFGF. The storage and loss moduli of PTOD-Au-CeO2 were greater than those of HASA-bFGF (Fig. 3E), indicating that PTOD-Au-CeO2 possesses higher rigidity. Further compression and tensile tests revealed that PTOD-Au-CeO₂ exhibits a high modulus and robust network structure, conferring strong resistance to deformation and ensuring structural integrity as the underlying support layer. In contrast, HASA-bFGF possesses a low modulus and high compressibility, enabling significant deformation under compression to conformally fill micro-irregularities of the wound surface and achieve initial sealing (Figs. S4A–S4B). The complementary mechanical properties of the two components synergistically enhance the hydrogel dressing's stability and tissue conformity during application and dynamic loading. Additionally, the adhesion of the hydrogels to mouse skin was evaluated via T-peel tests (Fig. S4C). The results demonstrate that HASA-bFGF exhibits higher adhesion strength compared to PTOD-Au-CeO₂. This difference in rheology ensures the mechanical stability of the bilayer structure: the lower layer provides structural support and rigidity, while the upper layer maintains flexibility and conformability, thereby achieving long-term in situ adaptation within the dynamic wound environment.

To further elucidate the physicochemical properties of the bilayer hydrogel, in vitro degradation experiments were conducted. In vitro degradation experiments further validated that in a simulated physiological environment (PBS solution), the bilayer hydrogel exhibited gradient degradation characteristics. The degradation rate of PTOD-Au-CeO2 was 14.27 ± 2.01% within 48 h, whereas that of HASA-bFGF was only 5.99 ± 1.73% over the same period, indicating that the degradation rate of PTOD-Au-CeO2 was slightly higher than that of HASA-bFGF (Fig. 3F). Subsequently, we investigated the degradation of PTOD-Au-CeO2 in PBS solutions containing varying concentrations of H2O2 to simulate oxidative stress. The results showed that the degradation rate of PTOD-Au-CeO2 increased significantly with rising H2O2 concentration (Fig. S4D). To better evaluate the ROS responsiveness of PTOD-Au-CeO2, we further examined its degradation and release kinetics in PBS containing 1 mM H2O2. During the degradation experiment of the bilayer hydrogel, the PTOD-Au-CeO2 was completely degraded within 48 h; although the degradation rate of the HASA-bFGF also increased, it reached only 26.05 ± 2.05% (Fig. 3G). This demonstrates that PTOD-Au-CeO2 possesses significant ROS responsiveness, while HASA-bFGF maintains structural stability. In drug release experiments using the bilayer hydrogel, both PTOD-Au-CeO2 and HASA-bFGF exhibited sustained release characteristics in PBS under simulated physiological conditions (Fig. 3H). However, release from PTOD-Au-CeO2 was accelerated under ROS stimulation, with a cumulative release rate of Au-CeO2 reaching 92.47 ± 4.06% within 48 h, significantly exceeding the 15.42 ± 1.56% observed in the H2O2-free group. In contrast, the HASA-bFGF consistently maintained a mild, sustained release profile (Fig. 3I). These results collectively indicate that the PTOD-Au-CeO2 layer functions in ROS-responsive release, while the HASA-bFGF layer serves the role of long-term sustained release; together, they synergistically and precisely adapt to the evolving needs of the dynamic wound microenvironment.

In addition, the bioactivity of the bFGF released from HASA-bFGF is critical for its therapeutic efficacy in subsequent applications. The biological function was evaluated by collecting the release supernatants of HASA-bFGF at 72 and 168 h and testing their effects on the proliferation of HUVECs. The results showed that bFGF released within 72 h significantly promoted HUVECs proliferation, whereas the proliferative capacity declined by 168 h (Fig. S5A–S5B). This decline may be attributed to the instability of bFGF as a protein; during the prolonged release process, it may lose activity due to degradation, aggregation, or adsorption. Meanwhile, SDS-PAGE was performed to verify the integrity of the released bFGF. Compared with normal purified bFGF, a corresponding protein band was observed for the 72 h, while the band intensity for the 168 h showed a marked decrease (Fig. S5C). This further validates that during the extended release period, the complete bFGF molecules became weakened due to significant depletion or degradation, thereby explaining the decrease in HUVECs proliferation capacity over time. In summary, HASA-bFGF is capable of releasing bFGF that maintains structural integrity and exhibits high bioactivity within 72 h.

Considering the typical presence of exudate at wound, the absorption performance of the dressing serves as a vital indicator. Therefore, the swelling ratio of HASA-bFGF was further evaluated. HASA-bFGF exhibited a significantly higher swelling ratio than PTOD-Au-CeO2—reaching approximately 400% at 24 h, compared to only approximately 100% for PTOD-Au-CeO2—indicating its superior liquid absorption and retention capacity (Fig. S6A). Moreover, using BSA-containing PBS to simulate wound exudate, HASA-bFGF demonstrated a higher exudate absorption rate than PTOD-Au-CeO2 (Fig. S6B). In addition, HASA-bFGF showed a more favorable water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) than PTOD-Au-CeO2 (Fig. S6C). Collectively, these results indicate that HASA-bFGF integrates excellent exudate management with optimal moisture regulation; coupled with its slow degradation profile, it can sustain a moist, breathable, and bioactive wound microenvironment over an extended period, thereby providing synergistic physicochemical and biological support for efficient wound healing.

Cells were stimulated with LPS to simulate an inflammatory environment, incubated with the hydrogels for a period, and then the cell culture supernatants were collected. Furthermore, the levels of inflammatory factors in the HASA and HASA-bFGF groups were similar. Macrophage polarization plays a pivotal role in regulating inflammatory progression. Typically, LPS induces macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, upregulating iNOS and leading to tissue damage, whereas the M2 phenotype exerts anti-inflammatory effects and promotes tissue repair through the high expression of Arg1. Therefore, M1 (iNOS) and M2 (Arg1) markers in macrophages were further detected to evaluate the inflammatory regulation capability of Au-CeO2-bFGF. These results indicate that the Au-CeO2-bFGF possesses the strongest anti-inflammatory effect. Its superior performance is attributed to the synergistic enhancement of Au-CeO2's resistance to inflammation and bFGF's promotion of cell repair. Moreover, the comparable anti-inflammatory efficacy of HASA and HASA-bFGF suggests that the addition of bFGF alone, without the synergistic effect of Au-CeO2, is insufficient to significantly improve anti-inflammatory performance. In summary, Au-CeO2-bFGF can significantly reduce ROS, inhibit apoptosis, and suppress inflammatory progression in oxidative stress and inflammatory environments.

Therefore, HUVECs were selected for subsequent experiments. Cell migration assay results showed that the migration distance in the Au-CeO2-bFGF and HASA-bFGF groups were greater than that in the other groups (Fig. 5A). Further quantitative analysis revealed that the Au-CeO2-bFGF group had the highest closure rate (Fig. 5B). This suggests that bFGF within the Au-CeO2-bFGF plays a positive role in promoting cell migration. Next, a tube formation assay further verified the pro-angiogenic effect of the Au-CeO2-bFGF. In vivo ROS-responsive behavior and release of Au-CeO2-bFGF

The ROS sensitivity of the lower layer and the sustained release of bFGF from the upper layer of the Au-CeO2-bFGF were preliminarily confirmed in vitro. To further investigate its in vivo ROS-responsive degradation and drug release profile, a diabetic wound model was established in mice. The PTOD-Au-CeO2 lower layer hydrogel was labeled with Cy5, while the HASA-bFGF upper-layer hydrogel was labeled with FITC. The results indicated that in the diabetic wound model, the PTOD-Au-CeO2 lower layer underwent rapid degradation, with the fluorescence signal almost completely disappearing by day 7. In contrast, the HASA-bFGF upper layer degraded slowly, maintaining strong fluorescence intensity at day 7 (Fig. 6A). Further quantitative fluorescence analysis showed that by day 3, the fluorescence intensity of the PTOD-Au-CeO2 lower layer had already decreased significantly, whereas the fluorescence intensity of the HASA-bFGF upper layer remained at a high level (Fig. 6B). In addition, the residual condition of the hydrogels on the wound surface was monitored to further evaluate the degradation behavior. The results showed that the residual mass of the PTOD-Au-CeO2 lower layer decreased significantly over time, whereas the HASA-bFGF upper layer retained a higher proportion of its weight (Fig. S9A), which was consistent with the fluorescence imaging results. This indicates that the PTOD-Au-CeO2 degrades faster in diabetic wounds, while the HASA-bFGF upper layer maintains structural integrity over a longer period. Subsequently, we evaluated the in vivo drug release profile of the Au-CeO2-bFGF. bFGF-FITC fluorescence labeling was employed to investigate bFGF release, while ICP-MS was used to detect Au content to characterize Au-CeO2 release. The results showed that by day 3, the majority of Au-CeO2 had been released into the wound tissue. By day 7, the cumulative release of Au-CeO2 exceeded 90%. In contrast, bFGF exhibited sustained release characteristics, even by day 7, the release rate of bFGF had not exceeded 30% (Fig. 6C). Meanwhile, to further verify the release profiles of Au-CeO2 and bFGF, the Ce content was quantitatively analyzed via ICP-MS, and the bFGF concentration was directly detected using an ELISA kit. Anti-inflammatory effects of Au-CeO2-bFGF in diabetic wounds

Persistent inflammation is a major factor hindering the healing of diabetic wounds. MPO, a marker of neutrophil infiltration, reflects the intensity of the inflammatory response (Nguyen et al., 2013). To further investigate the anti-inflammatory effects of the Au-CeO2-bFGF on diabetic wounds, we first performed MPO IHC staining on wound tissues from each group. On day 7, compared to the control group, the Au-CeO2-bFGF group showed a significant reduction in MPO positive cells, indicating a marked decrease in neutrophil infiltration. Both the PTOD-Au-CeO2 and HASA-bFGF groups exhibited fewer MPO positive cells than the control group, with the PTOD-Au-CeO2 group showing the lower count among the two (Fig. 8A). By day 14, the number of MPO positive cells decreased further in all groups, but the Au-CeO2-bFGF group still displayed the lowest level of MPO positivity, indicating that Au-CeO2-bFGF effectively inhibited neutrophil infiltration, thereby alleviating local inflammation. Quantitative analysis of the MPO IHC results revealed that the Au-CeO2-bFGF group had the lowest MPO positive rate on both days 7 and 14, while the PTOD-Au-CeO2 group ranked closest to it. This indicates that Au-CeO2 plays a dominant role in the anti-inflammatory effect. Although the HASA-bFGF group showed a downward trend in MPO positive rate compared to the control group, the reduction was less pronounced than in the groups containing Au-CeO2 and was similar to that of the HASA group. This suggests that bFGF alone has a limited inhibitory effect on inflammation, and the anti-inflammatory efficacy of the HASA-bFGF group is primarily derived from the HASA hydrogel itself (Fig. 8B). Concurrently, we measured the levels of key inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-12) in mouse wound tissues using ELISA. Results showed that on day 7, the levels of inflammatory cytokines in all experimental groups were significantly lower than those in the control group, with the Au-CeO2-bFGF group exhibiting the lowest levels. By day 14, inflammatory cytokine levels generally decreased across all groups, yet levels in the Au-CeO2-bFGF group remained significantly lower than in the other groups. Consistent with previous findings, the PTOD-Au-CeO2 group demonstrated significant inhibitory effects on all detected inflammatory markers, though its efficacy was slightly inferior to that of the Au-CeO2-bFGF group. The reductions in inflammatory cytokines in the HASA and HASA-bFGF groups were similar, further confirming that while HASA possesses some degree of anti-inflammatory capacity, its effect is limited, and the addition of bFGF did not significantly enhance HASA's anti-inflammatory capability (Fig. 8C–E; Fig. S11A). On day 7, the control group displayed high levels of iNOS and low levels of Arg1, corresponding to a typical M1-dominated pro-inflammatory state. Observation of wound tissue sections from different treatment groups on days 7 and 14 revealed that, compared to the control group, wound tissues in the Au-CeO2-bFGF group presented a denser, thicker network of bright red collagen fibers with more orderly arrangement, indicating higher structural strength and maturity of the neotissue (Fig. 10A). Quantitative image analysis further confirmed that collagen fiber deposition in the wound area of the Au-CeO2-bFGF group was the highest among all groups. The Au-CeO2-bFGF group exhibited the highest FN content in wound tissues on day 7; by day 14, FN content levels across all groups tended to be similar (Fig. 10G). Laminin (LN), a major component of the basement membrane, is critical for epithelial regeneration and the reconstruction of the dermal-epidermal junction. LN in the Au-CeO2-bFGF group showed changes similar to FN; on day 7, LN levels were significantly higher than in other groups, confirming that the hydrogel could effectively initiate and accelerate LN synthesis and deposition in the early stage of healing, constructing a solid and functionally complete basement “platform” for keratinocyte adhesion, migration, and differentiation. In diabetic wounds, persistent hyperglycemia and oxidative stress often lead to aberrant high expression of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP-2 and MMP-9, causing excessive ECM degradation and disordered remodeling (Kunkemoeller and Kyriakides, 2017), and forming a vicious cycle of "degradation exceeding synthesis". Therefore, we detected the levels of MMP-2 and MMP-9 in wound tissues. Its key antioxidant component (Au-CeO2) blocks the aberrant upregulation of MMPs from the upstream by scavenging ROS and alleviating oxidative stress, thereby stabilizing the dynamic balance of ECM degradation and remodeling. This provides protection for nascent collagen, FN and LN networks against excessive degradation, representing another core mechanism for its promotion of high-quality ECM remodeling. In comparison, although the HASA-bFGF group exerted some inhibitory effect on MMP-2 and MMP-9, its efficacy was inferior to that of the Au-CeO2-bFGF group. Taken together, the Au-CeO2-bFGF promotes ECM remodeling in diabetic wounds and lays a solid structural foundation for high-quality tissue regeneration through a dual strategy of "promoting collagen synthesis" (regulating dynamic collagen transition, elevating early deposition of FN and LN) and "inhibiting collagen degradation" (regulating MMP levels).

Pro-angiogenic effects of Au-CeO2-bFGF in diabetic wounds

Regulating the expression of key pro-angiogenic factors can effectively improve local blood supply, thereby accelerating healing. CD31 and α-SMA are classic markers for evaluating angiogenesis: CD31 specifically marks vascular endothelial cells and is used to directly observe microvessel density. α-SMA primarily marks pericytes and smooth muscle cells, and their presence marks the maturation and stability of neovascular structures (Pankoke et al., 2023). IHC staining for CD31 and α-SMA in wound tissues showed that, compared to the control group, the Au-CeO2-bFGF group had significantly increased expression of CD31 and α-SMA on days 7 and 14, and was also significantly higher than the PTOD-Au-CeO2 and HASA-bFGF groups, although the HASA-bFGF group was closer in expression level to the Au-CeO2-bFGF group (Fig. 11A–B). By day 14, the Au-CeO2-bFGF group maintained its advantage in CD31 and α-SMA expression (Fig. 11C–D). Therefore, the Au-CeO2-bFGF bilayer hydrogel possesses the dual efficacy of promoting angiogenesis and ensuring its maturity and stability, establishing a key and durable blood supply foundation for diabetic wound repair.

Advantages of Au-CeO2-bFGF bilayer hydrogel

To better highlight the functional advantage of the bilayer hydrogel design, a control group was established by in vitro thorough mixing of Au-CeO2 dispersion with bFGF solution to form a homogeneous mixture (Au-CeO2 + bFGF), which was then directly applied onto diabetic wounds—this was compared side-by-side with the Au-CeO2-bFGF bilayer hydrogel. Systematic experimental results demonstrate that, compared with the physically mixed Au-CeO2 + bFGF solution, the Au-CeO2-bFGF bilayer hydrogel confers comprehensive advantages in diabetic wound healing. Specifically, the Au-CeO2-bFGF group exhibited accelerated wound closure and significantly higher healing rates (Figs. S14A-S14B). Histological evaluation revealed reduced inflammatory infiltration and more abundant, well-organized collagen deposition (Figs. S14C-S14E). At Day 14, relative to the Au-CeO2 + bFGF group, the Au-CeO2-bFGF group showed markedly decreased TNF-α levels (Fig. S14F), enhanced antioxidant capacity (as indicated by reduced oxidative stress and elevated SOD activity) (Figs. S14G-S14I), increased collagen synthesis within the ECM (Figs. S14J-S14L), and significantly improved neovascularization—evidenced by upregulated CD31 expression and elevated VEGF levels (Figs. S14M-S14O). Critically, these advantages do not stem from compositional differences but rather from the structural design of the bilayer hydrogel, which enables functional compartmentalization and temporally coordinated delivery: the bottom hydrogel layer rapidly releases Au-CeO2 to provide immediate ROS scavenging, thereby stabilizing the wound microenvironment and preserving bFGF bioactivity; subsequently, the top hydrogel layer sustains localized bFGF release, synergistically counteracting the dual challenges of oxidative stress and growth factor inactivation inherent to diabetic wounds. In contrast, the physically mixed Au-CeO2 + bFGF solution—lacking structural integrity—suffers from rapid component clearance, uncontrolled release kinetics, and decoupled antioxidant and pro-angiogenic actions, ultimately resulting in suboptimal and transient repair efficacy. Thus, the core advantage of the Au-CeO2-bFGF bilayer hydrogel lies in its stage-specific, spatiotemporally programmed delivery strategy, rather than mere chemical composition.

Safety of Au-CeO2-bFGF

The in vivo safety of Au-CeO2-bFGF is a critical factor in evaluating its clinical feasibility. To comprehensively assess the biocompatibility of Au-CeO2-bFGF, the hydrogel was applied to wounds on healthy mice. At 3 weeks, major organs (liver, kidney, heart, lung and spleen) were harvested for histological analysis via HE staining. Compared to healthy mice, no obvious pathological changes or morphological abnormalities were observed in the major organs of mice treated with Au-CeO2-bFGF (Fig. S15A). This demonstrates that Au-CeO2-bFGF possesses good biocompatibility. Concurrently, blood was collected via the tail vein to detect blood cell counts and liver and kidney function indicators. Results showed no significant differences in white blood cells, red blood cells, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) or creatinine (Cr) between mice treated with Au-CeO2-bFGF and healthy mice (Fig. S15B–S15G), further verifying that the treatment had no obvious impact on major organ function in mice. ICP-MS quantification revealed that, at week 3, Au and Ce remained predominantly localized at the wound, with negligible accumulation in major organs—including heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney—indicating excellent local retention of Au-CeO2-bFGF and a reduced risk of systemic organ accumulation and associated toxicity (Fig. S15H–S15I). In summary, Au-CeO2-bFGF exhibits favorable in vivo biocompatibility, laying a solid foundation for its clinical application in the treatment of diabetic wounds.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully fabricated an Au-CeO2-bFGF bilayer hydrogel designed for lower layer ROS-responsive release of Au-CeO2 for antioxidant therapy and upper layer sustained release of bFGF to promote repair. The hydrogel utilizes the lower PTOD-Au-CeO2 layer to achieve rapid responsiveness to the high ROS microenvironment of diabetic wounds, efficiently scavenging excess ROS. This effectively inhibits the inflammatory response and mitigates oxidative damage. Following the amelioration of the inflammatory milieu, the system transitions sequentially to the upper HASA-bFGF layer, which slowly releases bFGF to promote ECM remodeling and angiogenesis, thereby facilitating wound healing. The Au-CeO2, synthesized via a self-redox reaction, exhibited excellent antioxidant activity, with in vitro experiments confirming its ability to protect cells against ROS attack. The lower layer hydrogel was constructed by crosslinking PVA, TSPBA and oxidized dextran to form a ROS-responsive network loaded with Au-CeO2, while the upper layer was formed by photocrosslinking HAMA and sodium alginate loaded with bFGF. Together, these constitute the Au-CeO2-bFGF. The Au-CeO2-bFGF demonstrated significant biocompatibility, as well as the capacity to enhance cell migration, promote angiogenesis and provide antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. In vivo animal experiments validated the significant synergistic therapeutic efficacy of Au-CeO2 and bFGF. Au-CeO2 halted inflammatory progression, restored redox homeostasis, and inhibited the overexpression of MMPs, thereby establishing a stable microenvironment for the optimal bioactivity of bFGF. This synergy effectively promoted the orderly deposition of collagen and high-quality ECM remodeling, and drove angiogenesis and maturation. While these findings are encouraging, it is important to acknowledge that this study is limited to a murine diabetic wound model with a relatively short follow-up period. Before clinical translation, further validation of biosafety and efficacy is required using diverse wound repair models, particularly large animal models. Additionally, in-depth investigations into aseptic preparation standards, scalability for mass production, and long-term biocompatibility are essential to lay a solid foundation for clinical application. Overall, this study presents a novel therapeutic strategy for the repair of chronic diabetic wounds, although further translational verification is warranted to realize its clinical potential.
