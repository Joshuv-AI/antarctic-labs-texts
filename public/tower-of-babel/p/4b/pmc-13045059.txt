Who uses the only mosquito net? Sex and age disparities
Authors: Eriko Ikeda, Noboru Minakawa, Wataru Kagaya, Yura K Ko, George Sonye, James Kongere, Bernard N Kanoi, Jesse Gitaka, Konosuke Morimoto, Lucy Atieno Okech, Nguka Ignation, Kyoko Futami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe percentage of the population sleeping under insecticide-treated nets (ITN) increased in sub-Saharan Africa from 3 to 59% between 2000 and 2023. However, achieving universal coverage remains far from realized. Because ITNs are the most effective tools for preventing malaria, it is important to identify barriers to their use and maximize their protective effects. We investigated sex and age disparities in ITN use in a region of western Kenya where Plasmodium transmission remains high.MethodsWe examined sex- and age-related disparities in ITN use under two scenarios: households with sufficient ITNs and those with insufficient ITNs, focusing specifically on households possessing only one ITN. Residents were asked whether they had slept under an ITN the previous night, as well as their age and sex. They were also tested for P. falciparum (PF) infection using a rapid diagnostic test (RDT).ResultsThe proportion of residents using ITNs was 91% in households with sufficient ITNs and 66% in households with insufficient ITNs. ITN availability averaged 1.7 persons per net (SD = 0.35; n = 1445) in households with sufficient ITNs and 4.4 persons per net in households with insufficient ITNs. A generalized additive mixed model revealed non-linear relationships between ITN use and age in households with insufficient ITNs. Adult females aged 19–40 years and infants were consistently prioritized, whereas residents aged 3–18 years exhibited lower levels of ITN use. In contrast, this pattern was not evident in households with sufficient ITNs. Among households possessing only one ITN, RDT-positive prevalence was below 15% among infants and adult females, whereas it exceeded 30% among young males and females. Overall, ITN use was associated with a 20% reduction in PF infection.ConclusionsIn households with insufficient ITNs, adult females and infants were typically prioritized for ITN use. Given that PF infection was negatively associated with ITN use, such prioritization represents an appropriate allocation strategy under conditions of limited net availability. However, the low level of ITN use and the high prevalence of PF infection among young residents remain a substantial concern.Keywords: ITN use, Priority, RDT, Age, Sex, Plasmodium falciparum

Background

Insecticide treated nets (ITNs) are the most effective tool for preventing malaria parasite infection [1–3]. With an increase of ITN coverage, the Plasmodium infection prevalence in endemic Africa was halved between 2000 and 2015, and the clinical cases declined by 40% [4]. ITNs, the most widely implemented intervention, accounted for nearly two-thirds of this reduction, followed by artemisinin-based combination therapies and indoor residual spray (IRS) [5]. Although ITNs continued to be distributed to high-risk areas, the rate of decline in malaria burden stalled markedly from 2014 to 2018 [6, 7]. After 2020, increases in malaria incidence and mortality were reported in many African countries [1]. Without substantial changes to current strategies, achieving the malaria elimination targets set by the Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030 will be difficult [7].

According to the World Malaria Report 2024, the percentage of households owning at least one ITN for every two people in sub-Saharan Africa increased from 1% in 2000 to 36% in 2015, and to 45% in 2023 [8]. Between 2000 and 2023, the percentage of the population sleeping under an ITN also increased from 3 to 59%. These figures indicate that achieving universal coverage (with one ITN for every two people) remains far from realized [9]. While substantial efforts are needed to achieve universal coverage, even maintaining current ITN coverage is challenging, as ITNs must be replaced every three to five years.

While waiting for that innovative intervention tool to become available, it is important to identify issues related to ITN use and maximize the benefits of what is currently the most effective intervention. In addition to the problem of insufficient ITNs, several other challenges related to ITN use have been documented. A meta-analysis of ITN use data from 29 sub-Saharan African countries reported that ITN use peaks among children aged 0–4 years and adults aged 30–40 years, but declines among individuals aged 5–14 years and those above 50 [10]. The same study showed that this sinusoidal pattern becomes more pronounced when ITNs are insufficient, and that ITN use tends to be higher among females than males. On the contrary, a study in Uganda reported that heads of household are prioritized for ITN use when ITNs are insufficient [11]. Young males aged 15–24 years were less likely to use ITNs than young females in southern Ethiopia [12], whereas poor women and rural pregnant women were more likely to use ITNs [13]. A study in western Kenya also reported that ITN use decreased with increasing age among children under five years of age [14, 15]. Collectively, these findings suggest that sex- and age-related disparities in ITN use are common.

We examined sex- and age-related disparities in insecticide-treated net (ITN) use under two scenarios: households with sufficient and insufficient ITNs. We focused specifically on households possessing only one ITN, as one of the ITN availability indicators recommended by the Roll Back Malaria Monitoring and Reference Group is the proportion of households owning at least one ITN [16]. While this indicator reflects the spatial coverage of ITN distribution activities, it does not capture whether ITN supply is sufficient at the population level and may therefore overestimate the success of an ITN distribution program [9]. Nevertheless, this indicator allows for a more rigorous assessment of prioritization in ITN use within households. By addressing this issue, we identified population groups that are more effectively protected by ITNs, as well as those that require greater protection in households with insufficient net availability.

Methods

Study area

The study area encompassed the Gambe East, Gambe North, Gambe Central, and Lambwe West sub-locations in Suba North Sub-County, western Kenya (Fig. 1). According to the Integrated Community Health System (ICHS), which is regularly updated by community health promoters (CHPs), the area contained 5398 households, 10,593 house structures, and a total population of 37,041 residents in 2024. The total land area is approximately 146 km2.

Study area in western Kenya

Most houses are constructed using a stick framework plastered with a mixture of mud and cow dung, and are roofed with corrugated iron sheets. The majority of residents belong to the Luo ethnic group. Dholuo is the primary language spoken, although most residents speak English and Kiswahili. The main sources of livelihood include fishing, traditional small-scale farming, and cattle keeping. Rainfall in the area follows a bimodal pattern, with the long rainy season typically occurring from early March to early June, and the short rainy season from October to early December, although these periods vary from year to year.

The study area was situated within the Lake Victoria Basin, a region where Plasmodium transmission remains persistently high. According to the Kenya Health Information System, there were 264.9 and 244.7 confirmed malaria cases per 1,000 population in Suba North Sub-County in 2024. The malaria vector species recorded in areas adjacent to the study area include Anopheles arabiensis, Anopheles gambiae s.s., and Anopheles funestus s.s. [17]. When the former two species are grouped as Anopheles gambiae s.l., a previous study in a nearby area reported that nearly 90% of A. gambiae s.l. individuals were A. arabiensis [18]. ITNs have been distributed at health facilities, primarily to protect infants and pregnant women. The most common treatment provided at local health facilities is artemisinin-based combination therapy. In this region, the last ITN distribution and IRS campaign were conducted in 2023, and the RTS,S malaria vaccine has been piloted since 2018 and 2019 [19].

Data collection

The present study forms part of the baseline assessment for a cluster randomized controlled trial designed to evaluate piperonyl butoxide–pyrethroid ceiling nets, a novel house-screening tool installed across ceilings, among high-risk households with poor ITN use in the study area [20–23]. The trial targeted households that either did not own any ITNs or owned only one ITN despite the recent mass distribution campaign. We estimated that a sample size of 500 households would be required for the trial. To ensure that at least 500 households meeting the predefined inclusion criteria could be recruited, the study area was gradually expanded during the planning phase to include five sub-locations, based on ICHS data. The baseline assessment consisted of a questionnaire survey that included items on ITN ownership, which were used as part of the eligibility assessment. Residents were also interviewed about whether they had slept under an ITN the previous night, a standard measure for assessing ITN use, and their sex and age were also recorded.

The baseline survey recorded a total of 4518 households with complete information on residents and ITNs and with open eaves. Of these, 3920 households possessed at least one ITN. In the present study, we used the entire population of 3920 households to examine sex- and age-related disparities in ITN use under two scenarios: households with sufficient and insufficient ITNs.

Of 4,518 households, 598 (13%) did not have any ITNs, and 1101 (24%) had only one ITN. A total of 927 eligible households with at least five members were identified for the cluster randomized controlled trial by September 2025. For households without any ITNs or with only one ITN, the baseline survey also included Plasmodium falciparum (PF) infection screening and an entomological survey. At the time of RDT screening, 501 of these households had at least five members available (Fig. 2). For a more rigorous assessment of prioritization in ITN use within households in the present study, we included 280 of the 501 households that possessed only one ITN, while the remaining 221 households did not have any ITNs.

Flowchart of sampling procedure

We tested all members of 501 households available at the time for PF infection using finger-prick blood samples analyzed with the Bioline Malaria Ag P.f/Pan RDT (Abbott Diagnostics Korea Inc., Republic of Korea). The cross-sectional survey excluded newborns aged 0–6 months due to the high burden associated with blood collection, their relatively low risk of PF infection, and the potential influence of maternally derived antibodies. Participants with severe chronic illnesses or those who had traveled for more than three weeks during the month preceding the survey were also excluded. Individuals who tested positive received artemether–lumefantrine in accordance with the dosing guidelines of the Kenyan Ministry of Health, after a review of their recent treatment history.

A composite socioeconomic status (SES) index was generated for 501 households using principal component analysis (PCA) based on household-level variables adapted from the 2020 Kenya Malaria Indicator Survey (KMIS), following standard procedures of the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) Program [24]. The first principal component was used as the SES index, with weights derived from the PCA loadings. A weighted score was then calculated for each household and subsequently used to categorize households into three SES groups.

Data management

All data from the baseline survey were captured using Microsoft Excel on electronic tablets. The data were then transferred to a secure computer at the Nagasaki University office in Mbita Sub-County, which is housed in a locked room. Access to the data and database management privileges was restricted to authorized study investigators and collaborators. After verification of the uploaded data, all locally stored information on the tablets was permanently deleted to minimize the risk of unauthorized access.

Confidentiality

To ensure confidentiality, each participant was assigned a unique study identifier. All collected data were labeled with this identifier and stored separately from the linkage key containing personally identifiable information (such as name, date of birth, household GPS location, and phone number). All publications used only aggregated data, and no personally identifiable information was disclosed.

Statistical analysis

Because the relationships between ITN use and PF infection and age and sex are often non-linear [10], we applied both generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) and generalized additive mixed model (GAMM) with a binomial distribution. Household was included as a random intercept. In the GAMM, we applied the smoothing function for age, and the interaction between age and sex was also considered. The number of household residents was included as a covariate in the analysis for 3,920 households. SES was also included in the analysis for a subset of 280 households. The optimal model was selected using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) or the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) in the case of Bayesian models.

After confirming the presence of non-linear association, we stratified the population into seven age–sex groups: infants (0–2 years, both sexes), young females (3–18 years), young males (3–18 years), female adults (19–40 years), male adults (19–40 years), older female adults (> 40 years), and older male adults (> 40 years). Infants were analyzed as a combined group due to minimal behavioral differences between sexes and the likelihood that they sleep with a caretaker. Dummy variables were constructed for each group using contrast coding to allow comparisons across groups [25].

We evaluated the influence of age and sex on the prevalence of ITN use and PF infection in each group using a log-binomial generalized linear mixed model. Spatial dependence was incorporated to improve model performance under a Bayesian framework (R package: R-INLA) [26–28]. Household was included as a random intercept.

Ethics

Ethics approvals were obtained from the Mount Kenya University Institutional Scientific Ethics Review Committee (MKU-ISERC) (approval number: 3861) and the Ethics Committee of Nagasaki University (approval number: 250619328).

Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior to the baseline survey. Participants were provided with detailed information regarding the collection, storage, and use of personal data and biological specimens during the study by trained study staff who were fully familiar with the study protocol and able to communicate study information in the local languages. The consent form clearly stated that the samples collected in this study may be used in future research. In such cases, additional ethical approval will be obtained, and participants will be given the opportunity to opt out. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with WHO guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Sex and age disparities in households with sufficient and insufficient ITNs

Among the 3920 households that possessed at least one ITN, we identified 25,933 residents (mean: 6.6 per household). Of these households, the number of ITNs was sufficient in 1,445 households and insufficient in 2475 households. The proportion of residents using ITNs was 91% in households with sufficient ITNs and 66% in households with insufficient ITNs. ITN availability, defined as the number of residents per ITN, was 1.7 persons per net (SD = 0.35; n = 1445) in households with sufficient ITNs and 4.4 persons per net (SD = 2.7; n = 2,475) in households with insufficient ITNs.

For ITN use in households with sufficient ITNs, the best-fitting model was a GLMM including an interaction term between age and sex (− 0.04, 95% CI − 0.06 to − 0.02). ITN use was positively associated with age (0.06, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.08) (Fig. 3). In this model, the association between ITN use and the number of household residents was not statistically significant (0.03, 95% CI − 0.08 to 0.11).

In contrast, a GAMM with a smooth function of age (edf = 7.6, χ2 = 1871, p < 0.001) and without the interaction between age and sex provided the best fit for ITN use among residents in households with insufficient ITNs. ITN use declined sharply during the school-age period, then increased and peaked at approximately 40 years of age for females and 50 years of age for males, before declining again in older age groups. Overall, the decline during the school-age period was more pronounced among males, and ITN use was generally higher among females except in the oldest male age group. Overall, the ITN use was significantly lower among males than females ( − 0.42, 95%CI − 0.53 to − 0.31) while the effect of the number of residents was not statistically significant ( − 0.01, 95%CI − 0.03 to 0.02).

Sex and age disparities in households with only one ITN

In 280 households that possessed only one ITN, we found 1993 residents (mean: 7.1 per household). As nearly 60% of residents (approximately 1196 persons) reported sleeping under an ITN the previous night, each ITN was shared by an average of 4.3 persons. After excluding records with missing data, a total of 1,593 residents were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2).

A GAMM including a smooth function of age (edf = 7.6, χ2 = 1871, p < 0.001) and no interaction terms provided the best fit for ITN use among residents in households with only one ITN (Fig. 4). Overall, the ITN use was significantly lower among males than females ( − 0.51, 95% CI − 0.87 to − 0.16), while the effects of SES and the number of residents were not statistically significant ( − 0.02, 95%CI − 0.65 to 0.61 for residents, and Chi-square test comparing two models with and without SES, p = 0.21 for SES).

Associations between age and ITN use stratified by sex

As we confirmed the non-linear relationships between ITN use and age, we stratified the population into seven age–sex groups. Female adults aged 19–40 years exhibited the highest ITN use (> 70%) among all groups (Table 1). More than 65% of infants and older adult males also used ITN. Log-binomial regression analysis provided strong evidence that age and sex are associated with increased ITN use in these groups. The analysis also indicated substantially lower ITN use among younger individuals of both sexes. ITN use was negatively associated with higher SES, whereas its association with the number of household residents was negligible.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that sex- and age-related disparities become more pronounced in households with insufficient ITNs. Households that possessed only a single ITN for all members provided an ideal setting for identifying which individuals are prioritized for ITN use. We found that adult females aged 19–40 years and infants were consistently prioritized, whereas residents aged 3–18 years exhibited lower levels of ITN use.

These findings were further confirmed by analyses of households with insufficient ITNs (more than two persons per net) across the entire population in the study area. A meta-analysis of ITN use data from 29 sub-Saharan African countries reported a similar sinusoidal pattern, with peaks among children aged 0–4 years and adults aged 30–40 years [10]. That analysis also showed that this pattern was more pronounced in households with insufficient ITNs than in those with adequate coverage. Consistent with the previous findings, this pronounced pattern was evident when households with sufficient and insufficient ITNs were compared in our study.

The greater adherence to ITN use observed among infants and adult females is closely interrelated. When mothers sleep under an ITN, their infants typically share the same net [29]. Results from the RDT screening suggest that this practice substantially reduces PF infection among both mothers and infants, consistent with the recommended standard of one net per two people [9]. A common assumption is that individuals with greater authority within a household are prioritized for ITN use [11]. In Luo culture, mothers play important decision-making roles, including determining planting schedules and overseeing household repairs. Regardless of this cultural context, prioritizing ITN use to protect the most vulnerable individuals within a household represents a rational and common-sense behavior. Infants have low blood volume, limited iron reserves, and immature immune systems, which can contribute to the rapid development of severe malaria [30]. Malaria during pregnancy poses a significant risk to maternal health [31] as well as to the developing fetus [32].

Kenya’s national malaria control policy prioritizes the distribution of ITNs to pregnant women and mothers with infants at local health facilities, free of charge. Antenatal care and child welfare clinics complement mass distribution campaigns as key sources of ITNs [33]. These health facilities repeatedly provide malaria prevention messages and education to women of reproductive age, even when they visit for non-malaria-related services such as immunization. These routine health services likely enhance access to ITNs and reinforce consistent preventive behaviors compared with other age groups [8].

The findings of our study reaffirm the persistent problem of low ITN adherence among young residents [10], particularly in households with an insufficient number of ITNs, where they are often given lower priority. Even when ITNs are available, they must be hung and removed daily, a task that is inconvenient and challenging for children sleeping on the floor. After the age of three, many children begin sleeping on the floor in living rooms, communal eating areas, kitchens, or small detached structures within the same compound, which frequently lack beds [29]. The absence of a bed frame prevents proper hanging of the net, leaving children’s extremities exposed and unprotected.

A previous study conducted in the same area reported that sleeping on the floor was associated with an increased risk of PF infection [14]. Consistent with this finding, our study demonstrated a strong association between low ITN adherence and higher PF infection among young residents. Although infection prevalence is high in these age groups, infections are often asymptomatic, which may lead to an underestimation of their need for protection and consequently contribute to lower ITN use [34, 35].

Young children often sleep with siblings under the same net after moving out of their mothers’ beds. In the present study, households reported a total of 280 ITNs for 1993 persons. As nearly 60% of residents (approximately 1196 persons) reported sleeping under an ITN the previous night, each ITN was shared by an average of 4.3 persons. When too many people sleep under a single net, their bodies may press against the netting, allowing Anopheles mosquitoes to bite through it, or some body parts may be pushed outside the net. A previous study conducted in the same area demonstrated that the risk of infection is associated with the number of people sharing the same ITN [36].

Despite the high risk of infection in the study area, only 30% of households possessed a sufficient number of ITNs to protect all household members, based on the criterion of one net per two persons. Moreover, more than 13% of the targeted households did not own any ITNs. This situation falls far short of achieving universal coverage and consequently exacerbates sex- and age-related disparities in ITN use.

Persistent ITN shortages are likely driven by both supply- and demand-side factors. On the supply side, mass free distribution campaigns occur only every few years, and during the intervening periods, household growth and net deterioration reduce coverage. Although coverage is high immediately after campaigns, access declines by approximately 10% per year [37]. In Kenya, more than half of ITNs are distributed through triennial mass campaigns, with routine distribution via antenatal care and child welfare clinics intended to fill the gaps [33]. However, these channels often function suboptimally because of incomplete household registration, weak data systems, limited community engagement, barriers to facility access, and unstable supply chains [38, 39].

On the demand side, discrepancies between ITN ownership and use further contribute to shortages. Some households delay using newly acquired ITNs to reserve them for future replacement [40], while others repurpose nets for non-sleeping uses [41, 42]. Together, these factors likely explain the observed ratio of 4.3 persons per ITN, which remains inconsistent with current policy recommendations.

Limitation

We did not account for other potential confounders, such as the distance to mosquito breeding sites or proximity to ITN distribution points. A study incorporating these factors would allow for more robust and well-supported conclusions. To detect PF infection, we used RDTs. Although RDTs are practical for field surveys, PCR-based detection provides greater accuracy, given the potential for both false-positive and false-negative results associated with RDTs [44]. Because no data were available to characterize the relationship between mothers and their young children, the inference drawn from the results, that women aged 19–40 who were prioritized for bed net use also confer protection to their children, cannot be clearly confirmed, and additional research is required. Data obtained through interviews, such as the number of ITNs owned and their use, may be subject to inaccuracy. However, data from the 280 households are considered more reliable because they were reconfirmed during RDT screening.

Conclusions

Insufficient ITN availability was associated with more pronounced sex- and age-related disparities in ITN use, which may reflect differences in the risk of PF infection. Reproductive females with infants were typically prioritized for its use. Given that PF infection was negatively associated with ITN use, such prioritization represents an appropriate allocation strategy under conditions of limited net availability [9]. However, the low ITN use and high PF infection prevalence among young residents were of considerable concern, as most infections in this age group are asymptomatic and therefore unlikely to be treated [43, 44]. Because these individuals may serve as a reservoir of gametocytes [45, 46], the development of innovative educational programs is warranted to promote behavioral change [47, 48]. Above all, the most critical priority is to improve universal ITN coverage, defined as one net for every two individuals.
