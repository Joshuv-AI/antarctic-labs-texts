Impact of iron dextran shortage on patients with intestinal failure receiving home parenteral nutrition: Experience from a multidisciplinary rehabilitation program
Authors: Sylvia Wong-Sterling, Christina Belza, Dianna Yanchis, Musa Nur, Elizabeth Garofalo, Carina Silva, Yaron Avitzur, Jessie M. Hulst, Daniela Gattini Valdes, Glenda Courtney-Martin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIron deficiency is prevalent in children with intestinal failure (IF), and enteral iron is not effective in this population. Iron deficiency (ID)in growing children is associated with neurocognitive deficits in adulthood despite correction of deficiency at diagnosis. While many intestinal rehabilitation programs (IRP) exclude iron from PN prescriptions, our center historically added iron dextran (iDex) daily until its unavailability in 2023. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of iDex shortage on children with IF on home PN (HPN), followed by the IRP.Material and methodsA retrospective chart review of HPN patients assessed the number of patients requiring intravenous iron post-discontinuation of iDex, biochemical markers of iron status, and responses to enteral iron supplementation. Data were compared pre (baseline) and post iron discontinuation using a related-sample T-test and analysis of variance.ResultsFifty-six HPN patients aged 8 ± 1.4 y (mean ± SD) were reviewed. Before iDex discontinuation, 3 % required IV iron infusions. Since discontinuation, 3 % tolerated enteral supplementation, with no biochemical improvement in iron status, and 27 % required IV iron infusion within 12 months due to development of iron deficiency.ConclusionThe addition of iron to PN seems to be advantageous for iron deficiency prevention in children with IF. Given the association of iron deficiency with neurocognitive deficits, management of iron status should focus on prevention of iron deficiency. Therefore, for patients with IF on long-term PN, iron can be beneficial as part of the PN prescription for adequate provision of a balance nutrient profile.

Introduction

Intestinal failure (IF) is a complex medical condition characterized by the inability of the intestines to absorb adequate fluids and nutrients to sustain life [1]. This necessitates the use of parenteral nutrition (PN) in which all essential nutrients should be provided intravenously. Iron is not routinely included as part of the PN prescription in many centers leading to increased risk of deficiency [2]. Iron deficiency is one of the most prevalent micronutrient deficiencies in children with IF [3] and worldwide [4], and the most common cause of anemia [5]. The consequences of iron deficiency have short- and long-term impacts including hematological manifestation, growth failure, compromised immune function and neurocognitive impairment. This is of particular concern in growing children as evidenced by data from Lozoff et al., [6] and East et al., [7], who noted the presence of adverse neurocognitive and educational outcomes in adults who were diagnosed with iron deficiency anemia in childhood. The neurocognitive deficits and poor educational outcomes were present in adulthood despite correction of the anemia when it was diagnosed. These results underscore the importance of prioritizing prevention as the key strategy for management of iron status in growing children.

Maintenance of adequate iron status in pediatric patients with IF is challenging due to the interplay between gastrointestinal dysfunction, impaired nutrient absorption, altered iron metabolism, iatrogenic factors and comorbidities such as chronic inflammation [8]. Pediatric patients with IF at the highest risk of iron deficiency include preterm neonates with pre-existing low iron stores in conjunction with high demand to support hemoglobin synthesis and neurodevelopment, as well as during adolescence to support blood volume expansion, menstruation, and muscle mass development [8] as such the prevalence of ID and iron deficiency anemia (IDA) is significantly elevated in the IF population compared to healthy controls [9] [10]. Diagnostic evaluation of iron deficiency in children with IF poses unique challenges due to confounding factors such as chronic illness, concomitant micronutrient deficiencies, and limitations of conventional laboratory markers [8], [11]. Moreover, the assessment of iron status must be contextualized within the dynamic interplay of inflammation, erythropoiesis, and iron metabolism characteristic of IF.

Management of iron status in this population necessitates a tailored, multidisciplinary approach encompassing nutritional optimization, parenteral, intravenous, and/or enteral iron supplementation, while addressing underlying causes of malabsorption and chronic inflammation. However, therapeutic decision-making is often hampered by a paucity of high-quality evidence and a lack of consensus guidelines specific to children with IF [5]. From a clinical perspective, enteral iron supplementation is often poorly absorbed and not well tolerated. In children receiving long-term PN, who cannot maintain adequate iron status using enteral iron supplements, intravenous infusion is needed. Parenteral iron can be given daily when added to PN solution or as intermittent infusions. However, some concerns regarding the use of iron in PN have been raised, specifically regarding the potential stability of the PN formulation and the formation of reactive oxidants potentially increasing the risk of precipitation, iron overload, and DNA adducts [5].

Many parenteral nutrition (PN) programs do not routinely add iron to PN solutions [5]. However, at our centre, for over 45 years standard practice was to add iron dextran (iDex) daily to all PN solutions for both inpatient and outpatient population. This approach was based on iron’s critical role as an essential nutrient, whose deficiency in growing children is associated with long term neurocognitive deficits in adulthood. This longstanding practice was discontinued in May 2023 when iDex became unavailable on the Canadian market.

The goal of the current study, therefore, was to formally document the effects of IV iron dextran discontinuation in patients with IF receiving home PN (HPN).

Material and methods

Pre iron dextran (iDex) discontinuation, it was routinely added to PN solutions at 0.5–1.0 mg daily and titrated based on biochemical parameters of iron status and stability in the 3 in 1 PN solutions. Biochemical parameters of iron status were monitored every 3–6 months.

Post iDex discontinuation, patients underwent biochemical monitoring every 1–3 months to evaluate their iron status. Based on iron status, enteral supplementation was prescribed at 6.0–10.0 mg/kg/day. Patients who didn’t tolerate enteral supplementation or had inadequate response in biochemical parameters were ordered intravenous iron infusion. Intravenous supplementation was provided as ferric derisomaltose (monoferric) at a dosage of 15–20 mg/kg/dose with ongoing monitoring every 1–3 months post to determine ongoing need for infusion. Decision for timing of iron infusion was based on clinical experience of the multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation team.

The primary study objective was to evaluate iron status before and after discontinuation of iron dextran. Our secondary objective was to evaluate frequency of iron infusions, and adverse side effects related to iron infusions.

A Retrospective review of patients charts between October 1, 2023, to May 30, 2024 was completed. Patients were included in the chart review if they had a diagnosis of IF (as per the current ASPEN definition [1], primarily managed by the IRP and were dependent on home PN during the time identified.

The following data was collected via manual review of the electronic medical records of all patients, i.e., demographics, IF characteristics, PN composition and parenteral nutrition dependency index (PNDI), enteral iron supplementation pre and post iDex discontinuation, biochemical markers of iron status (ferritin, CBC, transferrin saturation), intravenous iron infusion since iDex discontinuation and adverse events related to intravenous iron infusion.

Children with a PNDI ≥120% were considered very highly dependent, 80-120% were considered highly dependent whereas those <80% were considered moderately dependent on PN [12].•Transferrin saturation was calculated using a standard equation - (Iron/transferrin) x 70.9. Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were evaluated for all patients including means and standard deviations. Data was compared pre-iron discontinuation to post iron discontinuation using a related-sample T-test. Data was reported as means (standard deviations) and frequencies (proportions). Data was analyzed using excel and SAS Studio (Cary, NC). A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Research ethics approval for this study was obtained from the Hospital for Sick Children Research Ethics Board (REB #1000080474).

Adverse events related to iron infusion

Three patients developed adverse reactions either during or within 24 h of iron infusion. Two developed fever and rash whereas the other developed respiratory symptoms; coughing and shortness of breath. Patients who developed fever were admitted to assess for central line associated blood stream infections and started on iv antibiotics until the blood cultures came back negative after 48 h.

Discussion

This study describes the effect of discontinuation of iron dextran as an additive in HPN for a cohort of pediatric intestinal failure patients. As an initial response to iron dextran discontinuation, enteral supplementation was attempted on half the patients, based on tolerance of enteral feeding and medications. Of these patients, only five (18 %) tolerated enteral supplementation without gastrointestinal adverse effects. However, they demonstrated insufficient biochemical response in iron parameters suggesting inadequate absorption. Of the 56 patients in our cohort, 27 % required one or more infusions during the follow up period. Prior to discontinuation, two patients (0.05 %) required intermittent IV iron infusions due to recurrent GI bleeding secondary to anastomotic ulcers. Individuals in the cohort remained dependent on parenteral nutrition pre and post discontinuation of iron.

In the current study, the key parameter to determine the need for intermittent iron infusion was transferrin saturation. This was because traditional markers such as hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) and ferritin were insensitive in this patient population. Ferritin was especially problematic, since as an acute phase protein it was frequently elevated due to chronic or acute inflammation. NASPGHAN recently published guidelines on evaluation and management of iron deficiency in children undergoing intestinal rehabilitation [8]. However, the guidelines lack detail on the amount of iron to provide during infusions, particularly regarding the prevention of toxicity. In addition, no detail on how to prevent deficiency in patients who do not tolerate enteral supplementation was provided. Therefore, to prevent toxicity, clinical judgement was used; if ferritin level were > 300 μg/L and transferrin saturation low, infusion was not given until the ferritin dropped under 300 μg/L. Of note, transferrin saturation was inconsistently monitored prior to May of 2023 and measurements were only present for a fraction of patients. For these patients with measurements prior to May 2023, transferrin saturation was within normal limits. Decision for timing of iron infusion was based on clinical experience of the multidisciplinary intestinal rehabilitation team, and highlights need for standardized protocol to administer IV iron for prevention of iron deficiency.

Iron is an essential nutrient and iron deficiency remains a particular concern for pediatric patients with IF. Factors including prematurity, inadequate absorption, pediatric feeding disorder, altered motility, duodenal resections, chronic hemorrhage, kidney disease and bacterial overgrowth contribute to the pediatric IF patient’s vulnerability to iron deficiency and subsequent anemia [8]. Consequences of iron deficiency extend beyond hematological manifestations, early iron deficiency in infancy and early toddlerhood has been linked to changes in brain structure and neurofunctional connections [13], [14]. Neurocognitive deficits and lower educational attainment present in children with iron deficiency in infancy extends into adulthood long after correction of deficiency [7], [15]. Prevention as such should remain the ideal treatment for iron deficiency.

The diversity of etiology of intestinal failure necessitates patient specific approaches when selecting the most appropriate iron supplementation route. Enteral absorption of non-heme iron supplementation is estimated to be 10 % in individuals with an intact gut [16], which suggests an even lower bioavailability in patients with IF. The administration of parenteral IV iron supplementation is recommended for those who are unable or have poor tolerance to enteral supplementation [8], [11]. Administration of intermittent iron infusions are associated with concerns relating to infusion reactions as well as on rare occasions anaphylactic reactions [5]. Within the study cohort of those who received intermittent iron infusion, one patient developed hypersensitivity reaction and two fever and rash, other more minor infusion reactions such as flushing, nausea and abdominal pain were reported but poorly documented in electronic medical record. Dosage guidelines for intermittent parenteral iron administration are currently outlined for treatment of ID or correction of IDA [17], [18] with no current recommendations for prevention of deficiency. The highly individualized care needs of patients with IF poses a challenge of administering intermittent parenteral iron with the goal of prevention. At present, uncertainty exists regarding appropriate dosing, and administration frequency when iron deficiency vs treatment is the outcome of interest.

Inclusion of iron in PN is unconventional and there is a paucity of research that evaluates its safety. Concerns regarding peroxidation reactions, incompatibility, hypersensitive reaction, and iron overload risk [19] have been raised. The utilization of trace element admixtures containing iron has been used safely in Europe for several years providing 1.0–1.2 mg/day in PN solutions [20]. Delivery of parenteral iron at doses similar to low bioavailability of enteral iron [16] offers a more balanced approach for preventing iron deficiency. Prior to iron discontinuation, our patient received on average 1.0 mg/day of iron which is in keeping with NASPGHAN [8] and ESPGHAN’s estimated parenteral iron requirements of 50–100 mcg/kg/day [11]. Iron status remained within the normal range prior to discontinuation and no incident of iron overload has been documented in HPN managed at our centre.

The complexity of managing the nutritional needs of IF patients highlight the importance of the involvement and agility of a multidisciplinary team with complementary expertise [21]. Since discontinuation of iDex and the transition to intermittent IV iron infusions, our program has increased frequency of iron status monitoring from every 3–6 months to every 1–3 months. Although not documented in the current study, the increased frequency of clinical visits for laboratory evaluation and the need to attend intermittent infusions, has translated to an increased burden of care, and loss of productivity for patients and families. Families have reported a negative impact on their quality of life.

The limitations of this study are common in many investigations of patients with IF. This was a retrospective study, single center chart review, and was limited to only 56 patients. Incomplete data on certain parameters of iron status for various time points limited more extensive analysis and interpretation. We did not have the volume of patients for robust analysis to determine if there are anatomical subtypes or specific patient characteristics that are more susceptible to iron deficiency or evaluate predictors in those requiring more frequent infusions.

Conclusion

One third of patients on HPN needed at least one intravenous iron infusion to support their iron status within 12 months after discontinuation of iDex in HPN solutions. This data suggests a need to provide iron as part of the PN additives for prevention of iron deficiency. The association between iron deficiency and poor neurocognitive outcomes in children points to an urgent need for guidelines to avoid iron deficiency in children with IF who are dependent on PN and are unable to tolerate or do not respond to enteral iron supplementation. Guidelines addressing dose and frequency in younger children who are at greater risk of iron deficiency during periods of rapid growth are the most urgently needed. In addition, alternative intravenous iron formulations that can be safely added to PN should be explored to prevent the development of iron deficiency.
