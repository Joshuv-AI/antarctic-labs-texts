Advances and perspectives of functional nanomaterials in scavenging reactive oxygen species for acute kidney injury
Authors: Lei Luo, Shujun Lin, Lili Wang, Yu Meng, Mingshan Zhu, Li Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is characterized by a rapid decline in renal function with diverse etiologies, leading to high morbidity and mortality. Its pathophysiology involves the interwoven processes of oxidative stress, inflammation, ferroptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Conventional treatments have issues such as low bioavailability, inadequate renal targeting, and systemic toxicity, leaving clinical needs unmet. Nanotechnology addresses these bottlenecks via rational engineering and surface modification, endowing nanomaterials with enhanced stability, precise targeting, and multifunctional therapeutic potential. Herein, we systematically review reactive oxygen species (ROS)-scavenging nanomaterials for AKI therapy, categorized into inorganic, organic, organic-inorganic hybrid, and biological nanomaterials. The article focuses on the subtype-specific design, targeting strategies, and multi-functional integration principles for different AKI pathological mechanisms, and explores the oxidative stress-mediated toxicity mechanisms of nanomaterials as well as relief strategies based on degradable and surface-modified approaches. Finally, we highlight critical translational challenges and propose pathology-responsive and preclinical evaluation-oriented solutions. This review provides a mechanism-integrated, translation-oriented perspective on ROS-scavenging nanomaterials for AKI therapy, guiding the development of precise, safe, and clinically viable next-generation nanotherapeutics.Keywords: Nanomaterials, Acute kidney injury, Reactive oxygen species, Therapeutic mechanisms, Clinical translation

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is characterized by an abrupt decline in renal function within a short period of time, resulting in impaired glomerular filtration and dysregulation of fluid, electrolyte homeostasis and acid-base balance [1,2]. The global incidence among hospitalized patients is approximately 10-15%, and the incidence exceeds 50% in intensive care units [3], stage 2 and 3 AKI portends a high risk of mortality [4]. Notably, more than 50% of AKI survivors progress to chronic kidney disease (CKD), with a subset requiring lifelong renal replacement therapy (RRT) [5]. Current clinical management of AKI remains predominantly symptomatic and supportive. The lack of effective drug therapies has created a significant therapeutic gap, which is further widened by the complex pathophysiology of AKI.

Nanotechnology has emerged as a transformative approach for AKI therapy through rational material design. Functional nanomaterials overcome key pharmacological barriers by optimizing spatiotemporal drug release, enhancing renal accumulation of therapeutic payloads, and leveraging intrinsic bioactivity as molecular mimics such as nanozymes [6]. Their tunable parameters including size, surface charge, functional groups, and morphology critically influence protein corona formation, cellular uptake, and biodistribution [7,8]. Inorganic nanomaterials offer exceptional catalytic stability and intrinsic reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging for renal protection [9,10]. Organic nanosystems overcome solubility and bioavailability limitations while amplifying anti-inflammatory and antioxidant functions [[11], [12], [13]]. Hybrid nanomaterials combine organic and inorganic properties, enabling synergistic delivery to injured kidneys for free radical scavenging and anti-inflammation [14]. Biomimetic platforms leverage their biological origins to evade immune surveillance, scavenge ROS, and enhance biocompatibility and targeting [[15], [16], [17]]. Mechanistically, nanomaterials counteract AKI pathogenesis by eliminating ROS, inhibiting the inflammatory cascade, suppressing regulated cell death including apoptosis and ferroptosis, and restoring mitochondrial function [9,18,19]. Beyond microenvironment modulation and cytoprotection, they can also function as delivery vehicles for therapeutic agents, improving targeted retention, enabling controlled release, and promoting synergistic effects (Fig. 1) [20,21]. Consequently, leveraging the unique pathophysiology of AKI to tailor nanomaterial functions represents a promising strategy to significantly enhance therapeutic outcomes.

Overview of nanomaterials for AKI treatment. Four types of nanomaterials alleviate ROS-induced AKI through drug loading, microenvironment modulation, and cytoprotective mechanisms.

Previous reviews have provided valuable insights into nanomedicines for AKI from different angles. Li et al. [17] comprehensively summarized renal targeting strategies and ROS scavenging inorganic and organic nanodrugs but did not cover biomimetic or biological nanomaterials. Ba et al. [19] presented an extensive overview of nanozymes, ROS scavengers, MSC-EVs, and drug delivery systems, yet their discussion lacked systematic integration of AKI subtype specific pathological heterogeneity. Chang et al. [16] focused on antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory therapies, but their material classification was primarily strategy-oriented, and they did not provide a dedicated analysis of nanotoxicity mechanisms. Qin et al. [22] highlighted nanocarrier design strategies (liposomes, exosomes) but placed less emphasis on the intrinsic ROS scavenging activities of nanomaterials themselves. Consequently, these prior efforts have not systematically linked the heterogeneous pathological characteristics of different AKI subtypes to material design, nor have they integrated chain effects or synergistic actions among nanomaterial components, and they lack in-depth elaboration on translational considerations such as nanotoxicity mechanisms and clinical applicability.

To complement prior efforts, this review offers three innovative perspectives that confer unique academic and translational value. First, it provides a subtype-specific, mechanism-integrated, and transformation-oriented overview of ROS-scavenging nanomaterials across four key material classifications including inorganic, organic, hybrid, and biological nanomaterials, making up for the underexplored heterogeneity of AKI subtypes. Second, it links the pathological characteristics of the four AKI subtypes to the design of nanomaterials, constructing a three dimensional regulatory network that encompasses extracellular to intracellular regulation, upstream to downstream chain reactions, and multi-component synergistic actions, compensating for the lack of systematic integration in existing reviews. Third, it adds a dedicated chapter on nanomaterial biosafety, the challenges of nanoplatforms in clinical translation, and the key steps and obstacles in bench-to-bedside translation, filling the gap in-depth discussion of toxicity mechanisms and clinical applicability. Collectively, our analysis elucidates how multifunctional mechanisms synergistically achieve kidney protection, and offers theoretical guidance for the next generation of nano-therapies.

Pathogenesis of AKI and traditional treatment methods

Pathogenesis of AKI

Major etiological subtypes of AKI

The pathogenesis of AKI is inextricably linked to its etiology. Diverse initiating insults converge on common pathogenic pathways including oxidative stress, dysregulated inflammation, microvascular injury and tubular cell death [23]. Ischemia/reperfusion-induced AKI (I/R-AKI) is primarily caused by renal ischemia-induced disruption of microcirculation. Ischemia leads to insufficient tissue perfusion and oxygen supply, resulting in damage to mitochondrial structure and respiratory chain in tubular cells and endothelial cells. The reflow of blood triggers a burst of ROS production, further aggravating mitochondrial damage and causing oxidative damage to biomacromolecules including DNA, proteins, and lipids. Meanwhile, it activates the innate immune system, recruiting macrophages and neutrophils and activating NF-κB signaling to promote proinflammatory cytokine release (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), forming a severe inflammatory microenvironment. Drug-induced AKI, is characterized by severe tubulointerstitial damage. Drugs such as cisplatin (CP), antibiotics, and contrast agents directly damage renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) through multiple mechanisms including inhibition of mitochondrial DNA polymerase activity, disruption of mitochondrial structure and antioxidant defense systems, and induction of mitochondrial apoptosis. Concurrently, drug-induced oxidative stress overactivation promotes the release of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β and IL-6, disrupting local immune homeostasis, and aggravating tubulointerstitial inflammation [16]. Additionally, excessive oxidative stress reduces intracellular antioxidant enzyme activities including glutathione (GSH) peroxidase (GPx) and superoxide dismutase (SOD), further aggravating ROS accumulation and promoting tubular cell death [24]. The pathogenesis of infection-induced AKI, particularly sepsis-associated AKI (SA-AKI), centers on the massive release of proinflammatory cytokines induces endothelial cell apoptosis and vascular permeability, leading to reduced effective blood volume, diminished oxygen delivery, and inadequate renal perfusion and oxygenation [25]. Renal hypoxia and ischemia subsequently trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS burst, with excessive ROS further enhancing proinflammatory gene transcription and expression, forming an oxidative stress-inflammatory cascade that perpetuates renal injury [26]. Additionally, sepsis-induced neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) may drive the formation of renal microthrombi, which obstruct renal micro-vessels to exacerbate ischemia and hypoxia, ultimately contributing to renal function decline [27,28]. Rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI (RM-AKI) results from massive myoglobin release from damaged skeletal muscle cells into the blood circulation. Myoglobin induces iron overload in renal tubular cells, where free Fe2+ reacts with H2O2 via the Fenton reaction to generate hydroxyl radicals and other ROS. These free radicals cause severe oxidative stress, trigger ferroptosis of renal tubular cells through lipid peroxidation [29,30]. Oxidative stress also activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting IL-1β release and recruiting inflammatory cells to the injury site, resulting in prominent interstitial inflammation. Ferroptosis is the key regulated cell death mode in RM-AKI pathogenesis, with iron metabolism imbalance and oxidative stress as drivers and subsequent inflammatory response further amplifying tissue damage.

The driving role of ROS in AKI

Under physiological conditions, low levels of ROS act as redox signaling mediators to regulate renal vascular tone, and tubular transport. However, in AKI, the homeostatic balance between ROS generation and scavenging is fundamentally disrupted, establishing ROS as a central pathogenic nexus driving disease progression [2]. In AKI, ROS are predominantly generated by the mitochondrial electron transport chain, plasma membrane NADPH oxidases, and myeloperoxidase released by infiltrating neutrophils [31]. Key ROS species include superoxide anion (·O2−), H2O2, hydroxyl radical (·OH), and the reactive nitrogen species peroxynitrite (ONOO−) [32]. Excess ROS mediates renal tubular injury through multi-pathway cascade reactions. First, it depletes intracellular GSH to inactivate and deplete GPx4, triggering massive lipid peroxidation and induces ferroptosis in RTECs. Ferroptosis, together with apoptosis and pyroptosis, constitutes an ROS-driven programmed cell death network [33]. Second, ROS disrupts mitochondrial structure and function to initiate the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, to which proximal tubular cells are particularly vulnerable due to their exceptionally high mitochondrial density [34]. Third, ROS activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, mediating the maturation and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, inducing pyroptosis and amplifying the inflammatory cascade [35]. Fourth, ROS impairs lysosomal function and inactivates autophagy-related proteins, blocking protective autophagic flux and further exacerbating oxidative damage [31]. Meanwhile, excessive ROS overwhelm the endogenous antioxidant defense system: antioxidant enzymes including SOD, catalase (CAT), and GSH peroxidase are inactivated by massive ROS production, and the Nrf2-mediated endogenous antioxidant compensatory pathway is inhibited [36]. This establishes a vicious cycle of oxidative damage that cannot be reversed by the endogenous system alone, providing a pathological target for ROS-scavenging nanotherapeutic strategies for AKI.

Traditional treatment methods

Current clinical management of AKI primarily relies on symptomatic and supportive care, yet these approaches fail to directly reverse renal tissue damage driven by oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death. Some pharmacological agents have been investigated in preclinical and clinical studies, including antioxidants such as amifostine and N-acetylcysteine, and anti-inflammatory molecules [21,37,38]. These drugs aim to target pathological pathways including ROS scavenging, inflammation inhibition, and anti-apoptosis. However, their clinical application is severely limited by inherent pharmacological drawbacks: poor bioavailability leading to rapid metabolism and clearance, insufficient renal targeting to spatially localized injury sites such as proximal tubules and micro-vessels, and dose-limiting systemic toxicity that causes adverse effects on other organs when therapeutic doses are attempted [[39], [40], [41]]. The unique physicochemical properties of nanomaterials including tunable size, surface functionalization, and nanozyme activity offer precise solutions to these bottlenecks.

Renal targeting strategies for AKI

The etiological heterogeneity of AKI determines the model-specificity of its pathological mechanisms, rendering universal therapeutic applicability impractical. The efficacy of nanomaterial critically depends on the synergistic integration of complementary design dimensions: surface physicochemical properties that govern bio-interfacial interactions, and delivery pathways that exploit the unique pathological features of injured kidneys.

Surface modification strategies

By modifying their surface physicochemical properties or biological recognition sites, Nanoparticles (NPs) enable precise enrichment in injured renal tissues and specific responses to the pathological microenvironment. The surface charge of nanomaterials directly affects their binding ability to serum proteins, circulation time in vivo, and tissue targeting. Negatively charged nanomaterials reduce non-specific binding to negatively charged RTECs membrane via electrostatic repulsion, and avoid excessive serum protein adsorption and reticuloendothelial system clearance, prolonging targeted retention time in injured kidneys [17]. The surface charge of nanomaterials can be precisely controlled by coating with charged polymers, modifying charged functional groups, or adjusting charge balance during material synthesis. Ligand modification utilizes specific molecules highly expressed in AKI damaged kidney tissues such as Kim-1, E-selectin, and nicotinamide receptor 1 (NRK1) as targets. Corresponding ligand molecules are modified onto nanomaterial surfaces through chemical coupling, physical adsorption, or biotin avidin mediated ligation, achieving active targeting via ligand receptor specific binding to enhance enrichment efficiency in damaged kidney tissues while preserving ligand activity and targeting specificity [42,43]. Biomimetic modification leverages the inherent biocompatibility, immune evasion ability, and targeting characteristics of natural biological structures such as cell membranes and protein coronas to coat or modify nanomaterial surfaces, reducing immunogenicity while utilizing natural recognition sites on these biological structures to achieve precise targeting [[15], [16], [17]]. Common strategies include cell membrane coating, protein corona formation, and biomimetic surface construction through physical coating or biomolecular interactions.

Targeted delivery strategies

In I/R-AKI and RM-AKI, passive targeting leverages the pathological characteristics of AKI including impaired glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and increased vascular permeability, combined with the size effect of nanomaterials, to achieve non-specific enrichment in damaged renal tissues. In SA-AKI, systemic vascular dysfunction and marked GFR fluctuations may reduce the reliability of passive targeting, as GFR is a critical determinant of nanoparticle renal clearance [44,45]. In the setting of decreased GFR, particles below 10 nm that are normally filterable may become retained in the circulation, leading to increased hepatic and splenic accumulation. Conversely, when GFR is elevated, such particles may be cleared too quickly to reach effective concentrations in damaged tissues. NPs with a diameter below 10 nm can penetrate the damaged glomerular basement membrane and reach renal tubules, representing the ideal size for renal passive targeting [9]. Larger NPs achieve targeting via receptor-mediated endocytosis or ligand-directed guidance to specific sites [46,47]. NPs with a diameter of approximately 100 nm are enriched through increased vascular permeability at inflammatory sites [48]. Additionally, nanomaterial morphology influences passive targeting efficiency, with rod-shaped or sheet shaped NPs exhibiting higher renal enrichment than spherical counterparts [17]. Active targeting leverages the pathological markers during AKI, such as KIM-1 in CP-AKI, E-selectin in SA-AKI, NRK1 in I/R-AKI, as well as the hyperactivated receptor pathways within the inflammatory microenvironment. Combined with surface functionalization of nanomaterials, this strategy enables specific and precise enrichment in damaged renal tissues and diseased cells. The efficacy of active targeting strategies is influenced by target heterogeneity, protein corona shielding, and endocytosis efficiency [49]. KIM-1 is upregulated in CP-AKI, but its early expression is relatively low, which limits the effectiveness of early intervention [50]. Some ligand receptor complexes are internalized and subsequently degraded in lysosomes, preventing multiple rounds of recognition or sustained targeting [51]. E-selectin targeting is reliable in I/R-AKI due to endothelial activation [52], and NRK1 targeting is reliable in I/R-AKI [42], but their expression in other AKI subtypes needs to be verified.

Future targeting strategies for AKI nanotherapeutics can be designed as microenvironment responsive systems (e.g., pH, ROS) that integrate both passive and active targeting. These strategies may employ multivalent or branched ligands to enhance binding affinity and anti-masking capability, as well as cell membrane biomimetic coatings to reduce non-specific protein adsorption.

Nanomaterial-based therapeutic strategies for AKI

Based on their composition and origin, nanomaterials for AKI therapy are classified into four categories: inorganic nanomaterials, organic nanomaterial, organic-inorganic hybrid nanomaterial, and biological nanomaterials. The drug delivery functions of these materials are discussed within each corresponding category. Each category possesses unique compositional advantages and inherent limitation (Table 1).

Inorganic nanomaterials

Inorganic nanomaterials feature reversible valence transitions, tunable size, and controllable enzyme-mimetic catalytic activity that enables efficient passive renal targeting. They have been validated in addressing the transient ROS burst in I/R-AKI and the Fenton reaction-driven ROS overload in RM-AKI, as well as ferroptosis intervention in CP-AKI and cascade blockade in SA-AKI. Non-metallic, metallic, and metal oxide-based inorganic nanomaterials exert renal protective effects through mechanism-oriented strategies, with SOD, peroxidase (POD), and CAT like activities enabling ROS scavenging, anti-inflammation, DNA repair, mitochondrial protection, and cellular function maintenance (Fig. 2).

Nonmetallic inorganic nanomaterials exhibit intrinsic nanozyme activities, favorable renal biodistribution, with reported efficacy in RM-AKI, CP-AKI and I/R-AKI (Table 2). One-pot pyrolysis-derived carbon dots feature an ultrasmall size, negative surface charge, and abundant oxygen containing groups. They enable size mediated passive renal targeting and directly scavenge excessive ROS in RM-AKI through their surface active groups [71]. However, their preparation temperature must be precisely controlled, with particle size and activity sensitive to temperature variations. Fullerene C60 prepared by solution dispersion features a spherical cage-like structure and directly scavenges cadmium-induced ROS, alleviating DNA strand breaks. However, it also possesses certain genotoxicity at high concentrations or under specific exposure conditions [72]. Black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) prepared via liquid-phase exfoliation possess a layer-packed structure that enables size-mediated passive renal targeting. Their rapid electron transfer capability allows direct scavenging of Fenton reaction-driven ROS bursts in RM-AKI, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy compared with amifostine and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) at equivalent doses. Moreover, after exerting their therapeutic effects, BPNSs degrade into non-toxic phosphorus oxides, avoiding long-term retention toxicity. However, their sensitivity to water, oxygen, and light renders them prone to oxidative degradation [6,53]. Porous Se@SiO2 nanospheres feature a selenium core for activity and a silica shell for sustained release and toxicity reduction. In I/R-AKI, they scavenge ROS and suppress the NF-κB/NLRP3/caspase-1 axis, yet the narrow therapeutic window of selenium means that high concentrations remain hepatorenal toxic [54]. Se-doped carbon quantum dots (SeCQDs) possess an ultrathin structure, abundant surface functional groups, and broad-spectrum antioxidant activity, with size optimization enabling preferential renal accumulation after intravenous administration (Fig. 3a). Intravenous injection of 89Zr‐DFO‐SeCQDs 1 h before CP administration revealed that SeCQDs accumulated rapidly and almost completely in the kidneys (Fig. 3b). SeCQDs significantly reduced serum creatinine (CRE) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels in both RM-AKI and CP-AKI models, demonstrating superior efficacy compared to the amifostine (Fig. 3c and d) [24]. However, they exhibit prolonged renal retention, and chronic toxicity remains to be evaluated. SeNPs administered via prophylactic intragastric gavage in RM-AKI provide antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic protection by increasing GPx and SOD activities, inhibiting TNF-α and IL-1β, and regulating the Bax/Bcl-2/caspase-3 apoptotic pathway. Nevertheless, the narrow therapeutic window of Se and the lack of in-depth understanding of its upstream signaling pathways remain concerns [73].

Metallic nanomaterials feature atomically dispersed metal sites or metal-oxygen clusters with primarily SOD-like activity and reversible valence cycling, with reported efficacy in both I/R-AKI and CP-AKI, including polyoxometalate (POM) nanoclusters, single-atom nanozymes, and noble metal nanosystems. Ultrasmall Mo-based POM nanoclusters, synthesized via a one-pot method, achieve high renal uptake via size-mediated passive targeting, addressing the broad-spectrum ROS burst in both RM-AKI and CP-AKI, demonstrating superior efficacy to NAC at equivalent doses [51]. Their therapeutic efficacy derives from reversible Mo5+/Mo6+ redox cycling-mediated ROS elimination, significantly attenuating glycerol and CP-induced renal injury with excellent biocompatibility [51,74]. Moreover, Mo-based POMs can also upregulate the expression of SOD, POD, and CAT [75]. Tungsten POMs (W-POMs) exhibit exceptional antioxidant potential due to the strong redox activity of their W centers, which feature remarkably low reduction potential in aqueous media [76]. Ultrasmall tungsten POM nanodots (TWNDs), synthesized via alkaline reduction of phosphotungstic acid with tannic acid, achieve a high W5+/W6+ ratio and robust SOD-mimetic activity. Their negative surface charge enables specific mitochondrial targeting, which addresses the pathological feature of mitochondrial respiratory chain damage in RM-AKI and CP-AKI. TWNDs restored mitochondrial function, enhanced mitophagy flux, and attenuated renal injury at a dose as low as 1/32 of the antioxidant NAC, overcoming the high-dose toxicity bottleneck of traditional antioxidants [9,76]. But they are sensitive to water, oxygen, and light, prone to surface oxidation and inactivation. Platinum single-atom enzymes (Pt/SAEs), synthesized via pyrolysis of Pt-coordinated precursors on carbon dots, feature atomically dispersed Pt sites and multiple antioxidant enzyme activities. Pt/SAEs not only mimic SOD and CAT activities, but also exhibit hydroxyl radical scavenging capacity. They exert multimodal renoprotective effects in I/R-AKI by inhibiting lipid peroxidation, suppressing ferroptosis, and blocking M1 macrophage polarization [55]. A study has shown that the catalytic activity of Pt NPs can be modulated by the pH value of the environment. Pt NPs exhibit POD-like activity under acidic conditions, SOD-like activity under neutral conditions, and CAT-like activity under both neutral and alkaline conditions [77]. But it is sensitive to high temperatures, prone to structural inactivation. Other metallic nanozymes, such as gold and silver NPs, similarly alleviate AKI through size-dependent (5-8 nm) renal accumulation and synergistic anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [78,79].

Metal oxides possess reversible valence cycling and multi-enzyme activities, have been validated in RM-AKI, which is characterized by Fenton reaction-driven hydroxyl radical burst. Common examples include CeO2, Mn3O4 and RuO2 NPs. Through reversible Ce3+/Ce4+ valence cycling, CeO2 NPs scavenge ROS and ameliorate RM-AKI (Fig. 3e), demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy compared with amifostine. Citric acid-modified ultrasmall CeO2 achieve preferential renal accumulation via size-mediated passive targeting, directly addressing the pathological links of DNA damage and tubular necrosis in RM-AKI. In vitro, these NPs significantly reduced ROS levels in H2O2-stimulated HEK293T cells (Fig. 3f); in vivo, they markedly decreased 8-OHdG (DNA damage marker) levels, and downregulated serum CRE and BUN in RM-AKI models (Fig. 3g) [10]. Additionally, CeO2 NPs increased the glomerular filtration rate, diminished caspase-3 activation, suppressed renal inflammation and reduced apoptosis in polymicrobial SA-AKI [80]. Notably, CeO2 NPs activate the Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway following systemic administration, increasing endogenous antioxidant defenses while directly scavenging ROS at the physiological pH of the renal parenchyma (pH 7.4). This dual-action mechanism selectively mitigates chemotherapy-induced AKI without compromising the oncolytic efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents [81]. The catalytic activity of CeO2 nanozymes is intrinsically linked to their surface chemistry. The exposed {111} crystal facets tend to exhibit higher SOD mimetic activity under fully reduced conditions (high Ce3+ concentration), whereas they favor higher CAT mimetic activity under fully oxidized conditions (low Ce3+ concentration). Therefore, the proportion of {111} facets directly influences the mimetic efficiency of SOD and CAT, but this effect is strongly dependent on the surface Ce3+/Ce4+ ratio [82]. Unlike natural peroxidases, which possess highly specific catalytic activity, CeO2 nanozymes exhibit broad spectrum ROS scavenging ability through a surface defect mediated electron transfer mechanism, mimicking multiple antioxidant enzymes including SOD, CAT, POD, and oxidase. However, this surface defect driven non-specific mechanism also results in lower substrate specificity compared to their natural counterparts [83]. However, Ceria NPs tend to aggregate, leading to loss of their activity. In I/R-AKI, the Mn3O4 nanosystem scavenges ROS through Mn2+/Mn3+ redox cycling and achieves passive renal targeting via size control to precisely address these pathological requirements [56]. 1-Dodecanethiol-stabilized dMn3O4 encapsulated within ROS-responsive micelles resulted in prolonged circulation, inflammation-triggered drug release, significant oxidative stress suppression, and inhibition of tubular apoptosis in I/R-AKI [84]. However, it remains sensitive to water, oxygen, and light, its preparation process is relatively complex, and its long-term biosafety requires further evaluation. Ultrasmall RuO2 NPs synthesized via solvothermal method are tailored for RM-AKI, addressing the pathological features of mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress, and DNA damage. In RM-AKI models, intravenous administration of these NPs reduced oxidative stress markers, suppressed γ-H2AX expression (DNA damage marker), and decreased tubular necrosis scores, exerting multi-modal renoprotective effects [57]. However, they have a short blood circulation half-life and show non-specific accumulation in the liver, spleen, and lungs.

In addition to having inherent biological activity, inorganic nanomaterials can also function as drug carriers. Ultrasmall NAC-capped gold nanoclusters (Au NCs-NAC), specifically targeted the kidneys with prolonged retention, possessed excellent biocompatibility, and modulated inflammatory responses, alleviating RM-AKI (Fig. 4a). Au NCs-NAC, with a drug loading capacity of 50%, combine the intrinsic broad-spectrum antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities of gold nanoclusters with size-mediated passive renal targeting. In RM-AKI mice, IR800-labeled Au NCs-NAC showed rapid and sustained renal accumulation, with high fluorescence intensity in the kidneys at 2 and 4 h post-injection (Fig. 4b). Compared with free NAC, Au NCs-NAC significantly reduced serum CRE and BUN levels to near-normal ranges at 24 h (Fig. 4c), protecting renal tubular cells via ROS elimination and inflammation suppression, effectively alleviating RM-AKI [78]. However, they remain sensitive to water, oxygen, and light, with potential oxidative degradation. Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) enhances mitochondrial function and inhibits subsequent inflammatory responses; however, its poor delivery efficiency and lack of renal targeting limit its therapeutic application. To address this, the Fe3O4@NMN nanocomplex targets the dual pathological features of renal inflammation and renal anemia-associated mitochondrial dysfunction. The Fe3O4 served as both a delivery carrier and an iron supplementation agent (Fig. 4d), while the loaded NMN, a NAD+ precursor, achieves NRK1-mediated targeted delivery to injured renal cells (Fig. 4e). In I/R-AKI models, this system enabled visualized targeted delivery of NMN to the kidneys, downregulated serum IL-6 levels in AKI mice, exerting anti-inflammatory effects and enhancing mitochondrial function. Concurrently, iron supplementation brought the decreased RBC and HGB levels back to normal, corrected renal anemia, restored glomerular filtration function, and repaired damaged renal structures (Fig. 4f) [42]. While Fe3O4 NPs themselves are relatively stable, the surface modification with NMN may impose constraints on storage stability. By integrating renal targeting advantages with drug therapeutic effects, inorganic nanocarriers significantly improve the bioavailability and kidney specificity of AKI therapeutics, offering an efficient platform for optimizing traditional drug formulations.

In summary, inorganic nanomaterials for AKI therapy can be divided into three major subtypes based on their composition: non-metallic, metallic, and metal oxide nanomaterials. Each subtype exhibits distinct mechanisms of ROS scavenging, unique physicochemical advantages, and optimal applicability to specific AKI etiologies. However, inorganic nanomaterials face limitations in bio-persistence and long-term toxicity. Strategies such as size optimization, degradable design, and negative surface charge have been used to balance efficacy and safety. These strategies have further promoted the development of organic nanomaterials with better biocompatibility and hybrid systems that integrate the strengths of both while reducing their respective drawbacks. For clinical translation of inorganic nanozymes in AKI, biodegradable or easily excretable variants are preferred to prevent long-term retention. Surface engineering to improve catalytic specificity and minimize off-target ROS production, together with scalable Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant manufacturing, is critical.

Organic nanomaterials

Organic nanomaterials, constructed from natural polyphenols or synthetic organic molecules, offer inherent advantages including high biocompatibility during the short-term observation period and versatile surface functionalization. Importantly, nanoscale formulation overcomes the long-standing limitations of conventional organic drugs, including poor bioavailability and inadequate targeting capacity. They integrate active or passive targeting with the ability to scavenge reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), maintain mitochondrial homeostasis, inhibit apoptosis and ferroptosis, and suppress inflammatory cascades (Fig. 5), with reported efficacy in sustained oxidative stress and ferroptosis in CP-AKI, with significant renoprotective effects also verified in I/R-AKI and SA-AKI [85,86].

The composition and functions of organic NPs.(created with figdraw.com).

The clinical translation of natural polyphenols has been severely hindered by poor oral bioavailability, rapid metabolism, and premature renal clearance [87,88]. Dihydromyricetin (DHM) loaded chitosan-tripolyphosphate nanocarriers (CS-DMY-NPs) are designed to overcome the poor solubility and low bioavailability of free DHM, with applications optimized for both CP-AKI and SA-AKI. In CP-AKI models, these nanocarriers activate the Nrf2 pathway, enhance the antioxidant capacity of DHM, suppress proinflammatory cytokine release, and inhibit tubular apoptosis. In SA-AKI, they block the TLR4/NF-κB inflammatory cascade, alleviating renal injury [58,59]. Furthermore, CS-DMY-NPs exhibit good biocompatibility and biodegradability. However, their preparation is sensitive to pH and ionic strength, and high DHM concentrations tend to induce flocculation. For I/R-AKI, which is characterized by specific Kim-1 overexpression on injured proximal tubules, L-serine modification enables selective Kim-1 receptor-mediated active targeting, overcoming the poor renal accumulation of free rosmarinic acid. This strategy is exemplified by rosmarinic acid-loaded, L-serine-modified G4-PAMAM dendrimers (S-G-R NPs), which specifically enrich in injured renal tubules to exert coordinated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects. A series of experiments were conducted to confirm the therapeutic potential of S-G-R in preventing I/R-AKI (Fig. 6a). SOD and malondialdehyde (MDA, lipid peroxidation marker) were regarded as key indicators of oxidative stress damage. In I/R-AKI models, S-G-R NPs significantly enhanced endogenous SOD activity (Fig. 6b), reduced MDA levels(Fig. 6c), and downregulated proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) (Fig. 6d and e), restoring mitochondrial function and reducing tubular necrosis [43]. However, its complex preparation, dependence on Kim-1 expression level, and considerable hepatic non-specific distribution remain challenges. Moreover, the long-term degradation, metabolism, and potential cumulative toxicity of the polymer need further evaluation. A dual-targeted liposomal formulation (Cur-TPP/Que@D-S/TPGS) is rationally designed to address the pathological feature of concurrent tubular injury and mitochondrial dysfunction in CP-AKI, achieving hierarchical targeting from renal tubules to mitochondria. L-serine modification mediates Kim-1 mediated active targeting to injured tubules, while triphenylphosphine (TPP) enables subsequent mitochondrial localization, eliminating both intra-mitochondrial ROS, while Que eliminates extra-mitochondrial ROS in the cytoplasm (Fig. 6f). In CP-AKI models, Western blot analysis revealed that following treatment with the dual-targeting lipid formulation, the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins cleaved caspase-3 and Bax was decreased, while the level of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was significantly increased (Fig. 6g). Additionally, the expression of NF-κB p65 and P-NF-κB p65 was reduced, whereas Nrf2 expression approached normal levels (Fig. 6h). These results indicate that this platform inhibits tubular apoptosis by regulating Bax/Bcl-2 and cleaved caspase-3 expression, blocks the NF-κB inflammatory cascade, and upregulates the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, effectively alleviating CP-induced renal injury [89]. But its preparation process is relatively complex, and its long-term in vivo stability requires further evaluation.

Synthetic organic nanomaterials also demonstrate significant therapeutic potential. Fullerenol, a hydroxylated fullerene derivative, with multimodal renoprotective efficacy tailored to subtype-specific pathologies. In I/R-AKI, it targets reperfusion-induced ROS burst and mitochondrial apoptosis, scavenging pathogenic ROS, preserving mitochondrial membrane potential, and inhibiting cytochrome c release to attenuate renal dysfunction [61]. In CP-AKI, it suppresses CP-induced ferroptosis through three complementary mechanisms: blocking the lipid peroxidation cascade, regulating iron metabolism via ferritinophagy suppression, and maintaining GPx4 activity, preserving renal histoarchitecture and reducing tubular necrosis scores in murine models [90]. Fullerenol exhibits no obvious hemolysis or organ toxicity. Nevertheless, concerns remain regarding its potential long-term retention toxicity in the kidneys, as well as its sensitivity to water, oxygen, and light, which renders it susceptible to oxidative degradation.

Beyond possessing inherent bioactivity, inorganic nanomaterials are also capable of acting as drug carriers. Organic nanocarriers leverage the high biocompatibility under the test dose and flexible modification of organic matrices to deliver traditional anti-AKI drugs, addressing their limitations of poor solubility, low bioavailability, and insufficient renal targeting. SA-NPs were developed for the targeted treatment of I/R-AKI, enabling E-selectin receptor-mediated active targeting to renal vascular endothelial cells, with a drug loading of 4.56% ± 1.82% and an encapsulation efficiency of 66.77% ± 4.13% that enable sustained and acid-sensitive release of dexamethasone, directly scavenging excessive ROS and inhibiting inflammatory, significantly enhancing renal accumulation and therapeutic efficacy [52]. Moreover, SA-NPs prolong the in vivo half-life of the drug and reduce systemic side effects associated with long-term glucocorticoid use. However, the preparation process is relatively complex, and long-term in vivo stability and batch-to-batch consistency require further evaluation. Liposomal encapsulation, demonstrated similar efficacy alleviating I/R-AKI [91]. Edaravone-loaded mesoscale NPs (MNPs), which gradually degrades in vivo and releases the cargo, are designed to overcome the rapid systemic clearance and poor renal accumulation of free edaravone in CP-AKI. MNPs encapsulation optimizes the pharmacokinetics of edaravone, with a drug loading of 0.39%, prolonging circulation time and enabling high renal localization. With intrinsic ROS-scavenging activity, these NPs achieve selective delivery to injured kidneys, eliminating free radicals, reducing RTECs damage and death, and improving renal function in CP-AKI models [92]. However, their renal targeting is highly dependent on precise size and surface chemistry. Silymarin (Sm)-loaded solid lipid NPs are developed to address the limitations of free Sm: poor water solubility, intestinal degradation, and low oral bioavailability. Prepared via thermal homogenization, these NPs significantly improve its bioavailability, and demonstrate superior antioxidant and renoprotective efficacy compared with free Sm in D-galactosamine and TNF-α induced renal injury models [93]. However, their preparation process is relatively complex, and they remain dependent on surfactants and storage conditions. Ultrasmall pH-responsive gallic acid-NAD+ NPs (GA-NAD NPs) were developed to address the poor stability and low renal targeting of free NAD+. The pH-responsive design enables accelerated NAD+ release within the acidic inflammatory microenvironment of injured kidneys, while the ultrasmall size facilitates traversal of the damaged GFR and preferential accumulation in injured tubules. In I/R-AKI models, these NPs mediate renal functional recovery through three complementary mechanisms: modulation of immune homeostasis, preservation of mitochondrial integrity, and activation of the NAD+-Sirtuin-1 axis, exerting coordinated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects. Notably, this platform also exhibits exceptional colloidal stability, biocompatibility, and controlled biodegradability, satisfying key translational criteria [94]. However, its release under normal physiological conditions is relatively slow, and its long-term safety still requires further evaluation.

Organic nanomaterials offer excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and versatile surface functionalization for active targeting, yet they are limited by low catalytic stability and batch variability. To address these issues, they are frequently integrated with inorganic components into hybrid systems, where the inorganic confers sustained ROS scavenging and the organic shell enables targeting and toxicity reduction. Alternatively, organic nanocarriers can serve as delivery vehicles for potent antioxidants or anti-inflammatory agents, harnessing their biocompatibility while avoiding their catalytic drawbacks. The key challenge for organic nanosystems is to enhance in vivo stability while maintaining biodegradability. Future efforts should prioritize responsive organic nanocarriers (e.g., pH, ROS, enzyme) that release payloads solely at the lesion site, and evaluate their long-term safety in models of established renal impairment.

Organic-inorganic hybrid nanomaterials

To address the long-term toxicity and off-target accumulation of inorganic nanomaterials and the poor catalytic stability of organic counterparts, organic-inorganic hybrid nanomaterials have been rationally engineered. Organic-inorganic hybrid nanomaterials integrate the high catalytic activity and enzyme like functionality of inorganic components with the high biocompatibility after optimized surface modification and molecular recognition capabilities of organic materials, addressing the limitations of single material systems including insufficient targeting and single functionality. The design centers on functional synergy and targeted enhancement. The inorganic component executes therapeutic functions such as ROS scavenging and ferroptosis inhibition, while the organic component provides auxiliary capabilities including active renal targeting, stimulus-responsive drug release, and reduction of systemic toxicity. This strategic convergence increased renal targeting efficiency, improved therapeutic outcomes through catalytic cascade reactions, and reduced off-target toxicity via stimuli-responsive drug release kinetics (Fig. 7), which has been validated in addressing the dual pathologies of iron overload and ROS overproduction in RM-AKI, balancing nephroprotection and chemotherapeutic efficacy in CP-AKI, and enabling multi-mechanism intervention in I/R-AKI and SA-AKI.

Hybrid organic-inorganic NPs combine anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties with low toxicity and high targeting efficiency. (created with figdraw.com).

Quercetin (Que)-coordinated Ru-doped platinum (Pt) nanozymes integrate the high catalytic activity of noble metal nanozymes and the lipid peroxidation inhibitory effect of natural polyphenol Que, with amplified antioxidant activity optimized for RM-AKI and CP-AKI. They alleviate renal injury via enhanced broad-spectrum ROS scavenging and tubular apoptosis suppression in both disease models [63]. L-serine functionalized ultrafine rhodium NPs (Rh-Ser) address the pathological triad of renal hypoxia, ROS burst, and M1 macrophage mediated inflammatory storm. L-serine modification enables Kim-1 mediated active renal targeting to prolong renal retention, while the rhodium nanozyme catalytically generates oxygen at inflammatory sites to alleviate hypoxia and polarizes proinflammatory M1 macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, resolving the oxidative stress-inflammatory vicious cycle in RM-AKI, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy over NAC [14]. However, its long-term in vivo metabolic pathway and potential toxicity from rhodium ion release warrant further investigation. Self-assembled FGP nanodots, composed of iron ions, gallic acid, and polyvinylpyrrolidone with a diameter of 1-2 nm, preferentially accumulate in the kidney through size-dependent passive targeting. They mitigate RM-AKI and CP-AKI through broad-spectrum ROS/RNS clearance, achieving superior therapeutic efficacy compared to amifostine, while exhibiting enhanced biocompatibility and biodegradability (Fig. 8a). In both RM-AKI and CP-AKI models, all untreated mice died within 6 days, whereas all mice in the nanotherapy group survived for more than 14 days (Fig. 8b). Hematoxylin-eosin staining of renal tissues provided direct evidence of therapeutic efficacy. In both models, extensive renal damage including tubular injury and cast formation was observed in untreated mice, whereas the nanotherapy group exhibited markedly improved renal histology (Fig. 8c) [30]. However, the long-term safety of their degradation products warrant further investigation. Intravenously injected polyacrylic acid-coated Se-doped carbon dots (SeCDs) mainly accumulates in the kidneys, where it directly scavenges ROS, preserves mitochondrial integrity, and releases Se to facilitate GPX4 expression, counteracting ferroptosis and alleviating CP-AKI (Fig. 8d). HK-2 cells were treated with indicated concentrations of SeCDs for 4 h, or with 6 mg/L SeCDs for various time periods. Western blot analysis revealed that SeCDs increased GPx4 protein levels in a dose and time dependent manner (Fig. 8e). Additionally, SeCDs administration significantly reversed CP-induced loss of mitochondrial cristae (Fig. 8f). Importantly, tumor size and weight were comparable between mice treated with CP alone and those receiving CP combined with SeCDs (Fig. 8g). These findings demonstrate that SeCDs effectively inhibit renal tubular ferroptosis through kidney-specific clearance of ROS/RNS and upregulation of GPx4 expression, without affecting the anticancer effect of CP. This strategy thus resolves the contradiction between nephroprotection and chemotherapeutic efficacy [62]. But their long-term in vivo metabolic products and potential accumulation toxicity of Se warrant further investigation.

Hybrid nanomaterials not only exhibit intrinsic biological activity but can also function as platforms for drug delivery. This design enables flexible loading of therapeutic agents for customized multi-mechanism AKI therapy. Through multi-target modification and responsive design, they achieve precise delivery of therapeutic drugs and multi-mechanism synergistic treatment. Gel-SH and disulfide modified with polyethylene glycol2k (PEG2k) form nanocarriers with a drug loading capacity of 19.1% ± 5.9% to deliver vitamin E to damaged kidney cells, minimizing off-target effects and inhibiting oxidative stress and ferroptosis in I/R-AKI [60]. However, their release kinetics depend on the microenvironmental concentrations of ROS, which may affect the spatiotemporal precision of drug delivery. The Atv/PTP-TCeria NPs nanosystem addresses the pathological features of mitochondrial damage, ROS burst, and systemic inflammatory storm. TPP modification endows ceria NPs with mitochondrial targeting to eliminate the source of ROS overproduction, while the ROS-responsive polymer coating enables on-demand release of the loaded atorvastatin, exerting synergistic anti-inflammatory effects. In SA-AKI models, this system effectively reduces tubular apoptosis and necrosis, and enables significant dose reduction of ceria through the synergistic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, minimizing potential nanomaterial toxicity [95]. However, its release kinetics are highly dependent on the fluctuation of in vivo ROS levels, and the long-term biosafety of ceria NPs still requires further validation.

Hybrid nanomaterials provide synergistic benefits but are limited by complex manufacturing and uncertain degradation product toxicity. Future efforts should yield modular platforms with independently tunable inorganic cores and organic shells, along with standardized degradation assays. Establishing clear structure-activity relationships will enable rational design for specific AKI subtypes.

Biological nanomaterials

Beyond synthetic hybrid systems, biological nanomaterials have emerged as a new generation of platforms that leverage natural structures to achieve superior biocompatibility and targeting precision [96]. Cell membranes, exosomes, and self-assembled biomolecules provide inherent immune evasion and intrinsic targeting capabilities, further reducing the immunogenicity and off-target toxicity associated with synthetic materials. These biological nanomaterials exhibit therapeutic effects against SA-AKI, I/R-AKI and CP-AKI by regulating oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and cell death pathways (Fig. 9).

The composition and functions of biological NPs. Three types of biological nanomaterials target the kidneys and restore cellular function by exerting scavenging ROS, anti-inflammatory effects and repairing DNA and mitochondria (created with figdraw.com).

Cell membrane biomimetic nanomaterials are constructed by coating the surface of inorganic or organic nano-cores such as nanozymes and polymeric NPs with natural cell membranes derived from immune cells including neutrophils and macrophages, RTECs, and other cell types [97,98]. These nanomaterials retain the complete surface protein composition and biological functions of the source cells, perfectly combining the catalytic activity of the nanocore such as ROS scavenging with the biological targeting of the cell membrane, compensating for the therapeutic limitations of single component nanomaterials. The targeting ability of these nanomaterials is based on specific recognition between membrane proteins on source cells and ligands overexpressed in the injured renal microenvironment. Immune cell membranes carry chemokine receptors and adhesion molecules that can specifically bind to inflammatory factors and adhesion proteins in the AKI lesion site [99,100], achieving active targeting to inflammatory regions. Renal tubular epithelial cell membranes modified with kidney targeting peptides can be specifically internalized by damaged renal tubular cells through receptor mediated endocytosis, further improving renal targeting efficiency. In SA-AKI, where the pathological involves systemic inflammatory storm and ROS burst, neutrophil membrane-coated Mn3O4 nanozyme (MD@NM) target the renal inflammatory microenvironment, scavenge ROS and NETs, while DNase-1 degrades NETs-derived extracellular DNA, and inhibit the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, alleviating renal structural damage caused by systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) [26]. Moreover, MD@NM can be cleared via hepatic and renal metabolism after treatment, showing no obvious hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, or hemolysis. However, its therapeutic efficacy depends on the in vivo levels of NETs-DNA and ROS, and the active proteins derived from the neutrophil membrane may risk inactivation during storage. Macrophage membrane modified platinum nanozyme clusters (MM-PtNCs) retained cytokine receptors on the membrane surface enable active targeting the ischemic-reperfusion injury site of the kidney. The macrophage membrane neutralizes proinflammatory cytokines to control inflammatory progression, while ROS-reducible thioacetone linkers prolong renal retention of platinum nanozymes, which scavenge ROS, and inhibit lipid peroxidation and apoptosis, exerting multimodal renal protective effects in I/R-AKI (Fig. 10a). Dihydroethidium (DHE) staining was used to label ROS produced in cells, the saline group exhibited high levels of ROS, while the MM-PtNCs group showed significant reduced ROS levels, indicating that the nanocomposite system effectively reduces ROS levels in damaged tissues. Apoptosis was assessed using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) detection. The saline group showed significant apoptosis in the kidneys of AKI model mice. In contrast, the MM-PtNCs group showed no significant apoptotic signals, suggesting that MM-PtNCs further reduces the proportion of apoptotic cells in kidney tissue (Fig. 10b). To evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of the nanocomposite system, pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in kidney tissues were assessed at different time points post-treatment. In the MM-PtNCs group, the production of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 was significantly suppressed (Fig. 10c). These results indicate that MM-PtNCs promote kidney repair by lowering ROS levels, reducing apoptosis, and attenuating the inflammatory response in damaged kidneys [64]. However, attention should be paid to the optimization of crosslinker ratio and storage stability during preparation. Renal tubular epithelial cell membrane-coated zeolite imidazolate framework-8 NPs modified with kidney-targeting peptides (KMZ@FGF21) possess homologous targeting capability, enabling efficient internalization by injured renal tubular cells. This system prolongs in vivo circulation time, and its FGF21 component inhibits the NF-κB pathway and activates the Nrf2 pathway, exerting antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory effects, ultimately achieving targeted treatment of SA-AKI with excellent biocompatibility [65]. However, optimization of multi-component assembly efficiency is required during preparation, and long-term immunogenicity risks warrant further attention.

Exosomes are natural nanoscale extracellular vesicles with a diameter of 30 to 150 nm, formed by endocytosis and fusion of multivesicular bodies with the cell membrane, and carry a variety of bioactive molecules including RNA, proteins, and lipids [101]. Engineered vesicles are modified based on natural exosomes through strategies such as surface peptide modification and therapeutic drug loading, or artificially constructed by fusing membrane nanovesicles derived from different cell sources. These platforms retain the high biocompatibility and low immunogenicity of natural exosomes while gaining enhanced targeting and therapeutic functions through artificial modification. Natural exosomes achieve tissue and cell targeting through specific recognition between surface membrane proteins such as integrins and tetraspanins and receptor molecules on target cells [102]. Neutrophil membrane engineered hucMSC-sEVs (NEX) are constructed by fusing human neutrophil membrane nanovesicles with hucMSC-derived small extracellular vesicles. This hybrid design integrates the inflammatory site targeting capability of neutrophil membranes and the intrinsic renoprotective activity of hucMSC-sEVs, significantly enhancing damaged renal tissue targeting (Fig. 10d). In CP-AKI models, the same number of DIL-labeled vesicles were administered to AKI model mice, and imaging after 24 h of circulation in vivo showed that the NEX group exhibited more pronounced accumulation in damaged kidney tissue (Fig. 10e). NEX promoted renal cell proliferation, and inhibited oxidative stress (Fig. 10f), alleviating CP-induced renal tubular epithelial cell damage [66]. However, their fusion preparation efficiency is highly dependent on sonication or extrusion parameters, and long-term storage stability requires further optimization. Functionally modified fibroblast like cell derived exosomes selectively bind to renal tubular cells, promote mitochondrial autophagy, inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation and subsequent cell pyroptosis, improving renal function and increasing survival rate in SA-AKI mice [67]. Mesenchymal stem cell derived exosomes carry anti-inflammatory and antioxidant microRNAs and proteins that target injured kidneys, regulate renal tissue inflammatory responses, restore mitochondrial function, and promote repair of damaged renal tubular cells, effectively improving renal function [103].

Biological molecule self-assembled nanomaterials are nanostructures formed by natural biological molecules such as proteins, peptides, and nucleic acids or their modified derivatives through non-covalent interactions. They possess excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, facilitating the design of personalized treatment systems for different AKI pathologies. This self-assembling carrier efficiently delivers p53 siRNA to renal tubular tissue, silencing the p53 gene and reducing CP induced renal tubular cell apoptosis without significant hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity [104]. In I/R-AKI, platelet mediated p53 siRNA delivery system specifically accumulates in mouse kidneys, significantly downregulates p53 protein expression, inhibits renal tubular cell apoptosis. In SA-AKI, platelets respond to sepsis induced renal inflammatory signals and actively home to the injury site, delivering therapeutic siRNAs including p53 siRNA and TLR9 siRNA to damaged renal tubular cells while suppressing the systemic inflammatory response through downregulation of proinflammatory factors such as Cxcl2, and IL1β, avoiding off-target effects [69]. Chemokine receptor CXCR4 antagonist modified self-assembling materials designated PCX specifically bind to renal tubular cells and immune cells with high CXCR4 expression in AKI models, significantly enhancing renal enrichment efficiency. The PCX modified siRNA self-assembling polymer reduces CP induced renal tubular damage by simultaneously inhibiting CXCR4 and silencing p53 expression. Moreover, it alleviates renal tissue inflammatory injury in SA-AKI by inhibiting inflammatory cell infiltration [70]. However, the renal targeting efficiency may be affected by infiltrating CXCR4-positive immune cells in the early injury phase, leading to potential non-specific uptake. miR-500a-3P loaded lipid NPs self-assemble into nanomaterials that reduce CP induced renal tubular inflammation and cell death by targeting and inhibiting the RIPK3 MLKL mediated necroptosis pathway [105]. Notably, TAT-MKK3b NPs designated TMNPs self-assemble through fusion of the p38 kinase functional domain MKK3b with the cell penetrating sequence TAT followed by tyrosine enzyme oxidation. These NPs form a protein corona by binding to plasma albumin and achieve specific targeting of renal tubular cells through endocytosis mediated by highly expressed large protein receptors on proximal RTECs. By inhibiting p38 phosphorylation, they block downstream pathological signaling pathways while activating the SLC7A11/GPx4 axis to inhibit ferroptosis, improving renal function in I/R-AKI [106].

Apart from their own biological activity, biological nanomaterials can be utilized as drug carriers as well. M2 macrophage membrane coated dexamethasone loaded polydopamine NPs with an encapsulation efficiency of 93.2% were developed to address the systemic immunosuppression side effects of free glucocorticoids. The M2 macrophage membrane enables inflammatory site targeting via CD44 mediated recognition, improving biocompatibility and renal accumulation. The polydopamine scavenges ROS, while the loaded dexamethasone modulates renal immune cell phenotypes. Together, this system reduces inflammatory cell infiltration and ameliorates SA-AKI while minimizing the systemic side effects of free dexamethasone [107]. However, the preparation involves complex membrane extraction and coating procedures, long-term stability and scalable production remain to be improved, and membrane proteins may elicit individualized immune responses. Peptide-based therapies face several limitations, including conformational instability, proteolytic degradation, rapid metabolism, and poor bioavailability, which compromise the therapeutic efficacy of the mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant peptide SS31 [108]. Indocyanine Green (ICG), a near-infrared fluorescent dye widely used in imaging diagnostics, suffers from poor aqueous stability, rapid systemic clearance, and low cellular uptake. Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) offers favorable biocompatibility and a controllable degradation rate. To address these challenges, a platelet membrane-coated nanocomposite encapsulating ICG and SS31 within a PLGA (PISP) was developed. This platform enables active targeted delivery to injured kidneys. ICG serves as a diagnostic tracer for near-infrared fluorescence imaging of AKI, while SS31 exerts its mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant effects in RM-AKI. PISP nanosystem not only facilitates real-time imaging of AKI but also effectively scavenges mitochondrial ROS, significantly alleviating renal injury, all with favorable biocompatibility [109]. However, the preparation involves complex platelet membrane extraction and ultrasound coating steps, batch-to-batch consistency and long-term storage stability require further optimization, and inter-individual variability in platelet membrane proteins may affect targeting efficiency. Biological nanomaterials offer natural targeting and low immunogenicity, making them highly promising for AKI. Advancing GMP-compliant production and thorough immunogenicity assessment in humanized animal models will be essential to accelerate clinical translation.

Four types of nanomaterials achieve ROS clearance, ferroptosis inhibition, anti-inflammation and mitochondrial protection through different mechanism strategies (Table 4). This systematically clarifies the structural-functional-treatment effect relationship of ROS-clearing nanomaterials on AKI (Table 5). It should be noted that validation of nanomaterials in multiple AKI models, especially I/R-AKI and CP-AKI, does not guarantee equivalent efficacy across etiologies due to divergent pathophysiology. Future studies should select models based on the material's mechanism, not convenience, and clearly distinguish the primary applicable subtype when multiple models are tested.

The safety of nanomaterials

Notably, nanomaterials exert a bidirectional regulatory effect on ROS homeostasis, which is highly dependent on the microenvironment, dose, and exposure duration. In the high- ROS pathological setting of AKI, they predominantly scavenge ROS to block pathogenic cascades, whereas under normal physiological conditions, excessive or prolonged retention may trigger aberrant ROS generation and oxidative damage. This bidirectional effect underscores the critical importance of precise dose control, targeted delivery, and biodegradable material engineering for maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing toxicity. Therefore, the therapeutic and toxic effects of nanomaterials in AKI are not absolute opposites but rather different manifestations of the same material under distinct microenvironments, doses, and exposure durations. Through rational design of size, surface charge, degradability, and dosage, it is possible to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing toxicity risks.

Toxicity and mechanisms of nanomaterials

Despite the promising therapeutic prospects of nanomaterials in AKI management and numerous studies demonstrating favorable biocompatibility, comprehensive safety assessments remain imperative, as the primary prerequisite for safeguarding patient health. The toxic manifestations and mechanisms of nanomaterials are highly dependent on the physicochemical properties, with kidney specific toxicity constituting the toxicological concern. In vitro studies have documented that carbon based nanomaterials including carbon black, carbon nanotubes, and fullerenol, as well as metal based nanomaterials such as cerium, gold, iron, and zinc, can directly induce renal cell damage. This damage is characterized by reduced cell viability, oxidative stress activation, impairment of mitochondrial and cytoskeletal functions, and DNA damage [[111], [112], [113], [114], [115], [116], [117]]. In vivo experiments have further confirmed that these nanomaterials can trigger glomerular swelling, renal tubular epithelial cell necrosis, and renal interstitial fibrosis, accompanied by renal function abnormalities including elevated blood urea nitrogen and creatinine levels, as well as decreased antioxidant enzyme activity [114,[116], [117], [118]]. Additionally, certain nanomaterials may exert systemic toxic effects such as genotoxicity (Table 6) [119]. Notably, long-term biocompatibility data for nanomaterials remain scarce, with the majority of studies relying on in vitro cellular models and murine experiments, while evidence from higher mammals and human clinical cohorts is still lacking. Key factors modulating nanomaterial toxicity include particle size, surface charge, and surface modification. Smaller NPs exhibit broader in vivo distribution, enhanced renal penetration capacity, and more pronounced toxic effects [117,120]. Negative surface charge can mitigate serum protein adsorption, prevent particle aggregation, reduce off-target accumulation such as hepatic enrichment, and significantly improve biocompatibility [121,122]. Surface modifications with substances such as albumin can attenuate immunogenicity and cytotoxicity, optimizing biosafety profiles [123]. The toxicity mechanism of nanomaterials is centered on oxidative stress, which mediates kidney injury through five interconnected pathways: Amplification of oxidative stress, the high specific surface area of nanomaterials increases the exposure of surface active sites, which facilitates ROS generation, triggering lipid peroxidation and oxidative cell death [90]. Activation of inflammatory cascades, nanostructures activate macrophages and neutrophils, promoting sustained release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines to form a persistent inflammatory microenvironment. Mitochondrial damage, mitochondria are preferentially targeted, resulting in disrupted membrane potential and structural integrity, ultimately inducing apoptosis [93]. Genomic instability, direct interactions between nanomaterials and DNA cause double strand breaks, chromosomal aberrations, and mutations. Lysosomal dysfunction and autophagy imbalance, nanomaterials internalized by renal cells are preferentially enriched in lysosomes, which can impair lysosomal acidification and inactivate autophagy-related proteins, thereby blocking the protective autophagic flux, further aggravating oxidative damage and cell death [124]. These toxicity mechanisms constitute the fundamental basis for the biosafety design of AKI-targeted nanotherapeutics, and their toxic manifestations are further regulated by the unique pathophysiological microenvironment of AKI.

Notably, the long-term safety characteristics vary fundamentally among nanomaterial classes. For non-degradable inorganic nanozymes like CeO2, the main risk is bio-persistence: retained particles in the kidneys or liver may steadily produce low levels of ROS, potentially causing chronic inflammation, interstitial fibrosis, or granuloma formation [120]. For degradable organic nanomaterials such as liposomes, the carrier itself is typically cleared within days to weeks. However, in the setting of AKI induced renal clearance impairment, accumulated metabolites or degradation byproducts may exert unexpected toxicity [93]. For organic-inorganic hybrid nanomaterials, the risk stems from the persistence of the inorganic combined with the degradation intermediates of the organic shell [62]. For biological nanomaterials such as cell membranes, immunogenicity and the potential risk of pathogen transmission are the primary long-term concerns, whereas their degradation products typically exhibit good biocompatibility [100]. These differences mean that material selection should be tailored to the clinical scenario. For situations requiring repeated administration, rapidly biodegradable materials are preferred. For single use acute interventions, persistent inorganic nanozymes are acceptable provided rapid clearance can be ensured.

Biodistribution and clearance mechanisms

The in vivo biodistribution and clearance of nanomaterials directly influence renal accumulation efficiency and off-target toxicity, with physicochemical properties serving as the regulatory factors. NPs with a size below 10 nm can traverse the damaged glomerular basement membrane to achieve renal enrichment and are rapidly excreted via glomerular filtration, rendering them an ideal size for AKI treatment that balances therapeutic enrichment and safety. Larger NPs are prone to macrophage phagocytosis, primarily accumulating in the liver and spleen, which increases off-target toxicity risk [125,126]. Surface coatings such as polyethylene glycol and charge modifications can prolong circulation half-life, reduce hepatic accumulation, and such nanomaterials are predominantly cleared by the spleen following prolonged retention [127]. However, it is known that PEG may induce anti-PEG antibodies, leading to the clearance of such NPs and rendering subsequent administration ineffective. For nanomaterials designed for AKI treatment, the ideal clearance pathway involves renal enrichment to exert therapeutic effects followed by excretion through glomerular filtration, thereby avoiding long-term accumulation in renal tissue or other organs. This represents a key design principle for minimizing nephrotoxicity.

Toxicity mitigation strategies

Mitigating nanomaterial toxicity is essential for clinical translation of AKI nanotherapeutics, with the goal of preserving renal-targeted efficacy while minimizing off-target damage and long-term retention. Based on the toxicity mechanisms and AKI pathophysiology outlined above, three strategies are established. First, biodegradable material engineering serves as the foundational strategy to eliminate cumulative toxicity from long-term tissue retention. This approach focuses on constructing stimuli-responsive, renal-clearable biodegradable nanostructures that degrade into non-toxic metabolites after exerting ROS-scavenging effects in the injured kidney. As discussed in Section 4.2, PEG-encapsulated liposomes can be used to deliver prednisolone in I/R-AKI. However, PEG may elicit anti-PEG antibodies, leading to clearance of these NPs and making subsequent administration ineffective. Substituting PEG with degradable and low-immunogenic materials offers a promising strategy to circumvent anti-PEG antibody-mediated clearance and preserve therapeutic efficacy. Second, biosafety-oriented surface modification mitigates immunogenicity and non-specific organ accumulation through three complementary strategies. Negative charge optimization reduces serum protein adsorption and reticuloendothelial system clearance. Stealth coatings such as albumin modification minimize immune phagocytosis. Biomimetic cell membrane coating enables immune evasion while avoiding hypersensitivity reactions associated with synthetic ligands. For instance, biomimetic cell membrane coating and low-immunogenicity PEG derivatives help reduce anti-PEG immune responses and nanoparticle clearance, enhancing the efficacy of PEG-encapsulated liposomes for prednisolone delivery. Third, precise physicochemical property tuning balances therapeutic efficacy and safety. The 10 nm size is adopted to achieve optimal renal accumulation and rapid post-treatment excretion, minimize lysosomal damage and reduce the risk of chronic inflammation, and valence state engineering of nanozymes is used to enhance selective ROS scavenging while inhibiting pro-oxidant activity that could cause non-specific renal cell damage. For instance, to address the issue of PEG antibodies, using PEG chains with a molecular weight below 5 kDa or employing short-chain PEGylation, or utilizing polyzwitterions can reduce antibody recognition while still providing a certain degree of steric stability. For patients requiring repeated administration, anti-PEG antibody levels can be screened in advance. Future designs should prioritize biodegradable or non-immunogenic stealth materials.

Balancing therapeutic efficacy and nanotoxicity

The apparent paradox that nanomaterials designed to rescue injured kidneys may themselves carry nephrotoxic risk arises from their microenvironment dependent dual regulation of ROS homeostasis [128]. In the high ROS pathological environment of AKI, abundant surface active sites preferentially scavenge ROS via electron transfer or valence cycling, exerting therapeutic effects. However, under normal physiological conditions or at off-target sites, the same surface reactivity may, in the absence of sufficient endogenous ROS substrates, paradoxically promote aberrant ROS generation [129]. Furthermore, prolonged tissue retention of non-degradable particles may overwhelm local antioxidant defenses and trigger chronic inflammation [130]. To balance therapeutic efficacy against toxicity, four design parameters are critical: (i) Size optimization, particles below 10 nm are rapidly filtered and excreted via the kidneys, reducing long-term accumulation, while particles in the 100-400 nm range can passively target via leaky vasculature but risk retention in the liver and spleen [45]. (ii) Surface charge engineering, negatively charged surfaces reduce serum protein adsorption and aggregation, decrease reticuloendothelial system uptake, and consequently improve biocompatibility [131]. (iii) Biodegradability, stimuli-responsive materials degrade into non-toxic metabolites after completing their therapeutic function, eliminating chronic toxicity risks [132]. (iv) Dose minimization, Use the minimal effective dose determined by in vivo pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies to minimize off-target exposure [78].

Challenges for clinical translation

Despite encouraging preclinical achievements of nanotherapeutics in AKI models, the transition from laboratory to clinical application still faces multi-dimensional and systematic obstacles. First, the high heterogeneity of AKI etiology and the mismatch of nanomaterial targeting efficiency. Significant individual heterogeneity exists in renal pathological status among clinical AKI patients, making it difficult for laboratory optimized targeting strategies to achieve stable accumulation in clinical settings. For instance, Kim-1 expression is low in early stage CP-AKI patients, resulting in limited active targeting efficiency. In severe SA-AKI patients, systemic inflammatory responses can disrupt surface modifications of nanomaterials, further reducing renal targeting specificity [15,88]. Second, a standardized evaluation system for the long-term biological safety of nanomaterials has yet to be established. Most preclinical studies employ healthy animals or single etiology AKI models, which differ substantially from the complex clinical reality where AKI often occurs secondary to infections, tumors, and cardiovascular diseases. This discrepancy makes the feasibility of applying nanomaterials in complex patient populations highly challenging, and toxicity evaluation results difficult to extrapolate directly to humans [133]. Certain nanomaterials such as metal oxides and carbon based materials exhibit slow biodegradation, and their long-term retention may induce renal interstitial fibrosis and chronic inflammation. Additionally, the highly heterogeneous immune status of clinical patients may trigger unexpected immune responses such as complement activation and allergic reactions, which receive little attention in preclinical studies. Third, the scalable production and quality control of these materials suffer from significant bottlenecks. Laboratory scale preparation of nanomaterials mostly relies on small batch and refined operations, which can hardly meet the large scale production requirements for clinical application. Key physicochemical properties including particle size, surface charge, and drug loading are sensitive to preparation conditions, making batch differences prone to occur during scale up and potentially compromising therapeutic efficacy and safety. Most nanomaterials are susceptible to oxidation and degradation, necessitating special storage conditions that increase the difficulty of clinical translation. Furthermore, multifunctional nanomaterials including hybrid nanocarriers and biomimetic systems involve complex preparation processes and high raw material costs, posing major obstacles to their widespread clinical adoption. Moreover, the design of clinical trials for AKI presents unique challenges, as the pathological mechanisms vary significantly across different etiologies, thus necessitating targeted subgroup analyses. Existing regulatory frameworks, such as the FDA and EMA guidelines for nanomedicines, lack specificity for AKI scenarios [134].

Future research directions and transformation prospects

Regarding the research progress and translational bottlenecks in the field of AKI nanotherapy, future research should move beyond the current approaches of single-material optimization, single-target modification, and single-etiology model validation, and focus on achieving key breakthroughs in three dimensions, adaptability to real-world clinical scenarios, full-process safety assurance, and industrialization accessibility. First, clinically adaptive precision therapeutic approaches aligned with the inherent heterogeneity of AKI must be established. Most current AKI studies use single-etiology model, which fail to capture the features of clinical AKI, including overlapping etiologies, multimorbidity, variable baseline renal function, and biomarker heterogeneity. To overcome the disconnect, we advocate for a standardized database that connects subtype-specific pathological features, material properties, and targeting strategies to establish evidence-based design principles for etiology-specific nanotherapeutics. Concurrently, there is a critical need to develop biomarker-guided individualized nanotherapy regimens, to overcome key clinical barriers including low biomarker expression in early AKI and degradation of targeting moieties driven by the systemic inflammatory milieu in severe AKI, ultimately bridging the gap between preclinical efficacy and real-world patient outcomes. Second, a full-life-cycle biosafety evaluation framework specific to AKI must be established. Existing safety assessments are largely confined to short-term cytotoxicity and single-dose in vivo toxicity studies in healthy animals, with a striking lack of long-term, systematic data in models that reflect the impaired renal function inherent to AKI. The metabolism, tissue accumulation, and degradation kinetics of nanomaterials are fundamentally altered in patients with renal insufficiency, yet this clinical reality remains largely unaddressed in preclinical research. Future work must transition from static, endpoint-only toxicity testing to dynamic, full-life-cycle safety evaluation. This includes prioritizing the development of biodegradable nanomaterials that exert their therapeutic effects within the kidney, followed by rapid renal clearance to eliminate long-term tissue accumulation at the source. In parallel, we must implement a tiered safety evaluation pipeline aligned with clinical AKI, spanning patient-derived kidney organoids, humanized animal models of AKI with comorbidities and multi-organ dysfunction, and dose-escalation studies in early-phase clinical trials. This framework will enable rigorous characterization of nanomaterial pharmacokinetics, long-term accumulation risk, and immunogenicity in the setting of renal impairment, alongside the establishment of consensus safe dosage standards and risk mitigation protocols for AKI nanotherapeutics. Third, the end-to-end industrial translation pipeline and AKI-specific clinical trial design must be standardized. To date, AKI nanotherapy research has remained largely restricted to laboratory-scale synthesis and small animal model validation, with critical gaps in scalable manufacturing processes, robust quality control standards, and clinically compliant study designs, all of which represent fundamental bottlenecks to clinical translation. Future efforts must bridge the full translational continuum from basic research to preclinical development and clinical validation. On the manufacturing side, this requires establishing scalable, GMP-compatible preparation processes and standardized quality control system for the characterization of nanomaterials, developing AKI-specific targeting ligands (e.g., KIM-1, E-selectin, NRK1) to resolve batch-to-batch consistency between laboratory-scale synthesis and clinical-grade scale-up, alongside optimized storage and distribution workflows to reduce translational costs. On the clinical side, we must develop AKI-specific clinical research norms: given the acute onset, etiological heterogeneity, and highly variable prognosis of AKI, etiology-stratified prospective cohort studies with rapid enrollment mechanisms should be prioritized, with a composite efficacy endpoint integrating renal function recovery, long-term renal outcomes, and AKI-to-CKD progression rate, rather than overreliance on isolated biochemical markers. Complemented by standardized clinical in vivo monitoring protocols for nanomaterial biodistribution and safety, this framework will provide high-level evidence-based support for regulatory approval and clinical adoption.

Emerging microenvironment responsive systems (pH, ROS, or enzyme sensitive) enable precise drug release at the injury site with minimal off-target toxicity, representing a promising direction for AKI therapy [94,95]. Key challenges include variable signal levels across AKI patients, stability of responsive linkages in vivo, and potential immunogenicity. Future work should focus on tunable sensitivity, dual-responsive platforms, and validation in organoid or humanized models to accelerate clinical translation. Currently, clinical management of AKI remains largely supportive. No specific pharmacotherapy has been approved for AKI to date. Given these limitations, combination therapy strategies have emerged as another promising direction. Hierarchical targeted antioxidant nanodrugs (HAND) incorporate folic acid groups, allowing sequential targeting from the kidneys to injured proximal tubular epithelial cells, and subsequently to mitochondria and the nucleus, where they achieve in situ ROS scavenging and suppression of cGAS/STING-mediated sterile inflammation [135]. Chitosan-encapsulated aloe vera NPs (AVE-CSNPs) were developed to boost the therapeutic efficacy of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in amikacin-induced AKI. The MSCs plus AVE-CSNPs combination was superior to MSCs alone or with unencapsulated aloe vera extract in restoring renal function and normalizing oxidative stress and inflammatory markers [136]. SC@Se/GA NPs integrate anti-inflammatory gallic acid and antioxidant Se into a kidney-targeted platform. Gallic acid enhances the stability and renal targeting of Se NPs, while Se mitigates the toxicity of gallic acid. This system boosts GPx activity and prolongs renal retention, thereby synergistically alleviating CP-AKI [110]. These combination strategies highlight the ability of rationally designed nanoplatforms to overcome monotherapy limitations. Future work should explore further synergies, including integrating nanomaterials with CRRT or gene therapy, and developing multi-responsive systems for spatiotemporally controlled release of multiple payloads.

In conclusion, the transformative potential of AKI nanotherapy lies in addressing the longstanding clinical gap of disease-modifying, etiology-specific treatments for AKI. Future research must move beyond incremental development of novel materials and structures, and instead center on unmet clinical needs to resolve the bottlenecks of targeting efficiency, biosafety, and scalable translation. With this clinically focused approach, ROS-scavenging nanotherapeutics hold immense promise to deliver tangible benefit to patients with AKI of diverse etiologies, and ultimately redefine the standard of care for this high-mortality syndrome.
