Introduction

Keratoplasty is a surgical procedure which involves replacing the cornea with corneal tissue from a donor to restore vision. Keratoplasty is among the most successful solid organ transplants due to the immune-privilege, avascularity, and lack of lymphatics of the cornea [1]. Despite this favorable environment, graft rejection is the leading cause of corneal allograft failure [2]. The incidence of rejection is greatest in the first 10 months post-transplant, but has been reported to occur more than 20 years after a primary transplantation [3]. In low-risk settings, 2-year graft-survival rates exceed 90 % with graft survival rates of 90 % and 82 % at 5 years and 10 years, respectively, post-keratoplasty [3]. In recipients with high-risk factors for graft rejection, the graft survival rate decreases to between 35 % and 70 %, respectively [1,3]. The most well-established risk factors for corneal allograft rejection include the presence of stromal blood vessels in one or more quadrants of the recipient cornea, post-transplant corneal neovascularization, history of graft rejection, preoperative glaucoma, young age, prior anterior segment surgery, active ocular inflammation, ocular surface disease, herpes simplex keratitis, neurotrophic keratopathy, large and eccentric grafts, and anterior synechiae [3].

The goal of treatment for corneal graft rejection is to reverse the rejection episode at the earliest possible stage, minimize donor endothelial cell loss, and preserve function of the graft [1]. Prompt recognition followed by aggressive steroid therapy are key in the treatment of graft rejection, with the application of topical steroids being the mainstay of treatment in the management of acute graft rejection [3]. Topical steroids are also the primary treatment for postoperative prophylaxis for high-risk keratoplasty recipients [3]. Given the unfavorable safety profiles of prolonged corticosteroid use in keratoplasty, alternative treatment options are being explored [3].

Repository corticotropin injection (RCI; Acthar® Gel) is a naturally sourced complex mixture of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) analogs and other pituitary peptides [4]. Although this anti-inflammatory drug has been reported to have steroidogenic effects, RCI is thought to work differently from corticosteroids through its interaction with melanocortin receptors (MCRs) [4], expressed on immune cells and other tissues in the central nervous system and eye [5]. Studies have demonstrated substantially lower levels of cortisol production with RCI compared with synthetic ACTH1–24 depot in healthy subjects [6,7]. RCI has been approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for >70 years to treat several disease states involving noninfectious inflammation, including rheumatic, collagen, dermatologic, allergic state, ophthalmic, respiratory, and edematous disorders [4,8]. Specific approved indications of RCI include severe acute and chronic allergic and inflammatory processes of the eye, such as keratitis, iritis, iridocyclitis, diffuse posterior uveitis and choroiditis, optic neuritis, chorioretinitis, and anterior segment inflammation [4].

While RCI is not currently FDA-approved for use in corneal graft rejection prophylaxis, it is approved for use in keratitis, an inflammation of the cornea which can lead to corneal damage and loss of vision in severe cases [4]. Keratitis and its sequelae are often indications for keratoplasty [9]. A Phase 4 study in patients with treatment-resistant, refractory, severe, noninfectious keratitis demonstrated that treatment with 80 units of RCI subcutaneously twice weekly for 12 weeks was associated with rapid and sustained improvements in clinical and symptomatic assessments of keratitis [10]. The results of this consecutive, observational descriptive case report also showed that RCI was safe and well tolerated [10]. Given its proven safety and efficacy profile in patients with severe noninfectious keratitis, it was hypothesized that RCI would aid in preservation of the corneal graft in high-risk patients.

Materials and methods

High risk patients (keratoplasty failure ≥1 time [graft rejection], unstable milieux [chemical burns, Stevens-Johnson, etc., or ≥1 quadrant of deep stromal neovascularization]) who underwent keratoplasty were study eligible. Prior to beginning treatment with RCI, patient consent was obtained. Patients, who were enrolled consecutively, were administered 80 units of RCI subcutaneously twice weekly beginning 1 month prior to keratoplasty. Treatment with RCI continued twice weekly post-keratoplasty and was tapered to 40 units twice weekly, as appropriate. All patients received concurrent topical ophthalmic steroids, starting at 1 drop every 2 h and tapering slowly to twice daily, based on clarity and graft stability. In addition, all patients were administered topical ciprofloxacin for 4 weeks, lubricant eye drops every 2 h while awake, and 5 % sodium chloride hypertonicity ophthalmic ointment once daily. Intraocular pressure (IOP), graft integrity, and VA values were collected at each visit postoperatively (ranging from 0.5 to 3 years). All patients currently remain on 40 units of RCI twice weekly under the surveillance of Ophthalmology in conjunction with Endocrinology and Primary Care, who assist in treating comorbidities and monitoring for side effects.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS Guideline [11].

Results

Demographics and results from this consecutive, observational case report for the 8 patients included in this study are summarized in Table 1. Enrolled patients ranged in age from 35 to 89 years (mean 60 years) and 62.5 % were male. All cases were considered high-risk with severe ophthalmic pathologies and multiple comorbidities, including glaucoma/glaucoma surgery, retinal detachment, wet age-related macular degeneration, and the need for superficial keratectomy. Preoperative diagnoses for the keratoplasty included infections, corneal scarring, neovascularization, prior graft rejection, keratoconus, and corneal ulcer.

The majority of patients (87.5 %) receiving RCI demonstrated maintenance or improvement of VA post-keratoplasty as well as postoperative IOPs that fell within the normal range. Upon postoperative examination, the majority of patients (75 %) treated with RCI maintained a clear graft quality with no signs of rejection. One patient experienced graft failure and another patient's graft appeared edematous with 2 infiltrates. No treatment-emergent adverse effects were reported by the patients during treatment with RCI.

Discussion

Previous investigations of RCI have demonstrated the agent to be effective and well tolerated in the treatment of refractory severe noninfectious keratitis that has not improved with other approved therapies [10]. In addition to the improvements demonstrated in the Phase 4 study of RCI treatment in patients with noninfectious keratitis, as discussed in the introduction, RCI therapy has also shown ophthalmic improvements in patients with dry eye disease, optic neuritis, uveitis, and retinal vasculitis [8,10]. The results of the current study in high-risk, post-keratoplasty patients demonstrated maintenance or improvement in VA in the majority of patients treated prophylactically with RCI. The corneal graft quality remained clear in the majority of patients during their time on RCI therapy.

RCI is a naturally sourced complex mixture of ACTH analogs and other pituitary peptides that is thought to modulate the immune response by activating MCRs, inhibiting production of inflammatory cytokines, and minimizing IL-4/CD40L-stimulated activation and IgG production in human B cells [4,[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Through its interaction with MCRs, RCI is thought to produce an indirect and direct cell modulation effect [5,[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Furthermore, RCI is thought to produce an indirect anti-inflammatory effect through its engagement with MCR2 on the adrenal cortex, secreting free cortisol at levels slightly above endogenous range [6].

Topical steroids are the primary treatment for graft rejection as well as for postoperative prophylactic therapy for high-risk keratoplasty recipients [3]. However, these steroids have an unfavorable safety profile, especially with long-term use, and adverse effects may include cataract formations and ocular hypertension, possibly leading to glaucoma and vision loss [17]. A retrospective study of 211 patients without history of glaucoma who underwent keratoplasty assessed long-term topical steroid (prednisolone acetate 1 %) use for prevention of graft rejection [18]. The steroid was applied 4 times daily for 4 months and tapered to once daily [18]. The study demonstrated that at 1, 5, and 10 years, the cumulative risks of steroid-induced ocular hypertension were 29 %, 41 %, and 49 %, respectively, and the risks of requiring glaucoma treatment were 11 %, 17 %, and 25 %, respectively [18]. While the risk of steroid-induced ocular hypertension was greatest in the first year of treatment, patients developed ocular hypertension as late as 13 years postoperatively [18]. These results aligned with the reported rates of steroid-induced ocular hypertension treated with intravitreal corticosteroids in both patients with retinal vein occlusion [19] and diabetic macular edema [20]. This significant morbidity associated with long-term steroid use makes it reasonable to explore other treatment possibilities for corneal graft rejection prophylaxis, such as RCI.

Limitations of this present study investigating the use of RCI in the prophylaxis of corneal graft rejection included a small sample size, variable treatment durations, and lack of placebo comparator. However, given the level and complexity of ophthalmic disease in these high-risk patients, the majority of patients considered their corneal grafts to be successful through maintenance or improvement of some degree of functional vision. The results of this study also showed that RCI was safe and well tolerated. These findings are consistent with the known safety profile of RCI in other ophthalmic uses [4,8,10]. In the future, a randomized, placebo-controlled clinical study with longer treatment duration would be valuable to provide additional information on the efficacy of RCI as prophylaxis for corneal graft rejection.
