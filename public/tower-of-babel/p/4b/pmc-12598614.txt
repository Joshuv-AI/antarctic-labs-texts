Hospitalisations for bronchiectasis compared to COPD and asthma in the USA
Authors: Mohamad El Labban, Alwatheq Al-Itelat, Timothy R. Aksamit, Sarah J. Chalmers, Amjad N. Kanj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveUnderstanding how bronchiectasis hospitalisations differ from those of COPD and asthma could guide clinical practice and improve care. This study aims to compare outcomes of hospitalisations for bronchiectasis exacerbation with those for COPD and asthma exacerbations.MethodsThis study used a US all-payer database to evaluate hospitalisations for bronchiectasis, COPD and asthma exacerbations from January 2017 to December 2021. Demographic data, hospital characteristics, comorbidities, costs, length of stay and mortality were analysed. Associations between the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic and hospitalisation incidence and mortality were also assessed. A secondary analysis examined outcomes in bronchiectasis exacerbation with overlapping airway diseases.ResultsThe bronchiectasis cohort (n=232 825) had a median age of 72 years (IQR: 57–81); 59% were female and 73% were white. Compared to COPD and asthma, hospitalisations for bronchiectasis had longer median stays (5 versus 4 versus 3 days) and higher median costs (USD 50 393 versus USD 38 040 versus USD 31 262). After adjusting for age, year of hospitalisation and comorbidity burden, hospitalisations for bronchiectasis were 1.2 times more likely to result in death than for COPD, and 3.0 times more likely than for asthma (p<0.0001). During the pandemic years (2020–2021), hospitalisations for bronchiectasis declined by only 8%, compared to 26% for COPD and 28% for asthma, while mortality increased across all cohorts. Overlap of bronchiectasis exacerbation with COPD or asthma did not result in higher mortality than bronchiectasis alone.ConclusionsHospitalisations for bronchiectasis in the USA are associated with worse outcomes, including higher mortality, costs and length of stay, compared to those for COPD and asthma exacerbations.

Introduction

Bronchiectasis is an inflammatory airway disease characterised by permanent dilatation of the bronchi, often leading to frequent exacerbations, impaired lung function and reduced quality of life [1]. Bronchiectasis can be associated with various conditions, including COPD and asthma [2]. While bronchiectasis has garnered increased attention due to its rising prevalence and significant socioeconomic burden, awareness of the disease may not be as widespread as that of more common airway diseases [3, 4].

Despite the growing recognition of bronchiectasis as a distinct clinical entity, there remains a gap in understanding how the patterns and outcomes of hospitalisations for bronchiectasis exacerbation compare to those for acute exacerbation of common airway diseases, specifically COPD and asthma. This knowledge is important for informing clinical practice and guiding healthcare policies, in the hope of improving treatment outcomes for patients with bronchiectasis.

This study leverages a national all-payer database to address this gap by comparing hospitalisations for bronchiectasis exacerbation with those for COPD and asthma exacerbations. It examines patient and hospital characteristics, healthcare utilisation measures and mortality. The hypothesis is that hospitalisations for bronchiectasis exacerbation have distinct characteristics and are associated with higher costs, length of stay and mortality rates compared to those for COPD and asthma exacerbations. Since the 2019 SARS-CoV-2 pandemic occurred during the second half of the study period, its association with the incidence and outcomes of these hospitalisations across the three airway diseases is also examined.

Methods

We utilised the National Inpatient Sample (NIS), a publicly accessible all-payer database that comprises a stratified sample of ∼20% of inpatient hospital discharges across the USA. Sampling weights provided with the database were applied to raw counts to generate national estimates [5]. This study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board.

Adult patients (≥18 years old) hospitalised between 2017 and 2021 with a principal or secondary discharge diagnosis of bronchiectasis, COPD or asthma exacerbations were included (figure 1). Diagnoses were identified using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes, as detailed in supplementary table S1. To focus the analysis on bronchiectasis exacerbations (J47.0, J47.1), hospitalised patients with bronchiectasis exacerbation who also had a diagnosis of COPD or asthma exacerbation were categorised exclusively within the bronchiectasis cohort. Supplementary table S2 displays the most common principal diagnoses in each of the three cohorts.

Hospitalisations for bronchiectasis, COPD and asthma exacerbations in US adults. Patients with bronchiectasis who also have overlapping airway diseases (COPD and/or asthma) were included exclusively in the bronchiectasis cohort. A secondary analysis was performed in the bronchiectasis exacerbation cohort to assess differences in hospitalisations between those with bronchiectasis alone and those with overlapping COPD (bronchiectasis–COPD) and asthma (bronchiectasis–asthma). The ICD-10 codes used to define all cohorts remained unchanged throughout the study period (2017–2021). #: does not include hospitalisations with a bronchiectasis diagnosis.

Baseline demographics, including insurance payers, median household income quartiles and hospital characteristics (region, bed size, teaching status), were extracted directly from the database. Associated comorbidities, such as obstructive sleep apnoea and malnutrition, as well as established etiologies of bronchiectasis, such as α1 antitrypsin (A1AT) deficiency and cystic fibrosis (CF), were identified using ICD-10-CM codes. Ventilator use was identified using ICD-10 procedures (ICD-10-PCS) codes, also listed in supplementary table S1. Hospital costs, length of stay and mortality data were available within the database.

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA BE Version 17.0. The bronchiectasis cohort was compared separately to the COPD and asthma cohorts. A secondary analysis within the bronchiectasis cohort examined outcomes in patients with and without overlapping airway disease exacerbations (bronchiectasis-only, bronchiectasis–COPD overlap, bronchiectasis–asthma overlap). The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare non-normally distributed continuous variables (age, hospital costs and length of stay), reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Pearson's Chi-Square test was used for categorical variables, with odds ratios provided when informative. A multivariable logistic regression model, in accordance with recommended practices [6], was used to adjust hospital mortality for confounders selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature, irrespective of statistical significance. These included age, year of hospitalisation and comorbidities (measured by the Charlson Comorbidity Index). Age and comorbidities have been established as strong independent predictors of outcomes across airway diseases [7–9]. Additionally, given the impact of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, year of hospitalisation was included as an additional factor in the analysis. The odds ratio from the logistic regression model approximates the relative risk given the expected frequency of the outcome.

Results

Demographic and socioeconomic characteristics

The bronchiectasis cohort (n=232 825) had a median age of 72 years (IQR: 57–81) and consisted of 59% females, while the COPD cohort (n=8 403 302) had a median age of 70 years (IQR: 61–78) with 53.3% females, and the asthma cohort (n=1 278 839) had a median age of 56 years (IQR: 42–69) with 71% females. Most patients in the bronchiectasis cohort identified as white (73.2%), which is comparable to the COPD cohort (79.4%) but higher than the asthma cohort (51.9%).

Medicare usage was 72.0% in bronchiectasis, 74.0% in COPD and 43.2% in asthma. The largest proportion of patients in the COPD (37.4%) and asthma (35.7%) cohorts had a median household income in the lowest quartile, while the bronchiectasis cohort showed a more varied distribution, with the largest group (26.1%) falling into the highest median household income quartile.

The bronchiectasis cohort had the highest representation in large bed-size hospitals (52.1%) and urban teaching hospitals (75.1%), compared to the COPD cohort (43.6% and 60.5%, respectively) and the asthma cohort (43.8% and 70.6%, respectively). Complete results are detailed in table 1.

Clinical characteristics and comorbidities

Chronic cardiometabolic conditions, such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic heart failure and chronic kidney disease, were most prevalent in the COPD cohort. A diagnosis of COPD was frequent in both the bronchiectasis (20.1%) and asthma (31.1%) cohorts. Obesity was most common in the asthma cohort (32.0%) and significantly lower in the bronchiectasis cohort (7.6%). Conversely, malnutrition was highly prevalent in bronchiectasis (20.3%) compared to 9.4% in COPD and only 2.6% in asthma.

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) was prevalent across all cohorts, with the highest occurrence in the bronchiectasis cohort (31.4%). Other comorbidities associated with the development and progression of bronchiectasis were more common in the bronchiectasis cohort, including CF (6.0%), common variable immunodeficiency (1.1%) and A1AT deficiency (0.5%). Hospitalisation outcomes and mortality

The median (IQR) hospital length of stay was highest in the bronchiectasis cohort at 5 days [3–9] and lowest in the asthma cohort at 3 days [2–5]. Hospital costs were also highest in the bronchiectasis cohort, with a median (IQR) of USD 50 393 (28 432–96 242) per hospitalisation. Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) was preferred over invasive ventilation in both the COPD (11.8% versus 9.1%) and asthma (10% versus 5.9%) cohorts, whereas the use of these two modalities was equally prevalent in the bronchiectasis cohort (8.2% versus 8.1%). Hospitalisation outcomes and mortality results are presented in figure 2 and supplementary table S3.

Mortality rate, ventilator use, length of hospital stays and hospitalisation costs among the three cohorts of airway disease exacerbations: bronchiectasis, COPD and asthma. NIV: noninvasive ventilation; IMV: invasive mechanical ventilation.

Crude mortality was highest in bronchiectasis (5.8%), followed by COPD (5.0%, p<0.0001) and asthma cohorts (1.5%, p<0.0001). After adjusting for age, year of hospitalisation and burden of comorbid conditions as measured by the Charlson Comorbidity Index, patients hospitalised for bronchiectasis exacerbation were 1.2 times more likely to die compared to those with COPD (p<0.0001), and 3.0 times more likely compared to those with asthma exacerbations (p<0.0001) (table 3). To also account for potential confounding by nutritional status, an additional model including malnutrition as a covariate was performed. Results remained consistent and are presented in supplementary table S3.

Hospitalisation and mortality trends

The rate of hospitalisations for COPD and asthma exacerbations decreased by 26% and 28%, respectively, during the pandemic years (2020–21) compared to pre-pandemic levels, while the decrease was only 8% for bronchiectasis exacerbations (figure 3a). Although hospitalisations continued to decrease in 2021 for both COPD and asthma exacerbations, they increased from 42 374 in 2020 to 45 866 in 2021 for bronchiectasis exacerbations, approaching pre-pandemic levels (supplementary table S5).

Trends in a) hospitalisation and b) mortality rates for bronchiectasis, COPD and asthma exacerbations from 2017 to 2021. The dashed line indicates the onset of the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. Hospitalisation rates decreased in all three cohorts in 2020 but later increased in the bronchiectasis cohort, while they continued to decline in the COPD and asthma exacerbation cohorts. On the other hand, mortality rates continued to increase across all three cohorts in 2020 and 2021.

As for mortality rates, these were relatively stable over the pre-pandemic years but significantly increased in 2020 and continued to rise in 2021 across all three cohorts. This change was most striking in the bronchiectasis cohort, where the mortality rate increased from ∼4.3% pre-pandemic to 7.3% in 2020 and further to 9.3% in 2021 (figure 3b, supplementary table S5).

Impact of COPD, asthma exacerbations and nutritional status on bronchiectasis exacerbations

In a secondary analysis of the bronchiectasis cohort (n=232 825), we examined the association of a concomitant COPD or asthma exacerbation diagnosis on hospitalisation outcomes and mortality. Hospitalisations with ICD-10-CM codes representing all three airway diseases simultaneously – bronchiectasis exacerbation, COPD exacerbation and asthma exacerbation – were excluded from the analysis (n=1995, 0.9%). We found that hospitalisations for bronchiectasis alone (bronchiectasis-only, n=174 600) were associated with lower noninvasive but higher invasive ventilation use, and higher mortality rates compared to bronchiectasis–COPD (n=45 000) and bronchiectasis–asthma (n=11 230) overlap (figure 4, supplementary table S6). Specifically, those with bronchiectasis-only were more than three times more likely to die compared to those with bronchiectasis–asthma overlap (table 4).

Since malnutrition was documented in one-fifth of hospitalisations for bronchiectasis exacerbation, we also examined its association with outcomes in this cohort. Discussion

The introduction of ICD-10 codes in the previous decade has allowed for a more granular comparison of exacerbations of bronchiectasis with those of other common airway diseases, such as COPD and asthma. Despite advancements in understanding and managing bronchiectasis exacerbations, we found that hospitalisations for this condition in the USA are associated with longer hospital stays, higher healthcare costs and increased mortality rates compared to those for COPD and asthma exacerbations.

From 2017 to the end of 2021 in the USA, there were 36 times more hospitalisations for COPD and five times more for asthma exacerbations than for bronchiectasis exacerbations. The bronchiectasis cohort in our study was predominantly elderly (≥65 years), with a slight female majority, and most patients were white. The baseline demographics of this cohort more closely resembled those of the COPD cohort and were significantly different from the asthma cohort, which consisted of younger patients and had a higher proportion of Hispanic and African American individuals.

Patients hospitalised for bronchiectasis exacerbation demonstrated a distinct socioeconomic and healthcare utilisation profile, with a greater proportion in the higher income brackets compared to those with COPD and asthma exacerbations. While this might suggest that bronchiectasis exacerbations disproportionately affect more affluent individuals, it is more likely indicative of their greater access to diagnostic services, leading to more accurate diagnoses that may not be as accessible to lower-income groups. This interpretation is further supported by the higher tendency of our bronchiectasis cohort to be treated in large bed-size and urban teaching hospitals, which underscores a reliance on specialised, more resource-intensive care that may often be necessary for managing the complex nature of this disease.

The complexity of bronchiectasis is further highlighted by the examination of underlying comorbidities. As anticipated, we found a higher prevalence of rare etiologies of bronchiectasis, such as CF, common variable immunodeficiency, inflammatory bowel diseases and primary ciliary dyskinesia, in the bronchiectasis exacerbation cohort. OSA and other cardiovascular comorbidities were prevalent across all cohorts. GORD was also prevalent across all cohorts, but more so in bronchiectasis exacerbation, further highlighting the complex interrelationship between this disease and bronchiectasis [10].

Malnutrition was present in a fifth of the bronchiectasis exacerbation cohort, a significantly higher prevalence than in the other cohorts. Nutritional status has been consistently linked to outcomes in chronic respiratory diseases such as COPD where undernutrition has been linked to worse muscle weakness and overall prognosis [11, 12]. In bronchiectasis, a study using weight as a surrogate for nutrition found that underweight patients had lower lung function, though this did not correlate with other disease severity markers like exacerbation frequency or hospitalisation rates [13]. However, in the work deriving the Bronchiectasis Severity Index, patients with a body mass index (BMI) <18.5 kg·m−2 had an increased hospitalisation rate and a 31% 4-year mortality rate, compared to 7–11% for those with greater BMIs, indicating weight as an important prognostic factor in these patients [14]. Notably, while BMI in bronchiectasis patients often falls within the normal to overweight range, it can conceal deficits in functional capacity, body composition and nutritional status [15], and patients with a normal or elevated BMI can still have malnutrition. In a subgroup analysis of our bronchiectasis cohort, those with malnutrition had twice the mortality rate of those without.

A systematic review of the economic burden of bronchiectasis found that annual healthcare expenditures for adult patients with bronchiectasis were predominantly driven by hospitalisations [16]. Our analysis revealed that hospitalisations for bronchiectasis exacerbations incurred higher costs than those for COPD and asthma exacerbations. In a study comparing 891 patients with bronchiectasis to matched controls with asthma and COPD from the Lombardy healthcare utilisation databases, the mean costs of hospitalisations related to a respiratory diagnosis were EUR 635±3017 for bronchiectasis, falling between the costs for asthma (EUR 222±1294) and COPD (EUR 1468±4415) [17]. A likely driver of the higher costs in our study was the length of stay, which has been shown to be influenced by factors such as the number of exacerbations per year and Pseudomonas aeruginosa pneumonia [18]. Longer hospital stays in turn can lead to indirect costs such as lost income because of illness [16].

NIV is a key strategy in the management of both hypoxaemic and hypercapnic respiratory failure across various conditions [19]. However, unlike with COPD and asthma exacerbations, we found no preference for NIV over invasive ventilation in the bronchiectasis exacerbation cohort. This may be due to the association of bronchiectasis with an increased burden of respiratory secretions, which is often cited as a relative contraindication for NIV [20]. In addition, a study of critically ill bronchiectasis patients in Singapore found that one-third failed NIV, with the severity of hypoxaemia being the main predictor of this failure [21]. Nonetheless, further assessment of NIV's role in the management of bronchiectasis exacerbations is needed, including trials comparing it to invasive mechanical ventilation [21]. The use of respiratory support, including both invasive ventilation and NIV, may not have been applied consistently across patients due to variations in clinical practices and potential biases in treatment indications.

Mortality rates were highest in the bronchiectasis cohort (5.8%), slightly surpassing those in the COPD cohort (5.0%). After adjusting for age, year of hospitalisation and comorbidities, the odds of dying in the bronchiectasis cohort were 1.2 times greater than in the COPD cohort. Data on hospital mortality specific to bronchiectasis exacerbation is scarce, especially when compared to other airway disease exacerbations. A single-centre study conducted in the USA between 2003 and 2006 (n=43) found a 9% in-hospital mortality rate among bronchiectasis exacerbation patients. In intensive care settings, other studies found even higher mortality rates of 19% and 34%, respectively [22, 23]. In contrast, hospital mortality for COPD exacerbations has generally ranged from 6.5% to 8% [24, 25], while mortality from asthma exacerbations is typically below 2% [26, 27].

European studies have underscored the impact of other airway diseases on bronchiectasis outcomes. Diel et al. [28] reported significantly higher mortality in patients with COPD and bronchiectasis compared to those with bronchiectasis alone (35.9% versus 14.6%). Another study noted a nearly threefold increase in 5-year mortality for patients with both bronchiectasis and COPD (55%) compared to those with bronchiectasis alone (20%) [29]. There is limited information on bronchiectasis and asthma overlap [30], though some evidence suggests worse respiratory symptoms and lung function for these patients [31]. Notably, our cohort showed higher mortality in patients with bronchiectasis alone compared to those with overlapping airway diseases. It is possible that these patients with bronchiectasis and COPD or asthma exhibit a better response to specific treatments (e.g., glucocorticoids, bronchodilators, etc.) and ventilator strategies (e.g., NIV, increased expiratory time, etc.) targeting the COPD or asthma components, which may offer additional benefits compared to those with bronchiectasis alone.

The SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, which impacted the last 2 years of our study, significantly influenced hospitalisation and mortality trends across our three cohorts. We observed that the pandemic's effect on bronchiectasis hospitalisations was similar to COPD and asthma exacerbations during the first year but differed in the second year. The initial decrease in hospitalisations across all three cohorts is consistent with findings from other studies [32, 33] and is thought to be attributed to changes in healthcare utilisation and reduced exposure to viruses and other environmental triggers [32]. However, the subsequent rise in bronchiectasis exacerbation hospitalisations in the second year of the pandemic suggests that these effects may be less sustained in bronchiectasis patients and that there could be other factors contributing to bronchiectasis such as underlying respiratory microbiology and their heightened risk for exacerbations, even in the absence of environmental exposures and viral triggers. This aligns with the proposed “adapted island model”, which highlights how both exogenous (e.g., infections, pollution) and endogenous (e.g., immune dysregulation, comorbidities) factors can disrupt airway homeostasis in bronchiectasis, triggering exacerbations [34]. The pandemic's impact on mortality trends was similarly profound across all cohorts, and despite the rise in bronchiectasis exacerbation hospitalisations, mortality rates also increased. This surge in mortality post-2019 could be related to SARS-CoV-2 infections, as well as changes in healthcare utilisation. However, due to the limitations of the database, it would be challenging to separate these factors.

Several limitations of this study are also inherent to the features of the database used. The NIS database identifies hospital encounters rather than individual patients, which may lead to the over-representation of patients with recurrent hospitalisations across cohorts. Furthermore, the database relies on administrative codes, where variability in coding practices may affect data accuracy. This is particularly relevant since a higher percentage of the bronchiectasis cohort was treated at large teaching hospitals, where coding practices may differ from those in rural or smaller centres. Similarly, variations in billing practices across facilities could also introduce discrepancies in the hospitalisation cost data. Finally, while the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic may have introduced significant bias by disproportionately affecting certain groups or cohorts, we attempted to partly mitigate this by conducting a secondary analysis to assess changes in major hospitalisation outcomes with the pandemic.

Conclusions

This study underscores the distinct clinical and demographic characteristics, increased healthcare resource utilisation patterns and worse outcomes of hospitalisations for bronchiectasis exacerbations compared to those of COPD and asthma exacerbations. The findings highlight the need for more targeted management strategies and increased awareness of bronchiectasis to improve patient outcomes and further reduce healthcare burdens.
