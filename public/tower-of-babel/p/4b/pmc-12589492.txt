Hydrogel nanocomposite-based breathable non-adhesive coating for oxygen sensing film used within extracorporeal perfusion system
Authors: Badri Parshad, Xingyu Hu, Isabelle Nagle, Shatruhan Singh Rajput, Fei Peng, Emmanouil Roussakis, Juan Pedro Cascales, Tyler McPartland, Anna Wiatrowski, Julia Byrne Slade, Trong Nguyen, Rachel S Knipe, Mark W Grinstaff, Conor L Evans
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReal-time, quantitative biosensing within extracorporeal perfusion (ECP) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) systems is key to advancing this life-support technology beyond the hospital setting to emergency care. Measuring and monitoring concentration in blood is particularly important but is challenged by fouling of the sensor. Here we report a novel hydrogel nanocomposite coating, composed of polysulfobetaine, polyethylene glycol, and titanium dioxide nanoparticles, for a metalloporphyrin-based oxygen sensor. The hydrogel nanocomposite exhibits minimal cytotoxicity and hemolytic effects (< 5%), as well as non-adhesive and non-coagulant properties in contact with lung endothelial cells and human plasma, ensuring its compatibility with blood-contacting applications. The incorporation of titanium dioxide nanoparticles provides a white, light-scattering surface to improve sensor signal reflection, enhances the hydrogel’s mechanical strength (storage modulus > 1000 Pa), and maintains a pore area on the order of μm2 to facilitate efficient fluid and gas diffusion to the oxygen sensing film. Notably, the hydrogel nanocomposite enables rapid, accurate (within 2 mmHg of the analytical reference), and continuous monitoring of oxygen partial pressures () in PBS and blood for over 90 minutes within a miniaturized flow cell integrated in an extracorporeal perfusion-mimicking system. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a blood-compatible biomaterial that enables real-time monitoring in a lightweight, compact sensor, suitable for integration into ECP and ECMO circuits.Subject terms: Materials chemistry, Photochemistry

Introduction

Extracorporeal perfusion (ECP) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) systems are essential life-support technologies that temporarily take over the functions of the cardiopulmonary system to maintain circulation, promote vascular integrity, and preserve organ viability1,2. Widely used in surgery and organ transplantation procedures, ECP and ECMO systems facilitate temporary organ bypass by enabling the preservation and reconditioning of organs during transport from donor to recipient. The applications of ECP and ECMO are expanding significantly in emergency medical settings, addressing life-threatening complications such as cardiac arrest, cardiac failure, and respiratory failure3,4. By providing a continuous oxygen supply to organs and tissues, these systems prevent hypoxic or ischemic injury and support patient survival in critical conditions.

Continuous oxygenation monitoring within the circulating fluid is critical for ECP and ECMO systems to assure patient safety, prevent complications, and guide clinical decisions, particularly during ECMO weaning5. In particular, measurement of blood oxygenation parameters ensures sufficient oxygen delivery to tissues and organs, while avoiding oxygen toxicity from excessively high oxygenation levels. Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)-based tools are commonly employed for this purpose; however, they measure only the percentage of oxygen bound to hemoglobin, and are limited by a 100% measurement ceiling. Therefore, the direct measurement of dissolved oxygen concentration, or , is crucial. Unlike NIRS-based tools that measure oxygen saturation, or (the percentage of hemoglobing binding sites that carry oxygen molecules), the method relying on phosphorescence quenching directly measures oxygen tension within blood or tissues and can, in theory, measure up to a concentration of 100% molecular oxygen (corresponding to 760 mmHg under 1 Atm). We have, in fact, recently demonstrated that our method could measure up to nearly 760 mmHg within a transportable device designed to preserve donor kidneys under hypothermic oxygenated machine perfusion6. Prior literature studies have indicated that 100% in healthy subjects corresponds to a range of 100–120 mmHg arterial blood partial oxygen pressure7, representing a measurement ceiling for the NIRS techniques. This can be a severe limitation in critical care scenarios, for example in mechanically ventilated patients in the ICU, as oxygen delivery to arterial oxygen partial pressure levels beyond 120 mmHg through ventilation has been known to cause moderate or severe hyperoxemia that may be associated with higher mortality rates8. It is, therefore, of high importance that in certain clinical scenarios the partial pressure of oxygen delivered in the blood through ventilation is continuously monitored and managed to avoid hyperoxemia9. Current standard-of-care ECP and ECMO devices utilize electrochemical sensors integrated within the systems, but these sensors are relatively large, have limited sensitivity, are prone to fragility, and are affected by variations in temperature and pH10. Additionally, existing ECP and ECMO systems are typically large, cart-based devices designed for use in critical care units within hospitals or other definitive care settings. Consequently, there is considerable interest in extending ECP and ECMO technologies beyond these care units into ambulatory or even field use for lifesaving interventions, but the size and complexity of these systems remain significant barriers. Furthermore, to enable use outside of specialized, well-monitored healthcare settings, ECMO and ECP systems will also require methods for rapid and reliable, closed-loop feedback mechanisms.

Optochemical sensors based on phosphorescent materials offer significant potential to improve oxygenation monitoring in both current ECMO and ECP systems, as well as in the development of future miniaturized and automated lifesaving devices. These sensors utilize phosphorescence quenching, a process in which the lifetime and intensity of a phosphorescent material is inversely proportional to oxygen tension11. Such optochemical sensors are advantageous as they: 1) enable real-time and continuous measurement of oxygen levels; 2) can be readily miniaturized12; 3) are easily integrated into flow systems13; and 4) provide more rapid and sensitive readouts of oxygenation levels compared to conventional electrochemical methods11. Our team has worked for more than a decade on the design of highly sensitive sensors and integrated them into various form factors for diverse applications, including visible films and dressings14–18, bandages19,20, implantable and catheter-based devices21,22, and wearable devices23–25. These materials and devices have been successfully translated to multiple clinical studies11,18,25.

A key challenge in developing optochemical sensing technologies for ECP and ECMO is the interaction of artificial surfaces with biological components, such as blood. These interactions often trigger pathophysiological responses, including thrombosis26, postperfusion lung syndrome (i.e. acute respiratory distress syndrome)27, systemic inflammatory response syndrome28, sepsis29,30, and even organ failure31. To avoid such adverse effects, various biocompatible materials are engineered as endothelial surface-mimicking coatings to prevent clotting cascade activation, minimize immune rejection, lower the risk of infection, and enhance the durability and performance of blood-contacting devices32–36.

Of the various materials, hydrogels are highly suited due to their excellent biocompatibility, extensive surface area, high porosity, and tunable mechanical and chemical properties37–39. Their surfaces are well suited for modifications and bioconjugation, while their internal porous networks are ideal for embedding small molecules, proteins, polymers, and nanoparticles40–43. Hydrogel nanocomposites often exhibit superior properties and tailored functionalities, including enhanced mechanical strength, stimuli responsive behavior, and expanded bioactivity resulting from the interactions between the nanoparticles and polymeric chains44–46.

Hydrogels composed of antifouling polymers are extensively studied as coatings for blood-contacting devices, including ECMO systems, to prevent thrombus formation and reduce infection47–49. These coatings incorporate natural polymers, such as heparin, alginate and gelatin, as well as synthetic hydrophilic and/or zwitterionic polymers, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), polysulfobetaine, and polycarboxybetaine38,47–52. A combination of these materials is often used to balance anticoagulant efficacy with mechanical strength51. Additionally, PEG- or poly(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) (pHEMA)-based hydrogels embedded with oxygen-sensing metalloporphyrins and fluorophores enable real-time monitoring of oxygenation in subcutaneous tissue53–56. Therefore, hydrogels are highly promising for coating oxygen sensing films in ECP and ECMO systems, providing anticoagulant properties, good oxygen permeability, chemical stability, and biocompatibility.

Herein, we describe a miniaturized optical phosphorescent oxygen sensing unit featuring a hydrogel titanium dioxide () nanocomposite coating on a metalloporphyrin-based film, designed for continuous in-line oxygen measurement. Physical, mechanical and optical evaluations reveal a structurally intact hydrogel nanocomposite that prevents leaching of oxygen sensing film and avoids unwanted interactions with the perfusate, while providing real-time, quantitative monitoring of levels in artificially oxygenated blood via the oxygen-sensing platinum porphyrin, highlighting its effectiveness in complex biological fluids.

Results and discussion

Design criteria for hydrogel nanocomposite coating

To guide our studies, we first established a set of design criteria for the hydrogel coating : 1) mechanical strength and chemical stability to prevent sensor film leaching and undesired reactions with biological components; 2) biocompatibility and hemocompatibility to minimize toxicity and ensure safe integration with biological systems; 3) high breathability to allow the free diffusion of oxygen and other blood gases to the sensing film; 4) antifouling and anticoagulant properties to prevent blood clot formation and accumulation of biological components on its surface; and 5) optical opacity to improve sensor detection by scattering the sensor molecules’ luminescence back to the readout electronics.

To achieve these design requirements, we utilized UV-photoinitiated covalent crosslinking chemistry between a sulfate group-containing zwitterionic acrylate monomer and branched, acrylamide-bearing crosslinkers. Next, we embedded titanium dioxide nanoparticles ( NPs) into the hydrogel network to produce a white, light-scattering surface to improve sensor signal reflection, thereby enhancing signal quality and detection accuracy. Notably, in addition to providing a white surface, the embedded nanoparticles significantly improved the hydrogel’s structural properties. They enlarged the pore area, facilitating more efficient fluid and gas diffusion, while simultaneously reinforcing the mechanical strength of the hydrogel. This synergy of optical and mechanical improvements results in an advanced hydrogel nanocomposite, rendering it as highly suitable for coating applications. For oxygen sensing, we employed platinum porphyrin (Pt-porphyrin) embedded in poly(propyl methacrylate) matrix, resulting in a highly photo- and thermally-stable oxygen sensing material6,15. In our previous work, we utilized silicone/ coating as a white scattering layer for an oxygen sensing system for ex vivo kidney perfusion6. Herein, we advanced the design by replacing the silicone/ coating with a hydrogel-based coating that is biocompatible, anti-coagulant, and non-adhesive, thereby enhancing its clinical applicability.

Synthesis of the hydrogel nanocomposite

We employed covalent crosslinking chemistry involving the sulfate group-containing, zwitterionic compound 2-(N-3-Sulfopropyl-N,N-dimethyl ammonium)ethyl methacrylate (SPE), N,N’-methylenebisacrylamide (BIS) and 4-arm-PEG-acrylamide (4-arm PEG), in the presence of the UV initiator lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP), to fabricate the hydrogel network (Fig. 2). Specifically, we mixed NPs with the aqueous hydrogel curing solution (i.e., SPE, BIS, 4-arm-PEG, and LAP), drop-casted over the oxygen sensing Pt-porphyrin film, and UV-crosslinked the hydrogel for 20 min. The 4-arm PEG and SPE were selected due to their excellent biocompatibility and non-adhesive properties57. The zwitterionic nature of SPE is known to suppress cell adhesion and prevent coagulation38, crucial for accurate blood oxygen monitoring in a flow cell configuration. The incorporation of titanium dioxide nanoparticles provides a white, light-scattering surface to improve sensor signal reflection, enhances the hydrogel’s mechanical strength, and increases the pore area to facilitate efficient fluid and gas diffusion to the oxygen sensing film.

Hydrogel-coated oxygen sensing material preparation

To prepare the ready-to-use oxygen sensing flow cells, we first covered a glass slide with a polyvinyl chloride (PVC) film containing a 10 mm diameter central hole, forming a 10 mm circular mold. The exposed glass surface was treated with aqueous 1M NaOH to activate the surface functionalities, followed by functionalization with 3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane to introduce surface amine groups. These amine groups form covalent bonds with the acrylate groups in the hydrogel solution, improving adhesion and preventing detachment of the hydrogel from the glass dish under perfusate flow. The oxygen sensing material, consisting of 90 μM platinum porphyrin (Pt-porphyrin) and 5% poly(propyl methacrylate) (PPMA) (50 mg/mL) dissolved in dichloromethane as previously described15, was then cast onto the glass surface and allowed to dry for 15 min to achieve complete solvent evaporation. Subsequently, the hydrogel curing solution (SPE, BIS, 4-arm-PEG, NP, and LAP) was cast over the porphyrin-based sensing film and crosslinked under UV irradiation (Fig. 2). The coated glass slide was washed in deionized water for 24 h to remove unreacted materials and allow the hydrogel to fully swell. The as-prepared glass slide, with the hydrogel coated oxygen sensing film, was affixed onto a 3D-printed, hollow flow cell using double-sided tape. This flow cell was integrated in series within the tubing circuits of a mock perfusion system, as described in our previous work6, and tested for monitoring under a continuous mechanically-induced flow of PBS or buffer-diluted whole blood at different oxygenation levels.

Swelling ratio and gel fraction

As a coating for oxygen sensing films used in ECP and ECMO systems, hydrogel swelling in fluids must be controlled to maintain structural integrity and coating reproducibility. Excess swelling can negatively impact the system by: 1) causing hydrogel detachment due to increased mass; 2) obstructing perfusate flow and gas diffusion; and 3) delaying the material’s response to oxygen concentration changes, compromising sensitivity and accuracy. Therefore, testing the swelling ratio of the hydrogel is critical. We determined swelling ratios by incubating dried hydrogels in PBS for 48 h (Fig. 3A). The hydrogel displayed moderate swelling and reached equilibrium conditions within 24 h. Incorporating of NPs limited swelling to 240%, demonstrating a controlled interaction with fluids.

( A) Swelling ratio of the hydrogel nanocomposite over 48 h. (B) Rheological measurements of the hydrogel with or without NPs in PBS or water. (C) Gel fraction of hydrogels. (D) Degradation of the hydrogels over 14 days incubated at 37 °C in PBS or blood.

Additionally, we measured the gel fraction of the hydrogels to characterize their crosslinking density. Both formulations exhibited high gel fractions (> 90%), indicating effective network formation (Fig. 3C). Particularly, hydrogels with NPs achieved near 100% gel fraction. This enhancement is attributed to additional physical crosslinking facilitated by NPs, which engage in non-covalent interactions with the polymer network, including hydrogen bonding with amide groups and electrostatic interactions with the zwitterionic SPE component58–60. This reinforces the overall integrity of the network, preventing the leaching of hydrogel components when incorporated into ECP and ECMO systems.

Mechanical behavior

We performed rheological measurements to assess the mechanical properties of the hydrogel, with or without NPs, swollen in either water or PBS (Fig. 3B). The storage modulus (G’) consistently exceeded the loss modulus (G”), confirming the solid-like behavior of the hydrogels. Incorporation of NPs substantially increased the G’ from below 100 Pa to over 1,000 Pa, strengthening the mechanical properties of the hydrogel by forming aggregated NP clusters both on the surface and within the pores of the hydrogel (Fig. 4C). This mechanical enhancement is expected, as NPs act as inorganic fillers within the polymeric hydrogel matrix to promote physical crosslinking and distribute mechanical loads58,59,61,62. Hydrogels swollen in PBS exhibited similar G’ but considerably lower G” compared to those in water. This difference is attributed to the electrostatic interactions among SPE chains, which create a secondary, non-covalently crosslinked network in water38. In PBS, charge shielding effects disrupt this physical network, resulting in a more elastic, brittle gel in PBS and a more viscous, pliable gel in water. Overall, the NP-reinforced hydrogel nanocomposite exhibited good structural integrity and robust mechanical strength in both water and PBS, demonstrating its suitability for coating oxygen sensing films.

Stability and degradation

Depending on the clinical application, ECP typically lasts from minutes to a few hours, whereas ECMO often spans several days to a few weeks1,2. Accordingly, the hydrogel nanocomposite coating should retain structural stability and resist degradation for weeks. Following UV irradiation, SPE, BIS, and 4-arm PEG form chemically stable, carbon–carbon bonds that are generally resistant to degradation. Therefore, the primary mode of degradation is attributed to hydrolysis of the ester linkages in SPE and the amide linkages in 4-arm PEG and BIS. To assess stability under clinically relevant conditions, hydrogels with and without NPs were incubated in PBS and diluted rat whole blood at 37 °C for up to 14 days. By day 14, hydrogels containing NPs showed no overall mass loss, with statistically similar results observed between the blood and PBS groups (p = 0.369). In contrast, hydrogels without NPs exhibited a gradual reduction in mass starting from day 1 and lost approximately 17% of their initial mass by day 14 (Fig. 3D). This degradation trend aligns with the swelling behavior of the hydrogels (Fig. 3A), as -free hydrogels exhibited more than 1.5-fold higher swelling compared to those containing NPs. The increased degradation likely results from two factors: 1) a gel fraction of 92% in -free hydrogels indicates the presence of unreacted materials that may leach out over time (Fig. 3C); and 2) the higher swelling ratio of these hydrogels facilitates water ingress (Fig. 3A), which accelerates hydrolytic degradation. Importantly, hydrogels incorporating NPs remain structurally stable and maintain their integrity throughout the duration of clinically relevant ECP and ECMO applications.

SEM analysis

Next, the morphology and the pore area of the hydrogel were characterized via scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Both hydrogels, with and without NPs, showed a porous cross-sectional and a semi-porous surface (Fig. 4A–C), indicating a distinct phase separation between the hydrogel’s surface and core. The semi-porous surface likely results from the accumulation of charged hydrophilic components, such as SPE molecules and NPs, driven by their strong interaction with water. This behavior is consistent with findings in other poly(sulfobetaine)-containing hydrogels38,63. Clusters of NPs were evident both on the surface and within the pores of the hydrogel (Fig. 4C). Quantitative analysis of pore area at the hydrogel cross-section demonstrated a significant increase upon the addition of NPs (Fig. 4D). This observation contrasts with the swelling and mechanical test results, which suggest that the incorporation of NPs reduces hydrogel swelling, increases crosslinking density, and enhances mechanical strength—trends typically associated with smaller pore sizes. This discrepancy may be attributed to freeze-drying artifacts, as NPs likely serve as heterogeneous nucleation sites during freezing, promoting larger ice crystal formation and resulting in an altered pore morphology that does not reflect the native hydrogel structure64,65. Nevertheless, with pore areas on the order of μm2, the hydrogel permits efficient oxygen transport, enabling rapid and accurate monitoring of oxygen levels in the ECP and ECMO systems.

Biocompatibility and hemocompatibility

We performed cytotoxicity measurements to ensure the safety and biocompatibility of the hydrogel nanocomposite. Pre-formed hydrogel disks (12 mm diameter), prepared by casting and crosslinking 100 μl of hydrogel mixture, were placed into culture plates. NIH3T3 and OVCAR5 cells were seeded respectively into cell culture plates containing the hydrogel samples and cultured up to 72 h. Cell viability was assessed using an MTT assay, which revealed negligible cytotoxicity in both cell lines after 24, 48, and 72 h of exposure to the hydrogel (Fig. 5A). These results confirm the cyto-compatibility of the hydrogel, attributed to its biocompatible polymer components, and demonstrate its suitability for biological applications.

(A) Cell viability against OVCAR5 and NIH3T3 cells, measured using an MTT assay, following exposure to the synthesized hydrogel for periods of 24 h, 48 h and 72 h. (B) % hemolysis of hydrogels with and without NPs compared to 1% Triton X.

We next evaluated the hemocompatibility of the hydrogel, with and without NPs, by incubating them with rat red blood cells (RBCs) at 37 °C for 1 h. Both hydrogels exhibited minimal hemolytic effects (Fig. 5B), well within the safety threshold for blood-contacting biomaterials66,67. The hemolytic activity of NPs is concentration-, size-, and vehicle-dependent68–70. Our studies demonstrated that the SPE-based hydrogel containing 6.12 mg/ml NPs is hemocompatible, supporting its application as a blood-contacting, gas-permeable, and reflective coating for the oxygen-sensing films.

Non-adhesive properties

A non-adhesive surface is critical for components in ECP and ECMO systems to prevent interference from biological fluids. Adhesive surfaces attract proteins, cells, and other biological components, potentially interfering with the perfusion system and compromising oxygen level measurements by blocking gas diffusion to the sensing film. A non-adhesive hydrogel coating minimizes fouling and prevents the leaching of the sensing film, maintaining consistent functionality over time by minimizing interference from contaminants.

To evaluate the hydrogel’s cell adhesivity, we used human lung microvascular endothelial cells (HLMVEC), a cell line known to adhere and form monolayers on glass and polyacrylamide-based hydrogels in the presence of native extracellular matrix ligands such as collagen and fibronectin71,72. Cells were cultured on both bare uncoated hydrogels and collagen-coated hydrogels to assess adhesion behavior. A total of 200k cells were plated on each type of hydrogel and incubated under standard culture conditions for 24 h.

Post-incubation, optical microscopy revealed that cells on bare hydrogels remained non-adherent and floated in the media. In contrast, cells adhered robustly to the collagen-coated hydrogel substrate. To confirm these findings, media was replaced, and cells were further incubated for an additional 48 h. Live-cell staining for membrane protein vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin, cytoskeletal protein F-actin, and the nucleus, followed by confocal microscopy imaging, showed no cell growth on the bare hydrogel (Fig. 6A). Conversely, a well-defined monolayer of HLMVECs was observed on the collagen-coated hydrogel (Fig. 6B). These results highlight the negligible adhesivity of the bare SPE hydrogel to cells, thereby preventing the accumulation of biological components. This non-adhesive behavior is particularly important for maintaining unobstructed gas diffusion and ensuring accurate oxygen sensing in ECP and ECMO systems.

Representative confocal images of HLMVEC grown on (A) bare and (B) collagen-coated hydrogels. Cells were stained for VE-cadherin (green), F-actin (red), and nucleus (blue).

Blood clotting behavior

Non-coagulant surfaces are essential in ECP and ECMO systems to maintain blood compatibility and prevent clot formation. Coagulation on the hydrogel coating will compromise oxygen level measurements and the system’s stability, as blood clots may obstruct gas diffusion or lead to thrombosis by spreading beyond the contact area.

The classic coagulation cascade involves the tissue factor-activated extrinsic pathway and the foreign surface contact-activated intrinsic pathway, converging into the common pathway to form a stable blood clot73,74. To evaluate the hydrogel’s effect on coagulation, we used the activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) assay, which measures the fibrin clot formation time initiated by the intrinsic pathway. Hydrogels with and without NPs were tested, and their clotting times were compared to uncoated and heparin-coated wells. The results (Fig. 7A) showed that both heparin-coated and hydrogel-coated wells significantly extended clotting times beyond 54 s (Fig. 7B). Hydrogels with NPs displayed clotting times comparable to heparin coating, while hydrogels without NPs showed even longer clotting times, exceeding 74 s (Fig. 7B).

(A) Example absorbance graphs of the APTT assay for the uncoated control, heparin coating, NPs-containing hydrogel coating, and NPs-free hydrogel coating. (B) APTT calculated as the clotting time needed to reach half-maximal absorbance.

The hydrogel’s non-coagulant properties are attributed to its hydrophilic nature and its heparin-mimicking inhibition of the plasma coagulation pathways. The incorporation of PEG polymers and zwitterionic SPE imparts a highly hydrophilic surface that resists protein adsorption and cellular adherence, preventing coagulation38,50. Additionally, the sulfonate groups in the hydrogel impede the proteolytic conversion of fibrinogen to fibrin and inhibit the polymerization of fibrin monomers, thus preventing stable clot formation75,76. While NPs alone exhibit procoagulant potential through increased phosphatidylserine exposure68,77, embedding them within the SPE-based hydrogel mitigates this effect. The resulting hydrogel exhibited clotting times comparable to therapeutic levels of heparin coating (1.45 units/cm2) (Fig. 7B), confirming its non-coagulant behavior and suitability for use in ECP and ECMO systems.

To further evaluate the performance of the sensing Pt-porphyrin-based film coated with the hydrogel layer in a blood-contacting environment, the mock oxygenated perfusion experiment was repeated using diluted porcine blood as the circulating medium. Calibration parameters obtained from the PBS experiments were applied to calculate the of the perfusate. The results demonstrated that the prototype sensor’s measurements were accurate to with approximately 2 mmHg of the analytical reference, indicating excellent agreement. As previously reported, the signal resolution of the Pt-porphyrin sensor depends on the measured oxygen partial pressure range, with values ranging from 0.2 to 3 mmHg in the best and worst-case scenarios for the intensity-derived 81. The level of precision exceeds clinical requirements, which typically necessitate a resolution of 5–10 mmHg. Additionally, we evaluated the responsiveness of the sensor by analyzing the rate of change in readouts. The prototype flow cell demonstrated similar responsiveness to the analytical reference (Fig. 8C), rapidly detecting changes in within 10–15 s; several times faster than current requirements for ECP and ECMO systems. Notably, the prototype sensor displayed low signal-to-noise ratios and great signal stability, with very small variances as well as consistency throughout the measurement cycles as indicated by the smooth calibration plots (Fig. 8B,C). The reduced noise is attributed to the high breathability of the hydrogel coating, in conjunction with the strong brightness and sensitivity of Pt-porphyrin sensing film. Since measurement noise obscures critical changes in blood oxygen tension, the prototype sensor’s low signal variance and high stability offers significant advantages for future clinical applications.

These results confirm that the hydrogel coating is highly breathable, allowing for efficient oxygen diffusion to the sensing film and enabling rapid equilibration with the circulating perfusate. The coating demonstrated mechanical stability under the forces of circulation, and its oxygen sensing performance remained unaffected by the presence of blood. Given its breathability, non-coagulant properties, and biocompatibility, the hydrogel coating is well-suited for use in ECP and ECMO systems for ex vivo oxygen monitoring across diverse liquid flow environments.

Conclusion

We report the successful development of a hydrogel nanocomposite breathable coating for oxygen sensing films, for integration within ECP and ECMO systems. The hydrogel nanocomposite, synthesized via a UV-initiated cross-linking reaction, is hydrophilic and antifouling. The incorporation of nanoparticles significantly enhances the hydrogel’s performance by producing a light-scattering layer to improve sensor signal reflection, increasing the mechanical properties to enhance structural integrity, and preserving macropore sizes on the order of μm2 to ensure high gas permeability. Thus, the coating ensures mechanical stability and biocompatibility while addressing practical challenges associated with breathability, fouling and blood coagulation. When integrated within an ECP-mimicking reservoir system containing artificially oxygenated perfusate or blood, the hydrogel nanocomposite coated platinum porphyrin sensor rapidly and accurately measured levels in real-time. Although this is the first system to achieve such capability, future steps will include conducting studies with whole human blood as the perfusate and testing the hydrogel nanocomposite in clinical ECP and ECMO systems over prolonged circulation times (multiple days) using appropriate ex vivo models. Importantly, the technology and approach can be readily integrated within existing ECP and ECMO systems, providing rapid temporal response, enhanced measurement stability, and reduced readout costs. Further, the entire design is amenable to miniaturization into stand-alone, 5–10 mm diameter, battery powered sensors, supporting efforts to reduce the size of ECP and ECMO systems for greater usability and portability beyond major medical centers. This compact design is also well-suited for wearable ECMO systems, where lightweight, small gas sensors are critical82. While the primary focus of this work is on ECP and ECMO systems, the materials and approach presented here hold promise for a range of clinical applications. One immediate application is in ex vivo organ perfusion, where perfusates are typically maintained at super-physiological oxygenation levels, incompatible with current oxygen saturation-based readout methods81. Additionally, sensing flow cells constructed with the hydrogel material could be applied in vascular composite allograft perfusion to enable precise tissue monitoring and prevent hypoxia or ischemia83. Another potential application is oxygen sensing in urine, which could serve as a diagnostic tool for kidney diseases. In summary, we envision that this hydrogel nanocomposite coating will be of interest for a variety of diverse sensing and clinical applications that involve blood or other biological fluids, including the detection of physiologically relevant gases, ions, pH, or even small molecules.

Synthesis of hydrogel nanocomposite and preparation of ready-to-use sensing flow cell

To synthesize the hydrogel nanocomposite, we experimented with utilizing different ratios of SPE, BIS, 4-arm PEG and , and found that aqueous solutions of 1.0 g/mL of SPE, 0.02 g/mL of BIS, and 0.1 g/mL of 4-arm PEG mixed at a ratio of 5:2:1, provided optimal hydrogel formation at room temperature. The SPE monomer solution was blended with nanoparticles before mixing with BIS, PEG and crosslinking agents so that the nanoparticles are embedded into the formed hydrogel to provide stability and a white, reflective surface. Specifically, a 100 μl aqueous solution of 1.0 g/mL SPE was mixed with 1.0 mg nanoparticles. 60 μl of an aqueous solution of 0.02 g/mL BIS and 30 μl of an aqueous solution of 0.1 g/mL of 4-arm PEG were then added, and everything was mixed thoroughly with a pipette. The mixture was treated with 5 μl of 5% aqueous LAP before being drop-casted onto a preformed porphyrin film on a glass slide.

The ready-to-use, sensing flow cell was prepared as follows. A mold was created on a glass slide by applying double-sided tape with a 10 mm diameter hole in the middle. The glass slide was treated with 50 μl of a 1M aqueous NaOH solution for 1 h, and was then rinsed with ultrapure water (from a milli-Q purification system) and allowed to dry. 50 μl (3-aminopropyl) trimethoxysilane (APTMS) was applied and incubated for 10 min. The dish was then rinsed with ultrapure water and dried overnight. 10 μl of a solution containing 90 μM sensing metalloporphyrin and 5% poly(n-propyl methacrylate) co-dissolved in dichloromethane, was then drop-cast on the silane functionalized glass surface and dried at rt. 25 μl of the hydrogel mixture was then deposited on top of sensing film and placed under UV-radiation (Analytikjena UVP Crosslinker, 8 Watt, 365 nm) for 10 min to form the cross-linked hydrogel nanocomposite coating. The hydrogel coating formed onto the porphyrin film was washed thrice with distilled water in order to remove unreacted monomers, UV initiator, and unloaded nanoparticles. The developed glass slide bearing the sensing film coated with hydrogel nanocomposite was affixed onto a flow cell. The flow cell was then connected in line within the tubing circuitry of an ECP mimicking system that continuously circulated perfusate buffer solution.

Mechanical characterizations

The swelling ratio of the synthesized hydrogel nanocomposite was measured by completely submerging the hydrogel in an aqueous medium and weighing it. Each of the synthesized hydrogels was first dried completely at 50 °C and weighed up. It was then placed in PBS and allowed to swell for a period of 0.5 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 24 h, and 48 h. It was removed carefully from the water using tweezers and gently blotted with a tissue paper to remove the excess of water. Swelled hydrogel was then weighed up again, and the swelling ratio was calculated using the equation: Swelling Ratio (%) = (swelled mass/dried mass) 100.

To measure gel fraction, hydrogels were first oven dried at 50 °C overnight to obtain the initial dry weight (). The dried samples were then immersed in PBS at 37 °C for 24 h to remove any unreacted materials. Subsequently, the samples were oven dried again to constant weight (). Gel fraction was calculated as: Gel fraction (%) = (/) 100.

Rheological measurements were conducted using a TA Instruments DHR-2 rheometer following previously published protocols84. Hydrogels (100 μl) with and without NPs were cured in an 8 mm-diameter Teflon mold using an Analytik Jena UV lamp (365 nm, 2 h) and subsequently swollen in PBS or water for 2 h. Frequency sweeps were performed from 0.1 rad/s to 100 rad/s at 1% strain and 25 °C using 8 mm parallel plates (n = 3).

For stability studies, hydrogel samples were oven dried at 50 °C overnight to obtain the initial dry weight (). Separate sets of hydrogel samples were incubated in either PBS or diluted rat whole blood at 37 °C for up to 14 days. At designated time points (day 1, 2, 4, 7, and 14), samples were retrieved, rinsed with DI water to eliminate residual blood components, and oven dried at 50 °C to constant weight (). The percentage of mass remaining was calculated as: Hydrogel mass (%) = (/) 100.

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), SEM images were acquired using a Zeiss Supra 55VP at 3 kV, with a working distance of 9–12 mm and an aperture size of 30 μm. Hydrogels with and without NPs were swollen in water overnight, lyophilized, and sputter coated with AuPd for 20 s. Pore area was quantified and analyzed using the built-in threshold and particle analysis tools in ImageJ (v1.54f) following an established protocol20.

Cytotoxicity measurements

Studies to assess the cytotoxicity of the hydrogel films were carried out using MTT assays. In short, NIH3T3 (ATCC) and OVCAR5 (Fox Chase Cancer Institute) cells were seeded in a 6-well plate (50000 cells/well in 1 mL cell medium) and cultured at 37 °C overnight before adding the hydrogel samples (100 μl/ 12 mm diameter). For background subtraction, wells containing no cells but only hydrogels in medium were used. Cells were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C before the MTT (5mg/mL) solution (10 μl) was added. After an incubation period of 4 h, the hydrogel materials were removed and DMSO was added to dissolve the formed MTT-formazan crystal, and absorbance was recorded. The measurements were performed in triplicates and repeated three times. The cell viability was determined by adjusting the non-treated cells to 100%. GraphPad Prism was used for the data visualization.

Hemolysis assay

Hemolysis activity of the hydrogels, with and without the incorporation of NPs, was evaluated according to published protocols85,86. Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated from rat whole blood and triple-washed with PBS by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min. RBCs were diluted to 30% v/v in PBS and aliquoted as per 500 μl in tubes. 50 μl of hydrogel with or without NPs were added to each tube (n = 3) and incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 1 h. PBS and 1% Triton X were used as the negative control and positive control, respectively. The samples were then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm and the hemolysis ratio was calculated as follows: Hemolysis (percent) = (OD of sample – OD of negative) / (OD of positive – OD of negative) 100%.

Cell adhesion experiment

The cell adhesion experiments involved hydrogel preparation in a dish, where cells were then seeded and cultured onto hydrogels with or without collagen coating. Sample visualization was conducted using confocal laser scanning microscopy. For hydrogel preparation prior to cell deposition, dishes with a 20 mm well at the center with glass bottom were utilized. The steps for gel preparation were as follows: (a) Glass was treated with 1 ml of 1N aq. NaOH solution for 1 h; (b) the treated surface was rinsed with ultrapure water and allowed to dry; (c) 200 μl of (3-Aminopropyl)trimethoxysilane were applied on the glass surface and incubated for 7 min; (d) the dish was rinsed afterwards and dried overnight; (e) 30 μl of hydrogel solution was drop-cast on the glass, covered with a circular clean glass coverslip, and incubated for 30 min; (f) the dish was filled with 2 mL of ultrapure water and the top coverslip was removed using forceps. The bare hydrogel with a flat top surface was then ready for cell culture.

The collagen-coated hydrogel, which served as a control hydrogel with cell adhesive behavior, was prepared by coating its surface as follows: (a) the hydrogel was treated with 200 μl of SANPAH (2mg/ml in 0.1M HEPES buffer) and incubated under UV-light for 8 min; (b) it was rinsed thoroughly with 0.1 M HEPES solution; (c) 3% collagen-I was applied on the gel and incubated overnight; (d) the collagen solution was removed following incubation, and the hydrogel was rinsed with PBS several times.

For testing cell growth on the hydrogel, the Human Lung Microvascular Endothelial Cell (HLMVEC) line from Lonza Bioscience was cultured on both the bare and collagen-coated hydrogel under the following conditions: the cells were cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (EBM-2, from Lonza, Walkersville, MD, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% humidified incubation chamber. A total of 200k HLMVEC suspended in 2 ml of culture media were plated on the gels, and the media were changed after 24 h. The cells were allowed to grow for an additional 48 h before being imaged under the confocal microscope. For confocal microscopy, the samples, including cells cultured on both bare and collagen-coated hydrogels, were stained for VE-Cadherin (FITC CD144, 488 nm, green), F-actin (SiR-Actin kit, 647nm, red), and nucleus (Hoechst 33342, 405 nm, blue) for 30 min. Images for both samples were captured under similar experimental settings using the Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope. A step-wise description of the procedure employed to check HLMVEC cell adhesion on hydrogel samples is shown in Fig. S2.

APTT assay

The APTT assay was conducted with modifications to established protocols73,85,87. Hydrogels (20 μl) with and without NPs were cured into a donut shape on the bottom of a heparin-coated 96-well plate (3 μg heparin/well) using an Analytik Jena UV lamp (365 nm, 2 h). Non-coating 96-well plates served as the control. Normal human plasma (50 μl) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. APTT-XL reagent (50 μl) was subsequently added and incubated at 37 °C for 3 min to ensure optimum activation. Clotting was induced by adding 25 μl of 0.04 M solution. Absorbance measurements at 405 nm were recorded every 6 sec for 10 min across all samples (n = 6). APTT was calculated as the clotting time needed to reach half-maximal absorbance.

Calibrations

Calibrations were performed on lab-built ECMO circuit (Fig. 1) utilizing a PreSens laboratory oxygen sensing system (Microx 4) running in parallel (as a reference) to our prototype. The system was cycled between hypoxia (50 mmHg) and hyperoxia (450 mmHg) twice, running at ambient temperature for a total time of 1.5 h. From here, the Presens and oxygen sensing devices data were read into a Python calibration program to generate usable calibration files. These calibration files were then included into the analysis python program whenever data was acquired from an experiment.

monitoring experiment

Films with embedded oxygen sensing metalloporphyrin molecules (Fig. S3, supporting information), were created by drop casting a solution of the Pt-porphyrin and PPMA polymer co-dissolved in dichloromethane onto amino-functionalized glass surfaces. The hydrogel material was prepared and applied over the oxygen sensing Pt-porphyrin/PPMA film, and was cross-linked under UV-Vis irradiation for 20 min. The developed glass slide featuring the coated sensing film was affixed onto a flow cell, connected in line within the tubing circuitry of the ECP mimicking system, and tested under continuous circulation of perfusate buffer solution. The ECP mimicking system consists of a 2 liter sealed reservoir equipped with inlet/outlet ports for perfusate (buffer or blood) circulation through circulator pump and gas bubbling using compressed oxygen and nitrogen cylinders. Cycles varying oxygen concentration within perfusate (50 mmHg; hypoxia to 450 mmHg; hyperoxia) were tested. The code running the device and PreSens (reference) were run simultaneously and raw data obtained was processed using Python program as reported previously6,80. The limit of detection of our device can be as low as 0.2 mmHg in the absence of oxygen and 3 mmHg in an atmosphere of pure oxygen, as discussed in our previous work6.

Unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was performed to validate statistical differences between two conditions. One-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey’s test was performed for multiple comparisons.
