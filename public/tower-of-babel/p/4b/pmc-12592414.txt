Adsorption properties of the pharmaceutical contaminant tamoxifen on calcined bone char
Authors: İbrahim Bulduk, Ayşegül Türk Baydır, Çiğdem Aşcıoğlu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this study, calcined bone char was produced from waste bones and characterized by SEM-EDX and BET surface area analysis. The adsorption properties of tamoxifen, a cytotoxic cancer drug, by calcined bone char were investigated in batch experiments and the effects of adsorbent dose, pH, contact time, temperature, and initial concentration were evaluated. Calcined bone char exhibits a monolayer adsorption capacity of 98.30 mg/g for tamoxifen was achieved at an initial concentration of 40 mg/L, an adsorbent dose of 20 mg/50 mL, a temperature of 25 °C, and a pH of 7. The Langmuir model (R²: 0.9900) provided the best fit to the isothermal adsorption data and supported monolayer, homogeneous surface adsorption. The pseudo-second-order-kinetic model (R²: 0.9997) best described the adsorption kinetics, indicating that chemisorption was the dominant mechanism. Thermodynamic analysis revealed that the adsorption process was spontaneous, endothermic, and entropy-driven. These findings demonstrate that calcined bone char is a sustainable and effective adsorbent for the removal of pharmaceutical contaminants from aqueous solutions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22875-6.

Introduction

Water scarcity and pollution directly threaten the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, including ‘Clean Water and Sanitation’ (Goal 6), ‘Life Below Water’ (Goal 14), and ‘Health and Well-Being’ (Goal 3). Removing micropollutants, in particular, is critical for both protecting aquatic ecosystems and ensuring human health1.

Increasing population density, industrial activities, and agricultural production processes place significant pressure on freshwater resources. As a result of these processes, in addition to traditional pollutants, emerging contaminants (CECs) have also emerged as environmental threats in recent years. CECs include a wide range of chemicals, such as pharmaceutical compounds (e.g., antibiotics like sulfamethoxazole, analgesics like diclofenac, antidepressants like fluoxetine, and anticancer drugs like tamoxifen), hormones, pesticides, personal care products, illicit substances, and perfluorinated compounds. These substances have been detected in wastewater at concentrations ranging from nanograms per liter (ng/L) to milligrams per liter (mg/L)2. Pharmaceutical pollutants, in particular, can reach levels of up to mg/L in wastewater originating from the pharmaceutical industry3,4.

However, conventional wastewater treatment systems are often inadequate in removing CECs. This causes these substances to accumulate in the environment and, over time, to have serious adverse effects on aquatic life and human health5. Indeed, the sources, environmental persistence, and ecotoxic effects of CECs have become a major focus of scientific research today. The widespread prescription and consumption of pharmaceutical compounds, in particular, leads to these compounds passing through wastewater systems and reaching detectable levels in aquatic environments6–8. In this context, the development of sustainable water management policies and the implementation of advanced treatment technologies are of great importance for environmental security.

Among these pollutants, tamoxifen is a selective estrogen receptor modulator widely used in the treatment of estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer and is an important anticancer drug. It is excreted in urine and feces after being metabolized in the body, but because it cannot be completely removed in conventional wastewater treatment plants, it can reach surface waters9,10. Reaching tamoxifen and its metabolites in aquatic environments poses a serious environmental risk due to effects such as cell toxicity (cytotoxicity), DNA damage (genotoxicity), as well as mutagenic, carcinogenic, and endocrine-disrupting properties. Studies with marine organisms, in particular, have shown that this compound can cause oxidative stress, DNA damage, and endocrine system disorders in male individuals, even at low concentrations9,10. Furthermore, its half-life of approximately 60 days in freshwater environments indicates that tamoxifen is an environmentally persistent and bioaccumulative pollutant11. With these properties, tamoxifen poses a potential ecotoxicological threat to aquatic ecosystems12. Limited studies, such as those using activated carbon from hemp roots (qmax = 120 mg/g) and synthetic hydroxyapatite (qmax = 90 mg/g), have explored tamoxifen removal from aquatic environments13,14.

At this point, bone char, an environmentally friendly, economical, and effective treatment material, is one of the prominent alternative adsorbents. Bone char is a porous, solid material obtained by the thermochemical conversion of animal bones in an anoxic or low-oxygen environment15,16. This conversion both inactivates pathogens and creates porous structures with high surface area. Bone char mainly consists of 70–76% calcium hydroxyphosphate (hydroxyapatite, HAP [Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂]), 7–9% calcium carbonate, and 9–11% amorphous carbon14. Because the hydroxide or phosphate ions in the HAP structure can be replaced by carbonate ions, this material is also often called “carbonated hydroxyapatite (CHAp)”17,18.

Thanks to its porous structure and chemical composition, bone char can be used in many environmental applications such as adsorbent, soil conditioner, and catalyst carrier16. Studies on the use of bone char in heavy metal removal and organic pollutant adsorption16–38 reveal that this material has a wide potential in environmental treatment technologies.

This study aims to investigate the novel use of calcined bone char for the removal of the cytotoxic cancer drug tamoxifen from aqueous solutions via adsorption. To our knowledge, no prior study has explored the removal of cytotoxic drugs using this specific adsorbent. The objectives include determining the optimum adsorption conditions (adsorbent dose, pH, contact time, temperature, initial concentration) and performing kinetic, isothermal, and thermodynamic modeling of the process. The use of calcined bone char, derived from waste biomass, supports a circular economy approach and offers a sustainable, cost-effective, and innovative solution to combat pharmaceutical pollution.

Material and method

Materials and solution preparation

Leg bones of cattle obtained from a local butcher were used to prepare the adsorbent material.

For method development and analytical method validation in HPLC analyses, tamoxifen citrate (≥ 99% purity) was procured from Merck, Istanbul, and a 500 mg/L standard tamoxifen solution was used. Solutions containing different tamoxifen concentrations were obtained by diluting the stock tamoxifen citrate solution. pH adjustments were made using 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH solutions (Merck). Tamoxifen removal experiments were conducted using varying doses of calcined bone char (10–30 mg) as the adsorbent, with wastewater samples obtained from a local pharmacy in Afyonkarahisar.

Preparation of calcined bone char

First, the bones were boiled in water for one hour to easily remove adhering fat and tissues. The sticky fat and tissues were then scraped off and cleaned using a knife. To remove organic matter and collagen, the bones were deproteinized by soaking in acetone for 24 h, followed by washing with pure water. Subsequently, the bones were dried in an oven at 100 °C. The dried bones were broken into small pieces and ground into a fine powder using a ball mill. The resulting powder was calcined in a nitrogen gas atmosphere at 700 °C for two hours, with a heating rate of 5 °C per minute. After cooling and drying, the material was sieved through a 40-mesh sieve to obtain a uniform powder. Finally, the powder was stored in a sealed plastic container for use in characterization tests and adsorption experiments31.

Characterisation of calcined bone char

The specific surface area of calcined bone char was analyzed through the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method using a Micromeritics Gemini 2360 analyzer. High-purity nitrogen served as the adsorptive gas for surface area measurement. Prior to analysis, around 1 gram of each sample was degassed at 250 °C under a continuous nitrogen stream, and the dry mass was recorded using a precision analytical balance. The surface structure and distribution characteristics of the calcined bone char were further examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a LEO 1430 VP model instrument.

Determination of the pHpZc value of calcined bone char

The point of zero charge (pHpZc) of the calcined bone char was determined through a systematic procedure. Initially, a 0.1 M NaCl solution was prepared, and 50 mL portions were transferred into separate 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. The initial pH (pHi) of each solution was adjusted to values of 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 using either 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. Subsequently, 0.050 g of calcined bone char was added to each flask. The flasks were then agitated in a shaker at 25 °C and 150 rpm for 24 h. After equilibration, the suspensions were filtered through white band filter paper, and the final pH (pHf) of each solution was measured using a pH meter (S20 Seven Easy, Mettler Toledo). Finally, a ΔpH (pHf − pHi) versus pHi plot was generated to determine the pHpZc value32.

Batch adsorption experiments

All experiments were conducted in an orbital shaker at 180 rpm and room temperature (25 ± 1 °C). The pH of the prepared solutions was adjusted with 0.1 M NaOH and 0.1 M HCl. After the reaction, the solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm pore diameter syringe filter. In batch adsorption studies, adsorbent dose, initial tamoxifen concentration, pH, temperature, and contact time were studied, respectively.

The effect of adsorbent dose on tamoxifen removal was evaluated using different amounts of calcined bone powder. Experiments were conducted with 50 mL of tamoxifen solution with an initial concentration of 40 mg/L. Different amounts of calcined bone powder (10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 mg) were added to the 50 mL tamoxifen solution. The mixture was shaken at 180 rpm for 150 min. After shaking, the solutions were filtered using a syringe tip filter (0.22 μm pore size) membrane filter to ensure complete retention of adsorbent particles. This prevented any interference of the adsorbent in subsequent HPLC analysis.

The effect of the initial tamoxifen concentration on adsorption performance was investigated at different initial concentrations. 50 mL tamoxifen solutions (Co: 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, and 100 mg/L) were prepared. 50 mg of calcined bone powder was added to each solution, and the mixtures were shaken at 180 rpm for 150 min. After shaking, the solutions were filtered using a syringe tip filter (0.22 μm pore size) membrane filter to ensure complete retention of adsorbent particles. This prevented any interference of the adsorbent in subsequent HPLC analysis. The remaining tamoxifen concentration (Ce, mg/L) was determined by HPLC.

The effect of solution pH on tamoxifen adsorption was investigated at pH values of 4, 7, and 10. The pH values of 4, 7, and 10 were selected to represent acidic, neutral, and alkaline conditions, respectively, and to specifically probe the adsorption behavior around the pHpzc of the adsorbent (7.60) and the pKa of tamoxifen (~ 8.5), which are critical for understanding the electrostatic interactions governing the process. A broader range (e.g., 2–12) was not used to avoid potential structural degradation of the hydroxyapatite-based adsorbent at highly acidic pH and to focus on the environmentally relevant pH range where tamoxifen and the adsorbent surface charge are most dynamic. 50 mL of 50 mg/L tamoxifen solutions were prepared in three erlenmeyer flasks, and the solution pH was adjusted to 4, 7, and 10 using 0.1 M NaOH or 0.1 M HCl. 20 mg of calcined bone powder was added to each vial, and the mixtures were shaken at 180 rpm for 150 min. After shaking, the solutions were filtered using a syringe tip filter (0.22 μm pore size) membrane filter to ensure complete retention of adsorbent particles. This prevented any interference of the adsorbent in subsequent HPLC analysis. The remaining tamoxifen concentration (Ce, mg/L) was determined by HPLC.

The effect of temperature on tamoxifen adsorption was investigated at 25 °C, 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C. 50 mL solutions of 50 mg/L tamoxifen were prepared in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. 20 mg. of calcined bone powder was added to each flask, and the mixtures were shaken at 180 rpm for 150 min at 25 °C, 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C. After shaking, the solutions were filtered using a syringe tip filter (0.22 μm pore size) membrane filter to ensure complete retention of adsorbent particles. This prevented any interference of the adsorbent in subsequent HPLC analysis. The remaining tamoxifen concentration (Ce, mg/L) was determined by HPLC.

Adsorption experiments were conducted to investigate the effect of contact time. A 40 mg/L tamoxifen solution was placed in a 50 mL Erlenmeyer flask, and 20 mg. of calcined bone powder was added as an adsorbent. The mixture was shaken on a temperature-controlled shaker at 150 rpm for 180 min at ambient temperature (298 ± 1 K). Samples were taken at predetermined time intervals (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min), filtered through a 0.45-µm syringe filter, and analyzed for residual tamoxifen concentration. Tamoxifen concentration (Ce) in solutions was analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent 1260, USA). HPLC was chosen over UV-Vis due to its higher selectivity and accuracy in complex matrices, minimizing potential interferences from adsorbent leaks or dissolved organic carbon. Separation was carried out using an ODS-3 C18 column (5 μm, 250 × 4.6 mm) at 30 °C. The mobile phase was a mixture of acetonitrile and 0.1 M KH2PO4 solution (55:45 v/v) adjusted to pH 2.0 with phosphoric acid. Flow rate of mobile phase was 1.0 mL/min. Detection was carried out using a UV detector set at 278 nm. The injection volume was 5 µL. Determination of pHpzc value of calcined bone char

The pHpzc value of calcined bone char was determined to be approximately 7.60 (Fig. 3A). This may affect the adsorption of tamoxifen molecules from aqueous solutions as follows:

Tamoxifen charge state: Tamoxifen is a basic compound containing an amino group and has a pKa value of approximately 8.536. This means that tamoxifen is protonated to a positively charged (cationic) form below pH 8.5 and becomes neutral above pH 8.5. pH 7.60 (pHpzc) is close to this transition region.

pH < 7.60 (Acidic Environment): The surface of calcined bone char is positively charged. Tamoxifen is also positively charged in this pH range, so electrostatic repulsion (the repulsion of similar charges) can hinder adsorption. However, if adsorption occurs through hydrophobic interactions or chemical bonds (e.g., π-π interactions), this effect may be reduced.

pH ≈ 7.60 (Neutral Surface): At pHpzc, the adsorbent surface is neutral. In this case, electrostatic interactions are minimal, and adsorption will depend on chemical or physical interactions (e.g., van der Waals forces) between tamoxifen and the adsorbent surface. This pH can be considered a transition point for adsorption.

pH > 7.60 (Basic Environment): While the surface becomes negatively charged, tamoxifen remains positively charged below pH 8.5 (in the range of 7.54–8.5). This may create electrostatic attraction between the surface and tamoxifen, thereby increasing the adsorption capacity. This pH range between 7.54 and 8.5 may be potentially the most favorable region for tamoxifen adsorption.

Optimization of adsorption conditions

Adsorbent dose determination

The effect of adsorbent dose on tamoxifen removal is presented in Fig. 3B. As the adsorbent dose increased from 10 to 30 mg, the equilibrium concentration (Ce) decreased from 8.48 mg/L to 0.159 mg/L, corresponding to an increase in adsorption efficiency from 78.80% to 99.60%. This trend is attributed to the increase in the total number of available adsorption sites and surface area with a higher mass of adsorbent, providing more opportunities for tamoxifen molecules to be bound and removed from the solution. The adsorption capacity (qe) also increased from 78.80 mg/g to 99.60 mg/g. The significant reduction in Ce at higher doses indicates increased availability of adsorption sites, with near-complete removal achieved at 20 mg (98.06% efficiency).

The effect of solution pH

The influence of solution pH on tamoxifen adsorption is shown in Fig. 3C. As pH increased from 4 to 10, Ce decreased from 0.705 mg/L to 0.298 mg/L, with adsorption efficiency rising from 98.59% to 99.40%. The adsorption capacity (qe) increased slightly from 98.24 mg/g to 99.26 mg/g. The observed enhancement in adsorption under alkaline conditions is fundamentally governed by the surface charge of the adsorbent and the ionization state of the tamoxifen molecule. The pHpzc of the calcined bone char was determined to be 7.60, meaning its surface is positively charged at pH < 7.60 and negatively charged at pH > 7.60. Tamoxifen, with a pKa of 8.5, exists primarily in its protonated, cationic form below this value. Therefore, at pH 10 (above both pHpzc and pKa), the adsorbent surface is negatively charged while tamoxifen is still largely cationic, leading to strong electrostatic attraction that facilitates enhanced adsorption. At lower pH, both the surface and the adsorbate may be positively charged, resulting in electrostatic repulsion that slightly hinders the process. The results suggest that alkaline conditions (pH: 10) enhance adsorption, likely due to favorable interactions between tamoxifen and the adsorbent surface.

The effect of initial concentration

The effect of initial tamoxifen concentration is summarized in Fig. 3D. As C0 increased from 20 to 100 mg/L, Ce rose from 0.101 mg/L to 43.56 mg/L, and adsorption efficiency decreased from 99.50% to 56.44%. Conversely, qe increased from 49.75 mg/g to 141.10 mg/g, indicating higher uptake per unit mass at elevated concentrations, though with reduced efficiency due to saturation of adsorption sites. This behavior is classic in adsorption processes. The decrease in removal efficiency is due to the fixed number of available adsorption sites becoming saturated at higher initial concentrations. However, the increase in the equilibrium adsorption capacity (qe) is driven by the higher concentration gradient, which acts as a stronger driving force to overcome mass transfer resistances and push more molecules onto the adsorbent surface, thereby increasing the amount adsorbed per unit mass until saturation is reached.

The effect of temperature

The effect of temperature on tamoxifen adsorption was investigated at 25 °C, 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C. The effect of temperature on adsorption efficiency is given in Fig. 4. As temperature increased from 25 °C to 40 °C, Ce decreased from 0.626 mg/L to 0.291 mg/L, with adsorption efficiency rising from 98.75% to 99.42%. The adsorption capacity (qe) increased from 98.44 mg/g to 99.27 mg/g. The consistent improvement in adsorption performance with increasing temperature indicates an endothermic process. This can be explained by several factors: (1) enhanced diffusion rate of tamoxifen molecules across the external boundary layer and within the pores of the adsorbent due to reduced solution viscosity, (2) an increase in the kinetic energy of molecules leading to more frequent and effective collisions with active sites, and (3) potential enlargement of adsorbent pore sizes. This observation aligns perfectly with the positive ΔH° value obtained from thermodynamic analysis, further confirming the endothermic nature of the adsorption. These results suggest an endothermic adsorption process, with higher temperatures enhancing tamoxifen removal.

Analysis and mechanism

The high adsorption capacity of calcined bone char for tamoxifen (98.30 mg/g at optimal conditions) underscores its potential as an effective and sustainable adsorbent for pharmaceutical contaminant removal. The superior fit of both the Langmuir isotherm (R² = 0.9900) and the pseudo-second-order kinetic model (R² = 0.9997) indicates a chemisorption-dominated monolayer process on homogeneous sites. These sites are likely the calcium and phosphate groups of hydroxyapatite, which constitutes the majority of the material’s composition.

The apparent contradiction between the PSO model (suggesting chemisorption) and the D-R energy (6.68 kJ/mol, indicating physisorption) can be reconciled by considering the nature of the interactions. The value of ΔH° (37.15 kJ/mol) falls within a range that can be associated with both strong physisorption and weak chemisorption, such as ion-exchange or coordination reactions. The PSO model’s excellent fit suggests that chemical interaction (e.g., electrostatic attraction or surface complexation with hydroxyapatite sites) is the rate-determining step. The D-R energy, often used for microporous materials, might not fully capture the complexity of interactions on a composite material like bone char. Therefore, the adsorption is best described as being dominated by specific, chemical-based interactions rather than non-specific van der Waals forces, albeit with an energy magnitude that overlaps with the upper limit of physisorption.

The material’s performance is particularly notable given its relatively low BET surface area (9.1950 m²/g) compared to conventional adsorbents like activated carbon. This significant discrepancy between low surface area and high adsorption capacity strongly suggests that the process is not merely a function of physical surface area but is predominantly driven by highly specific chemical interactions between tamoxifen and the hydroxyapatite surface. The hydroxyapatite structure provides abundant calcium (Ca²⁺) and phosphate (PO₄³⁻) sites, which are known to facilitate ion exchange, surface complexation, and electrostatic interactions with organic contaminants, explaining the high uptake despite the modest surface area. The pHpzc of 7.60 and the pKa of tamoxifen (~ 8.5) provide a clear mechanistic explanation: at alkaline pH (e.g., pH 10), the adsorbent surface becomes negatively charged, electrostatically attracting the protonated tertiary amine group of tamoxifen. This is further supported by the enhanced adsorption efficiency observed at higher pH and temperature.

The thermodynamic parameters confirm an endothermic and spontaneous process, driven by an increase in entropy. This entropy gain is likely due to the displacement of water molecules from the hydroxyapatite surface upon tamoxifen adsorption, a phenomenon that becomes more favorable at elevated temperatures.

Compared to other adsorbents reported in the literature, such as activated carbon from hemp roots (qmax = 120 mg/g) and synthetic hydroxyapatite (qmax = 90 mg/g), calcined bone char demonstrates a competitive maximum capacity (149.25 mg/g) derived from the Langmuir model. Its primary advantage lies in its sustainability and cost-effectiveness, as it is synthesized from abundant animal waste, offering a circular economy solution to waste management and water treatment.

Preliminary experiments with real wastewater (COD: ≈200 mg/L, pH: 6.5–7.5) showed a similar adsorption trend, though the efficiency was slightly reduced to ~ 95% under optimal conditions, likely due to competitive adsorption from the organic matrix. This indicates a promising potential for real-world applications, albeit with a need for further optimization for complex matrices. However, challenges such as competitive adsorption in complex effluents and long-term regeneration strategies need to be addressed. Future research employing advanced spectroscopic techniques (e.g., FTIR, XPS) could further elucidate the specific interactions, such as surface complexation or ion exchange, to optimize the adsorbent’s performance and facilitate its scaling for industrial wastewater treatment.

However, for full-scale application, challenges such as competitive adsorption in complex wastewater matrices and the development of efficient adsorbent regeneration strategies need to be addressed. Future studies should focus on evaluating the effect of common competing ions (e.g., Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻) and exploring regeneration methods using suitable eluents.

Conclusion

The study demonstrates that calcined bone char is a highly effective and sustainable adsorbent for the removal of tamoxifen, a cytotoxic pharmaceutical contaminant, from aqueous solutions. Batch experiments revealed an impressive adsorption capacity of 98.30 mg/g under optimal conditions (initial tamoxifen concentration of 40 mg/L, adsorbent dose of 20 mg/50 mL, pH 7, and 25 °C). The Langmuir isotherm model (R² = 0.9900) best described the adsorption process, indicating monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface, primarily driven by interactions with hydroxyapatite’s calcium and phosphate groups. The pseudo-second-order kinetic model (R² = 0.9997) provided an excellent fit, with a calculated equilibrium adsorption capacity (qe = 98.30 mg/g) closely matching experimental values (qe = 96.33 mg/g), confirming chemisorption as the dominant mechanism. Thermodynamic analysis further revealed a spontaneous (ΔG° = -24.77 to -27.89 kJ/mol), endothermic (ΔH° = 37.15 kJ/mol), and entropy-driven (ΔS° = 0.208 kJ/mol·K) process, with adsorption efficiency improving at higher temperatures. The pHpzc value of 7.44 suggests optimal adsorption in slightly alkaline conditions (pH 7.54–8.5), where electrostatic attraction between protonated tamoxifen and the negatively charged adsorbent surface enhances uptake. These findings highlight calcined bone char’s potential as a cost-effective, environmentally friendly solution for mitigating pharmaceutical pollution in wastewater, supporting sustainable water management goals. Future research could focus on elucidating specific adsorption mechanisms, such as surface complexation or ion exchange, and exploring the scalability of calcined bone char-based treatment systems for industrial applications.
