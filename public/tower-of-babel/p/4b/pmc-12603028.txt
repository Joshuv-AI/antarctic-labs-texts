Recommendations for studying the association of the cancer diagnosis to treatment interval with overall survival: a modified Delphi process
Authors: Matthew Jalink, Will D. King, Benjamin O. Anderson, Rinku Sutradhar, Marie Louise Tørring, Michael D. Brundage, Patti A. Groome, Kelvin K. W. Chan, Robin Urquhart, Yingwei Peng, Antoine Eskander, Surbhi Grover, Michael J. Raphael, Richard Grieve, Cassidy Laub, Christopher M. Booth, Timothy P. Hanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe duration from diagnosis to primary treatment initiation (DTI) is an important interval for patients with cancer, as delayed treatment has been found to be associated with heightened recurrence rates and worsened survival. Studying the association between DTI duration and overall survival (OS) is biased and confounded by clinical triaging, heterogeneous definitions, and variation in analytic approaches.ObjectiveTo develop consensus-based guidance for conducting studies investigating the association of DTI duration and OS.MethodsA multidisciplinary panel was recruited to participate in a three-round modified Delphi approach to develop consensus recommendations on the best methodological practices when studying the association between DTI duration and OS.ResultsThe Delphi panel consisted of 15 experts in the fields of epidemiology, biostatistics, health services research, oncology, and health policy. A list of 24 recommendations with accompanying elaborations was generated including variable definition and measurement, cohort creation, confounder control, pertinent biases, analytic techniques, and balanced interpretation of results.ConclusionProviding valid evidence of the DTI effect on OS requires careful approaches. This paper offers recommendations on how to improve methodological quality. This will ensure that future studies effectively contribute to evidence-informed practice decisions on appropriate waiting times for patients with cancer.

Introduction

The time duration from diagnosis to treatment initiation (DTI), also referred to as the treatment interval in the Aarhus statement [1], is a crucial care interval for patients with cancer. Studies dating back to the mid-1990s have reported on the issue of surgical and radiotherapy treatment delay (prolonged DTI) for a number of cancer sites [2, 3]. The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the issue of treatment delay as many countries delayed cancer treatments because health system resources were reallocated to combat the pandemic [4–6]. The initiation of cancer treatment of patients can be delayed due to patient factors (e.g., delay of treatment due to social reasons), disease factors (e.g., additional pathology and imaging tests), and system-level factors (e.g., delay in specialist consultation and referral). Delaying treatment initiation has been observed to have adverse effects and the need for more extensive, toxic, and expensive treatments due to disease progression while waiting for treatment [7–9]. Summarized in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, delaying cancer treatment initiation has also been observed to be associated with worse survival in some settings [7].

Studies on the association of DTI duration and survival typically focus on overall survival (OS) given the wide availability of this measure in administrative data and the importance of this measure to patients, policy makers and practitioners. Studying the association of prolonging the DTI duration and OS has been restricted to observational research designs due to ethical concerns. However, these observational studies are challenging to conduct due to the inherent potential for bias and confounding. Studies include heterogeneous cancer types and patient populations where treatment pathways, prognostic factors, and the underlying effects of DTI duration on OS differ. The available literature on this topic has limitations and emphasizes the need to standardize methods for such studies [7, 10–12]. Existing approaches are inconsistent, employ a variety of observational study designs, and use heterogeneous methodologies with respect to defining patient populations, determining DTI duration and OS, data sources, managing confounding, and analytic techniques employed [7, 10]. Key outcome measures such as cause-specific death or recurrence are often missing. The quality of treatment (e.g. treatment completion) is not typically considered, though this is a major issue in the global context. The nature of the relationship between DTI duration and OS (e.g. continuous vs categorical or linear vs non-linear), and justification of the analytic model by verifying the appropriate assumptions (e.g., linearity assumption in Cox proportional hazards regression) is uncommon.

The literature investigating associations between DTI duration and cancer-related outcomes is challenged with potential biases. A major concern is the waiting time paradox, a phenomenon where those with advanced symptomatic disease are diagnosed and treated more quickly (resulting in a shorter DTI duration) but nonetheless have poorer outcomes due to late-stage disease (a form of confounding by indication) [7, 10]. If not addressed in the design or the analysis this phenomenon biases hazard ratio effect estimates (comparing long versus short DTI) downwards and may contribute to many of the null findings (i.e., no observed association between DTI duration and cancer-related outcomes) in the literature [7, 10]. Many studies attempt to control for relevant prognostic factors but do not conduct an analysis/sensitivity analysis to exclude patients with short DTIs (e.g., <2 weeks) or acute poor outcomes (e.g., death within two to four weeks of diagnosis) to mitigate confounding by indication due to the waiting time paradox [7].

Concerns of immortal time bias [13], are rarely addressed. Immortal time bias is a misclassification that arises when DTI duration, which is a time-varying exposure, is treated as fixed. It occurs when the patient’s person-time is counted according to their eventual DTI duration rather than their DTI duration at each individual follow-up. This misclassification results in subjects being immortal prior to their DTI duration and biases effect estimates for DTI duration downwards (long versus short DTI). Relatedly, survivorship bias [14] occurs when selection into the cohort is dependent on receiving treatment before death, and biases effect estimates towards the null when comparing long versus short DTI [15].

Different cancer tumor types and subtypes differ with respect to the urgency of initiating primary treatment initiation and thus have different implications for the significance of delay. Aggressive cancer sites such as pancreatic cancers progress rapidly and need to begin treatment sooner while for other cancer sites such as ER+ Luminal A breast cancers, the initiation of primary treatment is less urgent. Consequently, the methodological considerations and the potential impact of DTI on OS differ by cancer site and subtype.

Guidance for conducting studies examining the association between DTI and overall survival are needed to address potential bias, improve methodological rigor and ensure readily comparable results. Our objective was to develop consensus-based guidance for conducting studies investigating the association of DTI duration and OS with an observational study design.

Methods

Diagnosis to treatment interval duration and overall survival study domain development and Delphi structure

This study followed established guidance on consensus statement development employing a modified Delphi process (Fig. 1) to gather and refine epidemiologic and biostatistical principles and approaches to conducting studies of the association of DTI duration and OS [16]. The Delphi process is a methodology that incorporates relevant opinions and views from experts with knowledge of a particular topic through a systematic iterative process [16]. It facilitates consensus building and prioritization of issues and recommendations [16].Fig. 1Outline of Delphi process to develop recommendations for studying the association of the cancer diagnosis to treatment initiation duration with overall survival.

Outline of Delphi process to develop recommendations for studying the association of the cancer diagnosis to treatment initiation duration with overall survival.

Preceding the Delphi study, a broad-based survey was conducted to provide initial input on DTI and OS study methodology, and identify any pre-existing recommendations. It was distributed using the secure survey software and data repository (REDCap) housed at Queen’s University [17]. This strategy has been shown to be effective in previous guideline and best practices development [18]. A rigorous systematic review and meta-analysis [7] done by our team identified high-quality DTI duration and OS studies and was supplemented by an updated search capturing recent publications. Methodologies employed by these studies were used to populate the initial baseline survey to identify areas of high disagreement for the steering committee (composed of MJ, TH, WK, CB, MB) to focus on, as well as areas of high consensus that required less attention during the Delphi process. The survey asked respondents to select appropriate method(s) from a list of DTI and OS methodological domains, which included the option to add methods not previously provided. The study steering committee met to refine, word, and describe elements to be implemented into the subsequent Delphi process.

The initial survey targeted international content experts identified through personal and professional networks, and by contacting authors of high-quality publications in the field. The snowball technique was also used, asking individuals to recommend other experts within their network to participate, so a total denominator could not be calculated. The survey was also circulated via the Canadian Centre for Applied Research in Cancer Control [19] via email. We defined a content expert as someone with formal research training and experience in areas including but not limited to epidemiology, biostatistics, oncology, and health services research. Previous research in this area was not a prerequisite for participation.

Following the initial survey, a three-round modified Delphi process was employed. Three rounds were chosen a priori in an attempt to mitigate participant fatigue and retain participants while achieving stability in responses. Considering that the consensus statement was targeted towards researchers conducting treatment delay studies, as well as knowledge users tasked with applying study findings, the Delphi panel was constructed by recruiting 15 leading experts (minimum of 2–3 individuals per speciality area) in disciplines related to conducting treatment delay studies, and who had experience conducting research in this area. The study’s steering committee expertise included epidemiology, health systems research, radiation oncology, medical oncology, and Delphi methodology. To this, we added panelist members who were clinician scientists in the areas of radiation oncology, medical oncology and surgical oncology, as well as epidemiologists, biostatisticians, health services researchers, and health policy makers. These panelist members were recruited using personal and professional networks as well as interested responders of the initial baseline survey to ensure adequate representation and a discipline, career stage, and gender diverse group. The Delphi process began with a synchronous meeting to orientate the panel to the project and explain the study procedure. Questions or concerns related to the study processes were addressed.

Delphi round 1: asynchronous vote

Panelists were asked to complete an online, asynchronous, open-ended questionnaire on the most appropriate methodology to study the association of DTI duration and OS [20]. Informed by the previous survey, the questionnaire included the aforementioned methodological domains from the baseline survey and other domains not previously identified. Members only responded to questionnaire domains related to their area(s) of expertise. The Delphi round questionnaire was distributed using the same secure survey software and data repository REDCap [17]. Similar to the preceding survey, panelists had the option to add additional questions or domains to consider and were asked to rate the level of importance for each domain element on a 5-point Likert scale (Essential, Highly Preferable, Preferable, Optional, Not Necessary). Panelists were also provided two case-study examples (curative cervical cancer (stage I-IVA) and curative breast cancer (stage I-III)) in which to apply the methodological concepts. Results were collated in preparation for Delphi Round 2.

Delphi round 2: synchronous meeting

The second Delphi round involved two synchronous meetings of the panelists remotely (over Zoom (Version 6.5.9)) in order to accommodate participating members (Fig. 1). Feedback from one respondent unable to attend the larger team meeting was elicited in a separate synchronous meeting with the study team. Each candidate element within each methodological domain was first reviewed by the group by presenting questionnaire responses from Delphi Round 1, focusing on elements with discordant views. This was followed by an open discussion. Voting and endorsement of the candidate elements occurred asynchronously following the live discussion to allow panelists time to reflect on the meeting’s proceedings. We defined consensus as ≥75% agreement between panelists on domain elements. Essential elements were initially defined as those with 75% of members rating this element highly important (essential or highly preferred), and preferable elements defined as either 50–74% of members rating this element essential or ≥75% of panelists selecting “preferable” option [18]. After group discussion, these distinctions were thought to have low utility and were not used to present the results. Percent agreement was chosen instead of kappa statistics because kappa statistics become unstable with skewed distributions, and we did not expect agreement due to chance [21, 22]. Endorsed elements from this meeting were compiled into a post meeting ratification survey and sent to the panelists.

Delphi round 3 – post-meeting ratification survey

Each endorsed element from the live meeting in Delphi Round 2 was discussed by the steering committee for final wording. A brief final questionnaire was circulated to the Delphi panel members to determine level of agreement (yes/no) with the final wording of each domain element and final comments (Fig. 1).

Results

Updated search, preceding survey results, and Delphi panel formation

The updated search to our previous systematic review and meta-analysis identified no additional methodological approaches to supplement those previously identified from the high-quality systematic review and meta-analysis [7]. However, a systematic review by Drosdowsky et al. [15] identified five major methodological issues in DTI duration and OS research using colorectal cancer as a case-study that informed our study. These included: i) arbitrary categorization of DTI intervals, ii) lack of confounder adjustment, iii) paucity of conceptual frameworks and rationale for confounder adjustment, iv) the absence of adjustment or consideration for immortal time bias and v) confounding by indication [15].

The initial baseline survey elicited 29 responses from a diverse group of content experts representing relevant areas of expertise (epidemiology, biostatistics, medical oncology, radiation oncology, surgical oncology, health services research), health system and country contexts, and career stages. The survey identified multiple methodological domains employed to answer the same research question where there was high disagreement on what constituted a valid approach. Examples included defining homogenous cohorts, defining index date of follow-up, and the management of major biases (e.g., the waiting time paradox, immortal time bias). Additional domains of interest were also identified including considering the longitudinal nature of the patient’s cancer journey and the quality of cancer treatment received.

The Delphi panel consisted of 15 content experts representing 10 institutions spanning 5 countries. Panelist expertise included epidemiology, biostatistics, health services research, medical oncology, radiation oncology, surgical oncology and health policy, as well as previous experience conducting cancer DTI duration and OS studies in different cancer site, treatment modality, and health system contexts.

Delphi round 1 results

The first asynchronous survey was distributed to all 15 Delphi panelists with a 100% completion rate. All cancer DTI duration and OS methodological elements were voted on, and no additional elements were suggested for inclusion at this stage. Elements with high disagreement included: i) the measurement of OS and definition of the index date of follow-up, ii) accounting for the longitudinal nature of a patient’s journey, and iii) accounting for the effects of the COVID-19 pandemic on patient treatment and follow-up.

Delphi round 2 results

During the second Delphi round, live debate and discussion focused on areas of high disagreement, including defining homogenous patient populations, considering downstream covariates along the longitudinal nature of a cancer patient’s journey, accounting for the effects (i.e., different treatment indications, treatments received, data quality, and pathways of care) of the COVID-19 pandemic, the quality of cancer treatment provided, and establishing the index date of follow-up/measurement of OS. Some elements from Delphi Round 1 were consolidated, as they referred to the same underlying concept (restricting analysis groups to a single cancer site and characterizing cancer treatment modalities broadly became defining homogeneous patient populations). Another element was removed because it was considered vague and not specific to DTI duration and OS studies (‘Data used must be of high quality in order to adequately define your cohort and address confounding concerns’). Additional elements suggested for inclusion included not defining the cohort based on future events and responsible interpretation of findings. The Delphi Round 2 vote was completed by 14/15 (93%) of participants.

Employing a landmark analysis addresses this issue by selecting a minimum survival time for patient inclusion in the analysis that is greater than the maximum DTI duration such that all patients in the analysis have survived long enough to receive treatment [32]. The landmark date also represents the maximum DTI duration. If individuals did not receive treatment at this time it is considered the same as if they had received treatment after the landmark date (over the maximum DTI duration) and are not eligible to be included in the analysis. As a result, patient treatment receipt is known at the beginning of patient follow-up, reducing concerns of confounding by indication. It is important to note that findings of the landmark analysis apply only to the subgroup of patients surviving to the landmark time. Alternatively, a time-varying DTI duration variable can be employed such that a patient’s DTI duration is correctly classified at each time point, regardless of future treatment receipt. However, if a time dependent covariate is used, there should be a high level of confidence in the candidacy of all patients in the cohort for treatment (not including patients who were never eligible for treatment), or else confounding by indication becomes a concern.4.2 Where survivorship bias is a potential concern, all patients satisfying the cohort definition (including disease-specific criteria and treatment intent) should be included and DTI duration should be treated as time-dependent variable.Survivorship bias occurs when selection into the cohort is dependent on receiving treatment before death [14, 15]. It is a concern when the survival of the target population is poor. Modeling DTI duration as a time-dependent variable allows for the inclusion of all patients in the target population regardless of their survival status or treatment receipt status.4.3 The quality of initial cancer treatment (i.e., was standard of care treatment provided?), including whether it was completed, is important to describe and account for in the analysis.Patients who receive non-standard or incomplete treatment regimens may experience poor survival due to the quality of the treatment, irrespective of the DTI [24]. This point relates to the previous recommendation (Recommendation 1.6) regarding homogeneous cohorts. In high-income country contexts, patients who do not receive treatment are rare and likely were not eligible for curative treatment. Toxicity or disease progression are common reasons for not completing treatment in this context. In low- and middle-income country contexts, the failure to complete treatment is not uncommon and may also be a result of patient and system-level factors unrelated to disease factors or treatment factors such as patient loss to follow-up, treatment abandonment due to financial cost, or unavailability of radiotherapy care to complete treatment (e.g. no brachytherapy for cervical cancer or machine breakdown) [24, 46]. Describing the quality of treatment and accounting for it in the analysis is thus important.4.4 To account for the distorting effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, the study period should be restricted to a date prior to the pandemic, or a sensitivity analysis should be conducted.The COVID-19 pandemic had substantial and long-lasting effects on health systems globally [47]. Significant impacts include treatment protocol changes, inconsistent diagnostic procedures compared to pre-COVID pandemic, and concerns of the quality and timeliness of data reporting, so we do not expect treatment delay during the COVID-19 pandemic to have the same effect on survival as treatment delay pre- COVID-19 pandemic. Discussion

We report a list of best-practice recommendations for conducting research on the association of cancer DTI duration and OS, endorsed with high consensus from a diverse, multidisciplinary expert panel. We employed a rigorous modified Delphi technique and systematic iterative process to derive our consensus recommendations. To our knowledge, this is the first reported study seeking to provide consensus guidance on the best methodological and analytical approaches focusing on this topic. Our findings are relevant to researchers, funding bodies, journal editors and reviewers, and clinicians seeking to better understand the quality of the literature studying the association between DTI duration and OS.

The recommendations complement related work by Weller and colleagues and help answer calls for improved methodological rigor in this field [1, 7]. We believe that if these recommendations are considered when designing a study of the association of DTI duration and OS, common pitfalls observed in this space such as the waiting time paradox and lead time bias can be effectively managed. Less than 1% (7/177) of studies captured in a systematic review by Neal et al. accounted for the waiting time paradox [10]. A recent scoping review of the methodological quality of the treatment-delay literature found that 27% of studies did not specify the index date of follow-up and only one study discussed the potential for immortal time bias affecting their results [11]. This work is intended to be applicable to different cancer sites, treatment modalities, and health system contexts. However, additional cancer-site specific and health-system dependent considerations may be needed. For example, in many developing country contexts, large proportions of patients are unable to complete treatment, or are lost to follow-up [24].

Broader research best-practices not specific to studying the association of DTI duration and OS were not explicitly stated in our recommendations (i.e., ensuring data is of high quality), but certainly should be followed. Previous studies have generally sourced their populations from health administrative data and applied Cox proportional hazards regression models, which we have considered in the development of our recommendations [7, 10].

We acknowledge that there may be other methodological elements and pitfalls not addressed in our recommendations, though we undertook a rigorous and systematic approach to minimize this risk. We also recognize that it may not be possible to be certain whether the above recommendations fully address valid concerns of confounding and bias, and appropriate interpretations are required. At the same time, we highlight that the two most important biases in this field of study, immortal time bias and the waiting time paradox, both bias results downwards, which may have considerable implications on clinical practice guidelines. When we are not certain whether there is a risk of worse survival with increased time from diagnosis to treatment initiation, but the risk is plausible, it is imperative that we act with caution. This precautionary principle should be applied to clinical practice guidelines where relevant [6]. We also emphasize that our methods recommendations are specifically for one aspect of the time between the development of cancer, its diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up. Though many of our recommendations hold relevance in studying other intervals such as the time from first contact with the health care system to diagnosis (diagnostic interval) [1], there are likely other considerations. For example, extent of disease prior to cancer diagnosis is rarely available, and identification of the first health care encounter related to cancer diagnosis has its own methodological considerations.

Likely due to logistical challenges, prospective study designs have rarely been conducted in this field of research and should be considered in future research, such as quasi-experimental designs. Prospective studies also have the ability to collect data on key prognostic factors often absent from administrative data sources (e.g., symptoms at presentation) and could study other outcomes such as quality of life where the literature is limited longitudinally. Though randomized trials of delay increasing the duration of the DTI duration are unethical, randomized trials investigating the impact of shortening the DTI duration compared to the standard of care DTI duration require consideration.

In the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, there will likely be an influx of studies in this area investigating the effect of delays on patient outcomes. In some cases, there were shorter DTI durations during this period but fewer patients presenting with cancer due to delays in diagnosis (time from initial symptoms to diagnosis) due to the COVID pandemic [25]. Further research into other outcomes associated with differences in DTI duration are needed. Additionally, healthcare systems operating in constrained resource environments will need to prioritize care effectively and efficiently. High-quality studies investigating the effect of delayed treatment (prolonged DTI durations) will be needed to provide decision makers the necessary evidence to make these decisions.

Conclusion

Studying the association between the DTI duration and OS is a complicated field of study and careful approaches to managing the many biases affecting the literature are needed to produce valid effect estimates. Following the recommendations made in this statement will help achieve this, improve methodological rigor in the field and contribute to evidence-based decisions on appropriate waiting times for people with cancer.
