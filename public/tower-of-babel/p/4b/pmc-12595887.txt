Determinants of households’ access to improved drinking water sources in Somalia: insights from the Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey (SIHBS) 2022
Authors: Hassan Abdi Ahmed, Dahir Abdi Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited access to improved sources of water is a worldwide apprehension, predominantly in lower-middle-income nations with limited infrastructure. Limited and unsafe drinking water contributes to poor health outcomes, mainly among children. To safeguard the health and well-being of the community as a whole, it is essential to have access to safe drinking water. In Somalia, rural households have more limited access to drinking water fetched from improved sources compared to those living in urban areas. This research intended to analyse access to better drinking water sources for households and associated determinants, utilizing the 2022 Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey (SIHBS) data.MethodThe research employed the SIHBS 2022 dataset, a nationally representative dataset. To provide a concise summary of the findings, descriptive statistics were performed. The relations between the explanatory and predictor variables were studied utilizing the Pearson chi-square test. In contrast, a multivariate logit regression model was utilised to determine whether risk factors are significantly related to the criterion variable (water sources). The study analyzed the SIHBS 2022 dataset, which contains 7212 sample households, for its representativeness.ResultsThis study found that 77.1% of Somali households have access to better sources of drinking water. The gender of the head of household (AOR: 2.230, 95% CI: 2.005–2.472), education status of the family head (AOR: 2.938, 95% CI: 2.083–4.142), household size (AOR:1.472, 95% CI: 1.326–1.633), place of residence (AOR: 2.367, 95% CI: 2.095–2.675) as well as the region of the respondent (AOR: 1.903, 95%, CI: 1.368–2.645), were all statistically significant predictors associated to household’s access to better-quality sources of drinking water.ConclusionThis article examined the determinants that impact access to potable water from better sources. While overall national access to better-quality water is good, a gap between urban and rural water access exists. To ensure that households headed by females, particularly in rural settings, have adequate access to water from improved sources, the government and its value development partners should develop policies to inform the community about the benefits of improved water sources, thereby reducing WASH-related health problems.Keywords: Household access, Drinking water sources, Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey

Introduction

In 2010, the United Nations recognized that each and every human being had the basic right to have access to drinking water and sanitation [1]. It encouraged countries to make more efforts to guarantee that all people have access to drinking water and sanitation that is safe, clean, accessible, and reasonably priced [2]. There were 489 million people all over the world in 2020 who were unable to get facilities that provided improved potable water, including water sources that were able to provide safe water because of the way that they were designed and constructed. Among these individuals, 122 million people relied on surface water for their drinking needs. Surface water might come from sources like waterways, damming, ponds, lakes, streams, or irrigation canals [3]. Limited, unimproved water supplies and sanitation facilities lead to bad health conditions, particularly among children, such as skin illnesses, acute respiratory infections, diarrheal diseases, mortality, and other conditions [4]. Reliable access to clean and safe water is a basic human right and a major public health issue, chiefly in lower-middle-income nations with undeveloped infrastructure. This phenomenon is especially prevalent in countries with inadequately advanced water supplies [5]. For the sake of public health and community well-being, access to safe water for drinking is essential [6, 7].

Limited WASH facilities remain the primary cause of illness and death in Sub-Saharan Africa, contributing to 60% of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and 53% of deaths [8, 9]. In order to promote universal healthcare principles such as quality, fairness, and dignity for all individuals, WASH programs should be improved [10]. Access to better-quality water is associated with fewer illnesses, fewer fatalities, and a decreased incidence of diarrhea in children under five. Alternatively, young children, predominantly those less than five years old, face a higher probability of passing away due to relying on unclean sources. Additionally, the absence, insufficiency, or poor management of water supplies can increase the risk of infections, including polio, cholera, schistosomiasis, typhoid, diarrhea, dysentery, and hepatitis A [11]. In lower- and middle-income nations, 62% of diarrhea fatalities are attributed to the unavailability of clean and safe drinking water [12]. A nationwide report studied in Sub-Saharan Africa in twenty-fourteen revealed regional disparities in the extent of household access to improved water drinking supplies [13].

Prior studies performed in Nepal and Sub-Saharan African nations found significant regional (rural-urban) differences in households’ access to such sources of improved drinking water [14]. These disparities might be justified by the reality that the bulk of people in Sub-Saharan Africa have lower incomes and live in rural settings. As a result, they do not have the financial means to buy better drinking water [13]. Somalia is undergoing rising urbanization, propelled by internal displacement as a result of conflict and drought. The 2015 UNICEF WASH KAP Survey reported that 35% of people have access to piped water. Many of the deprived populations in cities and peri-urban settings, particularly those who are considered to be Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs), depend upon tiny-scale water providers who sell water of inferior quality at an expensive rate. As a result, impoverished communities spend up to five times more for water. On the contrary, rural water initiatives have focused on restoring infrastructure [15]. Operational pastoral water supply systems are critically rare, leading to environmental degradation by foraging around present water sources [16]. In 2018, the Federal Government of Somalia shaped the country’s first national WASH policy [15], whereas the WASH Sector Strategic Plan intends to operationalize the Policy and NDP’s WASH sector components [15]. Water scarcity and insufficient infrastructure in Somalia are the outcome of a long civil conflict, which has been worsened by weak sector governance and a paucity of competent personnel. Approximately 19% of the total population has a piped source of water, whereas 40% of the population has access to water from improved sources [17].

Previous studies have identified several predictors of access to potable water from better sources, including gender [18], household heads’ age [19], the size of the household [20], education status [11], type of residence, and region of the respondent [21]. Not only does the head of the household recognize the need to use safe drinking water sources, but they also play a significant role in the practice of policy-making. The household head and a knowledgeable family member recognize the hazards linked to utilizing unclean water sources, so they are more probably to prioritize access to safe drinking water [18]. Additionally, the usage of unimproved water sources is importantly affected by gender dynamics. Male household heads often have greater power and control over resources, which can influence a household’s choice between improved and unimproved water sources [13].

In Somalia, there are momentous discrepancies in residence areas, with rural and nomadic households having access to safe drinking water throughout the dry season compared to urban residents [22]. Piped water into residences is the best source of drinking water throughout the wet and dry seasons, followed by tube wells/boreholes [22]. Piped water is available to the majority of urban residents, but less so in rural regions, whereas tube wells/boreholes are more widespread in nomadic communities. Unimproved sources are also exploited, but to a slighter extent [22]. Primarily, during the rainy season, nomads depend more than others on undeveloped and natural water supplies, including lakes, rivers, dams, and water catchments. The rural inhabitants and households often travel for a minimum of 30 min to acquire water [22]. There is a research gap in Somalia that uses nationally representative survey data, which might provide useful information to help efforts to improve drinking water sources. The 2022 Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey (SIHBS) report contains descriptive data but does not include inferential data. For that reason, the target of this article is to (i) describe the magnitude of access to better potable water among households in that year 2022, as well as (ii) identify the predictors that determine access to drinking water from better-quality sources during that year. This would allow stakeholders to identify the underlying reasons for inequalities, allowing national efforts to be focused on fixing such challenges.

Method and materials

Study context

The Republic of Somalia is roughly 637,657 square kilometers in size and is located in the Horn of Africa. Most of its geography is composed of plateaus, prairies, and hills. The coastline of this country is 3,333 km long, putting it among the most extended in Africa. The nation has daily temperatures ranging from 30 to 40 degrees Celsius, with swings in the hot environment. During the course of the last year, the nation has seen a decline in annual rainfall that is statistically significant. With Gu and Deyr being the rainy periods and Haga and Jilal being the dry times, it features a climate that is classified as having four distinct seasons. During the wet season, 77.7% of people in Somalia are able to utilize potable water services, but in the dry season, 74.7% do. Although there are notable variations depending on place of residence, over 60.1% of the population had access to properly regulated water. Solely 14.2% of nomads, whereas 51.2% of rural inhabitants have access to sources of water that are safely managed compared with 71.9% of urban dwellers. Piped water into dwellings accounts for 44.7% of improved drinking water, with tube-wells coming in second at 11.7% [22]. Non-governmental organizations and civil society groups played a vital role in providing WASH services to the public. It is a sector that coexists and interacts with the state, value development partners, and private enterprise. However, over the past decades, there has been plenty of progress in improving accessibility to water sources. These collaborators made pivotal contributions in coping with catastrophes and humanitarian situations around the country.

Data source and sampling design

Our research applied a cross-sectional method with a dataset from the 2022 Integrated Household Budget Survey (SIHBS) carried out in Somalia, to identify the predictors affecting access to potable water sources among households. The target population is all households in Somalia, while the study population consisted of 7,212 households to measure diverse indicators at the national, regional, and municipal levels of residence. This dataset represents households from all regions of Somalia, except for the Middle Jubba, for security reasons [22]. The 2022 SIHBS sampling frame utilized a stratified multiple-step probabilistic clustered sampling strategy. For cities and rural zones, a three-phase stratified sampling approach was employed, while nomadic regions adopted a two-step stratified clustered sample. Primary sampling units (PSUs) were designated based on the number of housing buildings within the frame. Secondary sampling units (SSUs) for rural and urban regions were determined based on the number of listed households. Finally, ultimate sampling units (USUs) were randomly selected from the listed households within the clusters across all regions.

The first level encompassed all households in selected urban, rural, and nomadic areas designated as Enumeration Areas (EAs), along with information on the household head. An electronic listing questionnaire was used to gather data on these households. After cleaning the data, a brief overview of households per EA was created to construct sample frames for the next step of sampling, where 12 households were chosen from each EA within each stratum. The primary focus of the interviews was the household head (HH) and their spouse. If none of them were obtainable, the most knowledgeable household member was selected based on age criteria (the older person in the household was interviewed) [22]. The sample was drawn from 601 EAs across the country, with each of the 17 study areas containing 35 sampled EAs. In total, 12 households were questioned in each EA, resulting in 420 households surveyed per region [22].

Study variables

Outcome variable

The response factor is measured as “households’ access to better-quality water sources for drinking.” In SIHBS 2022, the head of household was interviewed about their primary supplies for potable water, in addition to their access to better drinking water. A variety of sources of drinkable water comprised piped water supplied directly to the home, water that is piped to a yard or plot, piped water accessible to a neighbours, standpipes (public water taps), boreholes or tube wells, protected wells, protected springs, or rainwater collection systems. A dichotomous variable was created, coded as (Yes = 1 for households with access to improved drinking water sources and No = 0 for those without such access). Household heads who responded affirmatively (Yes = 1) to any of the better drinking water source alternatives were classified as having access to better-quality drinking water sources [21].

Independent variable(s)

During the literature review, significant predictors for accessing better water sources for drinking were reported, including the gender of the household head (1 = male, 2 = female) [11], the age of the household head, the educational status of the household head (no formal education, primary, secondary, and university) [20], and household size [23]. In terms of measuring poverty, three components are required. First, there must be a measure of welfare. Second, a poverty line must be set in order to assess whether a household is poor or not. Finally, an aggregate poverty measure is needed [24]. In our study, the variable of poverty status was extracted from the SIHBS as a binary variable (0 = non-poor, 1 = poor), while the place of residence was originally divided into three categories (rural, urban, and nomadic) [22]; however, during data manipulation, rural and nomadic were combined into a single category labeled as “Rural area” [13]. Regarding region, we grouped them into seven categories because of the sensitivity and wide range of options [25]. Additional possible predictor variables available in SIHBS 2022 (for example, the distance of the source of water from the family, the time (in minutes) to get water and return, the kind of vessel utilized to collect water, and the main mode of transport for water collection) were excluded from the analysis due to significant missing values.

Operational definition

Drinking water refers to water utilized for domestic purposes, such as drinking, cooking, and maintaining cleanliness [11]. A piped water supply into the residence, piped water to a plot or yard, a public tap or standpipe, a tube well or borehole, a protected dug well, a protected spring, rainfall and protected spring, and rainwater collection are the water sources that are likely to be of appropriate quality, often known as sources that have been “improved.” An unprotected dug well, an unprotected spring, a cart with a small tank or drum, a water tanker-truck, bottled water, surface water, a water catchment, and other sources of water that meet the criteria for the term “unimproved” are all regarded as likely to be “unimproved water sources” [3, 17, 26, 27]. Limited access to better drinking water service refers to potable water sourced from an improved supply that requires more than 30 minutes to collect, including any waiting time [28]. In contrast, basic access to potable drinking water service is defined as drinking obtained from a better source with a fetching time of less than 30 minutes, including queuing [3].

Data management and statistical analysis

Within the analysis, a sampling weight was applied to address the survey’s inconsistent sample distribution over strata and locations, confirming that the data accurately represented the population. Subsequently, descriptive information was generated, including weighted frequencies and corresponding percentages, to portray the research population. To explore associations among each predictor and the output variable, the Pearson chi-square was utilized. In the multivariate logit regression model, variables with a p-value of less than 0.20 from the chi-square test were further analyzed [18]. The adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with a confidence interval of 95%, and a p-value < 0.05 were utilized to indicate the significant associations with the outcome variable. Lastly, the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was run to assess multicollinearity based on the designated predictor variables. The data were ultimately analyzed with STATA version 17.

Association between predictors of household access to an improved drinking water source

A chi-square test (χ2) was applied to explore the linkage between households’ access to drinking water from improved and unimproved sources and several independent variables, including the gender, the age, the education status of the head of household, household size, poverty status, place of residence, and region. An association was regarded statistically substantial when the p-value of the chi-square statistic was below 0.20. Table 2 demonstrates that the variables of gender, education level, place of residence, and region were statistically significant linked with access to potable water sources at the 0.20 significance level. The findings indicate that these factors impact households’ access to water from better sources.

Table 3 exhibits the findings of the multivariate logit regression analysis, indicating that several predictors are significantly linked with access to better drinkable water among families. These predictors comprise the gender of the head of household, education status, household size, place of residence, and region. Notably, female-headed families were 2.230 times more chance to access qualified drinkable water compared to male-headed households (AOR: 2.230, 95% CI: 2.005–2.472). Family heads with formal education status were greater probability to access potable water. Those with primary education had an odds ratio (AOR: 1.534, 95% CI: 1.322–1.781), implying that they were 1.534 times more likely to access these sources than their uneducated counterparts. The odds of households headed by individuals with secondary education (AOR: 1.847, 95% CI: 1.459–2.338) were 1.847 times more likely to have access to better water sources compared to families headed by individuals with lower levels of education. Likewise, those with a university education had an odds ratio of 2.938 (AOR: 2.938, 95% CI: 2.083–4.142), which means they were twice as likely to access enhanced water sources as their counterparts. Overall, as household head education rose, getting to drinking water from improved sources also increased. In comparison, households with more than six members had an odds ratio (AOR:1.472, 95% CI: 1.326–1.633), making them 1.472 times more chance to have access to better sources than those with fewer or equal to six members. Urban households were considerably 2.367 times more probability to have getting to potable water from better sources than those living in rural areas (AOR: 2.367, 95% CI: 2.095–2.675). In terms of region, households in groups 2, 3, 4,5 & 7 have considerably less chance to access better sources of drinking water compared to group 1, which is the reference category. Discussion

This research intended to explore the predictors related to families’ access to potable water sources, utilizing a nationwide representative dataset. This study discovered that 77.1% of Somali families have access to better-quality drinking water. It’s also equivalent to the reports published by most of the nations in Sub-Saharan Africa [9, 20]. In part, the observed progress in Somalia’s access to enhanced water sources may be ascribed to the beneficial impacts of government activities that are aimed at increasing the number of communities that have access to clean water [16]. These efforts aligned with the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 6, which strives for universal access to clean water by 2030 [29]. In multivariate logistic regression analysis, female-headed families had a 2.230 times greater likelihood of having access to potable water compared to families led by males (AOR: 2.230, 95% CI: 2.005–2.472). This finding is compatible with an earlier study [30]. In Africa, women and girls devote a greater proportion of their time to collecting water than men and boys. In particular, women collect water for a longer period than their counterparts [9, 31]. In Africa, women rely heavily on water and sanitation services for daily activities, including cooking, washing, and childcare [31, 32]. Regarding educational status, the household heads with a primary school education had an odds ratio (AOR: 1.534, 95% CI: 1.322–1.781), meaning that they had 1.534 times greater likelihood to have access to better drinking water than those without education. This finding is similar to previous research indicating that households headed by those with a primary level had access to drinking water from better sources than those without formal education [18], because they had more opportunities to get paid jobs, for greater access to such sources to potable drinking water [9]. In contrast, family heads with a second education level have a greater likelihood of access to potable water, with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR: 1.847, 95% 1.459–2.338). Likewise, the odds ratio for household heads who have graduated from university (AOR: 2.938, 95% CI: 2.083–4.142) indicates that they were 2.938 times more likely to have access to drinking water from better sources than those without education.

As the educational status of the family heads increased, access to qualified drinking water among families also increased. This conclusion was strengthened by a prior study that found household heads with higher educational accomplishment have greater opportunities for paid jobs and financial resources, resulting in access to better drinking [33]. People with lower levels of education could be less aware of how to preserve existing sources of drinking water and make changes to improve water quality. This outcome is aligned with previous studies done in sub-Saharan Africa [27, 34]. A variety of reasons contribute to this trend. Education raises awareness and understanding about the advantages of having obtainable drinking water that is of better quality, such as reduced health risks and environmental sustainability. Educated household heads are more likely to understand the long-standing cost savings and health merits of consuming clean water sources as opposed to unimproved water sources, such as polluted water. Households with six or more members (AOR: 1.472, 95% CI: 1.326–1.633) were more chance to have access to improved drinking water sources as contrasted to their counterparts. This finding is corroborated by a previous study [11].

Households residing in urban areas were more likely to have access to drinking water derived from qualified sources than those in rural parts, owing to financial restrictions that limit rural communities’ access to improved drinking water; instead, they tend to increase water consumption in agriculture (AOR: 2.367, 95% CI: 2.095–2.675). This result was maintained by other earlier studies [7, 26, 35]. We consolidated regions into groups due to sensitivity and a huge number of options. Households in group 5 were more likelihood to get potable water (AOR: 1.903, 95% CI: 1.368–2.645) compared to group 1, while those households in groups 2, 3, 4, 6 & 7 had a lower likelihood of having access to such a source of high-quality drinking water compared to group 1. Group 2 (AOR: 0.464, 95% CI: 0.365–0.591), group 3 (AOR: 0.576, 95% CI: 0.441–0.755), group 4 (AOR: 0.728, 95% CI: 0.553–0.958), group 6 (AOR: 0.203, 95% CI: 0.158–0.262), group 7(AOR: 0.209, 95% CI: 0.168–0.260).

Study strengths and limitations

The key contribution of this research is the practice of the SIHBS 2022 dataset, a nationally representative dataset, which allows for generalization of results that provide weighted statistics on the determinants linked with family access to potable water derived from improved sources. Since the SIHBS dataset lacked a household wealth index, we utilized household poverty status to assess a household’s economic condition. The research article has some restrictions: First, the conclusions are grounded on data at the cross-sectional level, limiting the capability to determine causal relations. Second, this study does not capture all possible predictors from the SIHBS dataset connected with improved drinking water sources due to missing values.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study has far-reaching implications despite the immediate availability of drinkable water collected from qualified sources. It emphasizes that gender, the educational status of the family head, the size of the household, residence place, and region had a significant impact on households’ access to drinkable water from better-quality sources. Grounded on these conclusions, the study suggests the following recommendations: first, the Somalia Water for Resilience Project, also known as Barwaaqo in Somali, should prioritize access to water obtained from better sources for rural families and those with large family sizes. Addressing gaps in household access to such sources, particularly in rural sites, will provide drinkable water. Second, the Barwaaqo intervention would provide support to female-headed families in accessing better-quality water sources, as they are among the most vulnerable. Third, the government and its partners should initiate education interventions to inform the community about the benefits of improved water sources to minimize WASH-related health problems and achieve the SDGs (Goal 6). Finally, the government should develop WASH intervention policies to track the effectiveness of such programs as well as the quality of drinking water in Somalia.
