The role of 3D printing in skeletal muscle-on-a-chip models: Current applications and future potential
Authors: Wei Bao, Junyan Liu, Chengcheng Du, Senrui Liu, Jiacheng Liu, Zhenglin Zhu, Liangbin Zhou, Zhong Alan Li, Wei Huang, Yiting Lei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Organ-on-a-chip (OoC) systems can simulate the key functions of human organs, combining microfluidics, cell culture, and biomaterials. 3D printing can be integrated into these technologies to facilitate the construction of OoC models. The high precision and layer-by-layer fabrication process of 3D printing not only enables the creation of complex structures for the microfluidic chip but also improves the cellular microenvironment within the chip by harnessing bioinks for 3D bioprinting. In recent years, OoC models established with 3D printing technology have successfully replicated the functions of various native organs, significantly advancing disease research and drug development. However, due to the complex anatomical structure and unique physiological functions of skeletal muscle, the application of 3D printing in skeletal muscle-on-a-chip (SMoC) models remains relatively limited. Based on existing research on engineered skeletal muscle and OoC, this review discusses the construction of SMoC models by 3D printing to recapitulate the anatomical structure and physiological functions of skeletal muscle. Furthermore, it explores the different applications of 3D printed SMoC models and the future challenges and prospects in this field.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle is the primary effector organ of human movement, responsible for various vital functions, including maintaining posture, generating heat, protecting essential organs, and regulating metabolism [1]. Skeletal muscle is composed of multiple muscle fibers and fascicles, with each muscle fiber containing numerous myofilaments [2]. These myofilaments enable muscle contraction through a sliding mechanism, thereby generating movement. Skeletal muscle disorders refer to diseases that affect the function, structure, or metabolism of skeletal muscle. The etiology of these disorders is complex and can result from various factors, including genetic defects, abnormal immune responses, metabolic disturbances, drug side effects, and aging [3]. Due to the intricate structure and function of skeletal muscle, as well as the diversity of causes underlying skeletal muscle disorders, traditional diagnostic and therapeutic approaches often struggle to effectively address these issues.

In this context, organ-on-a-chip (OoC) technology, which integrates microfluidics, bioengineering, and cell biology, has shown tremendous potential [[4], [5], [6]]. OoC technology enables the construction of in vitro miniature organ models that closely mimic the structure and function of native organs. Therefore, the development of skeletal muscle-on-a-chip (SMoC) models provides a new platform for studying the structure and function of skeletal muscles and opens new avenues for research on skeletal muscle disorders and personalized treatments [7]. On July 7, 2025, the FDA hosted a hybrid seminar focused on reducing animal testing, emphasizing the implementation of non-animal testing methods such as OoC, organoids, and artificial intelligence (AI) computational models. This initiative highlights the growing importance of SMoC as an in vitro model for studying skeletal muscle diseases.

It is worth noting that current OoC technology can be integrated with 3D printing technology in many aspects. 3D printing, also known as additive manufacturing, constructs three-dimensional (3D) objects by adding materials layer by layer [8]. The development of this technology in the biomedical field, particularly its application in regenerative medicine, has driven the rise of bioprinting. Bioprinting uses live cells and biomaterials as “bioinks” to construct biomaterials, tissues, and even organ models under precisely controlled conditions. In the construction of SMoC models, 3D printing technology can be used for the fabrication of the microfluidic device and the tissue components within the chip [9,10]. In the latter case, 3D bioprinting is utilized.

3D printing offers numerous advantages in the construction of SMoC models. First, 3D printing technology allows for precise control over materials deposition and cell arrangement, providing a unique advantage in building hierarchically structured skeletal muscle tissues in SMoC models [11]. In addition to traditional 3D printing techniques used for the design and fabrication of the microfluidic chip, various biomaterials can be combined with living cells and bioprinted via 3D bioprinting to construct organ models with biological functionality within the chip [12]. Furthermore, 3D printing technology can create the required cellular microenvironment for SMoC models and offer flexibility for adjustments based on experimental needs [13]. These features make 3D printing not only an invaluable tool for faithfully recapitulating skeletal muscle structure and function in basic research, but also an enabling technique in the study of skeletal muscle-related diseases, including genetic disorders (e.g., Duchenne muscular dystrophy), drug screening, metabolic conditions, and the interactions between skeletal muscle and other organs under pathological conditions.

Currently, the application of 3D printing-based OoC technology in the musculoskeletal system has primarily focused on the bone and cartilage tissue chips [14,15], while discussions and reviews focusing on SMoC remain relatively limited. In this review, we focus on SMoC models constructed using 3D printing technology and provide a detailed discussion of the current applications of these models. We also analyze the challenges faced by 3D printing in the construction of SMoC models, including issues related to material selection, cell culture optimization, and the validation of chip functionality. Finally, we explore the potential and future development trends of 3D printing technology in SMoC research, proposing possible solutions and technological breakthroughs to further advance the development of this field.

Structure and functions of skeletal muscle

Prior to the development of SMoC models, a comprehensive understanding of the anatomical structure and physiological functions of skeletal muscle is imperative. Skeletal muscle is composed of muscle fascicles, which are made up of muscle fibers. Muscle fibers are the primary structural and functional units of skeletal muscle and essentially represent skeletal muscle cells (also known as myocytes). Each muscle fiber contains many myofibrils, and myofibrils are composed of sarcomeres. Beyond muscle fibers, skeletal muscle comprises connective tissue formed by fibroblasts, as well as blood vessels, nerves, satellite cells, immune cells, and various stem cells [16,17] (Fig. 1A). Muscle contraction is a complex and finely tuned process, primarily relying on the mechanisms of muscle contraction, which include the transmission of nerve signals, calcium ion release, the sliding filament theory, and the cycle of muscle contraction and relaxation [[18], [19], [20]]. In addition, the structure of skeletal muscle exhibits significant differences under various pathological conditions [22]. For example, with the aging of skeletal muscle, a series of metabolic dysregulations and mitochondrial damage lead to changes in the muscle's ultrastructure [23]. Specifically, these changes can manifest as motor unit remodeling, a reduction in satellite cells, increased intermuscular fat, muscle fibrosis, and the loss and atrophy of muscle fibers (Fig. 1C) [24]. Furthermore, ultrastructural changes in aging skeletal muscle also involve mitochondrial structural damage. This damage primarily affects the integrity of the mitochondrial membrane and results in abnormal alterations in the morphology of mitochondrial cristae, such as disordered arrangement, reduced numbers, and even the disappearance of some cristae. These changes not only reflect the decline in mitochondrial function but also contribute to a decrease in the energy metabolism capacity of skeletal muscle cells, thereby accelerating the process of muscle aging (Fig. 1D) [25].

Skeletal muscle, as an important tissue in the human body, performs various physiological functions, including movement, maintaining posture, thermogenesis, protecting vital organs, and regulating metabolism [26]. Therefore, when constructing SMoC models, it is essential to focus on simulating these physiological functions. Muscle contraction and extension, which generate movement, are among its most important physiological functions. When building a SMoC, it is necessary to design an environment that can simulate mechanical tensile forces to promote muscle cell contraction and extension [27]. Muscle contraction is typically controlled by signals from the nervous system. Thus, the incorporation of nerve cells and their integration with muscle cells is crucial in SMoC models [28]. Skeletal muscle's metabolic functions include the demand for nutrients such as oxygen, glucose, and fatty acids. The chip system should ensure effective nutrient delivery and waste removal mechanisms [29]. Microfluidic systems can be used to simulate blood flow, helping to supply nutrients and remove waste, thereby maintaining normal muscle cell metabolism [30]. Skeletal muscle activity generates heat. Therefore, the chip system should also consider how to simulate this process during its design, such as through temperature sensing or external heating methods, to test the thermogenic response of skeletal muscle [27].

Organ-on-a-chip

OoC is a microfluidic technology-enabled platform that simulates the function of human organs or their specific structures. It recreates the physiological functions and microenvironments of real organs by culturing living cells within miniature chip structures and providing necessary nutrients through microfluidic channels/chambers [46]. The emergence of OoC technology originated from early research in organ models, which involved the cultivation of stem cells to form 3D tissue structures in vitro [[47], [48], [49]]. These early organ models were used to simulate the morphology and function of human organs for applications in disease modeling, drug screening, and regenerative medicine [7,50,51]. However, traditional 3D organ models, which are often limited in their cell types and physiological relevance, struggle to replicate the complex structures and true microenvironments of native organs, resulting in various drawbacks for practical applications. To address these shortcomings, OoC technology, with more refined structures and, in many cases, a microfluidic circulation system, has been developed [4,52].

To date, OoC technology has made significant progress in the construction of models for various organs, including the lung, heart, liver, kidney, intestine, and brain [[53], [54], [55], [56], [57], [58]]. These OoC systems provide more accurate physiological models for research in the respective fields. However, despite the widespread application of OoC technology across multiple organ systems, research specifically focusing on SMoC models remains relatively limited. Therefore, with rapid advancements in OoC technology, it is essential to further design and construct specialized OoC platforms tailored to recapitulate the structure and physiological functions of skeletal muscle, in order to meet the growing demands for skeletal muscle-related research.

Construction of SMoC

The essential steps of OoC construction are (1) the selection of cell sources, (2) the fabrication of the microfluidic device, (3) the establishment of the on-chip culture conditions, and (4) improving or adapting based on the research objectives [14]. To create SMoC platforms, design strategies that are aligned with the characteristics of skeletal muscle need to be adopted. First, the structure of the microfluidic device needs to be rigorously designed to meet the biological and biomechanical requirements of skeletal muscle. The device has to incorporate and provide microenvironmental support to relevant skeletal muscle cells [59]. Additionally, dynamic on-chip culture conditions need to be established to align with the metabolic characteristics of skeletal muscle [4,46,60]. Through these steps, the preliminary design of the SMoC is completed. Subsequently, it is crucial to comprehensively assess the SMoC system's ability to simulate the native structure and physiological functions of skeletal muscle, which is often followed by chip optimization [9,61]. Cell sources

Currently, the cell sources used in OoC research are diverse, allowing for the use of various cell types in model construction [5,51,62]. Different cell types offer distinct advantages depending on the application (Table 2). Primary cells are directly sourced from the body, retaining the natural physiological characteristics and functions of in vivo cells, thus directly reflecting the functional properties of the tissue. However, they have poor proliferative capacity and significant heterogeneity in organ models due to individual differences in the source [63]. Cell lines, which are immortalized cells, overcome the limitations of primary cells’ proliferative ability, offering high stability and convenience for long-term research. However, immortalization and prolonged culture may alter the natural characteristics of cells, and some cell lines lose their in vivo functions. Additionally, the types of commonly used cell lines are limited, potentially failing to fully simulate specific tissues or diseases [64]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are among the most commonly used cell sources for organ models, with the potential to differentiate into multiple cell types. They also have significant immunosuppressive and regulatory capabilities, making them a focus in cell therapy. MSCs can be extracted from various tissues such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord, and dental pulp. However, MSCs are sensitive to the in vitro environment, and strict control of the extracellular microenvironment is required when using MSCs as a cell source for organ models [65,66]. Moreover, MSCs exhibit limited differentiation maturity, making it challenging to form typical myofiber structures in SMoC applications [67]. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are a promising cell source for OoC models, as they can be reprogrammed from patient-derived somatic cells, making them particularly suitable for studying diseases with significant individual variability. For SMoC models, the proper function relies on the precise cooperation between various cell types, including myocytes, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and neurons. When selecting cell sources for constructing SMoC models, the choice is typically guided by the research objective: primary cells are preferred for high-fidelity muscle function reconstruction; cell lines for high-throughput and standardized platforms; MSCs for tissue repair and multi-organ interaction studies; and iPSCs for personalized and disease modeling [69,70]. For example, Neto E and his team used 3D printing technology to create a microfluidic-based neuromuscularized skeletal chip, simulating the interaction between bone, nerve, and blood vessels in the skeletal microenvironment (Fig. 4a). [71]. The primary aim of this study was to recapitulate bone–nerve–vascular interactions within the bone microenvironment; therefore, mouse primary osteoclasts and bone marrow stem cells were selected as the main cell sources.

Once the cell source is selected, further chip design can be supported by biological 3D printing. Using live cells from the chosen source as bioinks for bioprinting not only allows the creation of cell scaffolds with specific shapes but also facilitates the simultaneous printing of multiple bioinks to achieve various cellular interactions [[72], [73], [74]]. This approach has already been widely applied in the engineering of skeletal muscle tissue, which will be discussed in detail in the following sections.

Chip holder construction

The microfluidic device holder of an OoC is generally composed of biocompatible materials [46], and once the materials are selected, the chip's design needs to be developed. OoC models typically adopt a multi-layered design, with each layer simulating different physiological conditions. For instance, the chip may include upstream and downstream nutrient supply channels, an intermediate cell culture layer, and chambers that simulate various tissue environments [75]. In some designs, gas channels are also incorporated to simulate the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide [27,76]. The surface of the chip is usually functionalized to promote cell attachment and growth [77]. Different cell types are introduced into separate chambers or channels to simulate the structure of the organ [[78], [79], [80]]. For example, in a lung OoC, alveolar epithelial cells might be cultured in one chamber, while inflammatory macrophages are placed in another chamber to simulate the response of alveolar cells to anti-inflammatory drugs under inflammatory conditions [81].

Due to the intricate fiber arrangement, unique mechanical properties, and complex interactions with the surrounding environment, skeletal muscle tissue requires more stringent and complex design requirements for the construction of the SMoC holder. As a result, 3D printing technology provides crucial technical support for the construction of the SMoC holder. There are three main approaches to using 3D printing for constructing the SMoC holder. The first method involves directly using industrial-grade 3D printing to create a chip structure. This approach allows for more precise chip structural design and enables the adjustment of physical properties such as hardness and strength according to requirements [11]. However, this method is not suitable for large-scale production of chips, which increases the cost of repeated experiments [82]. The second method uses bioprinting technology to assist in constructing a cell-containing scaffold [74]. The cell-containing bioink is essentially a multicomponent aqueous mixture, which can simulate a 3D culture environment and auxiliary cell localization during the chip fabrication process. It is suitable for building soft tissue OoC models, such as those of the liver or lungs [83]. Moreover, due to the high biocompatibility of the bioink materials used in 3D bioprinting, they can be employed for surface modification of chip scaffolds, enhancing their suitability for cell growth. However, this technology has some limitations, such as the low mechanical strength of materials like hydrogels, which may not be suitable for long-term use. Additionally, printing precision and cell viability can be limited in some cases. The third method for constructing the OoC body is to first print a predesigned scaffold mold using a material with sufficient strength, then to inject hydrogel or polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) with excellent biocompatibility into the mold. After sufficient molding, the soft chip scaffold is created by casting the mold. This approach uses soft materials for the printed molds, which can be reused multiple times [84]. In the selection of raw materials for chip scaffolds, 3D printing technology also demonstrates significant diversity and advantages. Researchers can choose various types of biocompatible materials based on specific experimental requirements to ensure good compatibility and safety with cells [85]. Furthermore, materials with responsive characteristics can be selected, enabling the scaffold to undergo specific changes in response to external stimuli such as temperature, pH, or mechanical forces, thus mimicking the dynamic environment of real tissues [86]. Additionally, materials with excellent mechanical properties are widely applied to provide sufficient support and elasticity, allowing the scaffold to withstand biomechanical forces and maintain structural integrity [87]. This diverse selection of materials enables researchers to flexibly design and customize scaffold structures to meet the needs of specific experiments and applications. For example, Agrawal and his research team used two bio-inert hydrogel columns surrounding a gel network filled with cells to induce uniaxial cell alignment and serve as anchor points during the formation and maturation of encapsulated cells and muscle tissues [9]. Moreover, extracellular matrix obtained via decellularization techniques also serves as an excellent source of 3D printed biological scaffolds [88]. Li Wan and his team employed thermoresponsive polymers and micro-milling techniques to construct a template in a 3D-synthesized muscle extracellular matrix (ECM), integrating immortalized mouse myoblast cell line C2C12 into a 3D collagen matrix, thereby restoring vascularized features of skeletal muscle tissue with layered structures (Fig. 4b) [89]. 3D printing not only offers a wide range of material choices but can also be integrated with other fabrication techniques, such as electrospinning. Common strategies for combining these two technologies include layer-by-layer integration and post-processing coating. In the former approach, 3D printing and electrospinning are alternated to build a multilayered structure, whereas in the latter, a scaffold is first fabricated via 3D printing and subsequently coated with a nanofibrous membrane produced by electrospinning. This hybrid strategy enables the construction of complex architectures while simultaneously enhancing the microenvironment for cell growth [12,90].

On-chip culture conditions

The on-chip culture conditions are the core components of OoC systems, used to simulate physiological processes such as blood flow, gas exchange, and nutrient delivery within the body [7,46,76]. Typically, multiple channels are designed in the chip to replicate different tissue environments. For example, a liver OoC may include several channels to simulate interactions between blood flow, bile ducts, and hepatocytes [91]. To accurately mimic the in vivo environment, OoC systems are often equipped with systems that can regulate environmental factors such as temperature, pH, and oxygen concentration.

Compared to other tissues and organs, skeletal muscle has high metabolic and oxygen demands, leading to its highly vascularized structure with dense capillary networks [1,18,29]. Additionally, skeletal muscle is innervated and transmits signals through neuromuscular junctions (NMJ), with cell behavior being regulated by both mechanical and chemical signals [28,92]. These unique features of the skeletal muscle microenvironment create a need for dynamic balance between mechanical forces and metabolic support, making it highly sensitive to electrical stimulation and mechanical stretching. This complex microenvironment presents a significant challenge in constructing SMoC systems. To overcome this challenge, a multi-channel microfluidic system must be developed to provide dynamic nutrient and waste exchange through separate channels, further simulating the in vivo environment [93]. For instance, Dornhof and his team developed a microfluidic OoC platform that simulates cancer disease, allowing for the control of culture conditions including hypoxia and the monitoring of metabolic dynamics over a week. The microfluidic OoC platform designed in his study integrates a full array of electrochemical, chemical, and biosensors for energy metabolites such as oxygen, lactate, and glucose. By real-time monitoring of the consumption and production rates of metabolites, the system can quantitatively capture responses to changes in culture conditions and exposure to cancer drugs. This OoC precisely replicates the complex tumor microenvironment through dynamic, real-time monitoring and control of metabolic parameters. The study provides an innovative platform for investigating stem cell behavior, drug screening, and personalized medicine (Fig. 4C) [94]. Similarly, skeletal muscle exhibits a high metabolic rate and substantial oxygen demand. Therefore, the sensor designs employed in this study for monitoring oxygen and metabolic substrates can provide valuable insights for the development of SMoC. By appropriately adjusting the culture conditions and sensor parameters, it is possible to design SMoC models that more accurately recapitulate the skeletal muscle microenvironment.

Improvements for skeletal muscle

The most significant feature of skeletal muscle, compared to other organs, is its motor function. Reproducing muscle movement on an OoC essentially involves simulating the entire process from the neural impulse to muscle contraction and relaxation, which undoubtedly presents a challenge in constructing a SMoC [1,17,61,95]. The mechanical characteristics of skeletal muscle include its contraction force, elasticity, compliance, fatigue properties, and stretching and contraction speed. Understanding these mechanical properties is crucial for the design of SMoC systems [1,17,96].

Cardiac and skeletal muscles share several structural and functional similarities, such as being striated muscles, exhibiting excitation-contraction coupling, and generating rapid and forceful contractions. Therefore, advances in engineered cardiac tissues and heart-on-a-chip may offer valuable insights for the design and optimization of skeletal SMoC systems. In early cardiac organ research, to quantify contraction force, the beating of the heart was recorded as a video through a microscope, and motion vector algorithms were used for analysis [97]. This provided information commonly used in traditional cardiomyocyte studies, such as contraction amplitude, beat rate, and contraction speed. However, since the cells are not fixed in a single plane, the position of the tissue in the chip shifts with each heartbeat, reducing the accuracy of this quantification method. The 3D-printed chip scaffold facilitates the stabilization of skeletal muscle tissue and enables the quantification of muscle contractile force. The research team of Grosberg assembled a chip for analyzing the contractility of both striated and smooth muscle by planting cells onto a 3D printed organ chip, demonstrating the possibility of analyzing both types of muscle contraction on the same chip [27]. In addition to the aforementioned mechanical characteristics of skeletal muscle movement, its electrophysiological properties are also an essential part of constructing a SMoC. For skeletal muscle, its electrophysiological properties include a series of complex processes such as resting membrane potential, action potential, excitation-contraction coupling, and electrophysiological coupling [98]. For SMoC, it can be regarded as a skeletal muscle tissue where muscle cells are arranged by a special culture method. The key challenge is to convert electrical signals into biological signals within this tissue to replicate the normal electrophysiological characteristics of skeletal muscle. Currently, electrostimulation-responsive biomaterials based on 3D printing are the most direct approach to achieving this feature [99]. Mendes and his team designed a GelMA/GO hydrogel system loaded with cells, which, through the application of different electrical stimulation protocols, utilizes the inherent electrical characteristics of the biomaterial scaffold to regulate cell behavior [100]. In the study by Kalmykov's team, a 3D self-coiling biosensor array, including active field-effect transistors or passive microelectrodes, was used for three-dimensional interface coupling with human cardiac spheroids. These arrays are capable of performing continuous and stable multi-channel field potential recording with high sensitivity and spatiotemporal resolution, and also support simultaneous calcium imaging. This approach enables electrophysiological research and monitoring of complex signal transduction in organ models (Fig. 4D) [101].

Moreover, skeletal muscle is not an independent organ; its proper function requires interaction with other tissues and organs to form a complete physiological system, the musculoskeletal system [102]. Within the musculoskeletal system, skeletal muscle works in coordination with bones, joints, the nervous system, and the vascular network to perform a range of complex functions such as movement, support, and metabolism [103]. Therefore, in the study and construction of SMoC systems, a key concept needs to be introduced: constructing a basic functional unit to simulate its structural and functional characteristics in vivo. Subsequently, through the complex combination of multiple such functional units, a complete organ model can be gradually formed. Furthermore, this OoC complex, composed of multiple functional units, can interact and integrate with other types of functional complexes to enable multi-organ interactions within the chip [15,103]. In this process, the application of 3D printing technology provides crucial support. With its high precision, high reproducibility, and capability to construct complex structures, 3D printing makes the fabrication of basic functional units and the realization of multi-unit combinations possible. In Martin and his team's study, primary embryonic motor neurons and neonatal myoblasts were isolated from rats and co-cultured in a 3D-printed tissue-engineered fibrin hydrogel, successfully simulating the neuromuscular junction [104]. In a study by Morimoto et al. neuromuscular junction formation was promoted in a 3D independent tissue structure composed of skeletal muscle fibers and motor neurons. In this study, the researchers first placed a mixed muscle cell matrix into two 3D-printed chip molds. After the formation of cell aggregates, the molds were removed, and muscle fiber bundles were induced. Then, neural spheres derived from mouse neural stem cells were placed onto the muscle fibers. By altering the composition of the culture medium, the muscle and nerve tissues were integrated (Fig. 4E) [105].

In summary, 3D printing plays an important role in the construction of SMoC systems, including aspects such as cell sourcing, chip fabrication, and improvements specific to skeletal muscle chips. 3D printing technology not only enables the precise printing of compatible chips, providing the foundational physical platform for SMoC, but also allows the construction of 3D cellular culture models through 3D bioprinting. Additionally, 3D printing can reproduce the complex hierarchical structure of skeletal muscle tissue using precise spatial layering techniques, ensuring that cells within the chip exhibit physiologically relevant distribution patterns [106]. Through these contributions, 3D printing is not merely a tool in the construction of SMoC systems, but one of the core driving forces advancing the entire field. The following sections will provide further insights into 3D printing technology and review current applications of 3D-printed SMoC systems.

3D printing for OoC

3D printing technology is an advanced manufacturing method that utilizes computer control to create three-dimensional objects by sequentially adding material layer by layer [8,[106], [107], [108]]. Unlike traditional subtractive manufacturing methods such as cutting, grinding, etc, 3D printing builds the desired object from scratch by progressively stacking materials. The main advantages of this method are its high precision and flexibility, enabling the production of complex shapes and structures without the need for extensive molds or tools [11,109].

As mentioned earlier, the current application of industrial-grade 3D printing in OoC systems primarily involves the construction of the chip body or molds. This process can employ various industrial-grade 3D printing methods. The most commonly used industrial-grade 3D printing methods in the medical field are fused deposition modeling (FDM) and stereolithography (SLA) technologies [110]. These technologies are also applied in several areas, including the customization of personalized medical devices and prosthetics, drug formulation and controlled release, and the customization of surgical implants. FDM technology works by heating plastic materials until they melt and then depositing them layer by layer to form the desired shape. It typically uses biocompatible materials to provide sufficient structural support, making it suitable for fabricating basic scaffolds. Due to its low cost, this method is particularly well-suited for rapidly prototyping chips and conducting design testing [111]. SLA technology, on the other hand, constructs objects by curing liquid photopolymer resin layer by layer using ultraviolet (UV) laser. It offers higher printing precision and surface finish, making it ideal for applications requiring fine details. Due to the smooth surface produced by its liquid molding process, SLA is particularly suitable for manufacturing chip molds, facilitating subsequent casting processes [112].

To meet the demands of biological sciences and the medical field, 3D bioprinting technology has been developed to better align with the needs of biological research [113]. The core principle of 3D bioprinting is to use computer control to layer biological materials such as hydrogels, bioinks, or cells and growth factors, following a digital model, to form 3D structures [74,108,114]. The main types of 3D bioprinting technologies include inkjet-based bioprinting, extrusion-based bioprinting, light-cured bioprinting, and magnetic-driven printing [115]. Currently, 3D bioprinting is mainly applied in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, such as skin and soft tissue repair, cartilage and bone tissue regeneration, and organ model printing [116]. Additionally, 3D bioprinting holds vast potential in disease modeling, drug screening, personalized medicine, and biomanufacturing (Fig. 5) [117]. In current OoC research, while traditional industrial-grade 3D printing technologies can be used for the construction of the chip body, 3D bioprinting technology can be applied to cell positioning within the chip, 3D cell culture, and surface modification of the chip. 3D bioprinting has also seen extensive research in the field of organ modeling and OoC development [118]. Depending on the organ type, different 3D bioprinting methods can be flexibly selected. Engineering skeletal muscle based on 3D printing

Currently, the application of 3D printing technology in skeletal muscle-related research is primarily focused on the development of engineered skeletal muscle [137]. Engineered skeletal muscle refers to muscle tissue constructed in vitro under laboratory conditions using tissue culture and bioengineering techniques. These engineered muscles are created by seeding muscle precursor cells and stimulating their differentiation and organization to form structures and functions resembling natural skeletal muscle. The concept is similar to that of skeletal muscle OoC systems, both aiming to simulate the structure and function of natural skeletal muscle in vitro [138]. The key difference lies in the focus: engineered skeletal muscle emphasizes the restoration of natural skeletal muscle, while muscle-on-a-chip systems not only replicate the structural and functional aspects of natural muscle but also utilize microfluidic systems and intricate structures of the chip to further simulate the physiological environment of skeletal muscle and enable integrated studies with other tissues.

In current tissue-engineered skeletal muscle research, electrospinning is one of the most commonly used biotechnologies in addition to 3D printing. The combination of 3D printing and electrospinning can further enhance the surface bioactivity of the printed materials and effectively guide the orderly alignment of cells. For example, the Miji team combined electrospinning with 3D printing technology to design an engineered skeletal muscle with muscle tissue characteristics and microvascular-like structures. In this study, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were used to simulate angiogenesis within muscle tissue and to induce myogenic expression in C2C12 cells. Electrospinning promoted the transformation of HUVECs into elongated microvascular-like structures and enhanced the alignment of C2C12 cells, as well as the expression of myogenic gene markers (Fig. 6A) [139]. Electrospinning technology guides cell alignment by constructing ordered nanofiber structures, while other electrically-based technologies regulate muscle cell alignment through electric field forces, thereby promoting the formation of engineered skeletal muscle. In a subsequent study by the Miji team, a novel cell printing technology based on electrohydrodynamic direct writing (EHD-DW) was developed. This technique uses alginate as the bioink component for 3D bioprinting, with the addition of fibrin to enhance its biological activity. HUVECs were then loaded into the bioink and printed using EHD-DW to improve myogenesis in the C2C12 myofiber structure. The study also compared this new printing method with the earlier electrospinning approach. The results indicated that the engineered skeletal muscle constructed using this method exhibited a more pronounced synergistic effect between myogenic and endothelial cells, promoting muscle tube maturation. Additionally, the muscle regeneration effect was validated through a volumetric muscle loss model (Fig. 6B) [140]. In another study, the WonJin team designed an engineered muscle tissue based on bioink 3D printing, utilizing Au nanowires in conjunction with an applied electric field to accelerate cell alignment and simulate the electrical properties of muscle tissue. Due to the excellent conductivity and stability of Au nanowires, the electrostatic force generated by the applied electric field not only guided the alignment of muscle cells but also promoted the directional arrangement of the Au nanowires themselves, resulting in engineered skeletal muscle with enhanced cell viability and myogenic expression. Compared to conventional cell-loaded structures, the engineered skeletal muscle designed in this study exhibited significantly more oriented actin fibers and formed multinucleated myotubes. In vitro experiments showed that the aligned gold nanowires successfully facilitated muscle tissue regeneration in collagen-based bioink (Fig. 6C) [141].

Recent advances in electric field-assisted 3D printing have improved the fabrication of engineered skeletal muscle, leading to the development of dynamic 4D printing strategies. 4D printing is an emerging concept based on 3D printing technology, which adds the dimension of time, meaning that objects can undergo changes in shape, structure, or function in response to external stimuli (such as temperature, humidity, electric fields, etc.) after printing is completed. In short, 4D printed objects not only have a three-dimensional structure but also possess the ability to “deform” or “self-repair” under specific conditions, enabling dynamic responsiveness. In a study by the Gi Hoon team, an electric field-assisted 3D/4D bioprinted engineered skeletal muscle model was designed. The study exposed GelMA fibers loaded with C2C12 cells to electric stimulation, inducing cell alignment, and utilized the swelling properties of GelMA to roll up the cell-laden fibers, thus simulating the natural structure of skeletal muscle tissue. This 4D tissue-formed engineered skeletal muscle not only exhibited a tubular structure similar to natural skeletal muscle but also promoted the proliferation of myoblasts and myogenic differentiation (Fig. 6D) [142].

SMoC research based on 3D printing

At present, despite the widespread development and research on engineered skeletal muscle models, the progress of SMoC remains relatively limited. Currently, the construction of SMoC is relatively simple, typically involving the placement of two columnar structures on the chip to simulate the attachment sites of tendons at both ends of the muscle. Precursor muscle cells are then coiled with biomaterials in a specific manner between these two columns to form muscle bundles [143]. Although the development of SMoC is still in early stages, insights from engineered skeletal muscle research and advanced manufacturing technologies can be incorporated to enhance its development. For instance, the application of 3D printing technology has proven valuable. Gaurav and his team utilized photopolymerization-based 3D printing to construct a skeletal muscle chip for in vitro assessment of skeletal muscle tissue formation and repair. In this study, the skeletal muscle chip was composed of a microfluidic device with a central chamber, with a gelatin network loaded with cells fixed around two polyacrylamide (PAm) columns within the chip. The PAm columns serve both as anchoring points to induce skeletal muscle tissue alignment and as a means to apply elastic deformation and quantify muscle force. This study not only enabled the quantification and calculation of passive tension in the skeletal muscle chip but also further explored functional changes in skeletal muscle under injury conditions. The role of 3D printing in the construction of the chip was indispensable (Fig. 7B) [9]. Jaesang and his team also employed 3D printing technology to construct skeletal muscle chips. In this study, the printing method for the skeletal muscle OoC was the same as the molding approach mentioned earlier. They first designed a mold using SOLIDWORKS software, then fabricated the chip body using 3D printing and polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) molding. Subsequently, the research group utilized fluorescence lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM) to investigate the effects of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) on the skeletal muscle within the constructed in vitro chip. The results indicated that AGEs significantly altered the structure of the skeletal muscle tissue in the chip and impaired contraction function and cell viability. These findings provide an in vitro model for uncovering the mechanisms by which AGEs impair muscle function and structure, and offer guidance for therapeutic strategies to protect muscle health and improve the quality of life in affected populations (Fig. 7C) [145].

In the construction of SMoC, in addition to using 3D printing to fabricate the required chip structure, it is equally crucial to design auxiliary components and monitoring instruments that align with the physiological functions and culture conditions of skeletal muscle. The Rexius-Hall team developed an oxygen-controlled microphysiological system (MPS) based on 3D printed components to study paracrine interactions between skeletal muscle tissues under different oxygenation states. This MPS chip features a dual oxygen environment, allowing precise control of both hypoxic and normoxic states in skeletal muscle tissues and their paracrine interactions through a microfluidic device. This system provides a valuable approach for simulating the physiological functions of skeletal muscle at the chip level (Fig. 7D). [146]. In a recent study, Soochi and his team brought 3D printed skeletal muscle chips to the International Space Station National Laboratory to investigate the effects of microgravity on skeletal muscle OoC. The skeletal muscle chips were fabricated by 3D printing highly organized nanofibrillar collagen strips on a glass plate, followed by seeding primary human skeletal muscle cells onto these collagen strips to form muscle tissue. In this study, not only were the changes in muscle tissue in the SMoC under normal gravity and microgravity monitored, but the effects of two drugs—15-PDGH inhibitor (15-PDGH-i) and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1)—on microgravity-induced skeletal muscle were also screened using the chip. This innovative research explored the changes in skeletal muscle chips under microgravity and demonstrated their potential for drug screening, providing an important reference for future skeletal muscle chip research [147].

Based on the above, 3D printing (including bioprinting) plays a crucial role in the construction of SMoCs, particularly in chip holder fabrication, surface optimization, precise cell positioning, and the improvement of the culture environment. In addition to 3D printing, other technologies in OoC include soft lithography, electrospinning, microfluidic assembly, and cellular self-assembly. Integrating these techniques with 3D printing offers new strategies for the multidimensional construction of SMoCs. For example, Su's team combined 3D printing with electrospinning to develop an OoC model mimicking the muscle–tendon junction (MTJ). In this study, 3D printing was employed to fabricate the chip scaffold and connection structures, while electrospinning—using an alternating wet-dry method—induced aligned cell orientation. This approach successfully replicated the macroscopic M-shaped structure of the MTJ, providing valuable insights for the biomimetic reconstruction of skeletal muscle architecture (Fig. 7E) [148].

From the construction of engineered skeletal muscle to the research on SMoC, 3D printing technology has played a crucial role. Whether it is the application of traditional industrial-grade 3D printing in the construction of skeletal muscle chips or the use of bio-3D printing to guide the alignment of skeletal muscle cells by supporting live cells, these technologies have provided convenient and accelerated pathways for the further development of SMoC. As research continues to advance, 3D printing-based SMoC is expected to find applications in more fields, addressing current challenges related to skeletal muscle and associated diseases.

The applications of SMoC based on 3D printing

Currently, the application of SMoC primarily focuses on studying the structural and functional changes of skeletal muscle under disease or pathological conditions [149]. These changes in skeletal muscle structure and function are often closely associated with a range of skeletal muscle-related diseases, including those previously mentioned, such as muscular dystrophy, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, myasthenia gravis, inflammatory myopathies, and sarcopenia. These diseases not only impair the normal function of skeletal muscle but may also lead to a significant decline in mobility. The pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, and treatments of each disease vary, yet all involve cellular damage, functional decline, or metabolic dysregulation in skeletal muscle [150]. Therefore, based on these characteristics, SMoC can be developed and applied for research and therapeutic development purposes [151]. Disease modeling

SMoC models have been designed to recapitulate the structural and functional abnormalities observed in skeletal muscle disorders, such as the reduction in fiber number, diameter, and muscle contraction ability [17,26,70]. As mentioned earlier, skeletal muscle organ chips can precisely simulate the in vivo physical and mechanical environment of skeletal muscle through electrical stimulation, mechanical stretching, and other techniques. After constructing skeletal muscle disorder chip models under specific conditions, researchers can conveniently detect changes in the physicochemical properties of skeletal muscle in disease states and seek targeted solutions [152]. Alave and his research team, by improving 3D printing technology, developed a mini skeletal muscle organ chip containing human primary muscle precursor cells. They treated the muscle using muscle stimulants such as caffeine and Tirasemtiv, discovering that these compounds could acutely enhance the contraction force of the skeletal muscle model. The 3D bioprinting technique used in this study is a microvalve-based drop-on-demand (DOD) bioprinting approach. This new method enables the automatic construction of 3D human skeletal muscle organ models on chips [153]. Theo and his team used a chip system to assess the functional changes in skeletal muscle under different stress conditions. The study employed human primary muscle cells as the cellular source, with the chip body constructed from a stretchable PDMS scaffold and a biocompatible organic-inorganic hybrid light-structure material through SLA printing technology. During the fabrication process, the chip surface was micro-patterned to form longitudinal microgrooves, which facilitated the alignment of muscle fibers. Subsequently, the study designed a relatively standardized mechanical stimulation protocol based on previous experience to assess functional changes in skeletal muscle under different mechanical stresses. For skeletal muscle disorders caused by neurodegenerative conditions, such as myasthenia gravis and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, a SMoC model containing NMJ can be used to comprehensively study the functional changes and interactions between nerves and muscles under disease conditions [32,35,40]. Such an OoC model can simulate signal transmission between neurons and muscle fibers, synaptic function, as well as the structural and functional alterations under pathological conditions, providing an efficient and controllable in vitro platform to explore the pathogenesis of neuromuscular junction diseases [155]. Through such a model, the impact of neurodegeneration on muscle contraction dysfunction, impaired signal transmission, and muscle atrophy can be investigated [61,149]. Additionally, the OoC system can be used for screening and evaluating therapeutic drugs and intervention strategies for neuro-muscular diseases, such as molecular targets for synaptic function repair or neuroprotective agents. By simulating the neuromuscular interactions at different pathological stages, such a model aids in understanding the disease mechanisms, providing essential theoretical insights for clinical diagnosis and treatment [156].

The Virginia M team designed a chip system using iPSCs as the cellular source and incorporated anti-nicotinic acetylcholine receptor antibodies to successfully establish a myasthenia gravis model. The chip, constructed from PDMS, features two chambers, each loaded with human skeletal muscle myoblasts and motor neurons derived from iPSCs, effectively simulating NMJ. Further studies revealed that the functionality of the NMJ on the chip exhibited a dose-dependent change with increasing concentrations of anti-nicotinic acetylcholine receptor antibodies [157]. In this study, the chip system was also fabricated using a mold created by SLA, followed by PDMS casting. Specifically, SU-8 photoresist was patterned on 4-inch silicon wafers to generate microchannel structures using standard photolithography processes, including soft baking and UV exposure. SU-8 offers excellent chemical stability and enables submicron structural resolution, resulting in molds with high surface hardness and wear resistance, making them well-suited for repeated PDMS casting [158].

The Kazuki team designed a more refined neuromuscular chip that effectively integrates motor neurons with skeletal muscle systems. The chip system is based on a 24-well plate and features two chambers, one for housing motor neurons and the other for skeletal muscle cells. These two chambers are connected by a microtunnel that permits only axonal passage, allowing the motor neurons to transmit neural impulses to the skeletal muscle, triggering muscle contraction. Further studies demonstrated that this muscle contraction could be inhibited by tetrodotoxin or vecuronium bromide. The neuromuscular chip in this study more accurately replicates the complete muscle contraction mechanism triggered by neural impulses (Fig. 9C) [159]. In this study, the construction of the motoneuron compartment on the chip also involved the use of 3D printing-related techniques. Micro-patterned molds were fabricated on silicon wafers using SU-8 negative photoresist through standard photolithography processes, followed by PDMS soft lithography to replicate the desired microstructures.

Drug screening and evaluation

Currently, drug screening and evaluation for skeletal muscle disorders primarily relies on 2D cell culture and animal models. However, 2D cultures lack the three-dimensional architecture and mechanical cues necessary for proper muscle differentiation and function, while animal models often exhibit limited reproducibility and poor translational relevance due to interspecies differences. In contrast, SMoC platforms offer improved structural and functional mimicry, as well as controllable microenvironments, making them a promising alternative for more accurate drug screening and evaluation [72,156].

As previously mentioned, researchers can directly use primary cells derived from patients with skeletal muscle diseases to construct SMoC models. This approach not only enables the effective modeling of skeletal muscle disorders but also provides a reliable platform for drug screening [160,161]. Elena and her team employed this method by extracting primary myoblasts from both healthy donors and patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), successfully constructing an SMoC model of DMD. Using this chip platform, the researchers also validated the therapeutic effects of human mesoangioblasts on DMD. The results demonstrated that mesoangioblasts derived from the skeletal muscle vasculature were able to restore the expression and distribution of anti-atrophy proteins in the DMD SMoC model. This study provides valuable support for preclinical research and clinical trials for DMD treatment [162]. The chip system in this study was inspired by the 3D bioprinting approach developed by the Susi group. Specifically, the hydrogel substrate was fabricated via light-guided photopolymerization, followed by microcontact printing using a PDMS stamp to create micropatterned features. This strategy essentially represents a photopolymerization-based bioprinting method for constructing the main body of the chip [163].

Tatsuya's team designed a neuromuscular chip for drug screening in neuromuscular diseases. The chip consists of motor neurons coupled with skeletal muscle, with the NMJ interacting through a microfluidic device. This chip allows real-time imaging of axon growth, NMJ formation, and muscle maturation, as well as synchronized motor neuron activity and muscle contraction under optogenetic control. Subsequently, the study used patient-derived cells from individuals with sporadic amyotrophic lateral sclerosis to construct a disease model chip and explored its potential for drug screening and disease risk assessment (Fig. 9D) [164]. The chip molds in this study were similarly fabricated using SU-8 photoresist via high-resolution SLA printing. Following mold fabrication, all molds were treated overnight with (tridecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrooctyl)-1-trichlorosilane to facilitate the demolding of PDMS. PDMS was then used in the casting process to separately obtain the top microfluidic structure and the bottom pillar array [165].

Genetic research

For skeletal muscle disorders caused by genetic mutations, such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy, the common strategy is to repair the pathogenic gene responsible for muscle dysfunction through gene editing technologies [150,166]. SMoC platforms provide an ideal testing environment for gene editing by simulating the true physiological conditions of muscle tissue in a microphysiological system. Unlike traditional gene editing methods that rely on animal models to do gene knockouts or knock-ins, OoC systems enable gene editing experiments at the cellular level, offering more direct and efficient results [167]. This cellular-level gene editing not only reduces the time and ethical limitations associated with animal model experiments but also allows for precise control of the cellular environment, enabling a more accurate assessment of the gene editing effects on muscle cell repair and potential side effects [168,169].

Groen et al. combined 3D mold and shRNA technology to recapitulate the disease-associated pathology of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and limb-girdle muscular dystrophy type 2A (LGMD2A) in a chip system, caused by the loss of function of DMD and CAPN3, respectively. The study induced the loss of contractile function, disruption of tissue architecture, and disease-specific proteomic changes through shRNA-mediated knockdown of DMD or CAPN3. Additionally, in the DMD-deficient skeletal muscle chip model, candidate gene therapy using micro-dystrophin partially rescued the pathology, yielding results similar to those observed in animal models. This study demonstrates the feasibility of using SMoC technology for gene therapy applications [170]. The 3D models in this study were fabricated using Ecoflex, a two-component liquid silicone rubber that requires mixing and curing, making it unsuitable for conventional 3D printing. To construct the desired structures, an indirect molding approach was employed: positive molds were first created via 3D printing, followed by casting the mixed Ecoflex into these molds to form the final flexible 3D structures.

Similarly, OoC technology can also be used to study gene changes in skeletal muscle under different conditions [171]. Maddalena and his team compared the whole transcriptome differences of SMoC exposed to space and ground environments. In collaboration with NASA, the study integrated 16 SMoC into an autonomous CubeLab, which was sent to the International Space Station. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that exposure to spaceflight induced downregulation of genes associated with myosin heavy and light chains. This study uncovered age-related cellular and molecular changes in skeletal muscle chips under microgravity conditions, providing new insights into gene-level research using SMoC (Fig. 9B) [172]. In this study, chip molds were fabricated using SLA with Accura 60 resin. PDMS was prepared by mixing the elastomer base and curing agent at a 10:1 ratio, then poured into the 3D-printed molds containing 24 mm platinum electrodes. After curing, the PDMS structures were demolded and inlet/outlet ports were created using a 1 mm biopsy punch. Finally, the PDMS was bonded to glass slides of various sizes following plasma treatment, resulting in the assembly of complete microfluidic chip structures [173].

Multi-organ interaction

Skeletal muscle, as an effector capable of active contraction, works in conjunction with bones and joints to form the human musculoskeletal system [174]. Therefore, skeletal muscle disorders often involve other organs, such as bones and joints. Multi-organ chip models help us better understand the impact and interactions of skeletal muscle diseases on other organs. Currently, most multi-organ chip interaction studies focus on dual-chip systems, such as the synovium-cartilage organ chip used to study rheumatoid arthritis affecting both the synovium and cartilage [175,176].

When constructing multi-organ chips, a critical challenge is effectively connecting different organ chips to enable material exchange and functional interactions. Kacey and his team developed a multi-organ chip interaction system connected through vascular flow. In this study, individual organ chip systems for the human heart, liver, skeletal muscle, and skin were first constructed, and once each organ matured, they were linked through vascular flow containing circulating immune cells, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles, with the endothelial barrier separating the tissues from the vascular flow. The system utilizes modular chips made of polysulfone, with each chip featuring open channels that can be connected in any sequence to simulate various physiological scenarios. This study provides a crucial foundation for further investigating interactions between multi-organ chips (Fig. 9E) [177]. In this study, the chip structure and connectors were primarily fabricated using industrial-grade 3D printing and assembled with commercially available standard components. During the design phase, specifications of the commercial components intended for integration were systematically collected and analyzed. Based on these data, custom parts compatible with the assembly requirements were designed and subsequently fabricated using FDM technology.

The construction of multi-organ chip models also facilitates toxicological studies involving multiple organ systems. Aakash and his team developed a multi-organ chip model to investigate the effects of opioids on the nervous system, liver, heart, and skeletal muscle. For this study, the platform incorporated four cell types—hepatocytes, cardiomyocytes, skeletal muscle cells, and preBötzinger complex neurons—which were individually plated in their respective media to mature before being assembled into the multi-organ housing. This chip model enables the assessment of both the efficacy and off-target toxicity of naloxone in combination with opioids, providing clinically relevant functional data for the organs. This research expands the development and application of multi-organ chips [178]. In this study, the chip was fabricated using standard photolithographic patterning followed by precision processing with deep reactive ion etching (DRIE). DRIE is a highly anisotropic microfabrication technique capable of achieving etch depths ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers, making it well-suited for the creation of complex microstructures such as microchannels, micropores, and silicon needle arrays. Integrating DRIE with 3D printing can significantly enhance the resolution and structural precision of printed components, thereby contributing to the further optimization of 3D printing technologies [179,180].

In summary, SMoC platforms based on 3D printing technology are not only valuable for investigating skeletal muscle structural and functional abnormalities, but also hold broad applications in gene therapy, neuromuscular junction formation, disease modeling, drug screening, and studies of multi-organ interactions. In terms of 3D printing applications, SLA, a commonly used industrial-grade technique, is frequently employed to fabricate high-precision chip molds. In addition, bioprinting can be integrated to optimize cell-contact regions on the chip, thereby enhancing its compatibility for cell seeding. 10.13039/100014337Furthermore, the incorporation of advanced microfabrication techniques such as DRIE can further improve the resolution and structural fidelity of the chip, providing robust support for the reconstruction of complex physiological microenvironments.

Challenges of 3D printing in SMoC

3D printing technology has shown great potential in the application of SMoC, especially in the study of muscle-related diseases. It provides more accurate tools for disease modeling and drug screening [181]. By simulating the real structure and function of muscle tissues, 3D printing can replicate the biological characteristics of skeletal muscle at the micro level, enabling more effective research on muscle injuries, degenerative diseases, and other muscle-related disorders [182]. However, the widespread application of this technology still faces numerous challenges. Although current 3D printing technologies are capable of constructing millimeter-scale structures, their resolution remains inadequate for fabricating fine tissues such as skeletal muscle, which require cell-level alignment [[183], [184], [185], [186]]. Secondly, the biocompatibility and mechanical properties of current printing materials still need further improvement to ensure that the printed SMoC can accurately simulate the in vivo environment [46,60,82]. Additionally, the functionalization and dynamic simulation capabilities of the chip need enhancement. Future research should focus on better simulating muscle contraction, metabolism, and the interactions with other tissues [152,[187], [188], [189], [190], [191], [192], [193]].

Addressing these challenges requires improvements in printing performance, material selection, functional design, and evaluation systems (Fig. 10). First, in terms of printing performance, upgrading printing technologies or incorporating multi-nozzle systems can enhance resolution and speed, achieving high-precision SMoC structure construction more efficiently [[183], [184], [185], [186]]. Second, regarding material selection, develop materials with enhanced biocompatibility and mechanical properties, better mimicking the skeletal muscle microenvironment while supporting printability [[194], [195], [196], [197], [198], [199], [200]]. For functional design, the focus is on integrating dynamic simulation technologies to enable multi-organ interactions, essential for replicating physiological communication between muscle and other systems like nerves and vasculature [[201], [202], [203], [204], [205]]. Finally, the evaluation system of SMoCs must be refined. Meanwhile, the integration of 3D modeling and computer deep learning technologies will provide a significant boost to progress in this field [[213], [214], [215], [216], [217]]. Through interdisciplinary collaboration, utilizing deep learning to optimize the printing process, precisely predict tissue behavior, and simulate disease progression, research efficiency can be greatly enhanced, accelerating the translation of these advancements into clinical applications [218,219]. Therefore, overcoming these technological bottlenecks requires not only breakthroughs in specific fields but also collaborative innovation across disciplines, driving the practical application of 3D printed SMoC in disease research and treatment.

Prospects of 3D printing in SMoC

In the future, 3D printed SMoC is expected to achieve significant breakthroughs in various fields, particularly in precise modeling, personalized treatment, high-throughput screening, gene therapy testing, and clinical applications. With the continuous development of novel biomaterials, the improvement of dynamic microenvironment simulation technologies, the development of 4D printing technology and advances in AI data analysis, these chips will provide more accurate tools for the study of skeletal muscle diseases, aiding in the advancement of drug development and optimization of therapeutic strategies [[213], [214], [215], [216], [217],220,221]. Furthermore, the rapid development of personalized medicine and regenerative medicine will position 3D printed SMoC as a more practical and innovative research platform.

In the development of 3D technology, future advancements in 4D and even higher-dimensional printing technologies are expected [222,223]. As an extension of 3D printing, 4D printing technology not only enables precise construction in spatial dimensions but also allows for self-regulation and deformation in the temporal dimension [[224], [225], [226]]. The application of these technologies to SMoC systems holds the potential to simulate more dynamic and physiologically relevant skeletal muscle [227]. As this technology continues to mature, future SMoC systems will no longer be simple static models but will evolve into complex systems capable of self-regulation and adaptation to different environments and stimuli.

In terms of AI technology, the rapid advancements in deep learning will bring revolutionary impacts to the design of organ models and the 3D printing process [228,229]. With the automation design capabilities of AI, researchers can optimize the microstructure of skeletal muscle organs, achieving more biomimetic and complex organ models [230,231]. AI's real-time data analysis capabilities will refine the printing process by dynamically adjusting printing parameters to improve manufacturing precision, thereby reducing experimental errors and enhancing the functionality and reliability of the chips [[232], [233], [234]]. At the same time, AI-driven multi-organ chip research will provide new perspectives for disease studies by simulating the interactions of complex physiological systems, revealing the mechanisms of disease occurrence and progression [[213], [214], [215], [216], [217],220,221,232,235].

Current research on skeletal muscle OoC is still in its early stages, and further optimization of chip design, regulatory pathways, and achieving clinical translation remain significant challenges to overcome in the future. Continuously integrating advanced technologies with skeletal muscle OoC systems will be the key to future research. The integration of technology will not only significantly enhance research and development efficiency but also lead to widespread breakthroughs in the fields of precision medicine and regenerative medicine, promoting personalized treatment and early disease diagnosis [[236], [237], [238]]. Ultimately, multidisciplinary integration will enable more efficient and intelligent development of SMoC, making future medical care and treatments more precise and personalized, thereby contributing to greater human health and well-being.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SMoC systems provide a novel platform for in-depth exploration of skeletal muscle physiology and its related diseases. By combining OoC technology with 3D printing, researchers are able to construct more realistic and functional skeletal muscle models, which lay the foundation for disease mechanism studies, drug screening, and the development of personalized treatments. Improvements in the cell sources, chip culture conditions, biomaterial scaffold, and microfluidic chip design for SMoC enhance the accuracy and physiological relevance of the skeletal muscle models, which in turn improves the efficiency of disease simulation and drug testing. However, despite the significant potential of 3D printing technology in the construction of SMoC systems, it still faces numerous challenges, such as optimization of biomaterials, dynamic regulation of cell growth environments, and enhancement of printing precision. Addressing these issues requires not only technological innovation but also interdisciplinary collaboration. In the future, with the continuous development of 3D printing technology and the construction of multi-component microenvironments, 3D printed SMoC is expected to address many diagnostic and therapeutic challenges currently faced by diseases of the skeletal muscle. Overall, the ongoing advancement of SMoC technology will provide more effective and efficient tools for biomedical research and precision medicine, driving broader breakthroughs in the treatment of skeletal muscle and related diseases.
