Food insecurity and associated factors among adult cancer patients in south Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Samson Kastro Dake, Debritu Nane, Dibora Teferi Haile, Abiyu Legesse Munea, Abel Daniel Kuche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The incidence of cancer is increasing in Ethiopia, and it poses significant financial burden on households. However, data on the prevalence of household food insecurity and associated factors among adult cancer patients in Africa is scarce. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of food insecurity and identify factors associated with it among adult cancer patients in Southern Ethiopia. A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 338 cancer patients from November 2023 to February 2024. Binary logistic regression model was built in RStudio. The prevalence of food insecurity among cancer patients in this study was 64.2% (95% CI: 58.96–69.16). An increase in age (AOR = 0.97 95% CI; (0.94, 0.99), being married (AOR = 0.37 95% CI; (0.19, 0.74), taking chemotherapy (AOR = 0.27 95% CI; (0.14, 0.51), and an increase in the number of chemotherapy cycles (AOR = 0.83 95% CI; (0.74, 0.92) were associated with lower odds of food insecurity. On the other hand, the odds of being food insecure was higher among participants with poorer performance status (AOR = 1.65 95% CI; (1.21, 2.32). Integrating brief food-insecurity screening into routine oncology visits can help identify patients at risk. Addressing food insecurity should be a core component of comprehensive cancer care in low-resource settings.

Introduction

Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical, social, and economic access to sufficient, safe, and nutritious food that meets their dietary needs and preferences for an active and healthy life1. Despite global progress, nearly 29% of the world’s population (approximately 2.33 billion people) experience moderate or severe food insecurity, with the burden disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In Africa, this prevalence rises to 58%2, and more than half of Ethiopian households experience food insecurity3. A growing body of literature in cancer populations documents substantially higher prevalence of food insecurity than in the general population4.

In Ethiopia, cancer is increasingly diagnosed and contributes to an estimated 5.8% of all deaths. Although population-based cancer data are available only for Addis Ababa, national estimates suggest approximately 60,960 new cancer cases and over 44,000 deaths annually5. Concurrently, access to comprehensive cancer care remains limited6.

Food insecurity is associated with lower consumption of fresh, nutrient-rich foods such as fruits, vegetables, and dairy, resulting in reduced intake of essential vitamins and minerals, including vitamin A, vitamin B6, calcium, magnesium, and zinc7. In cancer populations, food insecurity is associated with greater symptom burden, lower health-related quality of life (QoL), reduced treatment adherence, and increased mortality4,8,9. Furthermore, food insecurity may contribute to suboptimal treatment outcomes, including hospitalizations and adjustments to chemotherapy regimens4.

Cancer patients are particularly vulnerable to nutritional threats due to multiple, interacting pathways4. Metabolic alterations associated with cancer (e.g., increased catabolism), treatment side effects such as nausea, taste alterations, mucositis, and fatigue, functional limitations, and financial toxicity (loss of income and rising out-of-pocket costs) can compromise both food access and dietary intake. These biological and socioeconomic factors create a feedback loop in which food insecurity exacerbates malnutrition, which in turn worsens treatment tolerance and clinical outcomes4,9,10. The predictors of food insecurity among cancer patients include sex11, age12, low economic level13, having children < 18 years of age13, and reduced health care utilization14. Existing interventions to mitigate food insecurity among cancer patients are limited in low-resource settings, and social protection mechanisms such as productive safety net program are not cancer-specific.

Despite the significant financial burden that a cancer diagnosis places on households, data on the prevalence of household food insecurity and associated factors among adult cancer patients in Ethiopia, and Africa at large, are scarce. Accordingly, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of food insecurity among adult cancer patients and to identify factors associated with food insecurity at Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Southern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting and period

The study was conducted at Wolaita Sodo University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (WSUCSH), located in Wolaita Sodo, Southern Ethiopia. Established in 1913, WSUCSH is the only hospital in South Ethiopia region that provides oncology care. The hospital serves as a teaching hospital in addition to providing curative and rehabilitation services. The hospital serves approximately 2 million people in Wolaita zone and neighboring zones. It has around 170 inpatient beds across major departments and an oncology center where 618 cancer patients were on follow-up during the study period (November 2023 to February 2024).

Study design and population

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted from November 2023 to February 2024 at the WSUCSH oncology center. The study included all adult (≥ 18 years) confirmed cancer patients attending the oncology center during the study period. Critically ill patients who were unable to respond to the questionnaire were excluded. Eligible participants were consecutively recruited until the calculated sample size was achieved.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The original sample size was calculated for the parent study based on the prevalence of undernutrition among adult cancer patients. The present analysis represents a secondary analysis examining food insecurity and associated factors using all available participants (n = 338). Although the sample size was not originally determined for this outcome, the adequacy of the sample for multivariable regression analysis was evaluated using established methodological recommendations. Specifically, assuming a food insecurity prevalence of approximately 53.65%15, the expected number of food insecurity cases (~ 172) exceeds the commonly recommended threshold of 10 outcome events per predictor variable16, supporting model stability and reliable estimation of associations. Therefore, the available sample size was considered adequate for the current analyses. All eligible adult cancer patients that come to WSUCSH oncology center during the study period were recruited consecutively until the sample size is achieved.

Outcome variable

Food insecurity was assessed using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) developed by the Food and Nutrition Technical Assistance (FANTA) project17. The HFIAS comprises nine occurrence questions that capture the experience of food insecurity in the past 30 days, the household (1) worried about not having enough food; (2) was unable to eat preferred foods; (3) ate a limited variety of foods; (4) ate foods they did not want to eat; (5) ate smaller meals than needed; (6) ate fewer meals in a day; (7) had no food of any kind in the household; (8) went to sleep hungry; and (9) went a whole day and night without eating. For each item, respondents first indicate whether the condition occurred (Yes/No). If “Yes,” frequency-of-occurrence is recorded as: rarely (once or twice), sometimes (3–10 times), or often (> 10 times) in the 30-day period. Each item is scored 0–3 (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often) and summed to produce a total HFIAS score ranging from 0 to 27, where higher scores indicate greater food-access insecurity.

Additionally, households were categorized as food secure, mildly food insecure, moderately food insecure, or severely food insecure based on the standard FANTA algorithm, which classifies respondents according to the pattern and severity of affirmative responses rather than the summed score alone17.

Exposure variables

The following covariates were assessed as potential factors associated with household food insecurity. Socio-demographic and economic factors included age, sex, place of residence, educational attainment, marital status, occupation, household income, and household size. Clinical and cancer-related factors included cancer type, stage, duration since diagnosis (months), receiving chemotherapy, number of chemotherapy cycles received, performance status, and presence of co-morbidities. Lifestyle and psychosocial factors included alcohol use, cigarette smoking, and khat chewing. We have used the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) Performance Status Scale to assess patient’s performance status. It describes a patient’s level of functioning in terms of their ability to care for themselves, daily activity, and physical ability. It is graded from zero for fully active to five for dead.

Data collection and quality assurance

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire programmed in KoboToolbox18,19. Socio-demographic and clinical data were extracted from patients’ medical charts using a predesigned abstraction form to ensure consistency. Household food insecurity was assessed using the HFIAS17. Data were collected by trained health professionals with prior experience in survey administration. Before data collection, all data collectors and supervisors participated in a two-day training covering the study objectives, questionnaire content, Kobo data collection procedures, participant recruitment, ethical considerations, and interviewing techniques.

Real-time data validation was performed within KoboToolbox to flag missing or out-of-range responses. The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample among cancer patients at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital cancer treatment center, and we did not include them in the main study. Based on the pretest findings, minor modifications were made to improve clarity and flow. Regular field supervision, spot checks, and feedback sessions were conducted throughout the data collection period to ensure adherence to study protocols and data integrity.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges, were computed to summarize socio-demographic, clinical, and food insecurity characteristics of the study participants. We have checked the linearity of quantitative continuous variables. The prevalence and distribution of food insecurity levels were described across key participant characteristics.

To identify factors associated with food insecurity, a binary logistic regression model was employed. Initially, we aimed to conduct ordinal regression analysis. However, because of very few count for moderate (14) and severe (1) food insecurity categories, we were unable to use ordinal regression. Thus, we dichotomized food insecurity into food secure and food insecure for regression analysis.

Data were analyzed using RStudio version 2025.09.1 + 401. Model selection was guided by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), with the model showing the lowest AIC value considered the best fitting model. Variables were selected based on prior evidence and clinical relevance to food insecurity among cancer patients, and we had 14 variables in the multivariable regression model. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was checked using the Variance Inflation Factor, with a score less than 10 indicating acceptable levels. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was applied to assess the fitness of the model, with a p-value = 0.063, indicating adequate model fit. The pseudo-R² statistics was conducted to assess the model explanatory power. The fitted model demonstrated good explanatory power (McFadden R² = 0.21; Cox & Snell R² = 0.24; Nagelkerke R² = 0.33). This indicates a considerable improvement in fit compared with the null model. Interaction terms were evaluated but not retained due to lack of significance, and due to minimal missing data, analyses were conducted using complete cases. Results were expressed as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was declared at a p-value < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Decalaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Wolaita Sodo University (ref: wsu41/40/231). Permission to conduct the study was also secured from the relevant hospital department. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. To ensure confidentiality, all collected data were anonymized using unique identification numbers, and no personally identifying information was recorded or reported.

Discussion

This study revealed a high burden of household food insecurity (64.2%) among cancer patients in South Ethiopia, indicating that food insecurity is a major yet often overlooked challenge in oncology settings. Several significant predictors including younger age, unmarried status, ongoing chemotherapy, higher chemotherapy cycles, and poor performance status highlight the combined social and clinical vulnerabilities faced by this population.

Given the scarcity of published data on household food insecurity among adult cancer patients in low- and middle-income countries, our observed prevalence (64.2%) represents an important new contribution. Studies from high- and middle-income settings report food insecurity rates ranging from 17% to 55% among cancer patients, supporting the notion that cancer can exacerbate difficulties in food access20. Our estimate also exceeds the pooled national prevalence of household food insecurity in the general Ethiopian population (51.5%)21.

Although few studies directly assess food insecurity among cancer patients in Africa, nutritional indicators further support this vulnerability. A meta-analysis from Africa reported a 32.8% prevalence of undernutrition among cancer patients22, while another meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia found an even higher burden (49.2%)23. Together, this evidence suggests that cancer patients in similar settings face substantial nutritional and food access challenges, which reinforces the significance of our findings. There are limited oncology facilities in Ethiopia, high out-of-pocket expenditures, and minimal nutrition services in routine cancer care. Thus, patients in Ethiopia bear the direct and indirect costs of treatment unlike high-income countries. These limitations exacerbate cancer patients’ and their households’ vulnerability to food insecurity.

This study revealed that certain socio-demographic variables were predictors of food insecurity among cancer patients. It was found in this study that as age increases, the odds of food insecurity decreases. Several prior studies confirmed that younger people with cancer are more likely to become food insecure compared to their older counter parts12,24–26. This might be because younger patients diagnosed with cancer are limited from working because of the illness, their income decreases, and they have fewer opportunities to accumulate wealth27. This finding reflects not only individual characteristics but also systemic inequalities in accessing care.

The current study documented that being married reduces the odds of food insecurity by 63%. Another study also reported that food insecurity was higher among unmarried28. Although there is scarcity of direct studies that explored being married with food insecurity among cancer patients, broader literature shows that having a partner is associated with better economic and social resources, which reduces the odds of food insecurity29. Having a marriage may lead to shared economic resources, dual income, larger household social capital and support networks, and thus reduced food insecurity compared to being single30,31.

This study found that receiving chemotherapy and completing more chemotherapy cycles were associated with food insecure. This finding contrasts with previous studies reporting that food insecurity is associated with treatment delay, or make changes to their treatment because of their financial constraints14,28. One possible explanation is that, in Ethiopian context, patients who receive chemotherapy or complete multiple chemotherapy cycles are a subgroup with better financial or social resources and support. However, the cross-sectional design of the current study does not allow determination of the direction of association. It remains unclear whether patients who are less food insecure are more likely to initiate or complete more chemotherapy cycles or whether receiving or continuing chemotherapy contributes to improved food security. There is a need for a longitudinal study to clarify the temporal relationship between receiving chemotherapy and household food insecurity.

Consistent with another study, this study reported that study participants with poorer performance status had increased odds of food insecurity32. Although direct studies on food insecurity and performance status are limited, Evidence from other chronic disease populations depict that poorer performance status might have reduced their ability to engage in income-generating activities or maintain their employment. Poorer functional status is also associated with increased medical and caregiver costs, and might divert household resources away from food33,34.

Our study utilized a cross-sectional design, and it did not identify whether the food insecurity was due to the financial burden arising from the burden of cancer care, making the direction of causality ambiguous. Moreover, food insecurity was assessed at a single point before, during, or after taking chemotherapy, and it may not reflect persistent food insecurity. Patients early in treatment may have family support or savings that can mask food insecurity temporarily.

Conclusion

Food insecurity is highly prevalent among cancer patients in South Ethiopia, and is associated with age, treatment status, marital status, functional limitations, and number of chemotherapy cycles. Thus, oncology services in Ethiopia should utilize context specific strategies to address food insecurity effectively. A brief food-insecurity screening should be integrated into routine oncology visits, with particular attention to younger, unmarried, and functionally limited patients. Targeted support such as connecting patients to productive safety net programs, and guidance on available services, especially for those unable to afford care should be implemented. Addressing food insecurity should be a core component of comprehensive cancer care in low-resource settings. Moreover, longitudinal studies with repeated food insecurity assessment are needed to clarify causal inference between food insecurity and receiving chemotherapy.
