Trends and Inequities in the Continuum of Care for Maternal Healthcare Services in Bangladesh: A National and Subnational Analysis
Authors: Md Badsha Alam, Md Arif Billah, Shimlin Jahan Khanam, Md Mostaured Ali Khan, Md Bakhtiar Uddin, Noor Shah Kamawal, Md Nuruzzaman Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEnsuring a continuum of care in accessing antenatal to postnatal healthcare services is crucial for improving maternal and child health outcomes. This study aims to explore trends in the continuum of care over the years, both nationally and across regions; to provide district‐level estimates; and to examine socio‐economic disparities and determinants of continuum of care uptake in Bangladesh.MethodsA total of 28,260 samples were analysed. Continuum of care was considered as the outcome variable. District, wealth index, and several socio‐demographic factors were included as explanatory variables. Trends of the continuum of care estimates were illustrated over survey years using descriptive statistics. Additional exploration of inequality was conducted across wealth quintiles, educational attainment and urban–rural residence through Equiplot. Finally, a multilevel multinomial logistic regression model was used to assess the factors associated with continuum of care.ResultsThe study revealed a significant increase in the highest level of continuum of care, rising from 6.0% in 2004 to 30.5% in 2017/2018, before declining to 24.8% in 2022. However, notable dropouts from the continuum of care were observed, particularly during the transitions from Antenatal Care to Skilled Birth Attendant and from Skilled Birth Attendant to postnatal Care. The analysis also identified district‐level variations, urban–rural disparities and differences across wealth quintiles. Maternal socio‐demographic characteristics, such as higher education and belonging to a higher wealth quintile, were associated with increased likelihoods of achieving moderate to the highest levels of continuum of care. In contrast, higher parity and rural residence were associated with lower likelihoods of attaining these levels of care.ConclusionThe findings underscore substantial progress in maternal healthcare services uptake in Bangladesh over the years, alongside persistent challenges in ensuring continuous care throughout the maternal healthcare continuum and district‐level variations. Addressing district‐level disparities and socioeconomic inequalities is crucial, necessitating tailored, area‐specific policies and programmes to achieve universal access to quality maternal healthcare services across the country.Keywords: Bangladesh, Bangladesh demographic and health survey, continuum of care, district‐level variation, socio‐economic inequality

Background

Maternal and child health continues to be a critical concern in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [1, 2]. Approximately 87%, while Sub‐Saharan Africa (SSA) accounted for 70% and Southern Asia for 17%, of global maternal deaths [3] and over 80% of global neonatal deaths occur in these regions [4], highlighting a disproportionate burden faced by women and newborns. This figure translates to an estimated 287,000 maternal deaths due to pregnancy‐related complications and 2.3 million newborn deaths within the first month of life in a year in LMICs [5, 6]. In addition to these adverse outcomes, the strain on healthcare facilities is also substantial, with rising hospitalisation rates and increasing healthcare costs due to pregnancy and delivery‐related complications exacerbating the challenges [7, 8]. The adverse outcomes linked to poor maternal and child health, including malnutritional and disability‐adjusted life years, further compound the crisis in LMICs [9, 10]. Despite efforts such as the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs, 2000–2015) and the ongoing implementation of the Sustainable Development Goals (2015–2030), which specifically target a substantial improvement in maternal and child health, the progress remains slow [11, 12]. Key factors contributing to adverse maternal and child health outcomes include poor coverage of maternal healthcare services, lack of continuity in accessing maternal healthcare services, and inadequate quality of available services in LMICs [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Additionally, higher occurrences of unintended pregnancies, short birth intervals and poor nutrition further worsen the outcomes; however, they also indirectly hinder access to maternal healthcare services for women in LMICs [13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Addressing these systemic interlinked issues is crucial for achieving meaningful progress in maternal and child health outcomes in LMICs.

The concept of Continuum of Care (CoC), introduced by Liebowitz and Brody in 1970, underscores the need for consistent and coordinated healthcare across different stages, settings, and providers [13, 24, 25]. Initially developed as a general healthcare model, it was later adapted to maternal health to ensure seamless care from the preconception stage through pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period [13]. The CoC model has two key dimensions: time and place. The time dimension ensures continuous maternal healthcare across all stages, reducing care gaps and improving outcomes. The place dimension integrates services across homes, communities and healthcare facilities, ensuring accessibility and consistency. These principles are aligned with ongoing global health priorities, including the promotion of universal access to sexual and reproductive healthcare services (SDG 3.7) and the achievement of universal health coverage (SDG 3.8) for women and children under five, as outlined in the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) to be accomplished by 2030 [26].

The CoC model in maternal healthcare services plays a pivotal role in improving maternal and child health outcomes by ensuring timely interventions. Evidence highlights that higher CoC adherence correlates with fewer maternal complications, reduced neonatal mortality, and better child health indicators, while also enhancing healthcare efficiency by minimising delays and emergency obstetric complications [27, 28, 29]. Despite the emphasis on CoC and the urgent need to improve maternal and child health, its prevalence remains notably low in LMICs [30]. A recent systematic review found significant variations in CoC rates across countries in LMICs, ranging from the highest 60% in Cambodia to the lowest 8% in Ghana [31, 32]. On average, CoC prevalence was less than 20%, with notably higher estimates observed in South Asian countries (24.5%), followed by African countries (13.9%) [32]—regions responsible for 87% of global mortality [3]. Socio‐demographic conditions and pregnancy‐related characteristics are the focus of these studies, among the factors associated with these lower rates. Geographical location also plays a significant role, as areas within LMICs differ in characteristics and pose unique challenges to accessing healthcare services consistently [13, 14, 33]. However, such geographical focus is limited in both the available literature and policy perspectives, where studies predominantly examine higher‐level administrative units and national‐level policies and programmes without considering the unique characteristics of specific areas [34, 35]. The primary reason for such focus is the lack of data at the smallest geographical unit within LMICs. Available research in LMICs primarily relies on Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data, where the smallest unit available is the region, making estimates below the regional level mostly unavailable. This gap is particularly evident in Bangladesh, where there has been inadequate exploration of the Continuum of Care (CoC). Only a 2014 DHS survey‐based study has reported a 12% utilisation of CoC, and the geographical areas considered have typically been limited to the regions studied thus far [13, 36]. This lack of granularity hinders policymakers' ability to understand CoC uptake at the local level and develop tailored policies and programmes accordingly. Therefore, we conducted this study to explore trends in the CoC over the years, both nationally and across regions; to provide district‐level estimates; and to examine socio‐economic disparities and determinants of CoC uptake in Bangladesh.

Methods

Study Design and Sampling Technique

We conducted a comprehensive analysis using data from six rounds of the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Survey (BDHS), spanning the years 2004 to 2022. Each of these surveys was nationally representative and was conducted as an integral component of the Demographic and Health Survey Program of the USA. Financial support for these surveys was provided by the United States Agency for International Development (USAID), with technical assistance rendered by ICF International in Calverton, USA. The survey sample comprised households selected using a two‐stage stratified random sampling technique. In the initial stage, primary sampling units (PSUs) were chosen across the country, utilising PSU lists derived from the most recent national population census conducted by the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics. Subsequently, a household listing operation was executed, and 30 households for 2004 to 2017/2018 and 45 households for the 2022 survey were randomly selected from each PSU in the second stage of sampling (Table S1). Data collection involved surveying women who met specific inclusion criteria: (i) married women of reproductive age (15–49 years old), and (ii) those who were either usual residents of the selected households or had spent the most recent night at the selected households on the day of the survey. Additionally, data were collected for their partners and children under the age of five. For further details, a comprehensive overview of these surveys can be found in the respective survey reports [37, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42].

Study Sample

We analysed a total of 28,260 sub‐samples of mother–child data derived from the six rounds of the BDHSs. Among these, 5416 samples were extracted from the 2004, 4905 samples were from the 2007, 4652 from the 2011, 4627 from the 2014, 5051 from the 2017, and 3609 were from the 2022 BDHS. The inclusion criteria for samples extracted from the original survey were as follows: (i) having given birth to at least one child within 2 years (BDHS 2022), 3 years (BDHS 2011, 2024, and 2017–2018), and 5 years (BDHS 2004 and 2007) of the survey and (ii) providing information for each component of maternal healthcare services: ANC, delivery healthcare services by SBA either in hospitals or at home, and PNC services for both mothers and newborns with medically trained providers (MTP).

Outcome Variable

The CoC was considered as the outcome variable, derived from mothers' responses regarding their utilisation or non‐utilisation of ANC, SBA and PNC services from MTP. This variable was categorised into three levels of CoC (Table S2): highest (four or more ANC visits, delivery aided by SBA, and PNC within 48 h postpartum); middle (at least two of the following: four or more ANC visits, SBA‐assisted delivery, and PNC services); and lowest (lack of SBA assistance, absence of PNC, and less than four ANC visits). We adhered to the maternal healthcare service utilisation guidelines outlined by WHO to define and measure these classifications, which have been published elsewhere in previous studies [13, 43]. According to WHO guidelines, each pregnant woman should ideally receive a minimum of four skilled ANC visits (changed to eight visits in 2016 [44], however, Bangladesh government still follow four visits), have an SBA during delivery, and obtain PNC for both herself and the newborn within 48 h of birth from medically trained providers. Several questions in the BDHS survey were utilised to gather data on maternal healthcare service utilisation and service providers. Specifically, two queries pertaining to ANC services were employed to ascertain whether women availed ANC and, if so, how frequently. Queries related to delivery care were posed to gather information on the attendants and locations of deliveries. Additionally, four extra questions were used to gather data on the timing of initial PNC for women and newborns, as well as the providers of PNC services [41].

Explanatory Variables

Respondents' geographical location (District—the second administrative level in Bangladesh), wealth quintile (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest), and socio‐demographic characteristics served as explanatory variables. The 2022 survey included data from respondents for both divisions (the first administrative level in Bangladesh) and districts. The wealth quintile (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest) also served as another explanatory variable, which was created based on household assets, such as roof types and ownership of televisions, using principal component analysis. Additionally, other explanatory variables considered encompassed socio‐demographic characteristics of the respondents, including women's age at childbirth (≤ 19 years, 20–34 years, and ≥ 35 years), educational attainment (no education, primary, secondary, and higher), employment status (not employed, employed), parity (1–2 children, > 2 children), the sex of the child (male, female), exposure to mass media (not exposed, moderately exposed, and highly exposed), place of residence (urban, rural) and division (Barishal, Chattogram, Dhaka, Khulna, Mymensingh, Rajshahi, Rangpur and Sylhet).

Statistical Analysis

We initially employed descriptive statistics to assess the utilisation of ANC, SBA and PNC, along with generating and presenting the level of CoC across different survey years. Dropout rates across the CoC continuum to PNC were calculated, and various types of CoC rates were examined across districts and wealth quintiles. Inequality in CoC was determined across wealth quintiles, educational attainment and place of residence using Stata's Equiplot Command of the International Center for Equity in Health (ICEH). We utilised a multilevel multinomial and multilevel mixed‐effect logistic regression model to investigate factors associated with various types of CoC and each component of maternal healthcare services use, respectively. This choice was driven by the hierarchical structure of the BDHSs, where individuals are nested within households, and households are nested within clusters. We accounted for this hierarchy in our analysis. Considering the union of data from multiple surveys, another hierarchical structure emerged, as responses from the same survey are likely to be more similar than responses from different surveys. We adopted a two‐level multilevel modelling approach: households and clusters level. All results were weighted by sampling weights to correct for the sample design and are presented as adjusted Relative Risk Ratios (aRRR) with their 95% CI (95% confidence interval). The entire analysis was conducted using STATA v17.0/MP and ArcGIS (version 10.5). This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.

Ethics Statement

The data analysed in this study were sourced from the Demographic and Health Survey Program of the USA. Before conducting the survey in Bangladesh, approval was secured from the institutional review board of ICF, USA and subsequently from the National Research Ethics Committee of the Bangladesh Medical Research Council. To ensure participants' consent, informed written consent was obtained from all individuals involved, using an appropriate institutional form. These consent forms were securely stored by the survey authority. In our research, we requested permission to access the data for analytical purposes, and the survey authority provided us with de‐identified data. Since the study involved secondary data analysis and adhered to relevant guidelines and regulations, no further ethical approval was necessary.

Trends of Maternal Healthcare Services Access and Their Continuum of Care (CoC)

We conducted an analysis of each component of maternal healthcare service utilisation and CoC over survey years, and the corresponding results are summarised in Table 2. A notable increase in the uptake of four or more ANC was observed, rising from 15.1% in 2004 to 43.5% in 2017/2018—an increase of 28 percentage points over the 13‐year period; however, the trend declined to 38.3% in 2022. On the other hand, the uptake of SBA services increased by 56 percentage points (from 14.2% in 2004 to 69.9% in 2022), while PNC services for both mothers and children increased by 39 percentage points (from 13.0% in 2004 to 51.3% in 2022). Upon comparing these rates to calculate CoC (Table 2), we found that the proportion of women with higher CoC was 30.5% in 2017/2018, representing a 24 percentage points increase from the mere 6% CoC observed in 2004, while the trend was observed downward in 2022 (24.8%). However, conversely, the lowest level of CoC was found to have declined by 53.3 percentage points (from 76.8% in 2004 to 23.5% in 2022).

Dropout Rate of Women From CoC From ANC to PNC Services Uptake

We analysed the dropout rates of women during the PNC phase, as presented in Figure 1a–g. We did not observe any significant changes in the dropout patterns over the survey years. In each survey, the most substantial dropout from CoC occurred when transitioning from at least one ANC visit to four or more ANC visits. Overall, 58.4% of mothers dropped out during this transition, while 31.5% dropped out when transitioning from four or more ANC visits to accessing SBA, and 14.5% dropped out when transitioning from SBA to PNC (a). When comparing across years, we observed mirrored estimates with similar overall patterns. The majority of dropouts were recorded during the transition from at least one ANC visit to four or more ANC visits, followed by the transition from four or more ANC visits to SBA, and then from SBA to PNC (b–g). However, the prevalence of dropout at each stage of transition was found to have declined.

(a–g) Women who dropped out (received a particular service but did not receive the next immediate service) from the continuum of maternal healthcare through to PNC, by survey year, in Bangladesh.

District‐Level Variations in CoC and Each Component of Maternal Healthcare Services Uptake

We calculated the district‐level variations of CoC and each component of maternal healthcare services used in CoC, and the relevant results are presented in Tables 3 and S3. The results indicated a significant difference, particularly for SBA during delivery and PNC. Once we considered them together and generated the CoC, we found that differences in CoC also varied significantly across the districts, with Meherpur, Chattogram, Dhaka, Narayanganj, Madaripur and Joypurhat districts reporting the highest level of CoC use based on the most recent BDHS 2022 survey data. Patuakhali, Narail, Lalmonirhat, Sirajganj, Kishoreganj and Kurigram districts had the lowest level of CoC. Considering each component of CoC, Gopalganj, Sunamganj, Rangamati, Jamalpur and Khagrachhari reported the lowest prevalence of maternal healthcare service use (Table 3). In terms of divisional‐level variance, the highest rate of CoC was reported for Dhaka and Khulna across survey years (Figure 2). A similar result was reported for the moderate level of CoC.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore trends in the CoC over the years, both nationally and across regions; to provide district‐level estimates; and to examine socio‐economic disparities and determinants of CoC uptake in Bangladesh. We observed a notable increase in the highest level of CoC, rising from just 6.0% in 2004 to 30.5% in 2017, before dropping to 25% in 2022. The most significant dropouts from CoC occurred between the transition from at least one ANC visit to four ANC visits and from SBA to PNC, with declining prevalence in recent survey years. CoC varied significantly across districts, urban–rural residences and wealth quintiles of the respondents. Significant predictors of achieving the highest and moderate levels of CoC included increased women's higher education levels, greater exposure to media, residence in households with improved wealth quintiles and residing in the Khulna division. Conversely, rural residents reported lower probabilities of achieving the highest or moderate levels of CoC. These findings suggest that while healthcare services have succeeded in increasing the prevalence of moderate and highest levels of CoC, more effective policies are needed. District‐level disparities indicate that universal nationwide policies may not adequately address these discrepancies, highlighting the necessity for tailored, area‐specific policies and programmes based on local needs.

Bangladesh reported around a 24 percentage point increase in CoC between 2004 and 2017/2018 and later declined by 5 percentage points in 2022. This decline is due to the impact of COVID‐19 and its non‐pharmaceutical preventive strategies. The overburden of healthcare services, perceived risk of getting infected with COVID‐19, community lockdown due to COVID‐19, and community misconceptions also reduced the maternal and newborn healthcare use [45]. Although the lack of trend data limits our ability to validate these findings, individual survey data support this observation. However, despite this rising trend, the CoC in Bangladesh is the lowest among its neighbouring countries, except for Pakistan, although the rate of increase is faster [46, 47, 48, 49]. Such increases are due to numerous factors, with the foremost role played by the concerted efforts of the government and various healthcare stakeholders to improve maternal and child healthcare services. These efforts were in line with the MDGs between 2000 and 2015 and the ongoing SDGs between 2015 and 2030. This includes initiatives aimed at increasing access to ANC, SBA, and PNC services, particularly in rural and underserved areas [13]. Healthcare infrastructure, including the expansion of healthcare facilities and the training of healthcare professionals, has also undergone massive advancement during this period, contributing to improved service delivery and increased CoC uptake [13, 33, 50, 51, 52]. Moreover, targeted interventions focusing on maternal and child health, such as awareness campaigns, community outreach programmes, and incentive schemes for healthcare providers, have played a pivotal role in raising awareness and encouraging the utilisation of maternal healthcare services throughout the CoC [53, 54, 55].

We observed a higher dropout rate from CoC, particularly in the transition from at least one ANC to four ANC. This finding aligns with previous studies conducted in Bangladesh and other LMICs [13, 14, 43]. Several factors contribute to this phenomenon. First, evidence suggests that a group of women, particularly disadvantaged women in Bangladesh, often initiate access to maternal healthcare services with the intention of including them in the government's conditional social health protection scheme such as Shasthyo Surokhsha Karmasuchi (SSK) and Maternal Health Voucher Scheme (MHVS), as enrolment in these programmes initially requires them to obtain maternal healthcare services [56, 57]. However, these programmes typically do not monitor CoC, leading to women discontinuing service uptake once they are enrolled [57]. Second, the poor quality of maternal healthcare services acts as a barrier to continued service uptake after initiation [57, 58]. This is compounded by community‐level challenges, such as restrictions on the movement of pregnant women during visible stages of pregnancy and a lack of awareness about the importance of CoC within communities [59]. There is also a prevailing perception that maternal healthcare services are only necessary when complications arise, further discouraging women from continuing with CoC. This perception is particularly responsible for the higher dropout rate in the transition from SBA to PNC and the low utilisation of PNC, especially from subsequent visits, as women often believe that pregnancy‐related issues are resolved once delivery occurs. Additionally, the high occurrence of unintended pregnancies, which are often detected late, along with associated consequences such as depression and anxiety, further deters women from receiving CoC in Bangladesh [13, 19, 60]. Moreover, such late detection prevents mothers from initiating ANC on time, leading to insufficient access to the recommended number of ANC visits.

The significant inequality observed in achieving the highest level of CoC across different socioeconomic strata aligns with previous observations regarding CoC and the uptake of individual components of maternal healthcare services [13, 31, 46, 48, 49]. Several factors contribute to this disparity. First, financial barriers play a crucial role, as women from poorer households may face challenges in accessing and affording maternal healthcare services, including transportation costs and out‐of‐pocket expenses for medical care [16, 61]. Additionally, disparities in education and awareness levels can influence healthcare‐seeking behaviours, with women from wealthier backgrounds often having better access to information and resources to navigate the healthcare system [50]. Moreover, geographical disparities in healthcare infrastructure and service availability, including differences between urban and rural areas, may disproportionately affect women from poorer regions, limiting their access to essential maternal healthcare services [33, 50].

These differences can also manifest across divisions, reflecting variations in the coverage of urban and rural areas, healthcare services, education and other cultural and social norms regarding gender roles and decision‐making [33, 50, 62]. Such division‐level barriers contribute to variations in CoC, as observed in this study, and are consistent with previously reported differences across higher administrative divisions [13, 30, 50, 63]. Geographical factors also play a significant role, with remote and hard‐to‐reach divisions facing challenges in accessing healthcare services due to inadequate transportation infrastructure and geographical barriers. Conversely, central divisions typically have better‐equipped facilities and a higher concentration of healthcare professionals [33]. However, these challenges can exacerbate health inequalities, particularly when compounded by social and cultural norms. Addressing these multifaceted determinants of health disparities is essential for promoting equitable access to healthcare across all districts and divisions in Bangladesh.

The association between increasing maternal age and a higher likelihood of achieving moderate to high levels of CoC in the pooled data suggests that older mothers may prioritise their health more effectively. However, this requires closer examination, as analysis of the most recent survey data indicates that this association has disappeared. One possible explanation for the earlier association could be greater awareness among older mothers regarding the importance of maternal healthcare services. With age often comes experience, leading older mothers to seek out healthcare services more diligently. Additionally, older mothers may have greater access to resources, such as financial stability and social support networks, which facilitate their consistent engagement with maternal healthcare services. Furthermore, previous pregnancy and childbirth experiences may make older mothers more attuned to the importance of seeking regular care during pregnancy. However, this explanation requires closer examination, as we also found lower likelihoods of moderate and higher levels of CoC among mothers with more than two children, who typically tend to be older. Another dimension that needs consideration here is the socioeconomic characteristics of the respondents. It is possible that women with more than two children are among the disadvantaged group, where maternal healthcare service use is often overlooked due to a lack of awareness and accessibility capacity [64]. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the complexities underlying these associations.

Strengths and Limitations

This paper's primary strength lies in analysing six rounds of nationally representative BDHS data, incorporating large samples. The analysis also presents CoC variation across wealth quintiles, education and urban–rural areas. Advanced statistical modelling explored predictors of CoC, considering sampling weights in all analyses. These comprehensive analyses provide substantial strength to leverage these findings for national‐level policy and programme development. However, the primary limitation is the use of cross‐sectional data, indicating a correlational rather than causal nature of our findings. Additionally, many factors other than the ones available in the dataset that we considered may influence CoC use, such as the distance of healthcare facilities. Unfortunately, we could not consider these variables in the analysis due to the lack of data. Nonetheless, despite these limitations, this study's findings highlight the inadequate coverage of CoC in Bangladesh with substantial division‐level variations. This suggests a need for comprehensive, segregated policies and programmes rather than current universal approaches.

Conclusion

This study reported an increase in the highest level of CoC, rising from 6.0% in 2004 to 30.5% in 2017/2018, followed by a decline to 24.8% in 2022. However, notable dropouts from the CoC—particularly during the transitions from ANC to SBA and from SBA to PNC—highlight ongoing challenges in ensuring continuous care throughout the maternal healthcare continuum. District‐level disparities, education and urban–rural discrepancies, and variations across wealth quintiles underscore the need for tailored, area‐specific policies and programmes to address gaps in CoC uptake. Financial barriers, disparities in education and awareness levels, and geographical disparities in healthcare infrastructure contribute to inequalities in CoC across different socioeconomic strata. Country‐level policies and programmes are important to address these challenges; particularly, countries need to focus on needs‐based policies and programmes in areas rather than current universal policies and programmes.
