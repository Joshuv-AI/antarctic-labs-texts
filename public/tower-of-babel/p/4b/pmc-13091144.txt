Comparative analysis of quality characteristics and flavor compounds in caged and cage-free eggs based on untargeted flavoromics
Authors: Hongbo Zeng, Yue Zhang, Xuezhen Guo, Jiamin Shen, Fei Wang, Xiaofeng Ji, Qiuwen Xu, Hua Yang, Yingping Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the quality characteristics and flavor compounds of caged and cage-free chicken eggs. Fresh unfertilized caged and cage-free Xianju chicken eggs were used for quality parameter and flavoromics analyses. Cage-free eggs had higher yolk weight and yolk ratio, whereas caged eggs had higher malondialdehyde level and Haugh units. There was no significant difference in amino acid composition between the caged and cage-free eggs. Flavoromics analysis revealed that cage-free eggs exhibited higher levels of alcohols, acids, and ketones, exhibiting more pronounced fruity and floral notes. In contrast, the caged eggs had higher levels of aromatic compounds, exhibiting a mild flavor. Furthermore, the integration of flavoromics with relative odor activity value analysis indicates that 1-Octen-3-ol, (R,R)-2,3-Butanediol, Dimethylthiomethane, (E)-2-Octenal, (E)-2-Decenal, and acetoin were potential indicators for differentiating between caged and cage-free eggs. This study provides important data to support the optimization of egg production systems and quality improvements.Keywords: Quality characteristics, Flavor compounds, Flavoromics, Eggs

Introduction

Eggs are among the most nutritionally rich foods, providing essential amino acids more efficiently than meat, legumes, and grains (Rehault-Godbert et al., 2019).

Cage-free environments allow for the expression of natural behaviors such as nesting, perching, and foraging, which can influence the physiological state of the hen and, consequently, the biochemical composition of the eggs (Erek and Matur, 2024, Yang et al., 2025). With the increasing demand for eggs by consumers, the rearing system of laying hens has gradually changed from traditional cage-free to intensive caged rearing models. Recent studies have shown that eggshell strength and nutritional composition differ significantly between caged and cage-free eggs (Kawamura et al., 2023; Rodriguez-Hernandez et al., 2024). The crude protein content may show minor variations, but the more notable difference lies in the composition of the umami amino acids (Goto et al., 2021; Nimalaratne et al., 2016). As consumption levels and health awareness increase, consumer demand for higher egg quality and improved flavor has also increased. Flavor compounds have a significant impact on the marketability of eggs. The key contributors to these compounds include hydrocarbons, ketones, aldehydes, and amino acids, all of which can be influenced by rearing models (Lordelo et al., 2017). Dong et al. (2021) find that cage-free eggs have stronger responses for sulfur-containing compounds and alcohols, which are associated with “creamier” and fuller aromas.

Flavoromics, which is considered a subarea within metabolomics, has been applied in food science, especially in eggs and meat (Liu et al., 2025; Mao et al., 2024). Untargeted flavoromics provides a comprehensive, unbiased screening of the entire volatile profile. This approach is powerful for discovering novel marker compounds and unexpected chemical relationships that underlie sensory differences, offering new insights beyond the scope of conventional analyses (Ronningen et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2025). Numerous volatile flavor compounds, such as alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, aromatics, furans, sulfides, and terpenes, can serve as fingerprint molecules to differentiate eggs from various breeds and origins (Wang et al., 2022; Zou et al., 2019). Xianju chicken is a renowned indigenous egg-laying breed in China, originating from Xianju city, Zhejiang province. It is highly regarded both domestically and internationally for its distinctive appearance, higher egg production, and superior quality (Yin et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2025). However, comprehensive untargeted flavoromics analysis using integrated statistical and sensory-prediction models comparing the subtleties in flavor profiles and their chemical underpinnings in caged versus cage-free Xianju chicken eggs has not been thoroughly explored. Understanding these differences is crucial for tailoring egg production to meet emerging consumer preferences for specific flavor profiles.

Given these considerations, this study specifically aims to investigate the quality characteristics, amino acids, and volatile flavor compounds of Xianju chicken eggs produced under caged and cage-free rearing conditions using gas chromatography quadruple time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC/Q-TOF-MS) flavoromics analysis. This study provides important data to support the improvement of egg quality for egg authentication.

Materials and methods

Egg samples collection

This study involved only the analysis of eggs as agricultural products; no direct interaction with animals occurred. Fresh unfertilized Xianju chicken eggs, laid within 24 h under standard farm management protocols, were procured post-collection from a local farm in Zhejiang Province, China. The chickens (approximately 210 days) in this farm were raised under caged (C) and cage-free (CF) rearing systems with standard dietary formulations. CF hens were reared on a litter-floor system with wood shavings as bedding, which may have allowed incidental ingestion of litter or insects. A total of 10 egg samples from each group (mixed with egg white and yolk) were manually separated from the eggshells. Every sample mixture containing at least 15 eggs was prepared for each experiment. All egg samples were quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for amino acid and flavor profile analyses. In addition, 50 fresh eggs from each group were stored at 4 °C for quality characteristic analysis.

Quality characteristics analysis

Haugh units and egg weight were measured using a multifunctional egg analyzer (EA-01; Orka Technology, Israel). The egg shape index was measured using a digital Vernier caliper (Model 300; Shanghai Saituo Hardware, China). Eggshell strength was evaluated using an automated eggshell strength tester (FPG-5; Robotmation Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The Malondialdehyde (MDA) and total-cholesterol were quantified using standardized enzymatic assays with commercial kits obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The albumen and yolk were separated using a precision egg divider (SY-ES0006; Yangjiang Fuli Hardware Co., Ltd., China) and weighed individually using an analytical balance (AB-105; Mettler Toledo, Switzerland). Amino acid analysis

The amino acid composition was analyzed using an automated amino acid analyzer (L-8900; Hitachi, Japan) equipped with a sulfonic acid cation exchange resin column (4.6 mm × 60 mm, 3 μm) (Rahman et al., 2018; Wen et al., 2025). The analysis employed a gradient elution program with the following parameters: 53 min run time, column temperature maintained at 57 °C, and reaction column temperature of 135 °C. Ninhydrin served as the derivatization reagent. The mobile phase flow rate was maintained at 0.35 mL/min throughout gradient elution. Detection was performed using a dual-wavelength system with signal acquisition at 570 nm (32 min integration time) and 440 nm (10 min integration time). The injection volume was 20 μL for each sample.

Flavoromics analysis

The sample preparation procedure was performed as described by Ma et al. (2024). Briefly, a 3 g egg sample was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial, followed by the addition of 3 mL of 10% sodium chloride solution and 5 μL of cyclohexanone (0.2 mg/mL) as an internal standard. The headspace vials were incubated at 60 °C for 40 min. Subsequently, the samples were adsorbed using an activated solid-phase microextraction (SPME) probe over a 30 min period. Following adsorption, the SPME fibers were introduced into the inlet of the 7890B-7250 GC/QTOF-MS detector (Agilent Technologies, USA) for thermal desorption at 250 °C for 5 min. The volatile flavor compounds were separated using an HP-5MS (30.00 m × 0.32 mm × 0.25 um, Agilent) elastic quartz capillary column, and helium gas (purity ≥99.99%) was utilized as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 3.0 mL/min. The temperature program was initially set at 50 °C for 3 min and then increased to 250 °C at a rate of 5 °C/min for 10 min. The mass spectrometer operated using the electron impact (EI) method, employing an ionization energy of 70 eV and maintaining the source temperature at 250 °C. Full-scan mode was implemented during MS analysis, with a mass range spanning from 29 to 500 m/z at a scanning rate of 5.2 scans/s, with no solvent delay. The quadruple temperature was set to 150 °C.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of volatile flavor compounds

After the GC/Q-TOF-MS analysis, the chromatographic and mass spectrometry data of volatile flavor components were analyzed using Agilent MassHunter Qualitative Analysis software (10.0.10305.0). The volatile compounds were identified by comparing them to the MS library (NIST 17.0) and further identified using the available retention indices (RI). Only substances with a matching factor exceeding 80 (the highest matching degree is 100) were retained. The quantification of compound concentrations in eggs utilized the internal standard (Cyclohexanone; CAS: 108–94-1) method.

Determination of characteristic volatile flavor compounds

The relative odor activity value (ROAV) was used to characterize the contribution of volatile flavor compounds (Ozkara et al., 2019), comparing the odor threshold of each flavor compound to its actual content to determine its contribution to the overall flavor profile. ROAV values were calculated using the following equation: ROAV = (Ci/Tmax) × (Cmax/Ti) × 100.

Where Ci represents the relative content of the volatile compound of interest; Tmax represents the relative content of the compound with the highest odor activity value; Cmax represents the odor threshold of the target volatile compound in the water; Ti represents the odor threshold in water for the compound with the highest odor activity value.

In this analysis, the compound that exhibited the most significant impact on flavor was assigned a reference ROAV value of 100. Key aroma-active compounds were defined as those with ROAV >1, while compounds with 0.1 ≤ ROAV≤1 were considered to have modifying effects on the overall flavor profile.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences were assessed for statistical significance using the t-test, with a significance level set at P-value <0.05. The volatile flavor compound data were subjected to orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) to obtain the variable importance in projection (VIP) and fold change (FC) of CF/C values (Galindoprieto et al., 2014) using the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 website (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) (Pang et al., 2024). Finally, flavor compounds with FC values >1 or < 1 and P-values <0.05 were defined as upregulated or downregulated in CF and C eggs, and flavor compounds with VIP values >1.0 and P-values <0.05 were defined as significantly different flavor compounds (DFCs). Data visualization was performed using the BioDeep website (https://www.biodeep.cn) and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

Results

The quality characteristics in the C and CF eggs

In this study, we examined fresh eggs from C and CF Xianju chickens (Fig. 1A). The egg yolk weight and yolk ratio were higher (P < 0.001) in the CF group than in the C group (Fig. 2C and D). The Haugh unit (P = 0.039) and MDA content (P = 0.019) were lower in the CF group than in the C group (Fig. 2B and F). However, no significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed between the two groups in the egg shape index, eggshell strength, egg weight, and total-cholesterol (Fig. 1B and C; Fig. 2A and E).

The amino acid composition in the C and CF eggs

Sixteen amino acids were identified in the C and CF eggs (Table 1), including seven essential amino acids (EAA) and nine nonessential amino acids (NEAA). No significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed between the C and CF eggs in the amino acid levels, including EAA and total amino acid (TAA) concentration. In addition, the proportion of essential amino acids (EAA/TAA) was nearly identical between C (41.99%) and CF eggs (41.96%). Similarly, the delicious amino acid (DAA) content exhibited minimal variation, with values of 5.13 g/100 g and 4.91 g/100 g for C and CF eggs, respectively.

The volatile flavor compounds in the C and CF eggs

Volatile flavor compounds were identified by matching them against the NIST mass spectrometry database, with 76 distinct substances remaining (Table 2). Among these, 58 compounds were found in C eggs and 67 in CF eggs, with 49 compounds common to both. C eggs contained 16 hydrocarbons, 10 ketones, 8 aromatic compounds, 6 aldehydes, 6 esters, 5 alcohols, 2 acids, 2 sulfur-containing compounds, 1 nitrogen-containing compound, 1 ether, and 1 furan. In contrast, the CF eggs contained 21 hydrocarbons, 10 ketones, 9 aldehydes, 9 aromatic compounds, 7 alcohols, 5 esters, 3 acids, 1 sulfur-containing compound, 1 nitrogen-containing compound, and 1 furan. Among these, hydrocarbons, ketones, aromatic compounds, aldehydes, alcohols, and esters were the predominant flavor compounds, representing 88.15% of the total volatile flavor compounds detected.

The key volatile flavor compounds through the ROAV analysis

Applying the criterion of ROAV >1 for key flavor contributors, among the 26 DFCs, six compounds were identified, including five (1-Octen-3-ol, (R,R)-2,3-Butanediol, (E)-2-Octenal, (E)-2-Decenal, and Acetoin) in CF eggs and two (Dimethylthiomethane and Acetoin) in C eggs. Notably, 1-Octen-3-ol emerged as the primary flavor contributor in CF eggs, whereas dimethylthiomethane emerged as the primary flavor contributor in C eggs (Fig. 5A). Discussion

Quality indicators directly influence the genetic, nutritional, and market value of eggs (Malfatti et al., 2021). The Haugh unit serves as the core parameter for assessing egg freshness, with higher values indicating greater freshness (Wang et al., 2021). In this study, the Haugh units in C eggs were 13.53% higher than those in CF eggs, but all exceeded 60% and aligned with A-grade standards under the USDA classification systems (https://www.ars.usda.gov). This finding is consistent with the observations by Rodriguez-Hernandez et al. (2024). Samiullah et al. (2014) also demonstrate that eggs from C systems generally exhibit better internal quality parameters than those from CF systems, further supporting the results of this experiment. This could be due to the intensive management of C systems, which may promote more uniform egg quality through stable nutrient supply and controlled environmental conditions. In contrast, the CF system, owing to individual variability and environmental heterogeneity, could lead to greater fluctuations in egg quality (Erek & Matur, 2024; Kawamura et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024).

The proportion of egg yolk is an important visual criterion for consumers when evaluating egg quality (Grcevic et al., 2019). In this study, the yolk proportion in CF eggs was higher than that in C eggs, suggesting that the CF rearing system enhances yolk deposition in eggs. Previous studies have noted that CF systems typically exhibit lower egg production rates than C systems, which may extend the yolk deposition period in the ovary, thereby facilitating greater yolk accumulation (Erek and Matur, 2024, Yang et al., 2025). Eggs are an excellent dietary source of essential amino acids because of their high-quality protein content. Amino acids play vital roles in maintaining normal physiological functions, with each type fulfilling a distinct biological role (Goto et al., 2021). Lordelo et al. (2017) report no statistically significant differences in the concentrations of free amino acids, essential amino acids, and total amino acids between CF and C eggs, which aligns with our findings that there were no differences in amino acid levels between the C and CF eggs. This suggests that different rearing systems have minimal impact on the amino acid composition of eggs. In addition to the absolute amino acid content, the proportional composition of amino acids is a crucial parameter for evaluating egg protein quality. Our data demonstrate that both C and CF eggs exhibited essential amino acid ratios (EAA/TAA) and essential-to-nonessential amino acid ratios (EAA/NEAA) exceeding 40% and 60%, respectively, meeting the ideal protein standards established by the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) (https://www.fao.org/home/en).

Flavoromics analysis detected 58 and 67 volatile compounds in C and CF eggs, respectively. These compounds comprised hydrocarbons, ketones, aldehydes, alcohols, esters, acids, ethers, and aromatic compounds, with hydrocarbons and aromatic compounds being the most abundant, followed by aldehydes and alcohols. OPLS-DA analysis revealed significant differences in volatile flavor profiles between C and CF eggs, indicating that the rearing system substantially influences egg flavor components. This influence is likely mediated by behavioral and physiological differences between housing systems. CF hens exhibit higher physical activity and reduced stress levels, which can modulate lipid metabolism and oxidative status, ultimately affecting the generation of lipid-derived volatiles such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols (Herrera-Sanchez et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Moreover, foraging behavior in CF systems may expose hens to additional bioactive compounds from litter or insects, further contributing to flavor diversity (Lordelo et al., 2017). In addition, we identified 26 differential flavor compounds between C and CF eggs, primarily hydrocarbons, alcohols, ketones, and aldehydes. Hydrocarbons and aromatic compounds mainly originate from lipid oxidative degradation, most of which have relatively high thresholds (Wang et al., 2022), contributing minimally to the overall aroma. MDA is one of the final products of lipid peroxidation and is an important indicator for evaluating the level of lipid peroxidation (Tian et al., 2025). In this study, C eggs had higher MDA level than CF eggs, which indicated that the higher degree of lipid peroxidation in C eggs is one of the reasons for the difference of flavor substances. Flavoromics studies suggest that they serve as important intermediates in the formation of heterocyclic compounds that contribute to flavor (Mao et al., 2024). Hydrocarbons typically produce “gasoline” and “orange peel” odors, while aromatic compounds generate “vanilla” and “floral” notes. Most aldehydes exhibit extremely low aroma thresholds, which means that they can significantly impact the overall flavor (Plagemann et al., 2011). Aldehydes typically contribute to “green grass,” “mortar,” “orange peel,” and “fatty” odors. (E)-2-Heptenal is specific to cage-free eggs and has a distinct fatty, grassy, and nutty aroma (Gao et al., 2023). In this study, the content of (E)-2-Decenal, (E)-2-Octenal, and (E)-2-Heptenal in CF eggs was higher, exhibiting aromas such as “fatty”, “floral”, and “fruity” notes. The richer profile of alcohols, acids, and ketones in CF eggs, contributing to more pronounced fruity and floral notes, may be linked to the more diverse diet and increased activity levels of hens in cage-free systems. While the basal diet remained identical, CF hens had access to litter substrates, where incidental ingestion of insects might have taken place. This could introduce additional precursors for volatile compound formation, such as plant-derived terpenes or microbial metabolites (Lordelo et al., 2017). Furthermore, the potential for lower stress levels in CF environments could influence lipid metabolism and oxidative processes (Herrera-Sanchez et al., 2024), thereby altering the volatile flavor spectrum.

Unsaturated alcohols, formed enzymatically from aldehydes, have lower perception thresholds than their saturated counterparts, making them more important for flavor (Suleman et al., 2022). While the flavor characteristics of the butanediol isomers remain unspecified, they likely resemble 2,3-butanediol's onion, fruity, and creamy notes. 2-Methyl-1-butanol contributes to baked, onion, malt, and wine aromas, while 1-Octen-3-ol provides earthy, mushroom-like scents (Swiegers et al., 2005). Ketones provide distinctive aromatic qualities that enhance food flavor profiles. They produce fruity, fresh, and floral notes that contribute to pleasant egg aromas (Zou et al., 2019). The predominant ketones were Acetoin, 2-Heptanone, and 2-Butanone. Acetoin generates sweet, fruity, creamy, and buttery flavors, while 2-Heptanone contributes waxy, woody, fruity, and coconut notes. In this study, there was no significant difference in the relative content of hydrocarbons between C and CF eggs, while tert-butanol was particularly abundant in C eggs, and the CF eggs showed higher concentrations of acetoin, (S,S)-2,3-Butanediol, (R,R)-2,3-Butanediol, 1-Octen-3-ol, and 2-Methyl-1-butanol. These findings demonstrate that flavor compounds differ between C and CF eggs. The flavor profile of caged eggs is primarily dominated by aromatic compounds, resulting in a milder taste, whereas CF eggs are richer in alcohol and ketone flavor substances, exhibiting more pronounced fruity and floral notes.

ROAV is an effective criterion for flavor evaluation, aiding in the identification of major taste bud contributors (Gao et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025). The perception of aroma by Homo sapiens occurs through the interaction between volatile organic compounds (VOCs) released by a substance and G protein-coupled olfactory receptors in the nasal cavity (Guan et al., 2025). Recent studies have leveraged flavoromics combined with ROAV analysis to identify potential biomarkers for distinguishing the flavors of fruits and eggs (Li et al., 2023). In this study, through integrated analysis of OPLS-DA (VIP > 1, P < 0.05) and ROAV (> 1), we identified six volatile compounds as potential indicators for distinguishing C and CF eggs: 1-Octen-3-ol, (R,R)-2,3-Butanediol, (E)-2-Octenal, (E)-2-Decenal, acetoin, and Dimethylthiomethane. These compounds originate from distinct biochemical pathways that may be differentially influenced by rearing systems. 1-Octen-3-ol, a unsaturated alcohol with a characteristic mushroom odor, is typically formed via enzymatic oxidation of linoleic acid (Yang et al., 2024). Its higher abundance in CF eggs may reflect altered lipid metabolism due to increased physical activity or stress. (R,R)-2,3-Butanediol and acetoin are products of microbial fermentation and can also arise from carbohydrate metabolism in the gut (Bartkiene et al., 2023). The higher levels in CF eggs might be linked to differences in gut microbiota composition associated with foraging behavior. (E)-2-Octenal and (E)-2-Decenal are lipid oxidation products derived from unsaturated fatty acids; their elevated levels in CF eggs suggest a higher degree of lipid oxidation, possibly due to greater oxidative stress from activity (Zhang et al., 2024). Dimethylthiomethane, a sulfur compound found only in C eggs, is likely generated from methionine degradation or microbial activity in the feed (Gao et al., 2023); its presence in C eggs could be related to differences in intestinal microflora or feed efficiency. It should be noted that these markers were identified based on statistical and sensory criteria within this single dataset; further validation using independent cohorts or sensory panels is warranted.

Implications for egg production system optimization and quality improvement

This study shifts the comparison of C and CF eggs from a simple catalog of differences to the identification of actionable flavor indicators. It offers a chemical road-map for producers to understand how rearing systems affect egg flavor and actively manage production parameters to meet specific, market-driven quality targets. First, by identifying key contributors to the distinctive flavor notes in CF eggs, producers can move beyond generic claims. These biomarkers offer a scientific basis for marketing eggs with defined sensory profiles, catering to niche markets that value specific flavor experiences. Second, understanding that CF systems tend to produce eggs richer in certain alcohols and ketones than C systems opens avenues for targeted nutritional interventions. Third, the identified differential compounds serve as potential tools for quality authentication. This application supports supply chain integrity and helps validate rearing system claims for consumers and regulators.

Limitations and future work

While this study offers valuable insights into the flavor disparities between C and CF eggs, certain limitations should be recognized. The sample size, although adequate for identifying significant chemical differences, was obtained from a single breed and region. Future studies that incorporate larger and more geographically diverse samples would enhance the generalization of our findings. Secondly, the identification of differential flavor compounds was based on instrumental analysis and ROAV modeling; it lacks direct sensory validation through human panel testing. Correlating these chemical profiles with quantitative descriptive analysis by a trained sensory panel is a crucial next step. Furthermore, this study did not investigate the impact of variables such as storage time or cooking methods, which are known to significantly modulate volatile profiles. Future research should explore how these post-laying factors interact with the rearing system to affect the final sensory experience for consumers. Finally, the use of a single internal standard (cyclohexanone) for semi-quantification, though common, may not fully account for the wide range of volatility and matrix effects among all compounds. Subsequent work would benefit from using a set of internal standards to enhance quantitative accuracy, and multi-omics could be used to elucidate the mechanism of how these parameters change.

Conclusion

In summary, CF eggs had higher yolk weights and yolk ratios, whereas C eggs had higher MDA level and Haugh units. There was no significant difference in amino acid composition between the C and CF eggs. Flavoromics analysis revealed that CF eggs exhibited higher levels of alcohols, acids, and ketones, exhibiting more pronounced fruity and floral notes. In contrast, the C eggs had higher levels of aromatic compounds, exhibiting a mild flavor. Furthermore, 1-Octen-3-ol, (R,R)-2,3-Butanediol, (E)-2-Octenal, (E)-2-Decenal, acetoin, and Dimethylthiomethane were identified as potential indicators that distinguish between the C and CF eggs. Our findings suggest that egg producers might tailor rearing conditions to optimize specific flavor profiles, potentially opening new market niches focused on sensory preferences.
