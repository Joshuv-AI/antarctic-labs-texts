Evaluating the Efficacy of AI-Assisted Quantitative Coronary Angiography in Reducing Procedural Time for Primary PCI in STEMI Patients: A Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Joon Ho Ahn, Seongho Park, Seung Hun Lee, Seok Oh, Yongwhan Lim, Dae Young Hyun, Kyung Hoon Cho, Min Chul Kim, Doo Sun Sim, Young Joon Hong, Juhan Kim, Youngkeun Ahn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid reperfusion is crucial in ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). We investigated whether AI-assisted quantitative coronary angiography (QCA) can reduce the procedure time for primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with STEMI.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective study compared primary PCI assisted by MPXA, an AI-based QCA software, with angiography-guided PCI in 1,041 patients with STEMI. To minimize potential bias, a 1:3 propensity score matching was performed between the treatment and control groups. The primary endpoint was the time from coronary angiography to PCI completion. The secondary endpoints included total contrast media volume during the procedure, in-hospital mortality, and in-hospital cardiac death.ResultsAfter propensity score matching, 164 and 492 patients in the AI-QCA-assisted and angiography-guided groups, respectively, were analyzed. The AI-QCA-assisted PCI group showed significant reduction in procedure time than the angiography-guided PCI group (34.9 ± 13.9 vs. 39.5 ± 17.1 minutes, P < 0.001). There was no significant difference in contrast media volume between the two groups (132.0 ± 42.2 vs. 135.8 ± 48.0 mL, P = 0.354). Furthermore, no significant differences were observed in in-hospital mortality (2.4% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.692) or cardiac mortality (2.4% vs. 2.8%, P = 0.786) between the AI-QCA-assisted PCI and control groups.ConclusionAI-assisted QCA significantly reduced the overall procedure time of primary PCI for STEMI, primarily by streamlining stent sizing and optimization processes without decreasing contrast use. Given that STEMI procedures typically begin with small-diameter balloon inflation to maintain blood flow and minimize complications, the time saved by optimal stent deployment could be clinically beneficial by allowing faster complete reperfusion. Larger studies are needed to confirm the potential clinical benefit of reducing the procedure time using AI-QCA in STEMI treatment.Keywords: Coronary Artery Disease, ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction, AI-QCA

INTRODUCTION

ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with total ischemic time being a critical determinant of patient outcomes.1,2,3 Despite extensive efforts to ensure timely reperfusion, the impact of STEMI on global health persists. Numerous studies have demonstrated that shorter ischemic times, achieved by minimizing both prehospital and in-hospital delays—typically measured by door-to-balloon (D2B) and onset-to-balloon (O2B) times—are strongly associated with an improvement in clinical outcomes. Prolonged D2B and O2B times correlate with larger infarct sizes, which in turn lead to increased mortality rates.4,5,6 Efforts to reduce ischemic time in STEMI management have largely focused on pre-catheterization processes, such as minimizing delays in diagnosis and transportation. However, strategies to shorten the percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) procedure itself have received less attention, despite their potential to improve outcomes in time-critical interventions.

With the limited global adoption of imaging-guided PCI, the GUIDE-DES study demonstrated that manual quantitative coronary angiography (QCA)-guided PCI is a feasible and practical alternative, showing non-inferiority to intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)-guided PCI for target lesion failure at one year.7,8 Recent advancements in computer vision have further opened new avenues to enhance PCI procedures. Among these innovations, AI-QCA, a deep learning-based tool for real-time automated angiographic analysis, has emerged as a promising solution. By reducing operator variability, AI-QCA has the potential to enhance procedural precision without increasing workload.9,10 Building on this innovation, the FLASH trial demonstrated that AI-QCA-assisted PCI is non-inferior to OCT-guided PCI in achieving minimal stent area, with similar procedural safety and 6-month outcomes. These findings position AI-QCA as a viable alternative, particularly in resource-limited settings or less complex cases. As interventional cardiology evolves, further research is needed to define AI-QCA’s role in optimizing STEMI management and improving patient outcomes.11

This study aims to investigate whether the use of AI-QCA can significantly reduce total procedure time in primary PCI for STEMI patients. By leveraging AI-QCA technology, we seek to explore a novel approach to further minimize ischemic time and potentially improve clinical outcomes in this critical patient population.

METHODS

Study design and population

This study is a retrospective, propensity score-matched, single-center study designed to evaluate the impact of AI-assisted PCI using MPXA-2000 (Medipixel, Inc., Seoul, Korea) on procedure time in STEMI patients. Procedure time is defined as the duration from the completion of the time-out to the closure of the puncture site. The study included patients admitted to Chonnam National University Hospital between January 1, 2021, and April 30, 2024. The study included adults aged 18 years or older who underwent emergency primary PCI for STEMI. Patients were excluded if they had intravascular imaging (IVI) such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) or IVUS during PCI. Additionally, exclusion criteria encompassed cases with a door-to-balloon time of 90 minutes or more, those with chronic total occlusion, and patients experiencing cardiogenic shock. These criteria were established to ensure a focused study population and minimize potential confounding factors in the analysis of AI-assisted PCI efficacy.

Data collection and analysis

We collected comprehensive patient data, including demographic information (sex, age), medical history (hypertension, diabetes, smoking, chronic kidney disease, previous coronary intervention, history of angina) and detailed coronary intervention imaging and procedure records (procedure date, culprit lesion location, multivessel disease and left main lesion, total procedure time, contrast agent volume, number of stents, stent length, stent diameter). To minimize potential bias, 1:3 propensity score matching will be performed between the treatment (AI-QCA-assisted) and control (Angio-guided) groups. Matching factors will include access site (radial or femoral artery), culprit lesion location, extent of coronary artery disease (multivessel disease, left main disease), number of stents, stent length, stent size, sex, age, presence of hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, smoking status, previous PCI history, previous angina history, and left ventricular ejection fraction. The primary outcome measure was total procedure time defined as the duration from the start of coronary angiography to the end of PCI. Secondary outcome measures included the volume of contrast agent used, in-hospital all-cause mortality, and in-hospital cardiac mortality.

Statistical analysis

We used propensity score matching to compare AI-QCA assisted PCI vs. angiography-guided PCI. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, incorporating patient characteristics including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, smoking status, left ventricular ejection fraction, number of stents, culprit-only PCI, lesion type B2/C, TIMI grade 2 flow, and use of IIb/IIIa inhibitors. Optimal matching was used to minimize total distances between treatment and control groups. We performed a 1:3 matching ratio, resulting in 164 patients in the AI-QCA assisted PCI group matched with 492 patients in the angiography-guided PCI group. The balance of covariate distribution post-matching was assessed using standardized mean differences and variance ratios.

In baseline characteristics, continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. For unmatched comparisons, we used χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Student’s t-test for continuous variables, as appropriate. In the propensity-matched cohort, primary and secondary outcomes were evaluated by both unadjusted analyses and adjusted analyses considering the effect of matched pairs. Continuous variables such as total procedure time and contrast media volume were compared using Student’s t-tests for unadjusted analyses and linear mixed models for adjusted analyses. In the linear mixed models, the matched pairs were accounted for by including a random intercept. For binary outcomes like in-house death and in-house cardiac death, we used logistic regression for unadjusted comparisons and conditional logistic regression for adjusted analyses. We calculated unadjusted and adjusted mean differences or odds ratios with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The average treatment effect on the treated was estimated using these regression models accounting for matched pairs. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, relevant laws and regulations, and after obtaining approval from the Institutional Review Board of Chonnam National University Hospital (CNUH-2024-148). The requirement for informed consent will be waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and minimal risk to participants. Measures will be taken to ensure the confidentiality and security of patient data throughout the study.

RESULTS

Study population

A total of 1,041 patients presenting with STEMI who underwent PCI at Chonnam National University Hospital were initially screened for eligibility. Following the exclusion of 246 patients—98 due to cardiogenic shock, 117 who underwent imaging-guided PCI (IVUS or OCT-guided PCI), 4 with a door-to-balloon time exceeding 90 minutes, and 27 with incomplete data—797 patients were ultimately included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Of these, 633 patients underwent angiography-guided PCI, while 164 patients received PCI with AI-QCA assistance. After 3:1 propensity score matching, 492 patients from the angiography-guided PCI group and 164 patients from the AI-QCA assisted PCI group were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Baseline and procedural characteristics before and after propensity score matching

Baseline and procedural characteristics of the study population before and after propensity score matching are detailed in Table 1. Prior to matching, there were no significant differences between the two groups in most baseline characteristics. However, the AI-QCA assisted PCI group exhibited a higher prevalence of previous angina (7.3% vs. 3.3%, P = 0.038) and increased use of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (6.7% vs. 2.1%, P = 0.003). Conversely, the angiography-guided PCI group had a greater prevalence of AHA/ACC lesion type B2/C (93.5% vs. 88.4%, P = 0.036), longer total stent length (36.6 ± 17.9 mm vs. 31.4 ± 12.7 mm, P = 0.001), and a higher incidence of pre-procedural TIMI flow grade 0-2 (84.7% vs. 75.0%, P = 0.005). After propensity score matching, the majority of baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups, with no statistically significant differences except for previous angina (7.3% vs. 3.0%, P = 0.031) and the use of glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (6.7% vs. 2.6%, P = 0.031).

Primary outcome: procedure time

In the propensity-matched cohort, the AI-QCA assisted PCI group demonstrated a significantly shorter total procedure time compared to the angiography-guided PCI group (34.9 vs. 39.5 minutes, P < 0.001) (Table 2, Fig. 2). This difference remained consistent in both unadjusted (mean difference, −4.7 minutes; 95% CI, −7.3 to −2.1; P < 0.001) and adjusted analyses. The Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT), which represents the causal effect of the treatment (AI-QCA-assisted PCI) specifically among patients who received it, was calculated to be −4.7 minutes (95% CI, −7.5 to −1.9; P < 0.001). This negative ATT indicates that, on average, patients who received AI-QCA assisted PCI experienced a 4.7-minute reduction in procedure time compared to what they would have experienced with conventional angiography-guided PCI.

Secondary outcomes: contrast media volume and in-house mortality

There was no significant difference in contrast media volume between the AI-QCA-assisted PCI group and the angiography-guided PCI group (132.0 mL vs. 135.8 mL, adjusted P = 0.354) (Table 2, Fig. 3A). Also, no significant differences were observed in in-hospital mortality (2.4% vs. 3.0%; adjusted OR, 0.8; 95% CI, 0.3 to 2.4; P = 0.692) or in-hospital cardiac death (2.4% vs. 2.8%; adjusted OR, 0.9; 95% CI, 0.3 to 2.6; P = 0.786) between the AI-QCA assisted PCI group and the angiography-guided PCI group (Table 2, Fig. 3B and C).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the impact of AI-QCA on procedural outcomes in patients undergoing PCI for STEMI, comparing it with traditional angiography-guided PCI. Our primary finding reveals a statistically significant reduction in total procedure time with AI-QCA assistance. Although we found no significant reductions in contrast medium utilization or immediate mortality rates, the observed decrease in procedure duration represents a potentially meaningful advancement in STEMI management.

The substantial reduction in total procedure time achieved with AI-QCA assistance (ATT of −4.7 minutes; 95% CI, −7.5 to −1.9; P < 0.001) represents a clinically meaningful improvement in procedural efficiency. This time savings is particularly relevant in STEMI, where every minute of delay in achieving reperfusion can impact myocardial salvage and patient outcomes.2,12

D2B time, measuring the interval between a patient's arrival at a PCI-capable facility and the initial angioplasty balloon inflation, has been a key quality metric in STEMI management. A considerable body of research suggests an association between D2B time and mortality outcomes.6,13 However, it may not fully capture the complexities of achieving optimal myocardial reperfusion.

STEMI often presents with highly unstable plaques and substantial plaque burden, potentially predisposing to microvascular obstruction. The ‘no-reflow’ phenomenon, characterized by slow, incomplete, or absent coronary flow despite apparent epicardial reperfusion, has been observed in a significant proportion (up to 60%) of patients undergoing primary PCI for STEMI.14 To mitigate this risk, interventionalists often employ initial dilatation with undersized balloons. These factors suggest that D2B time, while valuable, may not provide a complete picture of myocardial reperfusion status in all cases. Consequently, true reperfusion should be evaluated not merely by initial balloon inflation, but by the successful deployment of a stent coupled with the restoration and maintenance of optimal coronary flow, encompassing all procedural efforts to achieve sustained myocardial perfusion. Acknowledging these complexities, our study utilizes total procedure time—defined as the interval from the start to the end of the procedure—as a comprehensive metric to evaluate the overall efficacy in STEMI procedure.

Recent advancements in computer vision technology have facilitated the development of sophisticated deep learning-based algorithms capable of complex image analysis tasks in medical imaging, including region segmentation, feature extraction, and classification.10,15 Our study employed the MPXA system (Medipixel, Inc.), an AI-QCA software that that represents one approach to applying recent advancements in computer vision to coronary angiography analysis.

The system utilizes an ensemble of three deep learning models—DeepLabV3+ with EfficientNet-B4, U-Net++ with EfficientNet-B4, and U2-Net—to delineate major coronary vessel areas.10 For quantitative analysis, initial boundary contours and centerlines are derived from the predicted mask of the target major vessel. This AI-QCA system is designed to perform automated, real-time analyses of coronary angiography, potentially reducing the time and labor typically associated with manual quantitative assessments. By providing objective QCA data for real-time decision-making during interventions, such systems may address certain limitations inherent in conventional PCI guided by visual estimation or manual QCA.11 Fig. 4 illustrates a potential integration of AI-QCA into the workflow of a STEMI procedure. However, it is important to note that while this technology shows promise, its clinical efficacy and impact on patient outcomes require further rigorous evaluation.

While IVI-guided PCI has demonstrated benefits, its widespread adoption faces several challenges. These include limited equipment availability, requirements for specialized training, increased procedural time, and financial considerations, which are particularly salient in resource-constrained environments.16 Additionally, conventional angiography-guided PCI, which relies on visual estimation, has been associated with notable inter- and intra-observer variability.17

In an effort to address these challenges, alternative approaches to optimize stenting outcomes without relying on IVI have been explored. One such method involves the use of manual QCA to standardize stent selection and post-dilation procedures.7 The GUIDE-DES trial, for instance, suggested that QCA-guided PCI may yield clinical outcomes comparable to IVUS-guided PCI in certain contexts. However, questions remain regarding the real-time applicability and operator dependency of manual QCA-guided approaches. In this landscape, AI-QCA may offer a potential middle ground, addressing some limitations of both IVI and manual QCA-guided approaches. By providing rapid, standardized analyses, AI-QCA could potentially combine elements of IVI’s precision with the practicality necessary for broader implementation. However, it is important to note that the role and efficacy of AI-QCA across various clinical scenarios require further investigation. Additional research is needed to more fully understand its potential benefits and limitations in different contexts of coronary intervention.

While our study benefits from a substantial cohort of STEMI patients and the application of propensity score matching to mitigate selection bias, several limitations should be considered. Firstly, the retrospective, single-center design introduces potential biases and may limit generalizability, despite statistical adjustments. Second, operator matching was not feasible given the diverse backgrounds of the interventionalist pool. To address this limitation, we concentrated our analysis on procedures performed by interventionalists with 3–7 years of independent PCI experience (150–350 cases per year) to provide a more consistent basis for comparison. Further research is needed to better understand the potential impact of operator experience on procedural time. Third, the study design precluded high-resolution temporal analysis of specific procedural steps impacted by AI-QCA. Qualitative operator feedback, while suggesting enhanced efficiency in balloon and stent sizing, is susceptible to recall bias and lacks quantitative precision. Finally, Long-term outcomes were not assessed, limiting our understanding of AI-QCA’s impact on patient prognosis. Future investigations should employ prospective, controlled designs with rigorous quantitative methodologies to validate these findings and evaluate long-term outcomes.

In this study of patients undergoing PCI for STEMI, the use of AI-QCA was associated with a significant reduction in the procedure time. However, no significant differences were observed in contrast medium use or in-hospital mortality between the AI-QCA-assisted and angiography-guided PCI groups. These findings highlight the procedural efficiency of AI-QCA in time-sensitive interventions; however, its broader clinical implications require further investigation.
