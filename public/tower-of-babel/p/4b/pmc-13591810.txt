Cell-free synthesis and characterization of Salmonella, Escherichia coli, and Shigella-specific bacteriophages
Authors: Irem Iskender, Jaap Bosma, Paul Soudier, Yating Guo, Jinquan Li, Vincent Noireaux, Steven Bowden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCell-free gene expression (CFE) systems provide a rapid and modular platform for synthesizing bacteriophages without the need for living host cells. However, the generalizability of CFE-based phage synthesis across diverse phages, as well as the functional comparability of in vitro-synthesized phages to host-derived counterparts, has not yet been fully explored. In this study, we evaluated the ability of an Escherichia coli-based CFE platform to synthesize diverse bacteriophages relevant to food safety and synthetic biology applications.ResultsUsing an E. coli–based CFE system, we synthesized seven phages from four different families, achieving infectious titers ranging from 10⁶ to 10¹¹ plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL). Of these seven phages, five phages, vB_SalM-LPST153 (LPST153), vB_SenM-S16 (S16), SP6, vB_Sens_Jbel (Jbel), and vB_EcoM_Alf5 (Alf5), are reported here for the first time as successfully synthesized and characterized in a CFE system. CFE-synthesized phages remained capable of infecting host strains; however, significant differences in efficiency of plating (EOP) were observed across multiple Salmonella, E. coli, and Shigella hosts. Long-term storage tests for LPST153 showed that phage buffer (PB) stably maintained CFE titers above 10⁹ PFU/mL for 12 weeks, while purified CFE-synthesized and host-derived LPST153 lysates exhibited broadly comparable thermal stability profiles across the tested temperatures. Using the PHEIGES workflow, we also demonstrated complete in vitro assembly and expression of LPST153 from PCR fragments. In addition, phages were successfully synthesized using CFE lysates derived from the endotoxin-free E. coli strain ClearColi™, demonstrating the synthesis of infectious phages in an endotoxin-free system.ConclusionsThese findings expand the diversity of bacteriophages compatible with cell-free synthesis (CFS) and demonstrate that CFE-synthesized phages remain infectious and retain several functional properties, although some differences in host-dependent infectivity were observed. The ability to synthesize infectious phages in endotoxin-free systems and assemble phage genomes entirely in vitro highlights the potential of CFE platforms for scalable, decentralized, and cold–chain–independent manufacturing of phage-based therapeutics and food safety interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12934-026-03049-z.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health threat, with multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections already causing about 700,000 deaths annually [1]. Without intervention, AMR could cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050, surpassing current global cancer mortality [2, 3]. The overuse of antibiotics in healthcare and food production accelerates resistant pathogens, threatening global health and food security [4]. As emphasized by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), new antimicrobial strategies, such as bacteriophage therapy, are urgently needed [1, 5]. Foodborne pathogens such as Salmonella enterica are increasingly antibiotic-resistant, complicating infection control [6]. Bacteriophage therapy, a promising targeted alternative long overshadowed by antibiotics, is regaining attention with the rise of MDR pathogens [7].

Traditional phage production in industrial settings is typically carried out in bioreactors with their host bacteria, and it is hampered by several practical challenges [8]. Current methods amplify phages in live bacterial cultures, often using moderate-risk (Risk Group 2) organisms like Salmonella, which introduces safety concerns such as the presence of residual live cells and endotoxins [9–11]. Each specific phage requires empirical optimization, as the infection dynamics can significantly influence the final titer of the phage [8]. Phages can be produced in batch, continuous, or semi-continuous cultures in bioreactors. These can be labor-intensive and technically demanding, requiring fine-tuning of biological parameters like host growth rate, adsorption rate, and latent period. Across all systems, bacterial resistance can emerge over time, reducing phage yield [8, 12, 13]. After bioreactor production, phages must be purified to remove endotoxins, nucleic acids, proteins, and residual bacterial cells through multiple steps such as centrifugation, filtration, and chromatography [8]. Consequently, Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-certified facilities capable of producing therapeutic-grade phages for Risk Group 2 pathogens remain scarce [14]. These challenges highlight the need for scalable industrial phage production to increase titers and reduce costs [8].

To address these limitations, interest in CFE platforms has grown as an alternative method for phage manufacturing [15]. CFE systems are based on lysates containing transcriptional and translational machinery of organisms, often E. coli, to synthesize specific proteins or complex biological products, including phages. CFE uses DNA templates (linear or circular), focusing synthesis on the desired products [14–17]. By decoupling synthesis from live host cells, CFE platforms avoid phage resistance during synthesis and substantially reduce the introduction of whole-cell contaminants associated with bacterial propagation [16, 18, 19]. However, CFE systems are not inherently free of impurities. Because they rely on crude cell lysates, they contain residual host-derived components such as proteins, nucleic acids, and other endogenous biomolecules that remain active after cell lysis [15, 20–22]. As a result, downstream purification is often required for many applications, particularly for therapeutic use, where stringent purity and safety standards must be met [23–25]. Importantly, the nature and complexity of contaminants differ between CFE and host-based production systems. Although CFE reactions still contain residual host-derived components, including membrane fragments and endotoxin from the source lysates, they avoid the presence of live bacterial cells and substantially reduce intact whole-cell debris and host genomic DNA associated with traditional bacterial propagation [14, 22]. Using non-pathogenic E. coli strains such as BL21 (DE3) or ClearColi™, a genetically modified derivative whose lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is altered so that it is no longer endotoxic, further improves biosafety [26–28]. ClearColi™ lysates eliminate endotoxin at the source by removing the endotoxic component of lipid A, making them especially suitable for therapeutic applications where LPS contamination poses a risk of inflammation or septic shock [26, 29].

Here, we use the term “synthesis” to refer to the de novo generation of phages from DNA templates in cell-free systems, whereas “production” is used more broadly to refer to host-based amplification or large-scale manufacturing processes.

CFE systems have been used to synthesize several phages entirely in vitro, including T7, MS2, PhiX174, T4, and T6 [16, 17, 30–32]. Recent studies have shown that non-E. coli phages, such as MUC5 (targeting Klebsiella pneumoniae), PhiA1122 (targeting Yersinia pestis), Phi29 (targeting Bacillus subtilis), Goe1 (targeting Bacillus subtilis), and FelixO1 (targeting Salmonella enterica), can be synthesized in E. coli-based CFE systems [18, 33]. These findings highlight the potential of CFE for synthesizing phages targeting a broader range of clinically and agriculturally relevant pathogens. More recently, a fully cell-free PHEIGES (PHage Engineering by In vitro Gene Expression and Selection) workflow was developed to rapidly assemble, synthesize, and select phages entirely in vitro [17]. PHEIGES combines exonuclease-based genome assembly from PCR amplicons with E. coli-derived CFE reactions to synthesize infectious T7 phages within hours at titers of 1010 − 1011 PFU/mL. This platform accelerates the phage engineering cycle and allows genome editing, deletion, or host-range modification without cellular propagation.

Despite recent progress, it remains unclear how broadly CFE systems can be applied to synthesize diverse phages and whether CFE-synthesized phages retain key functional properties. In this study, we use CFE platforms to synthesize phages for food safety and synthetic biology, including four targeting S. enterica (LPST153, S16, SP6, and FelixO1), two targeting E. coli (Alf5 and vB_EcoM-VpaE1), and a polyvalent phage capable of infecting Salmonella, E. coli, and Shigella (Jbel) (Fig. 1). These lytic phages were selected for their broad host range and engineering potential. Using an E. coli-based CFE system, we evaluated whether diverse phages could be synthesized in vitro while retaining key functional properties. We further assessed infectivity, host range, and stability of representative phages and tested whether infectious phages could be synthesized using endotoxin-free ClearColi™ lysates. Together, these experiments aim to expand the diversity of phages compatible with CFE systems and demonstrate the potential of cell-free platforms for safer phage manufacturing.

Methods

Phage cultivation

Phages were propagated using traditional lytic infection in their respective bacterial hosts (Supplementary Table S1). The full panel of bacterial strains used for host-range and EOP assays, along with the corresponding phages tested on each strain, is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Host cultures were grown in Lennox L Broth (LB) broth at 37 °C (200 rpm) to mid-log phase (OD₆₀₀ ≈ 0.4–0.6), followed by infection at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01–0.1. After lysis, crude lysates were clarified by centrifugation (8,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), filtered through 0.22 μm PES membranes, supplemented with 10% (w/v) PEG8000 and 1 M NaCl, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature for precipitation. Lysates were centrifuged at 45,000 x g for 1 h to pellet phage particles. Supernatants were discarded, and pellets were air-dried for 10 min at room temperature before resuspension in 3 mL phage buffer (PB). Phage suspensions were centrifuged at 3,000 x g for 10 min to remove large aggregates, and the resulting supernatants were transferred to sterile microcentrifuge tubes.

High-purity phage stocks were obtained by cesium chloride (CsCl) density gradient ultracentrifugation following standard phage purification procedures with minor modifications [34]. Briefly, a three-layer CsCl step gradient was prepared in 12.5 mL open-top Beckman-Coulter ultracentrifuge tubes using molecular-grade CsCl dissolved in molecular-grade water. The bottom, middle, and upper layers contained CsCl densities of 1.7 g/mL, 1.5 g/mL, and 1.3 g/mL, respectively. 3 mL of concentrated phage suspension in PB was layered on top of the gradient and centrifuged for 24 h at 208,000 x g at room temperature using a Beckman SW 41 Ti rotor in a Beckman-Coulter Optima XE ultracentrifuge. Visible grey-blue phage bands were collected by syringe extraction, and residual CsCl was removed by four rounds of buffer exchange with PB using Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filter units (100 kDa molecular weight cutoff; MilliporeSigma, USA). Purified phages were subsequently used for downstream experiments.

PB was prepared by autoclaving a solution of 10 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgSO₄, and 4 g/L NaCl, followed by the addition of 1 mM CaCl₂ upon cooling. This formulation was used for all phage dilutions, long-term storage, and thermal stability assays.

Phage genomic DNA isolation

Genomic DNA was extracted from purified phage lysates using two distinct methods, depending on the phage. For LPST153, SP6, and S16, DNA was isolated via phenol-chloroform extraction, adapted from protocols described in the Methods in Molecular Biology series [35]. Briefly, lysates were adjusted to final concentrations of 10% (w/v) PEG8000 and 1 M NaCl and incubated overnight at 4 °C to precipitate phage particles. Samples were pelleted by centrifugation (10,000 x g, 30 min, 4 °C) and resuspended in 5 mM MgSO₄. DNase I (RNase-free; New England Biolabs, USA) and RNase A (Monarch®, New England Biolabs, USA) were added to remove residual host nucleic acids, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Proteinase K (20 mg/ml), SDS (10%), and 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8.0) were then added, and samples were incubated at 60 °C for 1 h. Following phenol: chloroform (1:1) extraction, DNA was precipitated using sodium acetate and ethanol overnight at -20 °C, washed twice with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and resuspended in TE Buffer. DNA quality and concentration were assessed spectrophotometrically. For FelixO1, VpaE1, Alf5, and Jbel, genomic DNA was extracted as described by McFarlane et al. [18]. Briefly, phage lysates were treated with DNase I and RNase A to remove host nucleic acids, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 x g for 1 h at 4 °C. Pellets were resuspended in SM buffer (100 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgSO₄·7H2O, 25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5), and genomic DNA was purified using the Promega Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Cat. No. A1120). Purified DNA was quantified by spectrophotometry and used directly in CFE reactions.

Preparation of CFE reaction mixture

CFE reactions were performed using in-house lysates prepared from E. coli BL21 Rosetta2 or from ClearColi™ BL21(DE3) cells by following the Toolbox 3.0 protocol [36]. Cells were lysed via a single pass through a cell press at 13,000 LBS, clarified by centrifugation at 30,000 x g for 25 min at 4 °C, incubated at 37 °C for 2 h for runoff reactions, and centrifuged at 25,000 x g for 13 min. Lysates were subsequently dialyzed against S30B buffer containing 14 mM magnesium glutamate and 150 mM potassium glutamate (pH 8.2). As previously described [37], reactions were supplemented with the Toolbox 2.0 [30] buffer components, including the 20 canonical amino acids at final concentrations of either 1.5 mM or 6 mM depending on the specific phage and reaction optimization conditions. The reaction buffer additionally contained 50 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 1.5 mM ATP and GTP, 0.9 mM CTP and UTP, 0.26 mM coenzyme A, 0.33 mM NAD, 0.75 mM cAMP, 0.068 mM folinic acid, 1 mM spermidine, 30 mM 3-phosphoglyceric acid (3-PGA), 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 1.5% PEG8000, 40 mM maltodextrin, and 30mM Ribose according to toolbox 3.0 protocol [36]. Unless otherwise specified, all the chemicals were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA).

For the ClearColi™ cell-free system, 5µM Chi6 DNA was added to the reaction to prevent the degradation of the linear phage genomes by the RecBCD complex present in the strain.

Cell-free phage synthesis

CFE reactions were assembled in a final volume of 10 µL, consisting of 7.5 µL of prepared cell-free reaction mixture and 2.5 µL supplements containing phage genomic DNA templates and, where appropriate, additional PEG8000 or dNTPs for ClearColi™ reactions. Phage genomic DNA templates were added at experimentally optimized concentrations specific to each phage (see Results section). Reactions were incubated at 29 °C for 5–10 h, and outputs were quantified by plaque assays [38]. Infectious phage synthesis was quantified via standard double-layer plaque assays by spotting serial dilutions of each reaction onto 0.7% soft agar overlays inoculated with the appropriate host strain. Plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. Negative controls included no-DNA CFE reactions, while positive controls included host-derived phage stocks of the corresponding phages with titers ranging from approximately 10⁸–10¹¹ PFU/mL, depending on the phage preparation.

Phage characterization and assays

Host range and EOP assay

To assess host range fidelity, spot assays were performed in triplicate using serially diluted lysates of both host-derived and CFE-synthesized phages. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, plaque formation was recorded. EOP values were calculated independently for host-derived and CFE-synthesized phages by normalizing PFU/mL values obtained on each test host to PFU/mL measured on the corresponding original host for that same phage preparation. Resulting EOP values were subsequently compared between host-derived and CFE-synthesized phage preparations to assess whether CFE synthesis altered host-range behavior. Only dilutions yielding countable plaques (10²–10⁸) were used for analysis. In cases where no discrete plaques were observed, but visible zones of clearing were detected in the spot assay, EOP was estimated based on the highest dilution showing clearing. We assumed a maximum of 1 PFU could be present at the last observable dilution, although it should be noted that lysis may have occurred at these dilutions without de novo synthesis of phage virions. The maximum possible titer (PFU/mL) was estimated from this dilution and converted into an approximate upper-bound EOP. Such estimates are denoted with a dot (•) in Fig. 3.

Storage stability assessment

LPST153 lysates (CFE-synthesized and host-derived) were stored at 4 °C for up to 12 weeks in PB or LB broth. Samples were taken on Day 0 and weekly thereafter. Infectivity was quantified by plaque assays. Experiments were performed in triplicate, and PFU/mL values were compared to assess long-term stability.

Thermal stability testing

Thermal stability of LPST153 was evaluated by incubating both CFE-synthesized and host-derived lysates at 4 °C, 37 °C, 50 °C, 55 °C, 60 °C, 65 °C, 70 °C, 75 °C, and 80 °C for up to 60 min. Prior to thermal testing, CFE-synthesized phages were purified to remove residual CFE reaction components using Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filter units (100 kDa molecular weight cutoff; MilliporeSigma, USA). Samples were washed five times with their respective storage buffers (LB or PB) by centrifugation at the manufacturer-recommended maximum speed for 10 min per wash cycle.

Thermal incubations were performed in a temperature-controlled water bath, with temperatures independently verified using an immersed thermometer throughout the experiment. Phage lysates stored in either LB or PB were tested in triplicate. Aliquots were collected at 0, 20, 40, and 60 min, serially diluted, and plated on 0.7% soft agar overlays with the original propagation host. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, PFU/mL values were determined. Average values were log₁₀-transformed and plotted to evaluate thermal inactivation kinetics.

Phage genome assembly by the PHEIGES method

LPST153 genomic DNA was amplified using the PHEIGES genome assembly workflow as previously described [17], with minor modifications. Two genome configurations were prepared to evaluate assembly performance. For the four-fragment design, the LPST153 genome (39.176 kb) was amplified as four overlapping PCR products (~ 9.8–9.9 kb each). For the two-fragment design, the genome was amplified as two overlapping fragments (~ 19.5–19.7 kb each). Fragment sizes and primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S3. Adjacent fragments contained ~ 50 bp overlapping terminal regions introduced through primer design, enabling exonuclease-mediated annealing during assembly. PCR amplifications were performed using KOD One™ PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO Research Reagents, Cat. No. KMM-201) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Assembly reactions were prepared by combining equimolar amounts of the PCR fragments and incubating them with a PHEIGES assembly mix containing Exonuclease III to generate complementary single-stranded overhangs, as previously described by Levrier et al. [17]. Briefly, fragments were mixed with a 2x assembly buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.9), 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM MgCl2, 10% (w/v) PEG8000, 2 mM DTT, and 1 U/µL Exonuclease III, heated at 75 °C for 1 min, and subsequently annealed at room temperature for 5 min prior to direct transfer into CFE reactions. CFE reactions were carried out using the E. coli-based CFE mix as described above and incubated at 29 °C overnight. Infectious phage synthesis was quantified by standard double-layer plaque assays using S. enterica ATCC 13311 as the host. Purified genomic DNA from T7 phage was included as a positive control and processed under identical assembly and CFE conditions. Titers were calculated as PFU/mL. The identity of recovered phages was confirmed by whole-genome sequencing. DNA extracted from assembled phages was sequenced and compared to the reference LPST153 genome (GenBank: MK907285.1) using BLAST analysis. Sequence variants relative to the reference genome were identified and annotated to evaluate the fidelity of the assembly process.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python in Google Colab, utilizing the scipy, numpy, and pandas libraries for hypothesis testing and regression analysis. To compare EOP values between CFE-synthesized and host-derived phages, Welch’s t-test (unpaired, two-tailed, unequal variance) was applied using the scipy.stats.ttest_ind_from_stats function. Mean and standard deviation (SD) values were derived from triplicate biological replicates (n = 3). P-values were interpreted as follows: p < 0.05 → *, p < 0.01 → **, p < 0.001 → ***. Tests were conducted per host–phage combination, and resulting p-values were used to annotate figures (e.g., EOP bar charts).

For phage storage stability experiments, adjacent time-point comparisons were performed using Welch’s t-tests on log₁₀-transformed titers, with Holm correction applied to account for multiple comparisons.

Phage thermal stability under heat stress was modeled using first-order decay kinetics. Log₁₀-transformed PFU/mL values over time (0–60 min) were linearly regressed using scipy.stats.linregress. D-values are reported for all conditions; however, only conditions with R² ≥ 0.90 were considered to exhibit strong first-order decay behavior. Conditions containing zero or near-zero PFU/mL values that prevented log transformation were excluded from regression analysis.

All experimental conditions were tested in triplicate (n = 3). Results are reported as mean ± SD. Statistical tests were used exclusively for paired, hypothesis-driven comparisons (e.g., generation method or buffer effects), and not for exploratory or omnibus group testing.

Results

In Vitro synthesis of phages via cell-free systems

To evaluate the feasibility and performance of CFE-based phage synthesis, genomic DNAs from seven phages, LPST153, S16, SP6, Jbel, FelixO1, Alf5, and VpaE1 (Fig. 1), were used as a template for CFE reactions based on an all-E. coli CFE system [36]. Phages were selected for their relevance to food safety and synthetic biology, as well as their varying genome sizes and morphotypes, which enable the assessment of CFE system compatibility across diverse phage families.

CFE reactions successfully yielded infectious phage particles for all seven phages, with titers ranging from 106 to 1011 PFU/mL (Fig. 1). Alf5 and VpaE1 yielded the highest titers (∼1011 PFU/mL), while SP6 showed a moderate titer (∼106 PFU/mL). Infectious phage synthesis was observed across members of the Autotranscriptaviridae, Autosignataviridae, Andersonviridae, and Straboviridae families.

We determined the genome concentration for each phage that maximized CFE phage yield (Fig. 1). While most phages were tested at two to three DNA concentrations to identify their optimal productivity range, we performed broader titration experiments for three representative phages, S16, FelixO1, and LPST153, representing the Straboviridae, Andersonviridae, and Autotranscriptaviridae families, respectively, by varying genome input across a broader range and quantifying the resulting phage titers (Fig. 2). Each phage showed a concentration-dependent response, with optimal yields reached at 1.0 nM for S16, 4.0–4.5 nM for FelixO1, and 4.5 nM for LPST153. Higher genomic DNA concentrations were not tested for S16 due to limitations in obtaining sufficiently concentrated high-molecular-weight DNA for this large-genome phage (~ 160 kb), which restricted the maximum achievable input DNA concentration in the CFE reactions [39]. For the phages evaluated in Fig. 2, 4.5 nM represented the highest genomic DNA concentration tested, as this represented the maximum concentration achievable across samples. While higher concentrations may have resulted in increased yields, these could not be evaluated. The range of DNA concentrations tested was intended to be consistent across phages; however, in practice, it was constrained by the ability to obtain sufficiently concentrated DNA for each genome.

Previous work has reported the successful CFS of FelixO1 and VpaE1, achieving titers of ~ 4.0 × 10⁸ PFU/mL and ~ 7.5 × 1010 PFU/mL, respectively [18]. In the current study, we obtained ~ 1.19 × 1010 PFU/mL for FelixO1 and ~ 1011 PFU/mL for VpaE1. This study also represents the first successful in vitro synthesis and characterization of five phages, S16, Jbel, SP6, LPST153, and Alf5.

Functional assessment of CFE-synthesized phages

To assess whether CFE synthesis altered host-range behavior, conventional EOP assays were performed independently for host-derived and CFE-synthesized phages across multiple bacterial hosts. We compared the efficiency of plating (EOP) across 12 to 22 host strains for five phages: S16, FelixO1, LPST153, SP6, and Jbel. Each phage was tested in both CFE-synthesized and host-derived forms, and statistical comparisons were performed to determine whether the generation method influenced infectivity (Figs. 3A–E). Across all phages tested, CFE-synthesized variants remained capable of infecting multiple host strains; however, EOP values varied across hosts, with significant differences relative to host-derived counterparts observed across many host-phage combinations. The extent and direction of these differences were dependent on the specific host-phage combination.

S16 showed the largest differences among all tested phages. Nine strains, including S. enterica ser. Chester, S. enterica ser. Montevideo, S. enterica ser. Newport, S. enterica ser. Brandenburg, S. enterica ser. Anatum, S. enterica ser. Typhimurium 4/74, S. enterica ser. Heidelberg, S. enterica ser. Derby, and S. enterica ser. Gloucester exhibited significantly reduced EOP in CFE-synthesized samples (p < 0.05), though the reduction was not greater than a 2-log. In contrast, CFE-synthesized S16 showed significantly greater EOP on S. enterica ser. Dublin (p = 0.0015), with an approximately 1-log difference (Fig. 3A).

FelixO1 exhibited high infectivity across most tested strains. Statistically significant EOP differences were detected on four hosts: S. enterica ser. Infantis, S. enterica ser. Paratyphi, S. enterica ser. Indiana, and S. enterica ser. Derby (p < 0.05). The first three showed reduced EOP in CFE-synthesized phage, though not more than a 3-log reduction, whereas S. enterica ser. Derby exhibited significantly higher EOP with CFE-synthesized FelixO1, with approximately a half-log difference (Fig. 3B).

LPST153 displayed a mixed infectivity profile across hosts, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) on S. enterica ser. Chester, S. enterica ser. Anatum, S. enterica ser. Heidelberg, S. enterica ser. Gloucester, and E. coli B (Fig. 3C). Both CFE- and host-derived phages showed lysis without plaque formation (denoted with a dot (•) in Fig. 3C) on S. enterica ser. Chester, S. enterica ser. Oranienburg, S. enterica ser. Heidelberg, S. enterica ser. Gloucester, and S. enterica ser. Enteritidis. CFE-synthesized LPST153 demonstrated significantly greater EOP on S. enterica ser. Chester, S. enterica ser. Heidelberg, and S. enterica ser. Gloucester, with an almost 2-log difference. On the other hand, host-derived phages showed significantly greater EOP on E. coli B and S. enterica ser. Anatum, with an almost 1-log difference.

SP6 showed statistically significant EOP reductions on seven strains when synthesized via CFE: S. enterica ser. Chester, S. enterica ser. Javiana, S. enterica ser. Derby, S. enterica ser. Typhimurium 14028s, S. enterica ser. Heidelberg, S. enterica ser. Indiana, and S. enterica ser. Agona (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3D). In all seven cases, host-derived phages showed significantly greater EOP values, although the difference did not exceed 2 logs.

Jbel exhibited broad infectivity across Salmonella, Shigella, and E. coli strains when synthesized in the CFE system. All statistically significant differences in EOP (p < 0.05) were associated with higher EOP values in CFE-synthesized Jbel. Seven strains, S. enterica ser. Infantis, S. enterica ser. Paratyphi, S. enterica ser. Newbrunswick, S. enterica ser. Bareilly, E. coli K-12 MG1655, E. coli O6, and E. coli O145:NM (p < 0.05) exhibited greater infectivity in the CFE-synthesized phage compared with host-derived phage; differences ranged from half a log to 2.5 logs (Fig. 3E).

Stability of CFE-synthesized vs. host-derived phages

To compare the shelf life and thermal resilience of CFE-synthesized phages against host-derived ones, LPST153 was selected as a model phage due to its robust synthesis in the CFE system, relevance as a Salmonella-targeting phage, and genomic similarity to the well-characterized model phage T7 [44]. Because detailed long-term stability and thermal characterization experiments are labor-intensive and resource-intensive, LPST153 was used as a representative system for comparative stability analyses under various storage conditions and temperatures. Over the 12-week storage period at 4 °C, all phage preparations remained stable, with titers consistently larger than 10⁸ PFU/mL in both PB (phage buffer) and LB (Fig. 4). No statistically significant changes in titer were observed for any group over 12 weeks, as confirmed by pairwise t-tests comparing Week 0 and Week 12 values (all p > 0.1). Although fluctuations in measured titers were observed between adjacent time points (e.g., W2–W3), pairwise comparisons between consecutive weeks using Welch’s t-tests with Holm correction for multiple comparisons did not identify any statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). These variations fall within the expected variability of plaque-based quantification rather than reflecting true changes in phage abundance.

Thermal stability testing showed similar stability profiles for purified CFE-synthesized and host-derived LPST153 phages across all tested temperatures (Fig. 5). At 4 °C, 37 °C, and 50 °C, all phage preparations maintained high infectivity over 60 min, with only minor reductions in titer observed (Fig. 5). Because phage decay under these conditions was minimal, regression-based D-value estimates showed substantial variability between replicates and were therefore not considered highly informative for comparative analysis (Supplementary Table S4).

At elevated temperatures (55–70 °C), progressive thermal inactivation was observed for all phage preparations, with increasingly rapid reductions in infectivity occurring as temperature increased. At 55 °C and 60 °C, phage titers gradually declined over time but remained detectable after 60 min of incubation (Fig. 5), with D-values generally ranging between ~ 10 and 30 min for conditions exhibiting strong first-order decay fits (R2 ≥ 0.90; Supplementary Table S4). At 65 °C and 70 °C, D-values decreased further to ~ 2–10 min (Supplementary Table S4), indicating more rapid thermal inactivation, and ~ 3–4 log10 reductions in PFU/mL were observed within the first 20 min of incubation (Fig. 5). Complete inactivation was observed at 75 °C and 80 °C within 20 min regardless of phage origin or storage buffer. Across all tested temperatures, purified CFE-synthesized phages exhibited thermal stability profiles comparable to those of host-derived phages, with broadly similar thermal inactivation trends observed in both LB and PB conditions.

Cell-free genome assembly of phages

To further evaluate the versatility of CFE systems for phage assembly, the PHEIGES workflow was applied to LPST153 to determine whether genome assembly from overlapping PCR products and rebooting could be achieved entirely in vitro [17]. Infectious phages were recovered following PHEIGES-based assembly. Two genome configurations containing ~ 50 bp homologous overlap regions between adjacent fragments were evaluated to compare assembly performance (Fig. 6A-B). The largest titer achieved with the four-PCR assembly was 8 × 102 PFU/mL, while the two-PCR assembly yielded 5 × 102 PFU/mL, compared to 3 × 107 PFU/mL for T7 under identical conditions. By contrast, both LPST153 and T7 typically reach 109 − 1011 PFU/mL when directly rebooted from genomic DNA (Fig. 1).

To verify that the reduced yields were not due to assembly errors or sequence rearrangements, the recovered phages were analyzed by whole-genome sequencing. The assembled genome showed 99.98% identity to the reference LPST153 phage (GenBank: MK907285.1) based on BLAST analysis. Six single-nucleotide substitutions were identified, all located within genes encoding tail-associated structural proteins (Supplementary Table S5). Four substitutions were nonsynonymous, and two were synonymous. Each missense change represented a conservative amino acid substitution, involving residues of similar chemical properties, and no nonsense or frameshift mutations were detected. Phages generated by the PHEIGES assembly displayed plaque morphologies, including characteristic halo zones surrounding plaques, that were visually comparable to those observed for host-derived and CFE-synthesized LPST153 phages (Fig. 6C-F). Plaques appeared within 5–10 h after CFE incubation.

Application of endotoxin-free ClearColi™ lysates

To assess phage synthesis in an endotoxin-free context, we established a cell-free system based on lysates prepared from ClearColi™ cells, an endotoxin-free E. coli strain [26]. Six phages, T7, LPST153, Jbel, FelixO1, VpaE1, and Alf5, representing diverse phage families and genomic sizes, were synthesized under two extract conditions: standard Δrec lysates prepared with E. coli BL21 Rosetta2 cells deleted for the recBCD gene cluster [46], and ClearColi™ lysates. Each condition was tested in triplicate, and infectious titers were determined by standard plaque assays (Fig. 7). The optimal conditions for these reactions are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Across all phages tested, Δrec extracts consistently yielded slightly greater titers, although the ClearColi™ system supported productive synthesis for all six phages. T7 and FelixO1 maintained high titers (> 1010 PFU/mL) under both conditions, with ClearColi™ yielding only modest reductions. VpaE1 and Alf5 also showed strong performance in ClearColi™ extracts, with titers near 109 − 1010 PFU/mL. LPST153 displayed an approximately 0.5-log reduction in ClearColi™ lysates but still achieved titers above 109 PFU/mL. Jbel was the most sensitive phage tested, with an approximately 1.5–2-log decrease in ClearColi™ extracts, to ~ 105 PFU/mL.

Discussion

Phage yields varied substantially across the seven phages tested and did not correlate directly with genome size or virion complexity. Instead, differences in synthesis might arise from phage-specific transcriptional programs, promoter recognition by the E. coli RNA polymerase, and dependencies on host-derived folding factors or assembly chaperones that are incompletely represented in the CFE system. In addition, some Salmonella-specific phages may require host-encoded cofactors or epigenetic DNA modifications such as methylation for efficient replication, assembly, or viability on their hosts, which may be absent or altered in the all-E. coli CFE system [14, 22, 33, 47–51].

Although smaller genomes might suggest lower DNA input needs, the higher concentration required for LPST153 suggests that genome length alone does not determine input efficiency. Differences in genome packaging efficiency, DNA stability, or capsid maturation kinetics may contribute to variation in DNA requirements across phages, even among those with similar morphologies [32, 52–56].

The improved FelixO1 and VpaE1 yields relative to previous work likely reflect differences in extract formulation and reaction optimization. While McFarlane et al. (2024) used the CFE Toolbox 2.0 system, our reactions employed the upgraded CFE Toolbox 3.0 platform, which features an enhanced ATP regeneration scheme based on a dual-carbohydrate system (maltodextrin + D-ribose) [18, 36]. These modifications extend reaction lifetime, improve protein folding efficiency, and increase total protein yield capacity (up to ~ 4 mg/mL in batch mode compared with ~ 2 mg/mL in earlier formulation) [18, 30, 36]. Although both studies used the same DNA isolation method, the earlier study used ~ 1 nM genomic DNA, whereas our experiments identified an optimal concentration of approximately 4 nM for both FelixO1 and VpaE1 (Fig. 1) [18]. Collectively, these differences likely contributed to the elevated phage titers observed in the present study.

The synthesis of S16, Jbel, SP6, LPST153, and Alf5 further expands the repertoire of phages amenable to CFE-based synthesis. These include large-genome Straboviridae phages (S16, Jbel), Autotranscriptaviridae phages (LPST153, SP6), and Andersonviridae phage (Alf5), demonstrating the versatility of CFE systems for expressing structurally diverse phages. Notably, four of these newly synthesized phages specifically target S. enterica, while Jbel additionally infects certain Shigella strains, highlighting the potential of CFE platforms for synthesizing therapeutic or diagnostic phages targeting multiple enteric pathogens.

Functional assays showed that CFE-synthesized phages remained capable of infecting host strains; however, significant differences in EOP were observed across multiple host–phage combinations, including both reductions and, in some cases, enhanced infectivity relative to host-derived phages. Reduced infectivity in some cases may reflect challenges in achieving native folding and assembly of complex adsorption or baseplate structures within the cell-free environment. Conversely, the enhanced infectivity observed in certain host–phage combinations suggests that CFE-synthesized phages may also differ in genome modification or particle composition in ways that influence host interactions [47, 48, 50]. Alternatively, these differences could be due to post-adsorption effects, such as epigenetic modifications introduced by E. coli CFE, that may alter the susceptibility of Salmonella-targeting phages to host restriction-modification (RM) systems [51, 57, 58].

In some cases, the enhanced performance observed for selected strains, particularly with Jbel, suggests that CFE assembly can sometimes confer functional benefits. These gains may reflect the absence of certain host-specific DNA modifications, such as methylation patterns or restriction-sensitive motifs, which could reduce susceptibility to RM defenses in non-E. coli hosts. Alternatively, the presence of DNA modification enzymes or complexes within the CFE extract may epigenetically modify the phage genome, potentially masking RM recognition sites in Salmonella and thus enhancing EOP. Such host-derived methylation-mediated protection has been shown to facilitate phage adaptation and RM in other systems, such as Helicobacter pylori phages acquiring host methylation marks [51, 57, 58].

LPST153 remained stable during long-term storage and showed similar thermal behavior whether synthesized in CFE or propagated in host cells. Following purification of CFE-synthesized phages to remove residual CFE reaction mix components, both host-derived and CFE-synthesized preparations displayed comparable thermal stability profiles across all tested temperatures. At lower temperatures (4–50 °C), infectivity remained largely stable over the 60-minute incubation period, whereas progressively faster inactivation occurred at elevated temperatures (55–70 °C), as reflected by decreasing D-values with increasing temperature. Complete inactivation occurred at 75 °C and 80 °C within 20 min regardless of phage origin or storage buffer, confirming the upper thermal limit for LPST153 virion stability, consistent with reported denaturation thresholds for tailed phage capsids [59, 60]. Comparable thermal inactivation trends were observed in both LB and PB conditions across the tested temperatures, suggesting that temperature was the primary factor influencing LPST153 stability under the tested conditions. Overall, these findings indicate that CFE-synthesized LPST153 maintained long-term storage stability and, after purification, exhibited thermal stability comparable to host-derived preparations.

Application of the PHEIGES workflow showed that LPST153 can be assembled and rebooted entirely in vitro from PCR products. Although yields were lower than those obtained with T7 or with direct rebooting from genomic DNA, the successful recovery of infectious LPST153 confirms that this workflow can be extended beyond model coliphages. The reduced productivity observed for the PCR-assembled genomes likely reflects properties intrinsic to the synthetic DNA rather than differences in host compatibility. In particular, the lack of native DNA termini, altered supercoiling, or epigenetic modifications in reconstituted genomes may limit initiation of replication or genome packaging efficiency, leading to lower overall yields [51, 61–63]. Although smaller in magnitude, the recovery of infectious LPST153 following in vitro assembly indicates that the PHEIGES genome assembly enables cell-free reconstruction of Salmonella phages beyond a single model system. Building on the prior assembly of FelixO1, this work further demonstrates that LPST153 is also amenable to this method, supporting the applicability of the approach across Salmonella phages [17].

The ClearColi™ experiments further extend the translational potential of CFE-based phage synthesis. Although ClearColi™ lysates synthesized slightly lower titers than Δrec extracts, they supported productive synthesis across all tested phages. This demonstrates the feasibility of synthesizing infectious phages in an endotoxin-free CFE system and highlights the potential of this approach for safer phage biomanufacturing.

Several limitations should be noted. Stability studies were performed only with LPST153, and the generality of these findings across other phages remains to be established. Mechanistic explanations for altered EOP, such as differences in methylation state or restriction sensitivity, were not directly tested. In addition, rebooting efficiency from PCR-assembled genomes remains substantially lower than rebooting from intact genomic DNA. Addressing these limitations in future work may further improve the robustness and applicability of CFE-based phage synthesis.

Conclusion

This study establishes E. coli-based CFE systems as a versatile platform for the synthesis of diverse bacteriophages spanning multiple families, genome sizes, and host specificities. We successfully synthesized seven phages and reported the first cell-free synthesis and characterization of five phages: S16, Jbel, SP6, LPST153, and Alf5. CFE-synthesized phages remained capable of infecting host strains, maintained long-term storage stability, and, following purification, exhibited thermal stability profiles comparable to host-derived phages; however, significant differences in EOP across host–phage combinations indicate that infectivity and host specificity are not fully preserved under CFE conditions. Integration of the PHEIGES genome assembly workflow further showed that Salmonella phage LPST153 can be reconstructed entirely in vitro from PCR fragments. Notably, infectious phages were also generated using lysates derived from the endotoxin-free E. coli strain ClearColi™, representing, to our knowledge, the first demonstration of phage synthesis in an endotoxin-free CFE system. Collectively, these findings expand the diversity of non-model phages accessible to in vitro synthesis and support the development of CFE platforms as safer and potentially scalable technologies for phage synthesis, engineering, and biomanufacturing. Further optimization and extension to additional phage–host systems will help enable clinical and industrial translation.
