E326K GBA polymorphism is associated with clinical and pathological features of synucleinopathy in the absence of overt Parkinson's disease or Lewy body dementia
Authors: Moriah R. Arnold, Shawn K. Westaway, Randy L. Woltjer, Nora E. Gray, Joseph F. Quinn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heterozygote carriers of Gaucher's disease mutations and other polymorphisms in the glucocerebrosidase (GBA) gene show an increased incidence of Parkinson's disease. We hypothesized that common GBA polymorphisms would be associated with subtle parkinsonian features, mild cognitive impairment, and “silent” Lewy body (LB) pathology in aging individuals without a clinical diagnosis of parkinsonism. The most prevalent GBA variants, T369M and E326K, appear in the general population at rates of approximately 0.6% and 1%, respectively. We evaluated 845 participants from the Oregon Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (OADRC) with SNP data generated by the National Centralized Repository of Alzheimer's Disease (NCRAD). Twenty-one subjects were E326K carriers and eighteen were T369M carriers. Clinical measures and postmortem neuropathology were compared between each SNP group and non-carriers. Although there were no statistically significant clinical differences related to synucleinopathy across groups, neuropathological analyses revealed a significantly higher prevalence of LB pathology in E326K carriers compared to T369M carriers. When stratifying each genetic group by LB status (LB+ or LB−), LB+ E326K carriers demonstrated a significant reduction in Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores compared with LB− non-carriers and a modest decrease compared with T369M carriers. These preliminary findings from a small, uni-center cohort suggest that the E326K GBA polymorphism may predict LB pathology and subtle cognitive decline in aging individuals who lack overt parkinsonian symptoms. Further validation in a larger cohort is warranted. Identifying at-risk individuals through targeted genetic screening may ultimately support earlier intervention and preventative care strategies.

Introduction

Parkinson's Disease (PD) is a highly prevalent neurodegenerative disease caused by a combination of genetic and environmental factors, manifesting into devastating movement and coordination deficits. The rate of cognitive decline and pattern of early cognitive deficits in PD are highly variable for reasons that are not well understood, but roughly 20% of patients with PD meet the criteria for Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) at the time of diagnosis,1,2 and up to 80% develop dementia during the course of disease.3,4 Recent studies have emerged, associating a number of common mutations with PD susceptibility and degree of cognitive deficits, one such loss-of-function mutation being of the glucocerebrosidase (GBA) gene.5–9 The association between PD and GBA variants was first discovered through the observation of parkinsonism qualities in individuals with Gaucher's disease, an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder with typical clinical features of hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, and osteoporosis.10–15 Since this finding, large international collaborative studies estimated that between 7 and 12% of patients with PD carry a GBA1 mutation. 16 A variety of heterozygous-carrier Gaucher's mutations have been found in patients with overt PD, the most common being R120W, K178T, N370S, N374L, D409H, and L444P. 17 Among patients with PD, GBA mutation carriers have an increased risk for developing dementia during the course of disease. 18

Interestingly, additional polymorphisms in the GBA gene, which are not associated with Gaucher's disease, 19 have also been associated with PD, specifically E326K and T369M.19–27 The carrier frequency of these milder GBA mutations appear in the general population at rates of 1% (E326K, rs2230288) and 0.6% (T369M, rs75548401). 28 A recent meta-analysis found that the number out of 200 people who will likely develop PD by the age of 80 years old for the E326K and T369M variants was 6 and 5, respectively, 24 roughly double the risk of the general population. However, GBA variant E326K has been associated with a distinct cognitive profile characterized by a greater impairment in working memory, executive function, and visuospatial abilities in PD patients.18,29 Despite these associations, most investigation of these polymorphisms derive from PD cohorts or populations at risk for PD, therefore direct evidence on parkinsonism or cognitive decline in strictly non-PD carriers of these variants remains limited. To date, studies have linked variant E326K to Machado Joseph/spinocerebellar ataxia 3, 30 supranuclear palsy-like phenotype, 31 and more broad neurodegenerative diseases. 32 These data raises the question of whether certain GBA variants are associated with a broader array of synucleinopathies with an equally diverse spectrum of clinical phenotypes and further longitudinal studies in unaffected carriers are needed to clarify the penetrance and clinical impact of these genotypes outside of manifest disease.

Furthermore, neuropathological investigation into how these common GBA variants affect Lewy body (LB) pathology and other neurodegeneration-associated pathology in post-mortem samples is limited, with most data focusing on E326K. A recent large-scale study with a cohort of 943 Lewy Body Dementia (LBD) cases, demonstrated that GBA variants, including E326K, were most frequent in cases with greater LB pathology and lower AD pathology in cortical regions. 33 Additional mechanistic studies in E326K knock-in mice 34 and human cell lines35,36 confirm that this variant promotes α-synuclein aggregation and lipid dyshomeostasis without necessarily causing a loss of glucocerebrosidase enzymatic activity, suggesting a pathogenic mechanism distinct from classical loss-of-function mutations.

Our study aims to fill these clinical and neuropathological gaps in the literature. Based on the possibility that this low penetrance may be related to insufficient time to develop clinically evident PD or Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), we hypothesized that these polymorphisms would be associated with mild signs of parkinsonism and cognitive impairment, as well as “silent” LB pathology in a population of aging subjects without manifest disease. Here, we identified 21 subjects with the E326K GBA variant and 18 subjects with the T369M GBA variant out of a cohort of 845 subjects participating in brain aging studies at the Oregon Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (OADRC) and compared clinical measurements and neuropathologic features of those that went on to postmortem brain autopsy between each SNP group and non-carriers.

Methods

Patient selection

All subjects were enrolled in the Oregon Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (OADRC) brain aging studies. eIRB 725 of Oregon Health and Science University ADRC gave ethical approval for this work. Informed consent was obtained from each subject for the retrieval of biological samples. All subjects had an annual battery of clinical, neuropsychologic, and other cognitive assessments, as described by the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC), 37 including Mini-Mental State exam (MMSE), and Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III (UPDRS). Participants were included in the study if: 1) they were diagnosed with a non-synucleinopathy (non-dementia, questionable dementia, or dementia), ie. they did not have a NACC diagnosis of PD, and 2) genetic sequencing was performed. The National Cell Repository for Alzheimer's Disease (NCRAD) coordinates the generation of SNP and other genotyping data from banked DNA from NIA Alzheimer's Disease Center participants and has provided genetic data on 1030 individuals in the OADRC, including data on E326K and T369M variants. Of the 845 OADRC participants with genotyping data in our cohort, E326K and T369M genotyping is available (as SNP or imputed data) on 21 (20 heterozygous, 1 homozygous) and 18 (12 heterozygous, 6 homozygous) subjects, respectively. Of the 845 patients in the cohort, 476 went on to postmortem brain autopsy (455 noncarriers, 12 E326K carriers, and 9 T369M carriers).

Neuropathological assessments

Neuropathological assessments were performed in a standardized manner with various pathologies assessed using hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunohistochemistry directed against tau, amyloid-β, α-synuclein, and TDP-43 species as appropriate and pathological diagnoses were assigned by expert neuropathologists.38–41 Alzheimer's disease (AD) neuropathological change was assigned according to NACC guidelines after Braak tau stage, CERAD stage, and Thal phase was determined.39,42 Distribution of LB pathology was determined via α-synuclein immunohistochemistry staining (αSyn MJFR1, Abcam) using slices from pons and/or midbrain, hippocampus, amygdala, and neocortical areas including temporal cortex and/or middle frontal cortex. 43

Statistical analysis

Clinical and pathological outcomes were assessed for normality. Those that did not meet assumptions were log transformed. However, even with the transformation, many outcome variables still violated assumptions. Because assumptions required for the Analysis of Variance were not met, we decided to use a nonparametric approach to test for group differences on all variables using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were assessed using a Dunn's test of all possible pairs to adjust alpha and account for multiple comparisons. We chose to use Kruskal-Wallis tests even for those variables that did meet assumptions for the parametric approach so that there was consistency across all outcomes for a more balanced interpretation. Because the Kruskal-Wallis test uses medians to test for group differences, we used Box-and-Whisker plots to visually display the data. For longitudinal data, simple linear regressions were completed to produce slope variables for each individual followed by One-Way ANOVA analysis to compare slopes between genotypes. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were assessed using Tukey's test. All non-independent clinical measurements that were deemed significant by either Kruskal-Wallis or One-Way ANOVA tests were further corrected for multiplicity with Bonferroni corrections (overall 6 non-independent clinical measurements with significant correction threshold of p < 0.008). We used chi-square Fisher's exact tests to test for group differences when outcome variables were categorical. All statistical testing was performed within Prism software.

Results

Demographics and cognitive and motor performance across GBA genotype

We first examined the baseline demographic and genetic characteristics of the cohort to ensure comparability between groups. Among the 845 OADC participants with genotyping data, 21 were carriers of the E326K variant and 18 carried the T369M variant. The carrier frequency of our data, 2.5% (E326K) and 2.1% (T369M) within our cohort was similar, yet increased compared to the carrier frequency in the general population as reported by NCBI, at rates of 1% (E326K, rs2230288) and 0.6% (T369M, rs75548401). 28 There were no significant differences in race or ethnicity between groups. Of the 845 OADC participants with genotyping data, 476 individuals underwent autopsy, including 12 E326K carriers, 9 T369M carriers, and 455 non-carriers. Of note, individuals that went on to autopsy, had a significantly lower age of death (Welch's t test p < 0.0001, t = 4.375), were significantly more likely to have an antemortem clinical diagnosis of “dementia” (Welch's t test p < 0.0001, t = 9.145), lower MMSE score at last visit (Welch's t test p < 0.0001, t = 10.78), higher CDR at last visit (Welch's t test p < 0.0001, t = 10.89), and higher UPDRS at last visit (Welch's t test p < 0.0001, t = 7.794), regardless of genotype. Importantly, the proportion of participants proceeding to autopsy was similar across groups (57%, 50%, and 54%, respectively), with no significant difference in last visit-to-death interval (data not shown), suggesting that our postmortem data are unlikely to be confounded by systematic differences in follow-up or tissue availability.

Of the individuals that went on to autopsy, and as summarized in Table 1 and Supplemental Table 1, there were no significant differences in sex, age at death, age at dementia onset, or APOE ε4 status across the three groups. The average age at dementia onset was comparable between groups (79.0 ± 5.3 years in E326K carriers, 77.3 ± 7.8 years in T369M carriers, and 80.0 ± 8.1 years in non-carriers; data not shown). Similarly, the frequency of APOE ε4 carriage, a well-established risk factor for AD, was evenly distributed (33.3%, 22.2%, and 38.7% in the three groups, respectively; data not shown). These similarities suggest that our genetic subgroups were broadly balanced at baseline, allowing subsequent clinical and neuropathological comparisons to be interpreted with confidence.

We next compared cognitive and motor measures at last NACC-recorded visit. MMSE scores at last visit were lowest in E326K carriers, intermediate in non-carriers, and highest in T369M carriers (χ2 = 2.649, p = 0.2659, Table 1, Figure 1A, Supplemental Table 1). CDR scores at last visit followed a similar pattern, with E326K carriers trending toward greater functional impairment compared with T369M carriers (χ2 = 1.426, p = 0.4902, Table 1, Figure 1B, Supplemental Table 1). At last clinical visit, the majority of participants across all genotypes met criteria for dementia, consistent with the age distribution of the cohort. Dementia diagnoses were present in 66.7% of E326K carriers, 55.6% of T369M carriers, and 61.8% of non-carriers. Notably, a higher proportion of T369M carriers remained non-demented at last evaluation (44.4%) compared with E326K carriers (25.0%) and non-carriers (17.7%) (Figure 1C, chi square p = 0.0163). Of note, no E326K or T369M carriers were clinically diagnosed with a synucleinopathy-related disease (PD or LBD) prior to death, whereas there were 3 individuals with PD diagnoses and 7 individuals with LBD diagnoses within the non-carrier group.

Clinical measures at last evaluation by GBA genotype.

Motor features were assessed using UPDRS, which again revealed a nonsignificant but suggestive pattern. E326K carriers had higher scores at last visit, consistent with more motor impairment, whereas T369M carriers had the lowest, and non-carriers were intermediate (χ2 = 3.138, p = 0.2082, Table 1, Figure 1D, Supplemental Table 1). When evaluating the slope of decline of UPDRS over longitudinal time points, E326K carriers on average had higher rates of decline compared to WT and T369M carriers (F = 0.08323, p = 0.9202, Table 1, Supplemental Table 1).

Neuropathological analysis across GBA genotype

Neuropathological evaluation revealed more striking genotype-specific differences. As detailed in Table 1, LB pathology was identified in nearly half of E326K carriers (41.7%), compared with approximately one-fifth of non-carriers (21.1%), and in none of the T369M carriers (Table 1). NACCLEWY scores mirrored this pattern, with the highest burden in E326K carriers, intermediate levels in non-carriers, and complete absence in T369M carriers (χ2 = 5.792, p = 0.0552, Table 1, Figure 2A, Supplemental Table 1). The regional distribution of pathology (as encompassed within the NACCLEWY grading system, see figure legend), further distinguished the groups. E326K carriers more often exhibited LB pathology that extended beyond the brainstem to limbic and neocortical regions, whereas non-carriers tended to have either brainstem-predominant involvement or no pathology at all (chi square p = 0.0451, Figure 2B).

Frequency and regional distribution of Lewy body pathology across genotypes.

We also examined measures of AD-related pathology. Neuritic plaque burden (NAACNEUR, CERAD Score) was lowest in T369M carriers compared with non-carriers and E326K carriers (χ2 = 2.877, p = 0.2372, Table 1, Figure 2C, Supplemental Table 1). While Braak stage and Thal amyloid phase did not differ significantly between groups, NACCAMY (cerebral amyloid angiopathy) scores were again lowest in T369M carriers, compared with non-carriers and E326K carriers (χ2 = 2.115, p = 0.3472, Table 1, Figure 2D, Supplemental Table 1).

Cognitive performance and neuropathological analysis stratified by Lewy body status

To better understand how LB pathology modifies the clinical and neuropathological trajectories in each genotype, we stratified participants into LB-positive (LB+) and LB-negative (LB−) subgroups (Table 2, Supplemental Table 2). As expected, across all genotypes, LB+ individuals performed worse on cognitive assessments than their LB− counterparts, however this was most pronounced between the non-carrier LB-positive and -negative groups (O-L+ and O-L−). For example, MMSE scores at last visit were significantly lower in the O-L+ group compared to the O-L− group (χ2 = 24.19, p < 0.0001, Dunn's comparison Z = 4.172, p = 0.0003, Table 2, Figure 3A, Supplemental Table 2) and this trend is upheld when comparing across longitudinal MMSE scores, where the rate of cognitive decline is significantly greater in the O-L+ group compared to the O-L− group (F = 6.239, p < 0.0001, Tukey's comparison p < 0.0001, Table 2, Figure 3B, Supplemental Table 2).

Similarly, CDR scores at last visit were significantly higher in the O-L+ group compared to the O-L− group (χ2 = 29.24, p < 0.0001, Dunn's comparison Z = 4.79, p < 0.0001, Table 2, Supplemental Table 2) and this trend is again upheld when comparing across longitudinal CDR scores, where the rate of cognitive decline is significantly greater in the O-L+ group compared to the O-L− group (F = 3.321, p = 0.0109, Tukey's comparison p = 0.006, Table 2, Supplemental Table 2). Interestingly, 60.4% of O-L+ patients were APOE ɛ4 carriers, whereas only 32.8% of O-L− patients were ɛ4 carriers (χ2 = 24.43, p < 0.0001, Dunn's comparison Z = 4.809, p < 0.0001, Table 2, Supplemental Table 2). However, the decline in cognitive function was most pronounced in the E326K subgroup regardless of APOE ɛ4 status. E326K carriers with LB pathology (E-L+) demonstrated lower MMSE scores at last visit compared with O-L− patients (χ2 = 24.19, p < 0.0001, Dunn's comparison Z = 2.862, p = 0.0073, Figure 3A, Supplemental Table 2), and this trend upheld when comparing slopes across longitudinal MMSE scores (F = 6.239, p < 0.0001, Figure 3B, Supplemental Table 2). E-L+ participants also trended lower than T369M carriers in both MMSE last visit (χ2 = 24.19, p < 0.0001, Figure 3A, Supplemental Table 2) and longitudinal slopes (F = 6.239, p < 0.0001, Figure 3B, Supplemental Table 2), though the difference did not reach statistical significance due to the small number of variant carriers. In contrast, E326K carriers without LB pathology (E-L−) performed similarly to O-L− (Table 2, Figure 3A, 3B). Interestingly, T369M carriers consistently occupy the upper range. These data reinforces the trends found in Figure 1 by showing that T369M carriers clustered at the higher end of the cognitive spectrum, while E326K carriers were shifted downward. Furthermore, the significance found for these non-independent clinical measures remained significant after Bonferroni corrections.

Further comparison in neuropathological measurements between genotypes stratified by LB pathology reveals consistent findings with those in Figure 2. As expected, O-L- patients had a significantly lower burden of disease-related pathology when compared to O-L+ patients, including NACCBRAA, NACCNEUR, NACCDIFF, and NACCAMY measurements (Table 2). Furthermore, although not significant, neuritic plaque burden (NAACNEUR, CERAD Score) was lowest in the T-L+ group compared with both O-L+ and O-L− groups and E326K carriers with and without LB pathology (χ2 = 13.47, p = 0.0092, Table 2, Figure 3C, Supplemental Table 2). There were no significant trends in Braak stage and Thal amyloid stage (Table 2).

Discussion

In this study, we set out to investigate the clinical and neuropathological features associated with two common GBA variants, E326K and T369M, within a well-characterized aging cohort. To our knowledge, this is the first analysis to combine detailed clinical outcomes with postmortem neuropathology in a population without overt PD or DLB. By leveraging both clinical and autopsy data, we were able to probe the preclinical impact of these variants in late life, offering new insights into their divergent biological roles. Our preliminary findings in this small, uni-center cohort indicate that E326K carriers exhibit a trend toward poorer cognitive and motor outcomes, accompanied by a higher frequency and wider distribution of LB pathology compared with non-carriers. In contrast, T369M carriers not only lacked LB pathology but also demonstrated lower measures of amyloid deposition, although this is an unexpected trend that warrants replication given its sample size.

Our findings preliminarily align with previous work in PD populations demonstrating that E326K is associated with greater cognitive impairment and LB formation. Studies have shown that PD patients with E326K had a higher prevalence of dementia and worse performance on tests of working memory, executive function, and visuospatial abilities.18,29 Similarly, other clinical studies have linked E326K to an elevated risk of neurodegenerative syndromes beyond PD, including progressive supranuclear palsy-like presentations. 31 In addition, studies have shown that PD patients with E326K are associated with increased LB pathology. 33 By contrast, we found no evidence of cognitive impairment or LB pathology among T369M carriers, consistent with meta-analyses suggesting no measurable clinical impact in PD cohorts.26,44,45 One novel contribution of our work is the suggestion that, in aging non-PD individuals, T369M may even confer protection against amyloid plaque accumulation, although these data warrants replication in a higher-powered cohort and is highly preliminary. Importantly, our data support the emerging view that GBA variants are not uniformly pathogenic but exert distinct effects on protein aggregation and clinical outcomes. 11

The contrast between E326K and T369M is likely rooted in their differing biochemical effects. E326K has been shown to reduce lysosomal glucocerebrosidase (GCase) activity and protein stability,36,46 impairing sphingolipid metabolism and favoring glucosylceramide accumulation. 47 This loss of enzymatic efficiency may result from disrupted trafficking or enhanced degradation of the mutant protein, limiting its availability within lysosomes. This lysosomal dysfunction promotes misfolding and aggregation of α-synuclein, consistent with the greater LB burden observed in our E326K carriers. In vitro studies emphasized that the E326K mutation significantly impacts protein interactions by altering the binding mode with the activator Saposin C, reducing the binding affinity with the inhibitor α-synuclein, and increasing the affinity for the Lysosomal integral membrane protein-2 transporter. 36 Furthermore, an in vivo mouse knock-in model found that the E326K mutation results in elevated α-synuclein deposition and enhanced neuroinflammation. 34 This inflammatory milieu further enhances α-synuclein aggregation and intercellular spread, establishing a self-reinforcing cycle of proteinopathy and immune activation. 34 Furthermore, α-synuclein pathology in these mice was accompanied by increased phosphorylation and accumulation of tau, suggesting that E326K fosters synergistic cross-seeding between α-synuclein and tau,both of which are major contributors to cognitive decline. 48 These findings dovetail with our clinical observation that E326K carriers with LB pathology had the lowest MMSE scores, supporting a mechanism in which E326K facilitates not only α-synuclein aggregation but also propagation of tau pathology, amplifying cognitive impairment in aging. In contrast, the underlying mechanism of the T369M variant has not been well investigated, with only a single study indicating that the T369M variant was associated with reduced GCase activity in patients with PD and controls compared to non-carriers. 46

An important question raised by our data is why E326K carriers in our cohort exhibited LB pathology without overt PD. Timing of disease processes is an important consideration here. Incidental LB pathology has long been recognized in clinically asymptomatic elderly individuals, raising the possibility that E326K carriers represent a prodromal subgroup at increased risk who may not live long enough to manifest overt PD or LBD. Incidental LB pathology in aging is often interpreted as a “preclinical” stage of synucleinopathy, and the cognitive and motor trends we observed in E326K carriers are consistent with the earliest manifestations of prodromal PD. Another, not mutually exclusive, explanation is that E326K confers risk primarily within a multi-hit framework, requiring additional genetic or neuropathological factors to push individuals over the threshold into clinically manifest disease, consistent with evidence that only a minority of GBA mutation carriers develop parkinsonism. Goldstein et al. 49 provided compelling evidence for such an oligogenic model, demonstrating that E326K alone was not associated with significant PD risk unless combined with another PD-related mutation, such as LRRK2-G2019S or another mild GBA allele. In their study, 37% of E326K carriers with PD harbored additional mutations, and risk was significantly elevated in double carriers compared to single carriers. 49 This finding supports the hypothesis that E326K may act as a “modifier” allele, increasing susceptibility when paired with other insults but insufficient on its own to drive disease. Within our cohort, it is therefore plausible that E326K carriers with LB pathology reflect either (i) individuals who would have eventually developed PD if given more time, or (ii) individuals in whom the absence of additional genetic or pathological “hits” prevented progression to overt parkinsonism.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. Our cohort size was small, particularly for autopsied variant carriers, which limited statistical power and prevented detection of subtle differences. Although we observed consistent trends, several findings did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, the origin of the cohort from the OADRC is poorly representative of the general population, ultimately limiting the generalizability of the findings to the general population of healthy older adults. Additionally, while we saw clinical differences in the E326K carriers as measured by MMSE scores, 1) the MMSE is not a particularly sensitive test for detecting mild cognitive impairment (MCI) as compared to other available tests, 2) the MMSE is not reliable for predicting progression of dementia, and 3) our data lacks the ability to distinguish between cognitive test patterns (i.e., short term recall, visual spatial, executive function and memory, or language), which could help clarify clinical etiology. We also lacked longitudinal biomarker or imaging data to directly assess progression of synuclein or amyloid pathology. Moreover, although neuropathological assessment followed standardized protocols, some variability in staging criteria may have influenced results. Future studies should incorporate larger multi-site cohorts and longitudinal follow-up to capture prodromal transitions. Integration with additional Parkinson's disease-associated variants, biomarkers of lysosomal dysfunction, synuclein pathology, and inflammation may clarify interactive mechanisms and improve clinical translation.

In summary, we preliminarily demonstrate that the E326K GBA polymorphism is associated with incidental LB pathology and cognitive decline in aging non-PD individuals, while the T369M polymorphism shows no clinical or pathological burden of synucleinopathy. Clinically, these findings are highly relevant for aging populations who do not meet criteria for PD or LBD but carry common GBA variants. Identification of E326K carriers could enable risk stratification for early cognitive decline associated with silent LB pathology. These findings may help inform interpretation of GBA1 variants in both research and emerging clinical genetic contexts, particularly as genetic testing becomes more common in neurology practice. However, these results should be considered hypothesis-generating; at present, variant-specific genotyping in older adults is most appropriately applied in research settings to enrich cohorts for biomarker discovery and disease-modifying trials, where understanding early genetic influences on neurodegenerative pathology may ultimately inform future preventive strategies.
