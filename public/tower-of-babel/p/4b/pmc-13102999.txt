A Benchmark X-ray Dataset for Pediatric Supracondylar Humerus Fractures with Improved YOLOv11-Based Detection
Authors: Zhu Xiong, Kaize Zheng, Huating Chen, Lingyun Hong, Lina Fu, Zhijie Lin, Suigu Tang, Yanyan Liang, Zhenhui Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSupracondylar humerus fractures (SHFs) are the most common type of elbow fracture in children, typically resulting from a fall onto an outstretched hand. Accurate and timely diagnosis is critical to avoid severe complications such as neurovascular injury, compartment syndrome, and malunion. To support the automated and precise diagnosis of pediatric SHFs, we present PediaSHF-DX, a high-quality benchmark dataset comprising 10,325 de-identified elbow X-ray images from 5,163 pediatric patients. Among them, 2,015 images were carefully annotated by two experienced orthopedic surgeons using a double-blind, cross-review protocol to ensure labeling accuracy and clinical reliability. We propose an improved YOLOv11-based detection model that incorporates a LocalAttention-enhanced Bottleneck module and an optimized transmission structure to enhance small-fracture sensitivity and improve fine-grained feature extraction. The model demonstrates high performance on a separate test set of 8,310 images, achieving a precision of 0.96 and showing strong generalization and robustness across various imaging conditions. PediaSHF-DX is publicly available on Figshare and serves as a valuable resource for developing AI-driven diagnostic tools for pediatric orthopedic care.Subject terms: Radiography, Bone

Background & Summary

Supracondylar humerus fractures (SHFs) represent the most prevalent form of elbow fracture in the pediatric population, predominantly arising from falls onto an outstretched hand1. The incidence of these fractures is highest among children aged 5 to 7 years, comprising approximately 50% to 70% of all elbow fractures in this demographic2,3. Timely and accurate diagnosis of SHFs is imperative, as delays or inaccuracies can result in significant complications, such as neurovascular injury, compartment syndrome, and malunion, which may subsequently impair elbow function4–6. Consequently, swift and precise diagnostic measures are crucial to avert severe sequelae and to promote optimal clinical outcomes. At present, the diagnosis of SHFs is primarily dependent on the use of elbow radiographs7,8.

In recent years, the development of intelligent diagnostic tools for pediatric fractures has been significantly accelerated by advancements in artificial intelligence9,10. Notably, the You Only Look Once (YOLO) series of deep learning-based algorithms has demonstrated considerable efficacy in medical image recognition due to its high efficiency and accuracy11,12. The latest version, YOLOv11, incorporates an enhanced backbone and neck architecture, which improves feature extraction capabilities and enhances performance in complex object detection tasks13. By integrating a streamlined architectural design with an optimized training pipeline, YOLOv11 achieves faster processing speeds while maintaining an optimal balance between accuracy and computational efficiency14,15. Building upon this foundation, the current study introduces a local attention mechanism, termed LocalAttention, to structurally enhance the Bottleneck modules within YOLOv1116. All Bottleneck units were replaced with augmented modules that integrate localized attention mechanisms. Additionally, the transmission structure was adaptively optimized to enhance the model’s expressiveness in fine-grained feature extraction16.

This study is noteworthy for its development of a high-quality radiographic dataset, designated as PediaSHF-DX, specifically tailored for the detection of pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures17. The dataset comprises 10,325 elbow X-ray images sourced from actual clinical cases at Shenzhen Children’s Hospital, representing 5,163 pediatric patients across a range of age groups. This broad representation ensures the dataset’s diversity and representativeness. To ensure annotation precision and clinical validity, two senior orthopedic surgeons with substantial clinical expertise conducted detailed annotations on a representative subset of 2,015 images using a double-blind methodology. This subset constitutes the high-quality training set for model development. The remaining 8,310 images, excluded from the training process, serve as an independent test set to thoroughly assess the generalizability and robustness of the proposed enhanced YOLOv11 model in real-world clinical environments. All image data were collected and processed in strict adherence to patient privacy regulations, with rigorous de-identification procedures implemented to ensure data security and regulatory compliance.

Constructing high-quality medical imaging datasets presents several challenges. Firstly, the acquisition of relevant clinical cases necessitates obtaining ethical approval and strictly adhering to patient privacy regulations18,19. Additionally, generating high-precision annotations requires consensus among multiple experienced physicians, rendering the process both time-consuming and resource-intensive20. Furthermore, existing publicly available X-ray datasets, such as VinDr-SpineXR, GRAZPEDWRI-DX, FracAtlas, and Bone Fracture Multi-Region, lack dedicated support for localization tasks involving specific pediatric elbow fractures, such as supracondylar humeral fractures (SHFs)21–34 (Supplementary Table 1). To address this gap, the PediaSHF-DX dataset was developed to provide targeted support for the detection and localization of SHFs in children. It is anticipated that the release of PediaSHF-DX will facilitate research on AI-based diagnostic tools in pediatric fracture scenarios, enhance the clinical utility of computer-aided diagnosis systems, and promote the broader development of open-access medical imaging resources.

Methods

Data collection

The elbow X-ray images used in this study were sourced from Shenzhen Children’s Hospital. The dataset spans January 2014 to December 2024, comprising a total of 10,325 elbow radiographs from 5,163 unique pediatric patients. These images include standard anteroposterior (AP) and lateral views. The original images were stored in DICOM format and subsequently converted to JPEG (.jpg) format to facilitate subsequent processing and annotation. To ensure patient privacy, all images underwent strict de-identification procedures prior to use, with all identifiable information such as patient ID, name, and date of birth thoroughly removed.

Annotation

Two experienced orthopedic clinicians annotated the fracture regions using the X-AnyLabeling software to ensure the clinical accuracy and reliability of the annotations. In total, 2,015 elbow X-ray images in the dataset were meticulously labeled with bounding boxes delineating the fracture sites, with particular focus on abnormal radiographic features in the distal humerus. To enhance annotation consistency and quality control, all labeling tasks were conducted under a dual-review protocol involving cross-validation by both annotators.

Image preprocessing

In this study, the original X-ray images in DICOM (.dcm) format were first exported from the hospital’s imaging system. To facilitate subsequent annotation and algorithmic processing while ensuring the effective protection of patient privacy, all images were uniformly converted to JPEG (.jpg) format. During the conversion process, the research team applied image cropping techniques to remove sensitive areas containing personally identifiable information, such as names, identification numbers, and examination codes. This anonymization process ensured compliance with ethical and data security standards. For efficient data management and model training, a standardized file naming convention was implemented: training images were labeled as “train1.jpg,” “train2.jpg,” and so on, while validation images were named “validation1.jpg,” “validation2.jpg,” and so forth. This clear and consistent naming scheme facilitates programmatic access and reproducibility of experimental procedures.

Optimized YOLOv11 detection model

In this study, we developed and trained an object detection framework specifically for pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures, utilizing an optimized YOLOv11 model. The detection of such fractures presents unique challenges, including the typically small size and indistinct boundaries of fracture lesions, the low contrast resulting from fracture lines often running parallel to trabecular orientation, and the difficulty in differentiating normal anatomical structures from pathological signs due to incomplete ossification in pediatric bones. To address these challenges, we implemented targeted optimizations on the YOLOv11 base architecture. A local attention mechanism was incorporated during training to enhance the model’s sensitivity to small-scale fracture features, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Architecture of the Optimized YOLOv11 Model.

In this study, an enhanced Bottleneck module, derived from the GhostNet architecture, was employed as the principal feature extraction unit. The module’s architecture is delineated as follows: initially, input features are processed through a GhostModule to achieve channel expansion, increasing the number of input channels to an intermediate level with an expansion ratio of 2. In instances where the stride exceeds 1, the expanded features undergo spatial downsampling via depthwise separable convolution. Subsequently, the features are processed through a second GhostModule for channel projection. The output from this projection is then integrated with the shortcut branch using a residual connection to yield the final output.

In contrast to traditional convolution operations, this study utilizes DynamicConv to substitute conventional convolution within the GhostModule, thereby facilitating input-adaptive feature generation. DynamicConv initially conducts global average pooling on the input features to derive a global context vector. Subsequently, it computes weight coefficients for each expert convolution kernel via a routing network. Finally, it generates input-adaptive convolution kernels through conditional convolution by employing a weighted combination of multiple expert kernels to perform the convolution operation. This approach allows the network to dynamically adjust the parameters of the convolution kernels based on the input feature content, thereby improving adaptability to varying fracture morphologies.

The GhostModule is designed to produce enriched feature maps with reduced computational overhead. Initially, input features are processed through a 1 × 1 DynamicConv layer to create preliminary features, which are subsequently divided into two distinct pathways: one pathway preserves the identity mapping, while the other generates ghost features via a 3 × 3 depthwise separable DynamicConv. These two pathways are then concatenated along the channel dimension to form the output features. The primary parameter configurations are as follows: the number of expert convolution kernels in DynamicConv is set to 4, the ghost feature ratio within the GhostModule is 2, and the kernel size for the depthwise separable convolution is 3. The enhanced Bottleneck module is strategically implemented at layers 4, 6, and 8 of the YOLOv11 backbone, as well as at layers 13, 19, and 22 of the head, to facilitate adaptive enhancement of multi-scale features.

Compared to global attention mechanisms, LocalAttention employs a localized window attention strategy that confines attention computation to local neighborhood windows. This approach allows the network to concentrate on fine-grained texture features and edge information in fracture regions, thereby enhancing its suitability for detecting subtle cortical discontinuities in the distal humerus. This design decision effectively addresses two primary limitations associated with global attention in small-object detection tasks: firstly, the quadratic increase in computational complexity with image size, which results in reduced processing efficiency for high-resolution medical images; and secondly, the potential for a global receptive field to introduce excessive irrelevant background information, thereby diluting the focus on small-scale lesions.

The training and validation loss curves—including Box Loss, Cls Loss, and DFL Loss—consistently decreased and stabilized, indicating good model convergence without signs of overfitting (Fig. 2).

For performance evaluation, the optimized YOLOv11 model achieved excellent results on the validation set. To verify that the PediaSHF-DX dataset can effectively support various mainstream object detection frameworks, this study evaluated the performance of multiple detection models under identical training configurations, with results summarized in Table 1. The two-stage detector Faster R-CNN achieved a precision of 0.921, the Transformer-based DETR achieved a precision of 0.894, and the standard YOLOv11 achieved a precision of 0.947. The relatively lower performance of DETR can be attributed to the higher data requirements of Transformer architectures, which typically necessitate larger-scale training data to fully exploit their potential. Specifically, the YOLOv11-LA model adopted in this study reached a precision of 0.963, a recall of 0.304, an mAP@0.5 of 0.308, and an mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.169, reflecting strong detection accuracy across different IoU thresholds. These results collectively validate that the PediaSHF-DX dataset provides high-quality annotations capable of supporting the training of various detection frameworks.

To further verify that the annotation quality of the dataset is sufficient to support fine-grained model optimization analysis, this study conducted ablation experiments to evaluate the individual contributions of each module, with results presented in Table 2. The ablation results demonstrate that each module contributes positively to overall performance: the LocalAttention module improves precision by 0.9 percentage points and mAP@0.5 by 0.7 percentage points, while the optimized transmission structure contributes 0.4 percentage points improvement in precision and 0.4 percentage point in mAP@0.5. When the two modules are used in combination, they exhibit a synergistic effect, with the performance improvement of the complete model exceeding the sum of individual contributions. These results indicate that the high-quality annotations in the PediaSHF-DX dataset can effectively differentiate performance variations caused by different architectural modifications, validating the dataset’s sensitivity and discriminative capability for model optimization research.

The ethics committee approved the use of the anonymized imaging data for research purposes and public data sharing.

Data Records

The structure of the PediaSHF-DX dataset constructed in this study is as follows:

Train dataset: Comprises 2,015 raw, unannotated X-ray images collected from real-world clinical settings. All images were rigorously de-identified prior to use in annotation tasks to ensure patient privacy.

Train_txt dataset: Derived from the Train dataset, this subset includes annotation files generated by two experienced orthopedic surgeons using the X-AnyLabeling tool. The initial JSON annotations were converted into YOLO-compatible.txt format via a custom Python script, serving as input labels for training the optimized YOLOv11 model.

Validation dataset: Consists of 8,310 unannotated elbow X-ray images from different pediatric patients. This dataset is used for independent testing and to evaluate the model’s generalization performance.

Detection_results dataset: Contains the prediction outputs generated by the optimized YOLOv11 model on the validation images, illustrating the model’s detection performance in real-world scenarios.

All images are stored in JPEG format with standardized naming conventions (e.g., train1.jpg, validation1.jpg). Annotation files share the same base name as their corresponding images (e.g., train1.txt), facilitating efficient data loading and experimental reproducibility (Fig. 5).

Technical Validation

To ensure the clinical accuracy and consistency of the annotation data, this study implemented a dual-annotator cross-review protocol. Two orthopedic surgeons with extensive clinical experience independently performed fine-grained annotations on 2,015 elbow X-ray images in the training set using the X-AnyLabeling tool, focusing on delineating bounding boxes for distal humerus fractures. After initial annotation completion, all labels were reviewed by the other annotator. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus meetings, ultimately forming a standardized reference annotation set. This rigorous process effectively reduced subjective bias and enhanced the clinical reliability of the annotation data. Furthermore, low-quality images exhibiting issues such as blurring, occlusion, or insufficient contrast underwent manual screening and marking to ensure all images included in the dataset met minimum diagnostic quality standards. To ensure the quality of the validation set, all images underwent preliminary quality control to confirm their diagnostic interpretability, even though they were not subjected to the same fine-grained annotation process.

To demonstrate the PediaSHF-DX dataset’s capability to effectively support the training and evaluation of various mainstream object detection frameworks, we evaluated the performance of multiple detection models under identical training configurations, with results summarized in Table 1. As indicated in Table 1, all evaluated models achieved reasonable detection performance when trained on the PediaSHF-DX dataset, thereby demonstrating its broad compatibility with diverse detection architectures. For instance, the two-stage detector Faster R-CNN achieved a precision of 0.921, while the Transformer-based DETR recorded a precision of 0.894, with its relatively lower performance attributed to the higher data requirements typical of Transformer architectures. Notably, the single-stage detector YOLOv11 exhibited the best baseline performance due to its efficient multi-scale feature extraction capabilities. These results collectively validate that the PediaSHF-DX dataset provides high-quality annotations suitable for training a variety of detection frameworks.

Furthermore, to ascertain that the dataset’s annotation quality is sufficiently granular for fine-grained model optimization analysis, ablation experiments were conducted to evaluate the individual contribution of each proposed module, with results presented in Table 2. The ablation study unequivocally demonstrated that each module contributed positively to the overall performance. Specifically, the LocalAttention module enhanced the network’s ability to capture fine-grained features in fracture regions, improving precision by 0.9 percentage points and mAP@0.5 by 0.7 percentage points. Similarly, the optimized transmission structure contributed a 0.4 percentage point improvement in both precision and mAP@0.5 by enhancing multi-scale feature fusion efficiency. When these two modules were combined, they exhibited a synergistic effect, resulting in a performance improvement for the complete model that surpassed the sum of their individual contributions. These findings underscore that the high-quality annotations within the PediaSHF-DX dataset can effectively differentiate performance variations stemming from distinct architectural modifications, thus validating the dataset’s sensitivity and discriminative capability for advanced model optimization research.

The improved YOLOv11 model exhibited robust performance on the validation set, notably maintaining its efficacy when confronted with unseen targets. This included fracture types, anatomical locations, or morphological variations not represented in the training data, thereby highlighting its strong generalization capability and ability to learn abstract visual features of fractures. This robust performance, beyond mere high mAP@0.5:0.95 scores, is crucial for clinical applicability. Furthermore, the model’s precise localization capability even under complex imaging conditions significantly enhances diagnostic robustness in real-world scenarios, thereby reducing the risk of missed diagnoses and misdiagnoses and demonstrating its potential for reliable clinical integration.

Usage Notes

The PediaSHF-DX dataset is well-suited for training and evaluating object detection algorithms, particularly for pediatric supracondylar humerus fracture localization tasks. With its strong clinical authenticity and structured organization, this dataset provides a valuable resource for medical artificial intelligence research and computer vision algorithm development.

Researchers should be aware of certain limitations inherent to clinical imaging when using the dataset. Variations in patient positioning or differences in projection angles may result in inconsistent display of elbow structures. Additionally, some fracture lesions are subtle or small in size, particularly in early-stage or non-displaced cases, where radiographic features may be faint, posing challenges for both manual annotation and algorithmic detection. To enhance model robustness and generalization capability, it is recommended to apply appropriate preprocessing techniques before training, such as image augmentation, contrast normalization, and horizontal flipping.
