Evaluating the impact of attachment designs on canine derotation in clear aligner therapy: a finite element analysis
Authors: Preeti Kulkarni, Puneet Batra, Nikhillesh Vaiid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuccessful canine derotation in Clear Aligner Therapy (CAT) relies on strategic attachment design, influencing force direction and root control. This study employs Finite Element Analysis (FEA) to compare the biomechanical efficacy of three attachment designs, aiming to optimize treatment strategies for canine derotation.MethodologyA three-dimensional finite element model of the maxillary arch was developed to simulate the rotational movement of maxillary canine in CAT. Three attachment groups used for evaluation were- VL: Vertical-angulated labial attachments only, VLP: Vertical-angulated labial and palatal attachments, VLPPP: Vertical-angulated labial attachments with palatal pressure points. Incremental rotational force of 10, 20, 30, 40 degrees was applied across all three groups. Tooth displacement, attachment deformation, periodontal ligament (PDL) stress and alveolar bone stress were analyzed and compared using ANSYS simulation software.ResultsVLPPP showed the highest tooth displacement pattern of 0.017 mm with incremental rotational force application but also exhibited highest stress concentration at PDL and alveolar bone level of 516.10 Mpa. VL showed the least tooth movement of 0.012 mm and lowest stress levels of 374.33 Mpa.ConclusionVLPPP generated the highest force output, but also induced the greatest biological stress. In contrast, VLP with dual surface attachments effectively facilitated tooth movement while minimizing biological stress. Multi-surface force application enhances biomechanical control in clear aligner canine derotation.Keywords: Attachment design, Canine derotation, Clear aligner therapy, Finite element analysis, Periodontal ligament stress

Background

The predictability of treatment outcomes in Clear Aligner Therapy (CAT) has become a central focus in contemporary orthodontics, driven by an evolving understanding of biomechanical principles, material properties, and auxiliary features [1, 2]. Key determinants of successful therapy include precise staging of tooth movements, strategic attachment design, and patient compliance [3, 4]. However, vertical movements and rotational corrections, particularly of canines, remain challenging due to anatomical factors such as crown morphology, long roots, and proximity to the buccal cortical plate [5–7]. Literature consistently reports lower accuracy in canine rotation correction averaging around 36%, compared to incisors, with errors often exceeding 15 degrees for maxillary canines [8]. The difficulty correlates with the rounded crown shape and geometric constraints influencing aligner-tooth interaction.

Given the canine’s critical role in aesthetic, functional, and structural aspects, achieving precise rotation is vital, often necessitating overcorrection, interproximal reduction, and optimized attachment design [9, 10]. While numerous studies highlight the importance of attachment design particularly vertical and rectangular attachments along with their strategic positioning (labial, palatal or pressure points), conflicting findings persist regarding their relative efficacy [11–13]. Recent evidence suggests attachments facilitate uprighting and root control by generating couple forces, yet comprehensive biomechanical validation remains limited [14].

Traditional assessment methods, such as model superimposition, suffer from landmark identification errors and stability issues, prompting a shift toward computational approaches [15]. FEA, a validated tool in orthodontic biomechanics, allows detailed simulation of force transfer, stress distribution within the PDL and resultant tooth movements under controlled variable manipulation [16]. This study leverages finite element method (FEM) to evaluate the effect of varying attachment designs - labial and palatal attachments, and the use of palatal pressure points on the biomechanical behaviour of maxillary canine derotation.

This investigation aims to investigate attachment design protocols with reliable precision and predictability of canine derotation in CAT. The null hypothesis posits that variations in attachment design and the use of palatal pressure points do not significantly influence the biomechanical efficacy of canine derotation in CAT, whereas the alternative hypothesis suggests that optimized attachment designs and strategic use of palatal pressure points can substantially improve biomechanical efficiency and treatment outcomes.

Methods

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee at Manav Rachna Dental College, SDS, MRIIRS Faridabad (Ref.No. MRIIRS/MRDC/SDS/IEC/2024/130). All procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the relevant institutional guidelines and ethical regulations. A three-dimensional (3D) CAD model of the maxillary arch (teeth 17 to 27), including surrounding alveolar bone and PDL, was developed using intraoral scans and Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) data. This was sourced from archived records of an adult male patient with healthy periodontal status, no craniofacial anomalies, and no history of orthodontic treatment (InVivo Dental ANATOMAGE, San Jose, CA, USA). DICOM data from the CBCT was processed using Geomagic Design X (2021) to segment teeth, bone, and PDL. The segmented files were converted to STL format and imported into SolidWorks 2022 (SolidWorks, Concord, MA, USA) for anatomical refinement and preparation for meshing. The model was subsequently imported into ANSYS 2022 (Ansys Inc., Southpointe, Canonsburg, PA, USA) for finite element simulation. Each anatomical structure including teeth, PDL, and alveolar bone, was modeled as an individual component with appropriate mechanical properties as summarized in Table 1. Specific material properties were defined based on recent biomechanical literature [19, 20]. Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio for each component were updated according to contemporary FEA studies and experimental work on periodontal ligament properties [19, 20]. Although the viscoelastic and stress-relaxation behaviors of the PDL and aligner materials were acknowledged, to maintain computational feasibility a linear-elastic, isotropic assumption was applied, consistent with prior validated orthodontic FEA models [16, 17].

The PDL was simulated with a uniform thickness of 0.25 mm based on previous literature [17, 21].

The aligners were simulated with a consistent material thickness of 0.75 mm, offset externally from the crown surfaces to simulate realistic fit. Vertical-angulated bar attachments (3.0 mm height × 2.2 mm width × 1.5 mm thickness) and palatal pressure points (2.0 mm diameter × 1.0 mm depth) were modelled.

This FEM study was conducted to investigate the stress and deformation patterns on teeth, alveolar bone, PDL, and attachments during aligner-induced rotation of the maxillary canines. The base finite element model was divided into three groups based on attachment design used for canine derotation. VL: Vertical-angulated labial attachments only, VLP: Vertical-angulated labial and palatal attachments, VLPPP: Vertical-angulated labial attachments with palatal pressure points. Labial attachments combined with palatal pressure points were designed to enhance rotational control. A standard horizontal bar (L 4 x W 2.2 x H 1.5 mm) attachment was used on maxillary premolars and molars in all three groups. For each group, a distal rotation force applied on maxillary canines in increments of 10, 20, 30, 40 degrees. This was used to study the displacement and stress pattern of teeth, attachments, bone and PDL with incremental force application. All material properties were set up as isotropic and homogenous [3]. The geometric model was meshed using Ansys 2022 (ANSYS Inc., Southpointe, Canonsburg, PA, USA) with tetrahedral elements to ensure computational efficiency and accuracy [5, 6]. These defined mechanical characteristics were crucial for simulating the stress-strain behavior of each anatomical structure under orthodontic forces.

The individual tooth was taken as a single structure and the difference in the material properties of enamel and dentin were not considered relevant. Teeth and attachments were modelled as a unified structure exhibiting rigid body behaviour during force application. The geometries of PDL, alveolar bone and teeth were reconstructed through a semi-automated workflow using Mesh Mixer (Autodesk, Inc., San Rafael, CA). The mesh size was taken as 0.1 mm for PDL, 0.5 mm for teeth and 0.15 mm for aligner. The process of discretization produced a total of 1,785,192 nodes and 2,689,577 linear elements on average. The contact applied between aligner and teeth was set to have friction with a standard 0.2 mm coefficient of friction. Each component including teeth, supporting structures (bone & PDL), and attachments were assigned appropriate material properties based on literature values [17, 18]. These defined mechanical properties were crucial for simulating the stress-strain behavior of each anatomical structure under orthodontic forces. Boundary conditions were defined to restrict displacement of nodes at the superior and posterior regions of the alveolar bone as well as along the mesiodistal borders of the model. A convergence analysis was performed by testing three different mesh sizes to determine the best configuration for model accuracy and stability. The results indicated that convergence was achieved with element sizes of 0.3 mm for the whole geometry. This mesh size combination was adopted as the standard for subsequent analyses.

Initial Rotation (10-degrees) and FEA Analysis: A 10-degree rotation was applied to the canines, simulating the desired movement. An aligner was designed to fit the rotated canine position. This “rotated” aligner was then virtually placed onto the initial, base tooth model. The 10-degree rotational discrepancy was converted into an equivalent force in Newtons based on the geometry of the tooth and the distance from the point of force application. In the present study, an equivalent force of 11.8 N was assigned to each 10° rotational discrepancy between the target al.igner position and the current tooth position. Past studies reported that a 0.5° rotational activation with the aligner produced approximately 0.6 N of rotational force on a mandibular canine [22].

A recent finite-element study applied a rotational load of 11.821 N to model lower-canine rotation with a thermoplastic aligner, thereby validating the loading magnitude used in the present study [23]. The FEA software calculated the stress distribution within the tooth, bone, PDL, and attachments resulting from the applied force. Stress Concentration Analysis was subsequently performed to identify high stress concentration areas which are critical as they indicate optimal sites for force application or areas where potential damage could occur.

Incremental Rotation Application (20, 30, 40 Degrees): Based on the stress concentration results from the initial 10-degree rotation, optimal force application points were determined. Rotational correction of the maxillary canine was simulated in incremental steps of 10°, 20°, 30°, and 40°. These values represent cumulative rotational stages corresponding approximately to the net correction achieved after 5, 10, 15, and 20 sequential aligners programmed at 2° of rotation per aligner, rather than a single-step clinical activation.

As stated above for each rotational stage, the canine was virtually rotated to the target angle and a corresponding aligner geometry was constructed. The aligner was then seated onto the current tooth position, generating a geometric discrepancy that produced the resulting force system and stress distribution. Applied incremental rotation forces were applied directly to the teeth within the FEA software. Each incremental rotation step was analyzed sequentially. To standardize the direction of rotational activation, the axis of rotation was aligned along the long axis of the tooth passing through its estimated center of resistance and a distal rotation force was applied [24]. This approach captures the initial peak mechanical response at aligner seating for each cumulative stage and allows evaluation of how different attachment configurations influence force transmission as derotation progresses.

Discussion

FEA quantitatively assesses direction of the tooth displacement and the physiological response of adjacent dental tissues such as the PDL and alveolar bone by analyzing the stress distribution areas [16]. FEA information aids to improve appliance design by predicting tooth movement, but its accuracy depends on precise material data and intra-oral environment simulation.

Aligners’ efficacy in rotating challenging teeth like canines is limited by reduced contact surface area, compromising force application and raising biomechanical constraints in predictable canine rotation [25]. Firstly, efficient rotation correction requires a couple force, which becomes difficult in a rounded tooth due to lack of undercuts impairing aligner grip on tooth surface [26]. Secondly, the viscoelastic properties of aligner, the temperature of oral cavity and the magnitude of forces to which aligner is subjected may lead to force dissipation before sufficient moment generation and also causes aligner deformation [27]. Scholarly literature indicates that attachments improve control of tooth movement in CAT compared to using aligners without attachments [14– 28]. This FEA aimed to evaluate and compare the efficacy of three attachment designs in facilitating maxillary canine derotation.

Labial attachments increase surface area, enhance aligner retention and assist force application; however they do not generate sufficient couple force for effective derotation, especially in teeth with minimal undercuts [29]. This explains the minimal tooth displacement observed in VL with only labial attachments. Aesthetically, labial attachments may raise patient concerns. In contrast, palatal and labial attachments can create a balanced couple force, improving rotational correction [12, 30, 31]. A published FEA study specified the optimum shape and placement of attachment in rotation correction emphasizing the addition of palatal attachment for better tooth control [23]. Tertiary data from FEA studies confirms that multi-surface forces produce predictable couple forces vital for effective derotation, supporting the current study’s findings [32]. However placement of palatal attachments may compromise precision due to anatomical irregularities, causing patient discomfort and occlusal interferences affecting compliance [31]. Palatal pressure points embedded in the aligner, offer a non-bonding alternative, delivering targeted forces for canine derotation [12, 31]. However, their application may lead to increased localized stress concentrations, as observed in VLPPP, indicating a trade-off between biomechanical efficacy and material strain. The present study’s findings are partially consistent with previous research that assessed the rotational performance of various aligner-auxiliary configurations using patient-specific FEA [12]. The study revealed limited effectiveness of dual-surface attachments, with enhanced rotation of conical teeth via palatal pressure points. FEA results aligned with D’Antò et al., showing VLPPP’s greater displacement and stress, while VLP offered more controlled response [12]. These findings underscore the importance of strategic auxiliary design to optimize clinical outcomes while preserving material integrity.

VLP and VLPPP demonstrated lower attachment deformation, indicating that dual attachments enhance surface area and distribute forces more effectively, resulting in improved grip and reduced stress concentration for rotational correction. (Fig. 6) Conversely VL, with only labial attachments, exhibited highest attachment deformation, suggesting limited resistance to rotational forces and reduced correction efficiency. PDL and alveolar bone stress levels reflect biological safety [33]. VLPPP showed highest tooth displacement with elevated stresses at the PDL, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Thus combining palatal pressure points with labial attachments can provide biomechanically advantageous approach with careful clinical evaluation. VL displayed minimal stresses but less effective rotation, while VLP exhibited moderate stress levels with effective tooth displacement offering a favorable compromise between biomechanics, tissue preservation, and clinical predictability.

To further contextualize the observed PDL and bone stresses, their biological relevance was examined. Physiologic PDL loading is typically described in terms of hydrostatic pressure rather than von Mises stress. Experimental and FEM studies report hydrostatic pressures of approximately 2–16 kPa (equivalent to 0.002–0.016 MPa) during physiologic tooth movement, approximating local capillary blood pressure; sustained pressures above this range have been associated with ischemia, hyalinization, and increased susceptibility to orthodontically induced root resorption [34, 35]. For alveolar bone, orthodontically compatible remodeling is generally associated with von Mises stresses in the 1–20 MPa range, while values above 20–30 MPa represent a cautionary zone for microdamage or accelerated resorption [36]. In the present study, the PDL of the rotated canine demonstrated peak von Mises stresses of 7.66 MPa (VL), 8.75 MPa (VLP), and 12.68 MPa (VLPPP) at 40° rotation, with proportionally lower values at earlier stages. Although these magnitudes reflect a high-load mechanical condition at the extreme cumulative rotation stage—particularly for VLPPP—they should be interpreted as the upper-bound estimates. Biological thresholds are expressed using hydrostatic pressure rather than von Mises stress; thus, direct numerical comparison will not be applicable. Moreover, the model is static and linear-elastic, capturing the peak load at aligner seating without accounting for viscoelastic relaxation of the PDL or time-dependent force decay in thermoplastic aligners. Accordingly, the values reflect relative biomechanical differences between attachment configurations rather than literal predictions of periodontal pathology. These findings suggest that while VLPPP provides superior rotational control, large cumulative rotations should be staged judiciously and monitored in patients with reduced periodontal support. Clinically, canine rotations approaching 40° are typically managed using staged aligner therapy supplemented with auxiliary biomechanical aids. Accordingly, the present simulations should be interpreted as an evaluation of the biomechanical capacity and comparative efficiency of different attachment designs under progressively demanding rotational conditions, rather than as aligner-only treatment protocols. Accordingly, the PDL and bone stresses reported—especially at 40° rotation—represent the upper-bound values, while the comparative pattern between VL, VLP and VLPPP remains robust because all configurations were modeled under identical assumptions.

In the present FEA, 10°, 20°, 30°, and 40° rotational increment configurations represent cumulative treatment stages, corresponding approximately to the net rotation achieved after 5, 10, 15, and 20 sequential aligners programmed at 2° rotational correction each. At each stage, a virtual aligner was constructed to the rotated position and then seated back onto the current tooth geometry, so that the resulting discrepancy and load state reflect the instantaneous biomechanical condition of the system at that cumulative rotation. This design allowed evaluation of how attachment configurations behave as derotation progresses, while remaining computationally tractable. The equivalent rotational force assigned to each 10° stage (11.8 N) was based on past literature [23, 37]. While clinical aligners typically express only 1–2° of rotational correction per aligner, the present model uses 10° increments to represent cumulative treatment stages (equivalent to five 2° aligners), with 11.8 N serving as the initial peak force at each cumulative stage. This provides a conservative upper-bound evaluation of the biomechanical response during progressive rotation, enabling meaningful assessment of attachment efficiency as derotation advances. Higher rotational increments up to 40° represent cumulative derotation scenarios observed in severely rotated maxillary canines requiring additional biomechanical auxiliaries in conjunction with staged aligner therapy in clinical scenarios.

This approach enhances the clinical interpretability of the findings and aligns the FEA framework with the way aligner staging is planned in practice, thereby providing meaningful insights into attachment biomechanics during advanced derotation.

Although full maxillary arch was reconstructed in the present study, the quantitative evaluation focused exclusively on the maxillary canine. The objective was to assess the efficiency and comparative performance of varied attachment configurations with incremental rotational correction. Clear aligners generate an integrated, arch-wide force system, but individual outcomes for adjacent incisors, premolars, and the contralateral canine were not quantified in the present study. Detailed mapping of whole-arch displacements and stress transmission patterns under the “integrated force application” of aligners is an important aspect and should be addressed in future finite element studies.

Nonetheless, secondary movements are inherent to clear aligner mechanics and provide important context for interpreting canine derotation behavior. Various secondary movements like intrusion, mesiodistal tipping and torque loss during canine derotation with CAT have been noted in the past studies [4, 5, 7, 14]. Unwanted vertical movements such as intrusion occur if the rotational force vector does not pass through the tooth’s center of resistance and aligner contacts over the attachment generating intrusive forces [37]. VLP and VLPPP, with palatal attachment components, may have exhibited increased intrusion due to vertical forces from aligner contact over multi-surface attachments away from center of resistance of the conical tooth. (Fig. 8) VL showed maximal distal tipping due to the absence of a counterbalancing force from the palatal side, leading to tipping rather than bodily rotation. Palatal attachments and pressure points improved torque control compared to the sole labial attachment in VL, supporting findings that combining labial and palatal attachments enhance root control and rotational stability in CAT [30].

This finite element model incorporates several simplifications that influence the magnitude and direction of the computed responses. The use of a single standardized tooth–bone morphology and uniform PDL thickness limits patient-specific applicability, as anatomical variations may produce higher or lower stresses than predicted. The stiff boundary conditions may reduce simulated anchorage loss and modestly inflate rotational efficiency, while underestimating far-field strain. The assumption of a homogeneous tooth structure marginally elevates tooth stiffness and consequently yields conservative, upper-bound stress predictions within the PDL and alveolar bone.While these simplifications influence absolute magnitudes, they do not alter the comparative force systems generated by the attachments; therefore, the central conclusion regarding the superior rotational efficiency of specific attachment designs remains valid.

The present simulation adopted linear-elastic assumptions for the PDL and aligner materials, which may not fully capture their time-dependent viscoelastic or stress-relaxation characteristics. Linear modeling provides a reliable approximation of the initial mechanical response when an aligner is first seated—representing the peak force condition, which is most relevant for evaluating biomechanical safety and efficiency. Incorporating nonlinear constitutive models could enhance the biological accuracy of future analyses [19, 20]. Future research should explore diverse tooth types, complex movements, aligner materials, and dynamic pressure application to enhance clinical relevance and optimize treatment strategies.
